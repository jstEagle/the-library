## 24. The Mountain Speaks

The tree-heart's chamber did not release us so much as forget to hold us.

One moment I was pressed against the living wood, both palms flat against the Rootcrown where it pulsed in its cradle of roots like a second heart beating inside the mountain's first one, and the next the ground simply wasn't there. Not falling—falling has a direction, and this had none. Theo shouted something that might have been my name. Pip, who'd insisted on coming down with us because "the roots talk to me, they don't talk to *you*, you're all elbows," made a sound like a delighted kettle.

Then light took us.

Not the clean white of storm-light or the gold of the gilded court's insufferable radiance. This was green-gold, deep as pond water, and it came from everywhere at once, which should have been blinding but instead felt like being *read*. Like something enormous had opened my ribs like a book and was skimming.

*So,* said a voice that was not a voice. It arrived the way pressure arrives before weather—not in the ears but in the teeth, the marrow, the water standing in a cup. *You are the latest hand.*

"I don't—" My mouth worked. Somewhere very far away Theo was still shouting. "*Latest hand* on what?"

*On the rope.* A pause, long enough for me to feel it in my spine. *Hold still. You keep trying to fall. There is nothing here to fall into.*

And then the mountain showed me.

* * *

We stood—or seemed to stand—on a ridge that no longer existed. I knew every stone of Thornfell the way you know your own hands, and these stones were strangers: younger, rawer, unweathered by the centuries of wind and smuggling traffic that had worn the passes smooth. The sky above was wrong too. It moved. Not clouds moving across it—the whole vault of it turned slowly, visibly, like the lid of a jar someone was loosening.

Below us, where Marrowgate would someday stand, there was only a valley of black soil and river mist, and in it two peoples facing each other across a line of standing stones.

On one side, humans—fewer than I expected. Maybe three hundred, wrapped in hides, holding iron tools the way frightened people hold anything: too tightly, pointed outward. On the other side, the fae.

I had seen fae before. Vess, hollow and gray as ash-paper. The gilded court's envoy, beautiful the way a knife is beautiful if you only look at the edge. But these were neither. They were *vivid*, is the only word I have—colors that hurt, faces that shifted between forms the way flame shifts, tall as trees and small as children and sometimes both at once. And they were afraid. That was the part no song had ever mentioned. The fae in the histories were always triumphant or terrible, never *afraid*, and yet here they stood at the edge of their own realm, watching the veil between worlds thin and fray, and they looked exactly like the villagers of Marrowgate had looked when we told them the winter was coming back.

Two peoples, equally frightened, equally armed, separated by stones and mutual ignorance.

"They're going to fight," Theo said beside me. He was there—of course he was there, the mountain had pulled him in too, and his face had gone scholar-pale, the look he got when a text confirmed something awful. "This is the war. The one before the histories start. Everyone fights over the last good land while the veil fails, and—"

The scene moved, the way dreams move, cutting past days or years in a breath.

The line of standing stones was broken now. Smoke. Bodies on both sides—I remember one image specifically, a fae woman with bark-colored skin cradling a human child who was not hers, both of them dead of the same wound, and how the mountain held that image a moment longer than the others, as if it mattered most. Perhaps it did.

Then: a figure walking out onto the broken field alone.

She was half-blood. I knew it before anyone in the vision said it, knew it the way you know your own reflection in a dark window—by the shape of her, the way she stood slightly apart from both lines, belonging fully to neither. Storm-gray eyes. Hair like wet slate. And around her wrists, old scars in a pattern I recognized with a lurch that nearly buckled me, because I have the same ones, made the same way, by my own hand, on the worst night of my exile: the marks of a storm-caller who tried to cut her power out of herself rather than be what she was.

"She called storms," I heard myself whisper.

*She called storms,* the mountain agreed. *As you do. As the order taught you to shame yourself for.*

"Who was she?"

*Her name was Yeva Halloran.*

The world went very quiet. Somewhere behind me Theo made a small sound, the kind scholars make when two texts suddenly agree.

Halloran. My name. Not a family name passed down through generations of lowland farmers, not an alias I'd invented for the smuggling trade, but *hers*—a thread running unbroken through four hundred years of women who thought they were nobody, who married quietly and died quietly and told their daughters stories about the weather like it was just the weather.

"All this time," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "I thought the name was nothing. I thought I was nothing."

*You were never nothing,* the mountain said, with a patience that was almost insulting. *You were hidden. There is a difference, though I grant it is a cruel one.*

The vision turned again.

Yeva walked between the armies—no, there were no armies anymore, only remnants, survivors staring at her—and she raised her hands, and the sky *answered*. Not violently. That was the thing that undid me. Every story the storm-callers' order ever told began with domination: the caller seizes the storm, bends it, breaks it to will. But when Yeva raised her hands the storm came to her like a dog comes home, like something that had been waiting, and she didn't seize it. She *greeted* it. She spoke in two tongues at once—one human, one that made my bones hum in recognition—and the veil itself seemed to lean down to listen.

I understood then, watching, what the covenant actually was. Not a treaty. Not words on vellum sealed with wax. A *voice*. Someone had to stand between two realms that could not hear each other and speak loudly enough for both. The keystones weren't locks. They were amplifiers. And the warden—

The warden was the voice.

Yeva gathered the shattered pieces of the old pact—the fragments I would spend a season of my life hunting—and she carried them to a spire of bare rock at the heart of Thornfell, and there, before both peoples, she did the thing the prophecy books leave vague because the truth is unbearable:

She gave herself away.

Not died, exactly. Died is an ending, and what happened to Yeva Halloran had no end in it. She let the mountain take her voice—her storms, her selfhood, the ordinary continuation of a life—and in exchange she became the speaking-between, the standing-in-the-doorway, the weather itself given a woman's stubbornness. I watched her do it willingly. I watched her *smile* as she did it, which was somehow worse, and I watched the veil close like a healing wound, and I watched the two frightened peoples lower their weapons and, eventually, build villages and chapels and smuggling routes through the peace she'd purchased with everything she was.

*Inheritance,* said the mountain.

"That's not inheritance," I said harshly. "That's a debt. That's a rope tied around my ankle before I was born."

*Yes,* said the mountain, agreeably. *Both things are true. Ropes are also how you climb.*

* * *

The vision released us near dawn, if dawn meant anything inside a mountain. We lay on the root-chamber floor gasping like landed fish, and Pip was already sitting up cross-legged, eating something she'd definitely stolen from Theo's pack.

"The roots showed me a badger," she announced. "It was rude."

"Lucky," I rasped.

Theo wasn't lucky. Theo was sitting with his head in his hands, and when he lifted his face I saw he'd been crying without noticing, the way men cry when they've been doing arithmetic instead of feeling. "Yeva Halloran," he said. "The author. Ulyana, the book—the prophecy book, my grandmother's book—she *copied* it. From older pages. Pages written by—" He stopped. Started again. "Your ancestor wrote the original oath. My grandmother spent her life copying it and died protecting the copy. Our families have been carrying the same rope for four hundred years and neither of us knew."

"Say it plainly, Theo."

"You're not just in the prophecy." He reached for my hand, hesitated, and I closed the distance myself, because pride is heavy and I was tired of carrying it alone. "You're its *author's blood*. The mountain didn't choose you. It *recognized* you."

I wanted to laugh. I wanted to say something wry and deflecting, the way I say everything, the way I've said everything since Serapha's face went carefully blank at my trial. What came out instead was smaller.

"I don't know how to do what she did."

"No one does," Theo said. "That's why it's called sacrifice and not a chore list."

Pip looked up from her stolen bread. "I think you'd be good at it," she said, with the terrifying directness of children. "You're bossy like the weather."

Before I could decide whether to be touched or offended, the chamber shuddered.

Not the mountain settling. Not the deep groan of stone remembering it's stone. This was sharp, percussive, *wrong*—and it came from above us, from the direction of the surface, and with it came a vibration through the roots that I felt in my teeth and recognized instantly, because I'd felt it once before across a burning village:

The taste of gilded-court magic. A lot of it. All at once.

Theo was on his feet. "The camp."

Pip's face had gone white under her freckles. "Dagny's up there. Ivo. Everyone—"

The mountain's voice returned one last time, and for the first time since I'd known it—which was all my life, in every thunderhead and every freak snow—it sounded *hurried*.

*They have stopped waiting, latest hand. Your family stands between them and the third keystone's road. Choose quickly what the rope is for.*

I was already running.
