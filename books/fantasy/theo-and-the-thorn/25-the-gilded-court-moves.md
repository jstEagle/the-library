## 25. The Gilded Court Moves

The trouble with beautiful things, I've learned, is that they never knock.

They arrive. They shimmer. They make you feel honored to be robbed.

I was mending the strap of my satchel when the light changed—not the gradual gold of late afternoon but something sudden and theatrical, as though the sun had decided, on a whim, to audition. The whole camp stopped. Dagny's whetstone paused mid-stroke against her axe. Ivo's head came up like a deer's, and Pip, who had been teaching Ulyana's fingers a clapping game with the ruthless patience of an eight-year-old, went absolutely still in a way children only go still when their blood knows something first.

"They're here," Pip said. Not frightened. Flat. Certain. "The shiny ones."

I want it recorded, in whatever book I eventually write about all this, that we had warning. We had days of warning. Vess had told us the gilded court would not wait forever; Ivo had explained that patience is not a virtue among the fae but a strategy, deployed until it isn't; Ulyana herself had said—more than once, with her arms crossed, in that voice she uses when she's being right at people—that envoys who leave without a yes always come back with soldiers instead of gifts.

And yet when the meadow at the camp's edge filled with radiance, every one of us stood there like idiots admiring it.

They came through the trees in procession. That's the only word for it. Twelve figures in armor that wasn't armor—plates of what looked like hammered dawn, gold shot through with veins of rose light—and before them walked the envoy from before, the one whose name I never learned because he introduced himself the way weather introduces itself: by arriving. He wore white and gold now, no more traveler's gray, and his smile was the same patient, lovely thing it had been at our first meeting, which should have told us everything.

"Master Bramble." He inclined his head to me first, which was either courtesy or a message. Possibly both. "Scholar. Author's blood. You look well."

"I've been sleeping badly," I said, because apparently my mouth makes decisions without me when I'm terrified.

The envoy's smile widened by a degree. Somewhere behind him, the twelve armored figures spread out along the treeline with the unhurried precision of people closing a net they'd already finished tying.

"We have come," he said, "to collect what was promised."

"Nothing was promised," Ulyana said.

She'd come forward while I was busy being witty, and I felt her presence before I heard her—the way you feel a storm front move into a valley. Her hands were loose at her sides. Her eyes were doing arithmetic.

"Nothing was promised *by us*," the envoy corrected gently. "The covenant itself made promises. The keystones are the property of both courts. One court has fallen silent. The other has not. Therefore—" he gestured, gracious, terrible, "—they revert."

"To you."

"To the crown that endures. Yes." He said it the way a banker says *yes* about interest. No cruelty in it. Worse than cruelty: procedure.

I watched Ulyana's jaw tighten, watched her weigh it—the same weighing I'd seen her do over smugglers' fees and mountain passes and the question of whether to trust a nervous scholar with a burned book. She counted exits the way other people counted money. And I could see, even then, even before it happened, that there were none. The twelve weren't guarding the treeline. They were *being* the treeline. The fae roads Pip used, the passes Ulyana knew, the river route Dagny favored—all of them, I understood suddenly, were already occupied or already closed. This hadn't begun today. It had probably begun the moment our envoy left without his answer, weeks ago. Perhaps the moment we took the Bell of Hours.

Perhaps the moment the prophecy named her.

"The answer," Ulyana said slowly, "is still no."

"Then I regret," said the envoy, and for just a heartbeat the beauty flickered—like lamplight guttering—and underneath it I saw something old and cold and hungry, something that had been waiting under all that polish since before my grandmother was born, "that regret is the one thing my court does not permit me to feel for long."

* * *

It happened fast after that, the way disasters do—slowly, and then all at once.

I'll give them this: they were efficient. There was no battle worth the name. Ivo threw lightning and it broke against the lead soldier's breastplate like rain against a window. Dagny put her axe into another one's side and the metal rang, rang like a bell struck by a fool, and the soldier turned its helmeted head with what I swear was mild interest, the way you'd glance at a dog barking at a carriage wheel.

Ulyana did the worst thing. She reached for the storm—I felt the air go taut around her, felt the pressure drop the way it does before hail—and the envoy simply raised one hand, almost apologetic, and the sky above the camp went smooth as glass.

"Ah," he said softly. "No. This is our weather now, warden-to-be. Haven't you heard? Winter belongs to the crown that survives it."

That was when I understood the shape of the trap, and understanding arrived too late to matter. The endless winter wasn't just failing covenant. It was *leverage*. Every village frozen, every border farm blackened, every desperate lowland prayer rising toward Thornfell—it was all of it pressure applied to a single point, and the point was her. The gilded court didn't need to defeat the storm-caller. It needed her to believe that only surrendering the keystones could stop the suffering. That was why the envoy's offer had been so generous. That was why sanctuary, legitimacy, everything. They weren't courting her.

They were acquiring her.

"Take them," the envoy said, and his voice had gone gentle again, which was somehow worse. "All of them. Gently. We are not savages."

Gently. I'll remember that word. The soldier who took my arm had hands like warm stone and grip like a vise wrapped in velvet, and it steered me among the tents while the camp came apart around us. Dagny fought until three of them pinned her, snarling curses in ferrywoman's cant that would've blistered paint. Ivo they took almost respectfully—a defector might be useful, or might be an example; the fae keep such options open. Ulyana they surrounded without touching, six of them forming a slow, glittering cage, because you don't grab a storm. You let it exhaust itself against glass.

And Pip—

Pip ran. Of course Pip ran. The child saw roads none of us could see, slipped between two soldiers like water through fingers, and was ten yards into the trees, actually *free*, actually gone, before one of the twelve moved faster than anything that size should move and simply picked her up.

Pip screamed. I have heard a lot of screams in the last year. War-cries, grief-screams, the scream Dagny made holding the Sorrow Bridge. Pip's was different. It was small, and furious, and betrayed, and it went through me like a nail through paper.

"Pip!" Ulyana's voice cracked across the meadow, and for one glorious second the glass-smooth sky shivered, a single thunderhead bulging up out of nothing like a fist through cloth. The envoy looked up at it with something that might have been appreciation.

"There she is," he murmured. "There's the keystone."

Then the pressure returned, the thunderhead flattened, and Ulyana stood in the center of six golden soldiers with her fists shaking at her sides and tears cutting clean lines down her dusty face, and she did not fight anymore. Because fighting meant Pip paying for it. Because the child hung limp in a soldier's grip, sobbing with rage, and the envoy was watching Ulyana watch her with the expression of a man confirming a purchase.

"You see how this goes," he said kindly. "Every time you reach for the sky, someone small pays for it. So don't. Walk. Quietly. And in a few days, when you've thought it over in comfort, you'll give me the two keystones you carry, and we'll discuss where the third sleeps, and everyone will live long, comfortable lives."

Ulyana looked at him. Then at Pip. Then—at the last—briefly, unbearably, at me.

I mouthed it before I knew I was going to: *"Don't give them anything."*

Her eyes flared. For a heartbeat I saw the whole argument we'd have later compressed into a single look: fury, terror, gratitude, and beneath all of it, iron. She'd spent her whole life learning how to survive captivity. She'd counted her exits out of every room since she was younger than Pip. And every exit was closed—but I saw her file that fact away the way she filed everything, cold and careful, a debt entered in a ledger to be collected later.

"Comfortable lives," she repeated flatly. "You mean quiet ones."

"I mean unremarkable ones," the envoy agreed. "Which is the same thing, from a certain height."

* * *

They marched us east, toward the light.

That sounds like poetry. It wasn't. It was a road of packed snow through country I didn't recognize, flanked by our twelve captors, and the strange thing—the thing I kept turning over in my head like a stone—was how pleasant they tried to make it. Fires were built each night, large and warm. Food appeared: bread that tasted of honey, meat that tasted of summer. Blankets that held heat like living things. The gilded court's cruelty, I realized on the second night, was upholstered. They wanted us healthy, cooperative, and quietly grateful, because gratitude is easier to manage than fear.

Dagny refused the food on principle and got hungrier and angrier in equal measure. Ivo ate what was given and watched everything, cataloguing, a defector reading his new owners' habits. Ulyana sat apart each evening, hands bound in silk—silk, because rope might bruise and bruises are ungracious—staring north toward Thornfell with an expression I couldn't read and was afraid to ask about.

I sat next to her on the third night, close enough that our shoulders touched, and said nothing for a long time. That's a skill I learned from her, actually. Silence as a shared room rather than a wall.

"They're taking us to the court," she finally said. "Not a prison. The court itself. He wants me compliant, and compliance grows better in gardens than cells."

"That's good, isn't it? Gardens have doors."

"Gardens have gardeners." She flexed her bound hands. "Theo. When we get there—and we will get there—they're going to show me things. Comforts. Belonging. A seat at a table I've been locked out of my whole life." Her voice didn't waver, but her hands did, just slightly, inside the silk. "And part of me is going to want it so badly it will feel like drowning in reverse. So I need you to remember something for me."

"Anything."

"If I start believing them—if I start sounding like someone who thinks the offer is fair—you tell me about Marrowgate." Her jaw tightened. "Tell me about the frost-black fields and the quarantine fires. Tell me exactly what their weather costs. Don't be kind about it."

"Marrowgate burns in my sleep," I said. "I can be very unkind."

Something that was almost a smile crossed her face. "I know. It's one of your better qualities."

Above us, past the glass-flat sky, the stars were wrong—too bright, too arranged, constellations rearranged like furniture. The gilded court's weather followed its prisoners. Even the heavens here were upholstered.

I lay back against the cold ground, under the borrowed warmth of a stolen blanket, and did what I always do when the world exceeds me: I thought about books. My grandmother had carried hers through fire. She'd died so that pages naming Ulyana could reach a waystation inn and a guide nobody else would hire. And now the book's other half—the hidden pages, *of both bloods*, the words that had gone through Ulyana like a hook—was sewn somewhere against my ribs, and the gilded court didn't know it existed.

They had taken the two keystones off us the first hour. They had not searched me thoroughly, because thoroughness is inelegant and the gilded court is nothing if not elegant.

Or perhaps they had searched me, and knew, and were waiting.

Both possibilities kept me awake until the false stars wheeled, and neither was comfortable company.

* * *

On the fifth day, the trees ended.

We came over a rise and the world opened onto a valley that hurt to look at—light pooled in it like honey in a bowl, towers of glass and gold rising in tiers, bridges arcing between them with no visible support, waterfalls that fell upward. Beautiful. Of course it was beautiful. Everything about the gilded court was beautiful, the way a lure is beautiful, and I stood there on the ridge with my hands bound and understood with total clarity that we were looking at the engine of the winter.

Villages froze so that this could shine.

"There it is," the envoy said beside me, and his voice had gone soft with something like love. "Home."

Below, gates of filigreed gold swung open to receive us, and trumpets sounded—actual trumpets, sweet and welcoming, a homecoming fanfare for prisoners. Dagny spat. Ivo went gray. Ulyana lifted her chin and walked down toward the light with the bearing of someone arriving rather than delivered, because she would die before she let them see otherwise.

And I walked beside her, the hidden pages pressing against my ribs like a second heart, and thought: *diplomacy has failed. Now we find out what's left.*

The gates closed behind us with a sound like a coin dropped in an empty cathedral, and high above, in the tallest tower, a figure in a crown of morning-light rose from a throne to watch us come.

The court had what it wanted.

Now it only had to keep us.
