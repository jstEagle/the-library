## 26. Parley Under Banners

There's a particular kind of silence that falls over a camp the night before someone walks into a trap. I'd never heard it before. It sounds like everyone breathing carefully, like the fire being fed just enough to stay alive but not enough to crackle and give anyone an excuse to speak.

I sat on my bedroll sharpening a knife I had no intention of using, because the whole point of walking into the gilded court alone was to arrive with nothing they could take from me except myself. The knife was habit. Habit is what you hold onto when everything else has been inventoried and confiscated in advance.

"You're going to get yourself killed," Dagny said from her spot by the fire. She was sitting up now—fever broken, shoulder bound, stubbornness fully operational. "Or worse. They'll dress you up."

"That's what frightens you? Tailoring?"

"Have you *seen* their clothes?" She jabbed the fire with a stick. "Buttons like beetle shells. You'd drown in it."

"I've been threatened with pardon, execution, and eternal winter," I said. "A wardrobe is not going to break me."

Nobody laughed. That was the thing about our little camp of castoffs—we laughed at all the wrong moments and went silent at the ones where laughing would have helped most. Pip sat cross-legged beside Theo with his head against her knee, pretending to sleep, which is what children do when they want to hear everything and be blamed for nothing. His eyes were closed too firmly. Sleepers don't concentrate that hard.

Theo wasn't looking at me at all, which was its own kind of conversation. He'd spent three days arguing against this plan and lost, and now he was performing the particular scholarly grief of a man whose citations have been ignored. Finally he said, without looking up: "The envoy promised sanctuary. Legitimacy. Everything." He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was shaved. "In negotiation theory, when one side offers *everything*, it's because they intend to leave you nothing to negotiate with afterward."

"Thank you, Bramble. Very comforting."

"You're welcome. Also, if they take you, I'm coming in after you, so try to delay any executions until I finish the approach."

I looked at him then. Firelight did him no favors—he was all elbows and ink-stains and a jaw that hadn't seen a razor since Marrowgate—but he met my eyes the way he met everything: directly, nervously, and anyway.

"They won't take me," I said. "They need me willing. That's the whole architecture of this trap. A warden who comes to them resentful makes storms that sulk. They want me *grateful*." I slid the knife back into its sheath. "So I'm going to walk in there and be the least grateful person they've ever hosted."

"And if gratitude isn't actually required?"

"Then I improvise. Improvising is most of what I do."

Dagny snorted. "It's what got you exiled."

"It's what got me exiled," I agreed pleasantly, "and look how well it's worked out for everyone who lied about me."

Pip opened one eye. "Take the small door," she said.

Everyone turned to look at her.

"The gilded court," she said, sitting up, all business, as though she hadn't been faking sleep for twenty minutes. "The pretty gate is the front. Fronts are for showing off. But courts like that always have a servants' road, a back way, because even beautiful people need someone to carry out the slops. If you go in through the front, they decide what you see. If you find the back—" she shrugged, a very adult shrug on a very small body, "—you decide what they see."

I looked at her for a long moment. "How do you know about servants' roads?"

"How do you know about storm-craft?" she said. "Somebody taught you something they weren't supposed to. Somebody taught me too."

I decided, once again, not to ask questions I didn't want answered yet. There were going to be a lot of those accumulating by the time this was over.

"All right," I said. "The small door. Anyone else have tactical advice before I sleep?"

Ivo, who had been quiet all evening in the way of men carrying old uniforms under new skins, spoke from the edge of the firelight. "When they offer you the pardon," he said, "ask them to say it in writing. In front of witnesses."

"Why?"

"Because I wrote pardons for five years before I learned what they were worth." His face did something complicated. "Nothing. They're worth nothing. But making them write it costs them something, and watching them refuse tells you exactly what they're afraid of."

I filed that away next to Pip's small door and Theo's negotiation theory and Dagny's opinions on buttons, and I lay down under a sky that had gone the wrong color again—that bruised pearl at the horizon where true winter kept testing the latch—and I slept better than I expected to.

That's the thing nobody warns you about courage. It doesn't feel like trumpets. It feels like finally putting down a bag you forgot you were carrying. For six years I had run—through passes, across borders, through other people's names and other people's fires. Running had become so much of me that I'd mistaken it for survival itself. But tonight, knowing I'd walk into the court of the people holding my family hostage, some knot in my chest simply... loosened. Not peace. More like the moment before a storm breaks, when the pressure drops and every joint in your body knows the sky has made its decision.

I'd made mine.

* * *

The gilded court didn't live in a place so much as in a persuasion. You walked up the eastern shoulder of Thornfell through snow that fell upward, and at some point the mountain stopped being stone and started being architecture, and there was no line you crossed, only a gradual realization that the cliffs around you had been carved into colonnades by hands patient enough to make geology look hasty.

The banners came first. Of course they did. Banners of gold thread on white silk, each one stitched with the sigil of the court—a crown woven through with lightning—and every banner hung perfectly still in air that should have been screaming with wind. That stillness was the first message: *even the weather obeys us here.* Which meant the second message, the one underneath, was *we're nervous.* Weather that has to be commanded into stillness is weather that wants to move.

The envoy waited at the gates. Same one who'd come to our camp—tall, radiant in the way of things polished until they've forgotten their original shape, with a smile calibrated to the half-degree. Her name was Lirienne. Everything about her was symmetrical except her intentions.

"Warden Zhou," she said. My title, offered like bait. "You honor us."

"Your court holds a child hostage," I said. "Honor's a strange word for it. Let's skip to the part where we both pretend the other is being reasonable."

Something flickered behind her beauty—annoyance, maybe, or respect; in fae courts I suspect they're the same emotion. "You prefer bluntness."

"I prefer honesty. Bluntness is what people settle for when honesty isn't available."

She gestured, and the gates—gold and glass and entirely too pleased with themselves—swung open onto a hall that had been designed by someone who hated empty space and feared silence in equal measure. Chandeliers like frozen waterfalls. Courtiers arranged along the walls in a gradient of radiance, brightest nearest the throne, dimmer toward the doors, as though proximity to power were literally luminous here and everyone knew their wattage.

And there, between two pillars of veined glass, stood my people.

They'd been given chairs, which was the court's idea of mercy, and fine clothes, which was its idea of humiliation—Dagny in a gown she wore like armor she despised, Ivo standing rigid in a uniform-cut coat that I could see fit him like a memory. Pip sat on a cushion too big for her, feet not reaching the floor, wearing a dress with actual lace, and when she saw me she didn't wave, didn't react at all, just went very carefully blank—the expression of a child who has learned that visible hope gets used against you.

I wanted to burn the room down. I noted the feeling, thanked it for its input, and set it aside. Later. There was a later, now. That was the difference between running and arriving.

On the throne—if you could call it that; it was more a suggestion of authority rendered in gold filigree, a chair that seemed embarrassed to bear weight—sat the Queen of the Gilded Court. Aurelith, they called her. She was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful: inviting, mirror-flat, and hiding the exact depth at which your lungs stop working.

"Warden." Her voice filled the hall without seeming to raise itself. "You've come unarmed, alone, and ahead of schedule. I confess you've disrupted several of our contingencies."

"Good. Contingencies are just plans for being surprised politely."

Murmurs along the walls. Courtiers don't laugh; they rustle. I kept my eyes on Aurelith and walked forward across a floor polished enough to show me my own reflection, and my reflection looked, I thought, remarkably calm for a woman whose entire plan consisted of the word *later.*

"You want the keystones," I said. "You want a warden. And you want both delivered willingly, because a covenant reforged under coercion fails the same way the last one failed—slowly, then all at once. So let's discuss terms."

"Terms." She smiled. "You speak like a smuggler."

"I *am* a smuggler. It's the only honest profession I've ever held. Everything else—warden, exile, prophecy—was assigned to me by other people." I stopped at the base of the dais, close enough to see that her crown wasn't metal at all but braided light, and that the light flickered, very slightly, at the edges. Interesting. "Here are mine. First: my people walk out free. All four. Before I agree to anything, not after. Hostages released on delivery is a smuggler's rule—you pay the guide at the start of the crossing, because guides know that promises made on mountaintops tend to evaporate with the altitude."

"Those are bold terms for someone with no leverage."

"I'm not finished. Second: I want the truth about the covenant. Not the version in your histories—the actual mechanics. Why it's failing. What the decay looks like from inside a court. Because I've noticed something, Your Majesty, in my limited time among your people." I let my gaze travel slowly along that gradient of glowing faces. "Your court is *bright*. Radiant. And the hollow court starves in the dark below. And the border villages freeze. Funny arrangement, isn't it? One realm shining while everything around it pays the bill."

The rustling stopped. Somewhere behind me, a chandelier hummed.

Lirienne moved a half-step forward, smile intact. "Warden, perhaps—"

"No." I didn't raise my voice. I lowered it. Storm-craft taught you that long ago: the quiet thunder is the one that moves the herd. "Third term. When I reforge the covenant—if I reforge the covenant—it binds *both* courts. Hollow and gilded alike. Equal shares of the mountain's favor, equal shares of its winter. No court gets to be the sun while the others live in its shadow."

Silence, of the expensive kind. Aurelith studied me for a long moment, and I watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes—the same arithmetic, I realized, that Serapha had done six years ago, the same arithmetic every institution does when confronted with a person they can't file anywhere. *What is she? What drawer does she go in?*

"You would bind us to *them*," Aurelith said. "To the hollow remnants. To faded things."

"To *people*," I said. "Faded, starving, forgotten people who lost their court when yours let the covenant rot. Or caused it to rot—I haven't decided which story I believe yet, and I notice you haven't rushed to correct me."

"We did not cause—"

"You also didn't fix it." I spread my hands. "For a hundred years. While the frost ate the lowlands and your neighbors hollowed out, your court shone. So either you broke it, or you watched it break and found the view acceptable. From where I stand, Majesty, those are the same crime with different paperwork."

Something shifted in the hall—not the courtiers, but the light itself. The chandeliers brightened by a degree, and I felt it on my skin like a held breath. Power gathering. The old reflex rose in me, the one that wanted to answer pressure with pressure, sky against sky, and I let it rise just far enough to warm my blood and no farther.

Then Aurelith laughed.

It was a lovely sound. That was the worst thing about it.

"Oh, I see it now," she said. "Why the prophecy chose you. Why the mountain marked you. You're not a diplomat. You're a *front*. A storm given a spine and a grudge." She rose from her embarrassed throne and descended the dais, and up close her radiance had seams in it, faint lines like cracks in glazed porcelain. "Very well, Warden. Terms. Your people go free—now, as you insist. The truth of the covenant will be shared, as much of it as serves stability. And the binding will include both courts." She paused, delicately, the way a knife pauses. "Provided the warden consents to reside *here*. At the heart of the gilded court. Where she can be... supported. Advised. Protected."

There it was. The whole architecture laid bare. Not a cage—they'd learned cages make sulky storms—but a gilded tether, a warden kept close, kept comfortable, kept *theirs*. Sanctuary, legitimacy, everything. And in exchange, every dawn of my forever spent in a hall of people who measured their own light.

"And if I decline?"

"Then we return to contingencies," she said pleasantly. "We are patient creatures, Warden. We waited a century. Winter does the rest of the persuading."

I thought of Pip's blank face. Dagny's gown. Ivo standing at attention in a dead man's coat. I thought of a page in a burned book with my name on it, and a night of forged evidence, and a mentor who stayed silent, and six years of exits counted and doors memorized and love treated like contraband.

And I heard myself say, clearly, in a hall full of beings who could hear a heartbeat at forty paces:

"Decline."

Aurelith's smile stayed perfectly in place. Only her eyes changed—widened by a millimeter, recalculating. "You'd condemn the lowlands to winter. Your village. Your *family*."

"My family," I said, "is walking out that door in the next ten minutes, because those were the first terms and I don't renegotiate downward. As for the lowlands—" I stepped closer, close enough that the seams in her radiance showed plainly, close enough that I could see the fear underneath the porcelain, real and cold and human-shaped after all, "—you seem to be operating under a misunderstanding about what I am. You think I'm here because you have hostages. I'm here because I needed to see your face while I told you how this ends."

The chandeliers flared. Every courtier in the hall straightened.

"You don't get the keystones," I said. "You don't get the warden. You get a choice, and you get it today, because the mountain is waking up—it woke when we took the Rootcrown, and it has *opinions* about who let its covenant rot. Stand with the covenant when it's reforged, share the winter and the spring alike, and you keep your court. Keep grasping, keep hoarding, keep waiting for a controllable warden—" I smiled, and I knew exactly what kind of smile it was, because I'd practiced it at smugglers' tables for years, the smile that says the price just went up, "—and the mountain settles its accounts with you first."

"You threaten us," Aurelith said softly. "In our own hall. Under our own banners."

"No," I said. "I *forecast*. Threats are things people choose. This is just weather."

And because the mountain was indeed waking beneath us—because somewhere far below, in the dark where the Rootcrown pulsed like a second heart, something ancient had rolled over and taken notice—the floor of the gilded court trembled. Just once. Just enough. The chandeliers rang, a faint glass chord, and every banner in that impossible still air lifted, all together, like a flock startled off a wire.

Aurelith looked at her trembling banners. Then at me.

"Release them," she said to Lirienne, without taking her eyes off my face. "All four. Now."

* * *

They walked out through the gates into the upward-falling snow, and I walked beside them, and nobody spoke until the cliffs stopped being colonnades and became ordinary rock again. Then Pip grabbed my hand—her fingers cold, her grip ferocious—and said, "You didn't take the small door."

"No," I said. "I took the front."

"Why?"

"Because sometimes," I said, "you want them to see you coming."

Behind us, deep in the hill, the light of the gilded court flickered—once, twice—and did not go out. But it dimmed. And in the dimming I heard, or imagined I heard, a sound like a very large door beginning, slowly, to open somewhere beneath the world.

Theo fell into step beside me. "They'll move," he said quietly. "Desperate courts don't parley twice."

"I know."

"So what's the plan?"

I looked up at Thornfell's black shoulders, at the storm-light gathering there like a thought forming, and for the first time in six years the answer wasn't a route, an exit, a ledger entry.

"Now," I said, "we go home. To the spire. Before they realize I never intended to negotiate anything except timing."
