## 27. The Court's True Winter

I want it on the record—I am not a brave man.

Scholars are not brave. Scholars are people who have learned to run toward danger only when the danger is made of paper. Give me a collapsing archive, a cursed folio, a shelf about to crush me under a thousand years of marginalia, and I'll walk in whistling. Ask me to stand in a hall of beings who glow like dawn breaking over gold leaf and negotiate for the life of a child, and I become exactly what I appear to be: a nervous man in a coat that has been mended too many times, holding his hat like it might bolt without him.

Which is why I was so surprised—and so suspicious—when they let me in.

"Author's blood," the envoy had said, smiling at me with teeth like piano keys. "The Bramble line is always welcome in the Gilded Court."

*Always welcome.* A century ago my grandmother had written a book that terrified two realms, and died protecting it, and now her grandson walks into the enemy's front door because of it. Welcome, they said. Like family. That should have been the first warning. Nobody welcomes you like family unless they want something, or unless they think you already belong to them.

So here I was, on the morning of Ulyana's parley, being *hospited*. That's a word I invented just now, standing in a guest chamber the size of the inn where I'd met her, staring at a bed canopy woven from what appeared to be actual sunlight. Hospitality as a weapon. They'd given me fruit that tasted like the memory of fruit. They'd given me wine that hummed faintly, like a struck glass. They'd given me a room with one window overlooking the court's great library.

One window. Overlooking the library.

I'm not stupid. I've spent my whole life around people who are smarter than me and meaner than me, and surviving that teaches you a particular skill: noticing when a door is left open *for* you. The window wasn't barred. The library below wasn't guarded—or rather, it was guarded by exactly one elderly fae archivist who looked up at me through the glass, considered me for a long moment, and then went back to his cataloging with the air of a man who had been told to look away.

They wanted me to see something. Or they wanted me to *think* I'd stolen a look at something, which is worse, because then you can never be sure which truths were bait.

But Ulyana was down in the negotiation hall trading her surrender for Pip's freedom, and Dagny was still feverish, and Ivo was pacing our quarters like a storm-caller who'd been told to sit still, and someone in this glittering hive knew exactly how the covenant had rotted from the inside. So I did the cowardly-brave thing, the scholar thing.

I climbed out the window.

* * *

The Gilded Court's library does not creak. That's the first thing you notice. Every human library I've ever loved creaks—floors settling, shelves groaning under the weight of what we've written down, the building itself complaining the way old people complain, comfortably, constantly. This place was silent as held breath. The shelves rose forty feet and were carved from amber-colored stone, and the books on them weren't paper. They were thin sheets of some translucent material, and when you pulled one from its niche, light moved *through* it, and images swam in the light like fish under ice.

The archivist didn't stop me. He pointed, once, with a stylus made of bone, toward a section deep in the stacks marked with a sigil I recognized from the prophecy book's spine—the seal of the original wardenship. Then he returned to his work with tremendous dedication, the way a man whistles past a graveyard.

I found the records in a niche behind a bust of somebody important-looking. Records are the same everywhere, thank all the merciful gods of scholarship. Even immortals need bookkeeping.

Ledgers. Maintenance logs for the covenant spire, going back three hundred years. And here is where I have to tell you what I found, and I have to do it plainly, because for an hour in that silent amber library I could not feel my own hands, and if I dress it up too prettily I'll start thinking I imagined it.

The decay was scheduled.

There. I said it. Let me say it again for my own benefit, because my mind kept sliding off it like rain off oiled leather: *the decay was scheduled.*

Every fifty years or so, the maintenance logs showed a "pruning." Small entries, bureaucratic, almost shy. *Attenuation of the western anchor, per directive.* *Reduction of keystone resonance by one-tenth, per directive.* *Veil-thinning exercise, authorized.* Each pruning signed off by a different hand, but every single authorization traced back, through layers of countersignatures and honorifics, to the same office. The Office of the Radiant Envoy.

The office that had come to us on the road with offers of sanctuary and legitimacy and everything.

I sat down on the floor between the shelves, which no proper scholar ever does except when his legs stop working, and I read faster. The pattern was elegant, I'll give them that—it was beautiful the way a spiderweb is beautiful if you're not the fly. The Gilded Court hadn't broken the covenant. Breaking it would have destroyed them too; the winter doesn't check your complexion before it freezes you. No. They had *thinned* it. Carefully. Patiently. A tenth here, an attenuation there, across three hundred years, each reduction small enough to blame on age, on neglect, on the human lowlands' failure of faith. And each thinning produced symptoms: failed harvests, cold summers, villages going quiet. Symptoms that frightened everyone. Symptoms that made both realms desperate for a solution.

And when things got desperate enough—when the frost reached Marrowgate in midsummer and the order started burning villages to stop the spread—then the beautiful option would arrive. The radiant envoy. Sanctuary. Legitimacy. Everything. All you have to do is gather the keystones and bring them *here*, to us, and we will make it right.

A controlled demolition, with a rescue operation attached. And the new warden, exhausted and hunted and grateful, installed at the heart of the spire wearing a leash made of gratitude.

They didn't just want a warden they could control. They'd spent three centuries manufacturing the conditions that would make anyone *want* to be controlled. Hunger makes excellent manners.

I found one more document, tucked at the back of the niche, older than the rest—a fragment, half its light gone dark. It was correspondence, and the handwriting made my chest hurt, because I knew it. I've spent eleven years reading my grandmother's letters. I know the way she crossed her t's like little lightning bolts, the way she pressed too hard on her descenders because she wrote angry.

It was dated the year before she died. It said:

*"They have asked me to draft the renewal oath 'with provisions.' I have refused. Vess of the Hollow Court stood beside me when the first words were spoken, and I will not write her descendants out of the covenant to please gilded appetites. If they mean to prune the tree, let them find another gardener. I would sooner bury the book than bend it."*

She'd known. My grandmother had sat in this same amber silence, three hundred years into their slow sabotage—no, less than that, a century, the math reeled and steadied—a century into it, and she'd refused them, and she'd hidden the second half of the prophecy in the binding of her own book because she knew they'd come for the pages she'd written openly.

And then she'd died protecting it, and everyone had called it tragedy, and I had grown up on stories of her martyrdom like it was weather—something that happened, something nobody caused.

Somebody caused it.

The archivist cleared his throat. I hadn't heard him approach; in this place, sound behaved like a well-trained dog. He stood over me holding a single sheet of the luminous material, fresh-written, and his ancient face was arranged into an expression I couldn't read until I realized I'd seen it before—on Serapha, the day she offered Ulyana a pardon. Pity wearing duty's uniform.

"The envoy asks," he said quietly, "that you attend the parley. Your presence was requested by name." A pause. "And scholar—the niche you found was not lost. But what you do with what it held, that part is yours. It always was, for your line."

He turned and walked away, and I sat there one moment longer with my grandmother's furious handwriting ghosting behind my eyes, and then I got up, and I put the ledger back exactly as I'd found it, because I am not brave, but I am careful, and careful people survive long enough to be useful.

Then I went to save Ulyana from the most beautiful trap ever built.

* * *

The negotiation hall was worse than I'd imagined, and I have a good imagination; it's my curse. Everything in it gleamed. The pillars were veined with gold like marrow in bone. The courtiers lining the walls wore faces of such perfect, compassionate beauty that looking at them too long felt like drinking—warm, then dizzy, then unsteady on your feet. At the far end, on a dais of pale glass, sat the envoy himself, radiating the specific authority of a sunrise, and across from him, small and straight-backed and armed with nothing but her stubbornness—

Ulyana.

She looked terrible. She looked magnificent. Three days in this place had sanded her down and polished what was left, and what was left was flint. She stood alone on the human side of a long table, because they'd separated her from Ivo, from me, from everyone who might steady her, and she was talking, and the envoy was listening with his chin propped on laced fingers like a professor enjoying a promising student.

"—the child goes free first," she was saying. "Pip, the ferrywoman, the defector. All of them across the threshold before I hand over anything. Those are terms, not suggestions. You taught me how courts bargain, remember? You wrote the book on taking hostages. Well. Read your own rules."

"Storm-caller." The envoy's voice was honey poured slowly. It reached me at the doors and wrapped around me like a warm coat, and I felt the pull of it even knowing what I knew—*sanctuary, legitimacy, everything*—and I understood suddenly why the court didn't need soldiers. They were the offer. Everyone in this room was being offered something, all the time, just by existing near them.

"You mistake our position," the envoy continued gently. "We don't hold your people hostage. We *shelter* them. There's a long winter coming either way—your order guarantees that with every village it burns—and we alone can end it. Bring us the keystones, accept the wardenship we offer, and no one need suffer at all. Isn't that worth more than a child's freedom?" He smiled, and the smile was kind, and that was the horror of it. "You've been homeless a long time, Ulyana Zhou. We are offering you a home. Why do you keep reaching for weapons?"

And she—gods, I love her, I can say it here in the privacy of my own skull since I've apparently decided this account is where I hide things—she didn't flinch. "Because I've been offered homes before," she said, "and the price was always my name."

I walked in on that sentence. Doors, apparently, open themselves for author's blood; the whole court watched me cross that gleaming floor, and every step I took I could feel my grandmother's letter against my memory like a coal in my pocket. *I would sooner bury the book than bend it.*

"I have something for the negotiation," I announced, and my voice cracked on *negotiation,* which, fine, honesty has costs. "From your own archive. Your own maintenance logs. Signed, sealed, and countersigned for three hundred years."

The temperature of the hall changed. Not literally—though with Ulyana present, literal was never off the table—but the quality of the attention shifted, the way a room shifts when a drunk says something true at a dinner party.

"Theo," Ulyana said carefully, "what did you find?"

"Pruning schedules," I said. "Directive after directive. Attenuation of the anchors. Thinning of the veil. One-tenth of the keystone resonance, removed every few decades, always small, always deniable, always blamed on age." I turned to face the dais, and my legs were shaking, and I let them. "You didn't fail to maintain the covenant. You've been *gardening* its collapse. Centuries of it. You starved the pact so the realms would get desperate enough to accept any warden you handed them—leashed, grateful, yours. Marrowgate froze because of an entry in your ledgers. The hollow court faded because of your *maintenance schedule*."

Silence. The beautiful, terrible silence of a court that had never once needed to defend itself.

And the envoy—this is the part I'll carry to my grave—he didn't rage. He didn't deny. He looked at me with something close to approval, the way you'd look at a student who finally understood the lesson, and he said:

"Now you understand why we needed a Bramble."

Ulyana's head turned toward me very slowly. On the dais, the envoy rose, unfolding to his full radiant height, and the light in the hall bent toward him like wheat toward sun.

"The parley," he said pleasantly, "is concluded. Guards—our guests are tired. See them to comfortable rooms." His gaze slid to Ulyana, warm as a hearth, cold as the winter he'd built. "All of them. We wouldn't want anyone wandering into places they shouldn't. Not anymore."

Beside me, barely audible, Ulyana exhaled one word, and it wasn't a curse and it wasn't a prayer. It was a name. *Pip.*

And somewhere above us, deep in the golden veins of that impossible palace, a child who could see roads adults couldn't was already looking for the way out.
