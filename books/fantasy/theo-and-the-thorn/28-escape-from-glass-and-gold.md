## 28. Escape from Glass and Gold

Here's the thing nobody tells you about being small: everyone forgets you're looking.

Grown-ups think children are like pockets — useful for holding things, but not worth searching too carefully. The gilded court had searched Ulyana's sleeves and Theo's boots and Dagny's braid (which, rude), and they'd taken the keystones off Ivo's neck while he was still bleeding from the fight. But me? They'd patted my head. One of them had given me a sweet. A *sweet*. Like I was a dog at a market stall.

So when the guards counted their prisoners that night in the glass hall, they counted four grown humans, trussed and kneeling on a floor you could see your own face in, and they did not count the feral child asleep — allegedly — in the corner behind a pillar of twisted gold.

Which meant they didn't see me open one eye, and they definitely didn't see the road.

* * *

Fae roads are hard to explain if you haven't got the seeing. Most people walk through the world like it's a floor. I walk through it like it's a door with a hundred hinges, most of them rusted shut, some of them just barely breathing open. There's a seam under every waterfall and behind every mirror and along the spine of every sleeping cat — don't ask how I know about the cat — where the world forgets itself for a moment, and if you slip through during that forgetting, you can go anywhere the forgetting reaches.

The gilded court was *made* of forgetting. That's what all the beauty was for. Gold and glass and light that came from nowhere, so thick and shiny you stopped noticing there was no sky. The whole palace was one enormous hinge held shut, and underneath it, the old roads were screaming.

I could hear them. All night. Like mice trapped in a wall.

"You hear them too."

I nearly jumped out of my skin, which would have been inconvenient, because I needed my skin. Vess was beside me — or mostly beside me. She'd gone thin as smoke since they dragged her out of her cavern-city, and now she was thinner still, a queen shaped from the last candle of a very long night. They'd put her in a cage of woven light at the far end of the hall, and apparently cages of light have poor corners to hide in, because here she was, sitting down next to a dirty child without a single word about how I smelled.

"Hear what?" I said, which is what you say when you're deciding whether to trust someone.

"The roads." Her eyes were the color of the sky before snow. "They sing to you. They've been singing all day, and you've been pretending to sleep, and you've been counting guards." Something like a smile moved across her face, faint as breath on glass. "You count like a soldier, little one. Where did a child learn to count?"

"Dagny taught me numbers. Ulyana taught me exits." I hugged my knees. "Nobody taught me anything else, really. I taught myself the rest."

"And what did you teach yourself?"

I looked at the prisoners. Ulyana's wrists were bound with gold wire that glowed faintly, humming the way a wasp hums. Theo was awake, watching the ceiling like it owed him money. Ivo sat very straight despite the bruises, storm-caller discipline even captured, and Dagny — Dagny was bad. Really bad. She was gray around the mouth and her breathing sounded like a bellows with a hole in it, and every hour that passed she got smaller, like the court was drinking her somehow.

"They took everything," I said. "So I'm going to take it back."

Vess went quiet for a long time. When she spoke again her voice had changed — less queen, more person, and somehow that was scarier. "Child, listen to me carefully. What you're describing is a road-walk through the heart of the gilded court, past wards older than your grandmother's grandmother, carrying four prisoners, one of whom cannot walk. If you are caught, they will not kill you quickly. If you succeed—" she stopped.

"If I succeed what?"

"If I help you, I will spend what's left of me. Do you understand? I am already spent, little thief. This would be spending the purse itself." She held up her hand, and I watched the light go through it like it wasn't sure the fingers were real. "There is perhaps enough of me left for one great act. I had been saving it. For revenge, I thought. Or for my court's name. But names are heavy things, and revenge is heavier, and neither of them will fit through a fae road. Only people fit through roads."

I didn't say anything for a bit. Grown-ups always want you to understand things before you do them, like understanding makes things safer. It doesn't. Nothing is safer. You just know more about why it's dangerous, and then you do it anyway, because the other option is worse.

"Dagny carried me across the Sorrow River when I was too tired to swim," I said finally. "She complained the whole time. Called me a sack of wet cats. But she carried me." I looked up at Vess. "That's what you do. You carry people. Even when it costs. Even when it costs everything. Isn't that what queens are supposed to be?"

Something flickered in her face — grief, maybe, or recognition, or the memory of a version of herself from before the hollowing. "You," she said softly, "are going to be a great deal of trouble when you grow up."

"I'm already loads of trouble."

"Yes. But *great* trouble. There's a difference." She rose, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked like a queen again, tall and terrible and lit from inside by something older than the gilded court's whole shining lie. "Now show me the seams, little one. Show me where this beautiful prison forgets itself."

* * *

The first seam was behind the throne.

Of course it was. The gilded court built its throne directly over the oldest road in the palace, because that's what conquerors do — they sit on top of what they broke. I'd felt it the first day, a cold draft coming up through the floor that nobody else seemed to notice, or maybe noticed and pretended not to, the way you pretend not to notice a bruise.

Getting to it was the problem. The hall between our corner and the dais had six guards, and the throne room proper had twelve more, plus the Warden-of-Doors, who was seven feet of polished antler and didn't blink, ever, which I found deeply upsetting on principle.

"Guards see what they expect to see," Vess murmured as we crouched in the shadow of a wine cask taller than Ivo. "Show them something expected, and they'll look straight through it."

"What's expected in a palace?"

"A servant. A pet. A mistake." She pressed something into my hand — a coin, old and green with age, worn smooth by a thousand years of thumbs. "My last treasure. Hold it, and walk slowly, and be boring. Boring is the best armor there is."

Being boring turned out to be the hardest thing I've ever done. I wanted to run. My legs *itched* to run. But I walked, slow as syrup, hugging the coin, head down, thinking boring thoughts — bread, puddles, the exact number of snails I once saw on a fencepost — and the guards' eyes slid off me like rain off oilcloth. One of them actually yawned. I passed within arm's reach of the Warden-of-Doors and he looked right at me and his eyes went soft and unfocused, the way eyes go when they're remembering something nicer than the present, and I walked under his nose like a draft of cold air.

Then I was behind the throne, and I put my palm flat against the stone, and I found the seam.

It opened like an eye.

Oh. Oh, it was *beautiful*. The road behind the gilded court's throne was old, old, old — older than gold, older than glass — a silver thread of a path winding away into a dark full of stars that weren't star-shaped, all wrong and wonderful, and it sang when I touched it, a low hungry note like a struck bell underwater. It had been shut for centuries and it wanted *out*.

"Not yet," Vess whispered. She'd followed me, or part of her had; the rest was stretched thin across the hall, holding something. "First the wire on the storm-caller's friends. The gold binds to their blood-oaths. It must be unmade, not cut — cutting wakes it. Can you unmake things, child?"

"I can make a lot of things worse," I said honestly.

"That will do."

* * *

Unmaking the gold wire was like picking a lock made of song. The wire hummed a note, and I had to hum it wrong — just slightly, the way you ruin a chord — until the magic got confused and let go. I learned it from Vess in whispers while I crept from prisoner to prisoner in the dark hours, when the court dimmed itself into something like sleep. Theo nearly shouted when I touched him; I clapped a hand over his mouth and put one finger to my lips, and he stared at me with those scholar's eyes going wide and wider, and mouthed: *you?*

"Me," I whispered. "Shut up and hold still. Your turn to be boring."

His wire came undone second. Then Ivo's — Ivo woke clean awake, no flinch at all, which impressed me; he just looked at the broken wire, then at me, and gave a single slow nod like I'd passed a test I hadn't known I was taking. Ulyana last, because hers was worst. The wire on her wrists had grown *into* her, threads sinking under the skin like roots, and when I hummed it wrong it screamed — a thin metallic shriek only I and the wire could hear — and I had to hum louder, wronger, until my own teeth ached, until it unraveled and slithered off her arms and died on the glass floor like a burnt snake.

Her eyes were open. In the dark they were storm-colored, and they were looking at me with an expression I'd never seen on her face before. Not the guide-look, not the wary-look, not even the almost-smile she saved for Dagny's terrible jokes.

It was the look you give someone when you realize they're not what you thought, and what they actually are is better.

"Pip," she breathed. "How—"

"Later," I said, because I'd heard her say it a hundred times and it turns out it works for anyone. "Can Dagny walk?"

Ulyana's face closed like a door. "Barely. Why?"

"Because we're leaving." I jerked my thumb at the throne. "Through the front door. Sort of."

* * *

Everything after that happened fast, the way the best and worst things do.

Theo got Dagny up — she weighed nothing anymore, which scared me more than any guard — and Ivo took point, and Ulyana took the rear, and I led them across that vast gleaming hall toward the throne with my heart banging like a drum in a procession. We were ten steps away when the lights came up.

All of them. At once. Every lamp and sconce and vein of gold in the walls blazed white, and the court woke like a single animal, and the Warden-of-Doors was simply *there*, antlers scraping the ceiling, and behind him ranks upon ranks of the beautiful terrible courtiers, and above it all, descending the far stair like poured honey, the gilded sovereign themselves — I never did learn whether it was king or queen or something with no word — smiling the smile of someone who has already won.

"Well," said the sovereign, and its voice was music played slightly too fast. "The mouse has been busy."

"Run!" I screamed, and threw myself at the seam.

But the seam was shut. Sealed — I could feel it, soldered over from the other side, the sovereign's magic poured into the crack like lead. My stomach dropped through the floor. I'd brought us all the way here, and the door was dead, and the guards were closing in, and Dagny made a sound that I will hear for the rest of my life—

And then Vess stood up.

She rose out of her cage of woven light, or through it, or she simply *wasn't in it anymore* because she had stopped being the kind of thing cages work on, and she walked toward the throne, and every step cost her. I could see it costing. She dimmed with each stride, a candle burning down in fast-forward, and her voice when she spoke filled the whole hall the way winter fills a valley.

"You sat upon my roads," she said. "You sealed them. You built your shine on top of the hollow places and called it a kingdom." She raised both hands, insubstantial, translucent, spent. "Queens carry things, little sovereign. Here is what I carry for you."

She brought her hands together like a woman breaking bread, and the seal *shattered*.

Not just the seal — the whole hinge. The road tore open beneath the throne in a bloom of silver dark, wild and roaring, and the gilded court's perfect floor split like ice on a spring river, and the sovereign's smile finally, finally cracked. Guards stumbled. Courtiers screamed prettily. And Vess, standing in the tearing heart of it, turned her fading face toward me and mouthed two words.

*Carry them.*

So I did.

I grabbed Ulyana's hand and pulled her to the edge of the opening road, and Ivo half-carried Dagny, and Theo clung to Ivo's belt, and we fell together into the silver dark as the gilded court howled behind us — and the last thing I saw before the road swallowed us was Vess, dissolving into light, wearing the expression of someone setting down a weight she'd carried for a hundred years.

* * *

We came out through a waterfall. Of course we did. Waterfalls are doors; I could've told them that weeks ago if anyone had asked.

We tumbled out onto wet stone behind the falling water, gasping, soaked, alive. Behind us the road sealed itself with a sigh, and ahead of us — I pushed the curtain of water aside to look — lay the high moors of Thornfell, black under a sky boiling with unnatural aurora, the veil-tear glowing on the northern horizon like a wound in the world. The long winter was still coming. The keystones were still in enemy hands. Everything was still wrong.

But we were out. We were free. And Dagny was breathing.

Ulyana knelt in the shallows and pulled me into her arms, hard, and she was crying, which I pretended not to notice because that's what family does. "Pip," she said into my hair. "Pip, Vess—"

"I know." My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "I know what she spent."

Theo stood at the waterfall's edge, staring back the way we'd come, his jaw tight. "The court will follow. The sovereign won't stop at a sealed road — not now. Not with the keystones." He looked at Ulyana, and something passed between them that had a plan inside it somewhere, or the start of one.

Ivo limped over and put a hand on my head, heavy as a roof beam. "You counted guards," he said. "For three days. While playing asleep."

"Boring is the best armor," I said, and Vess's green coin was still warm in my fist, and somewhere behind us in the dark, I could swear I heard the fae roads singing her name.

Ulyana stood up, wrung out her sleeves, and looked north at the wound-light in the sky. "Then we'd better be fast," she said. "Because I know exactly where the sovereign will take them — and exactly what happens if we're late."

Above the moor, the first true snow of the long winter began to fall.
