## 29. Vess's Last Light

I have been dying for a hundred years, so you will forgive me if I do not treat the final act as an inconvenience.

Fading is a strange thing to explain to the living. It is not pain, mostly. It is subtraction. First you lose your reflection—gilded folk keep theirs in mirrors and ponds, vain as magpies, but we hollow ones shed ours early, like a snake shedding skin it no longer has the appetite to fill. Then you lose your name. Not from anyone's memory; from your own. You wake some morning and reach for the word *Vess* the way you'd reach for a key on a ring, and find the ring empty, and stand there turning it over in your hands wondering whose door you were ever meant to open.

Then you lose your court.

Ours was called the Court of Rainroots. I want you to understand that I remember this now, at the end, when it does me the most good and the least use. For ninety years I could not have told you what we were called. I knew only that we had been, that the covenant breaking had taken our gate and our green and our songs and left us a cavern full of people who blinked at each other like strangers at a funeral for someone none of them could name. We were not dead. Dead would have been tidy. We were *unlisted*, unrecorded, struck from the ledger of things that matter, and the world has a way of forgetting what isn't written down.

The gilded court wrote us out themselves, of course. That is the part that curdles. When the covenant cracked and winter came crawling down the mountain, the Court of Gild and Glass looked at their fading cousins—the rainroot folk, the moss-singers, the small damp gods of streams and cellar-damp—and they made a calculation. A court can only carry so much weight across a failing bridge. They chose to be beautiful rather than complete. And then they spent a century telling everyone, with great shining sincerity, that the hollow ones had simply *faded on their own*. As though grief were a disease we caught from ourselves.

So yes. I am bitter. Bitterness keeps better than most things, when you are mostly gone.

But let me tell you about the girl instead, because she is the reason any of this happened, and because if I spend my last hour cataloguing grievances I will die exactly the creature they always said I was.

* * *

Ulyana Zhou came into my cavern-city smelling of storm and suspicion, which is to say she smelled correct. Half-bloods always do. The two bloods argue under the skin like cats in a sack, and the result has a scent—I knew it the moment she walked past me, before Theo Bramble stammered his introductions, before I saw her flinch when my people turned their hollow eyes on her. She smelled like the old stories. The true ones. The ones about wardens who stood between realms because they belonged to neither and therefore to both.

I did not tell her that her mother had sat in this very cavern. Some debts you make people pay in installments. Her mother—Aurelia, of the Rainroot line, third daughter of a house so minor even I had trouble recalling its crest—came to me the night before Ulyana's trial, or rather the night before the trial was staged, and begged me for something I could not give and did not fully understand until much later. She wanted her daughter forgotten. Scrubbed. Written out of every record, every prophecy, every ledger, so that the order and the courts alike would never look twice at a half-blood child growing up in the lowlands.

I granted it. I was already fading; what did I care for one more act of erasure? It cost me nothing but a thread of myself, and I had threads to spare.

It took me eighty years to learn what Aurelia had actually purchased. Not safety. *Invisibility.* A life spent counting exits, guiding smugglers through passes no honest map admits exist, sleeping with her back to walls in rooms where no one learned her name. Aurelia hadn't bought her daughter a shield. She had bought her a tomb with a view. And the order—the order had helped, had forged evidence against the girl precisely because a half-blood with storm-blood is either a catastrophe or a keystone, and institutions prefer catastrophes they can point to over keystones they cannot control.

When I finally told Ulyana all of this, in chapter and verse, expecting rage—she gave me something worse. She gave me understanding. "You did what my mother asked," she said, in that flat voice she uses when she is feeling everything at once. "You just didn't ask why."

I am a queen. Queens do not apologize to smugglers. But I bowed my head, and she saw it, and that was the whole of the apology, and it was accepted, and I have thought about that exchange every day since, because it was the first time in a century anyone had extended me grace I had not bargained for.

Grace. There's a currency I never learned to mint until it was nearly too late to spend.

* * *

The gilded court took the camp on a Tuesday, because cruelty prefers administrative days. They seized the keystones—all three, gathered by blood and grief and Pip's impossible roads—and they took hostages, and among the hostages was the child, Pip, who sees fae roads the way I once saw rain coming: effortlessly, and with a kind of bored contempt for those who cannot.

I want to be precise about what happened next, because history will be written by Theo Bramble, and Theo writes kindly, and kindness blurs.

Ulyana walked into the gilded court alone. Alone, carrying nothing, offering herself. I watched her go from the mouth of my cavern, and I thought: *there goes the last thing I will ever be right about.* Because I had told her, weeks before, when I traded her the Bell of Hours' location for a promise she couldn't yet name—that promise, as it happens, being nothing more than "remember my court's name," a bargain so cheap it shamed us both—I had told her that help always costs more than coin. And here she was, paying the whole bill at once, walking through gates of glass and gold to trade her freedom for a ferrywoman, a feral child, a defected storm-caller, and a scholar with ink on his fingers.

And it worked. Briefly. Long enough for Theo to find the rot at the center—to learn that the gilded court had *engineered* the covenant's decay, had starved the pact deliberately for a century, all to force a new warden into existence whom they could crown and collar and aim like a weapon at whatever annoyed them. Long enough for Pip to unpick their prison roads with fingers small enough to fit where their wards didn't bother looking. Long enough for the prisoners to run, and for me—

For me to understand that running would not be enough. The gilded court does not chase. They escalate. If their prize escaped, they would tear the veil fully open, drag true winter down on both realms at once, and let the world freeze until someone crawled back to them begging for terms. I had seen them do a smaller version of it to my own court, once. I knew the shape of their patience.

There was one gate that mattered. The great gate of the Court of Gild and Glass—not the pretty frontage where envoys shimmer in for parleys, but the working gate, the deep one, the hinge through which they moved armies and winters both. Shatter it, and their reach collapsed overnight. Their power had never been in their beauty. It was in their *access*, their ability to be anywhere in either realm within a day, and access is a door, and doors can break.

Breaking it required a queen.

Not a healthy one. Healthy queens hoard. But a fading one—a hollow one, ninety years subtracted, held together by habit and grievance and one remembered name—such a queen has nothing left to lose and, crucially, everything to spend. The gate was warded against armies, against storms, against keystones. It was not warded against a sovereign of the old blood choosing to unmake herself upon it. Sacrifice is the one key that fits every lock. The original covenant had been sealed the same way. My ancestor's ancestor had known it. The mountain knew it. And I, Vess, last queen of the Court of Rainroots, knew it too, and had known it for some time, the way you know a debt is coming due.

The only question was whether I'd die as a faded relic in a cave, blinking at strangers, or as a queen with her name in her mouth.

* * *

Pip found me first. Of course Pip found me first; the child finds everything. I was sitting at the cavern mouth doing arithmetic—which is to say, deciding how much of myself the gate would take, and whether there would be enough left over for anything resembling dignity.

"You're going," Pip said. Not asked. The child has the conversational manners of a crow.

"I'm going."

"Ulyana said wait for the others."

"Ulyana says many sensible things." I looked at the child—at the sharp face, the mud-colored eyes that saw roads no adult could, the terrible competence of the very small. "Listen to me, Pip. When the gate breaks, there will be a moment. One moment, maybe two, when every fae road in Thornfell stands open and unguarded, because the hinges that control them will be screaming. Do you understand? Everything opens at once. Every door. Every path. You'll know it because the air will taste like copper and bells."

Pip's eyes went very wide and very still. "That's when we run."

"That's when everyone runs. Everyone. Tell Dagny, tell Ivo, tell Theo—he'll want to stay for her, and he mustn't, because she'll need him alive more than she needs him brave. Tell them the queen of the Rainroots said so." I paused. "Tell them I said the name. Say the whole name."

"The Court of Rainroots," Pip recited, solemn as a psalm.

"And tell Ulyana—" I stopped. Considered. What do you tell a woman who is about to bind herself forever to a mountain? *Good luck*? *My condolences*? I settled on the truth, which was smaller and heavier. "Tell her the promise is paid. All of it. She doesn't owe me anything anymore, and she never owed me what she thinks she did."

Pip nodded once and was gone down the fae road like water finding a crack, and I sat alone at the mouth of my dying court, and I laughed, quietly, because after ninety years of subtraction it turned out the last thing I'd lose was my fear of losing.

* * *

The gate of the Court of Gild and Glass looks, from the outside, like nothing. That is their genius. It presents itself as a cliff face, a waterfall frozen mid-pour, an accident of geology. Travelers walk past it daily without a flicker of unease. Only when you approach with intent does it resolve—and by then the wards have already read your name, your lineage, your intentions, and your probable lifespan.

I approached with intent.

They knew I was coming. Of course they did. The envoy—they'd sent such a lovely envoy to seduce Ulyana, all honeyed light and reasonable offers—stood before the cliff with a contingent of guards, radiant and unhurried, the way you stand waiting for a moth to finish exhausting itself against a lamp.

"Queen of the Hollow Court." The envoy's voice was kind. That was the worst of it; their cruelty was always administered with excellent bedside manner. "You're a long way from your cave. Shall we send word for an escort? You look—" and here the pause, delicate as a surgeon's knife, "—unwell."

"I am unwell," I agreed. "I have been unwell for a hundred years. Your court saw to it."

"Our court grieved your passing—"

"My court is standing right in front of you." I took another step, and felt the wards taste me, and felt them hesitate, because sovereignty reads strangely on the fading. "You struck us from the rolls. You let the winter take our green while yours stayed gold. You told yourselves we faded naturally, the way one tells oneself the poor are naturally hungry. And now the covenant is breaking entirely, and your solution was to manufacture a warden you could leash, and when she slipped the leash you reached for the veil itself." I smiled. Fading queens smile poorly; mine felt like frost forming. "You have been very comfortable for a very long time. I've come to discuss the rent."

"Stand aside," the envoy said, and the kindness dropped away like a mask in a wind, and the guards raised their light, and the cliff began to open behind them, the great gate grinding wide because they intended to bring their whole strength out and end this nuisance permanently.

Good. Open doors are so much easier to break than closed ones.

I let go.

I don't have words adequate to describe letting go, and I am the last person who will ever be able to describe it firsthand, so bear with the approximation. Imagine holding your breath for a century. Imagine the ache of it becoming so familiar you mistake it for a body. And then imagine exhaling—everything, all at once, every withheld thing, every gripped and guarded scrap of self released in a single long catastrophic breath toward the gate.

The Court of Rainroots poured out of me. Not my memories—those went first, the little ones, the taste of rainroot wine, the sound of my court singing in the wet dark, I felt them go like coins slipping through torn cloth and I let them, gladly, because memory is fuel and I had somewhere to be. What came after was the name itself. The true name of a true court, spoken aloud for the first time in a hundred years, by its queen, in its own tongue, with the whole of her behind it.

The gate heard it.

Wards are built on definitions. They distinguish friend from foe, permitted from forbidden, real from unreal. And what I gave them was undefinable—a court that officially did not exist, a queen who was legally nothing, an attack composed entirely of legitimacy. The wards strained to classify me and found, at the bottom of me, beneath the bitterness and the fading and the borrowed time, the oldest thing in the world: a sovereign naming her own house. The gate hesitated for the length of a heartbeat.

One heartbeat was enough.

The crack started at the top and ran down like lightning deciding to be patient. The sound was not loud so much as *total*—a sound that arrived in the teeth and the bones and the roots of the mountain. The guards scattered like startled birds. The envoy stood their ground longest, staring at me with genuine incomprehension, as though unable to reconcile the ruined creature before them with the ruin unfolding around us, and I confess I enjoyed that expression more than any tribute my court ever paid me in its golden centuries.

"This doesn't restore anything," the envoy said. "You'll die for nothing. She'll die too, your half-blood warden—the sacrifice clause, didn't you read it?"

"She read it," I said. "She read the fine print. That's the difference between her and your court. You people never read the fine print, and it's going to bury you." The crack reached the ground. Light spilled out of it—not gilded light, but the grey-green gleam of rainwater on roots, the color of my court, my actual court, leaking through the wound I'd made. "And as for restoring something—"

The gate broke.

Not shattered—*broke*, the way a chain breaks, the way a sentence breaks when it's been false the whole way through. The deep gate of the Court of Gild and Glass fell inward into its own darkness, and every road it hinged went suddenly, gloriously open, and far away I felt—through the last fraying edges of myself—fae paths lighting up across Thornfell like veins remembering blood, and a child's laughter echoing down all of them at once.

"There," I said. "That restored something."

I sat down. Sitting seemed wise. The envoy was shouting orders into the chaos, and the guards were streaming out of the broken gate into a world where their court's reach had just been amputated, and none of them looked at me, because I was already mostly gone—reflectionless, nameless again, but this time by choice, this time emptied rather than erased, and there is all the difference in the world.

I had perhaps a minute. I spent it on the name. *Court of Rainroots. Court of Rainroots. Court of Rainroots.* Saying it over and over the way you'd hold a dying animal, keeping it warm, keeping it in the world. Someone would remember it now. The scholar would write it down—Theo Bramble, who writes kindly, who writes everything. It would be in a book. Books outlast courts. Ask me how I know.

The last thing I saw was not the broken gate. It was weather—far off, up the mountain, at the spire where a woman made of two bloods was standing in a circle of stones with three keystones and a choice. Stormclouds gathering around her like an audience taking their seats. And underneath the clouds, faint but unmistakable, the smell of rain coming to a place that had forgotten rain was possible.

She smelled correct.

Oh, I thought. Oh, we might actually win.

And then the hollow queen of the Court of Rainroots, who had been dying for a hundred years, finally finished—and the finishing, I am pleased to report, felt nothing like subtraction at all. It felt like arriving home to a house I'd forgotten I owned, with the door open and the lamps lit and the rain beginning, gentle and warm, on everything that grew.

* * *

Theo Bramble will tell you what happened next, in his careful, forgiving hand. He will tell you about the winter breaking, about the spire, about the woman who became the storm-warden of Thornfell. He will write that the hollow court's heir—a moss-singer named Fen, who wept when I died and organized my people's survival by breakfast—rekindled the Court of Rainroots in the spring, under its true name, in caverns hung with living green.

But I got the gate. Remember that, whoever reads this. Before the dawn, before the covenant, before any of the triumphs that follow: a fading queen with nothing left broke the unbreakable door, and her court's name went into a book, and books outlast everything.

We were the Court of Rainroots.

Say it sometimes. It costs you nothing.

It cost me everything, and it was worth it.
