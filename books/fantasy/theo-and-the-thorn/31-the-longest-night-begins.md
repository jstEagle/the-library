## 31. The Longest Night Begins

**Ulyana**

The mountain screamed before I did.

That's the thing nobody tells you about living stone — it can scream. Not with a mouth. With every fault line and buried river and root of sleeping tree-heart at once, a groan that came up through my boots and into my teeth and out of me as a sound I didn't recognize as mine until I heard the echo come back off the ridge.

I was running. Vess's ghost-grip had burned off my wrist the way frost burns, cold first and then nothing at all, and I ran because running was the only instruction I'd ever been good at.

Behind me, above me, *everywhere* above me, the sky opened.

Not clouds. The veil itself — that gauzy membrane between what humans get to see and what the fae let them see — tore like wet linen. I felt it go the way you feel a stitch pop in an old scar: intimate, structural, wrong. Light poured through that wasn't light. Cold followed it down in sheets.

"Ulyana!" Theo caught my elbow, hauled me behind a shelf of granite as the first true snow hit — not falling so much as *arriving*, everywhere at once, fat flakes the size of coins. "Talk to me. What does torn look like?"

I laughed. It came out ugly. "It looks like midsummer snowing hard enough to bury us by morning. It looks like—" I groped for the words Ivo used, the mechanics, the covenant-as-machine language that had always made my eyes glaze. "It looks like the thermostat breaking."

"The what?"

"Nothing. Lowland word. Forget it." I pressed my palm flat against the granite. The oath was still in me — the Unmade Oath, third keystone, a sentence spoken into my bones by a dead woman's ghost — and through it I could feel the mountain the way you feel your own heartbeat if you're frightened enough. "The keystones are supposed to hold the seam closed. Three stones, one warden. The gilded court just pulled two of the three supports out from under the door."

"And we have all three," Theo said slowly. "Which means—"

"Which means they don't need to take them from us anymore. They just need to make sure we never reach the spire." I looked up. Through the tear, far off, something golden moved. Not close. Not yet. But moving the way weather moves — inevitable, disinterested in whether you approved. "How far to the camp?"

"Half a night. Less, if Pip's shortcut holds." He hesitated, and in the white glare of the tearing sky I watched him decide not to say something. That was becoming a habit between us — the unsaid things stacking up like unpaid debts.

"We don't have half a night anymore," I said. "We have whatever's left of tonight. Run."

**Theo**

Here is what I learned about apocalypse on the longest night: it is mostly walking.

I had imagined, in the safe hours when I imagined such things at all — which was more often than a scholar should admit — that the end of the world would feel like a thunderclap. A single catastrophic moment. Instead it felt like sleet in my collar, Dagny swearing steadily at the terrain ahead, Pip riding her shoulders with his chin tucked against her neck, and Ivo walking backward every fourth step to check our backtrail with the calm of a man who had already decided how he'd die and found it acceptable.

The snow didn't fall. It *accumulated*, the way debt accumulates, the way dread does — silently, everywhere, all at once. By the second hour it was past my knees. Ulyana broke trail, and watching her do it I understood something new about her: she wasn't fighting the storm. She was *arguing* with it. Her hands moved constantly, small gestures, half-spoken words in a tongue I only knew from the book's margins, and where she passed the snow lay looser, slower, almost reluctant.

"You're holding it back," I said, catching up to her shoulder.

"I'm *negotiating*." She didn't look at me. "There's a difference. This isn't my winter yet. It doesn't know whose side I'm on." A pause. Snow crusted her lashes; she looked, absurdly, like a saint in a lowland chapel painting, if saints swore under their breath. "It will learn."

"That's either comforting or terrifying."

"Yes," she said.

Pip started singing somewhere around the third hour. Nobody told him to stop. It was one of the hollow-court lullabies, the ones Vess's fading people had taught him, and the tune was wrong for marching — slow, mournful, meant for rocking cradles in caverns — but Dagny picked up the harmony without being asked, and then Ivo, his voice surprisingly sweet, and I realized I was listening to the last music anyone might bother making, and that we were making it anyway, and that this was apparently what courage sounded like when it got tired.

Then the light came over the ridge behind us, gold and total, and the singing stopped.

"They've found us," Ivo said. Not panicked. Just noting it, the way you note rain.

"No," Ulyana said. She had gone very still, head tilted, listening to something beneath the wind. "That's not hunters. That's the court itself. The whole weight of it." She turned to look back, and the gold light washed her face, and I saw her make a decision the way other people blink. "It's not chasing us. It's going to the spire first. They want to be standing on the wound when it opens."

"Then we've lost," Dagny said flatly.

"We haven't lost until the oath says we've lost." Ulyana faced forward again. "And the oath hasn't finished its sentence yet. Move."

**Serapha**

I had commanded the Order of the Broken Gale for eleven years, and in all that time I had never once given the order I gave that night.

"Turn around," I told my storm-callers. Forty-two of them, arrayed on the frozen heights above Marrowgate's ashes, their grey cloaks snapping in wind that no longer obeyed any of us. "Turn around, and march toward Thornfell's heart, and stand behind her."

Cassian, my second — young, ambitious, the kind of man who sharpened doctrine into knives — stared at me as though I'd begun speaking fish. "Warden Halloran is exiled. The order's writ—"

"The order's writ burned with Marrowgate." I let the words sit there. Let them be as heavy as they were. Behind us, the village we had quarantined smoked faintly under six feet of new snow, and I did not permit myself to look at it, because I had looked at it every hour since, and looking had stopped helping. "You were there. You carried the torches. If the writ still exists, it's written in those ashes, and I am telling you what it says now."

He held my gaze for a long moment. To his credit — and I would spend years learning how much credit he deserved — he turned. And forty-one others turned with him, because soldiers follow momentum more than conviction, and I had finally given them somewhere to go that wasn't a burning village.

We marched into the longest night carrying no warrant but the truth, which is a terrible weapon, too heavy to swing and useless at range, and the only one left worth holding.

**Ivo**

I want it recorded — in whatever book the Bramble boy writes, in whatever ledger the new covenant keeps, wherever — that I knew what was coming before anyone, and that knowing didn't save anybody anything, and I went anyway.

Here are the mechanics, plain, because someone has to say them plainly or they'll get dressed up later into something bearable:

The covenant is a door. Three keystones are its hinges. One warden is the hand upon it. Tear the hinges away and the door doesn't merely open — it stops being a door. Both realms pour through each other like water finding level. The gilded court thought they could manage the flood, shape it, ride it, put a warden of their own choosing on the spire afterward with the whole drowning world grateful for rescue.

They were wrong about one thing, and I knew it because I had been their soldier once too, before I defected, before Dagny fished me out of a frozen river and Pip bit me and I learned what a family was: they were wrong about *her*. They believed Ulyana Zhou was a weapon to be aimed. Any fool who had watched her count exits in a smuggler's inn would have known better. You cannot aim a person who has spent her whole life refusing to be pointed anywhere.

But here is what I also knew, walking that night with snow past my waist and the gold light growing behind us: being unaimable does not make you safe. It makes you the thing everyone must eventually deal with directly.

When the sky above Thornfell's peak split fully open — not a tear now but a wound, wide as a valley, gold light and black cold pouring out together — I stopped walking and looked up, and I understood that the clock hadn't been running down.

It had run out. Everything after this was overtime.

"Pip," I said quietly. "Come here."

He came. He always came, for me, though he'd bite strangers. I crouched and took his small cold face in my hands. "Whatever happens up there — whatever you see — you remember the roads. You're the only one who sees them clean. You hear me? You're not a passenger tonight. You're the map."

His eyes were enormous. Fae-touched, road-seeing, seven years old and older than that in the ways that mattered. "Is the queen going to die?" he asked. Because children ask the real questions while adults are busy asking survivable ones.

"I don't know, little brother."

"She already told me she would." He said it simply, terribly. "She said she saved the last of herself for a gate. She's been saving it since we met her. Like keeping the last honey in the jar."

I couldn't answer that. So instead I did what the hollow queen had taught all of us, in her fading, patient way: I spent what I had on what was in front of me. I stood up, and I took my place at the front beside Ulyana and Theo, and we walked into the gold light together, four outcasts and a ghost's promise, toward the spire where eternity had decided to keep its appointment early.

**Vess**

I had been dying for three hundred years. It is astonishing how long you can stretch a death when you refuse to hurry it.

My court fell when the covenant cracked the first time — a hairline fracture, a century ago, small enough that the human chroniclers missed it entirely. We were the hinge-side of the pact, my people, the ones who lived *in* the threshold, and when the threshold thinned, we thinned with it. Hollow. Fading. Neither dead nor permitted to live. The gilded court called it natural attrition. They lit their lanterns brighter so they wouldn't see us through the walls.

But a fading queen keeps accounts. And I kept mine.

I spent a sliver of myself to whisper a prophecy into a scholar's grandmother's ear, a hundred years ago. I spent another to guide that book through fire and water and one very dense young man's hands. I spent pieces on a smuggler's path, on a child's sight, on a ferrywoman's luck. Every coin of myself, invested. And tonight, watching the veil tear open above Thornfell while four stubborn mortals climbed toward the wound, I gathered up the last of the principal — everything I had hoarded, the final jar of honey — and I began to walk.

There was a gate at the base of the spire. Gilded. Locked. Built to admit only the court that made it, so that they could install their puppet warden in comfort while both realms froze.

Gates are my oldest craft. My court lived in thresholds; we *were* thresholds.

I put my hand against the gold, and I began, at last, unhurriedly, to spend.

* * *

**Theo**

The last mile, Ulyana stopped negotiating with the storm and started commanding it.

I don't have better words. I was trained in archives, in marginalia, in the careful hedge-language of scholarship, and none of it covers what happened when the exile who calls storms lifted her face to the tearing sky and spoke the old oath aloud in both tongues for the first time — the human words rough in her mouth, the fae words native, and I understood then, with a scholar's belated clarity, why the prophecy had needed her specifically. Not a hero. A *translator*. Someone who could say the same sentence in both worlds and mean it identically in each.

The snow bent around her. Not stopped — bent, like iron filings around a lodestone, like a crowd turning to listen. And through the gap she made, we climbed.

Dagny carried Pip. Ivo carried the keystones wrapped against his chest, all three of them humming now, resonating, answering the wound in the sky like strings answering a bow. I carried the book, water-damaged, restored, its hidden pages open against my heart, my grandmother's handwriting swimming in the gold light. *Of both bloods,* she had written, a century ago, in ink she died for. *Only of both bloods.*

At the crest of the last ridge, the spire came into view.

It rose out of the mountain's crown like a needle of black glass, and coiled around it, ascending, was the vanguard of the gilded court — knights of spun light, heralds with trumpets of frozen sound, and at their center, descending from the tear itself on a stair of solid gold air, Ysolde. The envoy. Beautiful beyond bearing, radiant with borrowed legitimacy, wearing the smile she had offered Ulyana across a campfire as an invitation and now wore as a verdict.

Below her, blocking the spire's single door, stood the gate. Gold. Sealed. Impregnable.

Ulyana looked at it, and at the army, and at Ysolde's descending splendor, and I braced for despair.

Instead she smiled. It was not a nice smile. It was the smile of a woman who had counted her exits her whole life and had finally, at the end of everything, run out of them — and discovered that this was freedom.

"They built a beautiful cage," she said, "and forgot to ask whether I wanted in." She rolled her shoulders. Above us the tear widened, and true winter roared down toward both realms at once, and somewhere below, faint but rising, came the sound of forty-two grey cloaks marching through the snow toward a confession.

"Ysolde!" she called, in a voice that carried over the end of the world. "You wanted a warden. Come negotiate."

And the gate at the spire's foot — sealed, impregnable, gilded — *cracked*.
