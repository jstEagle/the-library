## 32. March to the Spire

**Theo**

The world ended quietly, and then it didn't.

I want to say there was a moment of silence before the vanguard came—some cinematic pause where we all exchanged meaningful glances and resolved to die well—but the truth is that Pip sneezed, Dagny swore, and the ice wolves arrived mid-argument about who was carrying the last keystone.

"Theo," Ulyana said, very calmly, in the way she does when calm is a weapon she's sharpening against her own fear. "How attached are you to your fingers?"

"Moderately."

"Then stop holding the bell so tightly. It wants to ring."

The Bell of Hours sat in my arms like a sleeping cat that might, at any moment, decide to be a tiger instead. Since the true winter fell—the real one, the veil-torn, sky-splitting winter that had swallowed both realms whole—it had been growing heavier. Not physically. I weighed it on the ferry scales at camp out of scholarly stubbornness, and it hadn't gained an ounce. But every mile north it pressed harder on something behind my ribs, as though it were slowly remembering what it was for and wanted me to know too.

Ahead of us, the pass into Thornfell's heart had become a corridor of white. Not snow—snow would have been merciful. This was the gilded court's vanguard: soldiers of glass and frost poured into shapes that only approximated human, their armor grown from the same beautiful, lying crystal that lined their court's halls. They stood in ranks along the frozen riverbed, hundreds deep, and above them the aurora writhed like something wounded.

"They're not attacking," Ivo said beside me. He'd been a storm-caller once, before he defected, and his voice had the flatness of a man reading his own death notice. "They're waiting. That's worse."

"Why is it worse?" Pip asked, from her perch on Dagny's pack. The child saw fae roads the way other people see footpaths, and she was currently squinting at the air over the soldiers' heads like a woman reading an insulting letter.

"Because they think they've already won," Ivo said. "They're not defending the spire. They're escorting us to it."

I looked at Ulyana. She was watching the vanguard with an expression I'd learned meant she was doing arithmetic with people's lives, and she always rounded down on herself.

"Well," she said. "Rude of them to assume. Let's disappoint them."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about marching into eternity: it's mostly walking.

There was no dramatic charge. There was Ulyana at the front with her staff planted like a surveyor's stake, calling a wind out of the mountain itself—not summoned, I realized later, but *requested*, the way you ask a favor of someone older than your whole civilization—and the wind came, and it parted the cold around us into a corridor perhaps ten paces wide. The glass soldiers watched us pass through it. Some of them inclined their heads, which was either mockery or courtesy, and with the gilded court those are frequently the same thing.

Dagny walked with her shoulder still bound from the Sorrow Bridge. She shouldn't have been walking. The fever had broken three days ago, but she moved like a ship with a cracked hull, all stubbornness and groaning timber, and she carried Pip on her back because Pip had declared, with the absolute authority of an eight-year-old, that walking was for people who couldn't see roads.

"I hate this plan," Dagny said, for roughly the fortieth time since dawn.

"It's not a plan," Ulyana said. "It's a direction."

"That's what makes it a terrible plan."

"You could stay at camp."

"And miss watching you two idiots save the world?" She snorted, a great foggy exhalation. "I'd never forgive myself. Besides, someone has to carry the scholar when he faints."

"I don't faint," I said with dignity. "I occasionally experience strategic unconsciousness."

Pip laughed so hard she nearly fell off Dagny's back.

But beneath the banter—which was our family's native language, the tongue we'd built from shared abandonment—I could feel the thing none of us said. Every step north was a step toward the sacrifice clause. Ivo had explained it by firelight weeks ago, in his flat, careful way: three keystones, one warden, a binding without end. Everyone hopes it's metaphorical. Nobody believes it is.

And Ulyana had gone quiet in a way that wasn't grief or fear but *accounting*. I knew that look. It was the look from the ledger nights at the waystation, back when she was just the smuggler's guide who counted her money and counted her exits and let no one close enough to cost her anything. She was counting now. And I could see, plain as ink, that she intended to be the sum.

I had made my own offer at the spire's threshold—weeks ago, it felt like another life—and the mountain had declined me politely, the way a door declines a key cut for its neighbor. So if I couldn't take her place, I would do the next best thing. I would walk beside her the entire way, and I would write everything down, because someone had to remember, and because the Brambles had always believed that words kept things alive.

My grandmother died keeping a book alive. It seemed fitting that her grandson should keep a person.

* * *

The fighting started at the second ridge, where the corridor of wind thinned and the vanguard finally remembered it was an army.

Ulyana

I'll give the gilded court this much: their soldiers are punctual.

They came up the ridge in perfect formation, spears of black ice leveled, and for one absurd moment I thought of the order's drill yards—Serapha making us form lines in the rain until our hands went purple, telling us discipline was love. It wasn't. I know that now. But the muscle memory of those years is still in my shoulders, and when the first rank broke into a run, some traitor part of me almost called the counter-formations aloud.

Instead I did what exile taught me. I improvised.

"Ivo! Left flank, give me a downdraft on my mark. Dagny, get behind me and do NOT be heroic. Theo—" He was clutching the bell like a man clutching a lifeboat, which, fair. "Theo, whatever happens, don't let it ring until the spire."

"What happens if it rings early?"

"We find out together, quickly."

The first spear came for my throat and I caught it—actually caught it, bare-handed, and the cold bit through my glove like teeth—and then I put everything the mountain had given me into the answering blow. The storm answered. That's the difference between what I was and what I am now, or am becoming: I used to *call* storms, hauling them across distances like a fisher hauling nets. Now I only have to ask. The thunder under the ice heard me coming and rose to meet me like a dog hearing its name after years.

The left flank of the vanguard went down in a white wave. Ivo's downdraft hit a heartbeat later, exactly on the mark, and I felt a fierce, unexpected pride in him—he'd spent twenty years being told his power was shameful, and here he was spending it freely for a half-blood smuggler and a bag of books.

We fought through them for an hour. Maybe less. Time goes strange when you're trying not to die; it stretches like taffy and snaps shut behind you. I remember pieces. Dagny's oar—I'd forgotten she'd brought the oar, her old ferry oar, iron-shod, the most honest weapon I've ever seen—sweeping two glass soldiers off the ridge edge. Pip shrieking a warning in a language I didn't know and understanding anyway. Theo, gods help him, swinging the Bell of Hours like a mace when a soldier got past me, and the bell *not ringing*, just going silent-heavy in his grip and cracking the soldier's chest like lake ice.

"You hit it with a priceless artifact," I said afterward, breathless.

"In my defense," he said, also breathless, "it seemed to enjoy it."

And underneath all of it, running through me like a second pulse: the mountain. I could feel Thornfell waking beneath our boots with every step, vast and slow and attentive, the way you feel a sleeping giant shift in the room. The land was a witness. Ivo had told me that once, and the mountain itself had shown me more—the covenant-making, my ancestor's hands on the same rope I was climbing. It had waited a hundred years for someone to come finish the sentence.

I intended to finish it. I just wished I knew how the sentence ended.

* * *

We broke through the vanguard at dusk—or what passed for dusk now, under a sky that hadn't decided whether to be night or apocalypse. Behind us, the frozen ranks lay scattered, and ahead of us the pass opened into the heart of Thornfell, and there it was.

The spire.

I'd seen it in visions. Visions lie by simplifying. In reality it was a pillar of stone that predated stone, wound with carvings worn nearly smooth, standing in a bowl of the mountains where the aurora bent downward like hands cupping a flame. Three sockets waited near its crown. Two keystones hung at my belt—the bell in Theo's arms, the Rootcrown wrapped in wool against my heart, still faintly warm, still faintly *alive*. The third wasn't an object. The third was an oath, held in a dead woman's memory, waiting to judge whether I was worth the trouble.

The cold coming off the spire was different from the battlefield cold. Battlefield cold wants to kill you. This cold wanted to *end* you—patiently, completely, forever. The true winter's source, pouring out of the torn veil above the spire like water from a split dam.

"Everyone breathe," I said. My voice shook and I let it. There was no one left to perform strength for except people who'd earned the truth. "This is it. Beyond that ridge, it's me and the oath and whatever the mountain decides I am."

"No," Theo said.

I turned. He was setting the bell down in the snow, carefully, the way he handled everything that mattered, and looking at me with that stubborn scholar's face that had ruined my careful invisible life from the moment he walked into my inn with a water-damaged book.

"No," he repeated. "It's us. You don't get to do the noble-loner speech. I've read the original texts, Ulyana. The covenant-making wasn't a solitary act. The first warden had witnesses. Had *people*. That's the whole point—that's why it held for a century. A warden alone is just a prisoner. A warden loved is a bridge."

Behind him, Dagny folded her arms. Ivo straightened his bad shoulder. Pip climbed onto a rock specifically to be taller than me.

"We're coming to the ridge," Dagny said. "Not past it. To it. And we'll stand there, and if the mountain wants you, it can take you knowing full well what you're leaving behind, because we're not letting you pretend you weren't—"

Her voice cracked. The hull, groaning.

"—weren't somebody's," she finished, gruffly. "You're somebody's, girl. Get used to it."

I looked at them—this ferrywoman with her iron-shod oar, this defector with his ashamed and generous power, this feral child who saw roads in the air, this scholar who'd crossed a realm carrying my name in a dead woman's handwriting—and I felt the loneliness I'd built my whole life on finally, finally fail to hold weight. Like ice in thaw. Like a wall discovering it was only ever mortar.

"Fine," I said, and my voice broke too, and none of us mentioned it. "To the ridge. Together."

* * *

Theo

We reached the ridge as the aurora tore fully open.

Above the spire, the veil ripped like wet paper, and beyond it was not sky. It was winter's origin—a whiteness so complete it made snow look like shadow—and through the tear the gilded court's power poured in a slow, glittering flood. The last of their honor guard knelt along the bowl's rim, and at their center, radiant even now, stood the envoy who had once offered Ulyana everything.

She saw us coming. She smiled, beautifully.

Ulyana picked up the Bell of Hours, kissed her fingertips and touched them to the Rootcrown, and stepped down onto the ice toward the spire, toward the ghost waiting in its memory, toward the oath that would take her name and keep it.

She didn't look back. She didn't need to. We were all looking at her.

The mountain began to speak.
