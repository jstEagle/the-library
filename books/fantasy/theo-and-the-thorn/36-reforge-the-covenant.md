## 36. Reforge the Covenant

The spire had been waiting a long time.

I could feel that the way you feel a kettle about to sing—not heat exactly, but attention, everything in the mountain leaning toward this one point of itself like a held breath. Thornfell's heart wasn't stone. It was stone *remembering* being something else, and it remembered loudly.

We came up out of the last tunnel with ice on our eyelashes and blood on our knuckles—most of it other people's, which I counted as a professional achievement—and there it stood: a column of black glass rising through the cavern's open throat to a circle of torn sky, veined now with silver light, humming like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

"Cozy," Dagny said. She was leaning on Ivo's shoulder and my spare cloak and pure spite, in roughly that order of load-bearing importance. The wound on her side had soaked through three bandages since the Sorrow Bridge. She'd refused to stay behind at the camp with the tone of a woman who would haunt us otherwise, and I believed her. Dagny struck me as someone who would be an *extremely* irritating ghost.

Pip pressed against Theo's leg, eyes huge. "It's loud," Pip whispered. "It's saying a word but it keeps forgetting it."

"That," I said, "makes two of us."

Theo touched my elbow. He'd gotten good at knowing when I needed touching versus when I needed space—a skill he'd apparently learned from a book, because everything Theo knew came from a book eventually, filtered through his grandmother's handwriting and his own stubborn decency. "Ulyana."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"Ivo." I turned to him before Theo could finish a sentence we'd both already had twice. "Tell me the fine print one more time. All of it. No mercy."

Ivo had been a storm-caller for thirty years before he defected, and he had the defector's habit of telling the truth only when cornered. He cornered himself now, jaw working. "Three keystones. Bell, Rootcrown, and the Oath. You set them in the spire's cradle, you speak the covenant in both tongues, and then—" He looked away. "Then the mountain chooses its warden. And the mountain doesn't choose casually. Warden means bound. Body and name and weather. You don't retire from it. You don't leave it. There are stories about wardens trying."

"What happened in the stories?"

"They became storms," he said simply. "And stayed storms."

Lovely. My former order had exiled me for a crime I didn't commit, and now the alternative career path on offer was *permanent meteorological employment*. Somewhere Serapha was probably laughing, if she'd survived holding the pass behind us. If she hadn't—no. Not yet. Grief could queue up with everything else. It was used to waiting.

"Theo takes the book," I said. "If this goes wrong, he restores what he can and gets everyone down the Goat's Teeth to the lowlands. Pip knows the fae roads. Dagny argues with anyone who tries to stop them, which will be very effective because no one has ever won."

"I have won arguments," Dagny said.

"Name one."

She thought about it. "Give me a minute."

* * *

I climbed.

The spire's base was a cradle of black glass shaped—for this purpose, and possibly only tonight—like hands. Three hollows waited in it, worn smooth by centuries of nothing filling them. I took the keystones from the wrapping Theo had made of his own coat lining, because he was the sort of man who lined his coat with prophecy-proofing and still got frostbite in one ear.

The Bell of Hours first. It was small, no bigger than a fist, bronze gone green with drowned years, and when I set it in the glass it rang once without being touched—a sound I felt in my back teeth, in the scar tissue over my ribs, in whatever part of me had been quietly counting hours toward this since Marrowgate. The frost on the cavern walls shivered.

The Rootcrown second. It had been a living knot of heartwood until four days ago; it was dead now, or paused, or sleeping, and it weighed far more than wood should. When it settled into its hollow the whole spire *groaned*, deep and tectonic, and somewhere far above snow slid off a peak with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

The third hollow stayed empty.

Because the third keystone wasn't in my pack. It never had been. The Unmade Oath lived in the memory of the first warden's ghost, who had spent the last hour standing at the edge of the torchlight looking like a woman carved out of winter and patience. Her name had been Yeva. The mountain had shown me that much, and more: Yeva half-blood, Yeva beloved by both realms and trusted by neither, Yeva speaking words in two tongues while her human family watched from below and her fae kin watched from above, and no one—not one soul—standing beside her when the mountain closed around her voice.

I understood why she'd demanded proof. I'd have been bitter too.

"You came," she said. Her voice was wind finding a crack under a door.

"You didn't give me a lot of scheduling flexibility."

That earned me something that might, in better centuries, have become a smile. She drifted closer, and where she passed, the torches bent toward her like grass. "They sent you alone."

"My family is right there." I nodded down at the ragged line of them—Theo with his burned hands and his grandmother's book held against his chest, Dagny propped upright through sheer cussedness, Ivo with tears freezing in his lashes because storm-callers feel storms even small ones, and Pip, who was waving at the ghost, because Pip was six and had not yet learned that ghosts are supposed to be frightening. "They're not allowed closer. Your rules, not mine."

"My rules kept the world warm for nine hundred years." Yeva looked at the empty hollow. "Say them, then. Both tongues. Both bloods. Say them true, or the spire will know, and it does not forgive flattery."

"And if I say them true?"

"Then the mountain chooses." Her eyes were the grey of sky deciding. "It chose me. It may refuse you. Half-bloods are rare, exile. Rarer than courage."

"I've been told I'm short on both."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the end of your life as you know it: it sounds like language.

I put my hand flat against the spire's glass and began with the lowland tongue, the plain one, the one of markets and grudges and my father's voice teaching me river-names before the order took me. *Sky above, root below. Storm my breath, stone my bone. What breaks, I bind. What binds, I carry.* The old words of the lowland villages, the ones grandmothers said over cradles without remembering they were half a contract.

The spire drank them. Frost crept backward off the walls.

Then the high tongue—the fae speech I'd been punished for dreaming in as a child, syllables like weather forming, the grammar of things that live longer than nations. My mother's tongue, though I hadn't known that until Vess told me, until the word *mother* stopped being a locked room and became a wound with a name on it. *Ael en tharra, oel en vane. Su'mael korith, su'vane koriel.* Sky above, root below. What breaks, I bind. What binds, I—

I stopped.

Because the last phrase was the sacrifice clause, the fine print Ivo had warned me about, and seeing it written in actual language rather than dread made it real in a way dread never managed: *su'veth anna* — I am the hinge, and the door swings through me. Forever. Not a job. A becoming. The mountain doesn't hire wardens. It marries them.

"Ulyana." Theo's voice from below, cracked straight down the middle. "Hey. Look at me."

I looked. He was holding up the book, open to the page that had started all of this, my name inked in water-damaged letters a century before my birth. *The exile who calls storms shall reforge the covenant or bury both realms in winter.* Prophecies, it turns out, don't predict. They just take attendance.

"If there's another way," he said, "we find it. We're good at finding ways. We found a way across the Goat's Teeth in a whiteout, we found a way past the gilded court's whole frozen vanguard, we can—"

"There isn't." I smiled at him, and it hurt, and I let him see it hurt, because we'd traded truths by firelight and lying to him now would undo every one of them. "But here's the thing nobody wrote down, Theo. Not the book. Not Yeva. Not the fine print." I turned back to the spire, to the empty hollow, to the ghost waiting with nine hundred years of loneliness in her face. "The clause says the warden is bound to the mountain. It says *nothing* about the mountain being bound to nothing. Covenants go both ways. That's the entire point of them. Everyone keeps reading this oath like a leash. It's a rope. Ropes have two ends."

Yeva went very still. Ghosts shouldn't be able to look surprised, but grief makes exceptions.

"You were alone when you spoke it," I said softly. "Both realms watching, neither helping. So the oath grew teeth, because you swore it out of abandonment, and oaths take the shape of the mouth that speaks them." I lifted my chin toward my people. "Mine won't."

*I speak it witnessed,* I said, in both tongues at once, letting the languages braid the way they must have braided in Yeva's mouth and in my mother's and in every half-blood throat the world had tried to split in two. *I speak it chosen. Sky above, root below. What breaks, I bind. What binds, I carry. Su'veth anna — I am the hinge, and I swing willingly.*

The third keystone arrived.

It didn't come from anywhere. It condensed—out of my spoken words, out of the space between the tongues, a shape made of the oath itself, luminous and cold and unmistakably *mine*, and I set it into the final hollow with the care of a woman laying down a blade she has carried so long she'd forgotten her hand was empty underneath it.

The mountain woke.

Not moved—woke. Every tunnel we'd crawled, every ridge we'd bled across, every buried chapel and tree-heart and hollow court: all of it turned its attention to this one chamber, and the attention had weight, and the weight settled onto me like snowfall in reverse, rising instead of falling, taking me at the ankles, the knees, the ribs, the throat.

It should have felt like drowning. Everyone had promised me drowning.

It felt like being *answered*.

All my life I'd called storms the way the order taught—commands, coercion, weather as a weapon you aimed. And the weather always obeyed the way a resentful soldier obeys: technically, minimally, waiting for you to turn your back. Now I opened whatever I had instead of a self and reached outward with no command at all, and the storms of two realms turned toward me like dogs hearing their names.

Not weapons. *Voice.* The gale tearing at the peaks above us gentled mid-scream into something almost curious. The endless winter grinding down on the lowlands faltered, confused, a machine suddenly aware it had a pilot. I could feel every flake between here and the sea. I could feel the pressure systems queued up beyond the horizon like armies awaiting orders, and beneath them, faint and green and stubborn, the pulse of spring, still alive, still waiting for permission.

"Hello," I said to all of it. Out loud, because some habits survive transcendence. "I'm the new warden. We're going to do this differently."

The mountain's answer was not words. It was the Bell of Hours ringing through my bones, the Rootcrown's deadwood flushing faintly brown, the spire's silver veins flooding gold, and Yeva—Yeva stepping forward through nine hundred years of solitary custody and pressing her winter-carved forehead to mine.

"Well sworn," she whispered. "Well *chosen*. Take it, exile. It was always going to belong to someone the rest of us couldn't keep."

And she let go—of the office, of the tether, of whatever had strung her ghost between two duties for ten centuries—and came apart into snowfall that fell upward, into the storm, into me, and I caught every flake of her because that was the job now. Catching things. Keeping them warm.

The last thing she gave me was her memory of the first morning after her own binding: dawn over two realms, frost lifting, and the terrible, radiant knowledge that she had traded everything and received, in exchange, *the sky*.

Fair trade, Yeva. Fair trade.

* * *

I came back to myself kneeling at the spire's base, and the first thing I saw was Pip's face, upside-down, extremely close.

"You're glowing," Pip announced. "Also your hair is doing weather."

"It does that now."

"Can you make it rain frogs?"

"Pip." Theo hauled the child back by the collar, but he was laughing, wet-eyed laughing, and when he pulled me up off the floor he held on longer than balance required. His hands were shaking. So were mine. For different reasons, or maybe the same one. "You did it. The veins are gold. The frost is—I can see it retreating, look, the whole eastern wall—" He stopped. Studied my face with those scholar's eyes that catalogued everything. "What did it cost? Tell me the truth. We agreed on truth."

"Truth." I flexed my fingers. They were translucent at the edges, faintly silver-veined, like the spire had lent me some of its material and forgotten to ask for it back. "I'm bound to the mountain. Its borders are mine and I'm theirs. I can't walk to the lowlands and buy bread ever again, if that was on your list of future plans."

His face did something complicated.

"But," I said, and watched hope hit him like a physical blow, "storms travel. Storms go everywhere. And it turns out that so do I, as long as I'm going where the weather goes—which, given current events, is *everywhere*. The mountain didn't cage me, Theo. It gave me the widest sky anyone's ever owned." I laughed, and the laugh came out with a little thunder in it, and I decided I could live with that. "Your grandmother wrote me into a book. The mountain wrote me into itself. Between the two of them, I've gone from invisible to unavoidable. There's a lesson in there about anonymity, but I'm too tired to learn it."

Dagny pushed off Ivo's shoulder, limped forward, and looked me up and down with the frank appraisal of a woman pricing cargo. "So what, you're the weather now."

"I'm the weather now."

"Good. It owes me money."

Ivo dropped to one knee, which embarrassed us both enormously, and then Dagny did it too, groaning, and Pip did it because Pip copied everything, and I said, "Oh, absolutely not, get up, all of you, we have a world to unfreeze and no time for—" But Theo knelt too, last and slowest, looking up at me with an expression I'd seen once before, in the drowned chapel, when the guardian showed him his grandmother alive and writing.

"Don't," I said.

"Storm-caller," he said, grinning through the tears he'd deny later. "Warden of Thornfell. Get used to it."

Above us, through the spire's broken crown, the torn sky was knitting. I could feel the seam closing under my new hands—feel the gilded court's desperate tear in the veil losing its grip, feel the true winter that had swallowed both realms meeting, for the first time in its long hungry existence, something it could not digest. It fought me. Of course it fought me. It had eaten border villages and hollow courts and a hundred years of covenant, and it did not intend to be evicted by a disgraced smuggler with translucent fingertips.

But it had never fought a warden with witnesses.

I reached up through the mountain's throat, gathered the storm the way you gather a frightened animal—slowly, honestly, with both palms open—and spoke the word Yeva had left lodged in my chest like a splinter of dawn.

Winter broke.

Not gently—it shrieked, it *shattered*, avalanches of loosened cold sliding off the peaks, the frozen veil splitting along seams of gold light, and beyond it, far below, in the lowlands where Marrowgate's survivors were rebuilding fields from ash, I felt the first thaw of a century land on upturned faces. I felt them look up.

My family looked up too—all except Pip, who was already asking whether the frogs were next—and somewhere in the gold light flooding down through the spire's crown, something answered my greeting that was not Yeva, not the mountain, not any voice I knew.

It sounded amused. And it said my mother's name.
