## 37. Winter Breaks

### Theo

I have read a great many accounts of miracles. My grandmother's library was full of them—saints splitting seas, kings waking from hundred-year sleeps, shepherds who talked to the moon and got answers. Every one of those books had the same flaw: they were written by people who weren't there.

So let the record show what it actually felt like, standing on the frozen slopes of Thornfell when the covenant reforged, watching Ulyana Zhou become something the world had been waiting a century to remember:

It felt like an exhale.

Not a bang. Not a shatter of glass and glory. An exhale—the whole mountain, both realms, every buried thing letting go of a breath it had been holding since before my grandmother was born.

We were running for the spire when it happened. That's the part nobody will believe later, so I'll write it plainly: we were losing. The gilded court's vanguard had come out of the torn veil like winter given teeth—things of rime and hunger, beautiful in the way avalanches are beautiful—and our little army of outcasts, ferrywomen, feral children, and one defected storm-caller with a bad shoulder, was being pushed back down the mountain step by bloody step.

And then the sky opened.

Not with thunder. With *color*. I have seen dawn over the lowland marshes, dawn over the sea off Marrowgate before it burned, dawn through the stained glass of six different archives. None of it prepared me for dawn happening *inside* a storm. The black clouds above the spire lit from within, gold and green and that impossible violet you only see in fae roads, and the light poured downward like someone had unstoppered a bottle held upside-down for a hundred years.

The vanguard stopped mid-charge. All of them. Hundreds of creatures of endless winter, frozen—forgive the word—in place, their faces turning upward with what I can only describe as confusion. They had been made for a war without end. Nobody had told them it could end.

Then the snow began to fall *upward*.

I watched flakes lift off my own sleeves, off Dagny's bloodied bandages, off Pip's wild hair, rising past us into the brightening clouds like the whole storm was being gathered home. The cold didn't break all at once—it *peeled*, layer by layer, the way grief does when you finally set it down. Underneath, impossibly, absurdly, the mountain was warm.

Pip grabbed my hand. "Look," she said, in the voice of a child seeing her first snowfall instead of her last one for a while. "Look look look."

Downslope, where the ice fields had stretched flat and white to the horizon since the Longest Night began, the ice was moving. Retreating. And beneath it—not grass yet, not yet, but the dark wet suggestion of grass, the color earth turns when it remembers spring—

Dagny, half-collapsed against a boulder, started to laugh. It was not a pretty laugh. It was the laugh of a woman who had rowed corpses out of frozen rivers, and it went on far too long, and I found I was laughing too, and Ivo was shouting something in the old high-tongue that was either a prayer or a curse, and none of it mattered, because the sun came over the eastern peaks and touched the snowmelt and turned the whole mountainside into falling diamonds.

Somewhere above us, at the spire, Ulyana stood in the heart of her own storm.

She couldn't hear us. I know that now. She said so later, in her flat way, the way she says things that cost her the most: *"It was very loud, being the weather."*

But I swear—I will swear it on any book you put in front of me—that for one moment, as the light broke over the ridge, she looked down the slope directly at where we stood. At our ragged, freezing, grieving, surviving handful of people.

And the rain that fell on us afterward was warm.

### Dagny

Here's what they don't put in the songs, because I've heard three of them already and they're all rubbish: the first thing that happened after the winter broke was that people started crying, and the second thing that happened was that people started working, because that's what people do.

The village at the mountain's foot didn't have a name yet. It barely had walls. What it had was forty-one survivors of Marrowgate, a defected storm-caller, a scholar with ink permanently stained up both arms, a child who could walk through doors that didn't exist, and me, with a hole in my side from the Sorrow Bridge that the healer said would've killed anyone less stubborn. Flattery. I've been called worse.

That first morning of thaw, we dug graves. Eleven of them, for the ones we'd lost on the march and the bridge and the long frozen weeks between. Ivo sang the high-tongue mourning and Pip put a river-stone on each mound because she said the dead get restless without something heavy to hold. Theo wrote all their names in his book, careful as a priest, asking me twice how to spell Ferro's surname because he refused to guess at a man's name even a month after burying him.

Good boy. Wrong trade, but good boy.

By noon we were hauling the frost-killed timber off the eastern palisade, and by evening somebody—Pip, naturally—found the old well under the ice and it was still sweet, still cold, still *there*, and I sat on its edge with my side screaming and drank water that hadn't seen the sun in a season and thought about Marrowgate.

Thought about the order's riders coming through with their quarantine banners and their tidy justifications. Thought about how they'd called it mercy, burning a village to save a country.

And then I looked up, because the wind changed—and there she was. A cloud, low over the western ridge, shaped like nothing and moving like something. Moving like it was *checking on us*.

"You're showing off," I told it.

A single drop of warm rain hit me square between the eyes.

Stubborn woman. Dead stubborn woman, if the order had gotten their way. Bound to the mountain forever, they tell me—no leaving, no ordinary life, no growing old in a house with a garden. When they explained it to me, I'll admit, I thought it was a raw deal.

But I've watched her these few weeks since. Watched the storms go where she goes. And I've watched her come down to the village every seventh day, walking on air-currents like a woman strolling a road only she can see, sitting on the well-edge with her boots swinging like anybody's, eating burnt bread with her hands like anybody's, listening to Pip talk for an hour straight like it was the finest music ever composed.

No ordinary life. No. But I never much cared for ordinary, myself. Neither did she.

The cloud drifted east toward the lowlands, where the rivers were running again and the first boats were going back in the water, and I went back to work, and the day was warm, and that was enough. That was everything.

### Pip

The hollow court woke up on the third day of spring, and I was the one who heard it first, because I hear everything first, because grown-ups have wax in their ears.

It sounded like this: *hummmmmm.* Down under the roots, under the Sorrow Bridge, under everywhere. Like a beehive made of bells. Like the mountain purring.

Vess's heir is named Sorrel. She was a hollow-court lady-in-waiting who'd faded so thin you could see lamplight through her, and when the covenant reforged, the light filled her up like water filling a dry cup, and now she glows faintly even in daylight, which Pip personally thinks is showing off but which everyone else thinks is beautiful, so.

Theo and me brought her down into the cavern-city the first week. It had been empty and gray and echo-y for a hundred years, and when we walked in with Sorrel, the gray *lifted*. Moss came back on the walls like green fire spreading. Little lights woke up in the ceilings—cave-crystals, Sorrel says, though I say they're eggs and I'm keeping my theory. And out of the deep galleries came the others. The hollow ones. Dozens. Some so faded they were mostly voices. They came crowding around her, touching her sleeves, making sounds that weren't words, and Sorrel stood in the middle of it crying light instead of tears, and Theo cried actual tears, and I stood on a rock and supervised.

"Will you be their queen?" Theo asked her.

"I was a *seamstress*," she said, horrified.

"They don't care," I said. "They followed Vess and Vess couldn't sew either."

Which settled it, apparently, because politics works exactly like that when you're nine.

The hollow folk are rebuilding now. Their city has a name again—Cindermoss, it used to be called, before the gilded court pretended it had never existed. There are banners going up, woven from Sorrel's own thread, silver-green. There are doors opening that haven't opened since before the Long Winter, and some of them open onto places I didn't even know about, and I have been *insufferable* about it and I regret nothing.

Ulyana came down for the rekindling ceremony. First time most of the hollow folk saw her up close. They knelt—all of them, the whole crowd, like wheat in wind—and she stood there looking extremely uncomfortable and finally said, "Get up, please. I'm not—" and stopped, and tried again, and said, "Your queen kept her promise to me. I intend to keep mine to you."

Sorrel took her hands. Half-blood to half-blood. Nobody had to explain it; everybody knew.

"The mountain chose well," Sorrel said.

"The mountain," said Ulyana, "didn't give me a lot of choice."

But she smiled when she said it. Sort of. If you knew her. Which I do.

Afterward she taught me a weather-game. You hold your palm flat and she sends one tiny cloud, small as a moth, to circle it. If you keep still, it rains on your hand. Warm rain. Pip's favorite thing in two realms, and I am including honey in that count, which tells you everything.

### Ivo

I was there when they stripped the gilded court. I want that written down somewhere other than Theo's careful pages, because Theo writes it like history and it wasn't history. It was a Tuesday.

The court hadn't fled when the winter broke. That was the mistake. Their gate was shattered—Vess spent herself breaking it, may her name shine—and their heralds had scattered, but the court itself had gone to ground in their palace of glass above the veil, waiting, certain the thaw was temporary. Certain the world would freeze again and come crawling back to whoever had kept the keys to the cold.

Arrogance is a kind of blindness. I should know; I wore the order's blindfold for thirty years.

They didn't count on the hollow folk remembering. A century of fading, and they remembered every debt. They remembered which gilded lords had voted to let their court starve. They remembered the names.

When Sorrel led the procession up through the fae roads to the glass palace—hollow folk, human witnesses, and me carrying the Bell of Hours because apparently I have an honest face—they didn't fight. The beautiful things never do, once beauty stops being armor. The court's remaining power cracked like river ice underfoot, and the old compacts surfaced, and the assembled courts of root and moss and cinder read out the ledger of what the gilded court had done: the engineered decay, the forced winter, the children of two bloods hunted and hidden for generations.

Stripped. That's the formal word. Their titles dissolved. Their gates sealed against them. Their names struck from the compacts so that no fae road would open at their call again. They are still there, in their glass palace, powerful as any rich exile and welcome nowhere—which is to say, they became what they always pretended everyone else was.

One of them, a lord with frost still in his hair, spat at Sorrel: "You'll beg us back within ten years."

"No," Sorrel said. And the terrible thing, the thing I think about at night, is that she sounded *certain*. Not angry. Certain. "You mistook endurance for weakness for a hundred years. We are done being mistaken about."

I stood in that hall of glass holding a bell older than every grudge in the room, and I thought about the order. About Serapha kneeling in the snow at the spire's base, confessing the forgery to every storm-caller present, her voice steady and her hands shaking. About pardon offered and refused and finally, quietly, accepted—not for herself, but so the order could be remade honest from the confession outward.

Institutions are just stories people agree to believe. The gilded court believed itself eternal. So did we.

Spring doesn't ask permission. That's the whole lesson. Spring just comes, and you're either rooted or you're not.

* * *

### Theo

On the last page of my grandmother's book, beneath the prophecy that ruined and remade so many lives, there is a line in her handwriting that the restoration magic missed until now—so small I'd taken it for a smudge:

*"Whoever reads this: the future is not a sentence. It is a door. Someone has to open it."*

I used to think she meant the prophecy was a door. I understand her better now. She meant Ulyana. She meant everyone.

Tonight I sat at the campfire with the book closed on my knees and did not open it. Dagny was mending nets that won't be needed till summer. Pip was asleep with her head on Ivo's pack, her fingers twitching—dreaming of fae roads, probably, running through them even now. Above us, the night sky was doing something it hadn't done in over a year: showing stars. Clean, cold-bright, ordinary stars.

And along the western ridge, a storm walked. Slow, unhurried, trailing warm rain across the thawing valleys, pausing over the rebuilt fields below Marrowgate the way a shepherd pauses over a flock. Everyone in camp felt it pass. Nobody flinched. Pip, half-asleep, smiled at it.

My grandmother wrote a century ago that the exile who calls storms would reforge the covenant or bury both realms in winter.

She left out the best part. She left out that the exile would find a family. That the family would stay.

Tomorrow I start writing the true account—no prophecy, no poetry, just what happened and who did it. But tonight I just sat with the people the prophecy never mentioned, and watched the weather keep watch over us, and for the first time since a burned archive and a dead grandmother set me on this road,

I was not going anywhere.

Above the ridge, the storm paused. And if I didn't know better—if I were still the man who arrived at a smuggler's inn clutching a ruined book and asking foolish questions—I'd have sworn it paused right over our fire.

Deliberately.

Like someone checking, one last time tonight, that her family was safe below her.

The rain that reached us smelled like lightning and green things. And then the storm moved on toward the lowlands, unhurried, and I picked up my pen, because someone has to write it all down—

—and that was when Pip sat bolt upright in her sleep, wide-eyed, and said: "There's a door in the mountain that's been shut since before the first covenant. And something behind it just knocked."
