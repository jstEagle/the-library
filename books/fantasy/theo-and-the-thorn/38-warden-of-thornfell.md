## 38. Warden of Thornfell

The first thing they don't tell you about becoming a mountain's storm-warden is that you will spend the rest of your life learning where your body ends.

Not in the poetic way. I mean it literally. On my third morning as Warden of Thornfell, I woke on a granite shelf above the Hollow Court with frost in my eyelashes and no memory of climbing there, and when I reached down to brush it off, my fingers passed through the stone like it was fog. I sat up too fast, heart hammering, and felt the whole ridge flinch under me — actually *flinch*, an apology rippling through six miles of bedrock — and I had to sit very still for a long while, breathing, while the mountain murmured its embarrassment through the soles of my feet.

*I'm sorry,* said Thornfell, in the wordless way it says things. *I forgot you were only visiting that part of yourself.*

*That part of myself.* Wonderful. I'd traded one cage for another and this one didn't even have walls. It just had me.

I want to be clear about something, because Theo keeps writing it down wrong in his book: I don't regret it. That's not the same as saying it doesn't hurt.

Here is what I gave up: the road. The real road, the one I loved, the smugglers' trails and goat paths and the long anonymous miles where nobody knew my name because I made sure of it. I can't take those roads anymore. Not on foot, not as a woman. When I walk now, I walk the way storms walk — which is to say everywhere at once and nowhere particular, arriving without having traveled. I stood at Marrowgate's edge last week watching Dagny argue with a ferryman about cargo weights, and she looked straight through me twice before Pip elbowed her and hissed, "She's *here*, you great barnacle, look at the rain." And sure enough, where I stood, the drizzle bent around me like water around a stone in a stream, drawing a clean dry outline in the wet air. That's how they see me now. A absence-shaped thing in the weather. My own family squinting at the space where I used to be.

Dagny apologized for an hour afterward. Brought me stew I couldn't eat and sat with me talking anyway, because that's what Dagny does — she decided years ago that grief is best handled like a leaky boat, by bailing constantly and refusing to discuss the ocean. She talked about ferry tariffs until I laughed, which was the point, and then she said, gruffly, into the fire, "You're still you. The weather's just louder about it."

That's Dagny for you. She should write proverbs.

But here is what I gained, and I keep a ledger of this too, because ledgers are the one habit from my smuggling days I refuse to surrender:

Item: I walked the whole spine of Thornfell yesterday. All forty leagues of it, peak to root, in the time it takes to brew tea. I have seen every fold of this mountain now, every hidden valley, every cave where something old and patient sleeps. It is the most beautiful map in the world and it lives inside my skin.

Item: When the first frost threatened the rebuilt fields at Marrowgate — a false autumn snap, nothing covenant-related, just ordinary weather being cruel — I turned it aside. Not with a battle. With a *suggestion*. The clouds parted over the barley like a curtain drawn by a considerate host, and the sun came down warm, and a farmer named Osla looked up at the sky and said "thank the saints" to no one, and I got to be the saints. Quietly. Anonymously. Which was, I'll admit, the entire pleasure of it.

Item: I am never cold. I am never lost. I cannot be followed unless the weather itself betrays me, and the weather and I are on excellent terms these days.

Item, and this is the big one, the one I circle back to at night when the mountain hums its slow granite lullaby and I lie awake being homesick for a life I chose to leave: I am *trusted* now. Openly. The order sent a formal delegation in high summer — Serapha at its head, gray-streaked and straight-backed, carrying documents with seals I once would have forged for pocket money. They came to apologize. Formally. With paperwork. Serapha read the full confession aloud at the foot of the mountain while forty storm-callers knelt in the mud, and when she finished she looked up at the peaks where she knew I'd be listening, and she said, "We were wrong. Not mistaken. *Wrong.*" And then she left the pardon documents weighted under a stone at the trailhead, addressed to Ulyana Zhou, exile.

I kept the stone. Threw the documents off a cliff. The wind took them apart over the valley, page by page, and I confess it felt better than forgiveness would have.

Because here's the truth of it, the thing I'm still learning to say out loud even to Theo: I don't want their pardon. Pardon implies I did something requiring it. What I want is what I have — a mountain that chose me, a family that stayed, and a name that finally means what I decide it means. Let the order keep its registers. I'm registered somewhere older.

* * *

Theo comes up the mountain most evenings, which shouldn't be possible, since the upper slopes are mine now and mortals find them inhospitable at best. But Theo has never met an impossibility he didn't immediately try to take notes on. He climbs with that battered prophecy book strapped to his back like a relic — it's not a prophecy book anymore, we saw to that; the pages went blank the night the covenant reforged, all except one, but he carries it anyway, out of respect for his grandmother and for everything it cost.

He built a shelter at the tree line. A proper little hut, absurdly well-made, with a stove and a desk and a window facing the summit. He calls it a research station. Everyone else calls it the warden's porch, because everyone knows exactly who the research is *for*.

Tonight I arrive the way I usually do — as a thickening of the evening mist off the glacier, coalescing at the edge of his firelight — and he looks up from his desk, quill paused, and does the thing he always does: that small startled smile, like he's surprised I came, even though I come every night. Even though I've told him repeatedly that I am functionally omniscient within my borders and he has never once been alone up here.

"One day," he says, "that will stop being unsettling."

"It won't."

"No," he agrees, and pulls out the second chair.

He keeps a chair for me. That detail undoes me more than any grand gesture ever could. A plain wooden chair, dragged up four thousand feet, positioned at his desk like I might sit down and grade his footnotes. He knows I can't really sit anymore — flesh is negotiable for me now, weight is a rumor — but he also knows that I can, if I concentrate, if I want to badly enough. And some nights I want to badly enough. Tonight is one of them. I gather myself into solidity, pull the chair out, and sit, and the wood creaks under me like it's greeting an old friend, and Theo pretends this isn't a miracle because he's learned that pretending things aren't miracles is how I survive them.

"What are you writing?" I ask.

"The record," he says. "Not prophecy. Just... what happened. What it cost. Who paid."

"And?"

"And I'm stuck on chapter thirty-eight." He taps the page. "There's supposed to be a scene here about the warden adjusting to her new existence, and honestly the source material is being difficult."

"I hear she's impossible."

"She is," he says warmly, "but she lets me stay anyway," and I throw a snowball at him, which is entirely within my rights and also the closest thing to blushing I've managed in months.

He tells me the news from below, because news is his love language. Pip has started school in the new village — actual lessons, taught by a defrocked order archivist who fled down from the north with a cartload of banned books, which Pip is stealing back one volume at a time on principle. Ivo is training a new generation of callers, teaching them the old craft without the old fear, and his first student is Osla's daughter, a solemn eight-year-old who apparently summoned a respectable thunderclap over the wash line last week and then burst into tears and apologized to the laundry. Vess's heir — young Briar, half-hollow-court, half-gilded, the first of both bloods born since the covenant broke — has formally reopened the Hollow Court, and the cavern city glows again at night, real light, not fading light. Theo visited last month and says the hollow-eyed ones are filling in, color returning to them like ink soaking back into paper.

"And the gilded court?" I ask, because someone always asks, and it may as well be me.

"Briar stripped them to ceremony," Theo says. "No army, no gate, no keystones. They keep the solstice festivals and complain about it beautifully. Apparently they've discovered gardening." He pauses. "They're surprisingly good at it."

Everyone is surprising me lately. It's disorienting. For twenty-nine years I planned my life around the assumption that power corrupts and beauty lies and help always costs more than coin. Now the gilded court grows roses, the order kneels in mud, and the most dangerous creature I know is teaching children to whistle up thunder responsibly. Either the world improved or I did, and I suspect the answer is both, and I suspect the mechanism was the same in each case: somebody stopped running.

"There's one more thing," Theo says, and his voice changes — the scholar's voice, careful, the voice he uses when he's been sitting on information waiting for the right hour. He reaches into his desk and sets something on the wood between us.

A letter. Sealed with wax I recognize instantly — storm-gray, struck with the sigil of the caller order — but the hand that wrote the address is not the order's formal script. It's looser. Older. Familiar in a way that makes my chest tighten before my mind catches up.

"That's Serapha's handwriting," I say. "Her real one. Not the official hand."

"Yes," Theo says. "It arrived at the village three days ago. Addressed to me, marked private, with instructions to pass it to you only when..." He hesitates.

"When what?"

"When I judged you were settled enough to receive bad news gently." His mouth twists. "I argued with the instruction for three days. Then I remembered who you are, and decided you'd rather have it badly than late."

I look at the letter a long time. The fire pops. Outside, the wind I半 own moves through the pines with a sound like breathing, and the mountain beneath me waits — patiently, granite-patient — to feel what I feel next.

"You could have waited longer," I say finally.

"I know."

"You wanted to read it yourself."

"I *am* going to read it myself," Theo says, "the moment you tell me what it says," and that, more than the chair or the stove or the four-thousand-foot climb, is the reason I chose him. He never pretends to carry my burdens for me. He just refuses to let me carry them alone.

I break the seal. The wax crumbles like old frost.

Serapha's true handwriting, the one she uses when no one is grading her, slants across the page in tight, urgent lines — and it is not an apology. We've had the apology. This is something else. My eyes catch only fragments before my heart starts pounding hard enough that the fire gutters, because I am the fire, technically, and it responds to me whether I like it or not:

*Ulyana — I found it in the sealed archives while cataloguing what remains. You need to see it. Your mother's name was not struck from the register. It was moved. There is a record of where she went after you were born, and Ulyana — the location is not in either realm. I've enclosed the coordinates in caller cipher. Come down from the mountain. Some cages, it turns out, have doors.*

I look up. Theo is watching me with the expression he wears when a text contradicts itself and he's thrilled about it.

"Well?" he says.

Outside my window — all my windows, every sky I own — the first stars are coming out over Thornfell, and far to the north, past the edge of my borders, past the edge of everything I am bound to, there is a direction I haven't looked in thirty years.

The wind changes.
