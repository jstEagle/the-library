## 40. Storm-Caller's Blessing

A year ago today, I gave away my life and got the sky in return. Most people would call that a bad trade. I've made worse.

I wake at dawn the way I always do now—not in a bed, exactly, though I keep one at the spire for appearances' sake, a narrow cot with a wool blanket Dagny dyed an aggressive shade of red because she said everything up here looked like it had given up. I wake because the mountain wakes me, a slow pressure behind my ribs like a hand uncurling. Thornfell doesn't sleep the way flesh sleeps. It dreams in stone-time, long and slow, and when its dreams brush mine I feel the whole range at once: the roots gripping the world's deep bones, the snowfields holding their breath, the little hotsprings steaming like kettles in hidden valleys where nothing human has ever walked.

And I feel the weather coming before it comes. That's the part I still haven't gotten used to. The old order taught us to *call* storms—yank them out of the sky like pulling teeth. Nobody taught us to *listen* to them first. Turns out the storm was always trying to talk. We just kept shouting over it.

Today there's a rain sitting offshore of my attention, patient as a cat at a mousehole, waiting for permission. I sit on the spire's broken crown with my legs dangling over four hundred feet of nothing and let the morning happen around me. Below, the world is doing something it hasn't done in living memory: it's been ordinary for twelve straight months. Crops came in. Children were born. Nobody burned anything down in the name of quarantine. I'm told this is what peace feels like from the inside. I'd only ever seen it from exile's window, pressed against the glass.

The anniversary. Theo reminded me last night, which is funny, because he's the one who keeps all our records now and you'd think the calendar would be his department anyway. He said it the way he says everything important—sideways, into his tea, pretending to adjust his spectacles. "A year since you became weather," he said. "We should mark it."

"Mark it how?" I asked. "Cake? A plaque? A modest festival with mediocre fiddle music?"

"All three," he said, "and also I've finished the book."

I didn't ask which book. There's only one book left to finish. Not prophecy—he burned the prophecy pages himself, ceremonially, on Vess's cairn, and Pip danced on the ashes until Ivo pretended to scold him and then danced too. No. This one is just a record. He calls it *the truest kind of boring*. Names, dates, what happened, who paid for it. He says history needs a clerk more than it needs another oracle. I think he's writing his grandmother back into the world one honest page at a time, and I have never loved him more than when he describes his own work as dull.

But that's tonight. Down in the valley they'll be lighting lamps and arguing about whether the fiddler is any good (he isn't; we love him). Right now it's just me and the mountain and the rain waiting offshore like a guest too polite to knock.

I decide to let it in early.

* * *

Sending rain over Marrowgate is not the thunderous act it would have been in my old life. No black clouds boiling up out of a clear sky, no order-bells ringing alarms, no one running for cover. The new craft is quieter. I open something in myself—a door I used to keep barred—and the weather walks through it like family coming home without bothering to announce itself.

The cloud arrives soft. Gray-white, low, unhurried. It settles over Marrowgate's fields the way a blanket settles over a sleeping child, and then it begins to rain: a fine, warm, deliberate rain, the kind farmers call *money from the sky* and never say loudly enough for the tax collector to hear.

I feel it land. That's the strange grace of what I am now—I feel every drop, not individually, that would be madness, but the way you feel a crowd's murmur. Ten thousand small arrivals on barley leaves and bean flowers and the new shingles of houses built by hands that remembered how to build. The earth drinks. I can feel the earth drinking, a long slow satisfaction moving through the soil like warmth through cold feet.

And then—this is the part I will never tell anyone but you, whoever finds Theo's book in some future library, reading about a warden who supposedly transcended all human pettiness—then I feel them look up.

Marrowgate looks up when it rains now. All of them. They can't help it. The first few times, last spring, they stood in their doorways with their faces wet and their eyes searching the peaks, and old Dagny—ferrywoman, scar-carrier, woman of approximately zero sentiment—actually raised her fist at the sky in salute, which nearly made me drop the whole cloud system into the fish pond. Now it's habit. Rain from a clear horizon means her. Means *she's thinking of us.* Means the warden remembers Marrowgate exists, remembers what it cost, remembers the day the order came with torches and called murder "quarantine."

They're looking up right now. I can feel it like sunlight on the back of my neck. Somewhere down there Pip has abandoned whatever chore Ivo assigned him to stand in the middle of a field with his arms out, mouth open, catching rain, because that child has never once in his life respected the concept of indoors during weather. Somewhere down there Dagny is pretending not to smile at him. Somewhere down there Ivo is doing his morning exercises and letting the rain hit his bare shoulders like a penance he's decided he's served enough of.

And somewhere down there is the house they built me.

I didn't ask for it. I want to be very clear about that, because the Ulyana of two years ago—the one who counted exits and kept her name off ledgers and slept facing doors—would have found a house with her name carved over it unbearable. But they built it anyway, at the edge of the village where the fields meet the tree line, a squat stone thing with a wide porch facing the mountains. "So you have somewhere to land," Theo said, "when being everywhere gets heavy." The door's never locked. Half the village uses the porch to shelter from storms, which delights me beyond all reason: my storms providing shelter. There's a sentence my old mentors would have needed a lie-down after hearing.

I don't live in it, not really. I live in the wind. But sometimes at dusk I walk down out of the sky—which still feels like cheating, honestly, stepping off a cloud onto a hillside path—and I sit on that porch, and someone always brings me tea without being asked, as if I'm expected, as if I'm *due*, and I drink it while the mountain hums under my feet like a cat.

Belonging. I spent thirty years believing it was a place you got into by passing a test. The order tested me and cast me out. The lowlands would have tested me and burned me. The fae courts tested me and tried to own me. It took becoming the border itself to understand that belonging was never a door with a guard. It's a porch with the latch worn smooth from use. You don't earn it. You just stop locking yourself out.

* * *

Midmorning, the dead come to visit.

Not ghosts—the mountain doesn't traffic in ghosts, or if it does, it keeps them private—but memories, which the high air serves up unbidden when it likes you. Vess's memory lives in the eastern crags, where she shattered the gilded gate and spent the last of herself doing it. On clear mornings I can feel the shape of her there, faint as a watermark: pride, mostly, and a queen's stubborn satisfaction. Her court is rekindled now under her heir, a sharp-eyed young fae named Liriope who visits me quarterly to argue about water rights with the seriousness of someone negotiating a war treaty, because for the hollow-born, water rights *are* a war treaty. She brings news. Last visit she told me the gilded court's survivors have petitioned for representation on the new covenant council, and I laughed so hard I started a localized hailstorm over the Goat's Teeth, which Liriope declared "an acceptable opening position."

Vess, wherever she is in the stone, approves. I know because the crag hums a particular note when I think of her, and I've decided to interpret that note as approval rather than indigestion. A warden is allowed her comforts.

My mother's memory lives deeper. In the root-chambers, where the covenant was first sung, the stone holds her like amber holds a fly—not trapped, preserved. I go down there maybe once a month and sit among the glowing veins of the mountain's heart and try to remember her face without the official record interfering. The order's version of my mother was a cautionary tale: fae seductress, traitor, the reason for every rule. The truth, as far as I can piece it together from the mountain's witness and Serapha's confession and Theo's research, is smaller and sadder and better: a noblewoman of the hollow court who fell in love with a human cartographer, saw the covenant failing years before anyone believed her, and hid her daughter in plain sight among the storm-callers because it was the only place no fae court and no human authority would think to look.

She was wrong. They looked. But she bought me twenty years of ordinary, and ordinary turned out to be enough to build a self on. When the mountain finally claimed me, I had something to give besides desperation. I had a whole invisible life's worth of stubbornness, hoarded like coins.

Mother, if any part of you is listening in the deep stone: your containment strategy failed spectacularly, and I could not be more grateful.

* * *

Afternoon brings visitors, because of course it does. Word travels faster uphill than downhill; the village knows it's the anniversary, and the village has opinions.

Dagny arrives first, rowing her ferry up the flooded gorge road that the new rains keep full—her idea, her design, a waterway connecting half the outcast settlements along Thornfell's foot, which means the gruffest woman alive now runs a thriving passenger service and complains about it daily in the tone of a woman who has never been happier. She climbs the switchbacks with a basket. She always brings a basket. Today it holds honey bread, a jar of the fierce plum spirit Ivo distills, and a rolled parchment she slaps against my knee like a summons.

"Ferry charter renewal," she says. "You signed last year's drunk. I need it witnessed proper."

"You forged my signature?"

"I improved it. Your handwriting's terrible." She sits beside me on the spire's crown, which terrifies visitors and bores her completely, and we watch the rain I sent this morning finishing its rounds over the far fields. For a while neither of us talks. With Dagny, silence is a shared meal, not an absence.

"They named the bridge after me," she says eventually. The Sorrow Bridge, rebuilt wider, renamed. "I hate it. Bridges should be named for crossings, not for bleeding on them."

"What would you call it?"

"The Second Crossing. Because that's what we did." She shrugs. "First crossing was surviving. Second was coming back."

I sign her parchment with the best handwriting I can manage, which remains terrible, and she witnesses it with a flourish, and we drink a toast of Ivo's plum spirit that strips paint and grief with equal efficiency.

Pip arrives next, obviously, because Pip can climb anything including sense, and he materializes over the crag edge soaked to the skin and delighted about it, having walked straight through my rain instead of around it like a sensible person. He's grown a hand-span in the year. His fae-sight has sharpened instead of fading, the healers say, because he uses it—maps the roads, leads travelers, teaches other children to see the shimmer between here and elsewhere. The gilded court's fall opened hundreds of sealed ways, and someone had to learn them all, and that someone is nine years old and considers it a perfectly reasonable career.

"I brought you something," he announces, and produces from inside his coat a river stone with a hole through it, threaded on twine. "It's a seeing-stone. Liriope says if you look through it at somebody you see their true self."

"That's nonsense," I say.

"Probably," he agrees cheerfully, looping it around my neck. "But you should have one anyway."

He looks at me through it, squints, and reports: "Yep. Still just you. Good." Then he's gone over the edge again, whooping, leaving me wearing a child's charm and absolutely refusing to take it off.

Theo comes last, as he always does, because he times his ascent to arrive at golden hour and pretends it's coincidence. He climbs the last stretch carrying his book bag and two cups of tea from the porch kettle, breathless, glasses fogged, hair doing something structurally ambitious. Two years ago he couldn't find the pass to Marrowgate with a map and a guide. Now he takes the Goat's Teeth switchbacks like he was born to them, which is what Thornfell does to people—it either kills them or makes them locals.

"A year," he says, sitting close, shoulder warm against mine. "You've been the mountain for a year, and you've only flattened one village."

"It was a very annoying village."

"And started, by my count, forty-one intentional rainfalls, six mercy-fogs, one hailstorm over a diplomatic delegation—"

"They deserved it."

"—and zero wars." He turns to look at me, and behind the fogged lenses his eyes are as steady as they were the day he put a century-old prophecy in front of me and watched my whole life catch fire. "The old covenant lasted three hundred years and ended in winter. Yours is one year old and nobody's frozen, nobody's burned, and both realms are sending delegates to a council that includes a defected storm-caller, a ferrywoman, and a nine-year-old mapmaker. I've checked the archives, Ulyana. You're not maintaining the old pact. You're writing a better one."

"I had help."

"You had a family," he corrects. "There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

We drink our tea while the sun goes long and gold across the valleys, and below us the lamps of Marrowgate begin to light one by one, and then the outlying farms, and then the ferry settlements strung along the waterway like beads on a bright thread. Music drifts up, thin and off-key. The fiddler, bless him, still can't play.

Tonight they'll feast. Tonight Theo will read aloud from his book—all the names, all the costs, all the ordinary days that add up to a miracle nobody planned. Tonight I'll walk down out of the sky and take my seat on a bench between a ferrywoman and a scholar, with a feral child asleep against my side and a defected soldier arguing politics with a fae envoy, and no one will test me, and no one will bar the door, and I will count nothing—no coins, no exits, no hours remaining—because for the first time in my life there is nothing I am waiting to escape.

The rain finishes its work as dark falls. I feel the last drops land, feel Marrowgate's fields sighing with it, feel the village looking up even into darkness, trusting the sky now, of all things. Trusting the sky. If you'd told the girl who fled the order's towers with frostbite and a false name that one day she'd be the thing people trusted most in the world, she'd have laughed herself sick.

I lift one hand, and over Marrowgate, just above the festival lights, I send a single ribbon of lightning—not a weapon, just a bright seam of silver stitched across the dark, a signature, a wave hello. The cheering carries all the way up the mountain.

Happy anniversary, I tell them, in the only voice I have left.

Below, my family looks up.
