## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The wards were quiet again this morning.

Astrid Pemberton knelt at the base of the eastern ward-stone with a brush of goat hair and a bowl of lime-wash, and she did the thing she had done every seventh day since she was nine years old: she waited for the stone to answer. She pressed her palm flat against its mossy flank and felt for the hum, that low sweet vibration like a struck bell heard through water, the sound that had meant *safe* for as long as there had been a valley at all.

Nothing.

Not silence, exactly. The valley never gave you clean silence. There was wind in the birches, the creek worrying its stones below the terrace, old Master Corwin's cough drifting down from the scriptorium window like a question nobody wanted to answer. But from the stone — nothing. It sat under her hand like any other rock, patient and dumb, and Astrid painted the sigil-rings anyway, because that was what the guild did. You mended the wards whether they sang or not. You kept the forms. The forms were all that was left.

"You're pressing too hard," said Sela behind her. "You'll wear the grooves smooth before your grandchildren are born."

"I don't have grandchildren."

"Then you'll wear them smooth out of spite." Sela was seventy and shaped like a question mark now, but her hands were still steady on the mixing bowl, and her eyes — pale, watery, unfooled — went where Astrid's eyes had already gone: down the line of ward-stones marching along the eastern ridge, eleven of them, twelve when the twelfth still stood, each one washed and ringed and silent as a graveyard.

"When did you last hear it?" Astrid asked. She didn't say *it* carelessly. In this valley you learned to speak around the thing you feared, the way you spoke around a wound.

Sela considered lying. Astrid watched her decide not to bother. "Winter before last," she said. "The northern stone. Just once, just at dusk. I told myself I'd imagined it." She scraped her brush along the bowl's rim. "I've stopped telling myself things, girl. It's less work."

Astrid laughed, because Sela expected her to, and because if she didn't laugh she would have to look down-valley at the terraces — half of them fallow now, the orchards gone woody and neglected, the mill wheel turning for no one since the Hollis family walked out through the pass three springs ago, waving until the fog took them. Forty-one souls in the Veiled Valley. She counted them sometimes the way you count coins you know won't last the year. Forty-one. When she was born there had been four hundred.

*But we are safe,* the elders said, every season, at every convocation, in voices worn smooth as river stones from saying it. *We are the one place his reach cannot touch. We are the price worth paying.*

The price. That was the phrase they used, and Astrid had never liked it, because prices implied a buyer, and buyers implied a bargain, and bargains implied someone on the other side of the table who wanted something. The elders never said who. They said *the founding accord*, and they touched iron, and they changed the subject.

She rinsed her brush in the creek and climbed back up through the terraces toward the hall, past the beehives that should have been split in spring and hadn't been, past the empty schoolhouse where no child had recited the warding canticles in six years. A kestrel hung in the wind above the ridge, holding itself still against all that moving air, and Astrid envied it with a sudden fierce ache — something that could choose its sky.

* * *

Master Corwin did not remember her name, but he remembered the ritual better than anyone living.

She found him in the scriptorium, which smelled of dust and tallow and the ghost of ink, sitting in his chair by the south window with a copy-book open on his knees though his eyes had long since stopped reading anything. He was ninety-one years old. He had been the guild's First Warden for thirty years, and the last person alive to have heard the stones sing in full chorus, and now he lived mostly in a country forty years gone, in a valley full of voices.

"Astrid," she said, kneeling by the chair, taking his cold hand. "It's Astrid. Pemberton."

His eyes came around slowly, like a man turning a heavy cart. "Pemberton," he repeated, tasting it. "Your mother was the one who kept bees."

"Your mother kept bees," he corrected himself, gently, before she could answer. Then, with sudden sharp clarity, the way lightning finds a crack: "The third ring is wrong. On the eastern stones. Whoever cut the third ring inverted the flow-glyph. It's been wrong since before your grandmother drew breath."

Astrid went very still. "Show me."

He couldn't, of course. The clarity drained away as quickly as it came, and he was looking at her with mild polite bafflement, an old man interrupted at his rest. "You'll want the master key," he said. "Ask whoever has it." He patted her hand. "Good girl. What was your name?"

"Astrid."

"Astrid." He nodded, satisfied, and turned back to the window, to the fog that stood at the valley's mouth like a held breath. "They'll come asking about the accord soon," he said, to no one, or to the window, or to forty years ago. "They always do, before the end. And then we'll have to tell them what we paid."

Her spine went cold. "Master. Paid for what?"

He smiled at the fog, sweet and vague and terrible. "Everything costs," he said. "That's the first lesson, and the last one, and the only one that matters. Everything costs, and the bill always comes due when the payer is too comfortable to rise from the chair."

She stayed with him until the light shifted. She changed the cloth on his forehead, and fed him the broth he took without noticing, and twice more he surfaced into that terrible lucidity — once to tell her the count of stones was sacred and must never fall below twelve (there were eleven), and once to grip her wrist with a strength that startled her and say, "Whatever knocks at our gate, girl — whatever shape it wears — remember that we invited it. Whatever comes, we asked it here."

Then he was gone again, murmuring to someone long dead about ink rations, and Astrid sat on the floor beside his chair and felt the quiet dread settle over her like the valley fog itself: soft, total, and impossible to push away.

Because here was the truth she carried everywhere and said to no one, the truth she tested each morning against the silent stones and found still true, still growing truer: *the valley is not dying. The valley is dead. We are the ones who haven't noticed yet.* The wards didn't hum because there was nothing left in them to sing. The magic-free sanctuary wasn't a sanctuary; it was an emptiness, and they were keeping its forms the way mourners keep a grave — tidying the grass, replacing the flowers, speaking softly to someone who can no longer hear.

Forty-one people, loving a corpse.

And the worst part — the part that made her press her palms flat against the floorboards as if checking for a pulse in the building itself — the worst part was that she loved it anyway. Loved the crooked lanes and the terraced apples, loved the smell of lime-wash and lamp oil, loved Sela's spite and Corwin's lightning-clarity and the fog standing faithful guard at the valley's mouth. Love, she thought, was just grief that hadn't finished its arithmetic yet. And hers was nowhere near done counting.

* * *

At dusk the guild gathered in the round hall for the evening observance, forty-one souls in a room built for four hundred, their candles making small brave circles of light against walls whose murals had faded to suggestion. Elder Maren led the canticle in her cracked voice, and they answered, and the words rose and fell and touched nothing, and Astrid sang anyway, watching the candle flames bend toward the door every time the wind came down off the ridge, as if even fire was listening for something.

Afterward there was barley stew and the low ritual gossip of a shrinking community: whose roof leaked, whose knees ached, whether the Hollises had sent word from the lowlands (they had not). Tam Voss would have called it a tomb with good lighting. Astrid caught herself thinking it and was ashamed, and then wasn't.

Elder Maren caught her arm as the hall emptied. "You were with Corwin today."

"For the tending, yes."

"He spoke to you." It wasn't quite a question. Maren's face was a map of careful decisions, every route marked and every road closed. "About the accord."

"He said—" Astrid hesitated. Lying to Maren was possible; she'd done it. But the dread wanted company. "He said everything costs. He said we'd have to tell what we paid. He said—" *we invited it,* she almost said, and didn't, because Maren's grip had tightened on her arm like a clamp.

"The master wanders," Maren said. "Pay his words no mind. The accord is settled law. It holds."

"It doesn't hold, Elder. It sits there. Like the stones. Like us." Astrid heard her own voice and was surprised by its steadiness. "When did you last hear a ward hum?"

Maren released her arm. For one naked moment something moved behind her eyes — not fear exactly. Recognition. The look of a woman hearing a debt read aloud that she had hoped to die before admitting. "Mind your duties, Pemberton," she said, and walked out into the dark, upright as a fence post, and Astrid understood that she had just been given an answer, and that the answer was *yes*, and that Maren had known longer than she had, maybe everyone had known longer than she had, and they had all agreed together, silently, generation after generation, to keep washing the stones and singing the canticles and calling the silence safety.

She banked the hall's fire herself. She checked the gate bar out of habit — thrown fast, as it had been every night of her life; no one entered the Veiled Valley uninvited, that was the whole point, that was the accord, whatever the accord was.

The fog lay thick past the palisade, thick as wool, thick as a held tongue. Somewhere beyond it, in the world, there were wars. The usurper Veylan was swallowing the free cities one by one, they said, though news reached the valley slow and secondhand and already old. Out there, magic moved like weather. In here, nothing moved at all.

Astrid stood a moment in the courtyard, looking up at stars blurred to smudges by the veil-fog, and let herself feel it one more time, honestly, completely: the love and the dread braided so tight she couldn't find the seam. Tomorrow she would wash the stones again. Tomorrow the stones would not answer. Tomorrow—

A knock sounded at the gate.

Three blows, spaced evenly, unhurried. Not frantic, not weak. Patient. The kind of knock a thing gives when it knows the door will open.

Astrid's hand went to the wall, to the cold stone above the gatepost where the smallest ward-sigil sat, the one carved for travelers' warning — and felt, beneath her fingers, faint as a memory of a memory, the stone tremble.

Every candle in the courtyard guttered toward the gate at once.

Behind her, up in the dark of the scriptorium, Master Corwin began — clearly, terribly, in a voice forty years strong — to laugh.
