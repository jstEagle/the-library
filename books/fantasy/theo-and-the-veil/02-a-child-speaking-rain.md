## 2. A Child Speaking Rain

The knock came at dusk, and by morning the whole valley knew about it.

But that night, Astrid was the only one awake to hear it — three small knocks, spaced wrong, like someone who had never knocked before learning the shape of it from watching doors.

She went down through the guildhouse alone, past the dormitory where Master Corwin slept with his window open even in autumn, past the scriptorium where the ward-ledgers sat in their dust. The lamps along the stair burned low. Everything in the Veiled Valley burned low, these days. Even the fires seemed to be conserving themselves for something.

She unbarred the gate and looked down at a child.

A girl, six years old at most, caked in mud from her bare feet to the crown of her head, as if she had been poured out of the earth rather than arrived upon it. Her hair was black and matted with burrs. Her eyes were the problem. They were too calm. Children who had walked as far as this one clearly had were supposed to cry, or shake, or at least look hungry in a way that asked you for something. This girl simply looked at Astrid the way you look at a door you have been expecting.

"Where is your mother?" Astrid asked.

The girl opened her mouth and spoke.

It was not any language Astrid knew. It was not the lowland trade-tongue, not the mountain dialects, not the clipped syllables of Veylan's occupied coast. The sounds came out round and wet and layered, like rain falling into a deep well, each syllable landing on the one before it. And underneath the words — there was no other way Astrid could describe it, though she would try for weeks afterward — underneath the words was *weight*. The air in the gatehouse thickened. The lamp flame leaned toward the child like a plant toward sun.

Astrid's skin prickled. That was impossible. Nothing pricked your skin in the valley. Magic did not reach here; that was the entire point of the place, the founding promise, the reason her grandmother had walked three hundred miles with a baby on her back. The valley was silence. The valley was the one room in the world where the fire never touched.

And yet the lamp flame leaned.

"Come inside," Astrid heard herself say.

* * *

By full dark she had fed the girl bread softened in broth, washed the worst of the mud away, and discovered two things: the child had a name, or at least a sound she answered to — something like *Senn*, but spoken with a click at the back of the throat that Astrid could not reproduce — and the oldest ward-stone in the valley had begun to glow.

That was the part that would break the guild open.

The heartstone stood in the undercroft, a slab of grey granite older than the guildhouse built around it, older than the town, older than any record of who had set it there. For four hundred years it had done nothing. It was the valley's anchor, the first thing the founder had raised, and when he raised it, the story went, all magic within its radius had gone quiet forever. Generations of apprentices had pressed their palms against it hoping for a flicker and received cold stone. Astrid had done it herself at nine years old, and again at twelve, and once more at nineteen, stubbornly, before accepting what every child in the valley learned: the stone was dead, and so, here, was everything like it.

Now it glowed a faint, patient blue, like a coal remembering fire.

Astrid stood in the undercroft doorway with the girl's hand in hers and felt her own heartbeat go strange. The light pulsed — slowly, arrhythmically, nothing like a flame — and each pulse matched something in the child's breathing. When Senn breathed in, the stone brightened. When she exhaled, it dimmed. As if they shared a lung.

"No," Astrid said aloud, to no one. "No, that's not how any of this works."

The girl looked up at her and said a single syllable. It landed in the stone room like a dropped bell. The glow flared, held, and settled.

Astrid did not sleep. She sat on the undercroft steps with a blanket around the sleeping child and watched the stone breathe, and somewhere in the smallest hours she made herself admit the thing she had been circling since the gate: the wards were answering her. Not failing. Not breaking. *Answering.* The valley's long silence was not being disturbed by this child. It was being *spoken to* — in a language old enough that the stones remembered it, even if no living person did.

At dawn she sent the kitchen boy for the elders.

* * *

They gathered in the council room by eight, seven of them, grey-robed and grim, and Astrid told them everything while the girl sat on her knee in the corner eating an apple with the solemn concentration of someone who has recently learned food can run out.

Elder Maren spoke first, as she always did. "You brought it inside the walls."

"I brought *her* inside the walls."

"It glowed the heartstone." Maren's voice had the flatness she used for pronouncements. "Four hundred years of silence, broken in one night, and your response was to feed it an apple."

"She's six years old."

"She is a breach," said Elder Hollis from the far end. He was the youngest of them, sixty perhaps, and had spent his whole life guarding a wall that had never been attacked, and it had made him a man who saw attack everywhere. "We don't know what she is. We don't know what followed her. The veil has held since the founding because we have kept it clean. One crack, and everything outside comes pouring in."

"Nothing came pouring in," Astrid said. "She came walking up the road alone, half-starved, and the first thing the valley did was give her light."

"The valley doesn't *do* things—"

"Then explain the stone."

Silence. They all looked at each other, the way people do when the explanation they share privately is too frightening to say in company. Astrid watched it move through them and understood, with a sinking feeling, that whatever they were all thinking, none of them would say it while the child could hear.

"Take her to the guest room," Maren said finally. "Bar the door from outside. We'll decide by noon."

"You're locking up a six-year-old."

"We are securing an anomaly. There's a difference, Astrid, and the fact that you can't see it is why you'll never sit on this council."

Astrid carried the girl upstairs herself, and Senn went without protest, which was somehow worse than crying would have been. At the threshold the child turned and said one of her wet, layered sentences, and patted Astrid's hand twice, deliberately, the way you'd steady someone else.

The elders voted at noon. Astrid was not permitted in the chamber, so she stood in the corridor and listened through the door, which everyone pretended not to know she was doing.

Seven voices. The vote was five to turn the child out at first light — taken beyond the ward-line, given provisions, left to whatever fate had delivered her. Two dissents: old Corwin, whose mind wandered more every month but who had voted, clearly and firmly, *she stays*; and Elder Ilsa, who said only, "We were all once children the road gave us," which was true of exactly no one in the room, and which no one argued with, because arguing with Ilsa was like arguing with weather.

Five to two. Done. Astrid stood in the corridor with her hand flat against the door and felt something in her chest settle into a shape she recognized from her grandmother's stories: the shape of a person deciding, alone, that the institution is wrong.

* * *

She went to the guest room before first light.

She had meant to argue. She had prepared arguments all evening — the stone, the language, the simple arithmetic that turning a child into the winter hills was murder with extra steps. But standing in the doorway, looking at Senn asleep on the narrow bed with the blanket pulled to her chin, Astrid found all her arguments had evaporated, leaving only the plain fact underneath them.

So instead of speaking, she picked the child up.

Senn woke instantly, silently, arms coming around Astrid's neck — trained, Astrid thought bitterly, by whatever had happened to her, trained to be lifted and carried and moved quickly. Astrid carried her down the back stairs, through the kitchen, out the postern door, and did not stop until they reached the undercroft.

The heartstone still glowed. Dimmer now, patient, waiting.

"I'm not going to let them put you outside the wall," Astrid told her. "I don't care what you are. I don't care what the vote says. Nobody gets thrown back into the dark because they frighten old men."

The girl studied her face with those too-calm eyes. Then she reached out and pressed her small palm flat against Astrid's sternum, over her heart, and spoke — a longer phrase this time, several of those rain-into-a-well syllables, gentle, almost conversational.

The heartstone flared white.

Every lamp in the undercroft lit at once. Every ward-line stitched through the guildhouse — the ones that hadn't hummed in decades, the ones Astrid had spent her life ritually tending like a nurse at a deathbed — sang. A low, harmonic note filled the stone room, rose through the floors, and Astrid felt it in her teeth, in her ribs, in the place where the child's hand rested.

And then, just as suddenly, it stopped. The stone dimmed to its patient blue. The lamps steadied. The silence of the valley closed back over everything like water.

But Astrid had heard it. The whole valley had heard it. In the morning there would be no vote that mattered, no elder with the authority to un-ring that bell. Whatever Senn was, she had just announced herself to every ward-stone from the guildhouse to the mountain pass.

The girl withdrew her hand and looked up at Astrid, and for the first time since the gate, her composure cracked — just slightly, just enough — and Astrid saw underneath it a six-year-old who was very tired and very far from home.

Above them, boots on the stairs. Voices. The elders, awake, alarmed, coming down to find out what their dead valley had just said.

Astrid tightened her arms around the child and turned to face the door.

Because Corwin was among the voices — and he was shouting words Astrid had never heard him use, words that didn't sound like alarm at all.

He sounded like a man recognizing a language he'd sworn he'd forgotten.
