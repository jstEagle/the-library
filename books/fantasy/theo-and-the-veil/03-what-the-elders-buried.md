## 3. What the Elders Buried

Astrid had not slept. The child slept instead, curled in the guild's oldest guest cot like a comma in a sentence no one dared finish, and Astrid sat across the room with a candle guttering and the girl's words written out phonetically on three sheets of paper that refused to make sense.

*Sel-eth varun. Sel-eth varun kei.*

She had copied them until her hand cramped. The syllables repeated — *sel-eth* above all, appearing eleven times in the first hour alone — and they were not nonsense. Nonsense wandered. These circled. They returned to the same shapes the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth.

At some point past midnight she gave up on paper and went down to the scriptorium, where the guild kept its dead languages the way other houses kept wine: in racks, gathering dust, mostly forgotten. She pulled grammars by candlelight. Old Imperial. First Tongue. The trade-pidgin of the drowned coast. Nothing.

It was Master Corwin who found her at dawn, standing amid an avalanche of opened folios, and it was Corwin who went very still when he saw the phonetic sheets.

"Say it," he said. "Out loud."

"Sel-eth varun kei."

The old man sat down. Not lowered himself — sat, abruptly, as if his knees had been repossessed. His face had gone the color of the morning fog outside.

"Astrid," he said. "Where did you hear that?"

"The child. It's what she says. Over and over." She held out the sheets. "You know it."

"I knew it once." He took the pages but didn't read them; he looked through them, at something sixty years gone. "When I was a novice, there was a text in the restricted case. A single page. We were told it was a forgery, a joke some founder's-era apprentice had played. But I was young and arrogant, and I copied it out before they locked it away." His voice dropped. "It was a prayer, Astrid. Or half of one. And it began *sel-eth varun* — *I return what was taken.*"

The candle flame leaned in a draft that wasn't there.

"It's not a language," Corwin said. "It's older than language. It's what magic spoke before we taught it to speak ours."

* * *

Corwin had buried a great many things in his ninety-one years, and he had told himself each burial was mercy. Mercy for the guild. Mercy for the valley. Mercy, most of all, for the dead man whose name they still carved into the lintel of every guildhouse door: **Aldous Vane, First Warden, Who Gave All**.

He had believed the inscription his whole life. That was the trick of a good burial — you attended the funeral so many times you forgot you'd dug the grave yourself.

Now he climbed the narrow stair to the archive vault with Astrid two steps behind him and the child — Wren, Astrid had named her, for the way she cocked her head at sounds — carried on Astrid's hip, watching everything with those unchildlike eyes. The elders had voted at sunrise to turn the girl out. Corwin had not spoken against it. Cowardice, he thought now, was just burial with better ceremony.

The vault door took three keys and a word of blood, and inside the air was cold and mineral, the air of deep places. Corwin lit the lamps one by one until the shelves emerged from the dark like a shipwreck surfacing.

"The founding records are here," he said. "What remains of them. There was a fire in Vane's last year — officially an accident. Unofficially—" He stopped himself. "Unofficially, nothing. We don't say unofficially anything in this valley. That is rather the point of it."

He found the ledger where he remembered it, wrapped in oilcloth behind the genealogies. The leather was stiff as bark. He opened it with the care owed to something that might bite.

Astrid read over his shoulder. He heard her breathing change before she said anything.

"This isn't a warding contract."

"No."

"This is a bill of sale."

"Yes."

The script was Vane's own — Corwin recognized the sharp downstrokes, the impatient crosses — though the hand grew shakier toward the end, as if written by a man bleeding slowly onto the page. He let Astrid read aloud, because he could not.

"*To the Court Beneath, which keeps its own counsel and its own names:*" she read. "*I, Aldous Vane, being the last mage of the old orders and the only one the armies have not yet burned, do sell into your keeping that portion of myself which cannot be cut out by any blade — my soul entire, its memory, its fire, and its name. In exchange:*"

She stopped.

"In exchange," Corwin prompted quietly.

"In exchange the valley is veiled. No magic enters. No magic finds us. Not his armies' sorceries, not plague-craft, not scrying, not anything." Her voice had gone flat, the voice of someone reading a map of their own house while it burns. "He bought us safety with himself. That's the story. That's the whole story we're built on."

"Keep reading."

"*And should my soul ever seek to come home,*" Astrid read, slower now, "*let it be known that I did not sell it gladly, and that I hid this key where only the desperate would look. The Court does not give refunds. But it honors debts. Bring the vessel to the gate beneath the drowned cathedral, speak my name over it, and the debt comes due.*"

Silence filled the vault like water filling a hull.

"The vessel," Astrid said.

Corwin turned. The child was looking at the ledger. Not at the pictures — there were none — at the words themselves, upside down and backwards, in a script two thousand years dead, and her lips were moving, shaping the syllables silently, fluently, the way a scholar reads.

Or the way an owner reads.

"Oh," Corwin said, and the sound came out of him like something breaking loose after decades under snow. "Oh, Aldous. You clever, damned, unforgivable man."

"What?" Astrid's arm had tightened around the girl without her seeming to notice. "Master, what?"

"He couldn't get his soul back himself. A sold soul can't bargain — that's the whole cruelty of it, the Court holds your name and a nameless thing can't knock on doors. So he left instructions. He left a route home." Corwin's eyes stung; he realized distantly that he was close to weeping for the first time since his wife died. "The soul has been circling for centuries, Astrid. Looking for a way back in. And it found one. It found a small body with no words of its own to get in the way."

"You're saying the child is—"

"I'm saying the child is a door. Or a letter. Or a lantern carried a very long way in the dark." He reached out — slowly, asking permission — and touched Wren's hair. The girl looked up at him, and for one unguarded moment her expression was not six years old at all. It was tired. It was ancient. It was homesick.

"*Sel-eth varun,*" she said clearly. *I return what was taken.*

Then she yawned, laid her head against Astrid's shoulder, and was asleep between one breath and the next, like any child, like nothing ancient at all, and somehow that was worse.

* * *

Astrid stood in the vault a long time after Corwin sank into his chair, and the horror came in layers, the way frost comes.

First layer: the guild was founded on a purchase. Every ritual she'd ever performed, every ward she'd mended, every hymn to Aldous Vane Who Gave All — all of it was maintenance on a transaction. They weren't a sanctuary. They were collateral.

Second layer: the valley's protection wasn't a shield. It was a lien. Somewhere beneath the mountain, a thing held the deed to their peace, and things that hold deeds eventually call them due.

Third layer, the deepest, the one that made her sit down on the cold floor with the sleeping child in her lap: the elders had known. Not these elders, maybe, not recently — but someone, in some generation, had read this ledger and wrapped it back in oilcloth and chosen the story over the truth, because the story kept the valley calm and the truth would make them ask questions like *what else was traded?* and *when does the buyer come to collect interest?*

Her whole life, Astrid had loved this valley the way you love a house that creaks — trusting the creaks were just age. Now every creak sounded like footsteps overhead.

Wren stirred. Astrid looked down at the small face and felt the fourth layer arrive, quiet and total:

The child hadn't come to them by accident. Souls that circle for centuries do not arrive anywhere by accident. Something had spent the girl like coin — or the girl had volunteered, or been made volunteer, or simply been the nearest open door — and either way, Astrid was holding the founding sin of her people in her arms, and it weighed about forty pounds and drooled slightly in its sleep.

"We can't turn her out," she said.

"The elders voted."

"The elders voted out of fear, which is the one vote they always count wrong." She rose, child and all. "Corwin. The ledger says the debt comes due at the gate beneath the drowned cathedral. Where is that?"

The old man didn't answer at once. He was looking at the last page of the ledger, at something appended in a different hand — newer, hurried, ink browned with age.

"Corwin?"

"There's more," he said. "Written after the fire. After Vane." He read it aloud, and his voice shook on the second line. "*The Court will not wait forever. When the vessel comes, the veil thins. And the usurper's spies will feel it thinning, as surely as sharks feel blood. Whoever carries that child out of this valley must go quickly, and armed, and must never travel the lowland roads.*"

Astrid felt the floor tilt beneath her, metaphorically and then — as the wards along the valley's rim chose that exact moment to shiver, faintly, for the first time in thirty years — perhaps not metaphorically at all.

"The veil is already thinning," she said.

Above them, muffled by stone and silence, the guild bell began to ring. Not the ritual hour. The alarm.

Someone was at the gate.

Not a child this time.
