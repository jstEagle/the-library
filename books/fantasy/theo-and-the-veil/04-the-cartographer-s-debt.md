## 4. The Cartographer's Debt

They caught him at the third mile-marker, where the pilgrim road bent around the shoulder of the mountain and the veil thinned to nothing. Tam Voss heard the wardens coming long before he saw them — the valley's guards moved like men who had forgotten what they were guarding for, all shuffling boots and muttered complaints — and he considered, briefly, running.

He didn't. Running would have confirmed everything.

So when they found him he was sitting on a flat stone with his sketchbook open on his knee, pencil moving in small, precise strokes, drawing exactly what they expected him to be drawing: the approaches to the Veiled Valley, rendered in the clean shorthand of the royal cartographic corps. Contour lines. Sightlines. The two passes a company of soldiers could move through, and the one only goats and fools would attempt.

"Put the book down," said the senior warden, a woman with grey in her beard and a crossbow that looked older than Tam.

"It's just paper," Tam said, not putting it down.

"So is a confession. Put it down."

He put it down. They tied his wrists anyway, which he supposed was fair, and marched him down into the valley through orchards going gold with autumn and fields that lay fallow in a way that struck him as wrong before he understood why: there were no scythes leaning on fences, no dogs barking, no children anywhere. The valley was tidy the way a tomb is tidy. Everything kept. Nothing living loud enough to be heard.

*This place is dying,* he thought, and thought it with a strange tenderness, because he had been sent to find out how fast.

* * *

The guild hall sat at the valley's heart like a stone dropped in milk — squat, grey, older than the town around it. They took him through its doors into a chamber where eleven elders sat in a crescent behind a table scarred by centuries of elbows, and they stood him in the middle of the floor like a specimen pinned for study.

Tam had faced worse rooms than this. He had faced Veylan's privy council, once, across a map table, explaining why the deep court could not be reached from the surface without drowning half a legion. He had lied beautifully that day. He wondered if he still had it in him.

"You are Tam Voss," said the eldest elder, a man whose voice sounded like a hinge that needed oiling. "Formerly of the Royal Cartographers. Currently of nowhere."

"That's generous," Tam said. "Currently of your floor."

"You were observed sketching the valley's approaches."

"I was observed finishing them. I started three days ago."

Murmurs. The grey-bearded warden shifted behind him. The eldest raised a hand and the room went quiet the way rooms do around old men who have spent decades being obeyed.

"Why does Veylan want maps of us?" the eldest asked. "We have nothing. We are nothing. That is the entire point of this place."

And there it was — the question, the real one, under all the others. Tam let the silence sit for a moment, because silence made liars out of everyone, including the people asking questions.

"Because nothing doesn't stay nothing," he said. "Because your valley is the last hole in his map, and Veylan hates holes. Because every free city between here and the coast has fallen or will fall inside two years, and when they're gone, the usurper is going to stand at the mouth of this valley and wonder why his armies can't walk in. And he'll want to know why. And someone will tell him about me, sitting on a rock with a pencil."

He turned his head, slowly, so he could see the whole crescent of faces. "You're not hidden. You're *unlisted*. There's a difference, and the difference has an expiration date."

"The wards—" began a woman at the far end.

"Are quiet. I know. I've walked your boundary. Whatever used to hum here doesn't anymore." He shrugged against the ropes, which was difficult and hurt his shoulders, and he did it anyway because looking helpless while saying dangerous things was a trick he'd learned young. "I'm not here to threaten you. I'm here because I need what you have and you need what I know, and I'd rather we discussed that like civilized people instead of me rotting in one of your cellars until Veylan's outriders arrive to collect me."

"And what do you need?" the eldest said.

"Asylum. A roof. A year, maybe less." He met the old man's eyes. "And in exchange I'll give you the thing no one in this room can buy: a way through Veylan's lines, for anyone in this valley who wants to leave before the leaving gets impossible."

More murmurs, sharper now. Somewhere behind him a door opened and closed. He didn't turn to look. He was watching the eldest, and the eldest was watching him back with the flat, exhausted expression of a man who had spent his whole life believing a bargain made generations ago would hold forever, and was only now beginning to do the arithmetic.

"We'll deliberate," the eldest said finally. "Take him—"

"No."

The voice came from the doorway Tam hadn't turned toward, and something about it — the refusal in it, the sheer unembarrassed stubbornness — made him turn after all.

She was younger than the elders, mid-twenties maybe, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of face that looked like it had been arguing with someone recently and expected to argue again within the hour. She walked into the middle of the crescent like she owned the floor, stopped next to Tam, and folded her arms.

"Astrid," the eldest said, in the tone of a man addressing weather. "You are not on the council."

"No. Which means I'm the only person here who isn't afraid of voting wrong." She looked at Tam, up and down, quick and assessing, the way she might read a page. "Say the part again. About the deep court."

Tam blinked. "I didn't say anything about the deep court."

"You didn't have to. You said 'the thing no one in this room can buy.' Passage through an army is worth money. Passage worth *bargaining* over is passage through somewhere else entirely." Her eyes narrowed. "You mapped it. Didn't you? The court beneath the mountain."

The room went very still. Even the hinges-voice elder had stopped breathing.

Tam considered lying. He was good at lying; it had been his trade as much as cartography ever was. But there was something about this woman standing an arm's length away with her arms folded and her ink-stained fingers tapping once against her elbow — something that suggested she had already priced his lie and found it cheap.

"Yes," he said. "I mapped it."

"Then you're the most valuable man to set foot in this valley in fifty years," Astrid said, "and you're also the most dangerous, and I don't trust either version of you yet. But I believe both." She turned to the elders. "Deliberate all night if you like. But when you vote, remember that turning him out and turning him loose are the same act, and one of them hands Veylan his maps."

"They'll hang him," someone said. "If he's caught—"

"He won't be caught. Not with me reading over his shoulder." She glanced sideways at Tam. "Assuming his maps are actually good."

A laugh escaped Tam before he could stop it — short, rusty, surprised out of him. It had been a long time since anyone had talked to him like he was a problem rather than a prisoner. "My maps," he said, "are the reason Veylan owns three cities instead of five. Draw your own conclusions."

"Conclusions drawn," Astrid said dryly. "Elders?"

The deliberation lasted four hours. Tam spent them in a cellar that smelled of apples and dust, and spent most of those hours annoyed at himself for how often he thought about the woman with the ink stains — replaying the exact angle of her chin when she'd said *I don't trust either version of you yet*, hearing again that dry note under her voice that wasn't quite mockery and wasn't quite hope.

Near midnight the door opened and she came down the stairs with a lantern, two cups of something steaming, and his confiscated sketchbook under her arm.

"They voted," she said.

"And?"

"Seven to four. You get a roof until spring, supervised. I'm your supervision." She set the cups down, cut his ropes with a knife that had seen honest work, and handed him the sketchbook. "I read all of it."

"I assumed you had."

"Your contour work is very good." She said it grudgingly, like admitting to a debt. "Your shading is showing off."

"My shading is *information*. Light tells you depth; depth tells you where water goes; water tells you where people live and where they'll run." He flexed his freed fingers, watching her over the rim of the cup she'd given him. The drink was cider, sharp and cold, better than anything he'd had in a year. "What did the other seven say about me?"

"That you're probably a spy. That you're definitely a risk. And that Astrid Pemberton has a talent for collecting trouble." She sat down on an apple crate across from him, close enough that the lantern light pooled between them. "They're right about the last one."

"Am I trouble?"

"You're a man who sold maps to Veylan and then ran. That's not a biography, that's a confession waiting for a crime." She sipped her own cup, watching him steadily. "So confess. Why did you really leave his service? Men like you don't run over nothing."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. He could give her the safe answer — the one about conscience, the one about the burning farms he'd watched from a hillside while his survey marks turned into artillery ranges. All true. All insufficient.

Or he could give her the true answer, which was heavier.

"I drew a map of a place I shouldn't have been able to draw," he said quietly. "Two years ago. Before the war got bad, when the court beneath the mountain still traded openly with the crown. They brought me down — blindfolded through the flooded cathedral, then the blindfold off, and there it was. Halls of purchased souls. Centuries of them. Bought and shelved like books." His hand had gone tight around the cup; he loosened it deliberately. "And in the deepest hall, on the oldest shelf, there was a space. Empty. Recently emptied. Like something had been taken out."

He looked up. "I mapped every hall except that one. When I got back to the surface I falsified my own survey — left the court off the royal charts entirely — and I ran. Because whatever bought souls down there had sold one back. Or lost one. Or had one *stolen*, and stolen things come home, and I did not want to be holding the map when it arrived."

Silence. The lantern flame leaned and straightened.

Astrid set down her cup. For the first time since he'd met her, she looked uncertain — not of him, he realized, but of something much older than him.

"Three days ago," she said slowly, "a child knocked on our gate. Six years old. Mute, except for words no living tongue should know. And the oldest ward-stone in this valley lit up for the first time in decades when she touched it."

Tam felt the cellar go cold around him, felt the map assemble itself in his head — the empty shelf, the stolen soul, the dying wards, the girl speaking rain — every line connecting, every contour closing into a shape he wished to God it didn't make.

"Astrid," he said. "That child isn't visiting your valley."

"She's coming home," Astrid whispered.

"And whatever sold her," Tam said, rising, already reaching for his sketchbook, "is going to want her back. Show me that ward-stone. Now. Tonight. Because if my map of the deep court is right—"

He spread the pages open across the apple crates, and the drowned cathedral rose between them in graphite and shadow, and neither of them slept again that night.
