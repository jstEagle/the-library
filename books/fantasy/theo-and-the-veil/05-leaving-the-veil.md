## 5. Leaving the Veil

They left before dawn, when the valley's mist lay thick as wool and the ward-stones along the gate had not yet begun their slow daily dimming. Astrid carried a satchel of bread, a skin of water, and the guild's oldest map — Corwin's map, drawn in ink that had faded to the color of old tea, showing the mountain pass as a single uncertain line trailing off into blank paper.

*Here be nothing,* the old cartographers used to write at the edges of their maps. Astrid had always thought it a confession of laziness. Now she understood it was a confession of fear.

The child walked between them, one small hand in Tam's, one in hers. She had refused to give her name — perhaps she didn't have one, or perhaps it was still tangled up with the older soul sleeping inside her — so they'd taken to calling her Wren, after the bird she'd mimicked on her first morning in the valley. She wore Astrid's spare cloak pinned twice at the throat and she did not look back at the guildhall.

Astrid did. Once. Just once, at the first bend of the pass, where the path switchbacked up through the pines and the whole valley opened below like a cupped hand: the terraced fields gone gold with autumn, the slate roofs, the thin thread of smoke from the morning fires, and above it all the Veil itself — that shimmer in the air you stopped seeing after a lifetime, the way you stopped seeing your own breath in summer. The place magic could not reach. The place that had kept them safe by keeping them empty.

Master Aldous had told her once, when she was young enough to still ask questions: *The Veil doesn't protect us from magic, child. It protects magic from us.* She hadn't understood then. She was beginning to.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said quietly beside her. "Where you stare like the valley might dissolve if you blink."

"Maybe it will."

"It won't." He shifted his map case higher on his shoulder. In the grey pre-dawn light his face was all angles and shadow, the bruise from his capture finally fading to yellow along his jaw. "Places are stubborn. They outlast us. That's the whole trick of them."

"That's supposed to comfort me?"

"No," he said. "It's supposed to be true. Comfort is extra."

Wren tugged her hand, pointing up the pass. A marmot whistled somewhere in the scree, and the girl made the sound back — perfect, uncanny, the mimicry that wasn't really mimicry but something older, something that tasted of words before words. Then she looked up at Astrid with those eyes too grave for six years old and said, in the rain-language that no living tongue should know: three soft syllables, rising like water.

"I know," Astrid said, though she didn't. "I know."

They climbed.

* * *

The pass took the whole day. It wound up through pine shadow and across scree fields where every footfall rang, past a shrine so old its god's name had weathered off the stone, until late afternoon brought them to the saddle of the ridge — and to the edge of the Veil.

You could see it, if you knew how to look. Not a wall, not a curtain. More like the air itself forgot something on one side and remembered it on the other. Astrid had crossed it only once before, as a girl, gathering herbs from the high meadows, and she remembered the crossing as nothing at all — a step, a shiver, a headache.

She braced for the same now.

Tam stopped first. He'd been quiet for an hour, checking and rechecking the angle of the sun against his pocket compass, and now he said, "Smoke."

She smelled it a moment later — woodsmoke, but wrong, too acrid, laced with something oily. Below the northern shoulder of the ridge, down where the foothills flattened into farmland, a brown smear rose against the sky. Two smears. Three.

"The lowland farms," Tam said. "Outriders. Veylan doesn't garrison territory he means to make an example of." His voice was flat in the way she'd learned meant he hated what he was saying. "He burns the harvest, drives the survivors toward the free cities, and lets hunger do his sieging for him. I've mapped the aftermath four times."

Wren made a sound Astrid had never heard from her — low, keening, almost the whistle of wind through a broken window. The girl stared down at the smoke with an expression that was not a child's expression at all.

"We can't help them," Astrid said. It came out harsher than she intended.

"No," Tam agreed. "We can't. And stopping to try gets us killed and gets her delivered to Veylan's priests, which is the only outcome worse than burning farms." He met her eyes. "I need you to hate me a little for agreeing with me, because if you don't, one of us is lying."

"I hate you a fair amount already. We'll manage."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth — not quite a smile, but the place a smile would live if it ever visited. He turned back to the shimmer in the air ahead, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent a life inside it, and said, "Crossing now. Whatever happens on the other side — whatever comes back into the world, or into *us* — we keep walking. Agreed?"

Astrid looked at Wren. Wren looked back, and nodded once, solemn as an executioner.

They stepped through together.

* * *

The world ended and began again in the space of a heartbeat.

There was no transition. No gradual flooding, no rising tide. One step Astrid was a woman walking on a mountainside; the next step she was drowning in a river of light.

It came in through her skin. Through her eyes, her breath, the roots of her teeth. Every sense she owned tore open at once — she could *hear* the mountain, a vast slow hum beneath the stone like a struck bell that had been ringing for ten thousand years and never finished; she could see the wind, actually see it, silver ribbons braiding through the pines; she could feel the small green insistence of the moss under her boots pushing up through rock, patient as scripture. Magic. Not the dead, silent absence she'd breathed her whole life, but the living thing itself, everywhere, everything, pressing against her like an ocean against a swimmer's ribs.

She went to her knees. Dimly she heard Tam shouting her name, heard Wren's voice rise in the rain-language, urgent and bright — and beneath both voices, underneath everything, she felt the power *notice her.*

That was the part no one had warned her about. Not Aldous with his riddles, not Corwin's buried journals, none of it. The books spoke of casting spells as though magic were a tool, a hammer waiting for a hand. Nothing said that the hammer was awake. Nothing said that when you finally stood outside the Veil and the ocean rushed in, the ocean would turn toward you — curious, hungry, *delighted* — the way a pack turns toward fresh blood.

Because it knew her. That was the obscene truth of it, arriving whole in her skull between one heartbeat and the next. The valley hadn't just blocked magic from reaching her. It had been holding a door shut on something that lived inside her, something that had been pressing against that door since she was born, growing stronger in the dark the way roots grow stronger against stone. Twenty-nine years of pressure. And now the door was open.

The power poured in and asked, in a voice that had no sound but that she understood perfectly: *more.*

Astrid gasped — and the gasp became light. Silver spilled from her open mouth, from her fingertips, threading up through the scree around her in luminous filigree, and where it touched, the mountain *answered*. Stones lifted off the ground. Not thrown — *invited*, hovering in a slow orbit around her kneeling body, each one humming its own low note. The wind bent toward her like a courtier. Somewhere below, the smoke of the burning farms shuddered and swirled into shapes she almost recognized.

It felt magnificent.

That was the horror. It felt like coming home to a house she'd never known she owned, every door flung open, every lamp lit. It felt like being seen completely for the first time in her life. And some deep, cold, rational sliver of her — the same sliver that had watched the elders vote to cast out a child, the same sliver that had read Corwin's confession and understood how a good man sells a soul — that sliver whispered: *this is how it starts. This is exactly how it started for him.*

The founder. Who had once knelt on some mountainside just like this one, flooded and shining, hearing the same voice say *more*, and who had followed it down and down until he stood in the court beneath the mountain trading souls like coin.

The stones fell. The light guttered. Astrid surfaced from the river gasping, on her hands and knees among the scree, ordinary again, mortal, shaking so hard her teeth chattered. Tam crouched beside her, one hand hovering near her shoulder without touching, his face pale.

"Astrid. Hey. Look at me. What happened?"

"I—" Her voice came out scraped raw. "The valley. It wasn't protecting us *from* magic." She swallowed. "It was protecting magic from *us*. From me. From— whatever this is." She looked down at her own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "Tam, there's something in me that liked that. Something that wanted more. Right away. Before I even knew what I was asking for."

He was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "When they had me in Veylan's cells, after the third day, I would have given him anything. My mother's name. My friends' names. I *did* give my friends' names." His voice didn't waver; she noticed he made it not waver. "Wanting isn't the same as doing. The wanting doesn't stop. You just decide, over and over, every day, what you won't trade for it."

Wren pressed close against Astrid's side, small and warm and smelling of woodsmoke and pine. She looked up and said something in the rain-language — gentle this time, almost tender — and touched Astrid's sternum with one finger, right over the heart, right over the place the power had poured through.

Then she pointed down the mountainside. Past the burning farms, past the smoking fields, to where the land folded down into darkness — toward the mountain that was not this mountain. The mountain beneath whose roots the deep court waited, patient as debt.

And Astrid, still trembling with borrowed fire, understood the child perfectly for the first time without needing to translate:

*It knows you're coming. It's been waiting for someone like you.*

Below them, the last light caught the smoke of the burning farms, and far to the south — where the free cities stood behind walls that had never yet been tested — a bell began to ring.

Not one bell. Many. Alarum, alarum, rolling across the darkening lowlands like a tide going out.

Veylan was moving.

Tam unrolled his map against a boulder with steady hands, and his finger found the pass down into occupied country, the long road through the burned lands to the drowned cathedral and the doors beneath. "We walk tonight," he said. "While the outriders are busy with the farms."

Astrid looked at her hands one last time. Then she closed them into fists, and followed him down.
