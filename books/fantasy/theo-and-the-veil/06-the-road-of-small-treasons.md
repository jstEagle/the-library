## 6. The Road of Small Treasons

Tam Voss had drawn maps of places he'd never been and maps of places he wished he could unsee. The road south from the Veiled Valley was the second kind.

He walked at the front because it was his map, his route, his fault if they died on it. Behind him came Astrid with her hood up and her jaw set, and behind her the child — Wren, Astrid called her, though the name was a placeholder, a kindness — riding on Astrid's back with her small hands knotted in the wool like a climber on a cliff face.

The pass had taken them two days. The lowlands took something worse.

From the ridge they could see the farms burning, five plumes of black standing up out of the green like fingers counting. Veylan's outriders worked methodically, west to east, torching granaries and leaving the houses — the houses became garrisons. Tam had mapped that policy too, two years ago, in a clean office in the capital, before he understood what he was drawing.

"They're ahead of schedule," he said.

"You said that yesterday," Astrid said.

"It's still true."

"Then say something useful instead." She didn't look at him when she said it, but she adjusted her stride so her shoulder brushed his, and that was its own kind of sentence. He'd been cataloguing those sentences for days. The way she handed him water without being asked. The way she didn't ask about the scars on his wrists, which meant she'd noticed them.

"The mill at Harrow's Bend," he said. "There's a cellar under the millstone. A resistance cell used to run messages through there. If any of them survived—"

"If?" Astrid said. "You don't know?"

"I know what I did to them," Tam said. "I don't know what happened after."

She was quiet for ten paces. Then: "We go anyway."

"Astrid—"

"You keep saying my name like it's an argument you're losing."

* * *

The mill at Harrow's Bend still stood, which surprised him. The wheel had been burned but the stone shell remained, and someone had hung a rag on the doorpost — grey, dyed with ash — that meant *shelter, but count your enemies first*. Old signal. Older than the war.

Tam made Astrid wait with Wren in the alder thicket while he went to the door alone, palms out, the way you approached people whose friends you had sold.

The door opened on a woman with a crossbow held low and steady, and Tam's stomach dropped through the floor of the world, because he knew her. Sera Iln. Thirty-one years old when he'd last seen her, across an interrogation table, her hands already broken. She'd held out for nine hours. He knew that number because he'd been the one keeping time.

"Voss," she said. No surprise in it. That was worst of all — that she'd imagined this meeting often enough to be finished with it.

"Sera."

"I heard you ran. I heard you were in the capital when it fell and you walked out through the sewers with the royal seal stamped on your backside." Her eyes went past him to the treeline. "Who's with you?"

"A cartographer's apprentice and a child."

"Liar," Sera said comfortably. "You were never an apprentice. You drew the approaches to every safehouse between here and the coast. My sister died in one of them." She said it the way you'd report weather. "Come inside before the patrol comes back. Not because I forgive you. Because the child shouldn't watch this."

Inside, the mill smelled of ash and turnips. Three others sat among the sacks — a boy of maybe sixteen, an old man missing an arm, a heavyset woman who looked at Tam the way farmers look at crows. Sera took his map case off his shoulder without asking and spread the charts on the floor, reading them upside down, fast, professional.

"You've marked the patrols," she said. "Fresh ink."

"I've been watching them for six days."

"Why?"

"Because I'm taking these two through the lines, and if they die on my route I'll have killed them twice."

Sera looked up at him then, really looked, and whatever she found made her mouth twist. "You always were honest at the wrong moments. It's why they broke you so fast."

"They broke me slow," Tam said. "Nine days. But yes."

The door scraped and Astrid came in with Wren on her hip, and the room changed temperature. The old man stood. The boy put his hand on a knife he clearly didn't know how to use.

"This is Astrid," Tam said. "Of the Veiled Valley. And the child is—"

"We know what the child is," Sera said quietly.

Tam turned. "What do you mean, we know?"

"Word moves faster than armies, Voss. There are stories in every market from here to the Salt Coast. A girl who speaks the old tongue. A ward-stone that lit after forty years dark." Sera's eyes were flat and bright at once. "The usurper has a price on her. Not dead. *Brought whole.* Whatever she's carrying, Veylan wants it."

Astrid's arms tightened around Wren, just slightly. Tam saw the child watching Sera with an expression no six-year-old should own — patient, ancient, faintly sad, like a grandmother at a funeral she'd attended many times.

Then Wren spoke.

Not the rain-language. Common speech, rough and accented, a man's cadence in a girl's small mouth:

*"Harrow's Bend. Good stone. We milled here in the famine year and took no coin from the desperate."*

The room went absolutely still.

"That's—" the old man started, and stopped. His face had gone the color of tallow. "That's Aldous. That's how Aldous talked. Founder Aldous, peace on him, that's his voice."

Wren blinked, and the ancient look drained out of her, and she was a tired child again, pressing her face into Astrid's neck. Astrid met Tam's eyes over the small head, and he read the question there — *did you hear it too* — and nodded once.

"She does that," Astrid said to the room. "It's not hers. Something old is surfacing in her, and we're taking her somewhere it can be... set down properly."

"Somewhere," Sera repeated. "Say the word."

Tam looked at the map. At the mountain range he'd charted from memory, the drowned cathedral valley, the entrance he'd drawn once as a young man with a royal commission and no idea what lived below. Every instinct said lie. Every instinct he'd learned under torture said lying was just a slower confession.

"The court beneath the mountain," he said.

Silence. Then the heavyset woman laughed, short and ugly. "The soul-market. You're walking into the soul-market."

"He's not walking anywhere," Sera said. "He's staying here. The other two can take the eastern ravine — I'll give them a guide who isn't a traitor."

"No," Astrid said.

Sera raised an eyebrow.

"He goes where we go," Astrid said. "That's the arrangement. You can hate him — gods know you've earned it. But you don't get to decide what I owe him or what he owes me. That ledger is ours."

Something moved through Tam's chest that he had no name for and didn't trust himself to examine. Nine days on a table and he hadn't cried. This nearly did it.

Sera studied Astrid for a long moment. "You love him," she said. Not a question. An accusation, almost.

"I'm deciding whether to," Astrid said. "So far the evidence is mixed."

The boy snorted despite himself. Even the old man's mouth twitched. Sera held out a moment longer, then let out a breath that seemed to carry years with it.

"Dawn patrol comes through at first light. You leave tonight, east ravine, and you're gone before they see smoke from your fire." She rolled up Tam's maps and shoved them against his chest. "I'm giving you these back, Voss, because the child matters more than my anger. Don't mistake it for anything else."

"I won't," Tam said. "Thank you."

"Don't thank me either." She turned away, then paused. "My sister's name was Dell. If you're going to carry what you did, carry her name with it. Names are heavier than guilt. They should be."

* * *

They left at moonrise, single file along the ravine floor, Sera's guide — the boy, whose name was Petch and who worshipped Sera with the pure ferocity of the young — leading them by paths only smugglers and goats remembered.

At midnight they halted in a hollow where a spring came up through moss, and drank, and ate hard bread. Wren slept against Astrid's side. Above them the stars burned in their cold formations, and Tam found himself tracing constellations out of habit, fixing their positions, because a mapmaker's mind never fully stops.

"She named her sister," Astrid said quietly. "Dell. Will you carry it?"

"I already am." He looked at his hands. "I carried all of them. That's the thing nobody tells you about breaking — afterward you remember everything perfectly. The torture gave me a flawless memory. Every name, every safehouse, every face. I can draw Dell Iln from life right now. Freckle by freckle."

Astrid was quiet. Then she shifted closer, until her shoulder rested against his, deliberate as everything she did.

"In the valley," she said, "we have a saying. The wards don't hold because they're strong. They hold because they're *mended*, every day, by people who stay." She turned her head, and in the starlight her eyes were very clear. "I watched you walk into that mill knowing what waited. I watched you tell the truth when a lie would've been easier. I'm not mended, Tam. Neither are you. But I'm telling you I intend to stay."

He wanted to answer and found his throat had closed. So instead he reached over and touched her hand, and she let it stay there, and above them the stars wheeled on, indifferent and beautiful.

Wren stirred in her sleep and spoke again — soft, half-dreaming, that borrowed voice like wind through a ruined hall:

*"The door beneath the water remembers my name. It will ask for yours. Do not give it freely. Nothing down there is given. Everything is bought."*

Her eyes stayed shut. Her breathing stayed even. Astrid and Tam looked at each other across the sleeping child, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that wasn't already written on both their faces.

Below them, far to the south, past the burning farms and the occupied roads, the mountain waited — and somewhere under its roots, behind a door beneath the water, something patient turned over in its long sleep and began, perhaps, to dream of them coming.
