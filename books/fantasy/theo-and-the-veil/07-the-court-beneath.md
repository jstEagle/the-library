## 7. The Court Beneath

The drowned cathedral announced itself by sound before sight: a slow drip echoing up through the rock like a heart that had forgotten how to beat quickly.

We came down the last of the stair — three hundred steps, Tam had counted them aloud to keep himself sane, and I had counted with him until the numbers stopped meaning anything. The child rode on my hip, heavier than she should have been, as if the soul inside her were settling downward, drawn toward whatever waited below. Her breath was warm against my neck. She hadn't spoken since the gate of the deep road, but her fingers kept moving against my collarbone, tracing patterns. Old patterns. The same ones the ward-stone in the valley had glowed with when it answered her.

"She knows where we are," Tam said quietly behind me.

"Then one of us does," I said. "Because I'm walking blind."

He unrolled the map he'd made from memory — the map that had gotten him dragged before the guild in chains, the map that had bought us this passage. In the light of my hand-lamp, the ink showed a cathedral rendered in careful cross-section: nave, transept, choir, and beneath the choir floor, a chamber marked not with a name but with a single symbol Tam refused to explain. A circle with a line through it. A coin, seen edge-on.

"The water comes in at spring melt," he said. "It's been flooding for two hundred years. The court never drained it."

"Why not?"

"Because," Tam said, "it likes the way things look underwater."

I thought he was being dramatic. Then we reached the nave.

* * *

The cathedral stood flooded to our waists, black water still and warm as bathwater, and everything in it had been arranged. That was the horror of it — not ruin but curation. Pews floated in perfect rows, aligned to a compass point that no longer existed. Chandeliers hung from chains that had been lengthened, deliberately, so they dangled just above the surface, their candles long melted into stalactites of wax. And everywhere, standing in the water or seated in the pews or kneeling at the drowned altar, were people.

They did not breathe. They did not move. But they were not statues — their skin gave when my lamp passed over it, soft as sleeping flesh, and their faces held expressions. Contentment, mostly. A few wore something closer to relief. One woman near the transept arch was smiling so gently that I found myself smiling back before I caught myself and stumbled.

"Don't touch them," Tam said. "And don't meet their eyes too long."

"What are they?"

"Purchased." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *drowned* or *buried*. "Every one of them traded something once. Their souls sit in the court's keeping, and these—" he gestured at the congregation of the still "—are what's left holding the receipt. Bodies without the part that argues."

The child lifted her head from my shoulder. She looked out over the water, over all those patient faces, and made a small sound — not grief exactly. Recognition.

"You knew some of them," I said. It wasn't a question. The founder's soul had been down here before, in another body, another century. Maybe in this very water.

She pressed her palm flat against my cheek. The syllables that came out of her were low and resonant, older than kingdoms, and for the first time I understood one without trying: *home*. Or possibly *prison*. In that language, I was beginning to suspect, the words shared a root.

We waded toward the choir.

* * *

The water shallowed as we climbed the chancel steps, and then ended altogether at a threshold of black stone polished mirror-bright, so that we seemed to step out of the world and onto its reflection. Beyond it stretched a hall that no architect had built — the walls were ribbed like the inside of a shell, veined with pale light that pulsed slowly, patiently, in time with nothing. And along both sides, set into niches carved from the living rock, burned lights.

Not lamps. Lights. Each one a different color, a different rhythm, and each one — I understood this the way you understand a thing you've always known — was a soul. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Some flickered like guttering candles; some burned steady and cold as stars. They hummed, faintly, a chord with no resolution, and the chord was the sound I'd been hearing since the stairs. The heartbeat of the place.

At the far end, on a throne grown rather than built from the same ribbed stone, sat the thing wearing Aldric Vane's face.

I had seen the founder only in woodcuts and the cracked fresco in the guildhall — a stern man with a cartographer's brow and a mage's tired eyes. The thing on the throne had those features exactly, rendered perfectly, and that was how I knew it was false. Nothing real is rendered perfectly. It wore his face the way a signet wears a seal: an instrument of authentication, nothing more.

"Astrid Pemberton," it said. Its voice was kind. That was worse than anything else. "Daughter of the valley my bargain built. Come at last to settle accounts."

Tam dropped to one knee beside me — habit, I realized, the reflex of a man who had once held rank here — and I felt him trembling. Not with fear. With memory.

"You mapped this hall," the thing said to him, almost fondly. "You drew my lights beautifully, Tam Voss. Such care with the shading. And then you sold the drawing to men who wanted to steal from me." It turned back to me before Tam could answer. "But you. You I've waited for. Sixty years since your master's master came sniffing at my door. You took longer than most."

"We're not here to trade," I said. My voice sounded smaller than I wanted it to. "We're here to take back what was stolen. The soul in this child. Aldric Vane's soul. It belongs to the guild."

"It belongs," the thing said, "to whoever holds it. That is what ownership means. Your founder understood this better than anyone — he taught it to me, in fact, across a table much like this one. He wanted his valley safe. I wanted his soul. We were both satisfied." It rose from the throne, and the movement was wrong — too smooth, like water pouring upward — and suddenly it stood before me, close enough that I could see there were no pupils in Aldric Vane's borrowed eyes, only depth. "But let us not be adversaries. You have come so far. Let me offer you courtesy first."

It extended one hand, palm up, and above it one of the wall-lights detached itself and drifted down — a small gold flame, steady, warm.

"Anything you name," it said. "That is not rhetoric, Astrid Pemberton. It is terms. Name your guild restored to its full strength. Name your valley's wards singing again. Name Veylan's armies turned to salt. Name your dead master young again, his mind whole, remembering your name. All of it is possible. All of it is *cheap*, compared to what I hold. You have only to say the word, and put your signature to it."

"And the price," I said.

"Your soul, eventually. But eventually is such a generous word. Your founder had forty years of glory before I collected. You could have sixty. Seventy. Long enough to save everyone you love, and die honored, and never feel the bill come due." The gold flame pulsed between us. "Everyone in this hall said the same thing at first, you know. *Never.* And then they named a smaller thing. Just one city saved. Just one person healed. Just one winter made kind. The small bargains are how I prefer to work. So much easier to sign."

I looked at the flame.

And gods forgive me, I wanted it.

Not the grand version — I told myself I didn't want the grand version. I wanted the small one. Master Corwin, lucid again, saying *Astrid* without hunting for it. The ward-stones humming. The valley green past any drought. It would cost me nothing today. It would cost me nothing for decades. And the guild — the dying, frightened, dwindling guild that had voted to turn a six-year-old child out into the dark — would live.

"That's how it starts," Tam said softly. He wasn't looking at me. He was looking at the niches, at the thousands of lights. "That's the exact sentence I said. *It costs me nothing today.*"

"Your friend is wise," the thing agreed pleasantly. "Wisdom has never once stopped anyone."

The flame drifted closer. Warmth touched my face like a hand.

* * *

Later — in the days after, when I tried to reconstruct the moment — I would remember that the hall went very quiet, the humming chord dimming as if the whole court leaned in to listen. What I remembered most clearly, though, was weight: a small hand closing around two of my fingers.

The child had slid down from my hip without my noticing. She stood beside me in her ruined shoes, mud still dried in the creases of her knees from a road she'd walked alone, and she was not looking at the flame. She was looking at me. And her eyes — brown, ordinary, six years old — held something ancient and unbearably sad, because she had seen this exact moment before. From the other side of the table. In a man's body, with a man's voice, saying *never* and meaning *yes*.

She squeezed my fingers. Once.

And the wanting broke.

Not all at once — it peeled away like ice off a branch, leaving something raw and cold underneath, and in that cold I saw clearly for the first time: every light in those walls had been someone who loved someone enough to sign. That was the whole business model. Love, leveraged. The thing on the throne had never taken a single soul by force in all its centuries. It had only ever needed to wait for people who cared.

"No," I said.

The word fell into the silence like a stone into the flooded nave.

"I don't name a price. I make a demand. The soul returns to the child. Freely. And in exchange—" my mouth went dry, because I already knew, I had known since the stair, since the valley, maybe since the day I was born inside a sanctuary paid for with a man's soul "—in exchange, I'll hear your terms. Whatever they are. But I bargain for her, not for me."

The thing wearing Aldric Vane's face smiled, and the smile was genuinely delighted, which was the most frightening thing yet.

"Oh," it said, "you are going to be *interesting*."

Behind us, at the mouth of the hall, the thousand lights flared all at once — and somewhere far above, muffled by two hundred feet of mountain, came a sound none of us expected.

Boots. Many boots. Descending the stair.

Veylan had found the door.
