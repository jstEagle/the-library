## 8. The Price of Names

I had drawn maps of places that did not want to be found. I had charted the drowned cathedral, the stair that went down past the roots of the mountain, the hall where a thousand lights burned without flame. I had even, in my arrogance, sketched the thing at the end of it — though I had never seen its face until now, and my sketch had been a coward's guess.

None of it prepared me for the sound of boots on the stair.

The entity heard them too. It tilted Aldric Vane's head — the founder's face, worn like a favorite coat — and listened with an expression I can only call *appetite*.

"Veylan's people," it said. "How industrious. He has wanted this court for years, you know. He sent three expeditions before yours. The first two are still here." It gestured, gently, at the lights. "Would you like to meet them?"

"Don't," Astrid said.

"I wasn't going to introduce you." The smile again. "I was making a point about time. You have very little of it, and I have all there is. So let us conclude our business."

Our business. As if we'd come to buy cloth.

The child — Wren, though her name had been a hundred other names in a hundred other mouths — stood between us with her small hand still wrapped in Astrid's fingers. Since we'd entered the hall she had been speaking. Not the rain-language, not fragments anymore. Whole sentences, in a voice pitched low and old, the voice of a man two centuries dead.

"The bargain was made," the dead man said through her mouth. "A soul for a sanctuary. The debt stands."

"Yes," the entity said fondly. "It does. And debts, little vessel, are what I collect."

"Then collect it," Tam said.

It took me a moment to realize the voice was mine.

Astrid turned to me fast — I saw it happen, saw the understanding arrive in her face half a second after the words left mine — but I was already talking, because if I stopped talking I would start thinking, and thinking would kill the only courage I had ever managed.

"You want a soul. The guild sold one two hundred years ago and never paid. Fine. Take mine instead of hers. Instead of *hers*." I nodded at Astrid. "One cartographer, lightly used, compromised morals, excellent memory. I've mapped every road Veylan's army will take into the free cities. That knowledge is worth more than a valley witch or a child who doesn't know what she's carrying."

"That's not—" Astrid started.

"It's a fair trade," I said, louder, over her. "Better than fair. You're getting interest on a two-hundred-year-old debt."

The entity regarded me with Aldric Vane's patient eyes, and I felt the weight of that gaze the way I'd once felt the weight of the deep court itself, pressing down through wet stone: the sense of being *read*. Every lie I'd told catalogued. Every betrayal under torture laid open like a book whose spine I'd cracked myself.

"Oh, cartographer," it said softly. "You have been selling pieces of yourself your whole life. To the crown. To Veylan's interrogators. To anyone who promised you'd survive the transaction." It stepped closer, boots silent on stone that should have echoed. "And now you offer me the last piece. Do you know why that moves me?"

"No," I said. My mouth had gone dry as old vellum.

"Because it's the first time you've ever sold something *for someone else*."

Behind me, Astrid made a sound — small, furious, wounded — and then her hand closed around my wrist.

"No," she said.

"Astrid—"

"*No.*" She stepped between me and the thing wearing the founder's face, and I had never seen her look like that. Not stubborn. Not brave. *Incandescent.* "You don't get him. You don't get any of them. This whole court runs on other people paying for each other — a founder who bought safety with his soul, a guild that let him, a usurper who burns cities because someone once told him power was a thing you could own. I am done watching people be currency."

The entity watched her with something close to pleasure. "Then name your price, Pemberton. You came down here to bargain. Everyone who comes to me bargains. The question is only ever *what*."

"The soul," she said. "All of it. Every fragment in these lights, and the piece inside Wren. Released. Free to go wherever souls go when they're not owned."

"And in exchange?"

She was quiet for a moment. Long enough that I understood, with a sick lurch, that she already knew what she would say. Had maybe known since the gate at dusk, since the ward-stone lit, since the moment she defied her elders and took a mud-caked stranger into her arms.

"My memories," Astrid said. "Of the valley."

The word landed in me like a knife between ribs.

"No," I said. "No. Astrid, that's everything. That's your whole life — your master, the wards, the—"

"Which is exactly why it's worth a soul." She didn't look at me. She couldn't; I understood that later, understood that looking at me would have broken her before the ink dried. "You said it yourself, Tam. Everything in this place is a trade. So trade me."

The entity went very still. Even the thousand lights seemed to lean in.

"Memories of the Veiled Valley," it repeated, tasting the words. "Every dawn on the eastern ridge. Your master's voice saying your name. The smell of the ward-smoke. The feeling—" it paused, delicately, "—of *home*."

"All of it."

"And you understand what remains? A woman who knows the valley exists, who can find it on a map, who will stand in its streets and feel nothing. Who will love the people there the way you love a place you've read about." Aldric Vane's face tilted. "That is a crueler price than death, Astrid Pemberton. I want you to know that I know."

"I know," she said.

"*Don't,*" I said, and my voice cracked on it, and I hated myself for the crack. "There has to be another way. Take my maps. Take ten years off my life. Take—"

"Tam." She finally looked at me, and her eyes were bright and absolutely steady. "You offered your whole self five minutes ago. Don't you dare begrudge me a few memories."

"That's different."

"Why? Because yours were shame and mine were home?" Her mouth twisted. "Because I'm the one who's supposed to be good at sacrifice, so you'll do the dying and I'll do the grieving? No. We don't get to make each other martyrs. That's just another way of owning someone."

Wren tugged at her hand. The dead man's voice came out of the child, quiet and grave:

"He will take more than you offer. They always do."

"I know," Astrid said. "So I'm offering first, and completely, and he can't claim interest on a gift freely given." She lifted her chin toward the entity. "Well? Do we have terms?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere above us, the boots had stopped descending. I found out later what that meant — that they'd reached the drowned cathedral, that Veylan's officers were reading my maps by torchlight, that the door we'd come through was being sealed behind us with the same patience the entity brought to everything.

"Terms," the entity said at last, and the hall changed.

The thousand lights rose from their niches and began to move — drifting, circling, converging on the space between Astrid and the throne where Aldric Vane sat. And in their center, the fragment inside Wren stirred; the child shivered and stepped forward, releasing Astrid's hand, walking to the edge of the light-storm like a girl approaching a shore.

"Wren—" I reached for her.

"She has to give it back herself," Astrid said. Her voice was already starting to fray. "It's her cargo. She carried it home."

The child opened her hands. Light poured out of her — not the cold gold of the hoarded souls but something warmer, older, threaded with rain-sounds and words no living tongue should know. It joined the storm above her, and for one heartbeat the whole hall blazed like noon, and I heard — everyone heard — a voice that was not Wren's and not the entity's, vast and relieved, sighing out of the mountain itself:

*Paid.*

Then the entity raised Aldric Vane's hand, and pointed one finger at Astrid.

"Your turn."

She swayed. I caught her arm. Up close I could see it already beginning — not gone, not yet, but *loosening*, the way color loosens from a leaf. She looked up at me and smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was brave.

"Tam. Listen. When this is done, I won't remember why I trust you. I just will. Don't explain it to me. Don't tell me what I gave up. Let me—" Her breath caught. "Let me meet you new. Maybe that's a gift too. Maybe—"

"Stop," I said. "Just — stop. Save your breath."

"I love you." She said it fast, before the words could be taken along with everything else. "I never said it on the road and I should have. I love you, you impossible, disgraced, beautiful liar. Remember enough for both of us."

Then she turned to the entity and said, clearly, in the rain-language that Wren had taught her syllable by syllable across a hundred cold campfires:

*I pay.*

The memories left her the way a tide leaves a shore.

I held her while it happened. There was nothing else to do. She sagged against me, and her eyes stayed open, fixed on my face, and I watched them change — not emptying, never emptying, but *rearranging*, the way a map rearranges when the borders are redrawn. When she spoke again her voice was hers, and calm, and wrong.

"Thank you for catching me," Astrid said politely. "Have we met?"

Above us, the thousand lights were rising — streaming upward through the vaulted dark, through two hundred feet of stone, toward the surface, toward the war. And the last light, the warmest one, paused beside Wren's ear and whispered something that made the child laugh out loud for the first time since I'd known her.

Then it was gone, and the mountain shook, and far above us the first screams of Veylan's vanguard came echoing down the stair —

— because the souls had reached the sky, and they were angry, and Astrid looked up at the trembling dark with a stranger's wonder and asked me, softly:

"What's happening?"
