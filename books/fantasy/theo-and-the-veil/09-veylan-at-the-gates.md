## 9. Veylan at the Gates

**Astrid**

The deep court did not have a sky, but it had weather.

Astrid felt it change as they climbed the drowned cathedral's broken stair — a pressure in her chest like the hour before a storm, except the storm was inside her now. The founder's soul sat where her memories of the valley used to be, and it was *loud*. Not words. Older than words. A knowledge that moved through her the way blood moves through a wound.

Beside her, the child — Sefa, though that was only half her name; the other half had come home two nights ago — walked without stumbling. She had not stopped speaking since the soul left her. Whole sentences, whole centuries, pouring out of a six-year-old mouth in a voice that sometimes belonged to a man nine hundred years dead.

"The gates," Sefa said, in the founder's flat cadence. "He will reach the gates by moonrise."

"Whose gates?" Tam asked. He had his maps out even here, ink-stained fingers bracing against the wet stone, as if paper could hold the world together.

"All of them." The child turned her dark eyes up at Astrid. "Veylan has spent twenty years buying small magics from small courts. He thinks he is collecting a treasury. He does not know what we kept in the cellar."

*We.* Astrid's skin prickled. The founder spoke through his own stolen vessel as easily as a man flexing an old hand.

"And what did you keep?" she asked.

The child smiled, and it was not a child's smile. "Everything he ever bought. He bought from us all along, Astrid Pemberton. Every coin Veylan paid, every soul his brokers traded — it came home to the deep court. He has been arming us for twenty years." The smile faded. "He just never asked what happens when the cellar opens."

Above them, far above, through leagues of stone and water, Astrid felt it begin: horns. The free cities' bells answering them. War arriving on schedule, the way wars do.

Tam caught her elbow. "Tell me you're not thinking what I think you're thinking."

"I'm thinking about the farms burning in the lowlands," Astrid said. "I'm thinking about outriders at the pass, and elders who voted to throw a child into the snow, and a valley I sold my memories to save that won't remember me saving it." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "I'm thinking somebody has to open the cellar."

"That thing down there offered you *anything*," Tam said. "That's how it starts. You know that's how it starts."

"I know." She looked at him — at this man who had betrayed cells of resisters under torture and then mapped every road back to them so they could escape him, who had handed her his own name like a confession. "That's why I need you to watch me. All the way through. If I start sounding like it — like the court, like the founder, like anything that calls power a gift — you pull me back. However you have to."

"How?"

"You're a cartographer," she said. "You'll find the line."

* * *

**Tam**

Tam had drawn battlefields he'd never seen. It was the great shame of his craft — kings paid for maps of victories before the armies marched, and a mapmaker learned to make slaughter look tidy. Rivers in blue. Advance in black arrows. Casualties as neat little crosses, like birds at rest.

Nothing in his cartography prepared him for watching Astrid Pemberton stand at the mouth of the deep court with a child's hand in one of hers and nine hundred years of hoarded souls rising around her like floodwater.

The entity had not tried to stop them. That was the part that frightened him most. It sat on its throne of purchased names wearing the founder's face, and when Astrid turned back toward the stair with the soul blazing in her, it had inclined its head almost courteously, the way a lender watches a debtor walk out the door knowing the interest is already accruing.

*Take it,* it had said. *It was always going to be spent. I simply prefer my customers to believe they chose.*

Now the three of them climbed through the cathedral's drowned nave, and the water around their knees glowed where Astrid stepped. Not reflected light. Given light. She wasn't carrying the power; she was becoming a place it wanted to be, the way a valley becomes a place rain wants to fall.

"Astrid." He said her name like checking a bearing.

"I'm still me."

"For now."

"For now," she agreed, and did not slow down.

They came up through the mountain's root, past the sealed door the guild had warded shut for generations — the ward-stone Sefa had made glow with her first word. It shattered outward as they passed, not violently, more like ice giving up on being ice, and then they were standing under actual sky, actual stars, and the world below was burning.

From the ridge above the drowned valley they could see it: Veylan's army spread across the plain before the nearest free city, ten thousand campfires arranged with the geometric patience of a man who believed history owed him order. Siege towers like stitched wounds against the walls. And at the army's heart, on a hill that had no business having a hill's shape, a pavilion of black silk that Tam knew from his royal years — he had mapped its position at three different sieges. It never moved closer to danger. It didn't have to.

"He's there," Tam said. "Under the black silk."

"I know." Astrid's voice had changed. There were harmonics in it now, voices underneath her voice, and Tam gripped her arm hard enough to bruise. She glanced down at his hand. "Noted," she said dryly. "Still me."

"Prove it. Tell me something only Astrid knows."

She thought. Around them the air began to move wrong, currents bending toward her like iron filings finding their magnet, and still she stood there searching her face for an ordinary memory while the sky waited.

"When Corwin forgot my name," she said finally, "he called me 'the girl with the good pen.' And I was furious. And then I went and sharpened every pen in the guildhall because I didn't know what else to do with being furious." Her eyes were bright and wet. "There. Ordinary enough?"

"Ordinary enough." He let go of her arm. Then, because he might not get another chance, because maps had taught him that every journey has a last point where turning back is still possible: "Whatever you take from that court — spend it all. Don't keep change."

Sefa tugged Astrid's sleeve. "Grandmother," the child said — she had started calling her that, in the founder's language, *the one who carries* — "it's time."

* * *

**Astrid**

Here is what the founder knew, and what Astrid understood the moment she opened herself fully to his stolen centuries: magic was never a force. It was a *debt*.

Every working borrowed from somewhere. Every court beneath every mountain was a ledger. Veylan had spent twenty years purchasing power and calling himself self-made, and he had never once read the terms.

Astrid called in the debt.

She did not shout. She raised the child's small hand in hers, and she spoke in the language older than kingdoms, the one Sefa had arrived speaking, the one that was less a language than a contract's grammar — and the deep court answered through her like a vault answering its owner.

The plain below lit white.

Not fire. Fire was Veylan's currency, bought and crude. This was older: every siege tower came apart into the honest timber it had been before someone made it a weapon. The black arrows of ten thousand men bent away from the city walls like compass needles forced south. The hill beneath the black pavilion — the one with no business being a hill — sank, gently, the way a lie sinks when it gets too heavy, and Tam would tell her later that he saw the silk collapse into ordinary ground and something vast and furious pour out of it and *run*, because even usurpers can do arithmetic.

The army did not die. That was the part Astrid felt proudest of, in the moment, before she understood why pride itself should frighten her. The soldiers woke on a disarmed plain holding weapons that had forgotten how to be weapons, and they fled, and the free cities' bells rang until dawn.

And through all of it, the power sang to her.

*More,* it said, in the founder's voice, in the entity's voice, in her own. *The valley next. Mend the wards. No — better. Make new wards, wards that hum, wards that will never age, wards with your name in them. Keep them safe forever. They will never forget you again.*

Her hands were shaking. Not from effort. From how *good* it felt.

She could see it all: the Veiled Valley remade, glowing, permanent, her name carved into every ward-stone, generations of guild mages learning her liturgies, Corwin remembering her, the whole valley owing its morning light to Astrid Pemberton, forever, forever, forever —

"Grandmother."

Small fingers interlaced with hers. Sefa looked up, and in the child's eyes Astrid saw both halves at once — the girl who had cried in the snow, and the old man who had sold his soul and spent nine centuries learning exactly what that cost.

"You gave the memories so no one would owe you," Sefa said. "Don't take them back by making everyone owe."

The singing stopped.

Astrid let the power drain out through her open fingers, deliberately, all of it, back into the ground, back into whatever ledger would hold it — and the silence afterward was the sweetest sound she had ever heard, sweeter than any chorus the hoard had offered. She stood on the ridge in the dark, empty again, half-remembered again, herself.

Tam put his coat around her shoulders. Neither of them spoke for a long time.

* * *

Below them, dawn came up over a plain of abandoned arms and a city still standing. Somewhere in the wreckage of the black pavilion, Tam found a scrap of silk and, out of habit older than sense, noted its coordinates.

Astrid watched the sun rise and felt the shape of a valley in her mind whose feeling was gone, like a house seen through frosted glass.

"We should go home," Sefa said, tugging her hand. Then the child paused, tilted her head, listening to something none of them could hear, and added quietly, in the founder's cadence rather than her own:

"But first — someone is waiting at the gate. And she remembers more than we thought she would."

Astrid went cold. "Who? Who remembers?"

Sefa smiled, small and strange and six years old again.

"The valley," she said, "remembers *you*."
