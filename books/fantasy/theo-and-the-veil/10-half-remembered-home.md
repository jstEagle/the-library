## 10. Half-Remembered Home

The pass opened onto the valley at dawn, and I did not recognize it.

I recognized the shape of it — the way the ridgeline folded like a hand cupped around green, the river's silver thread, the terraced orchards stitched up the slopes in rows my own hands had once pruned. I knew all of it the way you know a map, which was fitting, because that was how I knew it now: through Tam's ink. He had drawn the valley from memory on the road down from the mountain, every night by firelight, sketching what he'd never seen until three days ago, and I had watched his lines and felt nothing where feeling should have been.

That was the price. I had known it would be. The entity beneath the mountain had taken my memories of this place cleanly, surgically, the way it took everything — not stolen but *purchased*, and purchases leave receipts. What remained was data without warmth. The orchard existed. The orchard had meant something to me once. Between those two facts lay a gulf I could see clearly and could not cross.

The child walked ahead of me with her hand in Tam's, chattering — actually chattering now, in a language older than kingdoms and, since the soul came home to her, in ours too. Her name was Wren. She had told us that herself, two days after the court beneath the mountain cracked open like an egg and gave back what it had hoarded. Six years old, and she carried a dead man's century inside her small frame, and she bore it the way children bear everything: lightly, curiously, asking questions about the founder's memories at breakfast as if they were stories someone had read to her.

"Is that the gate?" she asked, pointing.

"That's the gate," Tam said.

"Is it where Astrid grew up?"

"It's where she lived," Tam said carefully. "Where she grew up is — " He glanced at me. "It's complicated."

I almost laughed. Almost. It caught somewhere in my chest and turned into something else, and I looked away at the mountains so no one would see.

* * *

The guild met us at the ward-stone.

Not all of them. Not the elders who had voted to turn Wren out into the dark — two of those were gone, fled east when Veylan's army broke at the free cities and the news came rippling back that magic had moved like a living thing across the battlefield, that the usurper's vanguard had simply stopped, unmade, their war-wards unraveled by hands none of the survivors could describe. The other elders stood in the gatehouse square with their hoods up and their faces averted, and when we passed through the gate they did not greet me. They watched, and flinched, and said nothing.

But the younger ones came. Maren, who had been my apprentice for four years before I left, ran the last hundred yards and stopped short of touching me, the way you stop short of a stranger wearing a familiar coat.

"You came back," she said.

"I came back."

"They said you might not. They said the trade took—" She faltered. "They said you wouldn't remember us."

"I don't," I said, and watched her face do the thing faces do when a wound arrives ahead of its explanation. "Maren. Listen to me. I don't remember you. But I know what I chose, and I know why, and I know that whatever I felt for you before I left was real, because I have never once made a decision that cheaply." I put out my hand. "You'll have to tell me who I was. All of it. And I'll have to believe you. That's the bargain now. That's what we're building on."

She took my hand. Her fingers were cold and her grip was fierce.

Wren, meanwhile, had already found the old ward-stone — the one that had glowed for her the night she arrived, the first light it had given in forty years. She pressed both palms against it and stood there humming, some fragment of the founder's speech, and the stone answered her in slow pulses of amber, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm again. The wards around the valley had never hummed in my lifetime. They hummed now. You could feel it in the ground if you knelt and laid your ear against the soil, a faint thrumming, patient and deep, as though the whole valley had woken from a long sleep and wasn't entirely sure it wanted to be awake.

Tam stood apart from all of it, near the gatehouse wall, sketching. Of course he was sketching. He had a fresh sheet pinned to a board and was drawing the valley as it stood this morning — the repaired roofs, the new graves, the scar on the eastern slope where Veylan's outriders had burned the lowland farms on our way out and the fire had crept up far enough to blacken three rows of apple trees. He drew the truth of places, not their postcards. It was why I had trusted him in the deep court when every instinct screamed otherwise, why I had believed him over the entity wearing the founder's beautiful borrowed face. Liars make smooth maps. Tam's maps were full of corrections.

He caught me watching him and held my gaze a moment longer than necessary, and neither of us said anything, because there was nothing yet that needed saying. Some things you build slowly or not at all.

* * *

The guild gathered that evening in the long hall, and I told them the truth, all of it, the way I wished someone had told me years ago.

I told them about the founder's bargain — the sin the elders had buried, the soul sold beneath the mountain to buy the valley its blindness, the protection that was really just a debt accruing interest for a hundred years. I told them about the court: the drowned cathedral, the shelves of purchased souls arranged like jars in an apothecary, the thing that wore the founder's face and spoke in his voice and offered me anything I named. I told them how close I had come. How its offer had sounded, for one shining moment, like salvation — name anything, it said, and I had nearly named the valley itself, nearly asked for the veil made permanent, made eternal, made safe forever. And then Wren's hand had closed around my finger, small and certain, and I had understood that safety bought at that price was just the debt with better terms.

And I told them what I had paid instead. My memories. Every one of them rooted in this soil. Twenty-nine years of mornings in this valley, gone, traded away so that the founder's soul could go home to the child and the court's hoard could be turned against Veylan and the free cities could keep breathing.

"The guild has operated on a single principle since its founding," I said. "One person holds the knowledge. One person carries the wards, the rites, the names. That is how the founder set it up, and that is exactly what let him sell us all without our consent — because when memory lives in one skull, one skull can be bought." I sat down among them instead of standing above them, which caused a small scandal and which I intend to repeat. "So we change it. From tonight, nothing lives in one place. Every rite copied in three hands. Every ward-signature taught to every member, elder and novice alike. Every story of this valley written down and kept in five houses, so that the next time something beneath a mountain comes shopping, it finds nothing it can carry off."

Old Master Corwin — half-blind now, most of his own memory gone to age, though he still remembered my name on good days, which was more than I could say for myself — raised his head at that.

"You're describing a guild that can survive losing any single member," he said.

"I'm describing a guild that survives losing *me*," I said. "That's the point. I'm the proof of concept. Look at me. Everything I was here is gone, and the valley still stands, because it never should have depended on what was in my head." I paused. "Memory kept by others. That's the whole doctrine. Write it down."

Maren wrote it down. By midnight it was carved into the lintel of the hall in letters deep enough to outlast paint: *NOTHING HELD ALONE.*

* * *

Winter came early that year, and gently, and I spent most of it being taught myself.

There is a strange discipline in receiving your own life secondhand. Maren would sit with me by the kitchen fire and tell me about the day I taught her to mend a ward-line in the rain, and I would listen, and nod, and feel nothing, and then weeks later I would catch myself doing it — holding the copper wire at that odd angle, tapping the stone twice before setting it — and understand that the knowledge had survived even where the feeling hadn't, lodged in my hands like sediment. The body remembers what the mind sold. I found my mother's grave in the third week, guided by Corwin's directions, and stood before the stone knowing her name and unable to summon one flicker of grief, and it was Tam who knelt beside me and placed the winter rose, because he knew I would want it done and couldn't do it alone.

He stayed. Through the winter, through the rebuilding, through the long evenings when the guild's novices crowded the hall to copy rites and Wren fell asleep in my lap mid-story, the founder's memories and her own six years braided together in her dreams. He fixed the roof. He redrew the valley's maps properly, surveyed them himself, corrected forty years of errors the old charts had carried. He hung them in the hall where anyone could read them, because cartography, too, should be nothing held alone.

And slowly, without either of us naming it, love assembled itself between us out of parts. Not the kind that arrives fully formed from a shared past — I had no shared past to give him. The kind that gets built. He remembered things for me. I trusted him with things I couldn't verify. When I woke some nights unable to recall whether I'd ever loved this valley at all, it was his voice that answered, steady and unsentimental: *You did. I watched you choose it over everything the mountain offered. I was there.* He was my witness. In a life where my own memory could no longer be believed, he became the thing my memory leaned on.

In spring, standing on the ridge above the orchards with the whole valley spread below us and the wards humming underfoot, he asked me — plainly, in his plain way — whether I meant to stay.

"I gave up my past to buy everyone a future," I said. "Seems poor form to leave before collecting on it."

"And me?"

"You drew the map," I said. "You stay where your map says home is." Then, because he deserved better than deflection: "Yes, Tam. With you. However that gets built."

He laughed, quietly, and sketched the two of us into the corner of the valley chart — two small figures on the ridge, ink-faint, permanent.

* * *

A year after we came home, on the anniversary of the night Wren knocked at the gate, the guild held its first true ritual in living memory. Not a mending — the wards needed no mending now. A remembering. We lit fires on the terraces, every household, and each family told one story of the valley aloud, into the open air, so the telling belonged to everyone and no one. Wren went first. She stood on the ward-stone and spoke the founder's account of the valley's founding — the true one, the ugly one, the one with the sale in it — because she had decided, in her six-year-old way, that a home that hides its sins isn't a home, it's a hiding place. The elders winced. The novices listened. The valley heard its own history spoken by a child carrying a dead man's soul, and nothing broke, and somehow that was the miracle.

I stood at the edge of the firelight and felt — not the past, that was gone beyond recovery — but something adjacent to it. Something I can only call forward-memory: the sense that the person I am becoming will have roots here, that ten years hence I will remember this night with feeling, my feeling, earned rather than inherited. Love, I have decided, is a kind of remembering. Not the only kind. But the kind that builds instead of mourns.

Late in the evening, Corwin found me at the wall. He was very old and very tired and he looked at me a long moment with his clouded eyes.

"You know," he said, "the master before me used to say the valley would end one day. Fade out. Forget itself." He smiled. "He was right. It ended. It forgot. And look — it's still here."

Below us, Wren was teaching Maren's novices the founder's ward-speech, correcting their pronunciation with the gravity of a much older teacher, amber light pulsing under her palms. Tam was sketching the fires. The valley hummed.

And far to the east, past the passes, past the free cities still rebuilding their broken walls, travelers had begun arriving with strange reports — of other courts beneath other mountains, older than the founder's, older than any kingdom, waking now that the deep court had fallen. Whispers of souls hoarded in the dark, waiting for someone to come asking questions.

Wren heard them first. She always does.

"Astrid," she said quietly, at the edge of the fire, in the founder's voice and her own at once, "there are more doors."

I looked at her, and at Tam's pen already moving toward a fresh sheet, and I understood that the valley was safe, and the guild was free, and the work — the work had only begun.
