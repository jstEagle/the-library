## 1. The Bone That Sang

The femur had been a soldier's, once. Ines Dunmore could tell from the old break, knitted crooked, that had never healed quite right—the man had limped through half a life before something else finally stopped him. Now she held the bone against the lamplight, turning it slow, and listened to what it remembered.

Bone always remembered. That was the trouble with it.

"Tell me about the river," she murmured to it, though the words were not really for the bone. They were for the woman sitting across the workbench, hands folded so tight her knuckles had gone white as tallow. Widow Maren Ostell had come up from Lowmark three days ago with a purse of silver and a request: something her son could be remembered in. Something *him*.

"He loved the river," Maren said, as she said every time the silence stretched too long. "Tomas. He'd fish it dawn to dusk, summer he turned twelve. Came home with his boots full of minnows and his mother near wept over the mess of him."

"Mm." Ines set her burin to the bone. The first cut was always the hardest; the dead woke grumbling, like old men roused from nap. A whisper stirred against her fingertips, faint as breath on a winter window. *Cold,* it said. *Cold, cold, the water's cold—*

Not Tomas. An older voice, sedimented deep in the bone's grain. She carved past it.

She had learned young that the gift was less a gift than a debt. Every relic she carved sang with the lives it had held, and Ines had spent twenty-nine years pretending she could not hear them, because in Thornfell—and everywhere the crown's long arm reached—women who heard the dead in bone were called witches, and witches burned. So she carved quietly. She carved *beautifully*, so that her work would be praised for its artistry and never questioned for its intimacy. She kept her eyes down and her prices fair and herself invisible, and for twenty-nine years it had worked.

The whisper sharpened. *Cold—cold—* and then, sliding up through the marrow like a fish rising through dark water, a younger voice. Bright. Startled. *Mother? Mother, I can't—*

Ines's burin stopped.

Tomas Ostell had drowned. She knew that much; Maren had said it in the flat way grief speaks of the unbearable. A river, a spring flood, a boy of nineteen who had gone in after a friend and not come out. The bone she'd bought from the charnel-man in Lowmark had been Tomas's, sold honest as bone was ever sold.

*It's cold, Mother. Why is it so cold?*

"Tomas," she said softly, under her breath, so low not even Maren across the bench could hear. "Tomas, you're past the cold now. You're through it."

*No.* The voice was urgent in a way the dead rarely were. The dead mostly wanted small things—a name spoken, a door closed, a lie confessed. This was different. This was a boy hammering on a window. *Mother. Listen. Soldiers on the pass. Grey cloaks. They're coming down the Thorn road, they're burning the waystation at Harrow's Ford, they're—*

Ines's hands had gone still on the bone. Outside, the autumn wind moved through the pines above the carving-house, and somewhere down the valley a cart creaked on the smuggler's road, the sound so ordinary it was almost an insult.

*They're coming,* Tomas said. *I can see them. I can see everything from here. Mother, tell her to run.*

"Ines?" Maren Ostell leaned forward. "Is something wrong? You've stopped."

She almost said *no*. She had said *no* a thousand times to a thousand warnings—the dead were like ravens, they cried at everything, they saw crows in every cornfield. A week ago a dead ferryman had warned her of *water rising*, and it had been only the autumn rains. She had a commission half-finished and a kiln to load and a mentor who would call her a frightened fool, and so she said—

"Nothing," Ines said. "Only deciding where the knot goes."

She carved. The whisper faded to embers. And if her hands were not quite steady after that, she told herself it was the lamplight, the long hours, the cheap tallow smoke stinging her eyes.

* * *

Old Sefa found her there at moonrise, when the carving-house had gone blue and cold and Ines was still hunched over the femur like a woman praying.

"You've the look of a girl who's been talking to her work again." Sefa stood in the doorway with a lamp, a small round woman of seventy years with forearms like a smith's and a face like a walnut. She had taken Ines in at nine years old, a mute, starving child who would not stop touching the bones in the midden, and she had never once asked what Ines heard. That was the whole of their love, and it was enough.

"The bone spoke," Ines said. "The Ostell boy. He said soldiers on the pass. Grey cloaks. Burning at Harrow's Ford."

Sefa was quiet a long moment. The lamp flame leaned in the draft. "Harrow's Ford is a day's walk," she said at last. "If it were true, we'd have seen the smoke."

"We'd have seen it *at night*."

"And if it's true," Sefa said, setting the lamp down with a click that sounded terribly loud, "then it doesn't matter whether we run tonight or at dawn, because grey cloaks ride faster than old women walk. So." She looked at her apprentice, her daughter in every way blood had nothing to do with. "Finish the widow's commission. We'll send it down with the morning cart. And you'll stop listening to drowned boys who want their mothers to worry."

Ines laughed—a small, cracked sound—and it was the last easy thing that happened for a long time.

She was loading the kiln when the light came.

It came up the valley like a false dawn, orange where no dawn should be, and beneath it a sound she had never heard but knew instantly, the way prey knows: the low continuous roar of many fires, and threading through it, thin and wrong, the bells of Harrow's Ford ringing the alarm and then, abruptly, not ringing at all.

"Sefa." Her voice came out strange, flattened. "Sefa, the ford—"

Her mentor was already in the doorway, and Ines would carry the next moment to her grave: Sefa silhouetted against the growing glow, lamp in hand, saying, with the calm of a woman who had survived three purges by being nobody, "Kiln's loaded, then. Good girl. Now listen—we take the mule and the strongbox and we go up the goat track, and we don't look back, and—"

The first riders came over the rise before she finished.

They did not look like men. They looked like a verdict. Grey cloaks, grey helms, and on each breast the crown's device burned in white enamel: a closed door, barred. Behind them came men with torches, and behind *them*—Ines's stomach turned over—came the wagons. Iron cages. She had heard of the cages. Everyone had heard of the cages; hearing was different from seeing them roll to a stop outside your door.

"Carving-house," one rider said, not a question. "Witch-marks reported. Two practitioners."

*Two.* Someone had talked. Someone had always talked; that was how the crown worked, not with hounds but with neighbors.

They moved fast, too fast for the old woman's plan. Ines remembered it afterward in shards: Sefa shoving the strongbox at her, Sefa's voice cracking at last after seventy years of calm, *run, girl, run up the track, don't you dare look back*—and Ines running, and the first torch arcing onto the shingle roof, and the terrible moment when she did look back, because she was twenty-nine years old and Sefa was everything, and she saw her mentor turn to face the riders with her lamp still in her hand like a woman greeting guests.

She did not see what happened after. She made herself not see. The light behind her went very bright, and Sefa did not come up the goat track, and Ines climbed into the black pines with her strongbox of tools hugged to her chest and her ears full of her own heartbeat, and she did not stop until the valley was a smear of orange far below and her lungs were knives.

* * *

Dawn found her in the high wood, lost in the way that only the hunted are lost—every direction equally wrong. Frost silvered the bracken. Her breath hung around her like her own ghost. Somewhere below, smoke still rose from the ruin of the only home she had ever had, and the silence of the dead pressed against her harder than the cold.

That was the worst of it, she realized. Not the burning. Not Sefa. But that the dead of Harrow's Ford and the carving-house were calling to her—she could feel them, dozens of new voices, raw and bewildered, tugging at the edges of her gift like hands on her sleeves—and she could not answer a single one without stopping, without carving, without marking herself as exactly what the grey cloaks said she was.

So she walked. And the dead walked with her, silent, patient, terrible.

She smelled the blood before she saw the wolf.

Iron and musk, thick on the frozen air, and beneath it something else—something that made the hair on her arms lift and her gift ring like a struck bell. She followed it through the pines to a clearing where a stream cut through the rocks, and there, caught in a hunter's snare of crown-forged steel, lay a wolf the size of a yearling calf.

It was silver. Not grey—*silver*, its coat holding the early light like frost holds it, and its flank was torn open from its own struggles, blood frozen black along the wound. A crown snare, Ines saw. The barbed kind, the kind used for nothing that walked on four legs. The kind used for things that *changed*.

The wolf's eyes found her. They were amber, and old, and they did not look at her the way an animal looks at a woman.

They looked at her the way the dead look through bone.

"Please," said the wolf.

Not in words. That was the thing Ines would struggle to explain, ever after, to anyone who asked—not that she understood it, but that there were no words at all. It was a voice without a throat, older than Tomas Ostell, older than any ghost in any bone she had ever carved, a voice like the deep places of the earth, and it said *please*, and beneath the please, beneath the blood and the pain, it said something else, something that stopped her breath:

*You hear me. At last, at last—you hear me, heir of Veyra.*

Ines took one step back. Then another. The strongbox of tools was heavy against her ribs, and the smoke of her burning life rose behind her, and the wolf in the snare watched her with its ancient amber eyes and did not beg again, because it was old enough to know that begging was a thing the young did.

Her foot found the edge of the clearing. The border of the choice.

The wolf closed its eyes. "Go, then," it said, in that voice like the roots of mountains. "They will come for you next, little carver. But go."

Ines Dunmore stood in the frost a long moment, listening to the dead of two countries, and then—cursing Sefa, cursing Tomas Ostell, cursing every bone that had ever sung to her—she set down the strongbox and knelt in the snow beside the snare.

"Hold still," she said to the last guardian of a country she had never heard of. "This is going to hurt."
