## 2. What the Wolf Remembers

The snare had been made for wolves but built by men, and it was cruel in the way only human ironwork could be—barbed at every joint so that the more the animal struggled, the deeper the teeth went. Ines worked by feel and by firelight, her bone-knife sliding under the spring-joint while the wolf lay perfectly still, which frightened her more than thrashing would have. Nothing that wounded stayed that calm. Only things that had been wounded a very long time learned to hold still like that.

"There," she said, and the trap fell open with a sound like a jaw unclenching.

The wolf did not move. Blood had frozen along its foreleg in a black lacework, and beneath the silver fur she could see the wrongness of the wound—not torn flesh exactly, but flesh that seemed reluctant to knit, as though even its own body had half forgotten how. She cleaned it with snow and bound it with strips of her sleeve, and all the while the animal watched her with eyes the color of deep water over old stone.

"You should have run," it said.

"I should have done a great many things." She sat back on her heels. "I'm Ines."

"I know what you are." The wolf's breath steamed between them. "I have smelled your gift since you crossed the ridge. It is why I called to you. Why I sang through the dead boy's bones until you heard me above his mother's grief." Its ears flattened, something almost like shame. "I am sorry for that. The widow will bury an empty warning."

Ines's hands went cold inside her gloves. "That was you. In the commission—the rib-carving. The voice under Tomas's voice."

"The dead of this country are loud," the wolf said, "and I am very quiet now. I must borrow their mouths."

She wanted to be angry. She found, examining herself honestly, that she mostly wasn't; the dead borrowed things from her all the time, her sleep, her hands, her peace. Perhaps it was only fair that something borrow them back.

"What do I call you?"

"Vael." He said it the way one sets down something heavy. "I was warden of Veyra's door. I am the last thing left of Veyra that remembers itself."

* * *

She did not intend to sleep. She built a fire in the hollow beneath the fallen cedar, fed the wolf broth from her stores, wrapped herself in her cloak with her back against stone—and woke inside a country she had never seen.

It was green there. That was the first thing, the impossible ordinary thing: green hills stitched with terraces, orchards heavy with fruit she had no name for, a river running silver through a valley where the houses were built of pale timber and roofed in blue slate, chimneys rising straight and unhurried into a sky without smoke. People moved among the fields. She could hear them—not their voices but their *gifts*, a great low chord of them, hundreds of souls each humming their own small note: a woman whose touch mended cracked clay, a man who spoke to bees, children who could find lost things simply by wanting to. No one hid. No one was quiet. The whole valley rang like a struck bell that never quite finished singing.

"This was Veyra," said Vael, beside her—or rather, all around her, for in the dream he was not a wolf but something larger, silver-flanked, crowned with antlers of white bone, a shape her dreaming mind refused to fully render. "Before the crown drew its maps over us. Before the Unmaking."

The dream turned, the way dreams do, and the green went out of it.

She saw the soldiers come—not witch-hunters in their red hoods but crown regulars, ranks of them, methodical as locusts. She saw the door: a seam of light standing open in the face of a mountain, tall as a cathedral gate, breathing warmth into the world. And she saw the ember—a coal of dragon-fire, red-gold, alive—set into the mountain's brow above it, tended by wardens in silver, of whom Vael had been the youngest.

Then the burning. The scattering. Families driven onto roads that forgot themselves behind them. Names unwritten from registers, songs unsung on pain of the lash, whole villages repopulated with people who were told, gently, firmly, generation after generation, that there had never been anything on the other side of that ridge at all. The kingdom did not just erase Veyra. It taught the erasure, taught it to children who taught it to their children, until forgetting became the land's native tongue.

Only the door remained, because the door could not be closed while the ember burned. So the crown had done the next best thing. It had hunted the wardens down across eighty years, patiently, thoroughly, until one wounded creature limped through Thornfell with a dying fire in his chest.

Ines woke with tears freezing on her cheeks and the taste of ash in her mouth, and Vael's head resting against her knee.

"Now you know," he said.

* * *

They traveled north by night, and the dreams came every time she slept.

Each night Veyra showed her more. The library carved into a hillside where books remembered being read aloud. The harbor town where the gifted had traded openly, blessing hulls and healing nets. Her grandmother—younger than Ines had ever known her, laughing, dark-haired, wearing a blue-slate pendant—in a kitchen full of rosemary, singing a song Ines had heard only once, hummed over a cradle thirty years ago. *Veyran,* Vael told her. *Fled in the last scattering. Carried the blood-line into Thornfell and buried it under a married name. You are the last of it that lives.*

"Why does that matter?" Ines asked, in the dream where the green hills stood. "You're the warden. You carry the ember."

"I carry what remains of it," Vael said, and opened, in the dream, the cage of his ribs.

Inside, where a heart should have burned steady, there was a coal the size of a walnut—and it was guttering. Light and dark chasing each other across its surface like weather. Each pulse dimmer than the last.

"The door holds while the ember burns. When it gutters out, the door closes forever, and everything the crown buried stays buried—including every gifted soul they have taken since. There are cells beneath the capital, little carver. There are children in those cells who hum when they think no one listens. If the door dies, they die in the dark of a country that says they never existed."

"And me?"

"The ember answers blood. A warden's blood, Veyran blood. It can be passed—but only to a hand that carries the line, and only while the carrier lives." His great eyes held hers. "I am asking you to inherit a dying fire and a locked door at the top of a spire, with the crown's hounds behind us. I am asking you to give up everything you have spent your life learning to protect."

"Which is?"

"Your invisibility."

* * *

By day, the world gave its answer to the dream.

They found the first waystation at Grey Ford, or rather they found its chimney, standing alone in a field of char. The smuggler's rest had stood forty years; it had burned in an afternoon, and not accidentally—the fire had been set in a ring, professional, thorough. Nailed to the blackened doorpost was a strip of parchment bearing the crown's seal and a single line of print: *SHELTER GIVEN TO HERETICS IS TREASON.*

The second waystation, two nights later, still smoldered. They watched it from the tree line, and Ines watched something else besides: a column of riders on the far ridge, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who knew exactly where their quarry was going. At their head rode a figure in a captain's cloak, and even at that distance, even after six years, her body knew him before her mind consented to. Aldric Vane. He did not ride like a man hunting. He rode like a man following.

"He knows the door," she said aloud. It was the first time she'd spoken of it outside the dreams. "He knows where we're going."

"Then he knew before we did," Vael said grimly. "The crown has had eighty years to learn where its fear lives. We are not running ahead of him, Ines. We are running beside him."

That night the dream showed her the spire: Thornfell's highest peak, where the mountain wore its broken crown of stone, and high upon it a seam of light so faint it was nearly memory. And the dream showed her the price again, more plainly than before—not as words but as weight, settling onto her chest like a stone laid there by gentle hands.

The ember needed kindling. Not wood. Not blood alone. It needed voices—it needed the accumulated song of the gifted dead, poured through a living hand that could hear them. Everything she had ever heard. Everyone.

She woke before dawn with frost in her hair and Vael watching her.

"You saw it," he said.

"I saw it." She looked north, toward the spire, toward the thin light at the top of the world. Then, because she was her grandmother's blood and hiding had only ever been a tactic, never a nature, she began packing her tools. "Vael. How many marked ones are left in Thornfell? How many are still alive, still hidden, still running?"

The wolf's ears pricked. "Dozens. Perhaps more."

"Good," said Ines Dunmore, and felt her old life close behind her like a door. "Because we're going to need every one of them—and I know a deaf boy who reads vibrations in bone, and a smuggler who owes me a debt he'll hate paying."
