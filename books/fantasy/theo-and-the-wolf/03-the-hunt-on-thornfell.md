## 3. The Hunt on Thornfell

The boy's name was Tam, and he heard with his hands.

Ines had found him three days after the burning, crouched in the ruins of a waystation Vane's men had already gutted, his bare palms pressed flat against a splintered doorpost. When she approached he did not startle—did not hear her coming, she realized—but lifted his face to the tremor of her boots on the floorboards and read her the way she read bone. He was perhaps eleven, with a burn scar crawling up one arm like ivy, and when the soldiers' horses had come down the pass, he had felt them through the earth a full hour before they arrived. He had run. His mother had not.

*You're like me,* Ines signed to him, clumsily, the gestures she'd learned from a deaf carver in her girlhood. *You feel what others can't.*

*The ground talks,* Tam signed back, and then, his hands hesitating, trembling: *It says the tall one is coming. The one who smells of ash and old love.*

She did not ask how he knew that. The dead told children things they would never tell the living.

* * *

Old Brammet owed her for two favors she had never called in—a forged bill of lading when the customs men came sniffing through Thornfell, and the silence she'd kept about the grave of a man Brammet had sworn never existed. She called them in now, standing in his smokeshed while the ember in her breast pocket pulsed like a second heart, warm through the leather.

"You're mad," Brammet said, packing his pipe with hands that shook. He was sixty if he was a day, gray-bearded, with a smuggler's map of the world folded into the creases of his face. "The spire's a dead climb. Nobody's been up Thornfell in forty years. The crown erased the road, girl. Erased the *door*. You can't walk to a place that isn't on any map."

"It's on Vael's map." The wolf lay at her feet, his wound from the snare healed to a silver seam, but the ember's guttering had begun to show in him—his flanks thinner, his eyes dimmer, as though he were a lamp burning down to its last oil. "And the crown erased it because it was real. That's the whole point of erasing, Brammet. You don't unmake what isn't there."

The old smuggler looked at her a long moment. Then at the wolf, who looked back with the patience of mountains. Then at the boy in the doorway, reading the floor's tremors, his hands half-raised as though catching rain.

"Forty years ago," Brammet said quietly, "I ran cargo past a door I was told never to speak of. There was a woman on the other side of it. She gave me bread. Veyran bread, black as peat and sweet as honey." He knocked out his pipe. "I've dreamed about that bread every winter since. Gods help me. I'll take you up."

* * *

They climbed by night, the three of them and the wolf, up the goat-track that had been Thornfell's road before the crown's cartographers burned their own charts and hanged the men who drew them. The mountain remembered anyway. Tam walked with one palm against the rockface the whole way, and twice he stopped them with a raised fist—*riders below*—and they pressed into shadow while Vane's patrols passed with torches that turned the scree to rivers of fire.

The ember burned hotter with every switchback. By the second night it was not warmth but pain, a coal against her sternum, and Ines understood that it was not only guttering—it was *calling*. The door knew it was coming home.

On the third night, in a hollow below the treeline, Vael spoke to her in the dream as he always did, his voice like wind through a valley that no longer existed.

*We are close, bone-carver. Close enough that the king's hounds can smell the door's breath.*

"Tell me what happens," she said, in the dream-meadow where Veyra's ghosts walked in the gold light of a country that had never officially existed. "When we open it. Tell me the truth."

The wolf was quiet a long time. *The door opens. The dead of Veyra come home. And the voices you have carried—the dead you have heard all your life—they will pass through it too. All of them. Everywhere.* His great head lowered. *You will not lose them the way the living lose things, Ines. You will lose them the way a river loses its rain.*

She woke with her cheeks wet and said nothing to Tam or Brammet, and when the boy signed *sad?* at her in the gray morning, she lied with her hands for the first time in her life.

* * *

The spire was worse than Brammet had promised.

It rose from the mountain's crown like a black tooth, a tower of fused stone that predated the kingdom, predated perhaps stone itself, its surface veined with channels that glowed faint ember-red when the wolf approached. There was no door at its base. There was no way up at all—except that when Ines pressed her palm to the rock, the channels warmed, and stairs unfolded from the stone like a hand opening.

They were halfway up when the horns sounded.

Not one horn. A dozen. And below them, spilling out of the treeline like spilled ink, came Vane's riders—thirty at least—and among them, on a gray horse that had once been her father's before the crown took everything, rode Captain Aldric Vane.

He climbed alone. Of course he did. He had always been a fool that way, and she had always loved him for it, and she hated that she still knew that about him.

He came up the unfolding stairs with his sword sheathed and his hands open, stopping ten steps below her, close enough that she could see the gray in his temples that hadn't been there a year ago.

"Ines."

"Captain."

"Don't." His voice cracked on it. "Don't do that. I burned your village because I was ordered to burn your village, and I have not slept since, and you know me well enough to know that's true."

"I know you well enough to know you did it anyway."

The wind tore at them. Below, Brammet had Tam against the stone, shielding him, and Vael stood at the stair's edge with his hackles silvered and raised, a statue of a wolf deciding.

"Give me the ember." Vane said it quietly, and here it was, the thing she'd known was coming since the pass. "The crown offers full amnesty. Pardon, land, a name cleared. You, the boy, the old man—papers, protection, whatever life you want. The hunt ends tonight. No one else burns." He swallowed. "Ines. I have seen what's in the cellars under the Citadel. I have seen what they keep of the *unmade*. The crown doesn't fear the ember. It fears what's behind the door. There are things in Veyra that were locked away with the country, and the king would rather burn every gifted child in the realm than let them out."

"Then the king," Ines said, "has finally told the truth about something."

"Ines—"

"You want to know what's behind the door, Aldric?" Her hand found the ember through the leather, burning, burning. "Dead people. That's what's behind it. A country of dead people who were murdered by a signature on a map. And I have spent my whole life hearing the dead scream, and I am going to open a door and let them *rest*, and no pardon you carry is worth the ink it's written in."

Something moved in his face—grief, or its corpse. "Then I'm sorry," he said, and drew his sword, and whistled, and the riders below began to climb.

What happened next happened in the space of a heartbeat, and Ines would spend the rest of her life inside that heartbeat.

Vane moved first—faster than she remembered, the crown had honed him—and it was not his sword that came for her but a knife, thrown low and hard at the pocket where the ember burned, and her hand was rising, too slow, too slow—

Vael was not slow.

The wolf came off the stair like a thrown spear. The knife meant for the ember—and the heart behind it—struck him instead, high in the chest, sinking to the hilt. He did not cry out. He landed between her and Vane, and he stood there, swaying, silver blood—*silver, like his fur, like the light of a country that no longer existed*—running down his foreleg, and he looked at Vane with eyes that had watched a kingdom die.

"Vael—" Ines dropped to her knees.

*The ember,* the wolf said, and his voice in her skull was thin now, a wind running out of breath. *The door. Climb, bone-carver. Finish it. I did not carry this fire across a hundred years to watch you bargain with a man who loves you more than his king and less than his fear.*

Vane stood frozen, the knife's twin still in his hand, staring at the wolf, at the silver blood on the stone, and Ines saw something break in him—not the soldier, the *man*, the boy who had once sat with her on a wall and asked her what the dead sounded like.

"Up," Brammet roared from below, hauling Tam past them. "Girl, *up*!"

Ines pressed her hand one last time to Vael's bleeding chest, feeling the ember in her pocket answer the ember in him, twin fires guttering toward the same dark.

"Hold on," she whispered. "Hold on, and I'll open it, and you'll go home."

Then she turned and climbed, the horns screaming below, Vane's men pouring up the stairs, and the spire's summit waiting above her like a held breath.

Behind her, she heard Vael turn to face the stairs alone.

She heard him growl.

And she heard Aldric Vane say, in a voice she had never heard from him before, small and young and ruined: *"God forgive me. Not the wolf. Not the wolf—"*

The first sword rang against the stone.

Ines climbed.
