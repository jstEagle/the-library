## 4. A Door Made of Ash

The summit of the Thornfell spire was not a place. It was a wound in the sky.

Wind screamed across the bare stone, tearing at Ines's hair, at her coat, at the ember burning through her pocket like a coal that had learned to want. Below her, the world fell away in gray terraces—the smuggler's roads she had walked all her life stitched thin as thread—and above her there was nothing but cloud and cold and a silence that did not feel like absence.

It felt like waiting.

She knelt where Vael had told her to kneel, in the shallow bowl worn into the rock by hands long unremembered, and drew out the ember with fingers that shook.

It was smaller than a heart. Smaller than a sparrow's skull. A sliver of dragon-fire, veined with gold gone nearly black, pulsing slow as a dying man's breath. When it touched the open air it keened—a sound below sound, felt in her teeth, in the bones of her wrists, in the hollow behind her ears where the dead had always lived.

*Hurry,* it said, or something like it. *Hurry, hurry.*

Behind her, down the stair, the sounds of battle rose and broke against the stone. Steel on steel. The wet percussion of bodies. And beneath it all, threaded through every clash like wire through cloth, a voice older than any ghost—Vael's growl, deepening, faltering, deepening again. He was holding the stair alone. He was buying her minutes with his blood.

Ines set the ember in the center of the bowl.

Nothing happened.

The wind gusted. The ember pulsed its failing pulse. The stone around it stayed stone.

"No," she whispered. "No, come on—"

*Blood-right,* Vael had said, in the dream where Veyra still had rivers. *But blood alone is not enough. The door was sealed from inside. It must be opened from inside—with what was given freely. With voices. The dead of Veyra sang it shut, little carver. Only their song can sing it open.*

Ines stared at the ember, and understood, and felt the understanding go through her like ice water.

Every voice of the dead she had ever heard. Every relic she had ever carved. Her gift was not a gift at all—it was a ledger, a debt collected over a lifetime, and now the whole of it would be called in.

She thought of Tam, the deaf boy crouched in the shelter of the stairwell door three steps below, reading the vibrations of the fight through the stone with his palm flat against the wall. She thought of old Maren, the smuggler who owed her, who owed her nothing really except the memory of a night when Ines had carved her drowned daughter's name into driftwood so that Maren could hear *goodbye*. She thought of the widow's son from the first commission, the boy who had warned her of soldiers on the pass—if she had listened—

If she had listened, she would not be here. And none of them would either.

Below, the fighting stopped.

Not ended—stopped, the way a held breath stops. And then she heard it: the ring of a sword being driven point-first into stone. Vane's voice, hoarse and strange: *"Hold. HOLD! Let him—"*

Then Vael's growl, closer now, dragging itself up the last of the stairs.

He came over the lip of the summit like a ruin coming home. Silver fur black with blood. One foreleg folded wrong. An arrow stood in his shoulder and another in his flank, and when he collapsed beside the bowl, the impact shivered through the whole spire.

"Ines." His voice was thinner than she had ever heard it, frayed down to the thread of it. "You came."

"Shut up," she said, and her voice cracked, and she pressed her hands into his fur because she could not think what else to do with them. "Shut up and stay alive, you stupid beautiful animal, I'm going to open this door and you're going to walk through it on your own four legs—"

"The ember." His eye found hers, gold going dim. "There isn't time. There is only enough fire left for one thing. You know what it costs."

She knew.

She had known since the bowl refused the ember. It wanted kindling. It wanted voices. And the only voices she had were hers to give.

Footsteps on the stairs. Slow ones. She looked up and saw Aldric Vane climb into the light.

He was ruined. Blood on his cheek that was not his own, sword abandoned somewhere below, and his eyes—his eyes were the worst, wide and wet and staring at Vael as though the wolf were a ghost he had been running from for twenty years.

"Ines." Her name in his mouth sounded like an apology he hadn't earned the right to make. "Don't. Whatever you're about to do—I've read the sealed reports. I know what the crown buried under those doors. But whatever price it asks—"

"It asks everything I have," she said quietly. "And I'm going to pay it."

"Ines—"

"You burned my village, Aldric." Not angry. Just true, laid out between them like a blade. "You burned the carving-house and you killed Master Odell and you hunted me across half of Thornfell. And the whole time, some part of you knew. Knew what was behind the doors they erased. So stand there. Stand there and watch me open one."

She turned back to Vael and took his great head in her lap, the way she had once cradled the skulls of stags before carving them, and she closed her eyes, and she began.

Not a spell. Nothing so clean. She simply opened herself—all the doors inside her, every chamber where the dead had lived—and let them pour toward the ember.

They came like a river breaking a dam.

The widow's son, laughing, telling her about soldiers on the pass. Master Odell, murmuring about grain and grain-directions, about patience, about how bone remembers what flesh forgets. Maren's drowned daughter, singing a lullaby with seawater in it. Every marked soul whose relic she had ever touched, every ghost who had ever brushed her hearing in a market or a graveyard or a smuggler's den—hundreds of them, thousands, a country of voices she had carried alone without ever knowing why.

*Oh,* she thought, as they streamed out of her. *I wasn't cursed. I was keeping them.*

The ember drank. Its veins flared gold. The pulse quickened, quickened, became a heartbeat, became a drum, became—

The stone began to sing.

It rose up out of the summit in threads of light, ancient and patient, and Ines felt her hearing begin to go—not fade, but *burn*, each voice leaving her taking a piece of the silence with it, and she understood at last what the cost was. Not deafness. Worse. Forever after, the world would be only the living. No whispers in the bone. No warnings in the dark. She would carve relics and hear nothing, and die, someday, into a silence that never ended.

She poured faster.

Vael pressed his muzzle against her wrist. "Enough," he rasped. "Ines. Enough—you'll burn yourself out with them—"

"They kept me alive," she said. "Let them keep everyone else alive too."

Light split the sky.

A seam opened above the bowl, thin as a knife-cut, then widened, unfurled, became a doorway tall as a tree—stone edges dissolving into gold, and beyond it, impossibly, *green*. Hills the color of new leaves. Rivers catching sunlight. A valley full of small stone houses with smoke rising from chimneys, and figures standing in doorways, turning, staring, as if they had been standing there for two hundred years, waiting.

The dead of Veyra, singing themselves home.

And then—one by one—the voices stopped.

Not violently. Gently. Like lamps being shuttered at the end of a long day. The widow's son went first, mid-laugh, and Ines felt him go the way you feel a hand slip out of yours. Then Odell. Then Maren's daughter. Then all of them, hundreds, the river running backward into a sea she would never touch again, until the last voice faded and the silence that remained was total and clean and terrible, and Ines Dunmore sat on the summit of the world and wept for the quiet.

When she could see again, Vane was kneeling.

She didn't know how long he had been there. He was staring through the doorway at the green hills, at the people in the doorways, and his face had come apart entirely. "There are children," he said stupidly. "Ines. There are *children* there. The reports said—crown records said the doors led to—" He stopped. Swallowed. "We burned villages for this. We burned villages to keep people from *that*."

"Yes," Ines said.

His jaw worked. Then, slowly, Aldric Vane rose, walked to the edge of the stair, and turned his back on her—on all of them—and faced down the mountain, toward his own men.

"Anyone comes up these stairs," he called, in the flat captain's voice that brooked no argument, "answers to me. Captain's orders. The summit is lost. We withdraw." And then, softer, almost too low to hear, meant only for the wind: "Go home, all of you. God knows somebody should."

* * *

Crossing over was easier than any border she had ever slipped.

Tam went first, hand on Maren's arm, feeling the threshold's hum through his bones and grinning like sunrise. Then Maren, cursing softly at the wonder of it. Then eleven others—marked souls gathered from the roads, bone-readers and dream-walkers and a woman whose shadow moved a heartbeat behind her—each one pausing at the golden seam, each one stepping through into a country that had been erased and was suddenly, impossibly, *there*.

Ines went last.

She paused with one foot in Thornfell and one in Veyra, and looked back once—at the gray terraces, at the smoke of the burning far below, at Vane's straight back. Then she stepped through, and the door sighed shut behind her like a book closing, and the wind of two worlds died at once.

The hills smelled of rain and honey.

They came down out of the light onto a road of white stone, and the people in the doorways came out to meet them—Veyrans, real and breathing, descendants of the scattered and the hidden who had somehow, somehow found their way back to wait. Someone put a blanket around Tam's shoulders. Someone pressed bread into Maren's hands. Nobody asked questions. It was as if they had all been expected for two hundred years, which perhaps they had.

Ines walked until her legs gave out, at the edge of a village where a fire was already lit in a stone hearth, and sank down beside it, and let herself shake.

Warm breath touched her ear.

She turned, and her heart turned over—for there, stretched beside the hearth where the wolf had been, was a man. Tall, silver-haired, dressed in simple homespun the color of his fur, with eyes of the same impossible gold. He was smiling. It transformed his face entirely.

"You're supposed to be dying," she managed.

"I was supposed to be a guardian." Vael settled beside her, close enough that she could feel his warmth, solid and human and alive. "The door is shut. The duty is finished. What remains—" he gestured, at the fire, at the village, at the refugees laughing and weeping among strangers who were not strangers at all, "—is ours to choose."

"I can't hear them anymore," she said. Her voice broke on it. "The dead. It's silent, Vael. All the way down. It's just—quiet."

He was quiet a moment. Then he took her hand—carver's hands, scarred, empty of ghosts—and pressed it gently against his chest, where the second ember had lived.

"There are other kinds of listening," he said. "Start here."

And beneath her palm, steady and warm and utterly alive, something answered.

Ines Dunmore closed her eyes, and for the first time in her life, silence did not feel like loss.

It felt like home, waiting to be built.
