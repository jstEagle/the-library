## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The goats knew before he did. They always did.

Yara Hartwell stood at the fence line with his hands buried to the wrist in the coarse wool of Marigold's neck, and watched the herd lift their heads as one—thirty small faces turning east toward the peaks like iron filings drawn by a lodestone. Their ears swiveled. Their nostrils flared. Somewhere beyond Thornvale's rim of grey stone and older pines, the air was doing something it should not.

"Nothing," he told them, and told himself. "It's nothing."

The valley heard him say that a hundred times a year. It was practically a prayer, and like all prayers in Thornvale, it went unanswered in the specific way that kept a man alive.

He finished the morning milking with the slow deliberation that ten years had carved into him. Everything in this place was slow. The river took its time around the mill stones. The elders took their time over disputes about boundary hedges and stolen apples. Even grief here moved at a crawl, worn down season by season until it fit comfortably inside a man like a river stone fits a boot heel—present, familiar, almost smooth.

Yara checked the sky again. Blue, high and hard, an autumn blue with no business holding weather. But the pressure behind his eyes said otherwise, that faint sweet ache like the moment before a sneeze, and beneath it, deeper, the old tide pulling at his blood.

*Storm coming.*

He pressed his tongue to the roof of his mouth and did what he had done every day for a decade: he swallowed it. There was a technique to it, though no one had ever taught him. You let the feeling rise until it brushed the back of your teeth—you could not stop it entirely, any more than you could stop your own heartbeat—and then you thought of dull things. Fence posts. The price of salt. The way Elder Bram's left eyelid drooped when he lied about the harvest tithe. The storm-sense would sulk, recede, coil up somewhere below his ribs like a snake in a cold cellar, and wait.

Some days it waited quietly. Some days it did not.

Today, the goats were still staring east.

* * *

Thornvale forgot things. That was its purpose, and its mercy, and its crime.

Yara had understood this only gradually, the way you understand you are going blind—not all at once but in small surrenders, each one reasonable on its own. The valley sat in a bowl of ward-stones, old ones, older than the kingdom, older than the roads, set into the earth by hands that had wanted a place where magic could not follow. Exiles came here. The gifted who wished to stop being gifted. The hunted who wished to stop being hunted. And the wards gave them what they asked for: they drank the power out of the blood and, because power and memory are twined like ivy and oak, they drank the memories too.

You forgot your enemies first. That was the kindness. Then your victories. Then, if you stayed long enough, the rest—the faces, the voices, the reasons you had run.

Yara had been twenty when he crossed the ward-line with frostbite in his fingers and his sister's name stitched inside his collar where no warden would think to look. He remembered that much. He remembered the crossing itself: the sensation of walking through a curtain of cold water, the sudden silence in his skull where the storm had always lived, so total that he had fallen to his knees in the mud of the high road and wept like a child.

He remembered her name. Lira. He held onto it the way a drowning man holds a plank—knowing even as he gripped that the sea was patient.

But her face—

He had a ritual for this, performed every evening after supper, alone in his cottage with the shutter closed. He would sit with his mother's storm-glass pendant cupped in both palms and try to assemble his sister out of fragments, the way a shepherd mends a net. Brown hair—was it brown? He believed it was brown. A gap between her front teeth; she'd been proud of it, or ashamed of it, he could not recall which, only that there had been strong feeling attached. She laughed through her nose sometimes, a snorting, undignified sound that she tried to hide and never could.

That detail still came whole. He treasured it precisely because it did. Most nights it was all he had.

*Lira,* he would think, pressing the pendant until its facets bit his palms. *Lira, Lira, Lira.* Saying it like a spell, because it was one. Because names were the last thing the valley took, and he meant to make it work for every scrap of her.

Ten years. The wards had eaten ten years of her, and he could feel the hunger continuing, patient as erosion, working at the edges of what remained. Some mornings now he woke and had to lie still for a long moment before he could remember why the name in his mouth mattered. Some evenings the ritual felt less like devotion and more like reading a stranger's letters.

The goats bumped his hip, demanding movement, and he moved.

* * *

The hail came at dusk, out of a sky that had promised nothing.

It began as a sound—a dry ticking against the bracken, like insects—and then the light went strange, greenish, submarine, and the first stones came down the size of dove eggs, then hen eggs, whistling, shattering on the slate roofs of the village below with a sound like applause from some vast indifferent audience.

Yara got the herd into the byre with wool burning under his hands and hail cracking off the doorframe an inch from his skull. He barred the door and stood in the dark among the frightened animals, breathing their warm animal smell, listening to the world outside get beaten flat.

And the storm called to him.

Not with thunder—with recognition. It rolled over the valley like a hand over a harp's strings, and every string in Yara's body sounded. Ten years of swallowing, and the taste of it flooded back in a single instant: the ozone bite, the pressure singing in his molars, the glorious terrible sense of being *addressed*. The sky above Thornvale was not natural weather. He knew that the way he knew his own heartbeat. Something had torn open in the upper air, something cold and wrong, and the storm was not falling on the valley so much as *testing* it.

His hands shook. Not with fear—with appetite. His fingertips had gone luminous at the edges, or felt as though they had; static crawled up his forearms and lifted the fine hairs, and deep in his chest the coiled thing uncoiled by a single luxurious inch.

"No," he said aloud, and the goats stared at him. "No, no."

He thought of fence posts. Of salt prices. Of Bram's drooping eyelid. He thought of the ledger of dullness, item by item, and clamped down until his jaw ached, until sweat ran cold down his spine despite the heat building under his skin. Outside, the hail redoubled, furious, as if it knew it was being refused.

Then a sound cut through everything: a crack, deep and mineral, like a mountain clearing its throat.

The goats screamed—all of them at once, a chorus of terror—and the floor of the byre shuddered. Yara felt it through his boots before he understood it: a pulse rolling outward from somewhere north and west, a wave of pressure like the boom of a struck bell, and riding on the wave, unmistakable, undeniable—

Magic. Raw and free, spilling across the valley floor like wine from a broken jar.

The wards. Something had cracked a ward-stone.

He was moving before he decided to move, out the byre door into the hammering white chaos, hail bouncing off his shoulders hard enough to bruise, his body drinking the storm's attention without his permission. The pulse had come from the ridge above the old quarry, and he climbed toward it through the shrieking half-light, slipping on ice-slick grass, lungs burning, telling himself he was going to assess damage, report to the elders, do the sensible thing.

Lying, in other words. To himself, which was the one person in Thornvale he had never managed to deceive.

* * *

The ward-stone sat in a hollow below the quarry lip, half-swallowed by turf, exactly as unremarkable as it had been for centuries. It was a grey finger of rock no taller than a man, and Yara had walked past it a thousand times feeling nothing at all, because that was the point of it—wards were felt only in their absence, like health, like happiness.

Now it was broken.

A fissure ran from crown to base, wide enough to fit two fingers, and through the crack glowed a light that was not light—something older than color, a shimmer like heat-haze in deep winter. And pouring through the gap, thin as smoke through a keyhole, came the *taste*.

Yara dropped to his knees in the hail and it hit him like his first drink of spirits after a decade of water.

Memories. Not his own—or yes, his own, all of them, everything the valley had skimmed off him year by year and stored in the stone like a larder. It came back in no order at all, a flood with debris in it: his father's voice saying *you'll be the death of us both, boy*, and the smell of the harbor at Callowmere, tar and fish and rope; a woman's hands braiding cord; rain on a tin roof making music; Lira at fourteen standing on the sea wall with her arms spread into the wind, shouting *make it lightning, Yara, just once, just for me*—

He made it lightning. Just once. Just for her.

The memory arrived whole and blazing and he sobbed aloud, there on his knees with hail drumming his bent back, because he had not known it was gone until it returned. That was the cruelty of the valley: you could not mourn what you could not remember losing. For ten years he had grieved a face he could not picture, and all along, *this* existed—her arms spread, her hair whipping, her delight bigger than her body, the bolt answering him out of a clear sky because love, apparently, was a language storms spoke fluently.

More came. Her fever when she was nine, and him sitting up all night counting her breaths. The debt collectors at the door, and their father's face going grey. The night they ran, and her hand in his, and her whisper: *they'll never take both of us.*

They hadn't taken both of them. They had taken one, and sold the other, and Yara's mind skidded away from that thought like a hoof on ice—because underneath the joy of return there was something else surfacing now, something the wards had pushed down so deep it had grown barnacles: a memory with a bill of sale in it. A memory with numbers. He reached for it and it slid away, not erased anymore, merely *buried*, and he dug with shaking hands at the edges of it while the storm overhead screamed—

And stopped.

The hail quit mid-fall, stones dropping out of the air like a curtain cut loose. Silence rushed in, enormous. In the east, past the quarry, past the peaks, the torn place in the sky was sealing itself, the greenish light fading to ordinary dusk, and Yara knelt in a field of melting ice with his sister's laughter still ringing in his ears and understood three things with perfect clarity.

First: the storm had not come for Thornvale. It had come *through* a hole in Thornvale, drawn by the crack, and it had found him kneeling at the break like a man kneeling at an open window, and it had looked at him. It knew he was here. Weather remembered its own.

Second: the ward-stone was dying, and with it, slowly or quickly, the valley's forgetting. He could feel the leak already widening, feel the stored years seeping back into the soil, into the wells, into the blood of everyone who had paid them away. By spring, half the valley would be dreaming in colors they'd sworn they'd never seen.

Third: he was not safe. He had not been safe for ten years; he had only been *hidden*, and hiding was a thing that ended. If the wards were failing, then the reason he had come here—the reason anyone came here—was failing too. The Queen's writ did not reach into Thornvale only because Thornvale did not officially exist. When it existed again, so would he.

Yara pressed his palm flat against the cracked stone. It was warm. Warm as a living thing, warm as a wound, and through the fissure the light pulsed in a rhythm he recognized with a jolt: the rhythm matched his heartbeat. Or his heartbeat matched it. The distinction had stopped mattering somewhere in the last hour.

"Lira," he whispered, testing the name against the returned weight of her, and it fit differently now—fuller, heavier, a name with a girl inside it instead of a ghost. "I remember you."

The stone flickered. And in the flicker, brief as a struck match, he saw—not with his eyes, he was sure of that, though his eyes were open—something far away and underground. A great dark hall. Rows upon rows of lights hanging in the blackness like lanterns, hundreds of them, thousands, each one burning a soft and sorrowful gold. And one lantern, third row from the left, flickering in time with his heart.

The vision broke. Yara gasped and sat back on his heels, hail-water soaking through his trousers, his breath smoking in the sudden cold.

Down in the village, a bell began to ring—the elders' bell, the assembly call, rung perhaps twice in his decade there. Its voice carried thin and urgent across the darkening fields, calling every soul in Thornvale to the mill square.

Yara rose on legs that did not feel like his own. Far to the south, down the valley road, a small figure was trudging toward the village through the wreckage of the hail—a traveler, a stranger, a stick of a man with a crier's staff silhouetted against the last light.

The valley had begun to remember. And tonight, it seemed, the world had begun to remember the valley.
