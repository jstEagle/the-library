## 2. Proclamation at the Mill

The crier came on a Tuesday, in the season when the wheat went gold and the goats grew restless before the autumn shearing.

Yara heard him before he saw him—the flat, carrying voice of a man trained to be heard across squares and marketplaces, rolling up the valley road like something with wheels. It was wrong for Thornvale. Voices here had learned to be soft, to fold themselves into the hillsides. A loud voice was an intrusion, the way a strange dog among the flock is an intrusion: not yet dangerous, but promising danger.

He was on the mill hill, mending a fence post that the spring floods had knocked askew, when he saw the procession come around the bend below. Three riders. The crier in front on a dun mare, wearing the crown's grey-and-gold even though the crown's reach had never legally touched this valley—wards or no wards, edicts or no edicts, Thornvale had been forgotten by every throne for a hundred years, and its people had worked hard at being forgettable. Behind him rode two soldiers in lacquered scale, their helms shaped like closed eyes.

Closed eyes. Yara's hands stilled on the fence rail. He knew that insignia from his mother's stories, from the years before Thornvale, before the forgetting. The Watchful Rest. The Queen's own hunters' escort. They were supposed to be a story mothers told to make children behave.

They were riding up the valley road in the full light of afternoon.

*Don't look,* he told himself, the way he'd told himself for ten years. *Don't run. Don't react. You are Yara Hartwell, goat-keeper, fence-mender, a man of no consequence whose blood is as quiet as standing water.* But his heart had already begun its old treachery, beating faster, and somewhere beneath his ribs he felt the faintest stirring, like wind moving through a house where all the shutters are closed.

He made himself keep mending the fence. Made his hands work the mallet. Below, the crier dismounted at the mill, where half the valley had already gathered—drawn by the same instinct that draws sheep to a gate, curiosity and dread braided together. Old Marta Tull was there, and the Hedley brothers, and the miller's widow with flour still dusting her forearms. Elder Sorrel Vane stood at the front, as she stood at everything, small and straight-backed, her white braid wound tight as a rope.

Yara came down the hill slowly, leading one of the goats as an excuse, and folded himself into the crowd's edge where the shadow of the mill wheel threw cool dark across the packed earth.

The crier unrolled a parchment sealed with wax the color of dried blood.

"Hear the word of Her Radiant Majesty, Vessia, First of Her Name, Sovereign of the Nine Reaches and the Sea Roads," he read, and his voice did not soften even in the valley's hush. "Let it be known throughout her realm that all persons gifted beyond nature—in storm-calling, in soul-speech, in the binding arts, in any craft of the old blood—shall present themselves to the crown's assessors within sixty days, to be registered, licensed, and set to lawful service."

A pause. The mill wheel turned behind them, slow and dripping.

"Those who conceal their gifts commit treason against the realm. Those who harbor the concealed share their guilt. Any person with knowledge of such a one who does not surrender that knowledge shall be held complicit." The crier lowered the parchment and looked out over them with pale, patient eyes. "There will be no exceptions. There will be no sanctuaries. The Queen's patience is long, but it is not infinite."

The words fell into the crowd like stones into a well. Yara watched them land—one face after another going carefully blank, the particular blankness of people performing innocence. He knew that performance. He'd been doing it for ten years. But watching it on fifty faces at once made his stomach turn cold, because he understood what it meant: they would all become liars now, together, or they would become accusers.

Sorrel Vane stepped forward. "This valley is warded ground," she said. "Old agreements. No crown officer has authority—"

"The wards," said the crier mildly, "are the Queen's concern now. She has found a way through them." He smiled, and it was a courteous smile, the kind you give to someone you have already decided about. "That is rather why we've come."

Behind him, one of the eye-helmed soldiers turned his head—slowly, deliberately—and let his gaze travel across the assembled villagers. Not searching, exactly. Cataloguing. Yara felt that gaze pass over him the way a hand passes over goods at market, weighing, pricing, moving on. He kept his eyes on the middle distance. He kept the goat's lead loose in his hand. Inside his chest, the thing he had spent a decade drowning stirred again, pressing up against the underside of his calm like ice under a frozen pond.

*Sixty days,* he thought. *Sixty days, and then what? Registration. Assessment. And everyone who ever saw me lose my temper in a thunderstorm, everyone whose well water I once sweetened with rain in the drought year, everyone who noticed that hail never falls on Hartwell's fields—they'll remember. They'll have to remember. Complicity has a price now.*

The crier nailed the proclamation to the mill door with four strokes of a hammer, each one ringing across the yard like a small bell tolling. Then he mounted, tipped two fingers to Sorrel Vane, and rode on toward the upper farms, the eye-helmed soldiers flanking him, leaving the parchment fluttering in the warm air.

For a long moment nobody spoke. Then the crowd began to dissolve—not scattering, but dissolving, people drifting off in twos and threes without looking at each other, the way water finds its level. Nobody said *we won't give anyone up.* Nobody said anything at all. That silence was the loudest sound Yara had ever heard.

He stayed until the yard was empty except for him and the proclamation, nailed to wood that his grandfather might have milled, its ink still sharp enough to cut.

*All persons gifted beyond nature.*

His mother had told him, once, what the word for people like him had been in the old court: *storm-born.* Not cursed. Not blessed. Just born with weather in the blood, the way some are born with music or melancholy. His father had hated the word. His father had sold things before—had sold, though Yara didn't know this yet, more than land—to keep the family fed. But his mother had said it like a name, and names were not shameful.

Yara reached into his shirt and touched the pendant hanging there: a teardrop of storm-glass, cloudy and shifting, strung on a leather cord gone soft with ten years of sweat and skin. His mother's. It had been clear as window glass once, they said, before the night she'd used her last storm to hold back floodwater from the lower village, and the lightning had gone through her and out, and the glass had drunk the color from the sky and never given it back. When she died, the elders had let him keep it because it was only glass. Because magic didn't work in Thornvale. Because the wards ate power like sand eats water.

Most days he could almost believe the pendant was dead weight. Most days.

But today, standing under the proclamation with its blood-wax seal, he felt the storm-glass shift against his chest—a slow turn of grey inside the teardrop, like clouds gathering in miniature—and he knew, with a certainty that felt like falling, that the wards were thinning. Had been thinning for years. That whatever the Queen had found, whatever way through she'd discovered, was less a breach than a tide going out.

And when the tide went out completely, everything hidden would stand revealed on the mudflats.

Including him.

* * *

The elders convened at sundown, in the meeting house with its smoke-blackened beams and its long table worn smooth by three generations of argument. Yara was not invited—he was no elder, merely a farmer—but the windows were open against the heat and the whole village seemed to press against the walls of that building like moths against a lantern, listening.

He stood in the dark between the woodshed and the well, close enough to hear every word.

"We should comply," said Tam Hedley, the elder of the two brothers, and his voice had the flat practicality of a man discussing drainage. "We're forty families. Forty. What do we do if the Queen sends a thousand? Die prettily?"

"There are no gifted among us," said Sorrel Vane. "There haven't been for a generation."

Someone coughed. In the dark, Yara went very still.

"That's not quite true, is it, Sorrel," said old Marta Tull, and her voice was dry as seed husks. "There's the Hartwell boy."

The name landed in Yara's chest like a hook finding flesh.

"He keeps his head down," said another voice—Bram Ostler, whose roof Yara had rethatched two winters ago, whose daughter he'd pulled from the flooded ford when she was nine. "He's harmless."

"Harmless isn't the question." That was Tam Hedley again. "The question is what happens to us when the assessors come and find him. Complicity, the edict says. We share his guilt. Is one man worth forty families?"

Nobody answered that. The silence stretched so long Yara could hear the well rope creaking in the wind, and the mill wheel turning its slow wet circles, and his own blood moving in his ears like a distant sea.

It was Sorrel Vane who finally spoke, and her voice was tired in a way that frightened him more than anger would have. "What would you have us do? Fight? With what? We're farmers. We mend fences. We don't—" She stopped. Started again. "The valley protected us for a hundred years. If the protection is failing, then we protect ourselves. That's all compliance is. Self-preservation."

"It's a death sentence," Yara whispered, to no one, to the dark. "You understand that, don't you? You're not saving yourselves. You're just deciding who dies first."

Because that was the arithmetic the Queen had built, and it was beautiful in its cruelty: she hadn't needed to send an army. She'd sent a man with a hammer and a piece of paper, and now the valley would hunt itself. Every neighbor a potential informer. Every friendship a debt waiting to be called in. Bram Ostler, whose daughter lived because of him. Marta Tull, who'd taught him which mushrooms wouldn't kill. Sorrel Vane, who'd buried his mother beside his father with her own hands. They would not betray him out of malice. They would betray him out of fear, gently, apologetically, with tears maybe, and they would tell themselves it was necessity, and they would mostly believe it.

And the worst of it—the part that sat in Yara's throat like a swallowed stone—was that he understood them. He would probably do the same. That was what the safe place had always been: a place where safety mattered more than anything else, including each other.

Inside his shirt, the storm-glass pendant had gone fully dark, cloud churning, and when he pressed his palm against it he felt a warmth there that had nothing to do with his skin.

*Time to go, then.*

* * *

He waited for deep night. Not because he feared pursuit—the valley slept like the dead, and its dogs were too old to care—but because leaving in daylight would mean seeing faces, and he wasn't sure he could bear the kindness in them. Kindness would be worse than betrayal. Betrayal he could hate. Kindness he would have to forgive, and he didn't have forgiveness in him tonight.

He took almost nothing. The valley had taught him that much: own little, lose little. A knife, a flint, a waterskin, a cloak with a hood. Dried fish wrapped in cloth. The small hoard of coins he'd earned over the years selling cheese at the border markets, sewn into his belt where no one counted a man's waist.

He stood a long time in the doorway of his mother's room, which he had kept exactly as she'd left it—blanket folded, herb bundles drying from the beam, the smell of rosemary and old wool. On the sill sat the clay pot where she'd kept seeds. He opened it. Beneath the seeds lay a scrap of oilcloth, and inside the oilcloth, a lock of hair tied with thread gone brittle—fine and dark, his sister's, cut when Lira was six years old, the year before their father sent her away to relatives in the east. The year before she vanished into whatever gap yawned between *sent away* and *never wrote, never returned.*

He couldn't say why he took it. Only that his hands decided before his mind did, closing around the small weight of it the way a fist closes around the last coin in a purse. He tucked it into the breast pocket over his heart, beside the storm-glass, and told himself it was sentiment, and knew it was something else. Some part of him already understood that Lira was not simply gone east and lost to distance. Some part of him, the storm-blooded part that had been waking for weeks now as the wards thinned, whispered that *gone* meant something older and darker, and that he would need proof, someday, of who she had been.

Then he went out into the blue dark and stood in his own yard and looked back once at the house he'd built with his hands, beam by beam, and at the goat shed, and at the fence line he'd spent the day mending as if fences mattered, as if tomorrow were a day like other days.

The proclamation hung on the mill door a quarter mile down-valley, white as a shroud in the starlight.

Yara touched the storm-glass pendant one last time—clouds swirling inside it now, restless, awake, hungry after ten years of drought—and turned his back on Thornvale.

He was a mile up the valley path, walking fast through the silvered grass with his hood up, when the first thunder rolled. Far off, over the mountains he was walking toward, a storm was building where no storm should be, out of a sky that had been clear at dusk.

The storm-glass against his chest had begun, very softly, to glow.

And behind him, from the sleeping village, a single bell began to ring—once, twice, three times—the alarm that every child in Thornvale learned before they learned their letters.

*Someone had seen him go.*
