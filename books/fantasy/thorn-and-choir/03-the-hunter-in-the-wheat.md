## 3. The Hunter in the Wheat

The wheat was taller than a man by midsummer, and Yara loved it for the way it lied.

From the ridge above Harrow Field it looked like gold poured over the valley's last open land, a sea without danger in it. But walk into it and the stalks closed behind you like water, erasing your path, whispering against your shoulders with ten thousand small dry voices until you could not say whether something moved ahead of you or only the wind deciding to be cruel. Yara had walked it every summer of his exile. He knew its lies the way he knew his own hands.

He was cutting a path along the field's western edge when he heard the dogs.

Not barking—barking was common enough, the shepherds' mastiffs ranging after hares. This was different: a rhythm, a cadence, three long cries and then silence, then three again, as if something were counting the valley's heartbeats and reporting them back to whoever held the leash. The sound went through him like a needle finding an old scar. He had heard that cadence once before, ten years ago, on the night the wagons left Thornvale carrying everyone the wards could not protect from themselves.

Yara stood very still among the wheat, sickle low, and let himself feel what he had trained himself not to feel for a decade.

The storm was there. It was always there—a pressure behind his sternum, a taste of iron at the back of the throat, the sky's whole weight folded up small inside his chest. For ten years he had kept it under glass, the way his mother's storm-glass pendant held a little grey squall that never broke. He breathed the way the elders had taught the gifted children to breathe in the last days before the exiles: slow, shallow, thinking of dry things. Dust. Wool. The inside of a stone.

The dogs cried again. Closer. And beneath their voices now, faint but unmistakable, came the creak of leather and the soft iron music of a man walking with purpose through ripe wheat.

* * *

Corvane had been in the valley for two days before anyone saw him.

That was the thing about hunters—they did not arrive the way men arrived, with noise and announcement. They arrived the way weather arrived, quietly, and by the time you noticed the light going strange, the storm was already overhead. He had come through the eastern pass dressed as a wool merchant, and for two days he had bought nothing and sold nothing and spoken to no one, only walked the lanes with his hood back and his face pleasant and empty, listening. Listening the way the valley itself listened, except that where Thornvale forgot, this man remembered everything he heard.

It was the miller's youngest daughter who finally spoke of him, at the evening well-drawing. *There's a man asks odd questions,* she'd said. *Asks which families had the sickness. Asks who left, and when, and whether any ever wrote back.* And old Marta had laughed her gap-toothed laugh and said the crown had forgotten them years ago, why trouble with us now, girl, we're nothing but goats and wheat and forgetting—

And Yara, standing at the edge of the circle with his pail, had felt the cold slide down his spine and understood: the forgetting was not protection anymore. It was a map. Every family the valley had erased from its own memory was a blank space, and blank spaces drew hunters the way blood draws wolves.

So he had packed in the night, and now here he was, one field from freedom, with the wheat whispering around him and the hunter's dogs singing their counting-song somewhere to the north.

He should have run then. Later, in all the nights he spent remembering this hour, Yara would understand that his mistake was stillness—that he stood in the wheat like a man at a graveside instead of moving, and stillness has a sound all its own, a hole in the world's noise shaped exactly like a hiding body.

The wheat parted thirty feet away.

* * *

The man was not what Yara expected. Hunters, in the stories the exile children told, were made of shadow and iron, faces hidden, eyes red as coals. Corvane was broad and sun-brown and ordinary-looking, with grey threading his beard and laugh-lines that seemed, horribly, genuine. He carried no drawn blade. In his left hand he held a length of chain, and at the end of the chain, straining into the wheat with nostrils wide, stood the dogs.

They were wrong in the way the crier's bell had been wrong. Too large for hounds. Their coats were the grey of old ash, and where their eyes should have been there was a pale luminous nothing, like frost on a window, and they did not blink. They did not need to. Whatever they tracked, they did not track by sight.

"Thornvale," the man said. Not a question. His voice was warm gravel, a hearth-voice, a voice built for telling stories to children. "I know the accent. I knew it once myself."

Yara said nothing. His grip shifted on the sickle, and the man's eyes—his real eyes, dark and tired and terribly kind—went to the movement and then back to Yara's face with something that was almost gentleness.

"You're wondering how I found you." Corvane let the chain pay out another hand's width. The ash-hounds quivered, tasting air that smelled of nothing human. "Everyone wonders. They think it's magic, some scrying-glass, some raven whispering in my ear. It's simpler than that." He tapped his own temple. "People leave marks. Guilt leaves marks. Fear leaves marks. A valley full of people pretending not to remember a boy who called lightning out of a clear sky—" he smiled, and it did not reach anywhere near his eyes, "—that leaves a mark big enough to see from the pass."

"I don't know what you mean," Yara said. His voice came out steady. He was almost proud of that.

"No," Corvane agreed. "You wouldn't. That's the ward-work, eating at you year after year. Makes my job easier, most places. People forget what they are, and forgotten things don't run." He unhooked something from his belt then, and Yara's stomach dropped through the earth, because he knew that shape. Everyone raised on the border stories knew that shape: a branding rod, its head a coiled sigil like a thorned O, still faintly warm, still smelling of hot metal and something sweeter underneath, something like burnt sugar, something like burnt souls.

"The edict says surrender," Corvane said. "Surrender is kinder than it sounds, whatever the elders told you. There's work, and bread, and a place in the Queen's new order for the gifted. The choir needs voices." Something crossed his face then, fast, like a fish under ice—something that might have been grief—and was gone. "Or there's this. The brand doesn't hurt much. But it means I find you wherever you go, forever, and the finding gets less gentle each time."

The dogs strained. The wheat whispered. Somewhere far off a goat bleated, absurdly domestic, and Yara thought: *ten years, ten years of dust and wool and stones, and it ends in a wheat field with a kind-eyed man holding a soul-brand.*

His mother's pendant lay against his breastbone, under his shirt. Inside its glass the little grey squall had begun, for the first time in a decade, to turn.

"I'll ask you once more," Corvane said, and took a step forward, and the chain went taut. "Will you come quietly?"

And Yara Hartwell—who had spent ten years practicing smallness, who had taught his own heart to whisper, who had buried his sister's name so deep even he sometimes couldn't dig it up—looked at the brand, and looked at the frost-blind hounds, and felt the glass inside him break.

* * *

Later, he would not be able to say whether he chose it. That was the terror of it, and the exhilaration too, braided together so tightly he could never again tell one strand from the other. Some part of him—the part that had been a boy on a hilltop with lightning in his fists—rose up from its decade of kneeling, and it did not ask permission.

The pressure behind his sternum opened.

It opened like a door in a house that had been sealed since childhood, and everything he had locked behind it came through at once. The sky, which had been high and white and harmless all afternoon, went the color of a bruise in the space of four heartbeats. The temperature fell so fast the wheat crackled with sudden frost. And Yara felt the storm come down the long channel of his blood like water returning to a dried riverbed, filling channels he had forgotten he owned, and he *laughed*—he actually laughed, one wild broken note—as the first thunder rolled across the valley that had been warded against thunder for ten years.

The hounds broke their chain.

They came for him through the wheat, silent, ash-grey, faster than anything with legs had a right to be, and Yara did not think—he *reached*, the way you reach with your whole body for a falling child, and the sky reached with him.

Lightning came down out of the bruised clouds in three white pillars. It did not strike the ground so much as *choose* it, and where each pillar touched, an ash-hound simply stopped—not burned, not scattered, but ended, undone so thoroughly that the wheat where they had stood bowed outward in a ring, untouched, around a circle of fused glass. The smell hit him a heartbeat later: scorched hair and burnt sugar, and he would carry that smell in his nose for the rest of his life.

The third bolt missed. Or the third hound dodged—it was close to him, so close he could see the frost-light swirling in the hollows of its eyes—and then it was on him, jaws closing on his forearm, and its bite was cold, colder than frost, a cold that went past flesh into something deeper, and Yara screamed and drove the sickle into its neck and the thing came apart like smoke held briefly in the shape of a dog.

Then it was quiet, except for the rain.

The storm he had called was already dying; he could feel it draining out of him as fast as it had come, leaving him hollowed and shaking, kneeling in flattened wheat with blood running down his arm from four punctures that did not look like bites so much as *stamps*. Rain fell in fat, warm drops. The bruised sky tore open down the west and let the evening sun through, gilding the ruined circle, the glass, the wet gold wheat.

Corvane stood where he had stood all along.

He had not run. He had not drawn a weapon. He had watched the whole of it with his chain dangling slack and empty from his fist, and his face—his terrible, ordinary, kind face—held an expression Yara needed a long moment to name, because it was the last thing he had expected to see there.

Recognition. And under it, grief.

"There you are," Corvane said softly, over the rain. "Ten years, and there you are." He looked at the circles of glass, at the smoking place where his hounds had been, and something in his jaw tightened. "Do you know what those were? Before she remade them?" He didn't wait for an answer. His eyes went to Yara's forearm, to the four punctures already turning black at their edges, spreading outward in thin tendrils like ink dropped in water. "Doesn't matter now. You've met the brand, boy. Just not the iron end of it."

Yara looked down. The wound did not hurt anymore. That was the worst part—the cold had become a warmth, a low banked heat sitting just under his skin, and he understood with a lurching certainty what it was. Not a mark on him. A hook *in* him. A thread with one end knotted to his soul and the other end spooling out across the miles into the fist of the man in the wheat.

"Every step you take," Corvane said, "you'll pull the thread tight. Every storm you call will burn it brighter. I don't have to chase you. I only have to walk toward the ache." He bent, slowly, and picked up the broken chain, coiling it with the unhurried care of a man who had done it ten thousand times. When he straightened, the sun had come fully through the torn clouds, and it lit his face, and Yara saw—for just a moment, before the hunter turned away—the wet tracks cutting through the dust on his cheeks.

"Run east," Corvane said, without looking back. "The pass road forks at the old shrine. Take the left fork, not the right; there's a warden post on the right fork since spring. You've got until morning before the thread pulls me after you, and maybe a day more before the Queen's riders get my raven." He paused, shoulders squared against the golden light, a silhouette with a broken leash in his hand. "I was valley-born too, once. Long before you. I know what it costs to forget." A breath of laughter, with no joy anywhere in it. "I know what it costs to remember, too. Go on, then. Run."

Yara ran.

He ran east through the wheat with the rain steaming off his shoulders and his blood singing with the aftermath of lightning, and the feeling that carried him was unbearable in its doubleness—terror and exhilaration in the same vessel, the same heartbeat, indistinguishable, inseparable. He had called a true storm. After ten years of dust and wool and stones, the sky had answered him like a dog answering its name, and he had *ended things*, and he was free, he was finally, impossibly free—

—and hooked. Threaded. Marked. Every mile he gained would be a mile the ache pulled tighter; every storm would be a beacon. He had traded the valley's slow forgetting for a hunter's patient remembering, and both of them, hunter and hunted, were walking the same road now, tied together by a wound that warmed like a hearth and would never, ever heal.

At the field's edge he stopped, just once, gasping, and pressed his hand over the pendant at his breastbone. Inside the glass, the little grey squall had gone wild, spinning, and for the first time in ten years it looked less like a prison and more like a promise.

Behind him, far off, a single raven rose from the wheat and flew east, faster than any bird had a reason to fly.

Ahead of him, the mountains waited, and somewhere beyond them, though he could not yet have said how he knew, something was waiting too—a ledger, a debt, a name.

Lira.

The word surfaced in him like a body rising in flooded water, and he did not know yet why it mattered, only that it burned brighter than the brand, and he ran toward it into the dark.
