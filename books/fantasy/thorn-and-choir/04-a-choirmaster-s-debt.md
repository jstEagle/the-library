## 4. A Choirmaster's Debt

The rain in Aldermist always smelled of iron. Sef had noticed it the first night he arrived, half-starved and half-mad with guilt, standing on the Salt Bridge while the harbor lamps smeared themselves across black water — the rain came down through the city's foundry smoke and tasted of blood, or what he imagined blood tasted like, though he had no right to imagine such things anymore. He had made a study, in the years since, of things he had no right to imagine.

Now he stood beneath the awning of a chandler's shop on Tallow Row, watching a young man with a storm-caller's brand-scar on his wrist buy a rope of dried fish and a flint striker, and Sef felt the old familiar sickness rise in his throat like tide water.

He had seen the scar from thirty paces. That was the terrible thing about the work he had done: it had trained his eyes too well. Most people, if they noticed the mark at all, would take it for a burn, an old scald, a rope-scar from some hard year at sea. But Sef had spent eleven years at the Queen's court beneath the mountain, and he had watched that same mark bloom on the wrists of a hundred gifted souls as the hunters dragged them in — the brand that hunters pressed into their prey not to punish but to *bind*, a leash of scar-tissue and old blood that let the one who made it follow its bearer across any distance, through any ward, past any sea. He had set the choir to singing the binding-verse while the brand cooled. He had taught the hunters' cantors the intervals that made it hold.

He knew that scar the way a father knows a child's cry in the dark.

The young man — tall, hollow-cheeked, moving with the careful economy of someone who had learned to take up as little of the world as possible — paid for his fish in clipped copper and did not look at the chandler's face. He wore a shepherd's oilskin gone green at the shoulders, and his boots were valley boots, the kind with the double-stitched welt that Thornvale's cobbler had made for forty years before the wards went up. Sef had sold boots like that, once, in another life. Or he had *heard* of them. It was difficult, some days, to know which of his memories were real and which were the ones the court had let him keep because they hurt less.

*Thornvale*, he thought. *A valley-born storm-caller, walking free in Aldermist ten days after the Proclamation.*

The boy — no, not a boy, Sef corrected himself; a man, perhaps twenty-five, with the kind of tiredness around the eyes that took years to earn — tucked the fish into his pack and stepped out into the rain. And Sef, who had spent four years in this city perfecting the art of being no one, found his feet moving before his courage caught up with him.

He followed the man down Tallow Row, past the tanneries with their reeking vats, past the shrine of the Drowned Mother where fishermen's widows left wreaths of woven kelp, past the gull-guanoed steps of the customs house where a crier was still nailing up copies of the Queen's edict, the parchment already going soft and grey in the wet. *All gifted persons to be surrendered to the crown's hunters within thirty days. Those who shelter them share their sentence.* The ink had run so the words looked like they were weeping.

The storm-caller walked like a man who had read that edict. He walked like a man who had heard it read aloud in a mill, perhaps, by a crown crier with a voice like a brass bell, while his neighbors of ten years looked at their hands and said nothing.

Sef knew that walk too. He had worn it out of the mountain court four years ago, with a stolen ledger sewn into the lining of his coat and the sound of a thousand bound voices still ringing in his ears.

He caught up with the man at the mouth of Cooper's Alley, where the rain came off the roofs in a single unbroken sheet, and said, quietly, "The brand will hold for nine days more. After that, Corvane won't need it — he'll have your scent in the storm you called."

The man turned fast — faster than Sef expected, and with a coiled readiness that spoke of the last two weeks rather than the last ten years. His hand went to the knife at his belt, a herdsman's knife, nothing warlike, and his eyes were the grey-green of a valley sky before hail. Up close, the brand-scar was worse than Sef had thought: it had been pressed crooked, in haste, in terror, and the binding-verse in it was therefore imperfect, which meant it *itched*, which meant the man had been scratching at it for days and driving the leash deeper with every pass of his fingernail.

"You're wrong," the man said. "It's just a burn."

"It's a hunter's brand, pressed by a soul-hunter's iron and sung closed with a verse in the old mode. Third mode, descending, if he was trained in the southern cantoria — and Corvane was, he trained under Cantor Vell himself before Vell drowned." Sef kept his hands visible and empty, palms out, the way you approached a spooked animal or a loaded storm. "You can rub it with ash, pack it with tallow, pray over it. It will still sing to him. I know because I wrote the verse."

The rain filled the silence between them, drumming on the awning, sheeting off the eaves, running in the gutters with a sound like whispering. The man's jaw worked. His hand did not leave the knife.

"Who are you?" he said at last.

"My name is Sef." He said it plainly, without title, because the title was the crime. *Choirmaster of the Court Beneath the Mountain. Master of the Queen's Choir. Voice of the Binding.* He had shed the name like a skin, but the skin was still out there somewhere, walking around wearing his face. "And I need to show you something before you leave this city, because if you leave without knowing it, you will die badly, and worse, you will die *uselessly*, and I have seen enough of that to last me two lifetimes."

* * *

The man's name was Yara. He gave it grudgingly, in the shelter of a rope-walk where the great twisted cables hung in rows like the strings of some drowned instrument, and the rain on the tarred rope made a sound Sef had never managed to notate: a low, fibrous thrum, a chord that no choir he had ever trained could have held.

They sat on a coil of hawser beneath the walk, out of the wet, and Sef took out the flask of plum brandy he carried for exactly this kind of conversation — the kind where you had to say the unsayable and needed your mouth to keep working while your soul tried to crawl out through your teeth.

"Ten days ago," Sef said, "a crier came to your valley."

Yara's face did something small and terrible. "You know that how?"

"Because the criers went to forty valleys and eleven free cities in the same week, and I have been listening to the reports the way a man listens to a fever. Thornvale's elders voted to comply. Three gifted souls were named in the valley's surrender list." Sef turned the flask in his hands. "You were one of them. The other two were a widow in the east hams who lights lamps with a word, and a child of six who makes the well-water rise when he cries. The widow was taken on the second day. The child's family ran for the coast. I don't know if they made it."

"You're very well informed for a man who sells rope-walk gossip."

"I was the Queen's choirmaster," Sef said. "For eleven years. I trained the choir that powers her armies, and I trained the hunters' cantors, and I wrote the binding-verse that is sitting in your wrist right now like a splinter of song, and four years ago I ran, and I have been running ever since, and I am telling you this because you deserve to know exactly what sort of man is offering you help."

He had rehearsed this confession a hundred times. In bathhouses and harbor taverns, in the grey hour before dawn, he had said the words to the dark: *I was the Queen's choirmaster.* He had imagined the responses — disgust, disbelief, a blade between the ribs. What he had not imagined was Yara's stillness: the man simply looked at him, rain-light moving in his eyes, and waited, the way valley people wait, the way people wait who have spent a decade learning that the world does not owe them a quick ending.

"The Queen's armies don't march on bread and steel," Sef went on, when it was clear no interruption was coming. "They march on song. Every soldier in Vessia's host carries a binding-knot at his throat — a thread of the choir's harmony, tied off and worn like a relic. It keeps them from fear, from doubt, from exhaustion. It keeps them from *thinking*, if the Queen wants them not to think. The choir sings the war-march, the knots drink the song, and the soldiers do what the song says. I built that. I trained the first hundred voices myself. I taught them to sing in the old mode, the mode their grandmothers used for lullabies and funerals, because the old mode binds deepest. And the voices —"

His voice failed him there. It had failed him in this exact place every time, the same crack in the same word, and he had come to think of it as a kind of honesty: the one part of the confession he could not polish.

"The voices aren't volunteers," he said. "They're souls. Taken by the hunters, bound into lanterns, hung in the galleries beneath the mountain. A soul in a lantern can sing forever. It can't stop. It can't refuse. It remembers just enough of itself to grieve, and the grief is — the grief is *useful*. The Queen's cantors found that grief makes the binding hold tighter. A joyful soul sings itself loose eventually. A grieving one holds the knot like a drowning man holds a rope." He drank from the flask, though the brandy did nothing anymore; he had drunk his tolerance into the sea years ago. "I stood in that gallery for eleven years and conducted. That is my crime. Not the verse, not the training. The conducting. I heard them, every voice, every night, and I made music of it, and the music was —"

"Beautiful," Yara said quietly.

Sef looked at him.

"I heard a choir once," Yara said. "Before the wards. My mother took us to the autumn festival in the lowlands. I was small. I don't remember the faces, but I remember the sound, and I remember thinking it was the most beautiful thing in the world, and I remember my mother crying, and I didn't understand why." His hand had gone to his wrist without his seeming to notice, thumb pressing the brand. "She knew, didn't she. What choirs were for, in the Queen's country. That's why she cried."

"Your mother was wiser than I was for eleven years," Sef said. "Yes."

The rain thrummed on the ropes. Somewhere down the harbor a ship's bell counted the hour, six strokes, muffled by the wet.

"Why tell me this?" Yara said. "You could have followed me out of the city and never said a word. You could have lied. A man who did what you did —" He stopped. Sef watched him decide not to finish the sentence, and was grateful, and hated himself for being grateful.

"Because of the brand," Sef said. "And because of this."

He reached into his coat, into the lining where the oilcloth packet had lived for four years, through two crossings and a winter in a smuggler's hold and a fever in Vhal that had nearly ended everything, and he brought out the ledger.

It was not impressive to look at. A fat book bound in grey calfskin, its corners worn round, its spine cracked and mended with wire. But Yara went still when he saw it — went still the way water goes still before it freezes — and Sef understood, with a lurch, that the man had *felt* it. Magic ran in him again, unwarded and raw, and the ledger was soaked through with it: every page a record of sale, every name written in the Queen's own hand in ink mixed with the seller's blood, every entry still faintly singing the note of the soul it described.

"What is it," Yara said. Not a question. His voice had dropped into a register Sef recognized from the galleries — the register of a soul that has just remembered what was taken from it.

"A ledger of names," Sef said. "Every soul the court beneath the mountain has bought in the last thirty years. Who sold them, and for what. What price was paid. Which gallery, which lantern, which knot of the choir they were bound into." He held it out across the hawser, and his hand was shaking, and he let it shake; he was done hiding what he was. "I stole it the night I ran. I told myself I stole it to hurt her. That was a lie. I stole it because every night for eleven years I stood in front of those lanterns and I could not tell you which voices had families, which ones had someone left alive somewhere who still set a place for them at the table. I stole it so that someday, someone could read a name and *know*. So that the grief could at least be aimed."

Yara did not take it.

His hands stayed in his lap, curled, and Sef watched him fight some battle in the space behind his eyes, and the rain came down on the rope-walk, and the harbor bell rang the half hour, and then Yara said, in a voice scraped nearly bare:

"My sister's name is in it. Isn't it. That's why you followed me. That's why you knew the valley. You read it."

"I read it four years ago," Sef said. "The first night I was free. I sat in a ditch outside the mountain with rain coming through my coat, and I read it cover to cover, because I needed to know the size of what I'd done, and I found a name on the third page of the ninth year. *Lira Hartwell. Sold by her father, Aldous Hartwell, for the settlement of debts. Bound to the war-choir, second gallery, lantern forty-one.*" He said it the way you pull a splinter: fast, straight, no kindness that would only make it worse. "I have carried that name for four years, Yara. I carried it to every city, looking for anyone it belonged to. And then ten days ago the reports said a storm-caller from Thornvale had killed a hunter's hounds and vanished, and I thought — *Hartwell*. There was a Hartwell in the ledger. And here you are, with valley boots and a hunter's brand, buying dried fish like a man who means to walk into the sea."

The silence that followed was the longest of Sef's life.

Yara's face did not break. That was the terrible thing; Sef had expected weeping, rage, denial — anything but this terrible, glacial stillness, the stillness of a man in whom something vast had shifted its weight. When he finally moved, it was only to hold out his hands, palms up, and Sef set the ledger into them and watched the man's fingers close on the oilcloth like a drowning man's on that rope, like the grieving souls on their knots, and he understood that he had just placed the whole of his redemption in hands that might crush it or might carry it, and that either way he deserved whatever came.

"My father sold her," Yara said. Flat. Testing the words the way you test ice.

"The ledger says so."

"For debts."

"For debts." Sef hesitated, then gave him the rest, because the man deserved the whole weight of it: "The court pays well for the gifted. The Queen's buyers keep a list of what each gift is worth. A storm-caller's soul —" he watched Yara's face as he said it — "a storm-caller's soul is the most expensive entry in the book. But a sister's gift is rarer. The entry says *resonant voice, empathic binding-sensitivity*. She was worth more than your father's debts. He profited."

Yara closed his eyes.

And Sef, who had conducted grief for eleven years and thought he knew every key it came in, sat on a coil of hawser in a rope-walk in Aldermist and listened to a man not weep, and understood that he had never heard anything so loud.

* * *

Later — how much later Sef could not have said; the light through the rope-walk had gone from grey to greyer — Yara opened the ledger.

He did it with the deliberation of a man defusing something, turning the oilcloth back corner by corner, and when the first page came into the lamplight Sef had lit from his pack, the air in the shelter changed. Sef felt it in his teeth, in the old conducting-calluses on his fingers: the ledger's chorus, faint and terrible, every name humming its note. Yara heard it too. Sef saw him hear it — saw the man's head tilt, the way a musician's tilts, involuntary, and something in Sef's chest tore along an old seam.

Because that was the thing he had not confessed yet, the thing at the bottom of the flask, and watching Yara's head tilt to a book's silent song, Sef knew he would have to say it before this was over: that the ledger was not the worst thing he had stolen. That the worst thing he had stolen was the *skill*, the trained ear, the ability to stand in any room in the world and hear a bound soul's note and know it, name it, conduct it — and that four years of running had not dulled it, and that when they came at last to the court beneath the mountain, if they came, Sef would be able to pick Lira Hartwell's voice out of a choir of a thousand, and he did not know whether that made him useful or made him the most obscene thing in the Queen's whole apparatus.

Yara turned pages. The rain kept on. At the ninth year, third page, he stopped, and his finger came down on the entry, and he read it twice, three times, his lips moving, and then he looked up, and his eyes were the eyes Sef had seen in the galleries — the eyes the lanterns kept, grief and purpose fused into one hard bright thing.

"Lantern forty-one," Yara said. "Second gallery. That's under the mountain."

"That's under the mountain," Sef said. "Through the free cities, past the blockade, past the hunters, past the gate where they sell entry in memories. Four hundred miles, if the smugglers are honest, and they never are."

Yara closed the ledger and held it against his chest the way you hold a child, or a wound.

"Then we'd better start," he said, "before the brand leads Corvane here. Because I'm not leaving without her, choirmaster. And if you wrote the verse that binds her—" his voice did not shake, and that was worse than shaking, "—then you're the only man alive who knows how it comes undone. So you're coming with me."

Sef looked at this storm-caller with his sister's name in his fists, and felt the old guilt turn over in his chest like a ship righting itself, and thought: *this is not redemption. Redemption is not something you get to feel. This is just the work.* And he heard himself say, in the voice that had once commanded a thousand grieving souls:

"Then we'll need a smuggler. And I know one who owes me a song."
