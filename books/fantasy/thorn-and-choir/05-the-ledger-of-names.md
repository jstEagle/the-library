## 5. The Ledger of Names

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Yara opened the ledger, and he was grateful for it. A full flame would have been too honest. It would have shown him every line at once, and he suspected there were lines in this book that a man should meet one at a time, the way you meet waves.

Sef had gone out to find them a room above a cooper's shop on Aldermist's salt-stained edge, leaving Yara alone with the stolen thing and the sound of the harbor breathing through the shutters. The ledger was smaller than he'd expected—smaller than it had any right to be, for what it held. Bound in grey leather gone soft as ear, its spine cracked like an old man's knees when he opened it. The first page smelled of tallow and iron and something sweeter underneath, something like the air after lightning.

*Ledger of Names,* said the title page, in a hand so fine it looked printed. *Court Beneath the Mountain. Accounts Receivable.*

Accounts receivable. He read those words four times before their meaning settled into him like cold water finding the seams of a boot. Not prisoners. Not captives. *Inventory.*

* * *

He began at the beginning because beginning anywhere else felt like cheating.

The early entries were decades old, the ink browned to the color of dried blood. Names in columns. Dates. Prices. And beside each price, in a smaller hand, a notation that made his stomach turn slowly over: *source.* Father, mother, husband, wife. Creditor. Landlord. King.

Yara's father had never been a cruel man. That was the terrible arithmetic of it—the cruelty wasn't needed. Their father had been a tired man with a failing farm and three hungry children and a debt to a grain-factor in Lowmarsh that grew each season like something rooted under the house. Yara remembered the factor's visits: the soft voice, the patient smile, the way he always sat facing the door. He remembered his father coming home from one such visit with his shoulders down around his ears, saying nothing, eating nothing, staring into the fire until the fire went out.

Yara had been nine. Lira had been six. She used to stand on the fence rail with her arms out like a heron and swear she could feel weather coming in her teeth—a lie, or a game, except that half the time she was right, and their father would laugh his tired laugh and say she'd make a fine sailor's wife someday, reading storms off the horizon.

She'd been gifted too. Nobody had ever said it aloud. In Thornvale you learned young which truths were load-bearing, and that one would have brought the whole roof down.

He turned the page.

The columns marched on. Names, dates, prices, sources. He made himself read them all—not skimming, *reading*, giving each name its moment, because someone had to, because no one else ever had. Somewhere past the middle of the book the handwriting changed, grew more hurried, more crowded, as if the court beneath the mountain had begun to run out of patience for ceremony. The prices stopped being listed in coin and started being listed in *years*. Years of harvest. Years of border-peace. Years of a rival's silence.

And then, near the bottom of a page dated eleven years past, in ink still black and wet-looking, as if the wound had never been allowed to close:

*Hartwell, Lira. Age 6. Source: debtor (father). Price: 40 crowns, outstanding grain-debt discharged. Bound: choir, third gallery, position 214.*

Yara did not remember standing up. He was standing. The stool lay on its side behind him and the candle guttered in the draft from his own ragged breathing, and outside the shutter the harbor went on slapping its little waves against the pilings as if the world had not just been turned inside out like a pocket.

Forty crowns. His sister. Forty crowns, and the debt was called *discharged*, as if anything about it had been discharged rather than merely transferred—from a man's fields to a girl's soul, from a ledger of grain to a ledger of names.

And the source line. *Father.* Not seized. Not taken in a raid. *Sold,* the way you'd sell a lame ewe, and for less than the ewe would have fetched.

He thought he had known grief. Ten years in Thornvale taught a man grief the way a riverbed teaches water—all the shapes of it, worn smooth. But this was not that. This was grief with edges. This was grief that stood up inside him wearing his sister's face and asked, in her six-year-old voice, *why didn't you come for me?*

He was nine years old. There had been nothing he could have done. He knew this the way he knew the valley's wards, the way he knew the weight of suppression—and knowing it changed nothing, because grief is not a reasonable tenant. Grief does not read the lease.

* * *

Here is what the valley took from him, and here is what came flooding back now that magic ran loose in his blood again, unwarded, unashamed:

Lira at four, asleep against their mother's side, one fist closed around a moth she'd refused to release. Lira at seven, the year after, standing in the doorway of the empty room that had been hers, asking when she was coming back from the place Papa sent her, and their father weeping into his hands where he thought no one could see. Lira's laugh, high and startled, like a bird flushed from cover. The exact weight of her asleep against his shoulder on the walk home from the low pasture, heavier than sleep, lighter than everything that came after.

In Thornvale these memories had been stones at the bottom of a well—present, but dark, and furred with silt. Now they surfaced whole, sharp-edged, lit from within, and each one cut him on the way up. The wards hadn't erased his sister. They had only made her bearable. And now, with the storm loose in him again, she was unbearable, and he found—to his shame, to his strange, fierce relief—that he preferred it. He would rather ache with all of her than rest with none.

Because there was one more memory, one that had never fully silted over even in ten years of warded forgetting: the night before the collectors came for her, when she climbed into his cot without asking, the way she did during storms, and whispered, "Yara. When I'm scared I can hear singing. Under the floor. Do you hear it too?"

He had said no. He had told her it was just the wind in the chimney. He had held her until she slept, and in the morning she was gone, and the singing—if it had ever been real—had gone with her.

Third gallery. Position 214.

She had heard the choir from Thornvale. From *beneath the floorboards of a farmhouse two hundred leagues away*, some thread of it had reached her, because she was bound to it before she was ever taken—or because souls that are going to be bound can hear the binding coming, like weather in the teeth.

His hands had stopped shaking. He noticed this distantly, the way you notice rain stopping. What replaced the shaking was quieter and far more dangerous. It was the feeling he got in the hour before a storm broke, when the pressure drops and every hair on your arms stands up and the whole sky holds its breath—except the sky was inside him now, and it was not going to hold its breath much longer.

* * *

Sef returned past midnight with bread, hard cheese, and a lantern he didn't light, and found Yara sitting exactly where he'd left him, the ledger open on his knees, the candle dead, the room full of the particular charged stillness that Sef, in his years at court, had learned to associate with either great grief or imminent catastrophe. Often both.

"Position 214," Yara said, without looking up. "Third gallery."

Sef set the food down very carefully, as if the floor might be listening. "You found her."

"I found her." Yara's voice came out level, which surprised him. Level the way deep water is level. "Forty crowns, Sef. Our father sold her for forty crowns and a discharged grain-debt. She was six." He finally looked up, and Sef took an involuntary step back, because the storm-caller's eyes had gone pale as the underside of a cloud. "Eleven years. She has been singing in that pit for eleven years while I hoed turnips and forgot her name."

"You didn't forget her name."

"No," Yara agreed. "That's the joke of it. The wards took everything else. Her face, her voice, her birthday, the color of the ribbon she wore to the harvest fair—they took all of it, ground it fine, washed it downstream. But the *name* stayed. Like a hook they couldn't pull out." He closed the ledger with a gentleness that was worse than slamming it. "Do you know what that means? It means some part of me has been holding onto her for ten years with my fingernails, in a valley designed to make letting go feel like mercy. And I let it. I *thanked* it. I called that valley safe."

"The valley kept you alive."

"The valley kept me *small*." He stood, and the air in the room tightened; the cooper's empty barrels downstairs knocked together like nervous teeth. "There's a difference, choirmaster. You of all people should know the difference. You built a career on it."

It was meant to wound, and it landed, and Yara watched it land and did not take it back. Sef absorbed it the way old timber absorbs weather—with a creak, and no complaint.

"Yes," Sef said quietly. "I do know the difference." He hesitated in the doorway, one hand on the frame. "The smuggler sails on the evening tide tomorrow. Saltmarket, dawn, if you want to be part of the bargain. If you'd rather stay here and read that book until it reads you back—I'd understand that too."

"Sef." Yara's voice stopped him. Outside, far off across the harbor, thunder muttered—a dry, distant sound, a throat-clearing, though the sky was clear and starry and had no business producing thunder at all. Neither man remarked on it. Both men heard it. "How many positions in the third gallery?"

Sef was silent long enough that the silence itself answered.

"How many?"

"Three hundred," Sef said. "The third gallery is the children."

Yara looked down at the ledger in his hands—at the grey leather, the cracked spine, the neat columns of the bought and sold—and felt the last of the boy who had fled Thornvale fall away like a chrysalis, and something else stand up in his place, something with weather in it.

"Then we free all of them," he said. "Or we die in the attempt and trouble no one further. Those are the terms. Tell your smuggler."

Sef nodded once and went out into the dark, and behind him Yara stood alone with the candle stub and the ledger and the storm gathering in his chest like a second heart, and spoke aloud to the empty room, to the harbor, to the mountain two hundred leagues north that could not possibly hear him but was going to anyway:

"Hold on, Lira. Position 214. I'm coming, and I am bringing the sky with me."

Far off across the water, the thunder answered—once, softly, like a promise being signed.
