## 6. Saltmarket Bargains

The smuggler-queen's court smelled of brine and burnt sugar, and Sef thought, not for the first time, that every throne he had ever stood before was built on something rotting underneath.

He followed the salt-warden down a corridor of stacked crates that breathed with the tide. Lanterns hung from fishhooks, their light greenish and unsteady, throwing shadows like drowned things across walls crusted white with old sea-salt. Somewhere below them, water slapped against pilings in a rhythm too patient to be natural—the harbor itself keeping time, as if the whole of Saltmarket were one vast instrument waiting to be played.

Sef had spent eleven years in a palace where music was power in its purest form, where a single sustained note could seal a door or open a throat. He had fled that palace believing he would never again hear singing used as a weapon. Now he was about to offer exactly that, priced by the pound, to a woman who trafficked in everything the crown had outlawed.

*Justice outside the law is still justice.* He turned the phrase over in his mind like a coin worn smooth from handling. It was Yara's suspicion he kept hearing, though Yara had said almost nothing since they'd come down from the free cities' road into Saltmarket's dripping warren. The storm-caller's silence had a texture all its own—dense, watchful, the quiet of a man standing in dry grass during lightning season.

The salt-warden stopped before a door banded in black iron, knocked twice, paused, then three times more.

"Names?" said a voice through the wood.

"Sef of the Queen's Own Choir," Sef said, "come to bargain."

A pause long enough for him to count his own heartbeats. Then: "The dead choirmaster. She'll want to see your hands."

The door opened onto a round chamber walled entirely in glass—thick panes, salvaged ship windows by the look of them, each one set at a different angle so the room caught the harbor light and broke it into pieces. Shelves climbed every wall, crowded with jars: pickled eels, candied kelp, things Sef chose not to identify, suspended in fluids of varying opacity. And presiding over it all, enthroned behind a desk made from half a ship's prow, sat the smuggler-queen herself.

She was smaller than her reputation. That was the first trick of her, Sef decided—everyone expected a giantess, a pirate out of ballads, and instead they got a compact woman of perhaps fifty with iron-gray hair bound in netting strung with tiny bells, hands stained to the wrist with indigo dye, and eyes the flat silver of a herring's belly. Her name, if she had one anyone dared speak, was Maren Voss.

"Choirmaster." She did not rise. "They told me you were dead."

"They told me *she* was merciful," Sef replied. "We're both disappointed."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth—not quite a smile, but the place where one might live. She gestured to the stools before her desk, and Sef sat, arranging his ruined hands in his lap where the tremor wouldn't show. Eleven years of royal performance had taught him that much: never let them see the instrument failing.

Behind him, the door opened again, and he felt rather than heard Yara enter—the particular pressure of a storm-caller filling a room, like the drop in air before rain. The boy—he had to remind himself Yara was not a boy, was nearly thirty, was older than Sef had been when he'd first sung souls into lantern-glass—stood just inside the doorway with his arms crossed and his mother's storm-glass pendant glinting at his throat.

"You brought the storm-caller." Voss's silver eyes went past Sef's shoulder. "Bold. The criers in every port from here to the capital have his face nailed to their boards. There's a bounty on him that would buy this district twice."

"There's a bounty on you that would buy it four times," Sef said. "I don't imagine you're eager to collect it either."

"I'm not eager to be *seen* collecting anything." She leaned back, and the tiny bells in her hair stirred with a sound like distant sleigh-bells, absurdly delicate. "So. The disgraced choirmaster and the hunted storm-caller walk into my market asking for passage east, past the blockade, toward the mountain everyone with sense is fleeing. Before you make your request, let me tell you what I already know, so we can skip the part where you insult me with a small opening bid."

She counted on dyed fingers.

"One: you need a ship, because the roads east are crawling with the Queen's vanguard and the passes are sealed. Two: you cannot pay in coin worth having, because exiles carry nothing but grief and lint. Three—" and here her voice dropped, went soft and terrible, the way a harbor goes still before ice, "—you have something I want. Something you took out of that mountain-palace when you ran, and which I have spent eleven years trying to acquire through less direct means."

Sef kept his face still. *Eleven years.* So she knew what he was. Had known, perhaps, since the moment his name crossed her threshold.

"The ledger," he said quietly.

"The ledger." She let the word sit between them like a stone dropped in still water. "Every hold on every ship in my fleet is sealed with crown-wax and choir-binding. Every cargo manifest runs through the capital. Every route I sail is a route the navy permits me to sail, because the alternative is a binding-song that turns my cargo to ash and my crews to puppets. Do you understand what I am telling you, choirmaster? I am the richest woman on this coast, and I am *leashed*. Every crate I move, I move with the Queen's permission, and the price of that permission is that I carry whatever she asks—soldiers, soul-lanterns, tribute—and ask no questions." Her hand closed slowly around a jar of something pale and drifting. "You are going to offer me the one thing in the world that cuts that leash. A song that unbinds what the choir has sealed."

It was not a question. Sex felt the floor tilt beneath his certainty—or perhaps that was only the harbor, breathing against the pilings.

"And if I teach it to you," Sef said carefully, "you understand what you'd be holding. That song isn't a key. It's a blade. In the wrong mouth—"

"In whose mouth, precisely?" Voss set the jar down with a click. "Yours? You gave that song to the Queen's wardens for six years. You sang seals around cargo-holds full of lanterns while the souls inside screamed in frequencies only dogs and the damned can hear. Don't lecture me on wrong mouths, choirmaster. I've watched yours work from a distance for a decade." She leaned forward, and the lamplight fractured across her face through the glass wall behind her, making her seem briefly like ten women, all watching. "Here is my offer. Teach my three best singers the unbinding. All of them, fully, no fragments held back—I'll know if you cheat; I've bought enough stolen choir-training to know what a truncated lesson looks like. In exchange: passage for you, the storm-caller, and whoever else you've collected, east past the blockade, to the last free anchorage before the mountain roads. My fastest hull. My best captain. No questions asked at any port."

"And the ledger stays with us," Sef said.

"The ledger was never part of the price." She waved a ringed hand. "I want the *song*, not the paper. Paper burns. Songs breed."

From the doorway, Yara spoke for the first time, and his voice came out low and rough as wind through wheat. "And when her singers use it to crack the Queen's seals on slave-ships and sell the cargo themselves? Unbinding works both ways, doesn't it. You'd be arming a smuggler."

"Boy," Voss said, without heat, "I have smuggled grain to starving cities the crown let rot. I have smuggled healers past blockades meant to kill them. I have also smuggled things I'm not proud of, because the leash chokes either way you pull it. You want clean hands in this war, go find a temple. They're all burned." She turned back to Sef. "Well, choirmaster? Does the storm-caller speak for you, or do you speak for yourself?"

Sef looked down at his hands. The tremor was worse tonight; it always was before he sang. Eleven years ago he had stood in a marble gallery beneath the mountain and taught forty children to sing souls into glass, and he had told himself it was service, it was duty, it was the law, and the law was the realm. Every compromise had felt reasonable at the time. Every one of them had been a plank in the scaffold he'd eventually fled.

And now here was another reasonable compromise, laid out on a smuggler's desk, smelling of brine and burnt sugar.

*Justice outside the law is still justice.* But whose justice? Hers? His? The kind that came back at midnight wearing your own voice?

He raised his head. "Three singers. Full instruction. And one condition of mine: the first hold they ever unbind will be one of hers—a slave-hold, a lantern-crate, something with living cargo. If the first thing that song does in Saltmarket is free someone, then maybe it stays a key longer than it stays a blade."

Voss studied him for a long moment. Outside, the water slapped its patient rhythm against the pilings, and somewhere above, gulls argued over something dead.

"Done," she said. "Passage departs on the morning tide. Your captain's name is Odile Brann; she has a face like a hatchet and no interest in your politics. Touch nothing, sing nothing, and keep your storm-caller below decks if the navy boards you—there's no bounty in the world worth what a storm-caller's panic could do to my hull." She rose at last, small and iron and ringing faintly, and extended a hand dyed blue to the wrist. "Pleasure doing business with a dead man, Choirmaster."

Sef took the hand. Her grip was stronger than her size suggested, and cold as deep water.

* * *

They walked back through Saltmarket's dripping corridors in silence, past stalls where lantern-fish glowed in barrels and vendors called prices in whispers, because in Saltmarket even commerce conspired. It was Yara who finally broke, when they reached the stair that climbed toward the rented room above the chandlery.

"You didn't even hesitate."

Sef climbed two steps, stopped. The lantern-light from below caught the back of his neck, gray-streaked hair thinning over the knob of his spine. "I hesitated for eleven years," he said. "In the palace. While I learned to call it duty. I'm rather hoping I've used up my lifetime allotment."

"That's not what I mean and you know it." Yara came up beside him on the stairs, and up close his eyes had the flat shine of a sky deciding. "You handed a weapon to a woman who moves *anything* for coin. You didn't ask what else she carries. You didn't ask who else buys passage on her ships. You heard 'smuggler' and thought *ally* because she's the Queen's enemy, and the enemy of my enemy—"

"—is a person with a ship," Sef finished. "Yes. I know how the proverb actually goes."

"This isn't a joke to me." Yara's voice cracked upward, and somewhere in the walls, pipes shuddered—a small sympathetic tremor, the building flinching away from him. He noticed it, Sef saw, and hated it, and pressed his lips together until the tremor passed. Ten years of suppression didn't vanish in a season. The storm in him was always looking for an excuse. "My sister is in a lantern under that mountain. Every day I spend bargaining with people like *her* is a day the choir sings her further away from herself. And you're teaching songs to pirates."

"To *one* pirate," Sef said. "Three singers. One condition. Did you miss the condition?"

"I heard the condition. Conditions are what guilty men attach to bargains so they can point at them later." Yara's jaw worked. "You want to believe there's a version of this where nobody gets hurt. Where the song only opens the right locks. There isn't. I grew up in a valley that taught me that every gift is a debt and every debt gets collected. You of all people should know what happens when powerful people get hold of a song that unbinds things."

"Yes," Sef said quietly. "I should. I do. That's rather the entire architecture of my guilt."

He started climbing again, slower now, one hand on the rail. When he reached the landing he turned, and in the narrow light he looked suddenly very old, and very tired, and very far from the marble galleries where he had once commanded forty voices with a lifted finger.

"Yara. I have asked myself every night for eleven years whether running from the choir was courage or cowardice, and I still don't know. What I know is this: the song exists. I put it into the world. I cannot take it back. The only question left to me—the only question left to *any* of us—is who holds it next. I would rather it be a leashed smuggler-queen who frees grain ships than the wardens who filled them. If that makes me a corruptor instead of a saint, then I'll be a corruptor. Saints die clean and useless. I intend to be neither."

Yara was silent a long moment. Below them, the harbor breathed. Above, through the chandlery's warped roof, the first stars were coming out over Saltmarket, hard and salt-white.

"My father sold my sister," Yara said at last, "to pay debts. To powerful people. With paperwork." His hand rose unconsciously to the storm-glass pendant at his throat. "So forgive me if paperwork and debts and powerful people make me want to burn the whole dock to the waterline."

"I know," Sef said. "I read the ledger too."

"No. You *read* it. I *lived* it." But some of the storm had gone out of his voice, replaced by something wearier. He rubbed his face with both hands. "Teach your singers. Take your passage. Just—if that song comes back to hurt someone, Sef, someday, somewhere—that's yours. Not mine. I want that clear between us."

"It's mine," Sef agreed. "It was always going to be mine. That's what a choirmaster's debt means—you keep paying it in installments until you die, and then someone else inherits the balance."

He turned toward the rented room, and Yara fell into step beside him, and neither spoke again until they reached the door. There, Yara paused, hand on the latch.

"The singer you're training first," he said. "The one who does the slave-hold. Make sure they understand what's inside those lanterns. Not theoretically. Make them listen to one."

Sef looked at him. "That," he said, "is the first wise bargain anyone's proposed all day."

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of low tide, and Sef stood on the deck of the *Kestrel's Debt*—Voss's fastest hull, black-sided, lateen-rigged, her figurehead a woman with a hatchet-face and no eyes—while three of the smuggler-queen's singers arranged themselves before him on the salt-crusted planks.

They were younger than he'd expected. The eldest couldn't have seen thirty. A wiry man with a broken tooth and a voice like gravel in honey; a woman with burn-scars lacing one forearm who hummed constantly, unconsciously, like a kettle; and a boy of perhaps sixteen with enormous frightened eyes and perfect pitch, the kind of voice that made Sef's hands ache with old memory, because he had heard that purity before, in a marble gallery, in mouths that had no idea what they were being trained to do.

He taught them the unbinding in the old way—slowly, note by note, the way you teach someone to handle fire by first showing them the burn. The interval that cracked crown-wax. The descending third that loosened a choir-seal. The final tone, held steady, that let a binding exhale instead of shatter. Three hours of repetition, of correction, of the boy's voice cracking and steadying and cracking again, until by noon all three could loosen a test-seal Voss's people had prepared on a condemned crate.

When it opened—when the wax split along its seam with a sound like a sigh, and the crate's contents spilled out unharmed, ordinary rope and salted fish, nothing screaming—the burn-scarred woman began to cry without any apparent awareness that she was doing it.

"That's the sound," Sef told her gently, closing her fingers around the memory of it. "Remember that. That's what a freed thing sounds like. If you ever use this song and hear something different—something *tighter*—stop. Stop and run."

The boy asked him, in the small voice of someone who has just realized the shape of the world, whether the song worked on people too.

Sef looked at him for a long moment. "It works on everything the choir ever sealed," he said finally. "Which is why you'll learn it last, use it least, and never, ever forget what's inside the glass."

* * *

By late afternoon the *Kestrel's Debt* rode at anchor in the outer roads, and Sef stood at the rail with Yara, watching Saltmarket's crooked skyline sink behind them in the haze. The blockade lay ahead—three days east, if the winds held, a picket line of navy cutters strung across the straits like beads on a wire. Captain Brann had looked at the weather, looked at the charts, and pronounced it "survivable, probably," which Sef gathered was her version of a blessing.

Below decks, Oren slept in a hammock, exhausted from hiding his gift all day, his small hands curled around the ledger as if it were a stuffed animal. The child clung to Yara now whenever patrols passed near; Sef had watched the boy's face track Yara across a crowded market with the naked devotion of someone who has decided, irrevocably, that this tall frightened man is what safety looks like.

"He trusts you," Sef said quietly, nodding toward the hatch.

"He shouldn't," Yara said. "Everyone who trusts me ends up sold or dead."

"Then prove him wrong."

Yara didn't answer. He stood at the rail with the wind pulling at his coat, and the storm-glass pendant at his throat had begun, very faintly, to cloud—a haze blooming inside the crystal like breath on a window, the old sailor's warning that weather was coming. Sef had seen the pendant clear as glass for weeks. He looked at it now, and then at the horizon east, where clouds were stacking themselves into towers with a patience that looked deliberate.

"Your pendant agrees with my knees," Sef said. "Storm within two days."

"It's not the weather." Yara's voice was strange, flat. He was staring at the pendant, and his other hand had risen to his brand-scarred forearm, where Corvane's mark sat under the sleeve like a buried coal. "The brand's been warm since yesterday. Since Saltmarket." He flexed his hand, and Sef saw the tendons stand out with the effort of stillness. "He's close, Sef. He's been close since the city. And if he's close, then the navy knows exactly which hull to stop."

The wind shifted. Out on the water, the light changed—the particular bruised gold that Sef had learned, in another life, to read as the opening bar of something terrible.

Captain Brann appeared at the companionway, hatchet-faced, squinting east. "Reef the topsails," she called to her crew, and then, to no one in particular, to the sky, to the sea, to whatever gods ran blockades: "Here it comes."

Sef gripped the rail. Ahead, the straits waited, and somewhere among the navy cutters a hunter with a brand's twin scar was reading the same wind, and smiling.
