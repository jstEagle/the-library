## 7. Storm Over the Straits

The *Salt Lark* rode the swells like a drunk walking a fence line, and Yara hated every minute of it.

He had never been on the open sea before Thornvale's exile, and ten years of dry valley air had not prepared him for the way the ocean moved—restless, enormous, indifferent. The deck pitched beneath his boots in long slow breaths, and each breath seemed to say: *I have drowned better men than you. I will drown you too, eventually, out of habit.*

"You're green," Sef said, appearing at his elbow with a mug of something that smelled like tar and vinegar.

"I'm fine."

"You're the color of old candle wax, and you've been holding that rail like it owes you money." Sef pressed the mug into his hands anyway. "Drink it. It's foul. It works."

Yara drank it. It was foul. It worked, mostly.

Above them the sails snapped taut as Captain Brann's crew took in canvas, reefing for weather that had not yet arrived but whose arrival everyone aboard could feel the way a dog feels an earthquake. The smuggler-queen's ship was fast, low-slung, black-hulled—a knife of a vessel—and she cut through the straits with the particular arrogance of something built to be chased and never caught.

But the wind had shifted. Yara felt it in his teeth before he felt it anywhere else, a pressure behind his eyes like the first ache of a fever. The storm coming out of the east was not his. Not yet. But it was *there*, rolling up from the open water in a wall of bruised cloud, and it was full of everything he had spent a decade strangling down to nothing.

"Brann says we make the narrows by dusk," Sef said quietly. "If we're through before the navy pickets see us—"

"They'll see us."

Sef looked at him. "You don't know that."

"I know it the way you know a song's last note before it comes." Yara set the empty mug down on the rail. "Something's already looking for us. I can feel it. Like a hook behind my ribs."

He did not say the name. He didn't need to. Both of them knew what lived under Yara's collarbone now, what had lived there since the wheat field at the valley's edge: Corvane's brand, seared into his skin in the moment before the lightning answered him. A hunter's mark, twin to the one the hunter wore. A thread that ran between them across any distance, thinning with miles but never breaking.

It had been quiet for three days. That was the worst part. Quiet meant far away, or quiet meant *waiting*.

* * *

The straits narrowed through the afternoon, cliffs rising on either side in gray terraces where gulls argued over fish heads. Brann worked her ship through the shipping lanes with a smuggler's contempt for the official channels, hugging the shadow of the northern shore, running dark even in daylight—no colors flying, no lanterns lit, just another piece of driftwood to the casual eye.

Yara stood at the stern rail and watched the water they were leaving behind.

That was where he saw them first: two sails, then four, then six, emerging from the haze off the southern headland in a line so precise it looked drawn with a ruler. Navy cutters, white hulls, the Queen's silver crown stitched broad on their mainsails so there could be no mistaking whose law they carried.

"They've made us," the lookout called down, voice cracking. "Six sail, closing from the sou'east! They're flying signal flags—"

"Colors up," Brann said, calm as a woman ordering breakfast. "All of them. If we're going to hang, we'll hang honest."

The *Salt Lark*'s own flag went up the halyard—not the Queen's crown but the coiled serpent of the free cities, and above it, because Brann had a sense of humor Yara was beginning to appreciate, a second banner of plain unbleached linen that said, in letters visible at half a mile: *COME AND TRY.*

"She's mocking them," Sef said faintly.

"She's buying time. Mocking is how she buys it." Yara's knuckles had gone white on the rail. "They're faster than us, Sef. Two of them, at least. They'll have us before the narrows."

"Then we'd best hope the narrows are narrow."

But Yara was barely listening anymore, because the hook behind his ribs had begun to burn.

It started as warmth, then heat, then a wire of white pain pulled tight through his chest, and he gasped and staggered and caught himself against the rail. Sef grabbed his shoulder. "What is it? What's wrong?"

"He's close." Yara's voice came out ragged. "Corvane. He's close enough that the brand knows it. He's on one of those ships—he's *coming*—" And underneath the pain, worse than the pain, something else stirred: the storm in the east answering him like a hound hearing its name. He could feel it now, all of it, every mile of it—the pressure systems stacked like held breath, the cold air sliding over warm water, the whole vast machinery of the sky waiting, patient, for someone to give it permission.

Ten years he had kept it leashed. Ten years of swallowing thunder, of counting goats instead of counting lightning strikes, of pressing his palms flat against the earth whenever the itch came so the ground could drink what the sky would not.

"Yara." Sef's grip tightened. "Whatever you're thinking—"

"The navy takes this ship, they take the ledger." He could see it all with terrible clarity: the cutters closing, grappling hooks, marines in the Queen's silver boarding across the gap, and somewhere among them a man with a scar like his own reading the wind and smiling. They would find the ledger in Sef's sea chest within the hour. They would find *him*, and the Queen wanted a storm-caller alive, which meant Lira's soul stayed in her lantern under the mountain, singing war-marches until the end of the world. "They take Oren's route home. They take everything."

"So we run. Brann is the best—"

"Brann can't outrun the weather." Yara turned to face him, and saw Sef understand one heartbeat too late. "But I can call it first."

"No." Flat. Immediate. "No, Yara, listen to me—you don't know what it costs yet. You called one storm in a wheat field and it nearly ate you alive. This is the open sea. There's nothing to bound it, nothing to spend itself on but water and men—"

"That's rather the point."

"*Men.*" Sef spat the word like a pit. "Sailors. Boys, most likely, pressed into service, the same as any crew. You'll drown them, Yara. You'll stand here and feel them drowning, because that's what your gift does—it doesn't kill at a distance, it kills *close*, it makes you a witness—"

"I know what it does!" The words tore out of him with a crack of static, and both of them froze, because the rigging above had begun to hum, every rope singing a different note, the whole ship become an instrument someone was tuning. Yara dragged his voice back down to a whisper. "You think I don't know? I killed his hounds, Sef. I felt every one of them go. I've been feeling it for a week. So don't tell me the price. Just tell me—if it were your choir down there, if it were your souls in those lanterns—would you pay it?"

Sef looked at him for a long moment. The wind pulled at his gray-streaked hair, and behind his eyes Yara saw the arithmetic of a man who had once made the opposite choice, who had sung souls into chains because the alternative was dangerous, because obedience was safe, because someone else would bear the cost.

"Yes," Sef said finally, very quietly. "God forgive me. Yes, I would."

Yara nodded once. Then he walked to the center of the deck, where the mainmast rose like a spine, and he did what he had taught himself never to do.

He opened the door.

* * *

It came in like a flood through a broken dam.

Every storm he had swallowed for ten years—the hailstorm at the ward-stone, the summer squalls he'd smothered in his sleep, the small daily tempests of a gifted body living ungifted—all of it came rushing home at once, and Yara went to his knees on the pitching deck with his arms flung wide and his mouth open, drinking the sky.

The world changed key.

He felt the eastern wall of cloud turn toward him like a head turning at its name. He felt the cold air aloft begin to fall, felt the warm water below begin to rise, felt the straits themselves sit up and pay attention. The pressure dropped so fast that the sailors' ears popped; Brann's crew exchanged glances and began, without being ordered, to double-lash everything that could move.

"Captain!" Sef shouted over the rising wind. "Your storm is coming aboard!"

"I can see that!" Brann shouted back, and then, to her crew: "Storm canvas! Double-reef! Anyone not lashed in thirty seconds gets lashed whether they like it or not!"

Yara raised his hands.

The first thunder was not sound but *decision*—a crack of intent that ran from the clouds to the sea and back again, and then the rain came, not falling but arriving, a gray curtain sweeping across the straits and swallowing the six white sails whole. Visibility collapsed to fifty feet, then twenty. The wind swung in a single sickening arc from southeast to north, and the *Salt Lark* heeled hard, then righted, then surged forward like a horse hearing the whip, running before a gale that existed nowhere else in the world but here, around this one ship, tailored to her lines like a coat.

"Steer us south!" Yara heard himself shout, and did not remember deciding to shout it. His voice came out doubled, harmonized with itself, with the thunder. "South of the shoal, there's deep water—I can *feel* it—"

Brann didn't ask how he knew. She spun her wheel and trusted him, and the *Salt Lark* carved a new line through the boiling straits while the navy cutters, caught aback by a wind that had turned traitor, fell into confusion astern.

For one bright, terrible moment, Yara thought it might be enough. The storm as shield, not weapon. Weather as a wall to hide behind.

Then the nearest cutter fired a warning shot that the wind carried harmlessly past them, and the brand under his collarbone flared white-hot, and he understood that Corvane was not confused at all. Corvane was *reading* him. Through the mark, through the thread that joined them, the hunter could feel exactly what Yara was doing—and more, he could feel what Yara was *not* doing. Holding back. Pulling punches. A storm-caller who refused to kill.

A storm-caller who could be pushed.

The lead cutter stopped trying to chase and simply turned to intercept, driving straight through the chaos of the squall toward them, guns run out, marines massed along her rail. Behind her, the others formed up in her wake. They would grapple and board, and no amount of wind would stop iron hooks and armed men, and when they boarded—

Yara looked at the cutter's deck through the rain and saw them clearly, the way his gift let him see: forty men, maybe fifty, clinging to ropes in the lurching dark. A boy at the wheel no older than Oren, knuckles white, eyes wide. An officer shouting orders no one could hear. And at the cutter's bow, apart from all of them, a still figure with a scarred throat and a face like a closed door, watching the *Salt Lark* across the water with an expression Yara felt rather than saw:

*Come on, then. Show me what you are.*

"Don't," Yara whispered—to Corvane, to himself, to the storm. "Don't make me."

The cutter fired again. This time the ball came close enough that he heard it pass, a tearing sound like cloth ripped by God, and somewhere aft a man screamed—the *Lark*'s bosun, hit through the shoulder, alive but bleeding.

And Yara Hartwell, who had spent ten years learning to be small, stopped being small.

* * *

Afterward, he would remember it in pieces, the way you remember a wound.

He remembered reaching *up*. Not calling the lightning down but hauling the whole weight of the sky down after it, dragging the storm's center across the straits like a man dragging an anchor, and setting it directly over the Queen's squadron.

He remembered the first strike hitting the lead cutter's mainmast, and the mast exploding into burning splinters, and the ship's powder store—God help him, he hadn't known, couldn't have known—going up in a bloom of orange light that turned the rain to gold for one impossible instant.

He remembered the sea rising. That was the part that shamed him most, later: not the lightning, which was quick and blind, but the wave. He had asked the water for a hand and the water had given him a fist, a swell twenty feet high that picked the crippled cutter up like a toy and set her down wrong, keel-first, and held her under while her people scrambled into water that the storm was already chewing to foam.

He remembered seeing them drown.

That was the thing Sef had tried to warn him about, the thing no warning could have prepared him for. His gift did not kill at a distance. It opened a channel, and everything on the other side of the channel poured through him—the cold, the dark, the desperate clawing, the boy at the wheel going down with his ship because he wouldn't leave his post, the officer surfacing twice, three times, each time finding less sky. Yara felt every life gutter out like candles pinched between wet fingers, and he kept his hands raised, and he kept the storm turning, because astern of the sinking cutter the other five ships were still coming, and if he stopped now it had all been for nothing.

Five ships turned back. One did not.

When it was over—when the last of the squadron fled east ahead of the gale and the straits were empty except for wreckage and rain—Yara lowered his arms and found he could not stand. Sef caught him before he hit the deck, and the two of them knelt together in the sloshing water while the *Salt Lark*'s exhausted crew stared at the stranger in their midst with the particular horror of people who have seen the sea used as a weapon.

"You saved us," Sef said. His voice shook. "You saved the ledger, the route, all of it. You did what had to be done."

"I know." Yara's teeth were chattering. His whole body felt scoured, hollowed, wrung out like a rag. "That's what makes it unbearable, Sef. I'd do it again."

He looked out at the debris field spreading astern in the gray light—spars, barrels, a shattered ship's boat, things he made himself not look at too closely. Somewhere under that water were fifty families who would get no letter, no explanation, only silence and a folded flag. He had used his power well. He had saved everything that mattered. And the cost was not paid by him alone, and never would be, and that was the lesson the valley had spent ten years trying to teach him and he had refused to learn until the sea taught it in an afternoon.

The brand flared again—softer now, almost gentle, almost *respectful*.

Far to the east, beyond the rain, beyond sight, Yara felt Corvane standing at some rail of his own, feeling everything Yara felt, understanding everything Yara understood. And through the thread that bound them, clear as a spoken word, came something that was not hatred and was not threat.

It was recognition. And grief. And beneath both, steady as a keel:

*I'm sorry it had to be you. I'm coming anyway.*

Yara closed his eyes and let the rain wash his face, and did not answer.

Behind him, Sef rose stiffly to his feet, looked east at the empty horizon, and said, very quietly, to no one: "Vhal. We have to reach Vhal before he reaches us. Whatever it costs."

Ahead, through the thinning storm, the free coast waited—gray cliffs, gray water, and somewhere beyond them a city of delegates too frightened to act, running out of time.
