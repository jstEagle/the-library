## 8. The Free Cities Muster

Vhal smelled of hot iron and old incense, and Sef hated it the way one hates a house where a funeral was held.

He had sung here once—years ago, in the days when his voice still belonged to himself. The Council Hall had been full then, its galleries packed with delegates from every free city on the coast, and he had stood beneath the great bronze dome and led them through the Anthem of Concord while the light came down in golden shafts and everyone believed, for the length of a song, that the world was governed by something kinder than appetite.

Now the dome was shuttered against the Queen's spies, and the light that fell into the hall came gray through arrow-slits, and the galleries were half-empty because half the cities had not dared to send anyone at all.

Sef counted heads from the visitors' bench and did not like the arithmetic. Saltmarket: present, sullen. Aldermist: present, frightened. Greyharbor and the river towns: absent entirely. The mountain cities had sent letters of regret, each one longer and more apologetic than the last, each one a small masterpiece of cowardice composed by men who knew exactly what was coming and had decided to be very polite about it.

Beside him, Yara sat with his hands folded and his storm-glass pendant hidden under his collar, and watched the council floor with the expression of a man watching water rise around his ankles.

"They're going to do nothing," Yara said quietly.

"They're going to debate doing nothing," Sef corrected. "It takes them three days. Then they'll pass a resolution deploring the situation."

"And after that?"

"After that," Sef said, "they'll go home and bar their doors, and Vessia's armies will arrive one city at a time, and each city will discover that no one else is coming to help." He kept his voice level. Ten years as a court musician had taught him to keep his voice level while everything inside him screamed; it was the one skill from those days he had never managed to unlearn. "I told you. Fear is a choir too. It only needs one voice to start it, and hers has been singing for years."

Yara said nothing. But Sef had learned to read him in the weeks since Aldermist—the set of his shoulders, the way his thumb moved over the pendant when he was thinking about Lira, the particular stillness that came over him before he did something reckless. That stillness was on him now like frost.

"Yara," Sef said carefully. "Whatever you're considering—"

The Marshal of the day rose, a grey-bearded man from Saltmarket named Ossler, and struck the floor with his staff of office. The sound echoed strangely in the dimmed hall, flat and dead, as though the dome itself were holding its breath.

"Delegates," Ossler said. "We convene under shadow. You all know why. The Queen's vanguard crossed the Ashen Ford eleven days ago. Vhal is the last muster point between her armies and the coast. I will hear proposals."

There was a silence with a texture to it—the specific, suffocating silence of two hundred people each waiting for someone else to speak first.

Then the delegate from Aldermist stood, an old woman with a chain of office and a voice like dry paper. "Proposal," she said. "We sue for terms."

A murmur ran through the hall—not outrage. Relief. That was the worst part, Sef thought. Not one face showed surprise.

"Terms," Yara repeated under his breath.

"She'll grant them," Sef murmured back. "That's how she works. She lets the first cities surrender gently. She even keeps their councils, at first. It makes the next cities surrender faster." He had watched it happen from inside the palace, had seen the maps in the war-room grow darker province by province, had sung at the celebrations. "By the time she breaks her word—and she always breaks her word—there's no one left to resist."

On the floor, a young delegate from the fishing towns got to his feet. Sef recognized her vaguely—Denna Corr, salt-stained coat, no chain of office, elected by people who owned nothing but boats. She looked out at the assembled wealth and power of the free coast and said, in a voice that carried despite its rawness:

"You're all going to kneel. You know that? Every one of you. And she'll take your children for the choir anyway, because she always does, and you'll have handed her the rope yourselves."

"Delegate Corr—" Ossler began.

"How many?" she demanded. "How many names has she taken so far? We've heard the numbers out of the occupied provinces. Whole villages. They mark the doors. Do you think a treaty changes what she eats?"

"Order," Ossler said, and struck the floor again, and the hall settled back into its grave-clothes of procedure, and Sef felt Yara move beside him.

Not stand. Not yet. Just—a gathering, the way air gathers before it falls.

"Don't," Sef said, very low.

"They need to see it."

"No." Sef turned, and took Yara's wrist, and felt under his fingers the faint tremor there, the hum of a man carrying weather in his blood. "Listen to me. I have stood in courts where men did exactly what you're about to do. I have seen it end well exactly never. These people aren't yours to save by force. If you call a storm in this hall, you won't galvanize them—you'll terrify them, and terrified councils do the Queen's recruiting for her. They'll hand you over within the week just to make the sky go quiet again."

Yara looked at him. In the gray light his eyes were pale as the underside of clouds, and there was something in them Sef recognized from mirrors—from the years when he had believed that if he could only sing loudly enough, purely enough, the crown would have to listen.

"They're already dead," Yara said. "You said it yourself. One city at a time. The only thing they haven't decided is whether to die kneeling." His thumb moved over the pendant. "My sister knelt. Someone sold her, and she went, and it didn't save her. Kneeling doesn't save anyone, Sef. It just decides who gets to watch."

"That's not—"

But he was already standing, and the hall, trained by centuries of protocol to notice disruption, noticed him.

* * *

Later—much later, in the long bad night that followed—Sef would try to reconstruct exactly how it happened, and would fail, because disasters never announce their hinges. There was no single moment when the day tipped. There was only the delegate from Aldermist droning through the terms of capitulation, clause by clause, and Yara Hartwell walking down the steps from the visitors' bench with his hands open at his sides, and the wardens at the doors deciding, wrongly, that he was some clerk with a message.

"Delegate," Ossler said, irritated. "The visitors' gallery is not—"

"I'm not a delegate," Yara said. "I'm what your treaties are for."

Murmurs. Ossler's staff came up. "Name yourself or be removed."

And Sef, rising too late, reaching the top step too late, heard Yara say: "My name is Yara Hartwell. Ten years ago I was exiled from Thornvale for calling storms. The Queen has promised a bounty for my head since spring. I am standing in your hall, and I am not hiding anymore."

The silence that followed was different from the other silences. Sharper. Two hundred faces turning, and among them Sef saw the whole spectrum play out in seconds—fear, contempt, hunger, and here and there, small and startled, hope.

"Guards," said the delegate from Aldermist.

"No one touch him," Denna Corr said, and her hand was on a knife, and the guards, who were paid by the council but lived in the fishing quarter, hesitated in the way hired men hesitate when the room splits.

Yara walked to the center of the floor. The gray light from the arrow-slits fell across him in bars, and Sef thought, absurdly, of a man standing in a cage of his own choosing.

"You want terms," Yara said. His voice wasn't loud. It didn't need to be; the dome gave it back doubled. "Here are hers. Surrender, and she takes your gifted children for her choir. Resist alone, and she burns you one at a time. Those are the only futures she's offering. But there's a third thing, and it's the one thing she fears, and none of you have ever seen it, so you don't believe in it."

"Blasphemy and theater," someone called from the right-hand benches.

"Perhaps," Yara said. And closed his eyes.

Sef felt it before anything visible happened—a pressure in the ears, a taste like copper and rain, the old animal knowledge that lives below thought in every creature that has ever stood under open sky. The shutters over the high windows began to rattle. Dust sifted down through the arrow-slit light, turning the gray beams to moving gold.

Outside, beyond the dome, beyond the walls, the afternoon sky over Vhal went dark as a bruise.

Wind found the hall through every gap and seam at once, moaning along the galleries, snatching at banners two hundred years old. Somewhere a shutter tore loose and clattered away over the rooftops. The delegates were on their feet now, all of them, gripping benches, and above the roar Sef heard Yara's voice, calm as a lake:

"The Queen's armies march under her banner and bind themselves with her songs. Every soldier in her vanguard wears a binding. Every siege-engine is warded. And wards"—the thunder came then, rolling over the city, close enough to feel in the floor, in the teeth, in the hollow of the chest—"wards are only songs, and songs can be broken."

He opened his eyes. Lightning walked the sky beyond the arrow-slits, white and enormous, and in its light his face looked carved, older than his years, terrible and grief-stricken at once.

"I am one man," he said. "One exile with a stolen ledger and ten lost years. And I can do this. Imagine what you could do—all of you—if you stopped negotiating with the thing that means to eat you."

Then he let it go—or reined it in, rather, pulled the storm back through his skin the way a rider draws a horse to a halt, and the wind died, and the light returned, ordinary and gray, and the dust settled on two hundred stunned faces like ash.

In the ringing quiet, Denna Corr started to laugh—a short, disbelieving bark—and then the delegate from Greyharbor's proxy, who had arrived late and silent, stood up and said, "Seconded. Whatever the motion is. Seconded."

* * *

They should have left that night. Sef knew it even as the hall erupted—even as delegates crowded the floor shouting over one another, even as Ossler pounded his staff and was ignored, even as Denna Corr clasped Yara's arm like a drowning woman and the old woman from Aldermist stared at him with an expression caught between horror and conversion. Even then, in the first flush of it, Sef stood at the edge of the crowd and felt the cold truth settle into him the way cold settles into stone.

Hope was a beacon. Beacons were visible from very far away.

He found Yara afterward in the council antechamber, being fed wine and questions by a dozen delegations at once, and pulled him aside by the sleeve into the windowless corridor beyond, where the torches burned behind iron cages and the stones sweated with the memory of the storm.

"Well?" Yara said. He was flushed, alive, lit from inside. Sef hadn't seen him look like this since the straits, and never for longer than a moment. "You were wrong, Sef. Look at them. They're talking about mustering. About a joint fleet. Corr says the fishing towns can put out forty ships in a month—"

"I heard."

"They needed to see it. I told you."

"Yes." Sef leaned against the wall and rubbed his eyes, and when he spoke again he made himself say it plainly, without music, without the courtier's softening. "And so did everyone else. Yara—there are spies in that hall. There are always spies in a hall like that; Vessia buys them by the pound, and most of them believe they're patriots. By tonight there will be a description of your face in a courier's saddlebag. By tomorrow it will be in Corvane's hands. You didn't just show them hope. You showed the Queen exactly where we are, and exactly what you are, and exactly how much you're worth to her crown."

Yara's flush faded slowly, like heat leaving stone. "So we leave tonight."

"So we leave tonight," Sef agreed. "And we pray the council moves faster than her cavalry, which—" he laughed, and it came out bitter, "—which is a prayer I have offered before, in a different life, to a god who has never once answered me."

They gathered their things in the lodging over the chandlery where the council had put them up: the ledger wrapped in oilcloth, the few clothes, the pendant. Oren was asleep on the pallet by the wall—he had insisted on coming to Vhal, had refused with the ferocious dignity available only to children to be left behind in Saltmarket—and Sef paused to pull the blanket higher over the boy's shoulder. Oren stirred and muttered something about ships.

Below, in the street, the city was loud with argument. Sef could hear it through the shutters—voices in doorways, in taverns, on corners, the whole city chewing on what it had seen. Some of the voices were afraid. Some were hungry. A few, here and there, were singing—not any song he knew, just noise, celebration, the sound people make when the sky has done something impossible over their heads.

"It worked," Yara said quietly. He was watching the street through the gap in the shutters, and the look on his face was the one Sef feared most: not triumph, not doubt, but certainty. "Whatever happens next—it worked. They'll remember tonight."

"Everyone remembers tonight," Sef said. "That's the problem."

He was shrugging into his coat when the knock came—three quick raps, soft, coded in the rhythm smugglers used. He froze. Yara's hand went under his collar to the pendant.

Sef crossed the room and lifted the latch.

Denna Corr slipped inside, wet to the shoulders, breathing hard, and the look on her face emptied the room of everything warm.

"Riders," she said. "Queen's outriders, maybe thirty, at the Ashgate road—they came fast, no baggage, no herald. They're asking for a storm-caller by name." She swallowed. "Your name, Hartwell. They have your name."

Behind her, through the shutters, far off toward the eastern hills, lightning flickered—and Sef, who had spent his life listening, heard what it was saying.

Not weather. Answer.
