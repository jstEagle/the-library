## 9. What the Brand Remembers

The archives of Vhal smelled of dust and tallow and the slow rot of paper that no one had loved in a hundred years.

Yara had come down alone, after the council adjourned into its third day of cowardice dressed as deliberation. Sef was upstairs charming delegates; Oren was asleep in a corner of the delegation hall with his head on a rolled cloak, dreaming whatever gifted children dreamed when they had run too far too fast. And Yara—Yara wanted one hour among the old maps of the mountain roads, because if the council would not act, he would find his own way beneath Thornmount, ledger or no ledger, allies or none.

He had not expected to be followed. He should have. The brand on his forearm had been humming all evening, a low heat like a coal wrapped in linen, and he had told himself it was only the archives' closeness, only the candle he carried guttering in stale air. But brands did not lie. Corvane's mark remembered its maker, and it had been singing his approach for an hour before Yara finally heard the door at the far end of the gallery ease shut behind him.

He did not turn around. He set the candle down on the reading table instead, slowly, and let the light pool across the map he'd unrolled: the old mining road, the switchbacks below the snowline, the gate marked with a warden's sigil faded almost to nothing.

"You read maps like a man planning a funeral," said Corvane.

His voice was exactly as Yara remembered it from the wheat field—the flat calm of deep water, the voice of a man who had made peace with what he was. Yara turned. The hunter stood between two shelves of bound decrees, and the candlelight did strange things to him: threw his shadow long up the shelves like something hanged, caught the silver chain at his throat where three hound-teeth hung, glinted off the brand-iron holstered at his hip—the twin of the scar Yara carried under his sleeve.

"You're alone," Yara said.

"I'm always alone. It's the nature of the work." Corvane stepped forward, unhurried, hands loose at his sides. "Your choirmaster is upstairs telling the Vhaline delegation that the Queen's armies are three weeks out when they are nine days out. Your smuggler friends have already weighed anchor. And you're down here memorizing a road that hasn't been walked since my grandmother's time."

"Then you know I won't run."

"No," Corvane agreed. "You won't. That's the problem with you valley-born. We never learned how."

*We.* The word landed strangely, and Yara felt his breath go shallow. He kept his hand near the candle, near the letter-opener lying beside it—a thin blade of blackened steel, more ornament than weapon, but steel nonetheless. "You've come to take me. The Queen wants me alive. So they say."

"The Queen wants your soul alive. What happens to the rest of you is a matter of some flexibility." Corvane stopped at the edge of the candlelight, and this close Yara could see him properly for the first time—not the silhouette from the wheat, not the shape of terror, but a man perhaps fifty years old, gray at the temples, with a face weathered the way faces got in high valleys, wind-scoured and narrow. A face Yara knew the shape of without knowing why. "But I haven't come to take you. Not yet. I've come to offer you the only mercy anyone is going to offer you, so I'd listen."

Yara said nothing. The brand on his arm burned steady now, patient as a held note.

"Surrender the ledger," Corvane said. "Give it to me tonight, walk out of Vhal before dawn, and the Queen will spare the free cities a season. One full turning—no raids, no levies, no hunters crossing the Salt Road. Her vanguard pulls back to the Ashford line. You'll have until first snow to decide what kind of corpse you want to be."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then Vessia takes Vhal the way she took Harrowfen. You've seen what's left of Harrowfen?" He didn't wait for an answer. "No. Of course you haven't. Nobody has. That's rather the point."

* * *

Yara made himself breathe. In through the nose, the way he'd taught Oren on the mining road, the way he taught himself every morning of ten years in Thornvale: *the storm is not the anger, the anger is not the storm.* The candle flame leaned toward Corvane as though drawn, and Yara realized with a cold start that the air in the room was moving—subtly, wrongly, a slow spiral centered on him. His gift waking without permission, the way it had begun to ever since the hailstorm broke the ward-stone, ever since magic came flooding back into him like blood into a sleeping limb.

"Control yourself," Corvane said quietly. "If you call lightning in here, you burn a thousand years of records and half the city above us, and the deal dies with them."

"You think I care about the deal?"

"I think you care about the free cities. I think that's why you stood up in front of that council and set yourself on fire like a signal beacon." Something moved at the corner of Corvane's mouth—not quite a smile, not quite grief. "That was either the bravest or the stupidest thing I've watched a gifted man do in twenty years of hunting them. Possibly both."

"It worked. Half those delegates found their spines."

"The other half sent word to the Queen's garrison at Ashford within the hour. You didn't galvanize Vhal, storm-caller. You divided it. And divided cities fall faster than frightened ones." Corvane let that sit in the dusty air. Then he reached slowly—telegraphing every inch of it, the way a man does when he doesn't want to die—and drew something from inside his coat. Not a weapon. A folded square of oilcloth, worn soft at the creases.

He laid it on the reading table between them and stepped back.

"What is that?"

"Open it."

Yara hesitated. Every instinct honed by ten years of hiding told him not to touch anything a hunter offered. But the brand on his arm had gone quiet—quieter than it had been in weeks, as though proximity to its maker soothed it—and there was something in Corvane's stillness that was not the stillness of ambush. It was the stillness of a man standing at a grave.

He unfolded the oilcloth.

Inside lay a pendant on a rotted leather cord: a small glass teardrop, clouded white, with a fleck of iron suspended at its heart. Storm-glass. Valley-made. The kind the goat-tenders and orchard-keepers of Thornvale wore against their chests to warn them of weather coming over the ridgeline—the kind Yara's mother had worn, the kind whose twin hung even now against Yara's own skin, hidden beneath his shirt.

The floor seemed to tilt. He gripped the table's edge.

"Where," he said, and his voice came out sanded rough, "did you get that."

"My mother's," Corvane said. "She was born in Thornvale. Lower terraces, near the mill race. Before the wards went up, before the valley became what it is. She was gifted—small things, weather-sense, a little calling. Enough to matter when the purges started. Enough to run." He looked at the pendant with an expression Yara could not bear to look at directly, so he looked at the glass instead, at the iron fleck frozen mid-fall inside it. "She ran north with a man she'd married too young, carrying a baby, and she made it as far as Aldermist before the fever took her. I was raised on charity and crown bread. When the Queen's choir-school opened its doors to orphans, I sang for my supper. I was good at it. They noticed."

"You were choir," Yara said. The word tasted like ash. "You trained under—"

"Under Sef Arden. Yes." Corvane's eyes flickered, the first true emotion Yara had seen cross them. "The great disgraced master. The man who fled his complicity. Ask him sometime what the third-year exercises were. Ask him what we sang to teach the lanterns obedience." He paused. "I left the choir before the worst of it. I told myself that mattered. Then I joined the hunt, and I told myself that mattered less. You see how it goes. Every year the story gets simpler, until one day it's just: *I chose the crown.* Four words. Very tidy. Fits on a coin."

Yara's hand had drifted, without his deciding it, to the pendant beneath his own shirt. His mother's storm-glass. The last thing he'd packed in the dark before fleeing Thornvale, the night the elders voted to give him up. He thought of the elders' faces in the mill's lamplight—Marta Ostrow who had mended his coats, old Bren who had taught him to shear—faces he had loved, choosing the safe thing, choosing the valley's silence over his life. And here stood what a man became when he made the opposite choice. When the silence broke him instead of holding him.

"Why show me this?" Yara asked. "Why tell me any of it? You could have taken me by now. You could have burned the archive door and called the garrison."

"Because I need you to understand the offer," Corvane said. "Not the terms. Anyone can recite terms. I need you to understand what refusing costs, because I paid it once, blind, and I've spent thirty years pretending the receipt wasn't real." He tapped the table beside the pendant, once, softly. "A season, storm-caller. The cities live a season. You take the ledger, you take the girl's name out of it—" Yara flinched; Corvane saw, and did not pretend he hadn't— "yes, I know what's written in it, I've read every page of that book, it was mine to guard for six years—you take her name and you run somewhere the wards are thick, and you live. She stays in the choir. The choir keeps singing. The armies keep marching. But a season, Yara Hartwell. A season is four thousand people in Vhal who don't burn. A season is the Saltmarket children who don't learn what Harrowfen learned. Is your sister worth more than that? Truly? More than all of them?"

It was a knife of a question, and he had aimed it well.

Yara looked down at the ledger's weight under his coat—he'd carried it against his ribs since Aldermist, since the candlelit night when he'd run his finger down columns of sold souls and found *Lira Hartwell, age eleven, sold by Toren Hartwell of Thornvale, debts of grain and wool, price: two mares and remission of levy.* Two mares. His sister, who had taught him which clouds meant hail, who had laughed at thunder, who had been eleven—two mares and a forgiven tax bill.

And then he thought of Oren asleep upstairs with his fists curled, a boy whose gift would get him hunted the moment this season ended anyway. A season bought with the ledger was a season bought with Lira's continued captivity, and seasons ended, and the hunt would begin again with better lists and sharper hunters, and every gifted child in every free city would be a column in a book.

"No," Yara said.

Corvane waited.

"She's not worth more than them. None of us is worth more than any of us—that's the whole rotten arithmetic the Queen built her crown on, souls weighed against souls, and everyone pays except her." Yara pulled the ledger out and held it, both hands, the way he'd held his sister's lantern would feel someday, though he didn't know that yet. "But it isn't a choice between her and them. That's the lie you brought me. The moment I hand you this book, the choice is over forever. The choir sings until the mountain crumbles. There's no season after this one, Corvane—there's just the long quiet where everyone forgot there was ever another way. I've lived in that quiet. Ten years of it. The valley taught me forgetting like a mother tongue." His voice cracked and he let it. "I'm done being fluent."

The air in the archive had gone taut as drumskin. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, unnaturally straight, and frost feathered across the window glass at the gallery's end despite the summer night. Somewhere above them a shutter banged. The building itself seemed to lean away from what Yara was becoming in that moment—or toward it.

Corvane watched the frost creep. He did not reach for his iron.

"There it is," he said softly. "There's the storm. Do you know what I felt, in the wheat field, when you killed my hounds? Everyone assumes I felt rage. I felt homesick." He laughed, short and terrible. "Ten years I told myself the valley was a soft place for soft people, a place that deserved what forgetting did to it. Then you called lightning down out of a clear sky and every hair on my arms stood up and I was eight years old again, watching storm-fronts roll over the lower terraces, before I knew what I'd lost." He picked up his mother's pendant from the table, folded the oilcloth around it with careful hands, and put it away inside his coat. "That's what the brand remembers, storm-caller. That's what it's been humming about since I crossed into Vhal. Not you. Home."

"Then come home," Yara said. "Put down the iron. Help us."

Something crossed Corvane's face then—something fast and enormous, a door opening onto a lit room—and then it closed, and what remained was older and wearier than before.

"I can't. You don't come back from four words on a coin. But—" and here he moved, quick, setting something else on the table, a slip of paper no bigger than a prayer-card, "—when you go under the mountain, and you will go, because men like us always go—the warden rotations change at the turn of each watch-bell. The east galleries are understaffed during the requiem hours. The Queen sleeps badly and her wardens sleep worse, but the lantern-keepers drink, and the ones who drink keep the keys to the lower stairs." He stepped back into the shadow of the shelves. "I never gave you that. If it's found I gave you that, I die badly. Remember that I died badly for it, if it comes to that."

"Corvane." Yara's voice caught him at the edge of the dark. "The wheat field. The hounds. Did you—"

"They were going to open your throat while I read you your rights. I loosed them early so you'd have a chance to run. Three animals, storm-caller. It's not absolution. It's arithmetic." His shadow shifted along the shelves, tall and hanged-looking. "One more thing. The Queen knows about the child. The gifted boy who travels with you. She has a use for young gifts—they bind cleaner. Whatever you do under that mountain, however it ends—" a pause, and for one heartbeat the hunter's voice was not a hunter's at all, but a valley boy's, thin and far away, "—don't let her get her hands on the boy."

Then the far door opened and shut, soft as a page turning, and Yara was alone with the candle and the frost and the maps of a road that hadn't been walked in a hundred years.

* * *

He stood a long time without moving. The frost on the window melted slowly, running down the panes in bright threads, and the candle recovered its ordinary flicker, and the archive resumed being merely an archive, dust and tallow and dead men's decrees.

On the table lay the prayer-card. Yara picked it up. On one side, in a firm clerk's hand, was a list: watch-bells, gallery names, a warden's count that changed at odd hours, a notation—*requiem, third bell, east*—that might have been a hymn schedule or might have been the most dangerous sentence ever written in Vhal. On the other side, in different ink, older, blotted, was a single line that had nothing to do with wardens at all:

*Lower terraces, past the mill race. The plums ripen late but they're sweeter for it.*

Valley writing. Someone's memory of home, kept the way exiles keep things—folded small, carried close, taken out in the dark where no one could see a grown man missing a hillside.

Yara closed his fist around it until the edges bit.

Upstairs, faintly, he could hear Sef's voice rising and falling in the delegation hall, spinning persuasion out of guilt and hope, and beneath it, barely, Oren's laugh at something someone had said—a child's laugh, unguarded, in a city with nine days left. Nine days, or a season, or forever; the arithmetic changed depending on what a man was willing to spend.

Yara tucked the prayer-card next to the ledger, over his heart, where the brand pulsed its low warm song. Then he snuffed the candle, took the map of the mining road, and climbed toward the light, already composing in his head the words he would have to say to Sef: *We leave tomorrow. And I know how we get in.*

Behind him, in the dark, the frost finished melting, and the last drop ran down the glass like the valley weeping for all its children, scattered and crowned and sold, coming home at last the hard way.
