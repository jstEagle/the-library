## 10. Ashes of Vhal

Sef had learned to read the weather in the faces of councils. He had sat in enough marble chambers in the Queen's capital to know that when powerful men begin speaking of *prudence*, they have already decided to do nothing. The council of Vhal confirmed his education on the third day of debate, when the eldest delegate—a woman with a voice like dry parchment and a chain of office older than the city walls—rose and declared that Vhal's neutrality was its shield, and that sheltering storm-callers would be an act of war.

Yara had stood before them for two hours. He had shown them the ledger. He had read aloud the names of their own citizens' children, sold south beneath the mountain, bound into the choir that marched behind Vessia's banners. He had sung—not the counter-song, not yet, but something small and human, a valley lullaby his mother had taught him—and for a moment the chamber had gone still as held breath.

Then the parchment-voiced delegate had said, gently, terribly: *The Queen has not yet asked anything of Vhal.*

*She will ask everything,* Yara had answered. *And by then there will be no one left to refuse her.*

They had voted at dusk. Fourteen against sheltering him. Nine abstaining. Three—only three—in favor, and one of those was a delegate whose granddaughter appeared in the ledger's pages, and who wept as she raised her hand.

Now, two days later, standing on the balcony of a rented room above a dyer's shop, Sef watched the vanguard's fires bloom along the northern road like a crop of terrible flowers, and understood that the council had purchased exactly what it had paid for: nothing.

"They came early," Oren said beside him. The boy's knuckles were white on the balustrade. "The scouts said six days. They said—"

"Scouts count soldiers," Sef said. "Queens count lies." He put a hand on the boy's shoulder, felt the tremor running through the small frame like current through wire. Oren was gifted—the kind of gift that leaked, that sparked, that could not be hidden for long. A child who made lamps gutter and doors swing and cats flee rooms he entered. In Thornvale such a child would have been smothered in kindness until the gift died. Here in Vhal he would simply have been surrendered, eventually, politely, with certificates and signatures.

Below them, the city was beginning to understand. Bells first—one bell, then three, then all of them, a chaos of alarm with no order in it, because no one had ever drilled for this because no one had believed it could come. Then smoke, thin gray threads rising from the tanners' quarter where the fires would spread fastest. Then people, pouring into the streets with the particular aimlessness of those who have never fled anything, carrying things that did not matter: mirrors, birdcages, a man with a framed portrait of a grandmother under one arm and nothing else in his hands.

"Sef." Yara stood in the doorway behind them, already dressed for travel, the ledger wrapped in oilcloth and strapped across his chest like armor. His face was lit orange from below, from the north, from everywhere the fire was. "How long do we have?"

"An hour. Perhaps less. The vanguard rides fast—they'll want the gates before the garrison thinks to close them."

"The garrison won't close them." Yara's voice was flat. "I watched the council. Half of them are already drafting terms of surrender. They think if they open the gates and hand over nothing, the Queen will take the city without burning it."

"And you know better."

"I've read her edicts." Yara came to the balustrade. Below, the street was filling. "Vessia doesn't occupy. She harvests. Every soul in Vhal is either a soldier or fuel, and she doesn't much care which."

Oren made a small sound. When Sef looked down, the boy was staring at the fire-line with an expression Sef recognized from mirrors he had avoided for years: the look of someone watching a thing they had been promised could never happen.

"My mother said the free cities were safe," Oren whispered. "She brought us here because of that. She said the wards—" His voice caught. His mother was somewhere in the lower city, in the weavers' rows near the river, and between the boy and her now stood four miles of burning streets and a Queen's cavalry.

Sef crouched so their eyes were level. It was a habit from his choirmaster years, from training children whose voices could crack stone—he had always knelt to them, because power deserved courtesy even when it was small. Especially when it was small.

"Listen to me," he said. "We are going to walk out of this city, and your mother is going to meet us at the south gate, because I am going to tell you exactly how to find her, and you are going to be brave in the way that matters, which is the boring way. One street at a time. One corner at a time. Can you do the boring bravery?"

Oren swallowed and nodded, and Sef thought: *I have lied to children before. I told forty of them their voices were instruments of glory, right up until the day I watched the Queen's wardens carry them below the mountain in silver cages.* But some lies are load-bearing. Some lies hold up just long enough for the truth to become survivable.

* * *

They went down into the city while it was still deciding what it was.

The dyer's shop had a back stair into an alley that smelled of mordant and wet wool, and the alley fed into the cloth market, and the cloth market was where the panic found its shape. Sef had seen crowds break before—had once conducted a coronation choir while rioters broke the cathedral glass overhead—but there is a specific sound a city makes when it stops believing in itself, a sound like a chord sliding out of key, and Vhal was making it now. Somewhere ahead a shopkeeper was nailing boards over his windows while his wife screamed at him that boards wouldn't stop dragons, and there were no dragons, there had never been dragons, but fear does not check its facts.

Yara moved through it like a man walking upstream in a flood, one arm around Oren's shoulders, the other clearing a path. People parted for him without knowing why—something in the set of his shoulders, perhaps, or the way the storm-glass pendant at his throat caught the firelight and threw it back in colors that didn't belong to fire. Twice, Sef saw strangers stare at Yara and then flinch away, rubbing their arms as though a cold wind had touched only them. The gift was awake in him now, unhidden, and ordinary folk could feel it the way animals feel weather.

"We should split up," Yara said quietly. "You take Oren to the weavers' rows. I'll draw any hunters toward the east wall."

"No."

"It's my face they want. My name on the proclamation. If Corvane's people are already inside—"

"If they are, they'll take you the moment you're alone, and then everything dies with you: the ledger, the counter-song, the boy's hope." Sef kept his voice level, but underneath it ran the old guilt, sharp as a tuning fork. *I trained the choir. I built the machine that makes this night possible. Whatever happens, I do not get to be sensible. Sensible men built the mountain court.* "We stay together. That's not strategy, Yara. That's penance. Mine."

Yara looked at him for a long moment, firelight moving across his face, and Sef braced for the argument—for the righteous fury of a man who had lost a sister to the machine Sef had tuned. Instead Yara said only, "Then we run when I say run," and took Oren's hand, and they went on.

The weavers' rows were worse than the market, because the weavers' rows had children in them. Families were flooding toward the river gates with bundles and buckets, and the smoke was thicker here, rolling down the narrow lanes in slow brown rivers, and through it all Sef could hear singing—someone, some grandmother probably, singing to keep frightened children walking, an old harvest song about grain coming home, and the absurdity of it cracked something in his chest. *Grain comes home. Souls don't. I know where souls go, and it is down, and it is dark, and I taught the door how to open.*

They found Oren's mother at the third row, hauling a loom-shuttle and two children who were not hers through a doorway choked with smoke. She saw her son and made a sound Sef would remember until he died—a sound with no words in it, just recognition, just *mine*—and folded him into her chest, and Oren clung to her and then, after a moment, looked up at Yara over her shoulder with wet furious eyes, as if to say: *you came back, you actually came back.*

"South gate," she said, breathless, ash in her hair. "Everyone says the south gate. Is it true? Is it open?"

"It's true that everyone says it," Sef said. "Which means it's true that the Queen knows it. We go south, but not through the gate square. There's a postern by the tannery—the old flood-door. I mapped the city walls when we arrived; old habit, from court, from knowing which windows a choirmaster might need to leave by." He almost smiled. "Come."

They had gone half a lane when the sky above the north quarter turned white.

Not fire-white. Not dawn-white. A column of light rose from the heart of the city, silent for one full heartbeat, and then the sound arrived—a crack like the world's largest bell being struck with the world's largest hammer—and every window within a mile burst outward in a bloom of glittering rain. Sef threw himself over Oren and his mother. Glass hissed off the cobbles around them like sleet.

When Sef raised his head, Yara was standing, staring north, and his eyes had gone the color of deep water before a squall.

"That wasn't artillery," Yara said. "That was a warden. That was a *soul-lantern* being used as a weapon." His jaw worked. "They're not besieging the city, Sef. They're emptying it. They're harvesting in the open. Right now. Tonight."

Somewhere in the white light's afterglow, the harvest song had stopped.

* * *

The tannery postern existed, exactly as Sef remembered it, and it was guarded, which he had also remembered, and the guard was a boy of perhaps seventeen with a spear he clearly did not know how to hold and a face slick with tears, which Sef had gambled on.

"You're fleeing," the guard said. "Council ordered the gates sealed. No one leaves. If the city stands together—"

"The city is dying," Sef said gently. "Look north, son. What do you see?"

The boy looked. The white column had faded, but the fires had merged now into one continuous glow, and the bells had stopped ringing one by one as their towers burned, and in the sudden quiet you could hear the other sound underneath, the sound Sef knew too well: horses. Many horses. Coming down the river road.

"My family's in the Tallow district," the guard whispered.

"Then open the door," Yara said, "and come with us."

For a moment the boy stood very straight, spear trembling, ten years of duty and one evening of horror fighting for ownership of his spine. Then he dropped the spear, unbarred the postern with hands that shook so badly the iron rattled, and said, "My name's Pell. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I have to—" and he was through the door and among them, running, a deserter at seventeen, saved and damned in the same motion.

They came out of Vhal through a wound in its wall meant for floodwater, and the river took them south in the black hours, a dozen of them in two leaky ferryboats—the weaver and her borrowed children, Pell the guard, a dyer with burned hands, an old priestess of some river cult who sang softly to keep time with the oars, and Oren, who would not sit. Oren stood in the bow with his small fists on the gunwale, watching Vhal burn behind them, and Sef watched the boy watch, and understood that something was being forged in that small body that would outlast them all.

Behind them, the city's skyline collapsed in sections, like a sentence being erased word by word.

"They let it happen," Oren said finally, not turning around. His voice was too calm. Children learn that calm in the worst classrooms. "The council. They voted, and then they hid, and the city burned anyway. Why vote at all? Why pretend?"

"Because pretending is easier than choosing," Sef said. "Because a coward can live a whole life inside the space between *maybe* and *too late*." He gripped the boy's shoulder. "Don't ever be a council, Oren. Be a door. Be the one who opens it."

The boy finally turned. He looked at Sef, then at Yara sitting hunched in the stern with the ledger strapped to his chest, and then he did the thing that undid Sef completely: he climbed back along the rocking boat, hand over hand, and wedged himself against Yara's side, and stayed there. Did not speak. Did not ask. Just leaned his weight against a stranger as though the stranger were a wall that had proven, tonight, that it would stand.

Yara stiffened. Sef saw it—the flinch of a man who had spent ten years teaching himself not to be leaned on, not to be known, not to love anything the Queen could price. Sef saw him look down at the boy's ash-streaked hair, and saw the war behind his eyes, and then saw him lose it—or win it, depending on how you counted—when his arm came up, slowly, awkwardly, and settled around Oren's shoulders.

"He's not yours," Sef said quietly, later, when the boy slept.

"No," Yara said. "He's the world's. And the world keeps selling them." He looked back at the glow on the horizon, at the place where nine thousand people's futures had been spent purchasing a council's comfort. "I stood in front of them, Sef. I showed them the names. Their own names. And they chose the maybe."

"I know."

"I should have forced them. Called a storm on their chamber floor. Made them feel what's coming."

"And become the monster in their story? They'd have surrendered you themselves, a week early, and called it peace." Sef shook his head. "No. The failure isn't yours. I've sat in halls like that for twenty years, Yara. I know their species. They will trade every child in the ledger for one more quiet winter, and when the winter comes anyway, they will say *no one could have known*."

Yara was silent a long moment. Then: "Corvane knew. He offered me the deal in the archives—surrender the ledger, spare the cities a season. The council got the same arithmetic and couldn't do the sum." His hand closed over the ledger through the oilcloth. "A hunter does the math faster than a statesman. There's a lesson in that I don't want to learn."

"Then don't," Sef said. "Learn the other one instead. The one the boy already knows." He nodded at Oren, asleep, still leaning against Yara even in sleep, following the warmth. "That some things cling to you whether you consent or not. And what you do with them—that's the whole of who you are."

Downriver, the first grey hint of dawn was touching the water, and the refugee boats were strung out behind them like beads on a dark thread, and somewhere far to the north a second column of white light rose briefly over the ruins of Vhal—the harvest continuing, methodical, patient—and Sef closed his eyes and composed, without meaning to, the opening bars of a new verse for his requiem.

But it was Yara who spoke the future aloud, staring south toward the mountains, his voice low and certain in a way Sef had not heard since the council chamber:

"She thinks burning Vhal ends the argument. She thinks fear is the last word." He touched the ledger. "It isn't. There's a counter-song, and it starts with names, and I'm going to carry every name in this book to her door. Oren stays with us. All of them stay with us. We're done arriving places one step behind her armies."

Ahead of them, past the dawn, the old mining road began where the river shallowed—a dark seam climbing into hills that rolled toward the mountain like waves frozen mid-flee. Sef looked at it and felt the weight settle onto his shoulders, familiar as an old instrument: the road beneath, the descent, the choir waiting below with his own crimes woven into its harmony.

"Hold on to that anger," he told Yara. "But ration it. Anger burns hot and fast, and the road is long."

Yara glanced down at the sleeping boy against his ribs, and something in his face softened and hardened at once.

"So is grief," he said. "I've carried mine ten years. I know how to pace it." He lifted his chin toward the southern hills. "Get some rest, Choirmaster. Tomorrow we teach a child to hold a storm—and the day after, we start walking into the dark under the world."

Behind them, Vhal's last tower fell, and the sound crossed the water like a door closing.

Ahead, the mountain waited, patient as debt.
