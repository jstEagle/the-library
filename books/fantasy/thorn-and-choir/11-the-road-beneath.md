## 11. The Road Beneath

The old mining road began where the pilgrim road ended, and it began badly.

It dropped from the hills in a long scar of broken shale, switchbacking down into a valley that had been dug hollow a thousand years ago and never quite healed. The Thornvale miners had called roads like this *veins*—Yara remembered that much, remembered his father saying it with coal dust still in the creases of his knuckles—and this one was the grandest vein of all, running west beneath three mountain ranges toward the court that ate souls.

He did not remember his father's face. That was the wards' doing, or had been. But he remembered the phrase, the way you remember a song's last note after the singer has gone.

"Is it true there are ghosts down there?" Oren asked.

They stood at the roadhead with the eleven other survivors from Vhal—two weavers, a ferryman, an old woman who had not spoken since the burning, a family of four whose youngest still smelled of smoke she could not wash from her hair. Behind them the city's ash hung in the sky like a bruise that would not yellow. Ahead, the dark under the world opened its mouth.

"Ghosts," Yara said, "are the least of what's down there."

Oren considered this with the terrible seriousness of eight-year-olds, then nodded as though Yara had confirmed something he'd already suspected about the universe.

* * *

They walked for two days before the first trouble came, and when it came, it came out of the boy like weather.

The mining road was built for carts and patience, not for children, and by the second evening Oren was fraying. He had not slept properly since Vhal; every time his eyes closed, Yara saw him jolt awake with his hands already raised, already reaching for something. On the second night they sheltered in a cut-through where the mountain had been carved open to let the road pass—a tunnel of raw stone forty paces long, its ceiling braced with timbers black with age. The refugees huddled at one end around a fire no larger than a cupped hand. Yara sat apart, as he always did, watching the dark at the tunnel's far mouth.

That was where the patrol came from.

He heard them before he saw them—the particular rhythm of soldiers on a forced march, boots striking shale in unison because someone had taught them to. Six men, maybe seven, torchlight wobbling up the road beyond the tunnel. Between them and the refugees lay forty paces of stone that would echo every sound made inside it.

"Down," Yara breathed. "Everyone down. Now."

They moved well for civilians. The weavers pulled the children flat. The ferryman smothered the fire with dirt and his own coat. Within ten heartbeats the tunnel held nothing but breathing.

Except Oren.

The boy had frozen halfway between sleep and waking, and now he sat upright in the dark with his eyes wide and unfocused, and Yara felt it happen—felt the air change pressure the way it did before hail, felt the fine hairs on his arms lift. Oren's gift was small, Sef had said. A spark-gift, candle-flame stuff. But fear does not care how small a gift is. Fear only cares that it is lit.

A blue-white glow kindled in the boy's palms, soft as foxfire, and with it came a sound—a low, rising hum, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. The torchlight beyond the tunnel stopped moving.

"Oren." Yara crossed the space between them without seeming to move at all, the way he'd learned to cross a room during storms, and crouched. "Look at me."

"I can't stop it," the boy whispered. The glow brightened. "I never can, that's why they—I couldn't hide it in Vhal either, I tried, I—"

"Breathe. Look at me. Not at your hands. At me."

The boy looked. His eyes were enormous and wet and reflected the light of his own failing control, and Yara thought: *this is what I looked like at twelve, kneeling in the goat pen while the sky came down.*

The boots resumed. Closer now. Thirty paces, maybe less, and the tunnel would carry their voices straight to the fire-scar and the flattened bodies and the glowing child.

Yara made a decision he would think about for years afterward.

He took Oren's hands in his own—not gently, but firmly, the way you grip a rope in wind—and he let the storm in his blood rise to meet the boy's spark.

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about teaching magic: you cannot teach it the way you teach letters. You can only show someone the door they have been standing against their whole life, and hope they forgive you for opening it.

Yara did not speak aloud. He spoke the way his mother had taught him, in the private grammar of storm-callers, pressing meaning through his palms into the boy's skin: *feel this. Feel what I'm doing.*

And Oren gasped, because what Yara was doing was nothing. That was the secret. He was doing nothing—holding the storm still, holding it whole, a thunderhead folded up inside his chest no bigger than a fist, and letting it sit there, patient, leashed, alive.

"You're not stopping mine," Oren breathed. "You're—it's still there. I can feel it. Why doesn't it come out?"

"Because I'm not afraid of it." The boots were twenty paces away. Yara kept his voice below hearing, a thread drawn taut between them. "Listen to me, Oren. Your gift isn't the thing that betrays you. Fear is. Fear shoves the power out of you because it wants the danger gone, any danger, even if the danger it makes is worse. So we don't fight the power. We fight the fear. And there's only one way I know to do that."

"How?"

"You give the storm something heavier than fear to hold."

The torchlight touched the near mouth of the tunnel. Yara could see them now: six soldiers in the Queen's grey, tired, mud-caked, one of them lifting his torch high to peer into the dark. Twenty paces of echoing stone between them and everything Yara had failed to save in Vhal.

"Hold my hands," he said. "And remember something that broke you."

"That's cruel," Oren whispered—but his hands tightened, and Yara knew the boy understood, because grief was the one country gifted children emigrated from early.

"It is," Yara said. "It's the cruelest thing I know. It's also the only door I ever found. My mother taught me the same way, on a night very much like this one. She told me: *don't push the storm down, Yara. Give it somewhere to live.* And the place it lives—" his voice caught, and he let it catch, deliberately, because the catching was the lesson— "the place it lives is the worst memory you own. You build the storm a house out of your grief, and it stays there, and it keeps the grief company, and neither one destroys you. Do you understand?"

"No," Oren said honestly.

"Good. Honest students learn faster." Yara bowed his head until his forehead nearly touched the boy's. "Now. Think of Vhal burning. Think of whoever you lost in it. Don't look away from it. Walk into it. And when the storm rises to meet you—let it live there."

Oren closed his eyes.

The glow in his palms did not go out. It changed. It sank, the way a coal sinks when it stops flaring and starts burning steady, blue-white going gold going deep, deep red, until the light was no light at all but warmth, contained, a hearth instead of a signal fire. The hum faded to silence.

And Yara felt it through their joined hands—the boy's grief, vast and specific, a mother's voice singing a lullaby that would never be finished—and felt his own storm lean toward it like a dog toward a familiar hand, settling, staying.

The soldiers passed the tunnel mouth. One paused. Held his torch into the dark. Saw forty paces of empty stone and old timber and nothing worth dying over, and moved on. Their bootfalls faded westward, toward the mountain, toward the court, and no one in the tunnel breathed fully again for a hundred count.

Then Oren opened his eyes, and they were dry, and old, and looking at Yara with an expression no eight-year-old should know how to make.

"It didn't hurt," the boy said. "It should have hurt. Remembering her. But it just—held me."

"Yes," Yara said, and found his own throat tight. "That's what grief is for, if you're built like us. It's not a wound. It's a wall. You put the storm behind it."

* * *

They walked on. The road descended in long, patient spirals, and the world above thinned.

By the fourth day they had passed the last farms, the last shrines, the last evidence that anyone living claimed this land. The air grew mineral and cold. Water dripped somewhere always, a metronome in the dark, and the walls of the cutting rose higher until they walked at the bottom of a canyon of their ancestors' making, the sky a ribbon of grey overhead. The refugees spoke less. Even the children went quiet, the way animals go quiet underground.

Sef caught up with Yara on the fifth morning, when the road passed through a gallery of abandoned mine-shafts, their mouths boarded shut with wood so old it had turned to lace.

"He slept last night," Sef said. "Really slept. No sparks."

"He'll sleep better every night now. The storm has a house." Yara watched the boy ahead of them, walking hand in hand with the silent old woman, showing her something—a beetle, a joke, some small ordinary treasure. "It's a terrible way to raise a child, Choirmaster. Teaching them that grief is furniture."

"It's the way you were raised."

"And look how well I turned out." He meant it as a joke. It didn't land as one, and Sef had the grace not to pretend otherwise.

They walked a while in the drip-and-echo quiet. Then Sef said, carefully, "What you gave him yesterday. The technique. Your mother's technique."

"What of it?"

"The choir uses something like it. Inverted." Sef's voice had gone flat, the voice he used for confessions, which was most of his voice these days. "The bound souls aren't held down by force—that would take more power than the Queen has. They're held by their own grief. The binding-song builds each soul a house out of its worst memory and locks the door from the inside. They stay because leaving hurts more than staying. That's why the counter-song has to be sung by a voice they trusted. Only someone they loved can convince them the door is safe to open."

Yara stopped walking.

Ahead, the ribbon of sky showed a jagged tooth of peak—the mountain, closer now, close enough to have weight, to press on the mind like a thumb on an eye. Somewhere beneath it, in galleries of lantern-light, Lira's soul sat in a house built of her own worst memory, and the door was locked from the inside, and the key was a voice she trusted.

"She was nine when Father sold her," Yara said. His voice came out level, which frightened him more than screaming would have. "Her worst memory will be that day. The study. The man from the court. Whatever Father said to her." He turned to Sef. "When I sing her free, I'll be standing in the middle of the worst moment of her life. I'll be part of it."

Sef said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and Yara was grateful for the silence. Some truths deserve to stand alone in a room.

* * *

On the seventh night, the road brought them to a waystation carved into the canyon wall—an old miner's rest, a hollow big enough for thirty people, with soot-blackened ceilings and niches in the rock where generations of travelers had left offerings for luck. Most were gone, taken or rotted, but a few remained: a child's shoe gone stiff as bark. A clay whistle. A strip of cloth embroidered with a name too worn to read.

Oren sat beside Yara at the entrance, legs dangling over the drop, sharing a heel of bread. Below them the road unspooled into darkness, and far off—so far it might have been imagination—a faint glow stained the underside of the clouds. The mountain's lights. The court's lights. Burning all night, every night, above the choir.

"Teach me another one tomorrow," Oren said.

"Another what?"

"Trick. For holding it." The boy tore his bread with great concentration. "The grief-house works. But sometimes the grief gets loud, and then the storm gets loud, and I need... layers. Like a roof over the house."

Yara almost smiled. *A roof over the house.* Out of the mouths of children and storm-callers, the same architecture.

"There is one more thing," he said slowly. "But it's harder than the grief-house, and I've only ever shown it to one person, and she—" He stopped. Started again. "She's the reason we're walking this road."

Oren waited. He was good at waiting. Grief had taught him that too.

"When the storm gets louder than the grief," Yara said, "you don't build a bigger wall. You open the door and you let them meet. Storm and grief, together, in the same room. Because a storm that knows why it's angry doesn't rage blind. It rages *aimed*." He looked down at his own hands, at the faint static shimmer that lived in them now always, since the wheat field, since Corvane's brand had woken everything he'd spent ten years burying. "Every storm I've ever called well, I called grieving. The ones I called angry—they drowned sailors."

Below them, in the dark, something flickered. Not the mountain's glow—closer. Lower. A line of torches on the road behind them, small as embers, moving with the disciplined rhythm he had learned to hate.

Yara went still. Oren felt him go still, and went still too, and did not light, did not hum, did not betray them—just reached over and gripped Yara's sleeve with a small, grim hand, and waited, the way he'd learned.

"Sef," Yara said softly, without turning. "How many days do you think we have before the road runs out of places to hide?"

Behind them, torches climbed. Ahead, the mountain glowed. And in the niche beside the boy's shoulder, the worn embroidered name on the strip of old cloth caught the last of the light—letters Yara could almost, almost read, in a hand that reminded him of home.
