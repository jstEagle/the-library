## 12. Sef's Requiem

The wayshrine stood where the mining road bent away from the river, a low stone shell half-swallowed by ivy, its roof long since fallen to leave the altar open to the sky. Sef had known such places all his life. In the capital they called them listening posts—old shrines to gods whose names had been sanded away by successive crowns, each usurper carving her own sigil over the last until the stone was nothing but scar tissue. This one had been spared even that. Whoever had built it had wanted only a quiet place, and the mountain road had obliged by forgetting it.

He was grateful for the forgetting. He had been carrying too much memory himself.

Sef set down his pack and unslung the case that held his reed-organ, the small portable one with pipes of silvered tin, and stood for a moment without opening it. His hands were not steady. They had not been steady since Vhal, since the burning streets, since Oren's small fingers gripping his sleeve as they ran through ash that fell like grey snow. But this was different. This was older.

Behind him, Yara was seeing to the camp with the economy of a man who had spent ten years making do in a valley designed to erase him. He moved through the motions—tethering the mule, banking what little fire the damp wood would allow, checking on Oren, who slept curled against the mule's flank like something trying to become small enough to be overlooked. The refugees had gone on ahead that morning with the others, toward the waystation at Grey Ford, promising to wait three days. Three days, and then they would assume the dead had caught up with them.

That left the two of them, and the boy, and the shrine, and the thing Sef had been carrying in his chest since Aldermist like a stone swallowed whole.

"Yara," he said.

Yara looked up. Firelight did strange things to his face—the storm-caller's face, the face that had galvanized a council and burned a city's cowardice into legend in a single night. He looked younger in firelight. He looked exhausted everywhere else.

"There's a spring behind the shrine," Sef said. "Cold, but clean. You should drink. Both of you."

"We drank at noon."

"Drink again." Sef heard how his own voice sounded—too careful, too arranged—and hated it. "Please."

Yara studied him for a long moment. He had gotten better at reading Sef, these weeks on the road; or perhaps Sef had simply gotten worse at hiding. Either way, the storm-caller saw something, because he said nothing more, only nodded once and went to wake Oren gently, and the two of them walked together into the trees toward the sound of water.

Which left Sef alone with the shrine, and the organ, and the dusk coming down violet between the broken rafters.

* * *

He opened the case.

The organ was the one indulgence he had permitted himself when he fled the court—no, not an indulgence. A witness. Every other instrument he had owned belonged to the Queen: the great choir-hall organs, the tuning forks of bone, the conducting baton carved from the femur of the first soul ever bound. Those he had left behind, laid out neatly on his desk like a confession no one would read. But this little traveling organ he had bought with his own coin, before any of it, back when he was still a choirmaster who taught children scales and thought music was a ladder out of a weaver's garret in Aldermist.

He set it on the altar stone. The ivy had grown through the cracks and made a green cushion of the surface, and he did not clear it away. Let the plant hold the instrument. Let something living touch it first.

His breath fogged in the cooling air. He pressed his palms flat against the keys—not playing, just resting, the way a penitent rests his forehead against a door he is afraid to knock upon.

There were forty-one names in the ledger besides Lira Hartwell's. No. That was wrong. There were forty-one names *in the ledger*. There were nine hundred and six souls in the choir beneath the mountain, and the ledger recorded only those bound within Sef's tenure as royal choirmaster. Nine hundred and six minus the ones before him, minus the ones after. Forty-one. He had counted them every night for eleven years, the way other men counted sheep. He had never once reached sleep before the count finished.

He knew their voices. That was the cruelty of it, the particular cruelty reserved for him. A choirmaster learns voices the way a mother learns heartbeats. Maren of the Saltmarket, whose alto could crack stone when she let it. The twins Pell and Perrin, bound together so their harmony would never separate. Old Tomas Wren, who had sold his voice to pay for his granddaughter's medicine and had sung bass in the cathedral choir Sef conducted every Feastday, sitting in the third row, smiling at him.

Smiling at him. While Sef tuned the hall above them. While Sef adjusted the resonances that carried their song down into the crown.

He had told himself, in the early years, that he had not known. That the binding rites were administrative, that the souls went to some peaceful archive, that the war-marches the choir sang were merely amplified hymns. He had told himself this the way the valley elders had voted to comply with the edict—the way any comfortable person tells themselves any necessary lie. And then one night, eleven years ago, he had descended into the galleries beneath the mountain to tune a dissonance in the lower registers, and he had heard Tomas Wren singing the Queen's war-march with a smile still fixed on his ghost-face, and Sef had understood that the smile was part of the binding. That they were made to look content. That somewhere in the design of the thing, someone had decided that the damned should seem happy, because happy choirs sing sweeter.

He had fled within the season. It had taken him a year to steal the ledger, another to find a city far enough from the crown's reach, and in all that time he had composed exactly one piece of music.

A requiem. For forty-one voices. Written for an instrument that could not possibly hold them, in a key that did not exist in any sanctioned mode, using intervals the court had forbidden since the first binding—intervals that pulled against resolution, that refused to come home, because the souls they mourned had never been permitted to come home.

He had never played it. Not once. Eleven years, and the pages had traveled with him folded into the organ's case, and he had never played it, because playing it would make it real, would make *them* real, would mean admitting that the requiem was not for the dead but for the living man who had helped kill them.

The sky above the broken roof had gone the color of a bruise healing. Somewhere in the trees, water chuckled over stones. And Sef Ashgrave, disgraced choirmaster of the court beneath the mountain, sat down on a fallen rafter before a tin-voiced traveler's organ, and began to play.

* * *

The first notes came out wrong. Of course they did. His hands shook, and the little organ's action was stiff from disuse, and the opening phrase—which he had written to sound like breathing, like the ragged first breath of a soul arriving somewhere it did not want to be—stuttered and broke.

He stopped. Closed his eyes. Breathed.

*They are already dead,* he told himself. *You cannot harm them further. The only thing left to harm is you.*

He began again.

This time the phrase held. Thin, wavering, a single line of melody climbing through the forbidden intervals, and the little organ gave it a silvery, incomplete voice—nothing like the human throats it mourned—but the shape of it was right, the shape of grief ascending and refusing to land. Sef played with his eyes closed and let the requiem rise into the roofless dark, and as he played he named them, silently, the way he had written the piece: one phrase per soul, forty-one phrases, each built from the intervals of that singer's own range, each ending unresolved.

Maren of the Saltmarket. The twins. Old Tomas Wren, third row, Feastdays, smiling.

He was deep into the twelfth phrase—he did not know which soul it belonged to anymore, the count had blurred, they had all blurred into one long cry—when he became aware that he was no longer playing alone.

Not accompanied. Nothing so kind. But there was a presence at the edge of the shrine, standing among the ivy and the fallen stone, and the music bent around it the way water bends around a standing figure in a stream.

He did not open his eyes. If he opened his eyes he would stop, and if he stopped he would never begin again.

The phrase ended, unresolved. The next began. And a voice joined it—not singing, not yet, but humming, low and rough, finding the tonal center the way a man finds a wall in the dark. Yara Hartwell hummed the drone beneath the twelfth phrase, and then the thirteenth, and when the melody climbed into the register where a second voice was wanted, he gave it—not skillfully, he was no trained singer, there were places where his pitch wavered like a candle in wind—but he gave it *weight*, the weight of a man who had spent ten years swallowing storms and knew what it cost to hold a note instead of lightning.

Sef played. Yara held the ground beneath the music. And the requiem, written for forty-one voices and performed by two, filled the ruined shrine like water filling a cup.

Oren appeared sometime during the twentieth phrase. Sef heard the boy's bare feet on the stone, saw nothing, kept playing. The child did not hum. He simply listened, the way gifted children listen—with his whole body, his gift rising in him like heat off a summer road, and Sef felt the air begin to prickle, felt the fine hairs on his arms lift, felt the pressure building that meant Oren's storm was waking in sympathy with the music.

*No*, Sef thought, desperate, still playing. *Not now, not here—*

But Yara moved. Sef heard it—a shift, a rustle—and then the storm-caller's hand must have found the boy's shoulder, because the pressure eased, gentled, sank back down like a wave that has decided, for the moment, not to break. Later, Sef would learn what Yara whispered to him. *Hold it the way I taught you. Hold it like grief. Grief keeps.*

The requiem went on. Phrase after phrase, name after name, and the dusk deepened into true dark, and stars came out above the broken roof in the cold indifferent way of stars, and Sef wept. Not dramatically. Tears simply arrived and went down his face and dripped onto the ivy-wrapped keys, and he played through them, because stopping now would have been a second abandonment, and he had sworn, somewhere in the eleventh year of silence, that he would never abandon these forty-one again.

The final phrase was Lira's.

He had written it last, though her name had come to him late—had come to him in Aldermist, when he read the ledger by candlelight beside a stranger with storm-grey eyes and watched that stranger's world end quietly, page by page. The phrase for Lira Hartwell was built from a range Sef had never heard, reconstructed from ledger notation alone, and it climbed higher than all the others and hung there, suspended, refusing every cadence, because she was not dead. Because hers was the one phrase in the requiem that might yet resolve.

He played it. Yara's drone rose to meet it, rough and steady, and for four bars the two lines existed together in the roofless dark—brother and stranger, guilt and grief, the man who had bound and the man who had lost—and then the phrase ended, unresolved, as written, and the silence afterward was enormous.

Sef lifted his hands from the keys. They left wet prints on the ivory.

For a long time, nobody spoke. The fire crackled. The spring chuckled. Oren had fallen asleep against Yara's side, the boy's storm settled to embers, his cheek smudged with ash that might have been days old or hours.

Then Yara said, quietly, "Tomas Wren. Third row. Feastdays."

Sef's breath caught. "You were listening."

"I listen to everything now. Since the wards broke." Yara came forward into the starlight, easing the sleeping boy down onto his bedroll with a tenderness that seemed to surprise even himself. "You named them. All forty-one. I heard the names under the music."

"I wrote one phrase for each."

"I know." Yara sat down on the opposite side of the altar, the organ between them, and for a moment he only looked at it—at the small, shabby, faithful instrument that had crossed a kingdom in a disgraced man's arms. "My sister sang. Did I tell you that? Before the soldiers took her. She wasn't gifted, not really—just a voice like everyone in our village had, the kind you use for harvest songs. She used to sing while she churned butter, badly, on purpose, to make me laugh." His jaw worked. "I keep thinking about whether she sings in that place. Whether the binding lets her choose anything. Whether she remembers butter."

"She remembers you." Sef said it before he could weigh it. "In the galleries, the newly bound remember longest. The binding eats outward from the edges—first the world, then the years, then the self. What it takes last is whatever the soul holds at its center. For most of them, that's a person." He swallowed. "Tomas Wren's center was his granddaughter. Maren's was the sea. When I tuned the lower registers, I could hear them—they weren't words, but they were names. Repeated. Like prayers."

Yara was silent for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking across ice. "You've said the counter-song can break the binding. You've never said how. Not truly. You give me fragments on the road and stop before the hard part, every time, and I've let you, because I need you, and because I'm afraid of the answer." He looked up, and his eyes in the starlight were the grey of weather coming. "Tell me the hard part, Sef."

Sef had known this question was coming since Aldermist. He had rehearsed answers—gentle ones, partial ones, the lies of kindness he had sworn off along with everything else. He looked at the sleeping boy, at the wet prints on the keys, at the man who had every right to hate him and had instead hummed a drone beneath his requiem.

"The counter-song cannot be sung from outside," he said. "I wrote it to be sung from outside, in the first years after I fled. I was wrong. The binding is not a wall—it's a weave. From outside, you can only cut threads, and cutting threads kills the soul the thread belongs to. I proved that once." His voice cracked on the admission; he let it. "There was a rescuer, eight years ago. A hedge-witch whose brother was in the choir. She reached the galleries. She sang my counter-song from the gallery floor, and it worked, and it killed him, and the wardens killed her, and I have carried both deaths ever since. So no. Not from outside."

"From inside, then." Yara's voice did not change. "Someone in the weave."

"Someone the weave trusts." Sef turned his hands over, looked at the palms as if the answer might be written there. "The binding holds because the souls consented—or were made to believe they consented. Contracts, debts, bargains. The magic of it is contractual, Yara. And a contract can be dissolved by the voice that witnessed it. The choir was assembled under a royal choirmaster's authority. My authority. Their binding carries my signature in its structure—I tuned the resonances, I set the harmonics, I am woven into the weave itself." He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as old paper. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? I am the hard part. The counter-song can only unbind from within, sung by a voice the bound souls trusted—and there is exactly one voice alive that they trusted, and it belongs to the man who bound them."

The silence stretched. Above them the stars wheeled, indifferent. Oren murmured in his sleep and turned toward the warmth of the dying fire.

"You're saying you have to go down there," Yara said at last. "Into the galleries. Among the lanterns. And sing."

"I'm saying I have to go down there and be heard. And the wardens will hear me, and the Queen will hear me, and the odds that either of us walks out are—" He shrugged, a small, precise gesture, the gesture of a man who had done the arithmetic long ago and kept the answer folded away like the requiem pages. "But that isn't the worst of it. The worst of it is that I don't know if they'll trust me. Eleven years. The binding eats outward. Maybe I'm eaten too, by now. Maybe to them I'm just another warden's face, another instrument of the thing that holds them. Maybe Tomas Wren looks up at me and sees only the crown." His hands had begun to shake again, and he pressed them flat against his knees. "I have imagined this moment ten thousand times, Yara. In every version, they forgive me. In none of them do I believe it."

Yara was quiet for a long time. Then he reached across the altar, past the organ, and took one of Sef's shaking hands—not gently, not cruelly, but firmly, the grip of a man anchoring another against wind.

"When I was fourteen," Yara said, "my father sold my sister, and I hid in the goat shed and did nothing. Ten years in that valley, I told myself I was keeping my head down to survive. The truth is I was a coward with good weather. Everything I've done since the wards broke—every storm, every reckless thing—I've been paying that debt. I understand debt, Sef. I understand doing monstrous arithmetic to stay alive." His grip tightened. "I hummed your requiem tonight. That's not forgiveness. Forgiveness is a door, and I'm not ready to open it. But I stood in the doorway. That's more than I thought I'd ever give you, and it's more than you think you deserve, and maybe we're both wrong about what we deserve. Maybe deserving doesn't enter into it. Maybe all that enters into it is what we do next."

Sef could not speak. He nodded, because nodding was all he had, and the tears came again, and this time he let them fall without playing anything at all.

* * *

Later, when Oren slept and the fire had burned to a red eye of coals, Sef sat apart with the organ case open on his knees, folding the requiem pages with hands that had finally gone still. Across the dead fire, Yara lay awake, staring at the stars through the broken roof, and Sef knew he was not seeing stars. He was seeing galleries. Lanterns. A sister's face at the center of the binding, holding on.

*She remembers you,* Sef had told him, and believed it, and feared it—for if Lira Hartwell remembered her brother, then she remembered a boy in a goat shed who did nothing, and then a man who came for her, and the binding would show her all of it, and what soul consents to be freed by someone it watched fail?

No. That was fear speaking, and fear had been his choirmaster long enough.

Tomorrow they would walk the mining road into the mountain's shadow. Tomorrow the waystation, the refugees, the last stretch of country where the Queen's writ still ran thin. And after that, the gate that sold entry in memories, and the descent, and the galleries, and the forty-one phrases resolved at last or not at all.

Sef closed the case. In the dark, very softly, so as not to wake anyone, he began to hum the drone again—the ground-bass Yara had given him, rough and steady, a coward's anchor, a debtor's promise.

And from across the ashes, half-asleep, Yara hummed it back.
