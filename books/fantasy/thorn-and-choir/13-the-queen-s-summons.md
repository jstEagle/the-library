## 13. The Queen's Summons

The summons came at dusk, as the Queen's summons always did, borne by a warden whose face had been smoothed to featureless porcelain by long service in the crown's presence. Corvane read the black-edged card without expression. *Come. Come now. Come alone.* The words were unnecessary—everyone in the mountain court came when Vessia called—but the ritual of it mattered to her, and ritual was the last thing she still clung to that resembled a woman.

He made his way down through the galleries, past the soul-lanterns swaying in their thousands like fruit too heavy for their branches, each one humming its single note in the great choir that never slept. He had walked this road so many times the lanterns no longer troubled him. That was the thing he could not forgive himself for: not the hounds, not the brands, not the men and women he had dragged up this road in chains. It was that he had stopped hearing them.

The throne room opened before him like the mouth of some patient beast. Vessia sat beneath the crown—a thing of black iron and fused light, ugly as a scar, beautiful the way a wildfire is beautiful from a safe distance—and she was smiling.

"Corvane." Her voice filled the vaulted dark the way water fills a cup. "My best hunter. My most faithful dog."

"Your Majesty."

"Do you know what I dreamed last night?" She rose from the throne, and the crown's light shifted with her, throwing shadows that moved wrong, that leaned toward her instead of away. "I dreamed of weather. Rain on my face. Do you know how long it has been since anything touched my face?" She laughed, and the sound was lovely and hollow, a bell with the clapper removed. "The crown does not permit it. The crown takes everything soft and burns it for fuel. But I dreamed of rain, and when I woke, my wardens told me why. A storm-caller walks free in my realm. Ten years he hid in Thornvale, and now he runs, and every mile he runs, the sky remembers what it used to do."

She descended the dais steps slowly, savoring it, and Corvane understood with sudden terrible clarity that she was not speaking to him. She was speaking to the storm-caller who was not there yet, the way a starving man speaks to a meal.

"I want him alive," she said. "Unmarked. Unbroken. His soul must come to me whole, or it will not feed the crown properly. Do you understand? Whole. Every storm in his blood, every year of his life, all of it poured into this iron, and then—" she spread her hands, and for one flickering moment her eyes were not the Queen's eyes at all but something starved and young and frightened behind them— "and then perhaps I will be able to feel the rain again."

*You will not,* Corvane thought. *It is never enough. You have fed it a choir of thousands and you cannot feel the rain.* But he bowed, because bowing was cheaper than truth.

"There is a complication," he said. "The choirmaster. Sef. He travels with the boy."

Something moved across Vessia's face—hunger sharpening into hatred. "Sef." She said the name the way one spits out a rotten tooth. "My traitor. Bring me his voice, then. I will wear it as a ring until it learns to sing my march again." She turned back toward the throne, dismissing him, then paused. "Corvane. You were valley-born once. Before the wards. Before your family sold you to pay their debts, as all good families do." She smiled over her shoulder. "Do you remember the valley?"

"No, Majesty," he lied, and the lie tasted like ash, because he remembered everything. He remembered wheat. He remembered a woman singing while she worked, though he could no longer see her face. The wards of Thornvale took memories from those inside them; the crown took everything else.

"Pity," said Vessia. "I am told it was green there. Go. Hunt well. And Corvane—" the crown flared, and every lantern in the galleries above them shuddered on its chain— "do not fail me. The last hunter who failed me sings soprano now. Listen for him, sometimes, when the choir changes key. He is very good."

* * *

He did not go to his chambers. He went to the kennels.

The hounds were new since Thornvale—he had lost four in the wheat, torn apart by a storm that should have been impossible from a man ten years leached of power—and they pressed against the bars of their cages, sensing his mood the way animals sense weather. He crouched before the largest, a brindled bitch called Wren, and let her press her hot muzzle against his palm.

"He wants the boy whole," Corvane told her. "Whole. Do you know what happens to souls that go into the crown, Wren? Neither do I. No one does. That is the joke of it. We hunt them across half the realm, we keep them unbruised, unburnt, unwept-over, and then they go up those stairs and through that door and no one ever asks about them again." The hound whined. He scratched her ears. "I asked once. When I was new. I asked what became of the ones we brought in. My handler laughed and said, *become? Boy, nothing becomes. That's rather the point.*"

Wren licked his hand, and he thought: *she tastes salt. She thinks I am grieving. Perhaps I am.*

He had been valley-born. He had been a child when his father signed the papers, and he remembered—the wards had not eaten this, because he had carried it out with him, sewn into his coat like contraband—he remembered standing in the doorway watching the crown's men load his mother's spinning wheel onto a cart, and his mother saying nothing, doing nothing, because what was there to do? The debt was real. The math was merciless. And the crown's men had been almost kind, in the way of men who have done a thing so often it has worn smooth as river stone.

He had chosen the crown afterward. That was the part he did not tell anyone. People assumed hunters were taken, broken, remade. He had stood in the ashes of his family's farm and thought: *the world is arithmetic, and I would rather be the hand that counts than the number counted.* He had been eleven years old and very proud of the thought. He had been proud of it for thirty years.

And now there was a boy in the world—young enough to be his son, if he had ever dared make one—who had hidden in a forgetting-valley for a decade rather than be counted, and who had called a storm out of a clear sky and killed four hounds and run, and who was, by every report, heading for the mountain with a stolen ledger and a disgraced choirmaster and, lately, a child of his own.

*A child of his own.* The reports from Vhal had mentioned that. A gifted boy clinging to the storm-caller's side through burning streets. Corvane had read the line three times.

He put Wren back in her cage and went up, alone, to the map room.

* * *

The map room occupied the crown of the mountain, a round chamber walled in glass, where the realm lay spread below in painted miniature: the free cities, the straits, the smoking ruin of Vhal, the green thread of Thornvale far to the south. Wardens stood watch here day and night, tracking the Queen's armies by candle-flame and scrying-glass. At this hour only one was awake, an old man half-dozing over the straits, and Corvane dismissed him with a flick of two fingers.

Then he stood over the map for a long time, alone with the realm and his conscience, which had begun, recently and inconveniently, to make noise.

He found the refugees' likely route with little trouble. There was only one road worth taking from the pass country toward the mountain: the old mining road, the deep way, the one the crown had sealed a generation ago and never bothered to reopen because nothing that went in ever needed to come out. If the storm-caller was wise, he would take it. If he was desperate—and everyone who ran from Vessia was eventually desperate—he already had.

Corvane took out his knife.

For a long moment he simply held it, feeling the balance of it, the familiarity. Then he knelt and, with small precise cuts, slit open the seam of his own left boot, where the brand-stone lived—the twin to the scar he had burned into Yara Hartwell's arm in the wheat field, the tether that let him feel his quarry's direction and distance like a compass needle under the skin. He worked the stone loose. It pulsed faintly, warm, eager, pointing south and east, toward the deep road, toward the boy.

He could crush it. That would end the tracking entirely—but the stones were warded, and a crushed brand-stone announced itself to the Queen's scryers within the hour, a flare of broken magic visible from the map room itself. Vessia would know. She would send others. Others who did not stand in kennels talking to hounds about the nature of souls.

So instead he did something cleverer, something that shamed him with how easily it came: he wrapped the stone in a strip of lead foil from a wine bottle, and around the lead a layer of wool, and around the wool wax, sealing it dull and mute. Not dead—the scryers would still feel its heartbeat, slow and steady, reporting *all is well, the hunt proceeds*—but muffled. Blinded. He would still feel the general pull of it when he chose to consult it, but the constant whisper, the live wire of the boy's location feeding the mountain hour by hour—that was gone.

When he finished, his hands were steady, and that frightened him more than trembling would have. How easy treachery was, once you started. Like opening a door you had leaned against for thirty years and finding it was never locked.

He set the boot back on his foot and stood, testing the weight. Nothing. Good.

Then he crossed to the war-table in the corner, where the vanguard's movements were marked in red pins, and he studied the disposition of the companies along the deep road's exits. Two garrisons. He memorized their rotation schedules, their strengths, their blind hours. And then, because a hunter's first skill is misdirection and his second is forgery, he sat down and wrote two orders in the hand of Captain Ilves of the eastern garrison—one pulling the pickets off the mine-road's lower mouth for "flood inspection," one rerouting a patrol to the wrong valley entirely—and sealed them with a seal he had no right to own and had owned anyway for eleven years, waiting.

He did not tell himself he was doing the right thing. He was done with stories like that; he had heard too many people tell them on the road to the throne room, right up until the lanterns took them. He told himself only this: *I want to see what he does with it. The storm. The ledger. The choice. I have spent my life delivering people to their endings. I would like, once, before my own ending, to see someone reach a beginning.*

But underneath that, quieter, in the place where Wren had tasted salt on his hand, he knew the truth. He remembered wheat. He remembered a woman singing. And somewhere in the galleries above, in a lantern he had passed ten thousand times without hearing, a voice that might have been hers—or might have been anyone's, might have been everyone's—was singing Vessia's war-march, and had been singing it for thirty years, and would sing it forever unless someone made it stop.

* * *

Dawn came gray through the map-room glass, and with it came the porcelain-faced warden again, with a second summons.

"The Queen asks after the hunt," the warden said. Its voice was smooth as poured cream. "She wishes to know when the storm-caller will be delivered."

"Tell her soon," Corvane said.

"She also asks—" the warden tilted its head, and for a moment the porcelain seemed to strain over something human beneath it, something that remembered asking questions was once allowed— "she asks whether you have encountered difficulty."

Corvane met the thing's blank gaze and thought of soprano voices in the choir. Thought of the crown eating everything soft. Thought of a woman singing over a spinning wheel, and a boy in the wheat with lightning in his fists, and a child named Oren he had never met clinging to that boy through fire.

"No difficulty," he said. "The trail is cold. The storm-caller is cunning. I will need time."

"Time," the warden repeated, without inflection, and turned to go. At the door it paused. "Hunter. A word of counsel, freely given, as one servant of the crown to another." The porcelain face caught the gray light. "She ate the last hunter who disappointed her. But she eats everything, in the end. The crown is never full. Consider whether your loyalty is a shelter or merely a longer queue."

Then it was gone, and Corvane stood alone in the map room with the realm spread beneath him and his forged orders in his sleeve and his blinded stone in his boot, and he began, quietly, methodically, to sabotage his own hunt.

By noon he had lost the trail in three places. By evening he had misfiled the garrison reports. And in the deepest hour of that night, standing once more at the map table, he looked down at the old mining road winding through the dark heart of the mountains like a vein of mercy through stone, and he permitted himself, for the first time in thirty years, to hope.

Somewhere on that road, a storm-caller was walking toward the mountain with his sister's name in a ledger and a child asleep against his shoulder. Somewhere above him, in the galleries, the choir sang on. And somewhere between those two things—between the song and the silence, the crown and the breaking of it—Corvane took out his knife and began, very carefully, to cut the next false order.

The mountain would eat him for this. He suspected he had known that since the wheat field, since the first hound died, since the moment he had felt the boy's storm through the brand and thought, unbidden, *yes. Yes. That is what power is for.*

Let it eat him, then. But not yet. Not until the choir was broken, and the lanterns were dark, and the woman who might have been his mother's ghost—if ghosts sang—was free to stop singing.

He blew out the lamp. In the dark, the realm glowed faintly with its pins and candles, its armies and its lies, and Corvane stood over it like a man standing over his own grave, digging.
