## 14. Gate of the Mountain Court

The mountain announced itself long before they saw it.

For three days they had climbed through country that grew stranger with every mile—forests where the trees leaned all one direction as if listening, streams that ran silver and slow and tasted faintly of iron, like blood diluted in water. Oren stopped asking questions somewhere on the second day. He simply walked closer to Yara, close enough that their shoulders brushed when the path narrowed, and kept his eyes on the road ahead the way soldiers do.

Now, cresting a ridge of black slate, they saw it: the mountain rising out of its own cloud-wreath like a head lifted from sleep, its flanks terraced and glittering, roads spiraling up toward a summit lost in permanent storm-light. And at its foot, cut into the living rock itself, the gate.

It was not a gate in any way Yara had imagined. It was a mouth—a vast arch of pale stone carved with thousands of faces, each one different, each one singing or weeping or laughing, their mouths open in an eternal chorus of stone. Light poured from between their lips. The whole arch hummed, a low note Yara felt in his back teeth, in the marrow of his arms, in the scar Corvane's brand had left on his wrist.

The brand was burning steadily now. Not the hot flare of pursuit—something quieter, more terrible. Recognition.

"There's a queue," Sef said quietly.

There was. A line of travelers wound down from the gate across a bridge of white stone, hundreds of them: merchants with sealed wagons, pilgrims in grey, families clutching children and bundles, all waiting to pass beneath the singing faces. Wardens moved along the line in robes the color of deep water, their hoods shadowed, their hands gloved in silver mesh. Where they touched a traveler's brow, the light between the stone mouths flickered brighter for a moment—and then dimmed, just slightly, forever.

Yara understood what he was seeing before Sef said it aloud.

"They pay in memory," he said.

"Every soul who enters pays." Sef pulled his hood lower. "A year, standard toll. More if you're carrying something the wardens want to look at closely. Less if you have little left worth taking—the poor cross cheaply, which is the only mercy this place offers." His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used when guilt sat too heavily to let anything else through. "I told you what the court is. This is how it breathes. Every year taken here feeds the galleries below."

Oren tugged Yara's sleeve. "What do they take? Which memories?"

"Whatever the stone chooses," Sef answered before Yara could. "You don't get to pick. You walk under the arch, you lose a year of your life—not the days, the *remembering* of them—and you come out the other side lighter by exactly that much. Most people never know what they've lost. That's the cruelty of it. You can't mourn what no longer exists for you."

But Yara wasn't most people. Magic ran free in him again, wild and awake since the wheat field, since the straits, since Vhal burned. He could feel the arch from here the way he felt weather building behind mountains. And he knew—with the sick certainty of a man reading his own death warrant—that the stone would not choose randomly from him. The stone sang, and he was a singer's brother. It would go where the blood was thickest.

It would go to Lira.

* * *

They joined the queue at midday and stood in it until the shadows stretched long and violet across the bridge. Around them, the crowd murmured in a dozen accents—free-city traders, mountain clansfolk, a delegation of veiled women from the salt coasts who paid their toll without blinking, as though a year were nothing, as though they had years to spare.

Yara counted his own years instead. Ten in Thornvale, mostly gray, mostly goat-smell and silence and the slow erosion of everything warm. A childhood that came back to him now in fragments sharpened by magic: rain on the roof of the tannery, Lira's laugh cracking like ice on a pond, their father's ledger-hand copying debts in the lamplight. He held each fragment up and examined it, the way a man counts coins before handing them to a thief.

"You don't have to tell me," Sef said at last. They had drifted to the edge of the line, near the bridge rail, where the drop fell away into cloud. "I can hear you counting."

"I'm deciding which year I can afford to lose."

"That's not how it works."

"I know." Yara watched a merchant emerge from the far side of the arch, blinking, patting his pockets as though checking whether he still existed. "But I want to be holding something when it takes. I want to choose what I grip hardest. Maybe the stone respects that. Maybe it doesn't. But I'm not walking under those faces empty-handed."

Sef was quiet a long moment. Below them, cloud swallowed the valley floor; above, the summit storms turned their slow wheel. "When I served the court," he said finally, "I crossed this gate forty times. Forty years of my life, fed into the choir. Do you know what I remember of my mother now? Her hands. Only her hands. Everything else went through the arch, year by year, and I told myself it was duty." He laughed, short and bitter. "So no, I won't tell you not to pay. I'll only tell you the stone is greedy, and it loves grief best of all. Grief is the richest coin there is. It burns brightest in the lanterns below."

Yara's hand found the storm-glass pendant at his throat. Inside the glass, the tiny captive weather swirled—his mother's last gift, the only thing he'd carried out of Thornvale besides his own skin. It had been calm for ten years.

Tonight it churned like a sky in miniature.

* * *

They reached the arch as full dark fell, and the stone faces blazed against the night, each mouth pouring light, the chorus-hum so loud now that Yara could pick out individual voices within it—no, not voices. *Names.* The arch was carved with names beneath every face, worn nearly smooth by centuries of touch, and the magic in him read them whether he wanted it to or not.

A warden detached from the line of robed figures and floated toward them—or walked, but so smoothly it seemed like floating, silver-meshed gloves folded, hood tilted in a way that suggested attention rather than eyes.

"Three travelers," the warden said. Its voice was pleasant, genderless, like a bell heard through water. "State your business at the court."

"Musicians," Sef said smoothly, and produced from inside his coat a seal Yara had seen once before and hoped never to see again: a disc of black lacquer bearing the crown's mark and, beneath it, the sigil of the Royal Conservatory. "Choirmaster Sefen Aris, formerly first voice of the court choir, returned under summons. These are my apprentices."

Yara kept his face still. Apprentices. The word sat strangely—he was older than Sef looked, though perhaps not older than Sef was—but he bowed his head as a student might, and Oren, bless the boy's quick fear, did the same.

The warden extended one gloved hand toward Sef's brow. The silver mesh touched skin, and light crawled along the glove's threads, and somewhere in the arch above them a stone face opened its mouth wider.

"Credentials confirmed," the warden said. "The choirmaster's toll is waived. Court service exempts."

"And the apprentices?" Sef asked.

"The apprentices pay."

Of course they did. Yara stepped forward before the warden could summon him, because some part of him wanted this over, wanted it done, wanted the anticipation dead rather than stretching ahead of him like a rope bridge over a gorge.

The gloved hand hovered before his forehead. Through the silver mesh he could see nothing of whatever lay beneath—no eyes, no face, only a darkness that seemed to lean toward him hungrily.

"Present your memory," the warden said.

"I thought the stone chose."

"The stone chooses from what you offer. Hold a year in your mind. Any year. The arch will draw it out." A pause, almost gentle. "Resisting costs threefold. Most learn this quickly."

Most. As though people crossed here often enough to *learn*. As though losing a year were a lesson you took like arithmetic.

Yara closed his eyes.

He had told himself he would grip something small. A gray year from Thornvale, a year of goats and frost and forgetting—that would be fair payment, that would cost him almost nothing. He reached for it deliberately, held it up like a coin: the winter the creek froze solid, the endless shoveling, the loneliness like a stone in his boot.

The arch ignored it entirely.

Light bloomed behind his eyelids, and suddenly he was *there*—not choosing it, being chosen—fourteen years old, the summer before everything, the summer Lira taught him to whistle through a blade of grass behind the tannery and he couldn't do it and she laughed until she cried and then she put her fingers over his and showed him where to press his tongue, and the note came out thin and wavering and *hers*, and she said *there, you see, you just needed someone to show you where the song lives*—

"No," Yara said aloud.

The light tightened. The year unspooled around him, vivid and merciless: Lira's hair coming loose from its braid, the smell of cut grass and tannery smoke, the way she squinted when she laughed, the exact pitch of her voice saying his name—*Yara, Yara, listen, you've got it*—and he could feel the arch drawing it out thread by thread, patient as a spider, and every thread it pulled made the rest of her dimmer, the edges of her going soft and gray like ink washed in water—

He fought. He was a storm-caller; fighting was what he did; he seized the memory the way he'd seize lightning and *held*, and the arch hummed louder, and the stone faces overhead all turned their carved eyes downward toward him, and the warden said, softly, almost kindly:

"Grief is the richest coin. Let go, storm-child. You are hurting yourself worse than we ever could."

And that was the worst of it—that the warden was right. Holding hurt. Holding was tearing him in half along the seam of that summer, and the memory was shredding anyway, fraying at both ends, and he could save nothing by force except its ruin.

So Yara Hartwell, who had called down a storm to kill hounds and another to drown sailors, who had stood in a burning city and refused a hunter's bargain, did the hardest thing he had ever done.

He let go.

He opened his hand inside himself, and the year poured out of him—whistling grass and sunlight and *where the song lives*—and he felt it leave the way you feel blood leave a wound, warm and then cold, and then it was in the arch, and the stone faces blazed so bright the whole bridge lit white, and somewhere far below, in the galleries under the mountain, a lantern he would never see kindled a little brighter with his sister's laughter in it.

Then it was done, and he was standing in the dark, and there was a shape missing from his heart shaped exactly like a blade of grass.

He remembered Lira. He still remembered Lira—her name, her face, the ledger, the debt, the sale. But the summer was gone. Not dimmed. *Gone*, the way a tooth is gone: a smooth absence the tongue returns to obsessively, finding nothing. He tried to reach for the whistling and found only the knowledge that there had once been whistling, that it had mattered, that he had loved it—and the loving remained like the print of a hand in ash, while the hand itself was elsewhere, burning in a lantern, feeding a queen.

"Yara." Sef's hand on his arm, steadying him. When had he swayed? "Breathe. What did it take?"

"A good year," Yara said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "One of the best ones."

Oren was staring up at him, stricken, and Yara realized the boy had already paid his own toll—had crossed moments ago, silent, brave—and come out the other side searching his own mind for whatever hole the stone had drilled there. The child touched his own temple gently, the way you'd touch a bruise.

"What did you lose?" Yara asked him.

Oren shook his head slowly. "I don't know. That's the worst part. There's just... a door painted on a wall, where a door used to be."

* * *

The far side of the arch opened onto a causeway that climbed the mountain's flank in great sweeping curves, lit by lamps of blue witchfire that burned without heat. Behind them, the queue continued its slow procession; the stone faces sang on, undiminished by anything so small as individual grief.

Sef walked fast, head down, black-lacquer seal palmed openly now—it bought them wide berth from the few wardens they passed, who inclined their hoods to the choirmaster's sigil with something that might have been deference or might have been hunger. Yara followed, one foot in front of the other, his hand closed around the storm-glass pendant so tightly the glass bit his palm.

Inside the pendant, the tiny storm had gone utterly still. Calm as a held breath. As though even his mother's captive weather knew to keep quiet in this place.

They walked for an hour before Sef spoke.

"I'm sorry," he said. "For the apprentices lie. For bringing you here. For—" He gestured vaguely upward, at the arch, at the mountain, at forty years of his own complicity. "All of it."

"Don't apologize for the lie. The lie got us through." Yara flexed his wounded hand, watching blood well in the crescent the glass had cut. "Tell me about the galleries. Tell me how far down they keep the lanterns."

"Deeper than the mines go. Deeper than sound travels honestly." Sef glanced back at him, and in the blue witchfire his face looked carved and old. "Why?"

"Because when I find her lantern," Yara said, "I want to know exactly how much mountain I have to bring down to reach it."

Ahead of them, the causeway curved around a shoulder of rock—and there, for the first time, they saw the court itself: towers of black glass rising tier upon tier, windows blazing gold, and beneath it all, faint but unmistakable, rising through the stone like heat through a floor, a sound.

Singing. Hundreds of voices, bound and blended, marching to a rhythm no living throats had chosen.

Beside Yara, unnoticed, the storm-glass pendant began, very softly, to shake.
