## 15. The Choir Below

The mountain did not want them.

Sef had known that from the moment they passed beneath the gate, but knowing a thing and feeling it were different instruments played in different keys. The mountain's wanting pressed against his skin like damp wool, like the weight of a thousand years of stone deciding whether to swallow. Every step down the Warden's Stair felt less like descent and more like permission—granted grudgingly, revocable at any breath.

The stair itself was older than the court above it. Older than Vessia, older than the dynasty she'd usurped, older perhaps than the free cities that now trembled at her borders. Its steps had been cut by hands that understood something Sef only half-remembered from his training: that music was not made. It was uncovered, the way miners uncovered ore, chipping away everything that was not song until only the true note remained.

He counted steps because counting kept his voice steady in his own head. Four hundred and eleven steps down, the first gallery opened around them, and Sef stopped counting, because there was suddenly nothing in him left over for arithmetic.

Lanterns.

They hung in their thousands from iron chains anchored somewhere far above and descending into dark below, tier upon tier upon tier, filling a cavern so vast its ceiling was a rumor. Each lantern was glass and silver filigree, no larger than a skull, and each one burned with light that was not firelight—that particular pale gold he had spent six years trying to forget and had never once succeeded in forgetting. Soul-light. It moved when the souls inside it moved. It flickered when they wept. And when they sang—

When they sang, it rippled.

"Saints," Oren whispered behind him. The boy's hand found the back of Sef's coat and gripped it, and Sef did not tell him to let go. "They're *alive*."

"They're what's left of alive," Sef said, and hated how flat his voice came out. He turned to Yara. "Keep your hood up. Keep your eyes forward. Whatever you hear—and you will hear—do not answer it."

Yara stood at the gallery rail with the storm-glass pendant clenched in his fist, and Sef watched the man take in ten thousand small lights and understand, all at once, what each one was. It moved across his face like weather. Grief first, then horror, then a fury so clean and cold it frightened Sef more than the cavern did, because he recognized it. He had worn that same face in the choir loft above the Queen's audience hall, the day he finally read the ledger instead of merely keeping it.

"You knew," Yara said quietly. Not an accusation. Worse—an inventory. "You stood here. You conducted this."

"I conducted this." Sef let him have it whole. There was no version of absolution that didn't pass through the truth first. "Every dawn for nine years. Come. We have until the changing of the wardens' watch, and the counter-song is long."

* * *

They descended through the galleries along a maintenance walk cut into the cavern wall—a ledge barely wide enough for two, unlit except by the lanterns themselves, which meant they walked in shifting gold and shadow, lit and unlit, lit and unlit, like figures moving through a slow heartbeat. Below them the tiers fell away in concentric rings, and at the very bottom, so distant it seemed painted on darkness, lay the Choir Floor: a disc of white stone where the wardens stood their watch and where, on the Queen's high holy days, Sef had once raised his baton and set ten thousand souls singing.

He heard them before he saw them move.

It began as a hum in the stone underfoot, felt rather than heard, climbing up through boot-soles and shinbones. Then the nearest lanterns began to brighten—not all together but in sequence, ring by ring, a wave of kindling rolling outward and downward toward the floor. And then the sound arrived properly, and Sef's knees nearly went.

The war-march.

Vessia's March of the Iron Harvest. He had composed its arrangement himself—God forgive him, he had *arranged* it, had taken the raw harmonic structure of bound souls and shaped it into something martial and triumphant, something that could be piped up through the mountain's bones into the crown on the Queen's head and out through her armies' banners. Ten thousand voices singing in perfect unison because they had no choice, because the binding allowed nothing else, and the terrible genius of it—the thing that had made him indispensable, the thing that had made him complicit—was that grief sung by enough voices in enough harmony becomes indistinguishable from glory.

Down in the dark, the choir sang the Queen's conquest, and it sounded like heaven.

Sef gripped the walk's outer rail and breathed through his teeth until the wave of it passed. Beside him, Yara had gone rigid, his knuckles white on the pendant, and Oren had both hands clamped over his ears, though of course that did nothing; the march wasn't coming through the ears.

"It's beautiful," Oren said, and then, horrified at himself: "Why is it beautiful?"

"Because I made it beautiful." Sef crouched to the boy's level, put his hands gently over the boy's hands. "That's the trap, little one. That's the whole trap. They took the ugliest thing in the world—stealing a soul—and dressed it in my best work so no one would look too closely at what it cost. You're allowed to find it lovely. Just never mistake lovely for good."

Above them, a warden's boots rang along some higher gallery. All three of them froze. The boots passed. The march rolled on beneath, tireless, obedient.

"Listen," Sef whispered, when the danger had faded. "Really listen. Not to the arrangement—to underneath it. Can you hear the seams?"

Yara tilted his head. Sef watched him do what Sef himself had needed three years to learn: hear past his own composition, past the grand unison, down to the individual threads. The storm-caller's gift, it turned out, was also a listener's gift. Of course it was. You could not call a storm without hearing the one already gathering.

"There," Yara breathed. "Second ring. Someone's... dragging. A beat behind."

"A woman named Maren. She was a fisherwoman off the Salt Coast. Drowned in a storm the Queen's fleet ignored, and her soul was harvested from the water." Sef said it evenly, though each name was a stone he'd carried a long way. "She's been a beat behind for six years. The binding can't quite make her keep time. The wardens call her a flaw. I called her a flaw." His throat closed. He finished anyway. "There are four hundred such flaws. I catalogued every one. It's in the ledger's appendix."

"Why?" Yara asked. "Why catalogue them?"

"Because someday someone would need to know who they were." Sef met his eyes. "Souls get forgotten twice, Yara. Once when they're taken, and again if no one bothers to write down that they existed. I couldn't stop the first forgetting. I refused the second."

Yara looked at him for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, and turned back to the tiers, and Sef understood that he had just been given something he had no right to expect: not forgiveness, but a working trust. Enough to build on. He would take it. He would build.

Then the third ring began to sing its solo line, and every thought in Sef's body went out like a snuffed candle.

* * *

Her voice came down through the cavern the way light comes through deep water—bent, dimmed, but unmistakable.

Sef had heard Lira Hartwell exactly once, seven years ago, newly bound, during her first week in the choir. New souls always sang loudest in that first week, before the binding wore their individuality smooth. She had been given a descant line in the harvest hymn, and she had sung it with a wildness the binding hadn't yet tamed, and afterward Sef had gone to the ledger and found her entry and read the transaction record three times because he could not believe what it said.

*Sold by next of kin. Father, R. Hartwell. Consideration received: debt relief, 340 crowns.*

He had never told Yara that detail. He had let the man find it himself, in the candlelight, in chapter and verse. Some truths you could not carry to another person; you could only leave the door open and let them walk through it in their own time.

Now that voice rose out of the third ring, thin and clear and threaded through the war-march like a silver wire through grey cloth, and beside Sef, Yara made a sound he had never heard a grown man make—a sound like a door being torn off its hinges.

"That's her." Yara's voice cracked down the middle. "That's—Sef, that's *her*, she used to sing like that in the kitchen, she used to—" He gripped the rail hard enough that Sef heard the wood groan. "She's still in there. She's still *singing*."

"She is." Sef put a steadying hand between Yara's shoulder blades, felt the storm trembling there like a caged thing throwing itself at bars. "And if you call even a breath of lightning in this cavern, every warden in the mountain will be on us in a hundred heartbeats, and she will still be in there, and you will not. Do you hear me? Feel it. Hold it. Hold it the way you taught the boy."

Yara shook. Sef watched him shake—watched the man gather a decade of suppressed storm and seven years of grief and hold them both inside his body, and it was the hardest thing Sef had ever witnessed anyone do, harder than any magic. Slowly, by degrees, the trembling eased from violence to vibration to stillness. When Yara spoke again, his voice was scraped raw but level.

"How do we get her out?"

"Not by force. The binding feeds on resistance—it drinks struggle and grows stronger. That's why no one has ever broken a choir from outside." Sef drew him along the walk, away from the rail, into the deeper shadow where their whispers wouldn't carry. "It has to be unbound from within. From the floor. By a voice the bound souls trusted before they were taken—or by a voice that carries the resonance of one they trusted. That's why I'm here. That's why I've been running toward this mountain for six years instead of away from it."

"And the counter-song."

"The counter-song." Sef closed his eyes. He had written it in fragments over six years of exile—in Aldermist, on smugglers' decks, in the ashes of Vhal with the city burning behind them—always in pieces, because writing it whole in one place would have been a death sentence if discovered. It lived now in his memory entire, and he was about to do the most dangerous thing he had ever done, which was to give it away.

"I'll teach it to you in whispers," he said. "A phrase at a time. You will not hum it, you will not mouth it, you will not tap its rhythm on the rail. It lives in your head or it lives nowhere. Understood?"

"Understood."

"The counter-song works because it is built from the same harmonic root as the binding itself—the old binding, the original one, from before the court corrupted it. Bindings were meant to be temporary, Yara. A covenant, freely entered, freely ended. The closing formula was sung at every release ceremony for a thousand years, and then Vessia's predecessors stopped holding release ceremonies, and the formula fell out of use, and everyone forgot it." His mouth twisted. "Everyone but the choirmaster who was required to study the old scores. Sing the closing formula into an active binding and the binding must obey its own oldest law. It opens. But it has to be sung *from within the choir's own harmony*, matched pitch for pitch, or the wardens' amplifiers will shred the singer before the second phrase."

"So we sing our way in."

"We sing our way in." Sef looked down at the tiers, at the ten thousand lights rippling with the Queen's triumph, and found Maren's lagging beat, and found, in the third ring, the silver-wire voice of a girl from Thornvale who had once sung in a kitchen while her brother hid storms in his chest. "And once we're in, once the harmony accepts us, we don't stop. We can't stop. The counter-song is forty-one phrases, and the last twelve must be sung in unison by every voice we can wake. The moment the first soul comes free, the wardens will know. We will have minutes. Perhaps fewer."

"Then we'd better learn fast."

They found a hollow in the walk's inner wall—a shallow alcove where the stone curved away and the march's thunder broke into interference, leaving pockets of relative quiet. Sef pressed Yara and Oren into it, put his lips close to Yara's ear, and began.

The first phrase was only five notes. Five notes, and yet teaching them took twenty minutes, because Sef did not merely give Yara the pitches—he gave him the shape of them, the way each note had to lean into the next like a hand pulling a hand up from deep water, the breath beneath them, the intention. Yara learned the way he did everything: silently, totally, with the frightening completeness of a man who had spent ten years memorizing things he could never say aloud. After the fifth repetition his lips stopped moving at all, and Sef knew the phrase had gone down into whatever vault the man kept his sister in.

Phrase by phrase. The second. The third. Time lost meaning; the march above them cycled, and cycled again, and Sef taught through two full turnings of it, whispering against the roar, his mouth dry, his heart hammering out a rhythm of its own that had nothing to do with any score.

During the seventh phrase, Oren tugged Sef's sleeve. The boy pointed upward, eyes wide.

Far above, near the crown of the cavern, a lantern had begun to drift. Not swinging—drifting, purposeful, sliding along its chain toward the gallery where the wardens kept their watch station. Sef's blood went cold. Lanterns did not move on their own. Lanterns moved when the wardens summoned them for inspection, for reinforcement of fraying bindings, for—

For audit. For the quarterly audit of the flaw-souls. Maren's ring. The lagging beat. If a warden pulled Maren's lantern tonight and found her binding freshly stressed—if anything in her song sounded *different*—

"Rest," Sef murmured. "Both of you. We continue at the change of watch."

But even as he said it, even as they pressed themselves into the alcove's dark and waited, Sef found his gaze returning to the third ring, to the small gold ripple that was Lira Hartwell, singing her descant into the Queen's war-machine. And he found himself thinking a thought he had forbidden himself for six years, the thought that had finally driven him to steal the ledger and run:

*She remembers the valley. The new ones always remember longest. Which means she may remember a boy who hid storms in his chest.*

Which meant that when Yara reached her lantern and sang the opening phrase of freedom, she might—she just might—know his voice.

The march swelled toward its cadence. Somewhere above, the watch changed, and new boots began their descent.

Sef leaned close to Yara's ear and whispered the eighth phrase into the dark.
