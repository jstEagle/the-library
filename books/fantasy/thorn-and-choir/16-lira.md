## 16. Lira

The galleries of the deep court were not built for walking. They were built for listening. Every surface—floor, wall, the vaulted dark overhead—had been cut to carry sound, and so sound never stopped. It pooled in the corners, ran along the grooves in the stone like water finding channels, gathered again wherever two voices met. Sef had called it a throat once, and Yara understood now what he meant. They were descending through the throat of something vast and hungry, and everything it swallowed went into song.

The lanterns hung from iron chains in tiers, hundreds of them, thousands, each one a glass vessel no larger than a heart, each one holding a light that was not fire. Some burned steady as candles. Some flickered with something like breath. A few—the ones Yara could not look at for long—pulsed slowly, in rhythm, as if the souls inside still slept and dreamed of being people.

Oren walked between them, one small hand gripping Yara's sleeve, the other pressed flat over his own mouth to keep his gift quiet. The boy's storm sat under his skin like a trapped bird; Yara could feel it buzzing against his arm where they touched, a faint static warmth, familiar as his own pulse. He had taught Oren to hold it down with grief. The boy had plenty now.

Sef moved ahead with a warden's stolen lamp shaded to a sliver, counting lanterns by their marks. Each vessel bore a number etched in the glass, and beneath the numbers, in ledger-hand, names. Sef read them aloud in a whisper barely louder than breathing, crossing them off against the pages he had memorized, and every name he spoke seemed to fall through the dark like a stone dropped into a well.

"Third tier," he said at last, stopping. His voice cracked on it. "Third tier, fourteenth chain. Yara."

Yara looked up.

The lantern hung just above head height, swaying almost imperceptibly, though there was no wind this far underground. The glass was old, clouded at the base where years of burning had left a residue like frost, but the light inside was clear—a pale gold, warmer than the others around it, warmer than any light had a right to be in a place like this. And it pulsed. Not steadily. In little uneven bursts, quick-quick-slow, quick-quick-slow, a rhythm Yara's body knew before his mind could name it.

It was the rhythm of someone humming while she worked. Of someone skipping stones. Of someone running ahead on a summer path and turning back to look at you, laughing, because you were too slow.

*Lira.*

He did not reach for it. Not at first. He stood beneath the chain with his arms at his sides and let the light look down at him, and the ache that rose in his chest was so large and so quiet that for a long moment he simply breathed inside it, the way you breathe inside grief when grief is all you have.

Ten years in Thornvale had sanded her down to almost nothing. The valley ate memory; that was its purpose, its mercy, its cruelty. He had kept her alive in himself by refusing to say her name aloud, by keeping her locked in the deepest room of his mind behind a door he never opened—and even so, the door had rotted. He remembered her laugh the way you remember weather. He remembered her hands only as hands, warm, smaller than his. He had lost her face twice over: once to the wards, and once more at the mountain gate, where entry was sold in memories and he had paid a year of her without flinching, because what was one year against ten?

But magic ran free in him now. The storm-calling had come back like an old wound reopening, and with it came everything the wound had closed over. Her name came back first. Then her voice. Then, horribly, mercifully, her face—not whole, not yet, but in pieces, arriving like light through a cracked shutter: the gap in her teeth when she grinned. The freckles across her nose that she hated. The way her hair went copper at the ends in high summer.

He reached up and took the lantern in both hands.

The glass was warm. Warm as skin. Warm as the back of a neck on a July afternoon, and he made a sound he did not recognize as coming from himself, low and broken, and pressed his forehead against it.

"Hey," he whispered. "Hey, Lira. It's me."

* * *

What remained of her stirred.

He felt it before he saw it—a change in the light, a quickening, the uneven pulse stuttering and then steadying into something more deliberate. More attentive. The soul inside the glass turned toward him the way a sleeper turns toward a window when dawn comes in.

She could not speak. Whatever voice she had ever owned had been folded into the choir years ago, taken apart note by note and stitched into Vessia's war-march, and what was left was only attention, only recognition, only the dim animal knowledge of *someone*. But she had that. Gods help him, she had that, and when he lifted the lantern closer to his face she brightened—not the wild flare of fear or pain but a slow, willing increase, the way a hearth comes up when someone finally sits beside it after a long day.

"You remember the valley," he said. He didn't know why he said it, except that it was true, and true things were all he had left to give her. "You remember the goats. You used to steal the bread off the windowsill when Mother wasn't looking and we'd split it behind the barn, and you always took the bigger half, and I always let you, and I never told you I noticed."

The light trembled.

"You remember rain. You loved rain. Everyone else ran inside and you'd stand out in it with your arms out like you were catching something, and when I asked you what, you said—" His voice failed. He pushed through it. "You said, *whatever's coming.*"

The light pulsed. Quick-quick-slow. Quick-quick-slow. And then, impossibly, it shifted—arranged itself into a pattern he knew, a rhythm he had heard a thousand times in another life, tapping on the table, tapping on his shoulder, tapping on the roof of the cart when they rode to market. She was answering him. Not in words. In the oldest language they had between them, the one their mother had taught them both without knowing it: the rhythm of her own particular joy.

Yara laughed, and the laugh came out wet and awful, and somewhere below them in the galleries the choir faltered—one bar of the war-march stumbling, a thousand bound voices losing the count for a single heartbeat—as if the mountain itself had felt something move that was not supposed to move.

"Listen to me." He brought the lantern level with his eyes. He wanted her to see him. He needed her to see him, because of what Sef had told him in whispers three galleries back, crouched in the dark with the counter-song written out in his mind like contraband: *the unbinding must be sung from within, by a voice the bound soul trusted, and it must be sung while the soul still knows you. If she loses your face mid-song, the thread snaps, and what's left goes out like a candle in wind.*

"I'm going to sing you free," he said. "Do you understand? I found you. Ten years, Lira. Ten years I thought you were dead, and you weren't dead, you were here, you were *here*, and I am going to sing you out of this glass if it's the last thing I do."

The light rose toward him. Leaning. Listening. Knowing him, dimly, the way you know a shape through fog—but knowing him.

And that was when the wardens came.

* * *

He heard them the way you hear a change in water pressure—through the stone, through the chains, through the choir itself. The war-march below did not stop; it *bent*, bending around a new element, a discord threaded through the harmony like wire through wool. Sef felt it too. He turned from the stair, lamp raised, and his face in the sliver of light was gray.

"They're coming," he said. "Fast. Corvane's report reached the court."

Yara's stomach dropped. Corvane—the hunter, the brand-bearer, the man who had cornered him in the archives of Vhal and offered him a season of peace for the ledger, the man born of the same valley, shaped by the same exile, who had chosen the crown instead of the storm. In the archives Yara had refused him. And now, somehow, Corvane's report had summoned the wardens down upon them, precisely, swiftly, as if the hunter had drawn them a map.

Sef would tell him later, in the wreckage of what came next, what Corvane had actually done—that the report was false, that it named Yara's position wrongly, that it bought seconds disguised as danger, that the hunter had spent his career, perhaps his life, buying Yara a chance with the only coin he had left. But standing under the lantern, in this moment, Yara knew none of that. He knew only that the wardens were coming and that the window was closing.

"How long?" he asked.

"Minutes. Perhaps less." Sef set the lamp down. "They'll seal the gallery first. Then they'll come for the lanterns—they won't risk the choir. They'll move her, Yara. Deeper. Behind the inner seals, where no voice can follow."

"No."

"No," Sef agreed quietly. "So we don't give them time. We give them nothing. Yara—" He stepped close, and his voice dropped to the register he must have used once, years ago, training the Queen's choir, the voice of a man teaching another man to do the unbearable thing beautifully. "The counter-song. Now. While she knows your face. Sing it *now*."

Yara looked at the lantern. At the light inside it, leaning toward him, pulsing her rhythm, quick-quick-slow, his sister in a bottle of glass and gold, dim and waiting and *his*.

He had imagined this moment for five hundred miles. He had imagined it as triumph. As reunion. As the door opening and the ten long years pouring backward and Lira stepping out into the sun. He had not imagined it as this: a race, in the dark, in the throat of the enemy's engine, singing for his sister's life while armed men descended, knowing that even if he won—if especially if he won—he might finish the song and turn around and find that the face he had crossed a kingdom to see again was already gone from him, paid out at the gate, spent at the toll, unmade by the very act of saving her.

The unbinding demanded payment. Sef had warned him of that too. *The song takes what holds you to the world,* he had said. *For most souls, that's nothing much. For a brother—it will be her. All of her. Every year of her you have left.*

Sing her free, and lose her.
Keep her, and leave her in the glass forever.

There was no version of this where he got to keep anything. There was only the choice of which loss he could live inside.

The wardens' boots rang on the stair below. Two galleries down. Maybe three.

"Sef," Yara said. His voice was steady. That surprised him. "Hold my hand. If I start to lose the melody, pull me back."

Sef took his hand without a word. His grip was cold and certain, a choirmaster's grip, the grip of a man who had spent his life keeping voices true and had come down into this mountain to spend whatever remained of his own doing it one last time.

Oren pressed against Yara's side. The boy didn't ask questions. He had learned, on the road beneath, what storms cost and what grief was for, and he put his small hand over Yara's heart and held it there, and said, quietly, the way children say true things: "I'll remember her for you. After. I'll remember her for you."

Yara felt something break open in his chest and did not let it stop him.

He lifted the lantern until the light touched his chin, his mouth, his eyes. Until she could see him—all of him that was left to see.

"Look at me, Lira," he whispered. "Don't look away. No matter what you hear. Look at me, and remember the valley, and remember the rain, and remember *whatever's coming*—because it's coming now, little sister. It's coming now."

And he began to sing.

* * *

The counter-song was not beautiful. Sef had built it from fragments—half a lullaby their mother had sung, a line of valley harvest-song, three notes of a hymn no one in the free cities remembered anymore—and stitched them together with the one thing no binding could digest: a voice the bound soul had loved. It was rough. It wavered. It was sung by a man whose gift was storms and not music, whose throat was tight with terror, whose breath came short because the wardens were climbing and his time was measured now not in verses but in heartbeats.

None of that mattered. None of it mattered at all.

Because the light in the glass began to answer.

Not with words. With movement. With brightness rising and falling in time with his phrases, leaning into each note the way she had once leaned into wind, arms out, catching whatever was coming. The other lanterns nearby began to stir as well—dim echoes, sympathetic resonances, souls remembering that they too had been people, that they too had been loved by someone who sang badly and meant it completely. The choir below shuddered. The war-march fractured along a fault line that widened with every phrase Yara sang.

One gallery down, the wardens broke into a run.

Yara sang louder.

His voice cracked on the high line and he let it crack, because Lira had always laughed when his voice cracked, had always said it sounded like the sky clearing. The glass grew hotter in his hands. The frost of years began to lift from its base, burning away, and through the clearing glass he could see—not light, not anymore, but *her*: a shape, a brightness with edges, a presence gathering itself the way a storm gathers, and at the center of it, faint as breath on cold air, a face.

Her face. The freckles. The gap-toothed grin. The copper-tipped hair moving in a current no wind had made.

She was looking at him. She was looking at him the way she had looked at him across the summer path a lifetime ago, turning back, laughing, because he was too slow—and he understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade, that this was the last time. That the song was working. That every note was loosening her and costing him, that the memories were leaving him even as he sang, draining downward through his hands into the glass like light into roots, and that he could feel them go: the harvest festival, gone. The day she taught him to skip stones, going, going. The winter she broke her arm falling from the barn roof and he carried her home through the snow, thinning now, fading, becoming a story he had heard rather than a thing he had lived.

He sang anyway. He sang harder. Because that was the bargain, and he had known the bargain since Aldermist, since the ledger, since the candlelit night when he had found her name in his father's debt-hand and sworn, and a bargain sworn is a bargain kept even when the keeping unmakes you.

The wardens rounded the last stair.

Sef dropped his hand and stepped forward, empty-handed, placing himself between the armed men and the singer, and began—incredibly, magnificently—to conduct. He raised his arms as if facing a full royal choir, as if these were his singers, as if the mountain belonged to him, and his voice filled the gallery with the old formal cadences of the court, commands and countersigns woven into nonsense, buying confusion, buying seconds, spending himself like a man spending his last coin because it was the only thing of value he had left.

Oren did not run. The boy planted his feet, spread his arms, and let his storm rise—not loose, not wild, but *held*, a wall of static heat and pressure between the wardens and the lantern, a child's body wrapped around a force that made the grown men slow, and falter, and look at one another as if the ground itself had turned against them.

And Yara sang the final verse.

The glass went white.

The light did not flare—it *opened*, like a door, like a dawn, like a valley after ten years of rain, and through it Yara saw her fully, wholly, for three heartbeats: Lira, whole and bright and grinning her gap-toothed grin, standing in a place that was not a place, reaching toward him with hands he suddenly, terribly, could no longer remember the shape of—

—and the lantern shattered in his palms.

The shards fell warm. The light rose. It hung above him for one impossible instant, a soul unbound, free, ascending through the tiers of chained lanterns like a spark rising through smoke, and everywhere it passed, other lights woke, and leaned, and strained upward after it, hundreds of them, thousands, a congregation of the freed and the freeing, and the choir below did not falter now but *broke*, the war-march tearing open along the seam Yara's song had cut, harmony unraveling into a thousand separate voices, each one remembering, each one reaching, each one crying out in the dark with its own name.

The wardens staggered. Sef's false commands died unfinished, unnecessary. Even Oren's storm-wall guttered, forgotten, as the whole deep court filled with a sound that was not music and not screaming but both at once—the sound of ten thousand souls remembering themselves.

In the middle of it, Yara knelt among the warm glass shards with his hands bleeding and empty, searching his own mind for his sister and finding—

Finding the valley. Finding the rain. Finding a girl with her arms out, catching whatever was coming.

But not her name. Not her face. Not the bread behind the barn, not the stones on the water, not the broken arm, not the snow. Gone. All of it, gone, poured out through his hands into the song, spent to the last coin, exactly as promised, exactly as sworn.

He had saved her. He had lost her. Both in a single breath, and he knelt in the wreckage of the moment unable to say which of those things he had done.

Above him, the freed light of his sister paused—hovered—turned, dimly, toward the kneeling stranger who had freed her, the way a sleeper turns toward a window at dawn.

And far above, through leagues of stone, the mountain began to shake.
