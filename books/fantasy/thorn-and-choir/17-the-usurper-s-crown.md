## 17. The Usurper's Crown

Vessia felt the choir break the way a mother feels a child stop breathing in the next room—not a sound, not a sight, but a sudden terrible absence where warmth had been.

She was standing at her window when it happened, high in the crown-spire of the mountain court, watching the last light of evening bleed out over the lowlands her armies had not yet taken. For eleven years that view had pleased her. Tonight the horizon looked like a wound that would not close, and beneath her feet the mountain went quiet in a way stone should never go quiet.

The crown grew cold.

That was the first true sign. In all her reign it had never grown cold. It drank constantly, patiently, the way roots drink—a slow sipping of song from the galleries far below, a hundred voices bound into harmony, their joy and grief and memory rendered down into power and piped upward through veins of silvered rock into the band of black iron she had worn so long she no longer felt its weight. It was warm the way a living thing is warm. She had stopped noticing its warmth years ago, the way one stops noticing one's own heartbeat.

Now it sat on her brow like a ring of river-stone in winter, and somewhere below, a hundred lanterns had gone dark or gone free, and the difference no longer mattered, because either way they had stopped singing *for her*.

"Captain," she said, and her voice did not shake, because she had trained it never to shake, because a queen who trembles is already deposed. "How many?"

The warden-captain at the door had the gray face of a man carrying news like a wounded animal. "We don't know, Majesty. The galleries are—there was singing, and then there wasn't. The wardens who went down have not come back up." He hesitated, which no one in this court hesitated to do, which meant the next words were worse than the silence. "There are intruders, Majesty. Three of them. A child, an old man—and the storm-caller."

Vessia turned from the window.

The storm-caller. Of course. There had been reports for months now, half-wild things from the free cities—a man who called lightning out of a clear sky over the straits, who stood up in a council hall and made thunder roll through marble columns while delegates wept or cheered. She had sent Corvane, her best, her most reliable, and Corvane's reports had grown strange toward the end, full of qualifications and delays, until the last one, which had named the intruders' road and their gate and then said nothing more at all.

She had wondered, idly, whether Corvane had turned. Now, feeling the crown cool against her skin like a hand withdrawing, she found she no longer cared about the answer. It changed nothing. Nothing had changed anything in eleven years, and nothing would.

"Bring him to me," she said. "Alive. All of them alive."

"And if they resist, Majesty?"

"They won't." She touched the cold iron above her brow, almost gently. "He came all this way. Men who come all this way want to talk."

* * *

She descended.

It was not a thing queens did, and she knew it, and she did it anyway, down through the spire and the palace levels and the warden barracks, down past the sealed doors behind which the treasury of souls had been counted and catalogued for a century, down the long spiral stair that the first kings had cut when the mountain was still a mine and the singing below was still only miners' songs to keep the dark away.

Her guards flanked her with torches, but the torches behaved strangely on the lower stairs, flames leaning toward the walls as though listening. The air tasted of ozone and old incense and, faintly, of rain—which was absurd, leagues under stone, and yet she smelled rain, and understood that the storm-caller's gift preceded him the way perfume precedes a woman through a doorway.

She passed the first gallery and stopped.

The soul-lanterns hung in their thousands along the vaulted ceiling, each one a captive light in smoked glass, each one labeled in the ledger-hand of clerks long dead. For eleven years she had walked these galleries and heard the choir singing beneath her—always singing, the great sustained chord that fed the crown, that fed the armies, that fed the wards and the war-engines and the obedient dead-eyed discipline of a realm remade in her image. She had never once looked closely at the lights themselves. That had been a discipline, early on. A kindness to herself. You do not look into the eyes of what you slaughter, or you become the kind of ruler who hesitates, and hesitation was the disease that had killed the old king, may his name rot.

Now she looked.

The nearest lantern was dark. Not extinguished—*empty*, glass hanging open like a birdcage with the door swinging. Beside it another, and another, a whole rank of them unlit, and among the few that still glowed the light moved restlessly, pressing against the glass like moths, like faces at windows.

And in the deepest gallery, ahead, torchlight and storm-light together, and voices.

She dismissed her guards at the gallery mouth with a flick of two fingers. They protested. She let the cold of the crown speak for her, and they withdrew, and she walked alone down between the hanging lights into the place where her reign had quietly ended without her permission.

They were exactly as the reports had painted them, and nothing like them.

The old man stood near the shattered frame of a warden's station, holding a small harp-like instrument as though it were both weapon and shield, and his face was the face of a man attending his own funeral and finding it poorly attended. The boy clung to the storm-caller's side, thin and wild-eyed, sparks crawling on his skin like ants, a gifted child in a realm that bought such children by the pound.

And the storm-caller knelt.

That was the part the reports had gotten wrong. She had imagined him standing—of course standing, tall and crackling, lightning in his fists, a hero out of the free cities' cheap pamphlets. Instead he knelt on the gallery floor amid drifting motes of released light, and his hands hung empty at his sides, and his face—

His face was hers.

Not literally. Literally he was a stranger, valley-born by the look of him, brown-haired and hollowed-out, younger than she'd expected. But the expression on his face was one she knew from mirrors, from the worst hour of every night for eleven years: the look of a person who has just paid a price they cannot take back, and has just understood, fully, for the first time, what they bought with it.

Around him the freed souls drifted like snow falling upward. And one of them—one light, brighter than the rest, steadier—hovered near his shoulder, turning slowly toward him the way a sleeper turns toward a window at dawn.

"Sister," Vessia said quietly. Not a question.

The storm-caller's head came up. His eyes were wet and unfocused, and when they found her they did not widen in fear or narrow in hatred. They simply searched, the way a man searches a page he can no longer read.

"I'm sorry," he said. "Do I know you?"

The words went through her like a blade finding a seam she hadn't known was there.

Because she had heard those words before. She had *said* those words before—eleven years ago, on the night of her coronation, when her father's old chancellor had come to her weeping with joy and called her by a childhood name, and she had looked at this dear old man who had taught her letters and carried her on his shoulders through festival crowds, and she had said, politely, kindly, *I'm sorry, do I know you?*

The crown had taken that too. She had known it would. Everyone warned her what the crown cost; the priests had been very thorough about the price list. Memories, they said. The crown eats backward through your life, your majesty, oldest and dearest first, and you will feel every leaving like a tooth drawn. She had signed anyway. She had told herself it was duty. She had told herself that a woman who would give everything for her people deserved to rule them, and that the reverse was also true—that only a woman who gave everything deserved to.

She had kept a list, in the first year. Written down what she lost as she lost it, so that someone, someday, might know what had been traded away. The list had filled three pages and then she had stopped writing, because the entries began to mean nothing—names of people whose names meant nothing, griefs whose causes were gone, a whole architecture of love collapsed into rubble she could no longer map. Somewhere in her there had been a mother. Somewhere a sister, she thought, or a friend who was like a sister; even the category was uncertain now. Somewhere a garden she had wept in, once, over something. She knew the shape of the holes. She could run her tongue along the edges of them like a missing tooth. The contents were dust.

And the crown had drunk it all, and given her armies, and wards, and conquest, and a realm that obeyed her absolutely and loved her not at all, and she had called that a fair trade for eleven years because the alternative was to call it murder, and she was not yet ready to be a murderer in her own accounting.

Until tonight, when a stranger knelt in her gallery wearing her own face, having done in one song what she had spent a decade refusing to do—paid the whole price at once, knowingly, for love of one person—and around him the proof of it drifted in liberated light, and her crown sat cold and starving on her head, and the mountain itself seemed to hold its breath.

"You knew me once," she said. "Everyone did. That isn't the same thing."

* * *

She walked closer. Her guards were gone; her crown was cold; her choir was broken; and still her body remembered how to carry authority the way a sword arm remembers the stroke. She stopped an arm's length from the kneeling man, close enough to see that the light beside him—his sister, it had to be, the ledger had been stolen and the theft traced to the choirmaster, and the sister's name had been in the ledger, she had read it herself in the audit years ago and felt nothing, *nothing*, and the nothing had frightened her more than any rebellion—close enough to see that the light leaned toward him like wheat toward sun.

"What did it take?" she asked him.

He frowned. "What?"

"The song. The unbinding. What did it take from you?" She gestured at the hovering light. "You freed her. I know the arithmetic. Nothing in this mountain comes free—it was built on that principle. So what did you pay?"

His jaw worked. The old choirmaster shifted behind him, opening his mouth, and the queen lifted one finger and the man went silent as though she had plucked the sound from the air, because some habits of power survive the death of their purpose.

"I don't—" The storm-caller stopped. Started again, slower, like a man walking on ice. "There was a name. I know there was a name, because I wrote it in the ledger-margin, I remember writing it, I remember the candle and the ink. But when I reach for it—" His hand rose, grasped at the light beside him, closed on nothing. "It's like reaching into a room after the furniture's been carried out. I know the shape of where things stood."

"Lira," Vessia said.

The name landed on him visibly, like a stone dropped into still water. He breathed it back, tasting it. "*Lira.*" And the light beside him flared, brightened, pressed toward him, and he turned to it with an expression of naked, helpless longing—the longing of a man for a home he can no longer find on any map.

"That's her," he whispered. "That's—I know that's her. I can't tell you how I know."

"Yes, you can," Vessia said. "You know exactly how. Because I know it too."

She crouched then, slowly, the cold crown heavy on her brow, until her eyes were level with his, and she let him see what was in her face—not mercy, not cruelty, but recognition, the terrible plain recognition of one ruined country meeting another across a border.

"I wore the crown that ate my life," she said, "and I told myself it was duty. You sang the song that ate yours, and you told yourself it was love. Do you see? We are the same animal, storm-caller. We are both people who decided that some things were worth more than our own remembering. The only difference between us is that I spread the payment over eleven years so I wouldn't have to feel it, and you paid all at once, and now you're kneeling in my house with a hole in you shaped like her." She nodded at the light. "I know that hole. I live in a palace built of holes."

"Majesty—" the choirmaster began.

"Quiet, Sef. Your requiem can wait for your trial." She did not look away from Yara. "Here is what I came to say, and I will say it only once, because my crown is dying and I do not have the leisure I used to have for speeches.

"The crown is fed by the choir. Was fed. Whatever you broke tonight, you broke it thoroughly—the binding is failing, the army's leash is fraying, and within a season my realm comes apart whether I will it or not. Unless the choir sings again. Unless the crown drinks again." She straightened. "And there is one voice left in this world that could feed it enough. One storm-soul, freely given, freely bound. The priests always said a storm-caller's soul would run the crown for a hundred years. I never hunted one before now because I never needed one. Now I need one, and here you are, kneeling, already half-emptied, already practiced at paying."

Yara rose. Slowly, joints cracking, the boy Oren clutching his sleeve, and when he stood he was taller than she had judged, and there was lightning somewhere far back in his eyes like a storm still hours off over the hills.

"You're offering me a bargain," he said.

"I'm offering you the only honest bargain anyone will ever offer you." Vessia's voice stayed level, but something underneath it had begun, very quietly, to shake, and she hated it, and continued. "Give me your soul—your storm, your gift, the thing that makes you what you are—and I will bind it to the crown, and the crown will live, and the realm will hold, and the hunt ends tomorrow, and no more children go into ledgers. And because I am not the monster the free cities' pamphlets print—" and here her voice did fracture, briefly, like ice underfoot— "because I know exactly what it is to stand in a room with a hole shaped like someone you loved, I will give you back what the song took. Every memory. Her name, her face, the bread behind the barn, the stones on the water, all of it. The crown can pour back what it drinks. It has never had cause to. It will have cause now."

The gallery was silent except for the restless whisper of the freed lights against their broken glass.

"You'd give me back my sister," Yara said slowly, "in exchange for making sure no one else ever gets theirs back."

"I'd give you back your sister," Vessia said, "in exchange for the realm. Yes. That is the trade. That has always been the trade. I made it eleven years ago with my own life, and I am telling you, as the only person in this world qualified to tell you—" and now she did not bother to steady her voice, because it did not matter anymore, because the crown was cold and the choir was broken and the truth was the only coin she had left— "*it is not worth it.* And I would make it again. That is what duty does. That is what it did to me. It devours the love out of you and calls the hunger virtue, and you keep signing, year after year, because stopping means admitting what you've already spent."

She held out her hand. Bare, ringless, trembling slightly, offered palm-up the way one offers a hand to a horse that might bolt.

"So. Storm-caller. Her name for your soul. Her face for your fire. Take the bargain and walk out of this mountain whole, and let the world go on being governed by women like me—or refuse, and leave here hollow, with nothing but her light and your empty hands, and let us both find out what a realm does when the music stops."

Above them, through leagues of stone, the mountain groaned—a long, deep sound, like a giant turning in a sleep it might not wake from. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. The remaining lit lanterns guttered. And in the doorway behind them, unannounced, unsanctioned, stood the hunter Corvane with his blade half-drawn and an expression on his scarred face that Vessia had never seen there before, and could not, for the life of her, name.

Yara looked at her offered hand. Then at the light that had been his sister. Then, terribly, at her—with pity, she realized. With pity, from the ruined man to the ruinous queen, as if *she* were the one kneeling.

"No," he said.
