## 18. Counter-Song

The Queen's offer hung in the air of the lantern-gallery like smoke that would not rise.

*Your sister's freedom for your storm-soul.* Vessia stood with the crown's light pooling in the hollows of her face, and Yara understood, suddenly and terribly, that she was not lying. She wanted Lira freed. She would even do it gladly—because she had learned, in whatever years it took to hollow herself into a crown, that everything could be bought if you were willing to pay in the only coin that mattered.

"No," Yara said.

The word came out small in that vast stone throat of a gallery, swallowed almost at once by the choir above—the endless war-march rolling through the dark like weather. But he said it again, louder, and this time he felt his blood answer, the old pressure rising behind his ribs like water behind a dam.

"I won't trade her," he said. "I won't trade *any* of them. Not one voice in this mountain. You built a kingdom out of people, Vessia. I'm here to take it apart."

Something moved across the Queen's face—not anger. Recognition. The look of a woman seeing her own reflection in a window and hating the glass.

"Then you are a fool," she said softly, "and you will die a fool, and the choir will sing on."

"Sef." Yara did not turn his head. He kept his eyes on the Queen because looking away felt like surrender. "Now."

Behind him, Sef drew breath.

Yara had heard the choirmaster sing before—in wayshrines, on roads, once in the ruins of Vhal with ash still falling—but nothing had prepared him for this. Sef's first note was low, almost beneath hearing, a sound more felt than heard, and the gallery *changed*. The suspended soul-lanterns swayed on their chains though there was no wind. The war-march overhead stuttered, a seam opening in its perfect weave.

The counter-song was not loud. That was the horror of it and the beauty both: it was quiet, patient, built note by note the way frost builds on a window, and every note was a name. Yara realized, with a lurch, that Sef had written it over years of sleepless guilt—that each phrase of it was stitched from a soul he had helped bind, sung back to them now as an apology no living mouth could make.

The choir faltered.

High above, where the galleries rose into darkness Yara could not see, voices wavered. The march lost its footing. And in the lanterns around them—hundreds of them, thousands, each one a person reduced to a held breath—a flicker. A stirring. Like sleepers turning toward a familiar voice calling from very far away.

"Stop them," Vessia said.

She did not shout. She never shouted; Yara understood that about her now. Her voice stayed level and cold as well-water, and the wardens came anyway, out of the shadowed arches on either side of the gallery, six of them, then eight, their blades drawn and burning with pale binding-light, the same light Yara had seen in Corvane's brand, in the navy's shackles, in every chain the crown had ever forged.

He stepped between them and Sef.

His hands were shaking. He noticed it distantly, the way you notice rain on someone else's roof. Ten years of hiding had taught him to be small, to be silent, to be a man who tended goats and apologized to elders and let the storm die unborn in his chest—and none of it mattered now, none of it had ever mattered, because here was what he actually was: a brother standing in front of his sister's prison with nothing but his voice and his grief and his refusal to leave.

"You'll have to go through me," he said, and called the storm.

It was not a storm like the one over the straits. There was no sky here, no clouds to gather, no rain to command. What he called instead was the thing *beneath* storms—the pressure, the charge, the terrible patience of the air before lightning. It came up through the soles of his boots, through the stone itself, and when he raised his hands the gallery filled with a blue-white shimmer, ozone and heat, his hair lifting from his brow.

The first warden hesitated.

The second did not. He came on fast, blade low, and Yara loosed a bolt that took him in the chest and threw him into the wall hard enough to crack stone. The man crumpled and did not rise, and Yara felt it land in him like a stone dropped down a well—the weight of another life, another family somewhere who would wait for a man who was not coming home—and he hated it, and he did not stop, because stopping meant Lira singing the Queen's war-march until the end of the world.

A blade grazed his shoulder. Fire-quick pain. He spun, lightning crawling up his forearm, and the warden nearest him staggered back, arm smoking, screaming.

Then the screaming stopped being the wardens'.

Corvane came out of the dark like something falling.

Yara had last seen him in the archives at Vhal, offering bargains with a valley-boy's ghost still living somewhere behind his eyes. Now the hunter moved through the wardens the way a scythe moves through wheat—no flourish, no rage, just economy, just purpose. His blade took the first man under the arm where the armor gapped. The second he caught by the wrist and turned, using the man's own momentum to put him on the ground, and his boot came down and did not lift again.

"*Corvane*—" Yara started.

"Don't." The hunter's voice was raw. "Don't thank me. Don't look at me. Sing."

There was blood on his face already—one of the wardens had marked him—and he was smiling, or something near a smile, crooked and terrible and free. "I told her I'd bring you alive," he said, backing toward the archway where more boots were hammering closer, many more, a whole garrison's worth. "I lied. First lie I've told her in twenty years. It felt—" he laughed, breathless, blocking the arch with his body, his blade coming up as torchlight bloomed beyond it, "—it felt like *breathing*."

"Corvane, they'll kill you."

"They were always going to kill me." He glanced back once, and for a heartbeat Yara saw it plainly: the boy from the valley, the one who had chosen the crown instead of the storm, standing at last on the other side of that choice with nothing left to lose by standing there. "Tell Thornvale—" He stopped. Shook his head. "No. Tell them nothing. Let them forget me. It's kinder."

The first rank of reinforcements came through the arch, and Corvane met them like a landslide meeting a river.

Yara turned away. It was the hardest thing he had ever done, harder than the storm over the straits, harder than the gate-toll, harder than ten years of silence—he turned his back on a man dying for him and walked toward the lantern that held his sister.

Sef was waiting beside it, hands trembling, song unbroken. Sweat ran down the choirmaster's face in the lantern-light. "I can't hold it long," he gasped between phrases, weaving the words into the melody so the music never stopped. "The counter-song needs a voice they trusted. Mine—they remember mine, but it's poisoned. I bound them. They hear me and they flinch." His eyes, wet and fierce, went to the lantern in its cradle of iron and glass. "But you. She knows you. Whatever's left of her—it's *you* it's reaching for. I've watched it since we came down. Take the lantern. Sing with me. Follow my line and give it your grief."

Yara lifted the lantern from its hook.

It was warm. That undid him more than anything else in that terrible place—the warmth of it, faint as a sleeping child's palm, as if all these years of captivity had not entirely quenched whatever burned inside. Through the smoked glass he could see the light within, gold shot through with threads of storm-grey, and he thought: *that's her. That grey is ours. That grey is the valley.*

"Lira," he whispered. "It's Yara. I'm here. I'm sorry it took me ten years."

The light pulsed. Once. Slow. Like a heartbeat remembering itself.

Above them the choir was fracturing. Sef's counter-song had spread through the galleries like fire through dry grass—here a voice dropping out of the march, there a whole section wavering, the great harmony developing cracks through which something older and sadder was bleeding: not the Queen's conquest-song anymore but fragments of other songs, harvest songs, lullabies, a work-chant from the saltmarshes, the kind of music people make when no one is making them. The bound souls were beginning, dimly, to remember what they had been before they were fuel.

And the mountain noticed.

The stone shuddered. Dust sifted from the vaulted ceiling. Somewhere far below, machinery of binding groaned against strain it had never been asked to bear, and Yara felt the whole vast structure of the court tighten around them like a fist—wards flaring along every wall, red-gold and furious, the crown's immune system waking to the infection in its heart.

Boots in the archway. More wardens. Corvane's blade ringing, ringing, and then—silence where the ringing had been.

Yara did not look. He could not afford to look. Later, he knew, he would carry the silence of that moment the way he carried the drowned sailors, the way he carried everything: forever, and without permission to set it down.

"Hold the line, Sef."

"I'm holding. *Sing*, Yara."

So he sang.

His voice was not beautiful. He knew that; a decade of disuse had left it cracked and rough as riverbank clay. But the counter-song did not ask for beauty. It asked for truth, and truth was the one thing he had in abundance—truth like a wound that had finally been allowed to speak. He followed Sef's melody the way you follow a rope through fog, hand over hand, and into it he poured the valley: hail on the ward-stones, goats' breath steaming at dawn, his mother's hands around the storm-glass pendant, the shape of a girl laughing in a wheat field whose face he had paid a year of memory to keep and could still, barely, see.

*Lira.*

The lantern blazed.

Light poured through the smoked glass like dawn through shutters, and the threads of storm-grey in it widened, brightened, spread, until the whole lantern looked less like a captive soul than like a piece of sky brought indoors. Within the glass something moved—shaped itself—pressed close to the surface, and Yara would have sworn, would have sworn on every storm he'd ever called, that he saw a hand. Small. Familiar. Pressed flat against the inside of the glass, the way she used to press her palm to his when they were children and the thunder scared her, *feel that? that's you, that's your weather, don't be afraid of it—*

"I've got you," he said, and his voice broke, and he sang through the breaking, because the song demanded everything and he intended to give it. "I've got you, Lira, I'm taking you *home*—"

The harmony fractured.

It happened all at once, everywhere, the way ice lets go of a pond. One moment the choir was a single instrument played by a queen; the next it was ten thousand instruments refusing, ten thousand voices tearing free of the march and scattering into their own keys, their own griefs, their own names. The sound was enormous and unbearable and holy—a cathedral's worth of weeping, a country's worth of remembering—and the wards along the walls burst like blisters, red-gold light spraying and dying, and the lanterns on their chains began, one by one, to swing loose.

Vessia screamed.

Yara heard it over everything—the Queen's composure cracking at last, the crown on her head blazing white-hot as the choir that fed it starved, and in her scream he heard not rage but *hunger*, vast and bottomless, the hunger of a woman who had sold everything she was and found it still wasn't enough. The crown was dying. She was dying with it, or worse than dying—

The lantern in his hands grew too hot to hold.

He held it anyway. He locked his fingers around the iron frame and he sang the final phrase of the counter-song, the one Sef had taught him in whispers, the unbinding, the door-opening, and the glass of the lantern went white and soft as morning mist—

—and something reached out of it and took the memories.

He felt it happen. That was the cruelty of it; the song did not steal, it *accepted payment*, and payment flowed out of him smooth and irreversible as blood. A year at the gate. Another year now, and another, whole seasons of her pouring out of him like water from a broken jar—the wheat field going first, her laughter thinning to a sound without a face attached, then the valley summers, then her voice saying his name, then her voice at all, then—

"No," he gasped, still singing, tears cutting through the grime on his face. "No, not that, not *her*, take anything else—"

The song took what the song required.

The last thing he had of her was a feeling with no story attached: warmth, and trust, and the certainty—fading even as he clung to it—that whoever this lantern held, he loved her more than the world entire. Then that, too, thinned. Then that, too, was gone, and he was singing an unbinding for a stranger, and the stranger's lantern burst open like a seedpod, and the light that rose out of it was free.

Free.

It rose past him, past Sef, up through the fractured galleries, and all around them the other lights were rising too—thousands upon thousands, souls unbound streaming upward through the mountain's throat like embers off a bonfire, and where they passed, the binding-machinery died, and the wards died, and the crown's long dominion guttered out room by room.

The gallery floor lurched. Cracks raced along the ceiling. The mountain, which had been a prison for so long it had forgotten how to be anything else, was coming apart around its own emptied heart.

"Yara!" Sef caught his arm. The choirmaster's face was streaked with sweat and tears and something that might have been the first peace he had felt in twenty years. "It's done. Do you hear me? It's *done*. We have to move—the whole court is coming down—"

Yara looked at his empty hands.

There was a shape in them still, he thought. A warmth. A weight he could no longer name. He stared at his palms as the dust fell and the mountain groaned and Sef pulled at his sleeve, and somewhere in the roaring dark a woman's voice—new-freed, ragged, alive—was calling a name down through the collapsing galleries.

Not his name. Someone else's. Someone's name, over and over, getting closer.

"Yara!" Sef shook him. "*Run!*"

He ran. The name chased him up through the dark, and he did not know why it made him want to turn around.
