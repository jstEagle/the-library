## 19. The Mountain Sings Back

The lantern was warm in his hands.

That was the thing no one had told him—that a soul would be warm. Yara had imagined the bound dead as cold things, glass and grief, but Lira's lantern pulsed against his palms like a bird's breast, like a throat mid-song, and through the amber glow he could see her: not her face, exactly, but the shape of her attention, turning toward him the way a flower turns toward a window.

He was singing. He did not remember beginning.

The counter-song came out of him in Sef's voice and then in his own, the old choirmaster's notation unraveling into something rawer, something that sounded less like music than like a name spoken over and over until it stopped being a word and became a door. Around them the great galleries of the mountain court rang with it. Ten thousand soul-lanterns hung from vaults of black stone on chains of silver wire, and one by one they began to answer—flickering, shuddering, their captive harmonies slipping out of the Queen's war-march like water finding cracks in a dam.

*Hold,* Sef had whispered, an hour ago or a lifetime ago, his hands shaking as he pressed the last pages of the counter-song into Yara's memory. *Hold to the end. If you stop halfway, you don't free them—you only wake them.*

Yara held.

The wardens were down. Corvane was down—he had gone under three wardens' blades with his sword still moving, buying them seconds, minutes, a lifetime measured in heartbeats, and Yara had seen it and could not stop singing and could not look away and so had done both at once, weeping without breaking pitch. The Queen stood at the far end of the gallery beneath the shattered scaffolding of her crown's cradle, and she was screaming something—the bargain again, *your storm for her freedom*, the same coin offered twice—and Yara sang louder.

Not at her. For her, maybe. He never decided.

The first lantern broke.

It happened high above, near the vaulted ceiling—a small green light that flared like a struck match and burst, and what came out of it was not light but voice. A single note, high and cracked and human, a woman's voice singing a lullaby fragment in a language Yara didn't know, and the sound hit the black stone walls and came back doubled, trebled, and the lantern next to it answered, and the next, and then it was not a chain of lights failing but a tide.

They broke like river ice in spring. They broke like windows in a gale. Ten thousand voices, freed all at once after years—decades?—of being forced into harmony, and they did not sing the Queen's march anymore. They sang everything. Every song they had ever been stolen from. Harvest rounds and funeral keens and drunk sailors' ballads and the small wordless humming of mothers over cradles, all of it colliding in the vaults of the mountain, none of it in key with anything else, and underneath it all a sound Yara felt in his teeth before he understood it:

Grief.

Not the Queen's ordered sorrow, not the polished mourning of a court. This was raw grief, unbound grief, ten thousand souls discovering at once how long they had been gone and what had been done with them while they were away. The temperature of the gallery dropped. Frost bloomed across the silver chains. The lanterns that had not yet broken began to swing on their cords, all in the same direction, all toward the Queen, and Vessia staggered back a step with her hands pressed flat over her ears as though she could push the sound out of her skull.

"Stop," she said. Then, louder, in the voice that had commanded armies: "*Stop.*"

The mountain did not stop.

Yara felt it building before he knew what it was—the same pressure he had known his whole life in his chest, in his wrists, in the backs of his eyes before every storm he'd ever called, but scaled up past anything his body alone could hold. The souls were grieving, and their grief needed somewhere to go, and he was standing in the deepest chamber of the mountain holding a storm-caller's blood and a sister's lantern and the last notes of an unfinished song.

The lightning did not come from the sky. There was no sky. It came up through the floor.

White fire sheeted across the black stone, arcing between the hanging chains, and every arc carried a voice, and every voice carried a wound, and the mountain—which had held this choir sealed for generations, which had been built for exactly this purpose, to be a throat that swallowed songs—began to groan along its own bones. Dust rained from the vaults. Somewhere far above, Yara heard the deep structural complaint of stone shifting against stone, the sound a house makes when it decides to fall down.

"It's too much!" Sef was shouting, hauling at his arm, his ruined choirmaster's hands leaving bloody prints on Yara's sleeve. "No vessel can hold this—not even you—"

"Then I won't hold it."

Yara didn't know where the words came from. Perhaps from the storm itself; perhaps from whatever was left of the girl in the lantern, whose warmth was spreading up his arms now, into his shoulders, his throat. He raised Lira's lantern above his head the way he had once raised his arms on the ridge above Thornvale, the way his mother must have raised hers in some valley before the wards took her, the way every storm-caller since the first one had ever stood against weather.

"You want out?" he said—to the grief, to the souls, to the mountain, to the sky that wasn't there. "Then go through me."

And he opened.

There are no words for it, and later he would not try very hard to find any. It was like being a riverbank when the flood comes. It was like being a bell struck by everything that had ever been lost. The grief of ten thousand souls poured through him and he shaped it—not tamed it, nothing so kind as taming—but gave it direction, gave it form, gave it the one shape his blood knew how to make.

He made it a storm.

It tore upward through the mountain. He felt it going, felt it punching through galleries and stairwells and the warded halls where memory was bought and sold, following the path of least resistance and the path of greatest hunger both, and where it passed, seals failed. Wards that had cost fortunes and lifetimes to raise peeled apart like wet paper. The binding on the Queen's armies—Sef had described it once, late at night, with the shame showing through his voice like a bruise through skin: *every soldier wears a thread of the choir inside him, that's why they don't break, that's why they don't run*—that binding snapped all at once, all across the realm, and Yara felt it snap, felt ten thousand threads go slack at once like cut harp strings, and somewhere beyond the mountain men who had forgotten how to be afraid remembered it all together and began, for the first time, to run.

Above him—through him—the crown shattered.

He heard it rather than saw it: a sound like a cathedral of glass coming down, and a scream that was half Vessia and half something older that had lived in the gold, and then a silence in one register of the world that Yara hadn't even noticed until it was gone. The Queen's power went out of the air the way heat leaves a room when a window breaks in winter. When he found her with his eyes—when he could see anything at all—she was on her knees among the falling frost, reaching for pieces of gold that crumbled at her touch, and her face, stripped of the crown's feeding, looked suddenly, terribly young.

She had sold her memories to wear it. She had said so. And now the crown was gone, and whatever years and loves and names she had traded for it were not coming back, and she knelt in the wreckage of her own purchase like any other beggar in her realm.

Yara had one heartbeat—one—to feel something about that. Pity, maybe. Or recognition.

Then the price came due.

*The unbinding demands payment.* Sef had said it at the wayshrine, quietly, not looking at him. *Every working takes its toll, and one this size will take something that matters. Be ready to choose what you can live without.*

But it turned out there was no choosing.

The song took what it wanted.

He felt it leave him the way you feel a tooth come loose—not pain, exactly, but absence arriving ahead of schedule, a hollow opening where something solid used to be. Memories. His memories. Of Lira.

"No," Yara said, and his voice cracked the counter-song, and the storm faltered. "No—wait—I need—I haven't finished telling her—"

But the song was not cruel and was not listening. The song was arithmetic. An unbinding of this magnitude required a payment of equivalent weight, and what in Yara Hartwell weighed as much as ten thousand souls?

Only one thing ever had.

He gripped the lantern tighter, as if he could hold himself to himself by holding her. The memories went in order of intimacy, oldest first—or perhaps newest first; afterward he could never say, because saying requires keeping. Her hand small in his on the road to market. Gone. The summer she taught him to whistle through a blade of grass. Gone. Their father's voice selling her, and Yara's fists against the door, and the wagon pulling away—gone, and good riddance, and oh, God, he wanted it back anyway, he wanted every ugly piece—

Lira laughing at him for falling off the fence. Lira braiding crow feathers into her hair because she said crows liked her best. Lira, aged six, solemnly informing him that storms were just the sky having feelings and someone ought to be nice about it.

Gone. Gone. Gone.

He fought it the way drowning men fight, which is to say beautifully and uselessly. He tried to sing faster, to finish the counter-song, to give the debt something else to collect—but the storm was already through the mountain, already doing its work, already spent, and the ledger of it was being balanced against him line by line. He felt his sister becoming a shape instead of a person. A name instead of a shape. And then—

And then even the name went soft in his mouth, like a word in a language he had studied long ago and never spoken since.

The last thing to go was the warmth of the lantern against his hands. Not the lantern itself—he kept the lantern, kept the physical fact of it, glass and glow and weight—but the knowledge of whose it was. He stood in the ruin of the greatest working ever sung in the mortal world, holding a soul he had crossed kingdoms to save, and could not have said, to save his life, why his cheeks were wet.

The storm ended the way real storms do: not all at once but in long withdrawing thunder, rolling up and out of the broken mountain, chasing itself across a sky that had finally opened overhead—the roof of the court had split, and stars showed through, actual stars, the first the mountain's depths had seen in centuries. The freed souls rose through the crack in silver streams, ten thousand lights climbing toward the dark, and some of them, passing him, brushed his face like snowfall, like fingertips, like thanks. One of them lingered. He felt it hover near the lantern in his hands—near its own body? near a brother?—and he felt it wait, patient, for something from him.

"I'm sorry," Yara said, because it seemed required. "I don't— I'm sorry, I don't know you."

The light trembled. Whether with laughter or grief he couldn't tell. Then it rose, and joined the others, and was gone.

The gallery settled around him in groans and whispers. Chains hung slack and empty, thousands of them, swaying in the aftermath like a forest of dead vines. Frost covered everything and was already melting. Somewhere below, Sef was calling his name—*Yara! YARA!*—in a voice scraped down to the wood, and Oren was crying somewhere, the small fierce crying of a child trying to be brave about it, and none of it meant anything to him yet. He was looking at the lantern. He was looking at the lantern and his mind kept reaching for a reason to be holding it and finding nothing, finding smooth blank wall where a person used to be.

Why am I crying, he thought. Why does this little light hurt to look at.

Footsteps. Hands on his shoulders—Sef, gray-faced, bloodied, alive somehow, alive, staring at him with an expression Yara had no framework for: joy and horror in equal parts, triumph and mourning wearing each other's clothes.

"It worked," Sef said hoarsely. "You did it. The crown is broken, the army is broken, the hunt is over. You did it." His eyes dropped to the lantern in Yara's hands, and his face did something complicated and terrible. "...Do you know what you're holding?"

Yara looked down at the warm glow. At the shape inside it, dim and turning toward him, waiting, waiting, waiting.

"No," he said, and heard how strange his own voice sounded, emptied out. "Should I?"

The mountain shuddered again—settling, dying, coming down in slow motion around them—and Sef grabbed his arm and pulled, shouting for Oren, shouting for the stairs, and Yara went with him because there was nothing else to do, clutching a stranger's soul to his chest as the dark came down.

Behind them, in the ruins, a queen without a crown wandered the frost and called out names that no longer belonged to anyone.

Ahead of them, on the mountain road, someone waited who knew his face better than he ever would again.
