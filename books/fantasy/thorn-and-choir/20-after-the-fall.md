## 20. After the Fall

The silence after the storm was the loudest thing Sef had ever heard.

He had spent his life among choirs. He knew what a thousand voices sounded like when they broke off mid-phrase—the wet, gasping hush of it, the way a cathedral seemed to lean into its own emptiness. He had thought he knew every species of silence a hall of song could hold.

He had been wrong. The mountain's galleries went on for miles beneath the rock, and every one of them had been singing since before Sef was born, and now they were not, and the absence had weight enough to crack stone.

Which it was doing. Even as he stood in the wreck of the choir-court with Oren pressed against his side and Yara swaying like a cut reed beside him, hairline fractures spidered up the pillars, and somewhere far above them a section of ceiling let go with a boom that rolled through the dark like a funeral drum.

"Yara." Sef caught the man's arm. "Yara, look at me."

Yara looked at him—or rather, his eyes found Sef's face and rested there without recognition settling in, without anything settling anywhere. That was the horror of it. He was present. He was breathing. But behind his eyes was a room from which all the furniture had been carried out.

"It worked," Yara said. His voice came out sanded smooth, empty of inflection. "Did it work?"

Sef could not answer that honestly and survive the answer himself. Around them, the soul-lanterns hung in their thousands from the gallery chains, and they were changing. For ten years—longer—they had burned the color of old honey, steady and dim, the light of souls fed upon. Now they flickered. Now they guttered and flared and guttered again, like candles remembering wind, and the light coming off them had gone white at the heart, restless, alive.

"They're waking," Oren whispered. The boy's hand had found Sef's sleeve and gripped it with the desperate strength of someone who had learned, in burning streets, to hold on to the people who were still standing. "Master Sef, they're waking up."

"Yes," Sef said. "And this place is going to come down around them if we don't move."

* * *

They moved.

It was slow going, and it should have been impossible, and it was neither, because the freed dead made way for them.

Sef understood later—he would spend years understanding it—that when the counter-song unbound the choir, it did not simply release the souls. It released *everything* the binding had held: the harmony, the structure, the long enforced order of it. And into that chaos poured ten years' worth of stolen grief, and grief, uncontained, becomes weather.

But for the length of one corridor, one stair, one gallery at a time, the souls still remembered Sef's voice. They had been bound to his music once. He had tuned them, trained them, taught them to sing Vessia's war-march sweetly enough to make slaughter feel like ceremony. It was the great crime of his life, and here, at the end of it, the crime became the key: where the falling stone blocked a passage, a lantern drifted ahead of them and the rock groaned aside. Where a bridge of black iron had sheared from its moorings, the lanterns clustered along the gap and burned a line across it bright enough to walk by, and Yara walked it barefoot with his arms full of lanterns because he refused, absolutely, to leave any of them behind.

"You can't carry them all," Sef told him, on the fourth gallery, watching Yara load another soul-lantern into the crook of his arm like a sleeping child. There must have been thirty already. Their combined light turned his hollow face silver.

"I can carry these," Yara said. He did not say whose they were. He did not say *I don't remember why I can't leave them.* He simply kept loading them, hands moving with a tenderness that had outlived its own reasons, and Sef's throat closed so tightly he could not speak at all.

Oren carried the ledger. He had insisted on it in the choir-court, wrapping the book in his shirt against the dust and the falling grit, and he held it now against his chest with both arms, walking close between the two men like a small ship between breakwaters. Every few steps he glanced back the way they'd come, toward the deep places where the Queen's wardens had been, where Corvane had—

Where Corvane had turned his blade on his own kind and bought them the seconds they were spending now.

Sef did not let himself think about that yet either. Grief could wait until they were above ground. Everything could wait until they were above ground. Survival first; survival was a discipline, and he had learned it young, in the palace, smiling at people who would have killed him if they'd known what he was carrying in his breast pocket.

"The stairs," he said aloud, because saying things aloud steadied his voice and his voice was the only tool he had left that still worked. "The pilgrim stairs. They run up inside the mountain's shoulder and come out above the treeline. The wardens won't be watching them—they've never needed to. No one has ever come down here to leave."

"No one's ever come down here at all," Oren said, "except to be sold."

The boy said it flatly, without drama, the way children state terrible arithmetic, and Sef felt it land in his chest like a dropped stone. He put his hand briefly on Oren's head. The boy ducked into the touch and then away, too old for comfort and too young to do without it.

They climbed.

Behind and below them, the mountain sang—not the choir anymore, but the mountain itself, groaning through its roots, shedding its galleries one by one into the dark. Dust came up the stairwell in pale breaths. Twice the whole staircase shuddered and tilted, and twice the lantern-light steadied, and they kept climbing.

And once—only once—a lantern spoke.

It drifted level with Sef's shoulder as he climbed, a woman's voice, thin as thread pulled through cloth: *"Choirmaster."*

He stopped. His hand found the cold wall. "Yes."

*"You came back."*

"I came back," he said. "I'm sorry I didn't come sooner."

The lantern flickered—something that might have been laughter, or weeping; after ten years in the dark, Sef supposed the two sounded alike. *"We used to wonder which you'd choose. The door or the song."*

"I chose the door," Sef said. "For six years I chose the door. I'm choosing the song now. Whatever's left of me chooses it."

The lantern rose a little, drifting up the stairwell ahead of them, lighting the next flight, and the next. It stayed with them the whole climb. He never learned her name. He had known all their names once—had entered them into the binding rolls with his own hand—and the shame of having lost track of a single one was a debt he intended to spend whatever remained of his life paying.

* * *

They came out of the mountain into a night that smelled like rain and iron.

The pilgrim stair emerged from a cleft in the mountainside above the terraced slopes of the court's outer gardens—gardens planted, Sef knew, with flowers chosen for how quietly they died in winter. Below, spread across the valley floor like a spilled jewel-box, lay the mountain court itself, and it was burning.

Not all of it. Not yet. But the crown-spires were dark—the great towers where Vessia's throne-room light had burned without pause for ten years—and where that light had been, fires were rising instead, small and scattered, the fires of a household waking to find its master gone mad or vanished or both. Bells were ringing badly, out of rhythm, each tower answering a different alarm.

And beyond the walls, on the plains, the army was failing.

Sef had seen the armies march. Everyone in the free cities had seen them, in report and rumor: the columns that never camped, never ate, never faltered, soldiers bound to the war-march the way puppets are bound to strings, advancing day and night with the terrible patience of tide. From the height of the cleft he watched what happened to tide when the moon lets go.

The plain below was dotted with watchfires, thousands of them, an arrangement of light that had looked, from Vhal's council chamber maps, like a net closing over the free cities. Now the fires were going out. Not extinguished—abandoned. Soldiers stood up from them and walked a few paces in no direction at all and sat back down, and did not rise again. Here and there a knot of men moved with purpose, officers perhaps, still half-bound, trying to rally troops who had woken mid-campaign with weapons in their hands and ten missing years in their heads. The sound of them reached the cleft faintly, thinned by distance: shouting, and then less shouting, and then a low confused murmur like wind through wheat.

"It's over," Oren said. He said it carefully, testing the words. "Isn't it? We won?"

"We survived," Sef said. "That comes first. Whether it's over—" He watched a banner detach from its pole in the night wind and wander off across the plain like a lost bird. "Ask me in a year."

Beside him, Yara had set his burden of lanterns down in the grass of the terrace, all of them, gently, in rows, and was standing very still, looking down at the valley with his hands hanging open at his sides.

"Yara?"

"There was someone," Yara said.

Sef went cold. "There was—"

"In the choir. Someone who—I brought the lanterns because—" Yara's brow furrowed, and Sef watched him reach for something that wasn't there, watched his fingers close on empty air, over and over, a man patting his pockets for keys to a house that had burned down years ago. "There was a reason. I know there was a reason. It's—it's like reaching into water and finding the bottom gone."

Sef closed his eyes. This was the price. This was what the unbinding had taken, what he had half-guessed and never dared name: the song demanded payment, and it had reached into Yara Hartwell and taken out his sister whole—her face, her voice, the ten years of grief that had kept him moving—and left the shape of her behind, an absence shaped exactly like a person.

"She'll find you," Sef said. It was the only true thing he could offer, and even that was a hope wearing truth's clothes. "Lira is freed. Freed souls go home, Yara. All the stories agree on that much."

"Do they?" Yara asked, and there was nothing accusing in it, which was worse. Just the flat honest question of a man who has forgotten whether he has ever trusted anyone, asking a stranger on a mountaintop whether the world keeps its promises.

"Yes," Sef said. "They do."

Below them, the bells kept ringing badly, and the army murmured like a sea going out, and the lanterns in the grass burned white and wakeful, waiting, all of them, for morning.

* * *

They found the Queen at dawn.

Sef would carry the sight of it for the rest of his life, and he already knew it, the way you know a wound is yours while the blood is still warm on your skin.

They had come down through the gardens—the long way, avoiding the gates, avoiding whatever remained of the wardens—and near the reflecting pool at the garden's heart, where ten years ago Sef had conducted the coronation choir from a marble platform that still stood, a woman in a white shift was walking among the flowerbeds, barefoot, touching the dead blooms.

Her crown was gone. Of course it was gone; Yara had shattered it, had channeled the freed choir's grief through his storms and broken the thing open like an egg, and Sef had seen the shards fall glittering into the dark. What remained of it lay scattered on the path around her feet—fragments of black glass, each one still faintly warm, each one humming almost inaudibly, like struck bells that would not finish dying.

Vessia did not look at them. She knelt by a bed of frost-burnt roses and touched one grey bloom with one finger, and her face—

Her face was the worst of it. Sef had hated her for six years with the disciplined, methodical hatred of a man who catalogued his hatred nightly like accounts. He had imagined her triumphant, cruel, magnificent in her hunger. He had imagined her dragged down screaming.

He had not imagined her *empty*. She looked at the roses the way a child looks at roses: with total attention and no history at all. The crown had eaten everything—Sef saw that now, understood it in one lurching moment. She had sold herself into it piece by piece, memory by memory, duty devouring love until love was gone entirely and duty had nothing left to guard. The choir had sung her wars. And now the choir was free, and what remained of Vessia was a woman in a white shift in a ruined garden who did not know her own name.

She looked up. She saw three travelers—one tall and hollow-eyed, one grey and stooped, one small and clutching a ledger—and she smiled at them the way strangers smile at strangers on a road.

"Is it morning?" she asked. Her voice was lovely. It had always been lovely; that had been the trap of her. "I've lost track of the nights."

"It's morning," Sef said.

"Good," she said. "I think I liked mornings. I think—" She frowned, delicately, like someone searching a drawer for an item she suspected had never been in it. "I think there was someone I was supposed to save. Wasn't there? There was someone."

Oren made a small sound. Sef put a hand on the boy's shoulder and felt him trembling.

"There was someone," Sef agreed quietly. "There are many someones. Go and sit by the pool. The sun will be warm there in an hour."

She went. She gathered up a handful of the crown-shards first, absently, the way one gathers pretty stones, and carried them with her, and sat down at the edge of the still water, and waited for the sun like anyone.

Yara watched her the entire time, and when she was settled, he said, in that sanded, emptied voice: "Should we—"

"No," Sef said. "Whatever justice wants from her, it isn't ours to give, and we have nothing left to give it with. Let her learn the garden. It's more than she let anyone learn."

They left her there. Sef did not look back. He was proud of that, later, and ashamed of it, later than that.

* * *

They walked out through the western gate at full daylight, and no one stopped them, because there was no one left with orders to stop anyone.

The refugee roads were already forming—Sef could see them from the ridge, threads of smoke and movement converging westward, soldiers and servants and court-folk spilling out of the failing machine of the mountain like water finding its level. Somewhere in that current were thousands of newly unbound souls walking home in borrowed flesh, dazed, hungry, ten years displaced. Somewhere in it—Sef believed this with the whole stubborn remainder of his faith—was a woman named Lira, following the oldest pull there is, the pull toward the last person who had loved her before the world got hold of her.

Yara walked beside him and did not know why his chest hurt. Sef watched him not-knowing, and decided that he would tell him. Not today. Today the man had carried thirty lanterns down a collapsing mountain on legs that barely held him; today he deserved the mercy of blankness. But soon—on the road, in some inn in the foothills, over bad wine—Sef would sit him down and say: *You had a sister. Her name was Lira. You crossed the world for her. Here is everything I know,* and he would keep saying it, every day, for as many days as it took, until testimony grew back into memory or into something better than memory.

"Master Sef." Oren tugged his sleeve. The boy nodded back the way they'd come.

On the ridge behind them, the last of the mountain court's towers folded inward with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and a column of dust rose straight up in the still air, pale gold in the morning light, unhurried, almost dignified. Out of the ruin, drifting upward on the thermal, rose points of light—dozens, then hundreds—the freed lanterns ascending, scattering on the wind, going wherever freed things go.

One light lingered longer than the others. It hung above the ruin for the space of ten heartbeats, facing west, the direction of Thornvale and the valley and the sea beyond it. Then it followed the rest.

Yara watched it with tears running down his face and no idea at all why he was crying.

Sef put his arm around the man's shoulders, and took the ledger's weight from Oren's arms for a while, and led them both down the road, into the ordinary enormous work of surviving what they had done.

The war was won. It had been won quietly, in the dark, by a stolen song and a broken man's voice, and no trumpets would ever play for it, and that was right. Trumpets had been the problem all along.

Ahead of them, the road ran west through the morning mist, and somewhere on it, walking toward them out of everything that had been taken, was the sister Yara could not remember, carrying a story he would have to hear for the first time.

Sef only hoped he would recognize her when she came.
