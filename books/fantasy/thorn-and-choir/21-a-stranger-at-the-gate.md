## 21. A Stranger at the Gate

The mountain road unwound below them like a grey ribbon someone had dropped and never bothered to gather up. Yara walked it in the hour after dawn, when the light came slantwise through the pines and turned the frost on the stones to a scatter of broken glass. His legs ached from the descent. His hands still smelled of lantern oil and something else beneath it—ozone, maybe, or grief, if grief had a smell. He supposed it did. He had been breathing it for days.

He did not remember his sister's face.

That was the fact of it, plain as the road, plain as the ache in his knees. He had gone down into the dark carrying her name like a coal cupped in both palms, and he had come out again with empty hands. The song had taken its price. He had known it would—he had sung anyway, had held her lantern while the counter-song unspooled through the galleries of the bound, while Sef's voice cracked and Corvane bled out on the wardens' blades—and he would do it again, he told himself, he would do it a thousand times, because somewhere above them now an army was stumbling free of its chains and a queen was wandering crownless through her own ruins, and Lira was *free*.

Free, and a stranger to him.

He kept walking. Oren was asleep against Sef's shoulder somewhere behind him; the boy had finally surrendered to exhaustion two miles back, mid-sentence, mid-argument about whether storm-clouds tasted like iron or like rain. The freed souls' lanterns rode in a crate on the smuggler's cart they'd hired at the last waystation, each one glowing faintly, each one holding a voice that no longer sang for Vessia's war-march but only for itself, small and private and alive.

Yara stopped where the road bent around a shoulder of granite, because there was a woman standing there.

She had been standing there a while, he thought. Long enough for frost to have crept up the hem of her cloak. She wore it like a shroud that had been cut down to fit—a dead woman's garment, borrowed, too long in the arms. Her hair was dark, shot through with early grey at the temples, tied back with what looked like a strip torn from a banner. Her eyes were the color of deep water over stone, and they were fixed on him with an intensity that should have frightened him and instead made something in his chest turn over slowly, like a key in a lock whose teeth had been filed away.

"You're real," she said. Her voice was rough, as if it hadn't been used in years—as if it had been used only for singing, and singing was not the same as speaking. "I watched you from the treeline for half the morning. I thought—I thought I'd imagined you. They tell you that, in there. That whoever comes for you will be imagined."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Started again. "You were in the choir."

"Yes." No flinch. No shame in it, or rather shame so old it had become architecture, load-bearing. "Ten years. Eleven. I stopped counting when counting stopped meaning anything." She took a step toward him, and the frost crackled under her boot, and Yara realized his hands had begun, very faintly, to hum—the storm-sense waking in him the way it always did before weather, except this wasn't weather. This was a woman walking toward him on a frozen road, and every nerve in his body insisted she mattered.

"Who are you?" he asked, and hated how gentle he made it, how careful, as though gentleness could disguise the cruelty of the question. As though she wouldn't hear the hole in it.

She heard it. Of course she heard it. Her face did something complicated and then settled into a kind of terrible steadiness, the steadiness of someone who has rehearsed a fall.

"My name is Lira Hartwell," she said. "And you're my brother."

* * *

They sat on a fallen pine at the roadside, because her knees had gone bad in the dark places and his had gone bad on the stairs, and between them they made one functioning set of legs. The sun climbed. Somewhere downslope a woodpecker went at a dead trunk like a debtor at a door. Yara wrapped his hands around a clay cup of tea Sef had pressed on him before falling asleep in the cart, and Lira did not touch hers, just held it, watching the steam rise as if steam were a language she was relearning.

"The song took me," she said at last. "Not all at once. It's not like dying. It's like being read aloud by someone who doesn't know the words. First it takes your future—you stop dreaming, because dreams are fuel. Then your past, a year at a time, whatever the ledgers say is worth least. Mine was cheap. A valley girl, no title, no gift—or so they thought." She glanced sideways at him, and there was something wry in it, something that had survived ten years of the dark. "They didn't know about our mother, did they. They didn't know what runs in us."

"No," Yara said. "I didn't either. Not until the wards broke."

"The wards." She said it softly, tasting it. "Thornvale. Tell me about Thornvale."

And he understood, then, what she was doing. What she had walked out of the treeline to do. She was going to tell him their story—that had been her plan, he saw it forming in her even now, the way a sailor reads weather—and she wanted to begin where the story began, and she needed him to say the names first so she could be sure they matched the ones she'd carried blind through the dark.

So he told her. About the valley that forgets, the ward-stones sunk in the earth like teeth, the goats and the mill and the elders who voted to hand their gifted children to the crier's list. About the hailstorm the day the wards cracked, magic leaking back into him like blood into a numb limb, pins and needles and then fire. About the night he packed, taking nothing but their mother's storm-glass pendant—

—and when he said *pendant*, Lira's breath caught, and she reached into the collar of her borrowed shroud and drew out a cord, and on the cord hung a second pendant, twin to his, storm-glass gone cloudy with years of darkness, and the two of them sat looking at each other across the steam of untouched tea, wearing halves of the same whole.

"She gave one to each of us," Lira whispered. "I'd forgotten. I'd forgotten everything except—" Her hand closed around the glass. "Except you. They couldn't price you. Every time the ledger-keepers came through with their little knives of assessment, they'd try to take the memory of my brother and it wouldn't go. It was nailed down too deep. So they left it, and it was the last thing left, in the end. Your face. Just your face, floating in the dark, younger than this, always younger, fourteen maybe, fifteen, laughing at something I can't remember you laughing at."

Yara felt his throat close. Because he knew what she was describing. He knew it from the other side—from the ledger, from the candlelit night when he had found her name in another man's handwriting, sold by their father for debts, bound to the choir beneath the mountain. He knew it from the toll-gate, where he had paid a year of her to walk inside, and felt her fade further, and paid anyway.

But he did not remember her. That was the truth sitting between them on the log, patient as frost. She remembered him. He did not remember her. And no amount of her remembering could put the memory back into him, any more than a widow's love could put breath back into the drowned.

"Lira," he said. Her name felt enormous in his mouth, a word he'd carried for months without knowing its weight. "I have to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide what it means."

She waited. She was good at waiting. Ten years in the dark will teach you that.

"When I sang the unbinding," he said, "the song demanded payment. That's how it works—Sef tried to warn me, or maybe he didn't, maybe we both knew and didn't say it aloud because saying it aloud would have stopped us. The choir ran on memory. Souls stripped down to their brightest threads. To unbind it, I had to give the song something of equal thread." He turned the storm-glass pendant over in his fingers. "It took you. All of you. Every year of you. I walked out of that mountain knowing your name because I wrote it on my own arm in ink before I started, and knowing nothing else. You could have passed me on this road an hour ago and I'd have seen a tired woman in a dead soldier's cloak."

Lira was quiet for a long time. The woodpecker hammered. Steam rose off the tea in thin ropes and unraveled.

"Do you regret it?" she asked finally. Not *do you grieve*. Not *are you sorry*. Regret. The question of a woman who had spent eleven years being spent by other people and wanted, desperately, to know if she had been worth spending.

"No," Yara said, and meant it so completely that it frightened him. "I'd pay it twice. I'd pay it every year for the rest of my life, one year at a time, at every gate between here and the sea. You're free. Whatever else is true, that's true, and it's the truest thing I've ever done."

Her eyes filled, and she let them—not wiping, not turning away, just letting the tears run down her face in the cold air until they froze in fine tracks along her jaw, which was perhaps the most honest thing he had ever watched a person do. Grief worn openly, in public, on a road, in daylight. The valley would never have permitted it. The choir certainly hadn't.

"Then we start here," she said. "Here, on this log, with cold tea and a woodpecker and two people who share a mother and a story and nothing else. I'll tell you everything. Everything I remember, which is more than I expected to have, thanks to you. And you'll tell me everything you know from the outside—the ledger, the hunt, the cities, the burning of Vhal, all of it, because I heard none of it and I want to know what the world cost while I was underground." She paused, and her voice dropped into something almost fierce. "And when we've told it all, we'll tell it again. And again. Until it stops being testimony and starts being memory. That's how it works, isn't it? That's what the song taught me, in reverse. Things repeated become things believed become things *known*. I built myself out of your face for eleven years. I can build you back out of stories. Both of us. Together."

Yara looked at her—at the frost-tracks on her jaw, at the pendant glinting at her throat, at the deep-water eyes that had already, in the space of an hour, become familiar to him in the way a landscape becomes familiar, not remembered but learned—and felt something shift in his chest that was not quite hope and not quite sorrow but some third thing braided from both, strong as rope.

"It might not work," he said quietly. "I might look at you in twenty years and feel nothing but what I feel right now. Politeness. Curiosity. Gratitude."

"Probably," she agreed, with a smile that nearly undid him. "And I might spend those twenty years telling you our story to a brother who nods politely and forgets it by morning. But Yara—my brother, who I know better than I know anything, who I *chose*, who I would choose again out of a crowd of thousands because you have our mother's exact way of tilting your head when you're about to argue—love isn't a memory. Memory is just one of its houses. If the house burns down, you rebuild. You don't stand in the ashes saying the family must have died in the fire."

He laughed. It startled him, the laugh—it came up from somewhere below the grief, below the exhaustion, some cellar of himself he'd thought the mountain had emptied. And when he laughed, Lira laughed, and the sound of the two of them together on the frozen road was so obviously, so painfully *right* that he understood she had been correct about the head-tilting, and about everything else.

"All right," he said. "Tell me our story."

* * *

They walked as she talked. The road descended through the pines in long switchbacks, and the day warmed enough that the frost gave up its hold on the stones, and behind them the cart creaked along with Sef snoring upright and Oren sleeping in a heap of blankets like a kitten, and ahead of them the valley opened by degrees—the foothills going gold and brown, a river appearing and disappearing, smoke from farmsteads rising straight in the windless air.

Lira told it in pieces, in the order things had happened, because chronology was a rope and she was not going to let go of it. She told him about the winter their father sold her, how he'd wept while he signed, how she had hated him for the weeping more than for the selling, because the weeping meant he knew. She told him about the journey south in a warden's wagon, the other children, the ones who cried themselves silent. She told him about the first descent, the gate that took a year of memory from every traveler, the galleries of lanterns hanging in the dark like orchards of impossible fruit.

"And yours was there," she said, stopping suddenly on the road, turning to face him. "Your lantern. I found it. Third gallery, east wall, between a fisherman from the Straits and a girl who could make flowers bloom out of season. Yours was dimmer than most—they'd barely skimmed you, just enough to register the gift and file it. Storm-caller, the tag said. Unharvested. Reserve." Her mouth twisted. "They were saving you. For the crown, probably. For whatever Vessia is."

"I know," Yara said. "Corvane told me. Before the end." He did not elaborate. He was not ready to talk about Corvane—the hunter, the valley-born exile, the man who had turned his blade on the wardens and bought them their seconds and died for it on the floor of the choir. That grief was still too new, too shapeless. Lira seemed to sense it; she moved on without pressing, the way you step around a fresh grave on a path you have to keep walking.

She told him about the songs. How the choirmaster—*Sef*, she said the name with a complicated softness, having learned it during the first hours of freedom when the freed souls compared notes like survivors of a shipwreck—had trained them, had shaped their stolen memories into harmonies, had made beauty out of theft and then fled it. How the war-march had lived in her bones for a decade, how she could still feel it sometimes, a phantom rhythm in her chest, the way an amputee feels a missing limb. How, in the last days before the unbinding, the march had begun to fray, notes going sour, voices dropping out, and how she had understood even from inside it that something was coming for them from the world above.

"That was you," she said. "Wasn't it. The sourness. You were already climbing down toward us."

"We were," Yara said. "Me, and Sef, and a boy named Oren who talks in his sleep and once lit a patrol's lanterns blue by accident."

"Oren." Lira glanced back at the cart, at the heap of blankets, and something in her expression changed—warmed, softened, went maternal in a way that made Yara suspect she had been older-than-a-sister to him once, long ago, in the life neither of them could fully reach. "I'd like to meet him properly. When he's awake. Anyone who survives that mountain at eight years old deserves to be met properly."

"He's nine. He'll lie about it. He lies about his age the way other children lie about brushing their teeth."

"Then he's already family," Lira said, and Yara felt the word *family* land in him like a stone dropped into a well—deep, ringing, echoing in spaces he couldn't see the bottom of.

By late afternoon they had come down out of the pines entirely, onto the broad lower road where the refugees' camp was visible ahead—a scatter of tents and cookfires in a fallow field, the freed souls' lanterns strung between poles in gentle arcs, glowing as the light failed, so that the whole camp looked ringed with low constellations. People moved among the fires. Someone was playing a fiddle, badly, joyfully. The army that had marched on the free cities was dissolving somewhere to the north, its binding broken, its soldiers waking in ditches and asking each other what year it was, and the world, improbably, was continuing.

Lira stopped at the edge of the field.

"One more thing," she said. "Before we go in there, before the noise and the fires and everyone wanting to touch the lantern-bearers. I want you to have this, because I've been carrying it since the treeline and it's heavy and it's yours." She reached into the shroud again and produced a scrap of paper, folded small, soft as cloth from handling. "When they pulled us out—when the choir broke and the wardens ran and the lanterns went dark one by one, freed, just *dark*, like eyes closing in sleep—I couldn't find mine at first. Everyone else was gathering theirs. And I stood in that gallery in the black, alone, and I thought: this is what it's like, this is what he'll feel, this is the shape of it, a person standing in the dark where a loved one used to hang. So I tore a page from the ledger-master's book—it was lying open on his desk, the fool never locked anything, why would he, who was going to rob a graveyard?—and I wrote on it. In the dark. By feel."

She unfolded the paper and handed it to him. The writing was jagged, pressed hard, the letters of someone writing blind:

*I am Lira Hartwell. My brother is Yara Hartwell. He has a storm in him and a laugh like our mother's. Find him. He will find you. We are each other's proof.*

Yara read it three times. His hands shook. Not the storm-shake, not power—just a man's hands, shaking, on a road at dusk, holding the only letter his sister had ever written him, written blind in a grave, addressed to a version of him who no longer existed and delivered, impossibly, to the one who did.

"Proof," he said. His voice came out wrecked. "You wrote *proof*."

"I told you," Lira said. "Love isn't memory. It's testimony. And testimony needs two witnesses." She tucked the paper back into his breast pocket herself, folding it over his heart with both hands, deliberate as a rite. "So witness me, Yara. From tonight forward. Every story, every year, every name. And I'll witness you. And in ten years, when someone asks how we know we're brother and sister, we won't say *we remember*. We'll say *we kept the record*. Between us. The whole time. Nothing lost that we didn't carry together."

The fiddle music swelled. Fires sparked. Down the slope, in the cart, Oren sat bolt upright, awake, pointing at the sky and shouting something about the clouds going green, and far to the north, over the mountains they had crawled out of, a thunderhead was building—huge, violet-black, lit from within, the first wild storm since the choir fell, unbound weather for an unbound world.

Yara felt it answer him across the distance, the way it always had, the way it always would: the pressure in his ears, the hum in his healed brand-scar, the old wound opening not in pain but in recognition. His storm. Their storm. Coming down out of the mountains to meet them.

Lira tilted her head—his mother's tilt, he'd swear to it now, he'd testify to it—and watched the lightning flicker inside the cloud like a pulse.

"That's ours," she said. "Isn't it?"

"That's ours," Yara said, and for the first time since the mountain, the words felt like memory.
