## 22. Thorn and Choir

The valley had learned to remember.

Yara felt it before he saw it—before the road bent past the first grey shoulder of the Thorn hills and gave them the whole of Thornvale at once. It was in the air itself, a thinness gone from it, a weight lifted. For ten years the air here had tasted of nothing at all, the way bread tastes when you have eaten only bread for a decade: not bad, merely absent. Now the wind came down off the ridge carrying woodsmoke and wet stone and the green rot of the millpond, and every one of those smells struck him like a hand laid gently on the back of his neck. *You know this,* the air said. *Say it back.*

"Say it back," Lira said beside him, laughing, because she had felt him go still.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You do that thing with your mouth."

"I don't have a thing."

"You absolutely have a thing." She walked backward a few steps to look at him, her boots crunching the frost-brittle grass, her breath steaming in the early spring cold. A year on the mountain roads had browned her and thinned her and put a scar through one eyebrow that he would never learn the story of—not because she refused to tell it, but because telling it did nothing; the story went into his ears and pooled there like water into sand, leaving no mark. She knew this. He knew she knew this. They had built an entire language around the shape of that absence, the way two people who share a house build their habits around the room where a wall used to be. "Well?" she said. "Is it how you left it?"

He looked at Thornvale.

The valley lay below them in its long green cradle—the wheat terraces silvered with last night's rain, the mill wheel turning slow and black, the crooked rooftops of the village huddled along the stream like sheep against weather. The ward-stones stood where they had always stood, but they were broken now, all nine of them, cracked open by the released souls' storm or pulled down deliberately in the months after; Oren swore the children of the free cities made a game of finding them, and Sef swore the elders of Thornvale had wept over each one like graves. From up here Yara could see the nearest stone's stump, furred with moss, no taller than a well-head.

And above it all, riding the ridgeline where the old wards had sealed the sky for sixty years, clouds were gathering. Real ones. Valley-born. The kind that came up from the sea uninvited and stayed as long as they pleased.

"They'll see us from the mill," Sef said behind them. He was carrying most of their gear out of some stubborn penance he still hadn't entirely forgiven himself for enjoying, and his voice was careful in the way it always was when they got close to home. "There'll be questions. There are always questions."

"There'll be bread," said Oren, fourteen now and suddenly all elbows, shouldering past with the ledger strapped across his back like a shield. He had carried it the whole way from the mountain without being asked. Nobody had asked him to stop. "Mill bread. You promised, Sef. Mill bread and honey."

"I promised nothing of the sort."

"You said *we'll see.* That's a promise where I'm from."

"Where you're from," Sef said gravely, "is a smuggling ship, so forgive me if I discount your standards."

They came down into the valley as the first drops fell.

* * *

The village had changed less than Yara feared and more than he hoped.

That was the thing about wards broken, he was learning. Everyone told him the forgetting would reverse—that the fog would lift and the valley would wake remembering itself—and everyone had been right, in the way that a drought ending is right. The rain comes back. But the wells don't refill overnight, and the fields don't green in a season, and the people of Thornvale, freed after a lifetime of slow erosion, woke into their own histories like divers surfacing too fast: gasping, disoriented, some of them grieving things they'd only just learned they'd lost. Old Maren had spent three days weeping over a husband she had buried twenty years ago and could suddenly recall perfectly. The cooper's son had discovered he had a half-sister in Vhal and taken the road to find her. Half the valley spent that first winter sitting very still, holding memories up to the light like coins they weren't sure were real.

Yara understood them better than anyone. His own well was still dry in exactly one place, and would be forever.

Lira walked ahead of him through the village street, greeting people by name, and every greeting was a small knife. Not because they were cruel—they weren't; the valley had folded her back in like she'd never gone, had mourned her properly once the fog lifted and found her alive, and there was joy everywhere she turned—but because of what happened next, every time. Someone would embrace her, then turn to Yara with shining eyes and say something like, *She has your mother's chin, doesn't she? Hasn't she always?* And Yara would say, "So I'm told," and mean it kindly, and watch the shine gutter just slightly. The valley remembered Lira. The valley remembered him. What the valley could not do—what nobody anywhere could do—was make the two remembrances touch.

Except in one place. One place only.

It started raining harder as they reached the square, fat warm drops that smelled of lightning even though the storm was still ten minutes out and building somewhere over the western ridge. Oren had already located the honey. Sef had already been recognized by three separate elders, each of whom gripped his hands with the complicated tenderness owed to a man who had confessed publicly, in the free cities' assembly, to everything he had done beneath the mountain—a confession that had cost him any hope of quiet and purchased, perhaps, something worth more. Yara watched an old woman press her forehead to Sef's knuckles and heard her say, "My brother is in that choir. Was. My brother *was* in that choir," and watched Sef close his eyes and accept the grief and the gratitude in the same breath, because after a year he had finally learned they arrived together.

Then the storm broke, and Yara stopped watching anything else.

* * *

It was not a large storm. That was the miracle of it, or part of the miracle. Ten years ago—in the other life, the warded life—he would have felt a storm like this building for hours as a pressure behind his eyes, a caged animal pacing in his blood, and he would have smothered it, starved it, bitten his tongue until he tasted copper rather than let one thread of it loose in a valley that forgot. Even after the wards fell, even after the mountain, the habit clung. Some mornings he woke with his fists clenched and didn't know why until Lira, from the doorway of whatever inn they occupied, said quietly, "Weather's turning," and his hands opened.

But this was Thornvale, and the sky above Thornvale belonged to whoever lived under it now.

So when the thunder rolled down the valley and the rain came slanting silver through the square, Yara did the thing that ten years ago would have been unthinkable and a year ago would have been impossible: he opened the door in himself and let it in.

The storm recognized him. It always did now—it had since the mountain, since the thousand voices' grief had poured through him and out and left the channel scoured clean. Lightning walked the ridge and he felt it walk his spine. The wind swung round and he felt it swing through his chest. He stood in the middle of the flooding square with his face tipped up and let the whole roaring gray weight of the sky pour into him and through him and out again, and it hurt, the way all true things hurt a little, and it healed, the way true things heal more.

And then Lira was beside him.

She was always beside him when it happened. He had noticed the pattern sometime around midwinter and never managed to ask her about it, partly because asking felt like pressing on a bruise, mostly because he suspected she didn't fully know herself. But whenever a storm rose, wherever they were—Vhal in the ash of its rebuilding, the strait coast in salt-wind, the high passes—she appeared at his shoulder within minutes, drawn the way she was drawn, and stood in the rain with her hair plastered flat and her face turned up, and she was not afraid. She had never once been afraid of it. Of him.

"It's beautiful," she said now, the same words, the same cadence, the fourth or fortieth time—she said it every time, and every time it landed like the first. Rain ran down her face and she was smiling into the storm with her whole self. "Yara. Look at it. It's beautiful."

And here was the place where memory lived when memory was gone:

His body knew her. That was all. That was everything. When she said *beautiful*, something in his chest answered—a chord struck on an instrument he couldn't name, in a key older than his knowing. He could not recall teaching her to whistle up wind on the summer pastures. He could not recall her falling out of the rowan tree, or the fever when she was seven, or her voice singing their mother's planting-song, or any single hour of the nineteen years they had shared before the ledger and the mountain took the price of freedom out of his skull. All of it was paid. All of it was gone. He had sung his sister free with the sum total of every moment he'd ever spent with her, and he would do it again tomorrow, and the day after, forever, and this was the arithmetic he had made his peace with or near enough.

But when she stood in the rain and called the storm beautiful, his heart turned toward her like a compass needle. Involuntary. True north. Whatever the song had taken, it had not taken *that*, and he had come to believe nothing ever would.

"Beautiful," he agreed, and meant her, and knew she knew it.

Thunder cracked overhead, close enough to feel in his teeth, and Oren whooped from under the mill eaves, and Sef laughed—actually laughed, unguarded, a sound still rare enough that Lira and Yara exchanged a glance about it afterward, always afterward—and the valley filled up with rain.

* * *

They stayed.

That was the decision, and it took no deciding at all, which was how Yara knew it was real. The free cities had offered him land, titles, a hall with his name carved over it; the council in Vhal, rebuilt from its own ashes and braver than its predecessor by exactly one burned city, had wanted to make him Marshal of Storms, which Sef found hilarious for reasons he explained at length and badly. Yara had thanked them and asked instead for goats. Somewhere in the negotiations Oren had declared, with the absolute authority of the recently parentless who have chosen their parents, that he was coming too, and nobody had dreamed of arguing.

The cottage sat on the south slope above the terraces—their mother's cottage, though Yara remembered none of it and Lira remembered all of it, and so the first weeks became a strange inverted archaeology. She walked him through his own childhood room. *This window stuck; Da never fixed it. You slept with your boots on until you were eight. You were terrified of the mill pond and lied about it constantly.* Each fact landed in him like a seed in stony ground—received, held, unable to root—but he kept them anyway. He kept them all. He had a book now, actually; Sef had started it without telling anyone, a plain ledger bought in Vhal, and on its first page in the choirmaster's precise hand: *Things Yara Hartwell ought to know.* It filled slowly. Lira added to it. Oren added to it, entries increasingly ridiculous. Yara read it at night sometimes, the way another man might pray.

The valley remembered him too, in its fashion. The elders came up the slope in the second week, formally, with bread and salt, and asked—carefully, the question clearly rehearsed—whether the storms would be welcome here now. Whether he would call them, or let them come, or stand aside.

Yara thought about it for a long moment, looking out at the terraces, the broken ward-stone furred with moss, the sky doing whatever the sky pleased for the first time in sixty years.

"They're not mine to call anymore," he said. "They're the valley's. I'll just... introduce myself."

Which was, in the end, what he did. He farmed goats. He fixed the south fence. He taught Oren to hold a storm inside himself by remembering grief, and then—this was the new lesson, the hard one, the one Yara was only now learning well enough to teach—to set the grief down again after. He sang, sometimes, alone on the ridge, fragments of the counter-song, not to unbind anything but because the melody had become a part of his body deeper than memory, and some evenings the wind seemed to sing it back.

* * *

On the evening of the anniversary—or what Lira insisted was the anniversary, the night they had fled the valley, the night of the proclamation at the mill—a storm came up the vale larger than any since the mountain. The whole household ended up on the porch: Sef with his pipe lit against the damp, Oren sprawled on the boards reading aloud from the ledger-book and correcting its inaccuracies, Lira standing at the rail's edge with rain beginning to bead her shoulders.

Lightning walked the ridge. Thunder followed, rolling down the valley like something vast turning over in its sleep, and Yara opened the door in himself and let the storm pour through, and it was sweet, it was sweeter every year, this thing that had once nearly killed him to contain.

"Beautiful," Lira said.

"Beautiful," he agreed.

She was quiet a moment. Rain drummed the porch roof. Then, in the tone she saved for the ledger-book entries, the tone of testimony, she said: "When you were eleven, you called a hailstorm over the barley because Da said you were too small to help with harvest. It hailed for an hour. He didn't even punish you. He just stood in the doorway getting pelted, and then he said, *Well. We won't starve this year anyway,* and went inside." She glanced at him sideways, rain in her lashes, checking—as she always checked, gently, without pity—that the story had landed somewhere. "I think about that a lot. How he knew. How he just... decided to love the storm instead of being afraid of it."

"I would have liked him, I think," Yara said. "Our father."

"He would have liked you." She said it simply. "He did like you. That's the whole point of the story."

Down in the valley, the mill wheel turned. The wheat stood green in the darkening rain. Somewhere below, doors were opening, faces tipping up, the whole valley learning again what it had spent sixty years forbidden to know: that the sky was not a ceiling but a country, and they were citizens of it.

Oren looked up from the ledger. "Can I write this one down?"

"What one?"

"This one. Right now. All four of us and the storm." He was already digging for his pencil. "Future evidence."

"Evidence of what?" Sef asked, amused.

"That somebody stayed," Oren said, and wrote it.

Yara leaned on the rail beside his sister, whose face he did not know and whose heart he knew better than his own, and watched the lightning stitch the mountains together, and the storm sang through him in a voice like a thousand freed souls, and it sounded, for the first time in either of his lives, exactly like home.
