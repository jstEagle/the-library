## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The bees knew before I did.

I had learned to read them the way other men read weather—the particular thrum of a hive settling in for rain, the restless spiral of scouts casting for new pasture. That morning, as I knelt in the clover with smoke curling from the tin between my hands, the hives of Hollowmere went quiet all at once, a held breath passing through six boxes of living gold. Not the silence of swarming. The silence of listening.

I lifted my head. The wind had turned.

It came down off the western crags now, and it carried with it a smell that did not belong in the valley: horse sweat, oiled leather, and beneath both something sharper—cold iron, and the faint tallow-stink of consecrated wicks. Incense made for burning out rather than blessing.

My hands stilled on the frame I'd been lifting. Wax clung warm to my fingers, and the comb gleamed up at me, hexagon upon hexagon, perfect as scripture. For eleven years this valley had asked nothing of me but patience. It gave back honey and barley and long grey evenings by Maren's stove, and in exchange I gave it the only thing I had left to give: my absence. My forgetting.

Magic could not follow me here. That was the whole of Hollowmere's promise, the reason Maren had carried me, half-dead and half-mad with grief, over three mountain passes to find it. The valley sat in a bowl of old granite shot through with veins of dead iron, and whatever current ran through living things elsewhere ran thin here, thinned to nothing, like a river losing itself in sand. Spells unraveled at the rim. Charms curdled. A witch's gift, if she were foolish enough to use it within those borders, simply guttered—snuffed like a wick pinched in wet fingers.

Which was precisely why I had buried mine so deep I sometimes pretended I'd never had it at all.

* * *

Maren found me at the hives near midday. She moved slowly these days, one hand pressed to the small of her back, her white braid silvered nearly to transparency. She had been beautiful once, I think, in the fierce way of a hawk; now she was beautiful the way winter is beautiful, spare and exacting and a little terrible.

"The bees are sulking," she said.

"The wind changed."

She stood beside me a moment, watching the clover shiver. She didn't touch me—she rarely touched anyone—but I felt her attention settle on me like a hand on the shoulder all the same. Eleven years, and still I couldn't decide whether that attention felt like shelter or like scrutiny.

"You felt it too," she said. Not a question.

"Iron," I said. "And incense. The kind they burn in—" I stopped myself, but the word was already loose in the air between us. "Court-work."

Maren's jaw tightened. "You imagine things. You always imagined things. It's what made you a liability as an apprentice and it makes you poor company now." But her eyes had gone to the western pass, and her hand had gone, without her seeming to notice, to the little iron key she wore on a cord at her throat. The key to nothing, she'd told me once. A habit from another life.

"Maren."

"Smoke your bees, Quentin. Winter stores won't lay themselves in."

She walked back toward the cottage with her spine very straight, the way she walked when she was most afraid. I had catalogued her fears over eleven years the way a naturalist catalogues birds: fear of open flame near the grimoire shelf, fear of my name spoken aloud, fear of the color of certain royal livery. Fear, above all, of riders on the western road.

I smoked the hives. My hands shook so badly I singed my sleeve.

* * *

That evening we ate barley soup and black bread at the long table, and neither of us spoke of the wind. Outside, the light died early behind the crags, as it always did in autumn, and the valley folded itself into its usual blue dusk. Somewhere downslope, Old Tam's dog began to bark—not the idle barking of a dog bored by sheep, but the low, rhythmic alarm of an animal marking something moving on a road where nothing should be moving after dark.

Maren set down her spoon.

"They'll come to the village first," she said quietly, as though continuing a conversation we'd never started. "They'll water their horses at Tamsin's well and ask their questions, and the villagers will tell them what villagers always tell crown-men, which is nothing useful, because no one in Hollowmere remembers anything they aren't reminded of. That's the valley's mercy. That's the valley's curse." She looked at me then, and her eyes were the pale grey of frost on slate. "But you're not a villager, Quentin. You remember everything."

"I don't have a past," I said. "Not here. Here I'm the beekeeper's boy who came down from the north with a bad cough and no family. That's the story. We've told it so long even I believe it."

"Do you?" Something flickered across her face—pity, or its cruel cousin, contempt. "Eleven years, boy. Do you truly believe it?"

I wanted to say yes. I reached for the yes the way a drowning man reaches for driftwood. And underneath the reaching, the way it is always underneath for people like us, the truth stirred and rolled over in its sleep: the taste of lightning on the tongue, the hum of power gathering in the palms, the night I had run from my own apprenticeship because the gift in me had grown teeth, and Maren had followed, and Maren had caught me, and Maren had—

No. I put the memory down the way I put down hot iron. Some weights you carry only by refusing to look at them.

A knock came at the door.

Three raps, spaced evenly, unhurried. The knock of someone who has never in his life needed to hurry for anyone.

Maren rose. She blew out the lamp as she passed it, and suddenly we were two shadows in a dark kitchen, and I understood with perfect clarity that she had done this a hundred times before, in other rooms, in other years, in the long career of hiding that had ended—for her—for both of us—here.

"Through the back," she breathed. "The cellar hatch. Stay under the floor and do not breathe until I call you up. Whatever you hear—" Her hand found my wrist in the dark, grip like a hawk's talon. "Whatever you hear, Quentin, you stay silent. Your silence is the only spell you have left. Guard it."

The knock came again. Then a voice, muffled by oak and distance, smooth as poured cream:

"Hedge-witch. We know you're home. The smoke's in your chimney, mistress, and smoke doesn't lie—not to us."

* * *

I went down into the cold earth and dragged the hatch shut over my head, and the dark closed around me like water.

Root-cellars smell of turnips and stone and old apple-rot, and I crouched among them with my heart slamming against my ribs, listening. Above, floorboards groaned under boots—heavy boots, several pairs. The front door opened on its complaining hinge.

"Mistress Maren." The cream-smooth voice again, closer now, filling the kitchen overhead. "Or shall we say the hedge-witch of Hollowmere? Though I confess the title puzzles me. There's precious little hedgerow in a place like this, and less magic. Iron in every rock, they say. Dead ground. Curious choice of retirement for a woman of your... reputation."

"You're mistaken, sir." Maren's voice, dry as autumn husks. "I'm a widow. I keep bees. My apprentice there is simple-minded and deaf in one ear, and he'll trouble no one."

"He certainly won't," said the voice, pleasantly, and something in the way he said it turned my blood to ice water. "We're not here for the boy. Yet. We're here for the woman who taught at Cael Rowan for nine years before she burned her name out of the rolls. Who trained apprentices in the old craft, unlicensed, unsworn. Whose last registered apprentice—a boy, fourteen years of age—ran from her care and vanished the same season the crown's writ extended to the northern provinces. A boy named Quentin Pemberton."

Silence. In the dark beneath the floor, I stopped breathing altogether.

"No Quentin here," said Maren, and her voice did not shake, and I marveled at it, hated it, loved it.

"No," the man agreed, softly. "There wouldn't be. Names are such slippery things. Souls, however—" a soft laugh, like stones knocking together in a sack— "souls leave records. Even sold souls. Especially sold souls. There's a ledger under the mountain, mistress, and your name is written in it twice. Once as seller. Once as—"

"Get out of my house."

"Ah. There she is. There's the hedge-witch." A chair scraped. Boots crossed the room, unhurried, savoring. "Witch-hunters of the Crown, mistress, are not permitted to drag a subject to court without cause. So let us speak of cause. Let us speak of the child you healed in Tamsin's village three winters past—the one whose fever broke overnight, impossibly, in dead ground where nothing breaks or bends. Let us speak of your garden, which grows in rows too straight, and your bees, which sting no man who wears your favor. Small magics, mistress. Residues. But residues are evidence, and evidence is cause, and cause—" the smile was audible— "is a writ."

Chains. The sound was unmistakable: chain pooling on flagstone, iron kissing iron.

"You'll come with us tonight," the man said. "The court beneath the mountain has questions. Answer them well, and perhaps no one goes looking for any simple-minded beekeeper's boys."

Above me, Maren said nothing at all. And in that silence I heard everything: the door, the boots, the chains, the horses stamping in the lane—and underneath it all, faint as a pulse through stone, the sound of my master being led away into the dark, and my own name waiting for her somewhere under the mountain, written in a ledger in ink I could not see, in a bargain I could not remember, in a debt that had been sold before I ever understood I was the coin.

The hatch above me stayed shut. No one called me up.

I knelt in the root-dark of Hollowmere with Maren's iron key-shaped nothing hanging cold against my chest where she'd pressed it into my hand at the table—when? Minutes ago, hours, a lifetime—and I understood that the valley had finally forgotten me exactly enough to let the world find me. That invisibility was a debt too, and mine had just come due.

In the corner of the cellar, beneath a false bottom Maren had built into the potato bin, her grimoire waited. It had waited eleven years. It had been waiting for this.

I went and got it.
