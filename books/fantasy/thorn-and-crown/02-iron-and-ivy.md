## 2. Iron and Ivy

The hunters took Maren on a morning so gray the bees refused to fly.

Quentin knew it was happening before he heard the hoofbeats—felt it first as a wrongness in his teeth, a taste like coins at the back of his tongue. The valley had no magic in it; that was why he'd come, why Maren had brought him here nine years ago, a boy of eleven with his gift still smoking from misuse. But magic-dead ground did not make a man deaf to dread. It only made the dread quieter, and therefore easier to carry.

He was in the east hive-yard when the riders came up the cart-track, four of them, in the king's iron-gray livery with ivy stamped in black on their pauldrons—the badge of the Office of Thorns, though everyone in Hollowmere had pretended for years that such an office was a story told to frighten children. Their horses picked their way through the bracken with the patience of animals that had done this work before, in villages whose names Quentin would never learn because no one left alive to speak them.

Maren stood in her doorway as if she'd been waiting all morning, which perhaps she had. She wore her good shawl, the one with the mended hem. Her hands were folded. She did not look toward the hive-yard, not once—not a flicker, not a glance—and that absence cut deeper than any look could have. *Don't come out,* that stillness said. *Don't you dare.*

"Mother Maren of Hollowmere," called the lead hunter, dismounting. He was young through the jaw and old through the eyes, and his voice had the flat courtesy of a man reading from a writ. "By order of the Crown Regent, you are summoned to answer charges of unlicensed craft."

"I keep bees," Maren said.

"You kept more than bees."

The other three dismounted. One carried a caged lantern, and inside it something shifted—not light exactly, but a hunger shaped like light, pressing against the glass the way a moth presses against a window. Quentin's skin crawled. He had seen such lanterns in Maren's grimoire, in pages she'd made him skip: *soul-lamps, by which the taken may be weighed.* He had thought them metaphor.

They put irons on her wrists—black iron, ivy-wrought—and she went without protest, without even the dignity of a struggle, and that frightened him most of all. Maren had once held off a flood with a word. Maren had argued with weather. And now she walked to the horses like a woman going to market, chin level, spine straight, and at the last moment—only then—she let her eyes find the hedge, find the shadow where Quentin stood among the hives with his heart hammering against his ribs.

She shook her head, once, small. *Stay.*

Then they were gone down the track, hooves striking sparks of mud, and the valley closed behind them like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

He gave himself until dusk. That was the bargain he struck with himself while his hands did useless things—split kindling that needed no splitting, fed hens that were already fed—as if obedience to small chores could cancel out the larger disobedience gathering in his chest like a storm.

By dusk he had lost the argument.

He went into the house and found it already rifled—not by the hunters, who'd been too certain of their catch to search properly, but by someone earlier, or perhaps by the hunters' advance man; drawers hung open, floorboards pried at the corners. They hadn't found what mattered. Under the cold hearthstone, wrapped in oilcloth against damp, lay the grimoire: Maren's whole life in ink and pressed herbs and marginalia in three hands, hers and her own master's and, in one early section, a fourth hand that Quentin had never dared ask about—smaller, hurried, a child's letters learning to be a woman's.

He took it. He also took the thing under the grimoire, which he had never been permitted to touch: a scrap of parchment no bigger than his palm, sealed with wax the color of dried blood, its surface crawling faintly when he looked at it too long. Maren had called it *the last door*, and had said, in the voice she used for things that must never be discussed: *If ever they come for me, boy, and I am not there to stop you—you burn it, do you hear? You never use it. You burn it.*

He stood in the ruined kitchen with the fire-poker in one hand and the scrap in the other, and the gray light died around him, and he thought of Maren's face turned away from the hive-yard, deliberately, so he could disobey her cleanly.

He did not burn it.

What it did, when he broke the seal and spoke the word stitched into the margin of the grimoire's final page—a word in no alphabet he knew, a word that tasted like swallowed lightning—it did quickly. There was no thunder. There was only a moment when the kitchen seemed to inhale, when every candle stub flared green, when the shadows of everything in the room leaned toward him as if listening. Then the wall of the chimney stood open like a mouth, showing not brickwork but a hillside in the dark beyond, wet fern and moonlight and the smell of rain coming.

A door-cutting. Forbidden craft, the kind Maren had burned treatises rather than teach, the kind the Office of Thorns existed to stamp out—because doors cut that way did not care what else came through with you. He felt something follow him as he stepped through, something thin and curious brushing past his ear like a moth made of winter, and he did not look at it. Looking, Maren had written, is how you invite it to stay.

The door sealed itself behind him with a sound like a sigh. On the far side, in the hills above Hollowmere, Quentin Pemberton stood shaking in the bracken with a stolen book against his chest, knowing two things with terrible clarity.

First: he had just done the exact thing that would make the hunters hunt him next.

Second: whatever debt he had just incurred—to the craft itself, to whatever had brushed past him in the door, to Maren who had spent nine years teaching him to bury his gift and whom he had repaid by digging it up within hours of her capture—he could not name it, and debts that cannot be named are the ones that collect interest.

* * *

He walked all night and half the next day, following the high shepherd paths north, because Maren had once said, apropos of nothing, that if ever he needed to run he should run north to the Graywater crossing, where the river forgot names. He'd thought it a figure of speech. Maren rarely wasted words on figures of speech.

The rain caught him on the second afternoon, a fine cold needles-rain that soaked through his coat and made the grimoire's wrapping heavy against his back. Below him the land fell away in long folds to a river the color of tarnished pewter, running wide and slow between alder banks, and on the near bank, half-hidden in the ivy-choked ruin of an old toll-house, there was firelight.

He should have walked on. Every instinct Maren had drilled into him—*be small, be dull, be nothing worth noticing*—said walk on. But the firelight was orange and human and he was seventeen years old and hollowed out with guilt, and his feet decided before his mind did.

There were four of them around the fire: a big man with a beard like storm-cloud going to rust, who sat whittling something with a knife that moved without his watching it; two young people, twins by their matching crooked noses, arguing in whispers over a chart spread between them; and a broad gray-haired woman stirring a pot with the economy of someone who resented every stir. A flat-bottomed boat lay pulled up in the reeds, riding low, its cargo lashed under tarpaulin that bulged in shapes no legal freight takes.

Smugglers. Or worse. At this point in Quentin's life, the distinction felt academic.

The big man saw him before he reached the fire—of course he did; the man had eyes like a hawk that has grown bored of hawking. He didn't reach for a weapon. He simply looked, and waited, and the waiting was somehow worse.

"You're dripping on my riverbank," the man said at last.

"I'm sorry," Quentin said, and then, because his mother had raised him honest until Maren had raised him careful: "I'm looking for the Graywater crossing."

"This is it."

"Is there—" His prepared speech collapsed. "I need to go north. I can pay. Some. Eventually."

The twins exchanged a look over their chart. The cook snorted without looking up from her pot. The big man set down his knife and studied Quentin the way he might study a coin suspected of being clipped.

"North," he repeated. "Toward the hunt, boy. There's royal riders on every road north of here asking after a runaway apprentice. Asking after him *by name*." He let that sit. "Quentin Pemberton. Hedge-witch's boy. Wanted for consorting."

The rain filled the silence. Quentin's hands had gone numb, or perhaps they had been numb since the chimney opened; it was hard to tell anymore.

"If you're turning me in," he said, and heard his own voice come out steadier than he felt, "you should know the bounty's probably better than anything in your boat."

That surprised a laugh out of the tall twin—the girl, he thought, or the boy; their laughter was identical. The big man didn't laugh. He picked his knife back up, and the whittling resumed, curls of wood falling into the mud.

"Brannock," the cook said warningly.

"I heard her, Oda." He glanced up, and for a moment something moved behind the boredom in his eyes—something that might have been pity, or memory. "Here's my problem, boy. My crew runs quiet cargo on quiet water. We don't take passengers, we don't take strays, and we especially don't take boys whose faces are on writs nailed to every toll-post from here to the capital. Taking you aboard is like strapping a lit lantern to the mast."

"I know."

"And yet." Brannock flicked a shaving off his knee. "And yet you're standing in the rain instead of running, which means either you're very stupid, or wherever you're running *to* matters more than the rope you're running *from*. Stupid I can't help. The other thing—" he shrugged, vast shoulders shifting like a hillside deciding—"that I know something about."

"He's got a book," said the male twin suddenly, nodding at Quentin's back. Everyone looked. Quentin realized the grimoire's corner was showing through the wet oilcloth, and that he'd been holding it so tight against his ribs that the shape of it must be printed on his body. "Under his coat. Been clutching it like a tithe-child clutches a saint's medal since he walked up."

"It's mine," Quentin said. "It's—my master's. They took her. The hunters. This morning."

The fire crackled. Oda stopped stirring. Even the rain seemed to lean closer, the way shadows had leaned in his kitchen, and Quentin thought again of the thin curious thing that had followed him through the door, and wondered briefly if it was listening still.

"They took her," Brannock said, not a question.

"To the court. Beneath the mountain." Saying it aloud made it real, made it a weight he'd be carrying the rest of his life. "Everyone knows what happens to witches taken there. No one comes back. And no one—" his voice cracked, traitorously, on the word— "no one does anything. So I'm doing something. Alone, apparently. In the rain."

Silence. The twins looked at each other. Oda looked at Brannock. Brannock looked at the river, at the dark water carrying its secrets north, and Quentin watched a whole silent conversation happen across that face like weather moving over hills.

"We're crossing at dawn," Brannock said finally. "You'll row. You'll row badly, and Sef will mock you, and you'll eat what Oda gives you and thank her for it, and if anyone asks, you're my sister's boy from downriver and you've never read a book in your life." He pointed the knife, briefly, at the lump under Quentin's coat. "And that stays wrapped. Whatever it is."

"Yes," Quentin said. Then, because the code of a lifetime of hiding demanded it: "Why?"

Brannock returned to his whittling. When he answered, he spoke to the wood, not the boy.

"Because ten years ago somebody crossed me at a bad ford, and didn't leave me to drown, though leaving me would have been the sensible thing." A shaving curled away. "Debts like that don't have names either, boy. You just pay them forward and hope the current keeps."

They gave him a blanket that smelled of tar and rosemary, and a place by the fire, and Oda shoved a bowl of barley soup into his hands with the expression of a woman who expressed love exclusively through force-feeding. The twins—Sef and Ila, though he couldn't yet tell which was which—made room for him without comment, and the chart between them showed the river's northern reaches marked in faded ink with little symbols he didn't recognize.

Quentin ate, and warmed, and did not sleep. Around him these strangers breathed and muttered and tended the fire, this small fragile architecture of belonging built on a riverbank in the rain, and he understood that he had already lied to them by omission—that the thing that had slipped through the door-cutting beside him was somewhere out in this dark night, that the debt he couldn't name was accruing, that Maren's head-shake in the doorway had meant *stay hidden* and he had chosen *come after me* instead.

Guilt sat down beside him at the fire like an old friend, patient and familiar.

But for the first time since the hoofbeats came up the track, it was not alone.
