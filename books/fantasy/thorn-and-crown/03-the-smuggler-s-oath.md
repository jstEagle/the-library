## 3. The Smuggler's Oath

The boy slept like a drowned man, and Brannock did not trust it.

He crouched at the edge of the campfire, turning a whetstone along the spine of his belt-knife, and watched Quentin Pemberton breathe. The lad had collapsed into the hollow between two roots not an hour after they'd pulled him from the river crossing, soaked to the bone, clutching Maren's grimoire against his chest the way other children clutched their mothers. Now he lay wrapped in Oda's spare blanket, firelight pooling gold across a face too young for the shadows under his eyes.

"You're staring," Ila said, settling onto the log beside him with the particular grace of someone who had never once startled in her life.

"I'm assessing."

"Assessing," Sef repeated, dropping down on Brannock's other side. The twins moved like bookends; where one went, the other followed, always arriving at the same moment from different directions. "That what we call it when you look at something like it owes you money?"

"Everything owes me money." Brannock set the whetstone aside. "Question is whether this one pays in trouble."

Across the fire, Oda stirred the pot without looking up. She was a broad woman built like a dockside winch, and she spoke perhaps forty words a day, each one load-bearing. She spoke none now. But her eyes flicked toward the sleeping boy, then back to her spoon, and that was as much as an opinion as she ever gave before supper.

"He crossed the Marlow at flood stage," Ila said quietly. "Alone. At night. With hunters on his trail and a book that'll get him hanged twice—once for carrying it, once for reading it."

"He didn't cross alone," Sef said. "We carried him. He fell into our boat, more like."

"He *chose* our boat. Out of every shadow on that riverbank, he chose ours." Ila tucked a strand of dark hair behind her ear. "That's either desperation or instinct, and both are dangerous."

Brannock said nothing. He was remembering the boy's hands when they'd hauled him aboard—white-knuckled around the grimoire, but steady. Not flailing. Steady. A drowning man who had already decided which thing he would not let go of, even if it dragged him under.

"So," Sef said, leaning forward, elbows on knees, firelight carving his face into planes and hollows. "Options. One: we put him ashore where we found him, wish him luck, sleep well knowing the hunters will have him inside three days. Two: we take the book and leave him. Three: we keep them both."

"And option four," Oda said.

Everyone looked at her. She kept stirring.

"We ask him what he wants."

"That's not how this works," Sef said.

"No," Oda agreed. "It's how it should work."

* * *

They woke him gently, or as gently as four smugglers knew how, which meant Sef shook his shoulder and Ila put a knife casually visible on her knee and Brannock sat opposite with his arms crossed and his most reasonable expression, the one he used on toll-keepers.

Quentin came awake all at once, no groggy blinking—the instant stillness of prey animals and apprentices of hedge-witches. His eyes went knife, fire, exits. Brannock counted the exits himself: two, both bad. Good. The boy saw them too.

"Name's Brannock. This is my crew. You're on my boat, eating my food, wearing my blanket. So we're going to talk."

Quentin pulled the blanket tighter. The grimoire sat beside him, wrapped in oilcloth, and his hand drifted toward it without seeming to know it moved. "I can pay."

"With what?"

The boy's jaw worked. "I don't know yet. But I will."

Honesty. That was worse than a lie. A lie Brannock could price. He glanced at the twins—Ila nearly imperceptible shrug, Sef's slow grin—and made his decision the way he made all of them: fast, then lived with slowly.

"Hunters took your mistress. Hedge-witch of Hollowmere. That right?"

Quentin flinched as if struck. "You know?"

"We know the crown's been sweeping the river valleys for a month. We know they pay silver for names and iron for witches. And we know—" Brannock leaned forward, letting the firelight do its work on his face, "—that whatever's in that book is worth killing for, because you swam a flooded river rather than give it up. So here's my offer, and I'll only make it once. Tell us what we've gotten ourselves into, or we drop you at the next landing with our blessing and your funeral."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere downstream a night bird called, low and mournful.

Then Quentin did the last thing Brannock expected. He unwrapped the oilcloth—not all the way, just enough—and laid his bare palm flat on the cover.

"She raised me since I was six. Maren. She taught me bees and herbs and how to listen to weather. And she taught me..." He swallowed. "Small things. Craft, she calls it. Nothing that would interest anyone. But the hunters came anyway, and they took her, and I ran, because running is the first thing she ever taught me." His voice cracked on the last word, and he hated it, Brannock could see him hating it. "She told me if they ever came for her, I was to run north and tell no one nothing. So I'm breaking the first rule she gave me by telling you anything at all."

"Why break it?" Ila asked softly.

Quentin looked at her, and there was something raw and level in his gaze. "Because I can't outrun the whole kingdom alone. And neither, I think, can you."

Sef laughed—one sharp bark. "Oh, I like him."

"Don't like him yet," Brannock warned. "Liking comes after he doesn't get us killed." He stood. "You sleep. Tomorrow we talk again."

But as he turned away, the boy said, "There are hunters on this bank. Two miles back, maybe less. They found my tracks at the ford."

Everyone stopped.

"How do you know?" Ila asked.

Quentin's hand curled closed over the wrapped book. "I listened to the river."

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold, and with it came proof. Brannock glassed the southern ridge with Oda's battered spyglass and found them: four riders, moving unhurried, the way men move who believe their quarry is already caught. Royal livery dulled with road dust. Iron cages slung behind two saddles, empty, waiting.

"They'll reach us by midday if we stay," Oda said.

"If we move, we leave sign. Mud's soft after the rain." Sef spat. "River's the only clean way out, but they'll watch the water."

Ila had gone very quiet, watching the boy instead of the riders. Watching the way he watched the riders—with recognition, not fear. Or rather: fear, and beneath it, calculation.

"There's another way," Quentin said.

"Out with it, then."

"The craft I told you about. Small things." He hesitated, and Brannock watched him weigh something, watch him decide to spend a coin he'd been hoarding. "My master used to say a hedge-witch's first duty is to be overlooked. There's a working—it doesn't hide a person. It hides a *place*. Makes eyes slide off it. Makes the mind say *nothing here* and move along." He looked around the camp, at the boat drawn up in the reeds, the cold firepit, the four of them. "I've only ever done it on a garden. A small garden."

"This isn't a garden," Sef said.

"No," Quentin agreed. "It isn't."

"It would take everything you have," Ila said suddenly. She'd stepped closer, and her voice had changed—sharper, quicker. "A working like that on open ground, with hunters close enough to smell. It would burn through you. You'd be empty for days. Maybe worse."

"You know craft?" Quentin asked her, surprised.

"I know people who knew it." Something old and closed passed behind her eyes. "Don't do it."

"If they find us, we hang. All of us. Guilt by proximity—that's the new law, isn't it? Harboring." The boy was already kneeling at the edge of the camp, pulling the grimoire into his lap, unwrapping it fully for the first time. The leather cover was scarred and mended, stitched in places with copper wire that gleamed dully even in the overcast light. "So either way I'm spending something. I'd rather spend it on all of us."

Oda spoke then, from the fire, without turning. "Let him."

Sef threw up his hands. "Wonderful. The cook has spoken. Let the sixteen-year-old do magic at us."

"Fifteen," Quentin said absently, already reading, lips shaping words that seemed to make the morning air go slightly thicker, slightly sweeter, like the moment before a summer storm.

What happened next, Brannock would struggle to describe for the rest of his life, and he was a man who had seen the Marlow freeze mid-current and a tax collector weep openly.

The boy began with the ground. He pressed both palms flat to the mud and *pulled*, and Brannock felt it—a sensation like a door closing somewhere behind his own eyes. The colors of the camp dimmed, not fading so much as stepping back. The reeds stopped being reeds-that-hid-a-boat and became simply reeds. The firepit's smoke thinned and bent, threading itself into the mist off the river until it was indistinguishable from morning fog. Even the sound of them softened; Brannock heard Sef swear and the curse arrived muted, polite, deniable.

And Quentin—Quentin knelt in the center of it like the axle of a wheel, sweat rolling down his face despite the cold, breath coming in ragged pulls, and the working poured out of him and out of the book both, copper wire on the cover glowing faint rose-gold, and still the boy held on. Held the whole valley-camp inside that sliding, forgetful hush. Held it while the sun climbed. Held it while hoofbeats sounded on the southern ridge and grew louder.

Four riders picked their way down toward the river. Brannock pressed himself flat in the reeds, knife drawn though it was useless, and watched the lead rider scan the bank—the same bank, twenty yards distant, where five people and a boat stood in plain view.

The rider's gaze slid past them. Slid and slid again, snagging like a boot on ice, unable to find purchase. The man frowned, rubbed his eyes, looked directly at Oda—who stood motionless among the reeds, a broad woman in a red shawl, impossible to miss—and his eyes watered and skated away. He said something to the others. They turned upstream. Within minutes the hoofbeats faded.

Nobody moved for a long count of fifty.

Then Quentin sagged face-first into the mud, and Ila caught him before he finished falling.

* * *

He slept until dusk. When he woke, Oda pressed a bowl of barley soup into his hands without a word, which from Oda was a knighthood. He ate like a starving thing, and the crew gathered around the rebuilt fire, and nobody spoke for a while, because there are moments when speech feels like trespassing.

Finally Sef broke it. "Right. I'll say it. What you did today, boy, hedge-witches three times your age can't do. My grandmother ran with the marsh covens before the purges, and she told me stories. None of them ended with a fifteen-year-old holding a veiling over five souls against royal iron." He poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Which means either you're lying about your age, or about your teacher, or about yourself. And I don't much care which. But I want it said aloud: this boy is not normal, and pretending otherwise is how we all die."

"I'm not lying," Quentin said simply. "I don't understand it either. Things come easier to me than they should. Maren said—" He stopped.

"Maren said what?" Ila asked.

"Nothing. It doesn't matter."

Oda looked up from her mending. "Say it anyway."

The boy stared into the fire. When he spoke, his voice was barely above the crackle of the flames. "She said sometimes she wondered what I was. Where I came from. I never knew my parents. She found me—or I was left with her, I don't remember—and she said the first time she tried to teach me the simplest listening-charm, I did it backwards and better than she could." He shrugged, a small, miserable motion. "I thought she was proud. Maybe she was scared too. Both, maybe."

Firelight. River-sound. Brannock found himself thinking of a night twenty years gone, a different fire, older faces, oaths sworn in wine and salt that had cost him two dead friends and a name he couldn't use anymore.

"Well," he said at last, standing. "Here's how it is. This crew runs salt, tin, and secrets up and down this river, and the law of it is simple: what happens on the boat stays on the boat, what's shared in camp stays in camp, and no one gets sold—no matter the price. That's the oath. We swore it in blood and bad brandy years ago, and everyone at this fire has bled for it." He looked around: Sef scowling, Ila unreadable, Oda stitching steadily, the boy pale and wrapped in his blanket like something half-hatched. "You've earned a place, Quentin Pemberton. Provisional. If you want it."

The boy's eyes went wide and wet and furious with themselves. "Why? You barely know me."

"We know you spent everything you had to keep strangers safe," Ila said. "That's the whole job description, near enough."

"And we know you're hunted, and hunted folk belong together," Sef added, "because scattered hunted folk are just a list."

Quentin looked at each of them in turn, and Brannock watched him do the arithmetic of a lifetime—the sum of every door closed, every warning given, every reason not to trust—and then watched him decide anyway.

"What does provisional mean?" he asked.

"It means," Brannock said, extending his hand, "you swear the oath, and we see if you survive us."

The boy took it. His grip was stronger than it should have been, warm from the soup, trembling only slightly. "I swear it. Whatever happens on the boat stays on the boat. No one gets sold."

"No one gets sold," the others echoed, and Oda, without looking up from her mending, said, "Supper's ready," which settled everything.

Later, when the twins had turned in and Oda had banked the fire, Brannock sat awake a while longer, watching the boy sleep. He told himself the unease crawling up his spine was just the river damp. But he kept thinking of the riders' empty cages. Kept thinking of a witch dragged toward the court beneath the mountain, and a book stitched with copper wire that glowed like a live coal, and a boy whose gift came too easy, as if it hadn't all grown from him—as if some of it had been *put* in him, long ago, by someone with a purpose.

Somewhere far to the east, under stone and silence, something slept and fed.

Brannock spat into the embers and told himself tomorrow they'd run cargo, same as always.

Tomorrow, the road said otherwise. At first light, a woman was waiting on the far bank—gray-cloaked, alone, arms folded, watching their camp with the patient certainty of someone who had already read the last page.
