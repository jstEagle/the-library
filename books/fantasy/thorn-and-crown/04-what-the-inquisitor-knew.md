## 4. What the Inquisitor Knew

Serah Vane had learned to read a camp the way other people read letters: by its smoke, its silence, its small betrayals of light. The smugglers' fire burned low and shielded in a hollow above the Greywater crossing, ringed with wet stones to kill the glare. They were careful. Careful meant guilty of something, or afraid of someone, and after eleven years wearing the crown's grey coat she had stopped believing those were different crimes.

She crouched at the edge of the alder stand and watched them.

Five figures—no, four and a half. A big man with a forester's shoulders sat whetting a knife that did not need it, the way soldiers sharpen when they are thinking. Two young ones, twins by the mirror of their movements, argued in whispers over a map weighted down with river stones. An older woman stirred a pot with the grim economy of someone who had fed armies and buried better cooks. And apart from all of them, half in shadow, a boy of perhaps sixteen held a book against his chest like a man holding a wound closed.

The grimoire. She knew it without seeing the cover. Maren of Hollowmere had written three; Serah had catalogued two of them herself, back when her hands were steady and her signature still carried weight beneath the mountain.

The boy was the one the hunters had missed. Which meant the boy was already dead, he simply didn't know it yet. Neither did they.

She waited for the watch change, because she was courteous even now, even disgraced, even hunted—and then she walked into their firelight with her hands open and empty.

* * *

It went the way such things always went. The knife man—Brannock, she would learn, though the name meant nothing yet—was up before her second step cleared the stones, blade out, voice low and flat as slate. One twin had a crossbow; the other had vanished entirely, which spoke well of their training and poorly of her evening. The cook lifted a ladle as if it were a weapon, which, in Oda's case, it very nearly was.

"Inquisitor," Brannock said. He'd seen the grey at her collar where she hadn't bothered to dye it out. Some things you kept even when keeping them cost you. "Long way from your mountain."

"I'm not from anywhere anymore." She turned slowly so they could see she carried no steel but the knife at her belt, and no warrant, and no seal. "And if I were still theirs, do you think I'd announce myself? I'd have let the twins find my body on the road tomorrow and wept over it convincingly."

The crossbow didn't waver. Good instincts, that one. "Then what are you?"

"A woman who knows what the hunters will do with the witch they took from Hollowmere." She let that sit in the smoke between them. "And a woman who knows what they'll do to the boy holding her book, once they trace him this far."

The boy flinched. She felt it more than saw it—the whole camp's temperature dropped a degree, the way air does before hail.

"You're lying," Brannock said.

"Almost certainly about some things. Not about this." She lowered herself onto a stone at the fire's edge, uninvited, because asking permission would have made her a petitioner and she needed, tonight, to be something worse: a truth. "May I? My knees are fifty-one years old and the river road is cruel."

No one answered. No one told her to leave either, which among smuggling folk constituted a treaty.

* * *

She gave them the shape of it plainly, because plainness was the only coin she had left and she intended to spend every piece of it.

"The crown doesn't run on taxes. Taxes buy bread, soldiers, bribes—the ordinary machinery. But the throne itself, the power the King's Justice carries, the wards on the palace gates, the compulsion-rites that make a rebellious duke kneel and mean it—that runs on souls. Taken, priced, sold under writ, and fed to the thing sleeping under the mountain. It sleeps, and the court feeds it, and while it sleeps contented, the crown can do things no army should be able to do."

Silence. Even the river seemed to lower its voice.

"That's a story," the boy said, finally. His first words. His voice cracked on the middle of it, and Serah thought: there it is, the sound of someone who has already half-known and hated knowing. "That's a story mothers tell children."

"It is. I helped write the official version that calls it a story." She looked at him directly, and did not soften it, because softening was a kindness the hour could not afford. "I served nine years as an inquisitor of the second chair. My work was finding witches. Do you know what I found, near the end? Ledgers. Beautiful ones, vellum and gold leaf, sealed with the Privy Signet. Names, dates, prices. Souls bought from desperate families, taken from condemned prisoners, purchased outright from magistrates who preferred coin to scandal. Every entry a transaction. Every transaction a feeding."

"Why are you telling us?" That was the other twin, returned from the dark, standing behind Brannock with a hand on his shoulder. Sef and Ila, she'd learn. "What's it cost us?"

"Nothing yet. Everything shortly." Serah drew a breath, and here was the hard part, the part she had rehearsed on the road until the words wore smooth. "The witch from Hollowmere—Maren. She was taken six days ago. Conveyed under guard to the mountain. And within a day of her arrival, her soul was sold."

The boy made a sound. Small. Like a door closing in an empty house.

"Bought by whom?"

"By the Office of the Interior Flame. That's the polite name. Her ember is in the vaults now, in glass, with ten thousand others. And here is the thing you must understand—" Serah leaned forward, firelight carving her face into something older than fifty-one years. "A soul, once sold, cannot be reclaimed by any rite of the church, any pardon of the court, any payment of gold. There is exactly one force in the world that can unmake those bargains. Dragon-fire. True fire. The last of it burns in the deep vaults, and it is dying."

* * *

They argued, of course. She had counted on the argument; argument was how honest people digested unbearable food.

Brannock wanted to know why a disgraced inquisitor would burn her own order. She told him—some of it. Enough. A raid ordered on a village of root-healers outside Vail, forty souls, on evidence she had forged because refusing the order would have meant joining them. She had signed the writ that sent the wagons. She had stood at the edge of the burning and understood, with the clarity of the damned, that she had not been deceived. She had been useful.

"After that I started reading the ledgers instead of writing them," she said. "Reading gets you stripped of rank and a cell, if you're lucky. If you're unlucky, reading gets you sold. I was lucky. Barely."

Oda, the cook, asked the question that cut closest: "Why come to us? Smugglers don't fight crowns. We go around them. That's the whole trade."

"Because going around is finished." Serah spread her hands. "The hunt is expanding. Hollowmere today, the valley towns next month, the free rivers within the year. Your routes, your clients, your children. You've been invisible because the crown hasn't bothered looking. It's bothering now. And you—" she nodded at the boy, gently, which was somehow crueller, "—are carrying a book that names names. Hedge-craft lineages. Who trained whom. Where the unregistered gifts sleep. To the court, that book is a treasure. To you, it is a lit torch in a powder store."

The twins exchanged a look that contained an entire conversation. Brannock's knife had stopped moving.

"And what do you want?" the boy asked quietly. "If you're guiding us. What do you get?"

He was sharper than his years. She liked him immediately, the way one likes a blade found in the wrong drawer.

"Passage," she said. "To the dragon's fire. I know the vault doors—I designed the ward-scheme on two of them, before I understood what they were warding. I know which of the forgotten doors still opens, because the Order forgets what it stops using, and forgetting is the only mercy left in that place. I can take you under the mountain alive. What happens after that depends on the dragon, the fire, and whatever gods have not yet been sold." She paused. "I also want it undone. All of it. The ledger, the feeding, the thing asleep beneath the coronation stone. Call it penance if you like. Penance is a selfish motive, but motives don't need to be clean. Only true."

* * *

They deliberated apart from her, at the far side of the hollow, voices pitched below hearing—but Serah had spent a career listening to people try not to be heard, and she caught the architecture if not the words. Brannock arguing caution. The girl, Ila, arguing something fiercer. Oda silent, which meant Oda was deciding. And the boy standing apart from all of it, book hugged to his chest, staring into the fire as though it might answer a question he hadn't dared ask aloud.

She used the interval to study him properly. Sixteen, maybe seventeen. Hollow-boned, river-mud to the knee, a healer's calluses on fingers that were trying very hard to look like a farmer's. Magic clung to him the way scent clings to smoke—a residue, banked deep, deliberately smothered. He had buried it. Well, mostly. Burying a gift was like burying a spring; the ground stayed wet forever.

When they came back, it was Brannock who spoke.

"We're not soldiers. We're not heroes. We move goods the crown says shouldn't move, and we keep our heads, and that's kept us alive." He crouched to her level, eye to eye, close enough that she could smell woodsmoke and river water on him. "But we don't leave our people behind. The witch fed the boy at her own table once, by his telling, and he's crew now, and crew means her blood is ours to answer for. So. You'll guide us. You'll tell us everything you know, when we ask, without trimming it to suit yourself. And the first time we catch you lying—"

"I'm dead. Or worse, sold. Yes." Serah almost smiled. It felt strange on her face, like wearing another woman's coat. "Those are fair terms. I accept them, with one amendment."

Brannock waited.

"When we reach the mountain, you'll want to turn back a dozen times. When you see what's in the vaults, you'll want to turn back a dozen more. The amendment is this: you don't get to turn back. Whatever you decide tonight binds you past the point of decency. Because the fire is dying, and it will not wait for courage to arrive on its own schedule."

The boy stepped forward then, out of the firelight's edge, and Serah saw his eyes clearly for the first time—grey-green, wary, older than his face.

"She sold me," he said. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't grief yet either; it was the sentence before grief, the cold antechamber. "In your ledgers. Years ago. There's a second name from Hollowmere, isn't there."

The hollow went utterly still. Even the twins stopped breathing.

Serah held his gaze, and gave him the only thing she had promised to give anyone on this road: the truth, untrimmed, immediately, like a blade offered hilt-first.

"Yes," she said. "There is. And you'd best sit down before I tell you whose."
