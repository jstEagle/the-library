## 5. Road of Ash

The first village was called Merrow's Ford, though by the time we reached it there was nothing left to ford but memory. The bridge had been pulled down and its stones scattered in the river like pulled teeth, and where the houses had stood there were only chimneys—dozens of them, black-fingered, rising out of the ash like the last witnesses to something no one had survived to tell.

We came down into it at dusk, single file along a drover's path, Brannock leading with his hood up and Oda's mule behind him loaded with casks of salted eel that had stopped mattering somewhere around noon. The smell reached us before the ruins did: wet char, and under it something sweeter and fouler that I did not let myself name, because naming it would have meant stopping, and stopping would have meant looking, and I could not look yet. I kept my eyes on Sef's back and counted his footsteps the way Maren had taught me to count breaths when the fear came. One for the ground. Two for the sky. Three for whatever walked between them.

"They were thorough," Ila said softly, from beside me. She said it the way other people said *it's raining*.

"They always are," Serah Vane answered, from the rear. Her voice had a flatness I'd come to distrust and depend on in equal measure. "You can tell how important they thought the place was by what they burned. A village that harbored nothing gets a warning fire. A village that harbored someone—" she nodded at the chimneys— "gets this."

"Someone," Sef said. "Or something." He didn't look back at me when he said it, which was worse than if he had.

We made camp that night inside the shell of a tithe-barn whose roof had gone but whose stone walls still held the day's heat like a cupped hand. Brannock set watches without discussion. Oda built a small fire, because Oda built a small fire everywhere, on the principle that a crew that ate warm slept well, and a crew that slept well fought better, and any hunter who could find us by cooksmoke deserved to be robbed anyway. It was one of her few complete sentences, delivered once over her stew, and it had become law.

I sat against the barn wall with Maren's grimoire in my lap and my hands flat on top of it, not opening it. The leather was warm from my body. It felt like holding a sleeping animal that might wake hungry.

"You knew her," Ila said, settling beside me. Not a question. Ila never asked questions; she offered openings and waited to see if you'd walk through them.

"I'm going to get her back," I said.

"I know. That's not what I asked."

I looked at her. Firelight moved in her eyes, and beyond her, past the broken doorway, the chimneys of Merrow's Ford stood against the last grey light like a congregation of the burned.

"She taught me everything," I said finally. "And then I ran away from it. And now they've taken her, and every mile of road behind us is—" I gestured at the valley of ash around us, helpless. "This is what following me costs."

"No," Ila said. "This is what following *anyone* costs. You just happen to be walking in front."

It should have comforted me. It didn't, quite. But I slept, or something near enough to sleep, and dreamed of bees.

* * *

The second village had no name left that anyone would say aloud. We passed through at midmorning, and it was worse than Merrow's Ford because some of it still stood—the mill, half the row of cottages—and because there were people in it. Or rather, there had been people in it, three days ago, according to Serah reading the cold ashes of their cookfires. Now there were only two figures left: an old man sitting motionless in a doorway, staring at nothing with his hands open on his knees as if waiting for them to be filled; and, in the shade of the collapsed byre, a child.

I saw the child before I understood why I was seeing him. He lay curled on his side in the trampled straw, maybe six years old, maybe seven, his skin the color of tallow and his breath coming in a thin, torn whistle, each exhale dragging like cloth over thorns. Fever-heat rolled off him even from ten feet away. His lips were cracked black. There was a wound in his side, long and ugly, dressed with filthy rags gone brown and stiff.

"Lung fever on top of a bad cut," Serah said quietly, when we drew level. She knelt, looked, and rose again with her face closed like a door. "He'll be dead by tomorrow. Tonight, more likely."

"Then we bury him properly," Brannock said. His voice was gentle in the way Brannock's voice only got when he was about to refuse something.

"We don't have time to dig graves," Sef snapped. "We don't have time to stop. Every hour we stand here is an hour the hunters gain, and they are *behind* us, Brannock, you know they're behind us—"

"He's right about the time," Serah said.

And I was already kneeling.

I don't remember deciding. That's the truth of it, the thing I turned over and over afterward on the road, trying to find the moment where conscience outran sense, and failing. There was no moment. There was only the boy's breath whistling, and my hands moving, and the old craft rising in me like water finding a crack—Maren's voice in my head saying *ground yourself, Quentin, root before branch*, and my palms coming down on either side of the ragged wound, and the words leaving me in a whisper so old it wasn't in any language anymore.

Warmth went out of me and into him. I felt it go, felt it leave through the heels of my hands like blood from a opened vein, carrying the fever with it, knitting what was torn, drawing the poison up and out and into the trampled straw where it darkened the ground. The boy's breathing changed. The tearing whistle smoothed. Color crept back into his face like dawn coming up over snow.

And I felt the working reach out from me like a bell rung in the dark—a ripple through everything alive within miles, every root and bird and beating heart feeling, however dimly, that something had been done. That magic had been spent. That a hedge-witch's apprentice had stood in a burned village and *worked*.

I knew, even as the warmth left me, that I had lit a beacon.

"—Quentin."

Sef's voice. Very quiet. Which meant very loud.

I looked up. The whole company was staring at me—Brannock with his jaw set, Ila with her head tilted, Oda standing frozen halfway to her pack—and Sef standing over me with an expression I had never seen on him, because Sef laughed at everything, and he wasn't laughing now.

"How far," he said.

"What?"

"How far does that carry? How far away can they feel it? Tell me exactly, since you clearly know so much, *how far*?"

"Sef." Brannock's voice, low.

"No, I want to know." Sef hadn't raised his voice. That was the frightening thing. "Three days we've walked through this. Through *this*—" he swept an arm at the ruined village, at the old man in the doorway who had begun, very slowly, to turn his head toward us— "and he hasn't touched a soul, because he knows better. And now, because of one dying brat he's never seen before, because it made him feel *good*, he lights a fire that says HERE, HERE I AM, COME AND GET ME—"

"It wasn't about feeling good—"

"Then what was it about?"

"Being *human*!" The word tore out of me louder than I meant, and the old man flinched at it, and I dropped my voice to a shaking whisper. "What do you think they burn villages for, Sef? Because magic is dangerous? Because witches eat babies?" I pointed at the boy, breathing easy now in the straw, his fever broken, alive. "They burn villages because people like me heal children like that, and the crown can't abide anything in these hills having power that isn't theirs. If I walk past a dying child to save my own skin, then I'm helping them. Then everything they've done here, I've done too."

"That is *not*—"

"Enough." Brannock. Not loud. But the word fell on all of us like a stone down a well. He crouched, put two fingers under the boy's jaw, checked his pulse with the same care he used checking a knot. "He lives," Brannock said. "That's done, and I won't unmake it, and neither will any of you. What's not done is decided: do we take him with us, or do we leave him here to be found?"

"With the old man," Ila said. "His grandfather, maybe. Someone will come back for them. Someone always comes back."

"And if the hunters come instead?" Serah asked. She was watching me with an expression I couldn't read—something between pity and appraisal, like a woman weighing coin. "If they follow your working here and find a healed child, they'll know exactly what passed through. They'll ask him questions. He's six. He'll answer."

Silence. Somewhere above us a crow called, once, twice, and got no answer.

"Then we make sure they can't trace it," I said. My hands were still trembling. I could feel the hollow where the warmth had been, a cold spot in my chest like a window left open in winter. "There's a masking. Maren taught—it's in the grimoire. A knot tied backwards. It won't hide what I did, but it'll hide *where*. They'll feel the working and find nothing, like smoke with no fire."

"Can you do it, drained as you are?" Serah asked.

"I can do it."

"Do it fast," Sef said. He had turned away, arms crossed, looking down the road south, the road we'd come by. "Because I'm telling you now—I heard it this morning, before any of you were awake. Hoofbeats. Far off, maybe, but on this road. We may already be too late."

My blood went to ice. While I had knelt in the straw pouring my gift into a stranger's child, the hunt had been riding toward us through the morning mist.

I dragged the grimoire onto my knees and began, fingers clumsy with exhaustion, to tie the knot backwards.

* * *

The masking took everything I had left. When it was done the working hung over the village like a held breath, and I hung with it, and Brannock had to half-carry me the last quarter mile off the road into a fold of the hills where Oda had found water. I drank until my stomach sloshed. The world kept tilting.

Sef did not speak to me. He checked the mule's harness, checked it again, walked the perimeter twice, and when I tried to catch his eye he looked through me as if I were weather.

It was Ila who finally sat down across from me while the light failed, and handed me a strip of dried apple, and said, in her flat way:

"He had a little brother. Before the crew. Back when he was somebody's son in somebody's village."

I bit the apple. Said nothing. Waited.

"A fever, like today. There was a cunning-man two valleys over who could have saved him. My brother wanted to go. Sef said no—said it was too far, too risky, too many soldiers on the roads since the edict. So they stayed home. And the fever took him in four days, and after that Sef learned everything there was to learn about risk, and none of it ever brought anyone back."

She stood, brushed off her skirts, and looked down at me with those firelight eyes even though there was no fire.

"So understand what you cost him today. And understand that he'll forgive you eventually, because he forgives everyone, but he'll do it slower than the rest of us, and he'll watch you the whole way to the mountain. Don't give him a reason. Not one more."

She walked away into the dusk to take her watch, and I sat alone with the hollow in my chest and the taste of apple, thinking about the price of things.

It was full dark when Serah Vane came and crouched beside me, and her face in the starlight was the strangest I'd ever seen it—not flat, not closed, but almost afraid.

"The boy," she said. "Before we left. He grabbed my sleeve."

"And?"

"He said thank you. Children say thank you." Her voice was careful, each word placed like a stone in a stream. "But then he said something else. He said, *the lady with the iron key says to tell the witch's boy that they already have what they need.*"

Cold walked up my spine, vertebra by vertebra.

"Serah. What lady with an iron key?"

"That," said the disgraced inquisitor slowly, "is a question I have been dreading since the day they stripped my rank. Because there is only one iron key in the court beneath the mountain—and the woman who carries it is supposed to be locked behind her own door."
