## 6. The Court Beneath

The door had no handle. That was the first thing Serah had loved about it, twenty years ago, when she was a novitiate with ink-stained fingers and a head full of the Order's beautiful lies: a door that admitted only those who already knew it existed. She had found it in the oldest of her mentor's ledgers, a marginal sketch in faded iron-gall ink—a rectangle, a keyhole drawn as an eye, and beneath it a single line in her mentor's cramped hand: *The Inquisitor's Door remembers its own.*

It remembered her still.

She stood before it now in the blue hour before dawn, at the base of the mountain's western flank where the rock wore a skin of frost and lichen, and she pressed her bare palm to the stone and spoke the words she had sworn never to speak again. The words were not magic. The Order had never needed magic; that was the horror of it, the thing she had understood too late. The Order needed only names, and signatures, and the patient machinery of law turned toward cruelty. The words were a password, nothing more—a sentence from the *Codex Vindictae*, the third confession of Saint Aldric, the part they never taught to novitiates because it confessed too much.

*We do not punish the guilty. We make the guilty.*

The stone shivered under her palm like a horse shaking off rain. Then a seam opened, thin as a hair, and cold air breathed out of it—air that smelled of tallow smoke and old parchment and, beneath both, something sweeter and far worse. Honey, almost. Rotting honey.

"Two hours," Serah said over her shoulder to Brannock, who crouched in the bracken with the twins flanking him like a pair of dark birds. "If I'm not back by the bell after matins, go on without me."

"We don't leave people," Sef said.

"You'll leave me," Serah said, "because if you come in after me you'll die, and so will the boy's chance of getting his master back." She met the smuggler's eyes—grey eyes, river eyes, eyes that had looked at her across three weeks of road with a suspicion she had earned—and she added, quietly: "I spent eleven years learning how this place kills people. Let me use what I know for once."

Brannock said nothing. He simply nodded, once, the way he did everything—economically, as though motion cost coin—and Serah turned and stepped through the seam in the mountain, and the stone closed behind her like water closing over a drowned man.

* * *

Inside, the dark was total and then, gradually, not. Lamps burned along the descending stair without flame, wicks glowing with a light that gave no warmth—the court's own power, soul-fed, soul-bought, burning cold because what fed it had no heat left to give. Serah counted the stairs out of habit. Three hundred and twelve down to the first landing. She had climbed them every day for eleven years, first as a novitiate, then as a full inquisitor with a grey cloak and a seal of black wax, and she had never once thought about what lit them.

Now she thought of nothing else.

The corridor at the bottom stretched ahead of her, ribbed with arches, each arch hung with a small brass bell that made no sound. Bells for the dead, she had been told as a girl. Bells that rang only when a bargain was struck. She had believed, then, that the bargains were few—that the crown took souls the way a surgeon takes a limb, rarely, reluctantly, only when the body politic demanded it. She had stood in this corridor on the night of her elevation and heard one bell give a single soft chime, and she had felt honored to witness it.

She knew better now. The bells did not ring because the bargains were rare. They rang because each one was registered, witnessed, filed. There was a clerk for it. There was a *form*. Somewhere below her feet, in the archive she was walking toward, lay forty years of those forms, and every bell in this silent corridor had rung for a name written on them, and not one of those names had been asked.

Her boots made no sound on the stone. That was deliberate; the floor was laid in a lattice that drank noise, another courtesy of the court's stolen fire. She passed the door to the Confessional Hall, where she had once sat across from a weeping woman accused of healing her neighbor's child with a charm her grandmother taught her, and Serah had watched the woman sign, and the woman had signed because the alternative was the pyre, and afterward the woman's eyes had gone strange and flat as coins, and everyone in the hall had agreed the proceedings were just.

Serah had gone back to her cell and slept well. She wanted, walking past that door now, to find the woman she had been and shake her until her teeth rattled. *You slept well. You slept well because you believed the signature was mercy.* It was not mercy. It was harvest. The woman had not given her soul; the court had taken it and called the taking consent, the way a thief calls theft borrowing.

She kept walking. Past the Hall of Seals, past the cloister where the inquisitors took their meals in silence, past the little chapel where the Order prayed to a god whose face had been chiseled off the altar two centuries ago and never replaced. The rot was everywhere once you knew to look for it—in the fine new locks on doors that guarded nothing but paper, in the way the novitiates she passed in the dark kept their eyes down and their steps quick, in the smell of that sweet rot-honey thickening as she descended, layer by layer, toward the archives.

A young inquisitor crossed her path at the third landing. Seventeen, perhaps. Grey cloak still stiff with newness. He glanced up, saw her face—an inquisitor's face, even disgraced, even stripped of seal and cloak—and went pale and pressed himself against the wall, murmuring, *"Sister."*

Serah stopped. Looked at him. He had her old mentor's jaw, or close enough; all the Order's children came to resemble each other eventually, worn smooth by the same doctrine.

"The bells," she said, before she could stop herself. "When did you last hear them ring?"

He blinked. "I—there was one yesterday, sister. A hedge-witch, brought up from the west valleys." His voice dropped, eager and awful. "They say her craft was considerable. They say the Emberwright himself attended the signing."

Maren. Her stomach turned over slowly, like a boat coming about. *They say her craft was considerable.* That was what they would say about Quentin, someday, if Serah failed—if they caught the boy and put a quill in his hand and fear in his heart and told him signing was the kind thing, the merciful thing, the just thing.

"Thank you, brother," she said, and walked on, and did not let herself shake until she was alone again in the dark between lamps.

* * *

The archive was called the Quiet Room, which was the court's idea of a joke, or would have been if the court had a sense of humor. It occupied a vault hollowed from the mountain's root, and it was cold—not the clean cold of winter but the cold of a place where warmth had been systematically removed, decade by decade, because warmth was for the living and the Quiet Room served the dead. Shelves rose into darkness. Ledgers lay on them in their thousands, spines cracked, corners furred with mold, each one stamped with the crown and the eye.

Serah had a key. Of course she had a key; keys were the one thing the Order never revoked, because revoking a key meant admitting there was something to lock away. She let herself into the annex marked *Provincial Registrations — Western Reaches*, and she pulled the lamp closer, and she began to search.

Hollowmere. She ran her finger down the index. Hollowmere, Hollowmere—small valley, unremarkable, flagged in the margin with the notation *low yield*, as though souls were wheat and the reeve had filed a disappointing harvest report. Two entries. Only two entries in forty years, and the second one recent: *Maren of Hollowmere, hedge-craft, taken under writ, signed under duress-provision nine, ember lodged Vault Four.* Duress-provision nine. The legal fiction that a person threatened with fire had freely chosen. Serah's hand shook as she copied the vault number onto a scrap of paper, because somewhere above her Maren's soul sat in a glass vessel in a locked drawer, and it was sitting there *legally*, and that was the word that made her want to scream until the mountain came down.

Then she turned to the first entry, and the world went quiet in a different way.

*Quentin. No surname recorded—foundling. Age at signing: six years. Sold by guardian (Maren of Hollowmere) under emergency provision: life-debt clause. Consideration rendered: the subject's survival, medical intervention granted by court physician. Ember lodged Vault Seven, shelf nineteen.*

Serah read it four times. She read it standing, then sitting on a crate of older ledgers, then standing again, because her legs would not decide what they wanted. Six years old. Six years old, and dying—she knew the shape of it without knowing the details; the life-debt clause existed for exactly one purpose, to let the court buy a child's soul while pretending to save its life, and the physicians who performed those interventions were paid triple rate and forbidden to speak of it. And Maren—Maren, who had raised the boy, taught him bees and herbs and the small honest magics of hedgerow and hive—Maren had carried a dying six-year-old to the court beneath the mountain and signed him over, because the alternative was a grave.

And then Maren had taken him home and never told him. Had raised him, trained him, loved him, let him flee her apprenticeship and hide in a dead valley—all while the boy's own soul sat in a glass ember ninety feet below where Serah now stood, shelf nineteen, Vault Seven, gathering dust and giving off that faint sweet rot-smell that Serah had been breathing since she came through the door.

*Sold twice over,* she thought. *Once by the court that bought him, once by the woman who never told him.*

She should have felt triumph. She had found it—the thing Brannock's crew needed, the leverage, the truth. Instead she felt sick, deep and structural, the way she had felt the day she finally read the *Codex* end to end and understood that the Order she had given her youth to was not corrupted but functioning exactly as designed. This record was not an aberration. It was a form, filled out neatly, in a fair hand, with margins ruled straight. Someone had filed a six-year-old's soul between supper and compline and gone to bed.

Serah sat in the cold a long time, listening to the mountain breathe around her. Above her, three hundred and twelve stairs up, a boy was waiting who trusted her—or trusted her the way you trust a knife you've decided to carry: carefully, watching the edge. He had fled his master. He had buried his gift. He had built a whole quiet life out of the conviction that his past held nothing worth reclaiming.

She was about to hand him the proof that his entire self had been a transaction before he was old enough to write his own name. That the woman he was risking his neck to rescue had been the seller. That his magic—his beesong, his green craft, whatever small bright thing he'd buried in that valley—had grown in soil the court technically owned.

He would hate her for telling him. He might hate Maren worse. He might break, right there on the road, with hunters behind them and the dragon's fire guttering ahead of them, and Serah would have done it anyway, because she had spent eleven years inside an order that believed truth was a tool to be rationed, dispensed by the powerful to the obedient, and she had left that order over precisely this: the decision, made in rooms like this one, that some people could not bear to know things.

People could bear to know things. What they could not bear was being managed.

She copied the entry in full, her handwriting steady now, the steadiness of a decision made. Vault Seven, shelf nineteen. She folded the page into her coat, next to her heart, where she used to keep her seal.

On her way out, passing the silent bells, she paused beneath the newest one—brass still bright, rung once for Maren of Hollowmere—and she did something she had never done in eleven years of service. She reached up and touched it, and willed it, with all the force of her faithless heart, to ring again.

It stayed silent. But somewhere below, in Vault Seven, an ember flickered—as if something sold had heard its name spoken, and stirred.

Serah climbed toward the door that remembered her, faster now, carrying a truth heavy enough to hurt everyone it touched. By the bell after matins, she would be back among the bracken, and Brannock would ask what she'd found, and she would look at Quentin Pemberton across the cold morning fire and tell him exactly what the court had filed under his name.

She only hoped the boy would still be standing when she finished.
