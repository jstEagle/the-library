## 7. Sold Twice Over

The fire had burned down to a red eye by the time Serah came back to them, and Quentin knew before she spoke.

He had learned to read people the way Maren had taught him to read weather—by the small tellings, the way a cloud's belly goes green before it splits. Serah Vane climbed the last of the bracken-slope with her shoulders squared like a woman walking into a headwind she'd chosen herself, and her eyes went past Brannock, past Sef and Ila and Oda's iron pot, and found him across the cold morning light as though he were the only fixed point in a turning world.

She sat down on the far side of the ashes. She did not warm her hands. That was the first telling.

"You found the records," Brannock said. Not a question. He was mending a net with hands that never stopped working, even when his voice did. "Maren of Hollowmere."

"Maren of Hollowmere." Serah's voice was level, the way ice is level until it isn't. "Captured on the third of Harrow-month. Soul sold within a day—standard procedure for a witch of her classification. The ember is in Vault Seven. It's alive. It answered when I read her name."

Oda set down her ladle. Sef and Ila exchanged the look twins exchange when one thought is moving through both skulls at once. Quentin sat very still with his hands in his lap and watched the ash drift, because if he looked at anyone he would have to be someone, and right now he wanted very badly to be no one at all.

"But," Brannock said, because Serah's *but* was hanging in the air like smoke that wouldn't rise.

Serah turned her head slowly and looked at Quentin again.

"There were two entries under Hollowmere," she said. "Two souls filed, two embers vaulted. The second sale predates the hunt by eleven years."

The valley went quiet in the way valleys do—the wind stopping, the bees in the heather holding their note, the river below them running on indifferent and loud.

"Eleven years," Quentin said. His own voice sounded like it belonged to a boy standing behind him.

"The record names the seller." Serah took something from inside her coat—not a document, not here, not where any of them could ever prove what she said next, only the memory of one, worn smooth by a day of reading. "A hedge-witch, licensed under the old covenant. The buyer's ledger notes the reason: *payment rendered in exchange for the child's life.* A fever. The kind that takes a whole village's children and leaves the parents counting."

"No," Quentin said.

"The child's name," Serah said, gently, which was worse than cruelty, "was Quentin Pemberton."

* * *

Afterward, he could not say how long he sat there. Time went strange, the way it did inside a working—stretched and sweetened and wrong. The crew talked around him; he heard the words as one hears rain on a roof, meaning nothing about him.

"—couldn't have known," Ila was saying. "She was his mother in every way that counts—"

"That's exactly what doesn't count," Sef snapped back. "You don't get to love someone and sell them. Pick one."

"She didn't sell him," Oda rumbled from the pot. "She bought his life. There's a difference, girl, and the difference is everything."

"Is it?" Sef said. "Is it, though? Ask him."

And they all turned, and asked him with their eyes, and Quentin stood up so fast the world tilted.

He walked away. He meant to walk away—he got perhaps forty paces into the bracken, far enough that the fire was a smudge and the voices a murmur, and then his legs gave out and he sat down hard among the wet ferns and shook.

Because here was the thing he could not stop doing: he was *counting*. Eleven years. Eleven years ago he'd been six years old, burning with fever in Maren's cottage while the fever ate its road through Hollowmere, and he remembered—God, he *remembered*—the taste of the poultice, bitter as grave-dirt; the sound of Maren singing low over him, an old song in a language the old covenant forbade; and then a night when the singing stopped and something cold moved through the room like a draft with intent, and after that night he'd gotten well.

He'd always thought the getting-well was Maren's craft. Her gift, poured out for him.

It hadn't been hers alone. Something under the mountain had taken the other half of the payment. Something under the mountain had been carrying a piece of him ever since, in a glass jar, in the dark, in Vault Seven—and he had felt it, hadn't he? All his life, the hollow spot in the middle of him, the place where joy didn't quite reach. He'd thought it was grief for parents lost to plague. He'd thought it was just how he was made.

*Sold twice over,* he thought. Once by fate, once by love, and there was no way to tell which cut deeper.

He became aware, without hearing footsteps, that Brannock was sitting nearby on a stone. That was a smuggler's trick—arriving without arrival.

"I'm not going to run," Quentin said hoarsely.

"I know."

"I want to run."

"I know that too." Brannock's big hands worked at nothing, pleating empty air. "Boy, I've carried cargo I wasn't proud of. Silk woven by slaves. Wine taxed off starving farms. You carry it anyway, because the alternative is putting it down, and some things—" he exhaled through his nose— "some things you don't get to put down. You just decide who carries them with you."

"He lied to me my whole life."

"Aye."

"He *chose* this. He chose the bargain, chose the silence, chose—eleven years of watching me feel like a cup with a hole in it, and never saying a word."

"Aye," Brannock said again, and did not soften it, because he was Brannock. "And he also kept you breathing. Both things are true, boy. The court's whole rotten power is built on making folk believe they can't both be true. Don't you go thinking like them."

Quentin stared at the ferns. "I need to see him."

Brannock nodded slowly. "Thought you might. Serah says there's a way down—a door that remembers her. We move tonight."

* * *

They went down in the dark before dawn, all six of them, because Brannock would not split the crew and Oda would not let Brannock go anywhere without breakfast first, and that small stubbornness—that absurd, ordinary kindness of hot bread wrapped in cloth for a descent into hell—was the only thing that kept Quentin walking.

The door remembered Serah. It opened on a stair of black stone worn hollow in the middle by centuries of feet, and the mountain closed over them like water.

He felt it before he saw anything: the pull. Faint, rhythmic, patient as a tide—the crown's hunger, drawing upward through the rock from whatever slept beneath. And threaded through it, thin as a hair, a frequency he recognized with a lurch that nearly buckled his knees. *Himself.* The missing piece of himself, humming somewhere below, answering the pull.

"This way," Serah whispered, and led them not toward the vaults but along a gallery of cells—because she was still, in her bones, an inquisitor, and inquisitors knew where prisoners were kept.

They found Maren in the ninth cell.

He was smaller than Quentin remembered. That was the first shock—that imprisonment could shave a man down like a whittled stick, that three weeks of the court's hospitality could do what twenty years of mountain winters had not. His hair had gone fully white at the temples. His hands, those clever hands that could coax honey from a wild hive and quiet a spooked horse with a touch, lay folded in his lap like borrowed tools.

But when the lock gave and the door swung open, Maren looked up, and his eyes found Quentin first, before Brannock's crossbow, before the torchlight, before anything—and filled with such naked relief and such naked fear, both at once, that Quentin understood the man had spent three weeks dreading exactly this moment more than he'd dreaded the pyre.

"Quentin." Maren's voice cracked on the name. "Oh, lad. You shouldn't have come. You should never have—"

"Don't." The word came out of Quentin like a struck spark. "Don't you *dare* tell me what I should or shouldn't have done. Eleven years. ELEVEN YEARS, and you never told me."

The cell was too small for what happened next. Quentin heard his own voice rising, breaking, and couldn't stop it—the words pouring out of the hole in him, eleven years of hollowness given a name and a shape and a seller.

"You let me lie awake feeling like half a person and you called it grief. You taught me to bury my gift deep—*your* lesson, your rules—and the whole time the deepest part of me was already gone, already SOLD, and you sat across the supper table from me every night of my life knowing it—"

"I was going to tell you." Maren struggled upright, and his chains sang against the wall, a thin bright terrible music. "When you were grown. When it couldn't hurt the working—I was going to take you to the vault myself, lad, I've planned it since you were twelve, every road, every risk—"

"You had *eleven years*—"

"And YOU HAD A LIFE!" 

It rang off the stone. Maren sagged back against the wall, breath ragged, and when he spoke again it was quiet, and that was so much worse.

"The fever was killing you. Do you understand? Not might. Was. I watched three children die on your street in one week, and I held your hand and felt your pulse slowing under my fingers like a stream freezing over, and there was one bargain left in the old covenant that could stop it, and it had a price, and the price was *you*—a tithe of you, the court takes a tenth of a soul, that's all, a tenth, and the rest of you would live." His eyes were wet and blazing. "I paid it. I paid it and I hated myself every single day since, and yes, I let you believe it was grief, because what would you have done at seven years old, Quentin? What would you have done at ten, at fourteen, learning what you were, learning what I'd traded of you—would you have lived? Would you have *been*? Or would you have spent your whole childhood dying of a debt you couldn't pay?"

Behind Quentin, in the doorway, nobody breathed.

"So don't stand there and ask me why I was silent," Maren said, and his voice finally broke, all the way through. "Ask yourself what my silence *bought*. Because it bought you every morning you ever woke up. It bought you these years in the valley, the bees, the hills, the friends standing in that doorway who crossed a kingdom for you. I chose your survival over your freedom, boy, and it was the only choice I ever made without flinching, and I will answer for it to the end of my days—but I will not *apologize* for keeping you alive."

Silence. Long enough for the torch to gutter and recover.

Quentin looked at his master—at the white in his hair, the chains, the ruined hands, the stubborn burning love that had cost a tenth of a boy and a lifetime of lying—and felt the fury drain out of him and leave behind something colder and harder to hold. Not forgiveness. Not yet, maybe not ever. But underneath the betrayal, unwelcome as a stone in a boot, was the terrible knowledge that he could not swear, honestly, that he would have chosen differently. That if it had been a child in front of him, feverish and fading, and one forbidden bargain between the child and the dark—

He turned away. Walked to the cell door. Stopped.

"Vault Seven," he said, without turning around. "Serah says the embers are alive. Mine answered when she read my name." He swallowed. "We're going to steal them back. Yours and mine. And then, Maren, you and I are going to have the conversation we should have had eleven years ago—and you're going to start by explaining why the ledger lists *two* payments from Hollowmere, when the covenant price is a tenth."

Behind him, he heard Maren go very still.

"...You read the ledger?"

"Serah did. Two entries. Two sales." Now he turned, and the cold thing in his chest had a purpose now, and the purpose kept him upright. "One for my life. What did you sell the *second* time, Maren? And what did the court give you for it?"

The old man's face, in the torchlight, went gray as the ash above ground.

And far below them, down through the black stone, through the galleries and the guard-posts and the locked iron doors, in Vault Seven, an ember began—very softly—to sing.
