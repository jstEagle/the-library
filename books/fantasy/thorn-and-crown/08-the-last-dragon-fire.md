## 8. The Last Dragon-Fire

Brannock had smuggled silk past customs men, gunpowder under a bishop's nose, and once — a story he told only when the ale was strong enough — a sleeping tiger in a crate marked *salted herring*. He had never smuggled hope down into the dark of a mountain, and it sat worse in his belly than any contraband he'd ever carried.

The passage Serah Vane had called *the inquisitor's door* was a seam in the rock behind a frozen waterfall, and it went down the way a throat goes down: narrow, wet, and with a sense that something vast was swallowing them by inches. Torches guttered in air that moved without wind. Sef went first with the rope, Ila behind him counting their steps aloud in a whisper, because Ila counted everything when she was afraid and had never once admitted to being afraid.

"Four hundred," she said. "Four hundred ten."

"Save your breath," Brannock muttered.

"For what?" But her voice shook less than it had an hour ago, and he took that for courage, which is what he took most things for.

Oda came last, as always, her cleaver riding her hip like a second spine. She hadn't spoken since they'd entered the mountain. Oda's silences were not like other people's silences; they were load-bearing.

Ahead of them all walked Quentin Pemberton, hedge-witch's runaway, seventeen years old and carrying a grimoire against his chest the way a soldier carries a wound. And beside him, half a step back as though she didn't trust the ground not to lie to her, walked Serah Vane, disgraced inquisitor, her gray eyes catching torchlight like coins at the bottom of a well.

They were going to steal souls out of the crown's own vault. Brannock turned the thought over and over as the dark deepened, looking for the flaw in it, the way his fingers found flaws in a lock. There had to be one. There was always one.

He couldn't find it. That frightened him more than anything else.

* * *

The heat came before the light.

It rose up through the stone like breath through a keyhole, warm and faintly sweet, smelling of cedar smoke and hot iron and — beneath that, so faint Brannock almost missed it — flowers. Somewhere in this mountain that had never known a summer, something was blooming or burning, and he could not tell which.

"The flame's failing," Serah said quietly. "Can you feel it? It used to reach the upper galleries. Now you have to come down to meet it, like visiting a sick man."

"How far down is the vault?" Quentin asked.

"Past the dragon."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The rope creaked. Ila's count went on: six hundred, six hundred twenty.

Then the passage opened.

Brannock had imagined a cavern, because mountains made caverns, but nothing had prepared him for a cathedral of glass. The walls were fused sand, translucent as sea-ice, and through them glowed a light the color of late afternoon — amber, honeyed, old. It came from below, from a vast chamber where the floor fell away into a bowl of coals, and coiled upon those coals, dim as a banked hearth-fire, lay the dragon.

She was smaller than the stories. That was the first cruelty of it. Brannock had grown up on tales of dragons that blotted out moons, whose wingbeats made weather. This one was gaunt, her scales gone dull as old pewter, her wings folded close like a beggar's cloak. She might have stretched from one end of the chamber to the other, if she'd had the strength to stretch. Her eye — one eye, open, watching them descend the glass stair cut into the wall — was the size of a cartwheel and the color of a dying sunset.

"She knows we're here," Sef whispered.

"She has always known," the dragon said.

Her voice did not boom. That was the second cruelty. It was thin and dry, like pages turning, like ash settling, and it filled the chamber anyway, coming from everywhere, from the glass itself.

"Thieves," she said. "Come to rob the dying. Or pilgrims. I have not had pilgrims in three hundred years."

"Pilgrims," Serah said, and knelt on the stair, which Brannock had never seen her do for any living thing. "Lady, we've come to ask a fire of you."

"A fire." The great eye turned, slow as a season changing, and fixed on Quentin. "I know what you carry, boy. I can smell the book. And I can smell what was taken from you — twice-taken, once given. You reek of bargains." A sound moved through her, deep and rattling, and Brannock realized it was laughter, and that it hurt her to laugh. "So does your friend there. The big one. He smells of a bargain too."

Brannock's mouth went dry. "I don't know what she means," he said, too fast, too loud.

"You will," the dragon said, "or you won't. It changes nothing tonight. Come down. My time is short, and yours is shorter."

* * *

The heat of the coal-bowl was gentle, which was somehow worse than if it had seared. It held them like a hand. Up close, the dragon's scales were cracked like drought-earth, and between the cracks glowed a dull red, the last embers of a fire that had burned since before the mountain had a name.

Serah explained it while the crew stood in a half-circle, hats in hands like mourners. Brannock listened with his arms crossed and his heart going like a smuggler's oars.

"Before the crown, there was the mountain, and the mountain slept, and its dreaming kept the land green. Then the first king came, and he was afraid — kings are always afraid — and he made a bargain. His line would rule, unbreakable, unquestioned. In exchange, the dragon's fire would be bound to the crown, fed, generation by generation, with souls freely sold. Every charter, every decree, every hanging judge's sentence — all of it runs on that fire. Unmake the founding bargain, and the whole edifice comes down. The court, the hunt, the witch-law. All of it."

"And the cost," the dragon said. Her head lifted, an effort that took visible time, tendons standing out under her throat like bridge cables. "The cost is this. I am nearly spent. What fire remains in me can unmake *one* bargain. One only. Choose it well, thieves, for I will not wake again to choose another."

Silence. Even the mountain seemed to hold its breath.

"One bargain," Ila repeated. "Out of how many?"

"Thousands," Serah said. "Souls sold for favor, for pardon, for a child's fever broken, for a harvest. Thousands of embers in the vault below, each one a person, each one a contract written in my order's hand." Her jaw tightened. "In mine, once."

"So we choose," Sef said. His voice was flat, careful, the voice he used when the cargo was dangerous. "One soul out of thousands. Whose? The queen's? Some lord who sold his brother's? How do we even—" He stopped. Brannock watched the arithmetic finish itself behind his eyes, watched him arrive where they'd all already arrived, and hated the gentleness with which Sef said the next part. "Maren's ember. Quentin's master. That's the one, isn't it? That's why we came down here."

"That's why *he* came down here," Sef said, and nodded at Quentin. "And I don't blame him. Gods know I don't. But one bargain, boy. One. And every soul we leave in that vault stays sold. Stays fueling the thing we're trying to kill." He spread his hands. "Unless—"

"Don't," Brannock said.

"—unless the founding bargain is the one to break. Not Maren's. The first king's. Cut the root and the whole tree dies, and every contract tied to the crown dies with it. Maybe. If the fire works the way she says."

"It works the way I say," the dragon murmured, "and the boy is right. Break the founding bargain and the lesser contracts starve. They are branches of one tree. But understand—" and here her eye swung back to Quentin, and the light in it was terrible and tender at once, "—to unmake the founding bargain, the fire must consume an ember of equal weight. A soul bound to the crown, freely offered. There is exactly one such ember in this mountain that qualifies."

The silence that followed had weight and texture. Brannock felt it settle on his shoulders like wet wool.

"No," he said.

"Brannock," Quentin began.

"*No.*" He heard his own voice crack on it, and didn't care. "You're not — nobody's asking a boy to—"

"I'm asking myself," Quentin said. Quiet. Steady. His hands were shaking, Brannock noticed. His voice wasn't. "That's the difference. Nobody's asking. That's the whole point of it — freely offered. It doesn't work otherwise. It's the one rule even kings couldn't cheat." He looked around at them, at each of them, and Brannock saw him doing what he'd done on the riverbank the day they met: measuring himself against strangers and finding, to his own surprise, that he belonged among them. "You pulled me out of a river and never asked what I was carrying. You hid me when the hunters came through Ashford. Oda taught me knife-work. Sef and Ila argued over who got to teach me cards. You made me family without making me pay for it." His breath caught. "Let me pay for it now. Let it be my choice. Please. Because if it isn't my choice, then I'm just being sold again, and I swore on Maren's cell floor that no one would ever sell me again — not even the people I love."

Oda spoke then. First words in hours. "Then we do it right," she said. "Family sees it done. All the way down. No one leaves him alone with it."

Brannock looked at his crew — his ragged, thieving, loyal crew — and found that his throat wouldn't work. So he did the only thing left to a man with no arguments and no authority: he took off his hat.

Sef followed. Then Ila. Then Oda, and Serah, bareheaded in the dragon's dying light like mourners at a funeral that hadn't happened yet.

Quentin smiled at them, small and crooked, and turned to the vault door.

* * *

The vault was a low round room behind a door of black iron, and it was full of light.

Shelves ran floor to ceiling, and on the shelves, rows upon rows upon rows, stood glass vessels no bigger than plums, each one holding a spark that pulsed slow as a sleeping heartbeat. Amber, green, white, gold. Thousands of them. The room breathed with them. Standing there, Brannock understood suddenly, horribly, that he was looking at a congregation — a congregation of everyone the crown had ever eaten, humming together in the dark, waiting.

Serah read the shelf-marks with a steady voice until she found the row she wanted. "Hollowmere. Here. Maren Ostwick, sold the ninth day of her capture." She lifted a small vessel, and inside it a green-gold ember flared, recognizing — what? Its name? Its own?

And beside it, older, its glass clouded with dust, a second vessel. Smaller. The ember within it burned a color Brannock had no name for.

"Quentin Pemberton," Serah read, "sold by Maren Ostwick, the winter he was six. Price: his life." Her voice finally broke, just slightly. "Sold twice over."

Quentin took both vessels in his hands. He held them a long moment against his chest, next to the grimoire, and Brannock saw his lips move — a word, or a name, or maybe just *yes*.

Then he turned back toward the dragon's chamber, toward the fire that waited to spend itself, and the mountain shuddered overhead as somewhere far above, boots began to ring on stone, and horns began to cry.

"They know we're here," Ila whispered.

"Then we'd best not keep a dying lady waiting," said Brannock, and drew his blade to hold the door.
