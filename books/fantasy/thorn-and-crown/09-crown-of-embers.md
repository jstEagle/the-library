## 9. Crown of Embers

The dragon's chamber was not what Quentin had imagined in the fevered hours of the road. He had pictured a lair — gold and bones, a darkness thick enough to drown in. What he found instead was a cathedral of glass.

The walls of the deep vault were veined with it, great panes of ancient crystal grown into the living rock like frost on a window, and behind every pane burned embers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one a soul the crown had bought or stolen or bargained away across two hundred years of quiet hunger, each one humming faintly against its prison, the way bees hummed in winter, half-asleep, waiting for a spring that had been sold out from under them.

The sound of it filled the chamber. Not silence at all — a chorus, low and endless, grief rendered in vibration until it stopped being sound and became pressure, became weight, settled on the chest like deep water.

And in the center, coiled around a stone basin where the last fire guttered, lay the dragon.

She was smaller than Quentin had expected, and that was the worst thing about her. The stories spoke of dragons as mountains with wings, as storms given scale and teeth. This one had wasted to almost nothing — hide stretched over ribs like the ribs of a wrecked boat, wings folded close as moth-eaten banners, eyes half-lidded and the color of cooling iron. Her breath came slow. With every exhale, the flame in the basin dimmed, and somewhere above them Quentin felt the mountain's heartbeat stutter, felt the whole vast engine of the court beneath falter like a lamp running dry.

"She knows we're here," Serah said quietly. She had come down the vault stairs ahead of him, and now she stood with her hands open at her sides, empty, the way one approached an altar. "She has known since we crossed the third seal."

Quentin's own hands were not empty. In his left, Maren's ember — a coal of pale green-gold light sealed in glass no larger than his thumbnail, warm as a sleeping bird against his palm. In his right, his own. His was a deeper color, amber shot through with veins of something darker, and when he turned it in the ember-light he could see inside it, faintly, a shape like a small boy standing very still in a snowfield.

He had carried both vessels up from the records room while horns cried overhead and boots rang down stairways built for armies. Brannock held the door behind them; Sef and Ila had gone to collapse the lift-shaft; Oda had taken the grimoire and the crew's escape route and stood guard at the vault mouth with a cleaver in one fist and a face carved from oak. And Quentin had walked into the cathedral of souls carrying the only two coins in the world that mattered, and he did not know yet which one he meant to spend.

*You know,* said a voice that was not a voice.

It came from everywhere and nowhere — from the embers, maybe, or through them. The dragon's eyes opened fully now, iron going briefly molten, and her head lifted from the stone with the grinding patience of a glacier deciding to move.

*You know already which you mean to spend. You have known since the records room. You are only waiting for someone to say it aloud so that it becomes real.*

"I came for my master," Quentin said. His voice sounded small under all that humming. "Her name is Maren Ostwick. Her bargain is twenty years old—"

*I know her bargain.* The dragon's exhale stirred his hair, and it smelled of ash and old honey and, beneath it, something clean that made his throat ache — the smell of a world before crowns. *I have kept the ledger of every bargain this court ever wrote. Two hundred years I have burned their debts into my memory because there was nothing else I could do. Do you know what it is to remember ten thousand thefts and be too weak to return a single one?*

The flame in the basin sank lower. Above them, muffled by a hundred yards of rock, something boomed — a door breached, or a wall. Oda's silhouette shifted at the vault mouth.

"Then take hers back," Quentin said. "Unmake her bargain. That's why we came. That's the deal Serah offered you—"

*Serah Vane offered you a rescue,* the dragon said, and there was something almost gentle in the vastness of it, the way a landslide might be gentle compared to the mountain falling. *But you did not walk past three seals and a garrison of hunters to rescue one hedge-witch. Look at your hands, child of Hollowmere.*

He looked.

Both vessels sat in his palms, and between them, unspoken, yawned the arithmetic he had been refusing to do since Serah first read his name from that ledger. One bargain could be unmade. The dragon had said it plainly in the dark of the smugglers' camp: *the fire that remains will buy one truth, and one only.* One soul returned. One debt cancelled. And then the fire would gutter to nothing, and the court's power would go on feeding itself off ten thousand sleeping prisoners for another century, another two hundred years, until some other thief climbed down here with better luck than his.

Unless the founding bargain went. Unless the first debt — the one the crown had written in blood and signed with a king's soul two hundred years ago, the one that taught the mountain how to eat — unless *that* one burned.

Maren's ember pulsed in his left hand, green-gold, alive.

His own sat in his right, and inside it the small boy in the snowfield seemed, tonight, to be looking back at him.

"There has to be another way," he said, and hated how young his voice sounded. "There's always another way. Maren taught me— she used to say magic never gives you a choice, it just shows you which choice you already made. But this isn't magic. This is just—" His hand closed tighter around her ember. "This is just a person choosing."

*Yes,* said the dragon.

Above them the booming came again, closer, and now shouting threaded down through the stone, thin as insects. Sef appeared at the top of the vault stairs, breathing hard, a cut above his eye bleeding freely. "They're through the gallery," he called. "Brannock says five minutes, and he's lying — he means two." Then he saw Quentin's face, and the dragon, and whatever passed between the two of them, and his voice dropped. "Oh," he said. "Oh, lad. No."

"It's not decided."

"It's decided." Sef came down the stairs anyway, slowly, like a man approaching something holy or something dying. "Ila would tell you the same if she weren't holding a rope bridge together with her teeth. You've got two embers and one flame, and you've been doing sums since the records room. We've all watched you do them." He stopped an arm's length away. "So don't insult us by pretending otherwise. Just — let us choose too. That's what family is. It's choosing the same losing bet together."

Quentin laughed, or something near it, wet and cracked. "That's a terrible definition."

"It's Oda's. She'll deny it later."

The chamber shuddered. Dust sifted from seams in the crystal, glittering down through the ember-light like snow falling upward. And the dragon raised her head fully at last, and looked at him, and Quentin understood that she had heard everything — the argument, the joke, the fear underneath both — and had weighed it, and found him ready, and was sorrier than any creature that old should have to be.

*Bring me the founding bargain,* she said. *It lies in the basin's heart, beneath the flame. The oldest debt burns lowest and darkest. You will know it by the cold.*

He stepped to the basin's edge. The remaining fire was barely a handspan tall now, blue-white at its core, and reaching into it was like plunging his arm into a river of winter. He felt around among the coals — real coals, ordinary stone gone white-hot — until his fingers closed on something that was not hot at all but cold, cold as the bottom of a well, a vessel of black glass no bigger than the others and heavier than it had any right to be. Inside it, instead of light, there was a darkness that moved. A king's soul, or what remained after two hundred years of a kingdom drinking from it. The first theft. The pattern-setter. The bargain that had taught a mountain to eat children.

He set it in the basin, in the heart of the guttering flame.

Then he knelt, and pressed Maren's ember gently against the stone beside the basin, where the dragon could reach it without spending anything, and his hands shook as he did it, because taking it out of his pocket felt like setting down the last piece of the only home he'd ever had.

"When it's done," he said, "she wakes up. Whatever's left of her. She gets her life back — the years she sold, the ones she spent raising me, all of it counts. Promise me."

*On my name,* said the dragon, *which is the last thing I own. Ashmarath. Remember it, if remembering survives this night.*

"And mine?" His voice caught. "The other vessel. When the founding bargain burns — does mine burn with it?"

*All bargains written under this crown will unravel,* Ashmarath said. *Yours was written under this crown. Yes, child. Your magic dies tonight. It was never truly yours; it was collateral, and collateral burns with the debt.* A pause, vast and careful. *But hear me. Magic is what was taken from you. It is not what you are. What you are, you carry in your body, and bodies cannot be pawned.*

From the stairs, Sef said quietly, "Quentin."

Quentin looked at the black vessel in the flame. At the green-gold ember waiting by the stone. At the blue-white fire, sinking, sinking, patient as a candle in a windless room.

"Maren," he said aloud, to the ember, though she couldn't hear him, though she was a hundred miles and twenty years away from hearing anything. "You sold me to save me. I get that now. I even forgive it — mostly. But you don't get to be the only one who pays a price for love in this story."

He put his hand flat against the basin's rim, closed his eyes, and thought of bees. Of a valley that forgot. Of a woman's hands steadying a frightened boy's over a candle flame, saying *feel that? that's yours, that's always going to be yours* —

"Burn," Quentin said.

The founding bargain broke with a sound like a bell the size of the sky.

Light did not flash so much as *leave* — the darkness inside the black vessel rushing outward through the flame and up through the crystal veins and out through the roots of the mountain, a tide of returning running backward through two hundred years of theft. The chamber filled with voices. Ten thousand embers flared at once behind their panes, and the humming that had weighed on Quentin's chest since he entered rose, and rose, and changed — grief becoming relief becoming, impossibly, singing.

Glass began to crack. All of it, everywhere, hairline fractures spidering across every pane, and through each fracture the light spilled free and streamed upward, out of the vault, out of the mountain, home to wherever souls go when they are finally, finally released from their paper.

The mountain screamed. Quentin felt it through his knees, through his teeth — the whole vast structure groaning as the power that had held it up for two centuries drained out of its stones. Somewhere far above, the crown's great engines died mid-turn. Somewhere far above, men who had never once questioned where their strength came from felt it go out of them like breath from a struck chest.

Ashmarath rose. For one moment — one impossible moment — the wasted dragon unfurled to her true size, wings spreading wide enough to shadow the whole cathedral of glass, and she was magnificent, and she was laughing, a sound like summer storms, like rivers breaking ice.

Then the last of the flame went into the breaking, and the light took Quentin's right hand with it.

He didn't scream. He wanted to. It was not pain exactly — it was subtraction, something pulled out through his skin thread by thread, every working he had ever done, every secret craft, the gift born in him before memory, all of it streaming away golden into the dark. The small boy in the snowfield dissolved into sparks and was gone, and Quentin felt the place where he had lived go cold and quiet and empty, and he understood that he would feel that quiet for the rest of his life.

Sef caught him before he fell. Behind them, the green-gold ember by the basin flared bright — brighter than anything else in the dying chamber — and then simply wasn't there anymore.

Gone home. Gone to a cell in the upper courts, to a woman waking from twenty years of sleep with tears already on her face and a boy's name in her mouth.

The mountain was coming down around them, dust and thunder and ringing stone, and through the roar Quentin heard Brannock bellowing from the vault mouth — "*Move, move, MOVE*" — and felt Sef haul him upright, and saw Oda's torch waving wild through the settling gloom, and realized distantly that his hands, for the first time since he was six years old, were only hands.

Nothing in them. Nothing behind them. Just a boy, running up out of a collapsing dark, with nothing left to give and everything, somehow, still to lose.

Behind him, in the ruins of the cathedral of glass, the last dragon breathed out once, softly, like a sigh at the end of a long story — and did not breathe in again.

* * *

They came up into daylight that was falling as well as shining.

The mountain's peak was shedding itself in sheets of grey, and the terraces of the hidden court — the marble halls, the soul-forges, the throne room where kings had sat fat and glowing on stolen light for two hundred years — were sliding, folding, burying themselves like a lie being swallowed by the ground that had hosted it. Hunters scattered across the slopes like ants from a kicked nest, dropping swords, tearing off livery, running for the lowlands with the look of men waking from a long and shameful dream.

The company ran with them, downhill, into the trees, Brannock dragging Quentin by the collar when his legs forgot their work, Ila laughing the high unhinged laugh she laughed when the world ended properly. They gained the ridge as the sun set behind them, and turned, all five of them together, to watch the mountain finish its work.

Where the crown had been, there was only a smoking wound in the earth, and drifting up out of it, thin and bright, threads of freed light rising into the evening sky like reversed rain.

Quentin stood among his people and shook, and wept, and felt nothing where his magic had been except a clean, terrible space, like a tooth lost from the jaw.

"We can't stay on the ridge," Oda said at last, practically, because someone had to be practical or they'd all stand there until dawn. "Valley folk will come looking by morning. Whole country will come looking."

"Let them," Brannock said. He was looking at Quentin. So were they all. "Let them look. Let them find us."

Quentin wiped his face with hands that were only hands, and thought of Maren waking in a cell that might already be rubble, and of the long road south to Hollowmere, and of a valley that would forget no more.

"South," he said. "First light. There's someone I have to see."

And below them, far off in the darkening lowlands, the first bell of a free morning began to ring.
