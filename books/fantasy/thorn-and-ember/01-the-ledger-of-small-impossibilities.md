## 1. The Ledger of Small Impossibilities

The trouble with trading in impossible things is that the impossible refuses to stay balanced.

I learned that in my first year at the Vellum Guild, when a widow sold us a door that only opened onto rooms she'd been happy in, and I entered it into the ledger as *one (1) threshold, sentimental*. The ink wouldn't dry. It sat there glistening for three days, and every time I passed the desk it had rearranged itself into a different honest description: *one (1) grief, load-bearing.* I finally carved a bone token to anchor the entry — that's what the tokens are for, anchors, ballast, little weights of the actual to hold down the weightless — and when my knife bit into the sheep knuckle, the dead woman who'd once owned the bone hummed through my fingertips and told me the door had belonged to her mother, and that she'd sold it because she couldn't afford to be happy in any room anymore.

I wrote that in the margin. The ink dried instantly.

That is more or less the whole of my profession, if you want it plainly, which the ledger always does. The Vellum Guild trades in impossible things — relics, thresholds, bottled last breaths, keys to houses that haven't been built yet — and every transaction must be recorded, valued, and verified. Verification is where I come in. Anyone can appraise a flying carpet by whether it flies. But to know whether it's *true* — whether the dead hands woven into its warp actually consented to the weaving — you need someone who can hear them.

You need a carver. You need, specifically, me.

My name is Freya Almeida, and I have kept the guild's ledgers for eleven years, and in eleven years I have made exactly four errors, all of them deliberate, none of which Corvane knows about, and two of which I'd make again.

* * *

The counting house sits at the top of the guildhouse like a skull sitting on a body of warehouses. Below us: the loading docks, the salt-fog off the Graywater, the carts that arrive at strange hours carrying crates that tick or hum or occasionally apologize. Above us: nothing but roof slate and gulls, because even impossible things need somewhere mundane to be counted from. The room smells of tallow, dust, and the faint sweet rot of old bone — which most people find unpleasant and which I find honest. Everything ends up bone eventually. At least bone tells you what it was.

I was at my desk before dawn, as always, working the quarter-turn reconciliation. Forty-one entries. Thirty-eight clean. The tokens for those sat in their felt-lined tray in neat rows, each one a small knuckle or tooth or sliver of rib, each one humming its single note when I touched it — the dead seller's voice, compressed to a chord of assent. *Yes, this was mine. Yes, I sold it freely. Yes, the price was fair.*

Most people think hearing the dead would sound like voices. It doesn't, quite. It sounds like carving. Like the difference between wood and meat under a blade — you feel truth the way you feel grain. A willing sale runs smooth. A coerced one catches, drags, splinters. And a lie — a lie feels like carving something that is already broken inside, hollow chambers where the strength should be. In eleven years I have never needed anyone to tell me they were cheating me. Their own bones do it, secondhand, through my knife.

The thirty-ninth entry was giving me trouble.

*One (1) memory, first snowfall of childhood, seller: anonymous.* Anonymous sellers are permitted — plenty of folk don't want their names attached to selling the warmest thing they own — but the token wouldn't settle. Every time I laid it beside the entry it buzzed like a trapped fly. I picked it up. Fox tooth, worn smooth, cheap carving, someone in a hurry.

And underneath the hum, for the first time in eleven years of this work, the voice in the bone said something that wasn't assent.

It said: *count again.*

I set the token down. I looked at it. Tokens do not talk. Tokens hum. That is the arrangement. The dead give their one note of witness and then they rest, and I have built an entire life on the tidiness of that arrangement — the way other people build lives on prayer, or drink, or the belief that their mothers loved them.

*Count again,* said the fox tooth.

So I counted again. Thirty-nine entries in the book. Forty-one tokens in the tray. I went back through the morning's work and found it: entry twenty-two, *one (1) key to a house not yet built*, matched against a token of whale ivory that did not belong to it. Someone had swapped the tokens. Not stolen — swapped. Two sales, quietly married, so that a false entry could ride into the ledger wearing a true one's clothes.

I sat very still for a moment, listening to the fog press against the windows, feeling the particular loneliness of being the only person in a building who has just noticed something wrong.

Then I got out my knife and began to unmake the lie.

* * *

"You've found it already."

Corvane's voice behind me, and I didn't jump, because jumping is a luxury for people whose nervous systems aren't employed by the Vellum Guild. Guildmaster Edran Corvane stood in the doorway of the counting house with his coat still beaded from the fog below, holding two cups of chicory, which is how he says good morning, since good morning is apparently too many words to spend before noon.

"Found what?" I said, which was stupid, because he was looking directly at the two tokens I'd separated from the herd and quarantined on the blotter like sick lambs.

"The swap," he said. "Marrow-and-Co tried it last turn too, with a reliquary. You caught that one in a day and a half." He set a cup by my elbow. "You caught this one before breakfast. I'd say you're getting faster, but we both know you were never slow."

"I count everything twice," I said. "Once for the guild and once for me."

"And do the two counts ever disagree?"

"Never." This was true, mostly, in the sense that a shipwreck is mostly water.

Corvane smiled. He is not a tall man but he takes up a room the way a keel takes up water — by displacement, steadily, from beneath. He raised me. I should say that plainly, since I am given to plainness in these pages if nowhere else: my mother died when I was nine, and Corvane found me in the guildhouse kitchens whittling buttons out of a soup bone, and instead of scolding me he handed me a better knife and said, *you'll want to learn what you're listening to.* Eleven years later I keep his ledgers and balance his impossibilities and trust him further than I have ever trusted any living person, which is a short distance, granted, but I have spent it all on him.

He leaned over my shoulder now, close enough that I could smell the road-dust on him — he'd been down at the docks since before light, which was unusual; Corvane leaves the dock work to undermasters — and he studied the swapped tokens with an expression I couldn't read, which almost never happens, because I can usually read Corvane the way I read bone.

"Marrow-and-Co," he said again, softly. "They're getting bolder."

"They're getting sloppier. Bolder I could respect."

That earned me a real laugh, short and rough. He tapped the fox tooth once with one finger — and I watched, with a small cold feeling I chose not to examine, to see whether it hummed for him.

It didn't. Of course it didn't. It never does. Only carvers hear them. Only carvers, and whatever else the dead decide to bother, which historically is no one, which is why the guild needs me, which is why I am valuable, which is a chain of reasoning I try not to follow all the way to its end, because its end is a room with one desk in it.

"Re-enter it cleanly," Corvane said, straightening. "Flag Marrow's factor. And Freya—" he paused at the door, chicory steaming in his hand, fog-light making him look briefly older than his sixty years, "—if anything comes through the night dock that seems worth your attention, I'd take it as a kindness if you brought it to me before you brought it to the ledger."

"Nothing comes through the night dock worth my attention," I said. "The night dock receives corpses, smuggled cheese, and once, memorably, a live bear."

"A bear is an impossible thing."

"That bear was entirely possible. That was the problem with it."

He laughed again and left me to my numbers, and I sat in the quiet and did not think about how he had asked that question — *before you brought it to the ledger* — as though he already knew the ledger would soon be faced with something it couldn't hold.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about keeping accounts for impossible things: the books are better company than people.

A ledger doesn't flatter. A ledger doesn't leave. When you write the truth in a ledger, it stays written, and when you write a lie in one, well — sometimes the ink won't dry, and the book itself snitches on you, which is a quality I admire more than loyalty. People say the dead are silent company. They're wrong. The dead are excellent company. They want so little. One note each, one small hum of witness, and in exchange they keep me honest, and I keep them remembered, and neither of us pretends it's friendship, which is why it works.

The living are another matter. The living want things. Corvane wants my discretion, and pays for it in chicory and praise. The factors want my silence. The sellers want absolution, usually, or occasionally just to be told their grandmother's ghost isn't angry about the spoon. And I want —

Well. I'm a ledger-keeper. Let's enter it accurately: *one (1) wanting, unspecified, carrier: F. Almeida.* Even I don't know what column it goes in. Some nights I think it's simply to be believed without having to carve proof first. Some nights I think it's smaller than that. Some nights I think it's to hear one voice that isn't dead asking something of me, though God knows why I'd want that; the dead ask so much less.

At midday I re-balanced the whole quarter-turn and flagged Marrow's factor with a note reading, in full, *We counted.* The guild's notes are famously terse. Terror through brevity; it saves on paper.

In the afternoon a seller came up from the docks himself — rare, that; usually it's factors and crates — a fisherman with a jarred last breath to sell, his grandfather's, caught by a hedge-priest on some island I'd need the atlas for. Last breaths are routine inventory. We pay by volume and vintage. But when I took the jar and held it to the window-light, the breath moved against the glass in a way that breaths don't, and the old man watched me watching it and said, hoarsely, "You can see it too, then."

"It's a strong sample," I said, which was a lie, and my own knife, lying on the desk between us, hummed low with disapproval at me, which was embarrassing.

"He talked to it," the fisherman said. "At the end. Talked to his own last breath, like it could carry the words somewhere. Do they — " and here he stopped, and looked at his hands, big ruined net-hands, and I understood that he was going to ask me whether the dead could hear him, and that he could not bear either answer.

"They carry what they're given," I said, which was true, and kind enough, and cost me nothing, and he paid for that kindness by accepting our price without haggling, and left, and the knife hummed at me again, and I wrote the entry, and the afternoon wore on.

That is the shape of a good day, in the counting house. Small transactions. Clean columns. One mercy, administered sideways, that doesn't appear anywhere in the books.

* * *

It was nearly dark when I finished the reconciliation and carried the ledger down to the vault — ledgers sleep in the vault, under lock, because a book that records impossible things becomes one if left unsupervised — and coming back up through the warehouse levels, I passed the long gallery of shelved relics, and did what I always do, which is walk it slowly and listen.

Eleven years ago this gallery terrified me. Thousands of objects, each humming its dead owner's single note, a choir of witnesses standing at attention in the salt-dark. Now it's just — the office. The murmur of colleagues. The whalebone cradle that hums a lullaby in a language extinct two hundred years. The wedding ring that will not stop humming its one note, which after forty years in inventory we have privately named Faithful, and which I have twice caught myself answering aloud, *yes, yes, she meant it, go to sleep.*

I was halfway down the gallery, fingers trailing the shelf-edge the way you trail a banister, when the bells rang for ninth hour, and the whole building shifted around me into its nighttime posture — lamps dimmed, doors barred, the dock crew stamping fog off their boots two floors down. Ordinary sounds. Home sounds, if I'm honest, which the ledger requires me to be: the Vellum Guild is the only home I remember, and I love it the way you love a well-run account, without illusion and without exit.

I was almost at the stairs when the night dock bell rang.

One pull. Then a second, ragged, dragging — the way a bell rings when someone is leaning on the rope rather than pulling it. Then nothing.

The dock crew had gone up to the mess. The undermaster's office was dark. I stood at the head of the stairs with my knife on my hip and my ledger-calluses catching the lamplight, and I thought, with the clarity I usually reserve for fraudulent entries: *nobody else heard it.*

Below me, through the floorboards, came the wet slap of footsteps — uneven ones, a stagger, someone crossing the dock planks toward the door — and then a knocking, weak and doubling over itself, and then a sound I knew from the fisherman's jarred breath and from every humming token in the tray above: the sound of something running out.

I should have gone for the undermaster. I should have rung for the watch, or woken Corvane, or done any of the ten prudent things available to a woman whose entire value lies in her discretion.

Instead I lifted the lamp, and went down toward the door, alone, with my knife loose in its sheath — because in eleven years, the dead have asked me for one note each, no more, and something out in the fog was asking for everything, and I found, entering it honestly into myself, that after eleven years of listening to the past, I could not refuse the present.

The bolt was cold under my hand. Beyond it, the fog stood against the door like something waiting to be let in.

I opened it.
