## 2. A Stranger at the Loading Dock

The dead are punctual. The living, in my experience, arrive bleeding.

I was balancing the third ledger of the evening — the *Ledger of Small Impossibilities*, which sounds like poetry and is actually arithmetic performed on items that should not exist — when the dock bell rang wrong. Not the double toll of a sanctioned delivery, not the single flat note that meant a guild officer wanted something carried somewhere quietly. This was a fist against iron, arrhythmic, desperate.

I set down my stylus. I did this reluctantly, because I had just reconciled forty vials of bottled grief against a widow's account in Harrowmere, and if you have never tried to make numbers agree about bottled grief, I don't recommend starting now. Grief is notoriously bad at sums. It always comes up short or overflows.

*Stay*, said the ledger, in the way ledgers do, which is to say silently but with enormous moral pressure.

I went anyway. That is the whole tragedy of my life in miniature, if you're taking notes: Freya Almeida, who trusts paper more than people, abandoning her ledger because someone knocked.

* * *

The night dock sits below the guildhall like a mouth that forgot how to smile. Salt fog comes off the harbor thick enough to chew, and the lamps burn orange halos with no centers, and everything smells of brine and tallow and the particular cold-stone patience of buildings older than the kingdom they serve. I took a lamp from its hook and descended the wet stairs, telling myself it was probably Ossler with a late shipment of mermaid scales that were definitely fish scales, we all agreed never to discuss it further.

It was not Ossler.

He came out of the fog at a stagger, one hand pressed to his side where his coat had gone black and shining with blood, the other hand thrust before him as though he were feeling for a door in the air. Young, maybe. Hard to tell. Blood ages people, and so does running. His boots had been good once; now they were ruined, soaked through, one sole flapping loose like a tongue.

"Guild," he rasped. "You're — the bone-carver. Almeida."

"I'm the accountant," I said, which was true and also the safest thing I've ever said to anyone.

"You carve." He reached me at the foot of the stairs and grabbed my sleeve with fingers that left red prints on the wool. "They said — Corvane's carver. The one who *hears*."

There it is. The sentence that ends conversations and starts catastrophes. I should have denied it. I should have said *you want the receiving clerk, third door, mind the step.* Instead I stood there with my lamp raised and let him see my face do whatever it does when someone names the thing I pretend isn't my whole life.

"They told me to find you specifically," he said. "Not Corvane. You."

That should have warned me. Everything about him should have warned me: the blood, the hour, the fact that he knew my name, the way he kept glancing back at the fog as though it might be following him. But there is a loneliness so complete that even catastrophe looks like company, and I had been living inside mine for eleven years, listening to nothing but the dead murmur through bone while the living passed me by with their warm hands and their closed mouths.

"What do you have?" I asked.

He laughed. It turned into a cough, and the cough brought up something dark that hit the stones between us. "What do I have," he repeated, delighted. "Right answer. Wrong question, but right answer." And he pressed into my free hand a rolled tube of oiled leather, still warm from being carried against a body that was rapidly losing the ability to be warm.

It was heavier than a map has any right to be. Maps weigh nothing; they're promises made by ink. This felt like a promise made by stone.

"Thirteen turns," he whispered.

Then he said my name again — *Almeida* — as if confirming delivery, as if some part of him needed the receipt signed. And then his knees went, and I caught what I could, which was not enough, and he slid down the wall to sit in the puddle of himself, and I knelt there holding a lamp and a map and a stranger's wrist while his pulse counted down under my fingers like a ledger closing.

I am not a healer. I am an accountant of impossible things. The only mercy I know how to perform is accurate bookkeeping, and so I sat with him while the entries ran out, and I noted the time — two hours past midnight, the fourth of the Ember moon — because someone ought to record it, and I was the only one there.

His last breath smelled of salt and horse and fear. The fog took it politely.

* * *

Here is what most people misunderstand about hearing the dead: it is not a gift. It is a job nobody applied for. Bone remembers — that's the whole secret of my trade, the reason the Vellum Guild pays me in coin instead of questions — and when I carve bone, the memory in it loosens and speaks. A ring finger hums with its owner's last lie. A knucklebone recites a childhood. It is never useful and never quiet and never, ever *alive*. The dead speak the way water speaks through pipes: something passing through, going elsewhere, indifferent to the listener.

So when the courier died at my feet and I felt for the pulse out of habit rather than hope, and found none, I expected nothing from him. Dead men on docks are cargo now. Cargo goes in the canal boat at dawn.

Except his lips moved.

No sound. Just the shape of words, the way a dying man's mouth keeps working after the engine stops. *Take it north,* the mouth said. *Don't show Corvane. Don't show anyone. Thirteen turns.*

I am not easily frightened. You cannot be, in my profession, or the humming would drive you mad within a month. But a corpse mouthing instructions is a different order of horror than a knucklebone murmuring about a lost dog, and I sat back hard on the wet stones and the lamp guttered and the fog pressed in like something reading over my shoulder.

"You're dead," I informed him. This is another occupational hazard: I talk to the dead as though they can hear me, which they cannot, which has never stopped me.

His mouth kept moving. *North. The thirteenth turn. She'll understand.*

"She?"

But the lips had stilled, and whatever animus had borrowed them was gone, and I was alone on the dock with a corpse, a lamp, a map, and the distinct sensation of having been handed something by a man who died specifically so that it could not be taken back from him.

I should have put the map in the dead-letter vault. That is procedure. Unclaimed deliveries go to the vault, the vault goes to Corvane, Corvane decides, and Freya balances her ledgers and sleeps the dreamless sleep of the professionally discreet.

I unrolled it instead. Right there, kneeling beside a cooling body, in the fog, against every rule I'd built my careful life upon.

* * *

It was a chart. That was my first thought, and my second, and then I looked closer and it stopped being anything so simple.

A map of the capital, drawn in ink so fine it might have been laid down with a needle — streets, walls, the palace crown sitting on its hill above the river like a stone diadem. Around the palace, concentric rings. Twelve of them, each labeled in a cramped, elegant hand: *First Turn. Second Turn. Third Turn.* Each ring slightly tighter than the last, each drawn in progressively darker ink, until the twelfth circle around the palace was nearly black.

And in the center, where the palace itself should have been drawn in detail, there was only a small, precise symbol: a crown, rendered in a single unbroken line.

The line was moving.

I want to be clear about this, because later everyone asked me how I could be sure, whether the fog wasn't playing tricks, whether grief and bloodloss hadn't addled me. The ink line of that little crown was crawling. Slowly, patiently, the way ivy climbs a wall, the unbroken stroke was extending itself outward, millimeter by millimeter, drawing the beginning of a thirteenth ring.

I dropped it. Of course I dropped it. Any sane person drops a self-drawing map. It landed face-up in the courier's blood, and the ink drank the blood the way dry ground drinks rain, and the crawling line paused — and then resumed, faster.

"No," I said, to a map. My dignity was already beyond saving; I committed fully.

And my carving knife answered.

Now. Let me explain the knife, because it matters. It's the first tool I ever carved with, whalebone handle worn to the shape of my grip, blade of grey steel that Corvane gave me when I was nine years old and crying in his workshop because the dead wouldn't stop talking. It has never spoken. Tools don't speak. Bone speaks; steel is silent; that is the natural law of my life, as fixed as tides.

But lying there on the stones between the corpse and the map, the knife hummed. Not the bone-hum I know, that distant underwater murmur. This was a voice. Clear, dry, tired, and unmistakably, appallingly *alive* in its cadence — a living voice coming out of a dead woman's instrument.

"You dropped me," said the knife, in a woman's voice.

I did the reasonable thing. I picked up my lamp, stood, and walked three steps toward the stairs.

"The courier is dead," the voice continued, patient as tide, "and the map is drinking his blood, and every minute you stand there deciding whether you've gone mad, the thirteenth ring gets closer to finished. So perhaps pick me up, Almeida, and we can argue about your sanity on the road."

I turned around. The knife lay exactly where it had fallen. The map lay in its widening pool. The corpse sat slumped against the wall with his ruined boots and his open eyes, and the fog rolled past all of us, indifferent as arithmetic.

"Who are you?" I asked the knife.

"Maren Ostreth. Cartographer. Formerly of the Guild of Wayfarers, latterly of nowhere, currently of this knife, which I chose because you were the only carver in this city who might listen." A pause, and when she spoke again there was something underneath the dryness, something that sounded almost like pity. "You heard him die and you stayed. Good. That matters more than you think. Now — the map. Pick it up carefully. Don't touch the wet ink."

"Why not?"

"Because the thirteenth turn is a binding, child, and binding ink binds. Ask me what it binds."

I crouched. I picked up the knife first — it was warm, warmer than steel has any business being, warm like a held hand — and then, using the blade, I lifted the corner of the map and turned it away from the blood, and the crawling line of the thirteenth ring shivered and slowed.

"Ask me what it binds," Maren said again.

"I don't want to ask."

"Nobody ever wants to ask. That's how the Vellum Guild stays in business." The voice softened by a degree I didn't trust. "Ask anyway, Almeida. You're the one holding the knife."

The fog breathed. Somewhere across the harbor a bell counted the half-hour, and I realized I had been kneeling in a dead man's blood for longer than any discreet accountant should, and that the guildhall above me was full of sleeping colleagues who would wake soon, and that Corvane — who noticed everything, who had taught me that noticing everything was a form of love — would notice this too.

"What does it bind?" I asked.

"A king," said the dead cartographer through my knife. "Or rather, it binds a thing *into* a king, turn by turn, ring by ring. Twelve turns done. One remaining. On the thirteenth moon-turn of Aldric's reign, the crown finishes its work, and the man is gone, and what's left wearing his face will rule this kingdom until the bones wear out." A pause. "Your bones, incidentally, are going to matter. But that conversation can wait until you've stopped shaking."

"I'm not shaking."

"You're shaking. It's fine. Shake and walk. Canalside stairs, third landing, the boat with the cracked blue lantern — the courier paid for it before he paid with everything else. We have four turns of the moon, a long road, and a great deal I need to tell you about your mother."

The word went through me like the knife going through bone. My mother, dead eleven years, whose hands I inherited without ever inheriting her, whose workshop I never saw, whose silence Corvane always changed the subject away from with such gentle, practiced grace.

"You knew her," I said. It wasn't a question, and my voice didn't sound like mine.

"I drew maps with her," said Maren. "Pick up the body's boots' laces — no, never mind, leave him, the guild will find him, and finding him is part of what buys you time. Take the map. Take me. Walk."

I looked at the courier one last time. His face had relaxed into something almost peaceful, the look of a man whose errand is done, and I thought about the ledgers upstairs waiting for my stylus, forty vials of grief and a widow's account, my whole tidy world of balanced columns where nothing bleeds and nothing speaks unless carved.

Then I wrapped the map in oilcloth, tucked it inside my coat against my heart like a second, colder heartbeat, and picked up my knife.

"Third landing," I said.

"Third landing," Maren agreed. "And Almeida — walk quickly. Corvane's window overlooks the dock. It has overlooked the dock for thirty years. And I promise you the light in it just came on."

I looked up. High above, in the guildhall's dark face, one window had bloomed amber.

Someone was awake. Someone had seen.
