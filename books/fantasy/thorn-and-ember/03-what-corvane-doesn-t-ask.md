## 3. What Corvane Doesn't Ask

The map spent the night in the flour jar.

Not because I thought the guild's kitchen was safe — nothing in this building is safe, that is rather the point of a guild that trades in impossible things — but because Corvane never goes into the kitchen. He says the smell of bread reminds him of his mother, and he has never once, in twenty years of knowing him, said anything else about his mother. A man who gives you exactly one fact about himself and then guards it like a vault door teaches you to do the same with your own.

So: flour jar, third shelf, wrapped in oilcloth, beneath a layer of rye that I had no intention of baking into anything. The knife went back on my workbench where it belonged, lying among the chisels and burnishers like it had never spoken to me. Which was a lie, of course. Everything in my life was a lie now, arranged neatly on shelves, labeled in my own careful hand.

I want to say I slept. What I actually did was lie in my narrow bed above the counting room listening to the guild settle around me — the timbers ticking as they cooled, the river slapping at the pilings below, and underneath all of it, faint as a pulse through a wall, the hum of the ledger tokens in their drawers. Two hundred and eleven voices of the dead, each one bound to a little carved bone disc, each one waiting for an audit.

I had always found that sound comforting. That night it sounded like a crowd pressing its face against a window.

* * *

Morning came gray off the water, and with it came Corvane.

He arrived at my workbench at the hour he always did — mid-morning, after the dock crews had finished unloading whatever the tide had brought us, before the afternoon appointments with sellers who preferred their transactions unwitnessed. He carried two cups of chicory, which he set down without asking whether I wanted one, because he knew I did, because he had raised me, because for twenty years this small ritual had been the truest thing in my life.

"You look tired," he said.

"I balanced the Vessin account until late."

"Mm." He drank his chicory and looked out the window at the fog coming off the harbor. "And how is the Vessin account?"

"Settled."

"Good." Another sip. "You know, Freya, when you lie to me, you always reach for accounts. It's very tidy of you. Very professional." He smiled at me over the rim of his cup. "I find it charming."

My hands, which had been smoothing a blank token of elk antler, went still.

"What would you like me to say instead?"

"The truth, ideally. Though I've always suspected the truth is your second language." He set the cup down and turned to face me fully, and there it was — the expression I had catalogued across two decades of ledgers and death-voices and quiet mornings exactly like this one. Not anger. Not suspicion. Something worse. Patience. The patience of a man watching a ledger balance itself, line by line, toward a figure he already knows.

"A courier died on our dock last night," he said mildly. "Stabbed twice through the chest, dragged himself forty feet, and pressed something into my bone-carver's hands before he bled out onto the good planks. The watch hasn't been notified. The body hasn't been moved. Do you know why?"

"No," I said, which was true.

"Because I haven't decided what to do about it yet." He picked up the blank antler token from my bench, turned it over in his long fingers. His hands were beautiful hands; everyone said so; I had learned my own craft trying to imitate them. "I've been sitting in my office since dawn, listening to the fog, thinking. And what I keep coming back to is this: you didn't call anyone. You didn't come to me. You took whatever he gave you, and you hid it, and then you sat down at this bench and pretended to work." He turned the token over again. "Which tells me either you don't trust me anymore, or you're protecting me. And I confess I've been unable to decide which possibility wounds me more."

The knife lay six inches from his hand. I thought about Maren's voice moving through it last night, dry as old vellum: *Thirteen turns. She must see the map. She must—*

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said.

Corvane laughed. It was a real laugh, warm, and it hurt worse than anger would have.

"There she is," he said. "There's my Freya. You know, when you were nine years old and I caught you carving tokens from the scrap bin — selling them, if you please, to the dock boys, forging my mark and everything — you stood there with your chin up and told me the scrap bin was abandoned property. Abandoned property. Nine years old." He shook his head fondly. "I didn't punish you. Do you remember why?"

"Because the forgery was good."

"Because the forgery was *very* good." He set the token down. "But also because I saw what you could hear in them. The dead speak to maybe one carver in a generation, Freya. One. And the guild has been waiting for you since before you were born — not me, the guild, the whole rotten institution — waiting for a pair of hands like yours. Your mother's hands, if it comes to that."

The word landed in my chest like a dropped coin.

"My mother," I repeated carefully, "died when I was four."

"Yes," Corvane said. "She did." And then he simply looked at me, and let the silence do its work, the way he always did in negotiations — the silence that said *I could tell you more, and I might, depending entirely on what you tell me.*

Twenty years. Twenty years of chicory and ledgers and his hand on my shoulder when the dead got too loud, and in all that time he had never once mentioned my mother's hands. Now he set the phrase down between us like a token on a table, face up, and waited to see if I'd pick it up.

That was when the first crack went through — not loud, not dramatic, just a fine hairline running through everything I believed about the man who raised me. Because here is what I understood, standing there with chicory going cold and my heart doing something arrhythmic against my ribs:

Corvane was not asking what the courier gave me.

A man who genuinely didn't know would ask. A man who suspected would press. But Corvane had built an empire on knowing precisely what people carried before they knew it themselves, and he was circling the question the way he circled every deal — letting the other party reveal the shape of the thing by what they refused to name. He didn't need to ask. He already knew a courier had come. He already knew the courier had carried something worth killing for. He was measuring, instead, what *I* would do with it. Whether I'd bring it to him. Whether I'd lie. Whether I was still, after everything, his.

Or — and this thought arrived cold and unwelcome, sliding under the door of my mind like fog under a sill — whether I needed handling.

"Corvane," I said, and my voice came out steadier than I deserved, "if someone brought something dangerous into this guild, what would you do?"

"Depends on the thing."

"Say it was a map."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Small. Fast. But I have spent my life reading faces the way other people read weather, and I caught it — the fractional tightening, the half-second where the warmth drained out and something older and colder looked through.

"A map," he said pleasantly. "Of where? The Vessin salt flats? There's money in salt these days."

"Say it was a map of something impossible."

"Ah." He picked up both cups, crossed to the basin, and poured mine out without ceremony. "Then I'd say the person holding it should bring it to me. Quickly. Before other people started looking for it. Because other people *will* look, Freya. They're already looking. The man on our dock wasn't killed by accident, and whoever killed him knows exactly what he carried, and they will follow the blood to the door." He turned back, drying his hands on his apron, and smiled the smile he used on sellers who thought they had leverage. "And I would tell that person — that foolish, stubborn, beloved person — that the guild protects its own. That has always been true. Whatever you're carrying, whatever you've heard, whatever you think you know about—" he paused, delicately, "—*turns*, or moons, or kings — bring it to me, and I will make it safe. As I made *you* safe."

The pause before *kings* lasted perhaps half a heartbeat. Half a heartbeat too long to be innocent. Half a heartbeat too short to be accidental.

He knew. He knew about the thirteenth turn. Maybe not the details, maybe not what the map showed, but he knew the shape of it, the way you know the shape of a body under a sheet. And he had come to my bench with chicory and childhood stories and his beautiful patient smile to see whether I'd hand it over willingly — because a thing given freely binds tighter than a thing taken.

I am a bone-carver. I know about bindings. You carve the vessel first, and then you wait for the spirit to trust you enough to climb inside.

"I'll think about it," I said.

Corvane studied me for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, as though I'd passed some examination whose terms I hadn't known.

"Do that," he said. "Take the day. Balance whatever needs balancing." At the door he stopped, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "Freya. Whatever you decide — decide knowing that I love you. That has never been a lie. In a life full of necessary fictions, it is the one thing I have kept clean."

And he left, and his footsteps went down the stairs, unhurried, even, a man with nowhere to be and all the time in the world to get there.

* * *

I did not go to the flour jar.

I wanted to — my whole body wanted to, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — but I had learned something in the last ten minutes, and the lesson was this: Corvane watches what I touch. So instead I sat at my bench and worked, properly worked, taking a commission token from the drawer — old Widow Serra, deceased eleven years, selling her late husband's luck, a genuine article, faintly warm to the touch — and I carved her voice clean and clear while my mind ran elsewhere.

Because here was the arithmetic I couldn't escape. The courier had been stabbed. Not robbed — stabbed, precisely, twice through the chest, by someone who knew what he carried and wanted him dead before delivery. And yet he'd crawled those forty feet anyway, dying, to put the map in *my* hands specifically. Not Corvane's. Mine. Maren's voice had come through *my* knife, called to *my* craft. And Corvane — who missed nothing, who had built the Vellum Guild's entire reputation on missing nothing — had somehow failed to notice a dying man on his own dock until morning?

No. He hadn't failed to notice. He'd noticed, and he'd waited, and he'd watched to see what I would do. Which meant the killer and Corvane were either the same interest or competing ones, and I genuinely could not tell which possibility frightened me more.

And underneath all of it, quieter and worse: *your mother's hands.* He had said it like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known my body contained. My mother, dead when I was four, whom Corvane had never discussed, whose tools I inherited and whose technique lived in my fingers without my permission — my mother had *hands* that mattered to Corvane, mattered enough to deploy at the exact moment he needed leverage.

What had she carved?

I put the thought down carefully, the way you put down something hot.

By late afternoon the fog had thickened until the windows went white, and the guild grew quiet around me, and I heard — distantly, through floors and walls — the sound I had been dreading all day: boots on the dock stairs. More than one pair. Moving with the particular unhurried rhythm of men who are paid regardless of what they find.

I closed Widow Serra's token in my fist, felt her dead husband's luck go warm against my palm, and made the only decision available to a woman who had just discovered that home was a trap with excellent chicory.

The flour jar was on my way to the back stair. I broke it against the floor, took the oilcloth bundle, and left the rye for whoever swept up.

Behind me, in the drawers of my bench, two hundred and eleven voices of the dead began, very softly, to sing.
