## 5. Flight from the Vellum Guild

There is a particular sound a guild house makes at three in the morning when it has decided to betray you.

Most people would call it nothing. A settling of beams. The tide knocking politely at the pilings beneath the loading dock. But I had spent eleven years listening to that building breathe, and I knew the difference between its ordinary insomnia and the other kind — the held-breath kind, the kind where every door on the upper floors closes very softly, one after another, like a row of dominoes falling in reverse.

I was in the ledger room when I heard it, third floor, surrounded by eleven years of my own careful handwriting. The lamp guttered. Outside, the salt-fog had come up off the harbor thick as wool, which meant the night watch would be blind and the dock gates would be chained early.

Which meant someone had chosen tonight deliberately.

"Thirteen turns," I said to the empty room, because saying things aloud to empty rooms is what happens to a woman who talks to dead ones for a living. "You could have picked a fogless night, Maren."

The knife on the desk beside me gave a faint, sardonic hum. Dead cartographers are not famous for their sympathy.

Let me explain what I did next, because I want it on the record that it was strategy and not sentiment. Mostly strategy.

Eleven years of ledgers sat on those shelves. Every impossible thing the Vellum Guild had ever bought or sold, entered in my hand: the bottled griefs, the left-handed shadows, the saint's knuckle that whispered stock tips, the debt a fisherman's widow sold against her own future tears. Corvane called me his discreet right hand. What he meant was that I wrote down everything and asked about nothing, and that the asking-nothing had become so thorough it had eaten the rest of me from the inside out.

I took the ledgers off the shelves — all of them, arms straining, three trips — and I stacked them in the hearth with the efficiency of a woman who has balanced accounts her whole life and knows exactly what an asset is worth when it becomes a liability.

Then I burned them.

Not for warmth. Not even, entirely, for spite. Burning the ledgers meant smoke, and smoke meant the enforcers would come to the third floor first, because whatever else the Vellum Guild was, it was a business, and businesses do not tolerate unrecorded transactions. Fire is the loudest argument a ledger can make. By the time they climbed the stairs and found their history curling into ash, I intended to be several fog-banks away with a map that moved and a knife that talked.

That was the plan. I want to be clear that the plan was good.

It's just that no plan survives contact with the smell of your own life burning.

* * *

Paper burns faster than you'd think, when the paper is vellum and the vellum has been treated with guild compounds to resist damp, rot, and honest questions. It went up green and eager, flames the color of old copper, and the room filled with a smell like hot metal and church incense, and I stood there feeding pages into the fire with both hands while my eyes stung and something in my chest did a thing I refused to name.

Here is what nobody tells you about betrayal: it doesn't feel like anger first. It feels like inventory. You stand there in the smoke doing arithmetic. Eleven years. Four thousand entries. One mentor who taught me to hold a graver the way you hold a bird — firmly enough that it can't escape, gently enough that it doesn't know it's caught. One man who found me at fourteen, orphaned, half-starved, carving tokens off corpse-bones in a paupers' yard because the voices were the only company I had, and who said, *you waste yourself, girl; come work where the dead are properly catalogued.*

He had fed me. He had trained me. He had praised my discretion in front of the full guild court as though discretion were a virtue and not a slow way of disappearing.

And he had known. He had stood in this very doorway two nights ago, asking after my health with that terrible gentleness — *you seem tired, child; is something weighing on you?* — and I had thought, how kind, he notices. When what he was actually doing was checking whether the courier's cargo had reached me before the courier stopped moving.

The last ledger went in. My hand hesitated over it — Volume Nine, the year of my apprenticeship, the first entries in a hand still learning its letters, back when I pressed too hard and tore the vellum and Corvane laughed and showed me how to let the knife do the thinking. The fire took it anyway. It always does. That's rather fire's whole position on the matter.

"You're stalling," said the knife.

Maren's voice came through the blade the way it always did — not sound exactly, more a pressure behind my teeth, a dead woman's diction living in my jaw. She had been dead forty years and still managed to sound impatient with me, which I suppose is what mastery looks like.

"I'm grieving," I said. "It's a process."

"Grieving is a process. This is procrastination with extra smoke." A pause, the cartographic equivalent of tapping a foot. "Third floor window, north side. The enforcers split at the stairwell — four up, two around to the dock. Your Guildmaster does love symmetry."

"How do you—"

"I drew maps of this building for thirty years, girl. I know where its doors are better than it does." Another pause, softer. "And I watched men burn my charts once. In Vell Hall, when the guild decided my coastlines were treasonous. So I know what you're looking at right now, and I'm sorry, but sorry doesn't outrun anyone. Move."

I moved.

* * *

The north window opened onto the roof of the counting-house annex, which opened onto a drainpipe, which opened onto an alley that smelled of fish heads and old rope, which is to say it smelled like home, and I hated how much that hurt.

The fog swallowed me whole. That was the one mercy of the night — the same fog that blinded the watch blinded everyone, and I went through it low and quick, hood up, knife inside my coat against my ribs where it hummed like a second heartbeat. The map rode in the oilskin tube across my back, and I could feel it through the canvas, faintly warm, faintly restless, like a sleeping animal dreaming of running.

Behind me, above me, the guild house began to shout.

They found the fire fast — of course they did; you don't run a trade in impossible things without excellent fire response — and the windows of the third floor bloomed orange, and silhouettes crossed the light carrying buckets and shouting words I couldn't make out and didn't need to. Somewhere in that noise was Corvane's voice. I knew it the way you know a bell. He wasn't shouting about the ledgers.

He was shouting my name.

*Freya.* Just that. Once, pitched to carry, the way he pitched instructions when he wanted them obeyed without discussion. Not angry. Worse than angry. Disappointed-teacher angry, we-will-discuss-this-later angry, and some traitor part of me — the part that was fourteen years old and hungry and grateful — turned toward it like a plant toward a window.

I made myself keep walking.

"Good girl," said the knife, quietly, and I chose to forgive the condescension on account of the circumstances.

The docks at Saltrow were a maze by design. The guild had built them that way generations ago — crooked piers, warehouses stacked like bad teeth, walkways that dead-ended into water or into other walkways, so that any smuggler, thief, or tax assessor foolish enough to come looking would get lost in the geometry. I had grown up in that maze. I had drawn parts of it myself, in the margins of ledgers, for my own amusement. And now I fled through it knowing — with the sick certainty of a woman reading her own handwriting on a death warrant — that the people hunting me had grown up in it too.

They were colleagues. That was the word I kept snagging on. Colleagues. Hesper from acquisitions, who had taught me dice games during the slow winter months. The Brandt twins, who catalogued relics and argued about whose turn it was to clean the reliquary tanks. Ondine—

No. Not Ondine. Ondine had left years ago, gone north on guild business and never written, and I was not going to think about why my mind produced her name now, in the fog, like a warning.

"Left," said the knife. "At the tar barrels. Then down the ladder ramp, along the water line. They've posted two at the pier gate, but they're watching the gate, not under it."

"There's no *under* the pier gate."

"There is if you're willing to get wet. Are you willing to get wet?"

I looked back the way I'd come. Lantern-light was swinging through the fog in disciplined pairs, closing on the maze mouth. Two figures detached from the nearest pair and started down the pier road at a jog — and even half-glimpsed through the murk, I knew the stride. Hesper. Of course it was Hesper. Hesper ran everywhere; she'd been late to her own induction ceremony.

"Hesper's coming," I said, and heard my own voice crack on the name, and hated that too.

"Mm," said Maren. "Grieve later. Wet now."

I went over the rail.

* * *

The harbor in autumn is not cold. Cold implies a temperature you might recover from. The harbor in autumn is a personal insult delivered directly to the marrow, and I surfaced gasping under the pier with my lungs full of knives and my dignity somewhere on the surface above, receding.

But the underside of the pier gate was exactly what Maren promised — a crawl-space between the pilings where the tide had rotted out a section of planking, and beyond it the open water of the channel, black and breathing. I hauled myself along the barnacled crossbeams, teeth chattering hard enough to bite through my own cheek, and listened to the boots above me stop at the gate.

"—saw her go over, I'm telling you—"

"Over where? There's nothing over there but the channel."

"She wouldn't swim it. Not in this fog. Not alone."

A pause. Then Hesper's voice, lower, and I will spend the rest of my life hearing it: "Corvane says she's not to be hurt. Says she's confused. Says bring her home."

Home. The word dropped through the planking like a coin through a grate, and I pressed my forehead against wet wood and let myself, for exactly five seconds, feel all of it. The burning ledgers. The praise that had been surveillance. The man who had saved me and shaped me and — if Maren was right, and the bones said he was — spent eleven years sharpening me toward a purpose I had never agreed to. Grief for the only home I had ever known, going down in me like ballast.

Then the five seconds ended, because that's what I'd given them, and I pushed off from the pilings into the channel.

Swimming the Saltrow channel in fog is the sort of thing you only do once, mostly because afterward you have earned the wisdom not to repeat it. The current wanted me for the open sea. The cold wanted me for the bottom. The map-tube on my back wanted to be a sail. I swam by dead reckoning — ha — counting strokes, angling for the far shore where the fishing quarter began and the guild's writ got thin, and somewhere around stroke two hundred the knife started humming a rhythm against my ribs, steady as a coxswain, and I matched my strokes to it and did not think about anything at all except the next stroke, and the next, and the next.

I came out on the shingle below the fish-curing sheds looking, I imagine, like something the tide had rejected on quality grounds. I lay there a moment, cheek against stones, breathing fog, alive.

Alive. Which was, I reflected, the entire extent of my assets. One knife. One map. One set of soaked clothes. No guild, no mentor, no ledgers, no home. Liabilities: a guild of trained relic-runners who knew my face, my habits, my gait, and my fondness for high ground, plus a binding ritual scheduled to complete in — I did the arithmetic, and my stomach did something worse than the cold had managed — nine days.

Nine moon-turns had passed since the crown settled onto Aldric's head. Four remained. On the thirteenth, whatever wore the king finished wearing him.

"Congratulations," I told the fog. "You've ruined your life. How does it feel?"

"Wet," said the knife.

I laughed. It came out cracked and half-drowned and slightly unhinged, and it was the first honest sound I'd made in eleven years, and I lay on the shingle and let it shake through me until it turned into something else, something with salt in it that wasn't seawater, and I let that happen too, briefly, privately, the way you balance a private account.

Then I got up.

* * *

The fishing quarter received me the way it receives everything: without enthusiasm, but without questions either. Fisherfolk know better than to ask drowned women where they've been. I traded a bone token — plain work, no voices in it, just craft — for dry clothes and a place by a curing-fire, and I sat there steaming and turning the map-tube over in my hands while the old woman who owned the shed pretended not to watch me.

North, then. The map said north, and Maren said north, and the bones in my kit — I still carried a small roll of working bone, habit older than loyalty — had been whispering north since the courier died. North past the toll roads, past the guild's easy reach, toward a capital I'd never seen and a king I'd never met and a truth I already wished I could unhear: that the crown was a vessel, and vessels are made by carvers, and somewhere in my mother's hands — the hands that became my hands, the training that lived in my grip before Corvane ever found me — the first cut of that binding had been made.

My mother. Dead twelve years. Whose workshop I had never seen, whose tools I had never touched, whose silence Corvane had never once explained to my satisfaction.

"Girl," said the curing-woman suddenly, not looking up from her knives. "Whatever's chasing you — it comes through here, you'll want to be gone before dawn. Fog lifts, roads show footprints."

I looked at her. She looked at her knives.

"Thank you," I said.

"Don't thank me. Thank the fog." She paused. "And eat something. Dead weight slows you down worse than live weight."

So I ate. Dried fish and hard bread, mechanically, watching the door. And outside, far off across the water, the guild house still glowed against the fog like a coal under ash — my whole history burning down to a stain — and I understood, sitting there in borrowed clothes with a dead woman's map on my back, that the burning hadn't been cover at all. Cover was what I told myself. The truth was simpler and less strategic: some things deserve to burn, and I had finally, at twenty-five, developed opinions.

The knife hummed against my hip. Restless. Pointing.

"North," I said, and shouldered the tube, and walked out of the only life I'd ever had, into the fog, toward the king.
