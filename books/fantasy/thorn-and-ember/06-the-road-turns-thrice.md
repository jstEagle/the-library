## 6. The Road Turns Thrice

There are three ways to desert the royal guard. The first is to run at night with your boots in your hand and your shame in your throat, which is what most men do. The second is to die honorably somewhere convenient, which is what I'd have preferred. The third is to walk out the north gate at midday, in uniform, past a gate sergeant who owed me eleven crowns and a grudge, and let him decide whether to stop you.

He didn't stop me. He never liked me much, but he liked the crown less lately, though he'd have cut out his own tongue before saying so. That was the mood of the whole city, if you listened — and I had spent nine years learning to listen, because listening is what a guard does when he isn't allowed to speak.

So: north road, midday, uniform under a plain oilskin cloak that fooled no one. A man who wants to be invisible doesn't walk like a soldier. I knew that. I walked like a soldier anyway. Old habits are heavier than armor and fit worse.

The north road out of Vael Harrow climbs through orchards gone feral and then through gorse country where the wind comes off the salt marshes and everything smells faintly of rot and iodine. Good country for thinking. Bad country for being followed, which is why I'd chosen it, and why it unsettled me when I realized someone *was* following me — not behind, but ahead. A figure walking the same direction I was, small, hooded, moving with the particular economy of someone who has learned not to waste motion because attention costs more than coin.

I'd been watching her for an hour before she knew it. Or she'd been watching me for two hours before I knew it. On reflection, I suspect the second.

* * *

She stopped at the shrine where the road forks three ways — the old stone one, moss-eaten, with the three faces worn smooth by weather and by travelers' thumbs, because everyone touches something when they're choosing a direction they don't believe in. She stood there a long time. Then she took out a knife — a carving knife, bone-handled, the kind of blade that looks like it has opinions — and held it against the stone of the shrine like she was taking its pulse.

I should have kept walking. Any sensible deserter keeps walking. But I'd seen the roll of vellum sticking out of her satchel when she shifted it off her shoulder, and I'd seen the seal on the end of it, or rather the wax stub where a seal had been broken away, and there is exactly one seal in this kingdom pressed in black wax with a three-tined mark, and I had sworn an oath over it once, and oaths are the only things I've ever been any good at keeping.

"Lost?" I said, coming up the road.

She didn't startle. That was my first warning about her. Ordinary people startle; she just turned her head a fraction, the way a cat acknowledges a dog it has already assessed as irrelevant.

"No," she said.

"The road turns thrice here," I said, nodding at the shrine. "That's what the old marker says, anyway. Coast, capital, or the fens. Most people stand here longer than they need to."

"I'm not most people."

"No," I agreed. "Most people don't take a dead shrine's pulse."

Her hand closed around the knife. "You were watching me."

"For an hour," I said. "You were watching me for two. I'd like to know how, since I'm supposed to be good at not being watched."

Something moved at the corner of her mouth. Not quite a smile. The memory of one, maybe, kept in a jar. "You walk like a wall," she said. "Walls are easy to watch. They don't change."

"Tam Vess," I said, and then immediately regretted giving a name to a stranger carrying guild-sealed vellum on a road where, by now, people were certainly looking for both of us.

She looked at me properly then, and I saw her eyes for the first time — grey, level, tired in the specific way of someone who hasn't slept in a bed in days. "Freya Almeida," she said, after a pause long enough to tell me it wasn't her real hesitation about the name but about whether names were a currency she could afford to spend.

"And where are you walking to, Freya Almeida?"

"Away."

"That's not a direction."

"It's the only honest one left."

I liked her immediately, which should have frightened me. Liking people is how guards get killed. You hold a door for the wrong man, you laugh at the wrong joke, you decide the nervous servant is harmless, and one morning your throat opens and the last thing you learn about trust is its price. Nine years in the palace had taught me to be pleasant and empty, a well-polished surface with nothing behind it worth stealing.

But she said *away* like it was a wound, and wounds are the one language I've stayed fluent in.

"You're running from the guild," I said.

Her stillness was answer enough. It was also, I noticed, the stillness of someone deciding whether to lie, and finding, with visible irritation, that lying required energy she hadn't budgeted.

"And you're running from the palace," she said. "Your cloak is palace-issue. Your boots are guard-issue. You've got a sword callus on your right hand and a pen callus on your left, which means reports, which means officer track, which means you didn't leave because you were bored." Her chin tipped toward the shrine. "And you looked at the fork and went pale when you thought about the capital road. So. Two liars at a crossroads. Shall we pretend to be pilgrims together?"

I laughed. It came out rusty, like a gate that hadn't swung in years. "You're very rude for someone half my size."

"I'm very accurate for someone half your size. Rude is incidental."

* * *

We walked. Not together, exactly — ten paces apart, the traditional distance between strangers who are lying to each other — but in the same direction, which on the north road means the same thing for a while.

I'll say this for her: she didn't ask questions, and I didn't ask questions, and the silence between us had the shape of a truce rather than a wall. The gorse gave way to low hills, the hills to a long grey stretch of moor with cairns standing in it like teeth. Somewhere behind us, Vael Harrow sat under its bells and its fog, and somewhere ahead of us, whatever we were each running toward waited with the patience of all bad things.

It was near dusk when she finally spoke without being prompted, which I came to understand was how she did most of her talking — in bursts, like a woman paying out rope carefully, hating every foot of it.

"The courier died," she said. "Two nights ago. In the loading dock of the Vellum Guild. He put a map in my hands and said *thirteen turns*, and then he died, and everyone assumed I'd hand it up the chain because that's what I do. Hand things up the chain. Balance the ledgers. Keep the accounts of impossible things." She glanced sideways at me. "Do you know what the guild trades?"

"I know the name."

"Nobody knows the name. They know the rumors. Love potions that work. Storms sold by the barrel. Letters from the dead, certified authentic." Her voice was flat, recitation-flat, ledger-flat. "The truth is smaller and worse. We trade in bindings. Things held where they shouldn't be, doing what they shouldn't do. And I kept the books for six years and told myself the numbers were just numbers."

"And now?"

"Now the numbers have a face." She touched the satchel. "The map charts the king's reign. Thirteen moon-turns. And on the thirteenth—"

She stopped. Not because she'd decided to stop. Because the arrow came out of the gorse and buried itself in the cairn beside her head with a sound like a giant knuckle cracking.

I had my sword out before my mind caught up — nine years of drill will do that, will keep the body loyal long after the heart resigns. Three figures came up out of the heather where they'd been flat as stones, and even at distance I knew the cut of their cloaks. Guild enforcers. Grey wool, no livery, short crossbows and long knives, moving with the unhurried competence of men who had done this many times and been paid well for it.

"Almeida!" the lead one called. Not unkindly. That was the worst part, later — how reasonable they always sounded. "Guildmaster Corvane asks you to come home. No harm intended. Bring the item."

Freya had gone white but not still. Her knife was in her hand, that little bone-handled thing, useless against crossbows, and she held it anyway the way people hold onto railings in floods.

"He sent three," she said quietly. "For me. He used to send flowers when I was ill."

"People change," I said. "Or they were always this and you were too close to see it. Both, usually." I stepped in front of her, which was stupid, and did it anyway, which was the story of my life. "Can you run?"

"In these boots? Like a wounded ox."

"Good. Wounded oxen go left. There's a streambed down the slope, maybe four hundred yards. They'll expect right, toward the coast road." I rolled my shoulders. "I'll make noise."

"You'll die."

"Probably not. I'm very annoying to kill. Ask anyone I've ever guarded." Which was a lie, because the one person whose death I'd actually failed to prevent was the reason I was on this road at all, and his name lived in my chest like a swallowed stone. "Go."

She went. And to her credit — and I want it recorded that I noted this, because noting things is all I have left of my profession — she didn't hesitate over leaving me. She ran like a woman who had already done the arithmetic on strangers' lives and found them cheaper than hers, and I respected it even as the first bolt hissed past my ear.

I made noise. I've always been good at noise. I shouted, I crashed through the gorse, I threw my cloak over a cairn so it looked like a man diving for cover, and two of the three bolts meant for me spent themselves on rock. The third grazed my arm, a hot line like a brand, and then I was running too, downhill, laughing — actually laughing, because there is a particular hysteria that lives in the body of a man who has finally, after months of gray dread, been given something simple to do.

Run. Fight or flee. Simple. The palace had never once offered me anything simple.

* * *

I found her at the streambed, chest heaving, holding her knife like it might negotiate on her behalf. Blood was running down my forearm and dripping off my fingers into the water, and she looked at it, and then at me, and her expression did something complicated.

"You're hurt."

"It's a scratch."

"It's bleeding."

"That's what scratches do. It's their whole personality."

She tore a strip off her sleeve without asking — another thing I noted, the not-asking, the assumption that bodies are just problems to be solved — and bound the arm with hands that were steadier than mine. Carver's hands. Precise. While she worked, her fingers brushed the wound's edge and she flinched, hard, like she'd heard something.

"You hear voices," I said. It wasn't a question. Everyone in the capital had heard the stories about the guild's bone-carvers, the ones who could pull whispers out of relics. I'd filed those stories under superstition for nine years. Her flinch refiled them.

"I hear the dead," she said, tying off the bandage. "Through carved bone. It's not a gift, whatever the guild calls it. It's a debt collector. Every voice I ever woke, I owe attention forever." She sat back on her heels. "Yours aren't talking. Too fresh, probably. Give it a week."

"That's grim."

"That's Tuesday." She looked up at me, and the last light was going out of the sky, and her face in the blue dusk was young and old at once. "Why did you step in front of me? You don't know me. You're a deserter with a scratched arm and no future, and you spent your only asset — surprise — on a stranger with a stolen map."

I could have lied. I'm a good liar; it was practically my job description. *I'm a decent sort. I couldn't watch them shoot a woman in the back.* All true, none of it true.

Instead I heard myself say: "Because I reported something once. Something wrong, at the top. And the answer I got wasn't *you're mistaken*, it was *be silent or be gone*. And I chose gone, and I've hated myself every mile of it, because silence felt like the thing I accused them of." My jaw ached. I hadn't said any of this aloud, ever. "When that bolt came at you, I thought — here's a chance to be the man I claimed I was when I left. Cheap chance. Small chance. But I've learned to take what's offered."

She was quiet a long moment. The stream talked instead, shallow and cold over stones.

"They trained me to keep secrets," she said finally. "Six years. Corvane used to say discretion was the highest form of loyalty. And I believed him, because he fed me and taught me my trade and never once raised his voice, and I mistook all of that for love." Her thumb moved along the bone handle of her knife, back and forth, a carver's unconscious caress. "Today he sent three men with crossbows and a message full of *no harm intended*. And the worst part, Tam Vess, the part I can't say to anyone—" her voice dropped, not for drama but because the words were heavy, "—is that some part of me still wants to go home. Still wants him to be right. Loyalty doesn't die when it's betrayed. It just starts lying to you."

I knew that feeling the way you know your own handwriting.

"So," I said. "Two liars."

"Two liars," she agreed.

"With one map between us, three hunters behind us, and a king going somewhere bad at the end of thirteen moon-turns." I stood, tested the arm, found it functional. "How many turns are left?"

She pulled the map from her satchel and unrolled it just far enough, angling it away from me until she'd apparently decided, with visible reluctance, to trust me with the rest. The ink moved. I want that on the record too — the ink *moved*, lines crawling like veins, and a coastline redrew itself while I watched, and I am a man who has seen the inside of the royal treasury and thought himself beyond surprise.

"Four," she said. "Four turns. And the nearest turn-marker is three days east, and between here and there is the Bone and Ash villages, where—" she hesitated again, and this time the hesitation had fear in it, "—where something has been making the dead go quiet."

"Quiet how?"

"Silent. All of them. Whole villages of charms that don't sing anymore." She rolled the map up. "Someone is severing the dead from their voices. Someone guild-trained."

The wind came down off the moor and pushed at our backs, toward the east, toward the fens, toward whatever waited in the villages where the bones had stopped speaking. I thought about turning south, losing myself in the coast towns, becoming a fisherman with a soldier's shoulders and a bad conscience. It would have been easy. Easy things had a way of presenting themselves to me right up until the moment I chose them, and then they turned to ash in my mouth.

"Well," I said. "Since we're both liars, I'll make you a truthful offer. I can fight, I can march thirty miles a day, and I can read a room faster than anyone you've ever met. In exchange, you tell me everything that map says, and neither of us pretends the other is anything but damned."

Freya Almeida looked at me for a long moment, weighing, the way she'd weighed everything since the crossroads — cost, risk, interest owed.

"Damned," she repeated. "Together, then."

"Together," I said, and we turned east, and behind us, far off across the darkening moor, a single bell began to ring from a village that, according to every map I'd ever read, had no church.
