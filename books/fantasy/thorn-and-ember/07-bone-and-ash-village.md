## 7. Bone and Ash Village

The horse died two miles short of the village, which I took personally.

Not because I'd grown fond of it — it had bitten me twice and smelled like a wet ledger — but because everything else in my life had also chosen that week to stop working, and the horse was simply following a trend. Tam Vess said something consoling about walking being good for the soul. Tam Vess had been saying a great deal of things since the road turned thrice, most of them designed to make me forget he'd deserted his post and I'd burned down my only home, and I'll admit some of them worked.

"Village ahead," he said, shading his eyes. "Smoke."

"Smoke means people. People mean questions." I flexed my right hand, which had been cramping since we crossed the river. The knife rode against my ribs where it always did now, warm as a sleeping animal. Maren — the dead cartographer who lived in my carving knife and had opinions about everything, including my posture — stirred at the edge of my awareness.

*Ask them about roads,* she murmured. *Dead men are terrible guides. We only ever draw the way we went.*

"You're dead," I said under my breath.

*I'm aware. It's made me humble.*

Tam gave me the look he'd perfected over four days of travel — the one that said *I have decided not to ask about the voice you keep hearing, in exchange for you deciding not to ask why I flinch at bells.* A fair trade, as trades go. I'd spent nine years keeping the Vellum Guild's ledgers of impossible things; I knew a fair trade when I was inside one.

We walked the last stretch through ash-fall so fine it looked like the sky was shedding. That should have been my first warning. Villages burn for reasons, and the reasons are usually recent.

* * *

Bone and Ash Village — that was its actual name, burned into a signpost in letters someone had tried to scratch out and then thought better of — sat in a fold of hills like a coin dropped in a purse. Slate roofs, a mill, a shrine with a bell tower, and everywhere, on every person we passed, a carved bone charm.

I stopped in the road and stared.

Now, understand: bone charms are common enough. Farmers wear them against blight, mothers against fever, soldiers against the specific terror of dying far from home. Half of them are carved by frauds and the other half by the mediocre. But these were *good*. These were very good. Whoever cut them understood grain and density, understood that bone remembers the body it came from, understood —

Understood, I realized with a cold slide of recognition, exactly what I understood. These were guild-trained hands. Guild-trained and better than guild-trained.

And every single one of them was silent.

Let me explain what silence means to someone like me, because I don't think I can explain anything else until I do. When I carve bone, I hear the dead. Not their words, usually — their *weight*. A lamb's knuckle hums with the small dumb grief of a spring cut short. A grandmother's finger-bone carries sixty years of kneading bread and holding children's faces. It isn't conversation. It's more like pressing your ear to a door and hearing a life on the other side. For nine years I kept the Guild's accounts by listening to whether a seller's relic rang true or hollow, and for nine years I told myself the noise in my head was a fair price for a trade.

Every charm in that village was quiet. Not blank — blank is what uncarved bone is, a shut door. These were doors with the hinges torn off. These were voices that had been *removed*, cleanly, deliberately, the way you'd pull a tooth and leave the socket.

"Freya." Tam's hand was on my elbow. "You've gone white. Is it the knife? Is she saying something?"

"No," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

We found lodging at an inn called the Kneeling Hart, run by a woman named Brey whose charm — a lark, wings spread, exquisite work — sat mute against her throat. She charged us fairly, fed us stew that tasted mostly of salt, and answered our questions with the flat courtesy of someone who has answered them before.

"The fire?" she said, when Tam asked. "Three years back. Kiln accident up at the charnel house. Took the roof and half the ossuary records."

"And the charms?" I asked. "Everyone wears one here. Even the children."

Her hand rose to the lark without her seeming to notice she'd done it. "After the fire, folk wanted... closeness. To the ones we lost. Sister Ondine carved for the whole valley. Didn't charge a copper." Something moved behind her eyes — gratitude wearing grief's clothes, or grief wearing gratitude's, I couldn't tell which. "You'll want to see her while you're here, if you're needing work done. Though I'd say your friend there has her own talent." She nodded at my hands, which are ink-stained and knife-scarred and apparently legible to anyone who looks.

"I dabble," I said.

"Mm," said Brey, in the tone of a woman who had just watched me lie competently and filed it away.

That night I sat in our room with the shutter open and did something I hadn't done since fleeing the Guild: I carved. Just a scrap of lamb bone from the stew pot, nothing precious. My hands needed it the way Tam's hands needed a sword hilt — some part of me had started shaking around dusk every day and only the knife stilled it.

Maren woke the moment steel touched bone.

*Ah,* she said. *Home.*

"Don't be sentimental. It's a soup bone."

*Everything is a soup bone if you're honest about death.* A pause, the particular quality of pause that meant she was looking through my eyes at something. *Freya. Look at the innkeeper's charm again. Hold it.*

"It's her property."

*Hold it anyway. You want to know what happened here. So do I, and I'm the one who's dead.*

So I knocked on Brey's door with some nonsense about a loose shutter, apologized my way into the parlor, and asked — with the shameless directness of a woman who had already lost everything worth protecting — if I might examine her charm. She studied me for a long moment. Then she unpinned the lark from her throat and set it in my palm, and I swear the woman exhaled like she'd been carrying it for three years.

It was beautiful. It was flawless. And when I listened — really listened, opening that sense I'd kept leashed since the docks — I heard nothing. Worse than nothing. I heard the *shape* of where a voice had been, the way a bed holds the shape of a sleeper after they rise. Someone had gone into this charm with a blade finer than mine and cut the dead woman's voice out whole, without cracking the bone, without waking the neighbors' pieces, with a precision that made my fingers ache with envy and my stomach turn with something that wasn't envy at all.

"She was my mother," Brey said quietly. "The lark. She sang in the shrine choir forty years. When Ondine finished the charm, I thought — I thought I'd finally sleep again. No dreams of the fire. No hearing her voice in the walls." Her jaw tightened. "And I do sleep. I sleep like the drowned. Is that what you wanted to know?"

I set the lark down as gently as if it were an ember. "Who is Sister Ondine?"

"Our carver. Guild-trained, like yourself — don't look surprised, girl, we all know guild work when we wear it. She came after the fire. She says the dead deserve rest, not tenancy. She says—" Brey stopped. Started again, slower. "She says grief is a debt collectors never stop calling on. And she forgives it."

*Freya.* Maren's voice had gone thin and cold. *I've seen this before. In the capital, before I died. Whole districts going quiet. I thought it was plague. It wasn't plague.*

"What was it?"

*I didn't stay to find out. Staying is how I became a map.*

* * *

Tam found me at the well past midnight, because of course he did; the man had a gift for appearing whenever I was doing something I'd rather not explain. He leaned on the stones beside me and didn't speak for a while, which is one of perhaps three things I genuinely liked about him.

"There's a shrine bell tower," he said eventually. "Been staring at it all evening. No rope."

"So they ring it by hand."

"There's no hammer either, Freya. There's a bell and no way to ring it. Who builds a bell tower and takes away the sound?"

I looked at him. In the dark his face had the particular honesty of a man who has decided lying is more effort than truth. "Someone who doesn't want to wake anything," I said. "Or someone who wants to practice."

He turned the thought over visibly. "The charms. They're not keepsakes, are they. They're containers."

"They're *emptied* containers." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "Do you know what the Guild pays for a true relic? What kings pay? Every voice in this valley — every grandmother, every stillborn child, every man who died in that kiln fire — someone harvested them. Neatly. Kindly, even, maybe. And that's what frightens me, Tam. Cruelty I could account for. I kept ledgers. Cruelty balances. But this — this was done with care. This was done by someone who believes she's *helping*."

"*She*?"

"Sister Ondine." I heard myself say the name and realized I'd already decided to hate her, which was premature, and probably wrong, and I did it anyway. "Guild-trained. Better than guild-trained. Corvane only ever produced two carvers he called flawless." The words came out of me before I could stop them, and once they started they finished themselves: "Me. And one other. A girl who left the Guild six years ago because she believed listening to the dead was a violence we committed daily and called a sacrament."

Tam was quiet. Then: "Corvane's other protégé."

"My sister in everything but blood." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "I used to write to her. She never wrote back. I told myself she was busy."

*You told yourself what you needed,* Maren said, gentler than I'd ever heard her. *Dead women can afford gentleness. It's the living who have to be cruel to themselves to survive. Now listen to me, girl, because this matters: a silencing this clean, across a whole valley, sustained for years — that's not one talented carver working alone. That's a method. That's a doctrine. And doctrines travel.*

"How many regions?" I asked her aloud, forgetting Tam couldn't hear her, or perhaps not forgetting, perhaps wanting him to ask.

"Regions what?" he said.

"How many places have gone quiet." I stood, pacing. "Maren saw it near the capital. This valley. If it's spreading along the trade roads — the Guild's roads — then it's not a village problem, it's a *network*. Someone is teaching this. Someone is supplying the technique the way the Guild supplies relics. And everyone in this valley is walking around grateful for it, sleeping like the drowned, wearing their emptied dead at their throats like—"

"Like receipts," Tam said.

I stopped pacing. Of course. Of course he'd see it; he'd guarded a king who was disappearing hour by hour and reported it, and been ordered silenced for his trouble. Silenced. The word landed between us like a dropped coin.

"They tried to silence you," I said.

"They gave me a choice between a posting at the world's edge and a quieter option." His mouth twisted. "I chose the world's edge and then kept walking. But Freya — if someone could do to the dead what they wanted done to me—" He shook his head. "There are worse fates than dying. I've started a list."

* * *

I didn't sleep. Instead I sat by candlelight with the lamb-bone scrap and did the thing I'd been afraid to do since the courier died in my arms: I listened outward. Past the inn. Past the village. Out along the roads, the way you'd strain to hear music from a distant hall.

For nine years I had heard the dead as a constant low chorus, the background hum of the world, so familiar I'd stopped noticing it the way you stop noticing your own heartbeat. Tonight I mapped its absences. Bone and Ash Village: a hole in the song. The next valley east, where we'd bought the horse: thinner than it should be, thinned. The hills north, toward the capital: ragged, patchy, silence spreading through the chorus like frost through a windowpane.

Someone was erasing the dead of half a kingdom. Patiently. Gratefully received at every step.

And underneath the horror, in the place where I kept my worst thoughts locked, a smaller and more personal fear turned over: *if they can cut a voice out of a charm, can they cut one out of a person?* Because my gift lived in my hands and my hands lived in me, and somewhere out there was a woman trained in the same school of blade-work who believed that what I carried was not a sacrament but a wound — and who had, by every account, the skill to close it.

Near dawn, footsteps on the stairs. Tam, dressed, armed, awake. He set a folded paper on the table between us. On it, drawn in his careful soldier's hand: the valley, the roads, the villages marked where Brey and the miller and the shrine-warden had mentioned kin. Seven marks. All silent.

"Bells," he said. "This morning the shrine-warden told me why there's no hammer. Sister Ondine is coming. First bell of the season, and she rings it herself, every year, by striking it with her carving mallet." He met my eyes. "Today, Freya. She's coming today."

Outside, the ash was falling again, soft as snow, soft as sleep, and down the eastern road — I would swear to this on any ledger, living or dead — I heard, faint and clear and utterly alone in a valley full of silence, the sound of a single bell beginning to ring.
