## 9. What the King Remembers

I have kept a journal since I was nine years old. My father started me on it — *a king who cannot remember his own reign will be remembered by other men's versions of it*, he said, which was the closest thing to tenderness the man ever managed. Forty-one volumes sit in a locked case beside my bed, each one written in my own hand, each one a small act of defiance against forgetting.

Last night I read Volume Thirty-Nine and found an entry I do not remember writing.

It was dated the ninth of Harvestmoon. It described, in careful detail, my judgment of a dispute between the Saltwrights' Guild and the harbor master over dredging rights. The handwriting was mine. The phrasing was mine — even down to the little joke about mud that I would have made, because it is exactly the sort of joke I make when I am bored and trying not to show it. And yet I have no memory of that afternoon at all. Not the parties involved, not the mud, not the joke. It is as though someone read my journals for twenty years and then wrote one page of their own, and did it well enough to fool even me.

The trouble with being king is that everyone around you has an interest in your confidence. Tell a chamberlain you slept poorly and by noon there are physicians at your door. Tell a minister you feel strange and by evening there are three factions drafting arguments about regency. So I have told no one. I have smiled at breakfast, held audiences, signed what needed signing, and every night I have sat alone with forty-one volumes and tried to find the seams.

There are more seams now. That is the thing I cannot say aloud, even here, even to paper: they are getting worse.

* * *

My wife watches me.

Isolde has always watched me — that is what love looks like on her, a kind of vigilant attention she would deny under oath. She watches the way a falconer watches a bird she suspects has gone quiet in the hood. But lately her watching has changed its weather. She laughs at my jokes half a beat too late, as though checking them against something first. When I reach for her hand across the breakfast table she gives it willingly, warmly, but her eyes go somewhere else while our fingers touch — up to my temple, or down to my ring hand, someplace she thinks the wrongness might be visible if she could only catch it unawares.

This morning she caught something. I saw her see it.

I had been telling her about the harvest festival — the plans, the bells, the ridiculous ceremonial cake that weighs as much as a pony and tastes like sweetened plaster. All of this from memory, all of it true, all of it mine. And then I said, "You'll wear the blue, I think. You looked like winter ending in it last year."

She went still. Not dramatically — Isolde does nothing dramatically; she is a queen, she does everything at a volume just below scandal. But her cup stopped halfway to her mouth and hung there, and her face did the arithmetic of a woman deciding whether to say a dangerous thing.

"Aldric," she said carefully, "you told me yesterday that I should wear the green."

"Did I?"

"You were very firm about it. You said the blue made me look tired." A pause, and then, softer, with something underneath it that frightened me more than accusation would have: "You said you liked that I looked tired. You said it meant I wasn't going anywhere."

I laughed. I heard myself laugh — a warm, kingly, easy sound, the laugh of a man who misremembers trivial things, who teases his wife, who is fine. And inside the laugh I was screaming, because I had no memory of saying either thing, and one of them was cruel, and I do not know which of us said it.

"I'm sure I said both," I told her. "A husband is entitled to contradict himself. It keeps wives sharp."

She smiled. She let it go. And when she kissed my cheek on her way out, her lips lingered a moment longer than affection requires — at the temple, where her eyes had been going all week — and I understood that my wife was conducting an investigation into me, and that she was doing it alone, and that whatever she feared, she feared it more than she trusted me with it.

I sat alone after she left and finished her tea, because wasting tea is a sin my mother beat out of me early, and I thought: *she is right to investigate. I would investigate me.*

* * *

Here is what I can account for, if I am honest, and honesty is the one luxury this crown has never taxed:

Three nights ago I lost two hours. I know this because I remember sitting down after supper to review the border petitions, and I remember the candle at shoulder-height, and I remember thinking the third petition was tedious. Then I remember the candle burned down to the socket and the petitions were signed — all of them, including the third, including annotations in my hand that were *better* than my usual annotations, sharper, more decisive, the work of a king having an excellent evening. I simply have no memory of being him.

Two nights ago, Master Corvane of the Vellum Guild came to court. He is an old friend of the crown — guildmaster, scholar, the man who brokered peace during the succession crisis before my coronation, back when the realm stood a bad week away from tearing itself apart. I remember greeting him. I remember his voice, dry and warm, like paper settling. I remember him presenting a gift for my approval — something small, ceremonial, wrapped in vellum — and I remember the hall going quiet around us in that way halls do when everyone pretends not to watch a king receive a thing.

And then I remember waking the next morning with the taste of wax in my mouth and a faint ache behind my eyes, and Corvane's gift already installed above the throne dais where the old crown used to rest between ceremonies.

The new crown. Yes. Let me write it plainly, since apparently I am the only man in Vellmark willing to: there is a new crown, and I do not remember agreeing to it, and it sits heavier than any crown I have worn, though every goldsmith in the palace swears it weighs less than the old one did. It fits perfectly. That is perhaps the worst part. Crowns are supposed to fight you a little. This one settles onto my head like it was poured in the shape of me, like it has been waiting a long time for a head exactly this size.

When I wear it, I am magnificent. I want to be honest about that too. Decisions come easily. Words arrive fully formed. Ministers who spent years tying me in knots find themselves agreeing before they've finished their objections. Last week I resolved a grain dispute in eleven minutes that had been festering since spring, and the room applauded, and I felt — I felt *wonderful*. Clear. Certain. Like the version of myself my portraitists keep insisting on painting.

The problem is that the magnificence isn't always mine.

I know how that sounds. I have rehearsed saying it to the mirror and it sounds mad every time. But a king learns to know his own mind the way a swordsman knows his own arm — its weight, its habits, the little delays and preferences that make it his. And lately there are hours — more of them, always more — when the arm moves and it is not quite my arm. The judgments are good ones. That's the trap of it. If the thing wearing my certainty were stupid or cruel, I'd have raised the alarm weeks ago. But it is *better* than me. Faster. Kinder when kindness is useful, hard when hardness pays. It governs the way I always wished I governed, and it does it with my hands, and every morning I wake and read the record of what "I" decided and think: *this is what Father wanted me to be,* and then, a heartbeat later: *then why do I feel like a guest?*

* * *

Tonight I tried an experiment. I tell you this so that if the experiment goes strangely, the record shows it was deliberate.

I took the crown from its stand — servants scurried, of course; kings are not permitted to carry their own hats — and I set it on the table across from me instead of on my head, and I sat looking at it for an hour with a candle between us like a chaperone.

It is beautiful. That must be recorded too. The goldwork is unlike anything the royal smiths produce — finer, older-feeling, with a pattern worked into the band that I couldn't follow no matter how I traced it. My eye kept sliding off it. Every time I tried to count the points or map the filigree, I found I'd lost my place, found my thoughts had drifted pleasantly elsewhere, toward nothing in particular, toward a warm and agreeable blankness. Four times I caught myself. On the fourth time I realized my lips had been moving, and I pressed them shut, and I made myself look at the thing the way you look at a man you suspect of lying to you.

And for just a moment — one candle-flicker of a moment — I had the distinct impression that it looked back.

Not eyes. Nothing so vulgar. Just... attention. The way a door that you thought was closed is, in fact, ajar. I felt considered. I felt, absurdly, *approved of*. Like a horse being measured for harness by someone who already knows it will fit.

I put it back on its stand. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Kings practice steadiness the way monks practice prayer.

Then I did the second part of the experiment, the part I have been building toward for a week. I opened Volume Forty — the current volume — and on a fresh page I wrote, in letters larger than my usual hand, a message addressed to whoever else has been writing in these books:

*I know you are here. I don't know what you are. But this body is mine, this name is mine, and I am telling you now: I will find the seam, and I will pick it open, and everything you have done in my shape I will undo in my own.*

Then I blew out the candle and went to bed, and lay awake listening to my own heartbeat, waiting to see if anything answered.

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. Things that steal a kingdom do not reply to notes slid under their door.

But this morning, rereading my message in daylight, I found that beneath it, in my own handwriting, in ink I do not own, someone has added a single line:

*You agreed. Check the ninth of Harvestmoon. You were there.*

* * *

I spent the whole day with Volume Thirty-Nine. Cancelled two audiences, pleaded headache — the physicians came anyway, prescribed rest, were dismissed. I turned pages until my fingers ached, comparing entries, hunting the gap the way a miser hunts a leak in his strongbox.

The ninth of Harvestmoon is there. The Saltwrights' dispute. The mud. The joke. Everything accounted for, everything in my hand.

Except.

I have been writing in the margins of my journals since I was nine. Little marks — a star for a good day, a dash for a bad one, the occasional furious scribble when a minister earned it. Forty-one volumes of marginalia, as consistent as a heartbeat.

Volume Thirty-Nine has none. Not one mark, from the ninth of Harvestmoon forward. Twenty days of clean, confident, unannotated rule.

I went back through the earlier volumes to be sure. Stars, dashes, scribbles — everywhere, always, right up to that date. And then nothing. As though the man who made those marks walked out of his own book and something tidier moved in.

So it is real. I am not mad, or if I am mad, I am mad in a way that leaves receipts. Something began on the ninth of Harvestmoon, something wearing my patience and my signature, and it has been governing in my place ever since, and improving on me, God help me, improving on me so smoothly that only a wife's too-long kiss and a missing asterisk stand between it and forever.

Tomorrow I will do what I should have done weeks ago. I cannot trust the council — half of them would cheer my replacement if it came with better grain prices. I cannot trust the physicians. But there are records the crown-thing would not think to check, because they are not the crown's records. The Vellum Guild trades in impossible things; Corvane built the peace I inherited; and if anyone in this kingdom knows what was done to me on the ninth of Harvestmoon, it is the guild that made the gift.

I will send for their ledgers. Quietly. Through channels even my other self doesn't watch.

Because tonight, washing my face, I caught my reflection watching me back — and it smiled a half-second before I did.
