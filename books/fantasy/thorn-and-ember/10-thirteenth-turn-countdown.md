## 10. Thirteenth Turn Countdown

The map had been trying to tell us for three days before I finally listened.

I want to say that in my defense — and I have very little defense these days — that a moving map is a noisy thing. The ink crawled like something alive because it was something alive, or at least something inhabited, and Maren the cartographer had never been what you'd call concise when she had a living audience. She'd spent two days narrating road conditions to me through my carving knife. *Mud past the ford. Bandits at the toll bridge, but lazy ones. Avoid the shrine at Kiln's Cross, the priest sells confessions back to you at interest.*

Useful. Endless. Not, unfortunately, an explanation of the thing I actually needed explained.

It took Tam Vess — disgraced guard, terrible liar, worse cook — to notice what I'd been too close to see. He spread the map flat on the floor of the abandoned mill we'd claimed for the night, weighted the corners with river stones, and stared at it the way he stared at everything: like it might be lying to him.

"Freya," he said finally. "Count."

"Count what?"

"The moons." He pointed. Along the map's lower border, where a proper cartographer would put her name or a scale bar, Maren had inked thirteen small circles. Twelve of them were filled in — solid, dark, finished. The thirteenth was an outline only. An empty circle, waiting.

And beneath each filled moon, in Maren's cramped hand, a date. A reign-date. King Aldric's twelfth year, month by month, all the way to the present.

"Oh," I said.

"*Oh*," Tam agreed, with the particular grimness of a man who has just confirmed his worst suspicion about his own king.

Because here is what I knew, and what I had been refusing to arrange into a straight line: the courier had said *thirteen turns*. The binding began twelve moon-turns ago. And there were four turns left until midwinter, when the court would hold its Night of Bells and the whole capital would be too drunk on ceremony to notice anything amiss with its king.

Four turns. One empty circle. That was the arithmetic, and arithmetic does not care how much you wish it were otherwise.

* * *

Let me explain what the map actually showed, because it took me another full day of listening to Maren's ghost to understand it properly, and I would spare you the same confusion if I could.

The crown — the one sitting on Aldric's head right now, in every portrait, every coin, every ceremonial appearance — was not jewelry. It was a vessel. Someone had built it to hold a thing, and the thing had been pouring itself into the king a little more with every turn of the moon since his twelfth year began. Each moon-turn was a stitch. Thirteen stitches, and the seam closed forever: the king gone, the thing wearing him complete, indistinguishable, permanent.

"It's patient," Maren told me through the knife that night, while Tam kept watch at the mill door and pretended not to listen, which was a kindness neither of us acknowledged. "That's the worst of it. Whatever it is, it doesn't rush. It lets him keep being king. It lets him sign the decrees, ride the hunts, kiss his queen at the festivals. It just... takes the hours. Small ones at first. Then whole nights. By the ninth turn he stopped noticing the gaps. By the eleventh, the gaps started noticing him."

"How do you know all this?" I asked. "You've been dead for — how long?"

"Six years," she said, without rancor. "I charted the binding from the outside. I was guild-trained, like you. Like your mother." She paused there, deliberately, the way the dead do when they want you to sit with something. "I tried to warn the palace. I sent the map ahead of me. The courier was meant to reach someone who could still act. Instead he reached a bone-carver who balances ledgers for the people who made the thing in the first place."

"I didn't know," I said. "What the guild traded in. What Corvane—"

"No," Maren said. "You didn't. That's not the same as innocent, Freya Almeida, but it isn't nothing either. You balanced their books. You didn't write them." Another pause. "Yet."

I did not enjoy the word *yet*. I turned the knife over on my knee and looked at the fire instead, and the fire looked back at me the way fires do, which is to say without offering any comfort whatsoever.

* * *

Here is what four turns means, practically, for two fugitives traveling north toward a capital that has hunters looking for them:

It means no more detours. No more sleeping cold rather than risk a lit window. No more of Tam's careful, soldierly insistence on doubling back to check whether we're followed — because yes, we are followed, we have been followed since the salt-fog docks, and checking again will not change it.

It also means conversations at night that wouldn't happen at a reasonable pace. Urgency does that. It strips the padding off things.

On the fifth night out from the mill, Tam sat across the fire from me sharpening a knife that was not my carving knife, and asked, casually, in the tone of a man asking about weather:

"When did you start hearing them? The dead."

I should have deflected. I was, by then, quite good at deflection; I'd learned it from Corvane, who could deflect a direct question so gracefully you thanked him for the privilege. But the fire was low, and Tam had spent six days feeding me half his rations and standing the worse watch, and lies cost more than they used to.

"My mother died when I was nine," I said. "Corvane took me in. First relic I carved was a keepsake bead from one of her finger bones. It sang to me before I finished polishing it." I turned my hands over, palms up, the way I did when the truth needed somewhere to sit. "She told me she was sorry. About the workshop. About what she'd made. I didn't understand what she meant for twenty years, and now I understand it completely, and I'd give the understanding back if I could."

Tam was quiet for a long moment. "You never told me about the finger bone."

"You never told me why you really deserted," I said. "You told me the version where you got tired of palace politics. Nobody deserts over politics. Politics is the reason people *stay*."

He almost smiled. "No," he agreed. "Nobody deserts over politics." And then he didn't tell me. Not that night. He looked at the fire and sharpened the knife and I let him, because shared maps are one kind of trust and shared silences are another, and we were building both.

* * *

The map, meanwhile, continued its campaign of revelation.

Two nights after the mill, the ink rearranged itself along the eastern edge — roads I hadn't noticed before, fine as hair, converging on the capital from directions that shouldn't have existed. Maren had drawn routes that only a dead woman could survey: through cellars, under rivers, along the tops of walls. "Smuggler's ways," she said, when I asked. "The guild's ways. Your guildmaster knows every one of them, which is why you can't use them. He'll have watchers on all of them by now."

"So we walk in the front door," Tam said, "of a palace, during a festival, carrying a treasonous map, to save a king who may already be mostly gone."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"It sounds insane in every register I own, Freya."

But he was already studying the festival dates on the map's margin, working out watch rotations from memory, doing the quiet competent arithmetic of a man who had guarded that palace for eleven years and knew which post slept and which post pretended to. I watched him do it and felt something inconvenient settle in my chest, and I decided — firmly, in the voice I used on difficult ledger entries — not to examine it until after the thirteenth turn. There would be time after the thirteenth turn. There had to be. That was rather the point of preventing it.

* * *

We argued, of course. We argued about whether to go to the queen first or the king first; about whether Maren's route through the Bone Gallery was real or a dead woman's sentimentality; about whether I was brave or merely cornered. (Tam held for cornered. I held for brave. We compromised on *stubborn*, which was accurate.)

The worst argument was about the empty circle.

Because here is the thing I hadn't said aloud, even to myself: the map showed the binding strengthening with each turn. It did not show it stopping. Four turns left meant four turns of Aldric getting worse — four turns of the thing wearing him taking more hours, more nights, more of *him* — and every plan we had required approaching a man who might, by then, not entirely be there to approach.

"What if we're too late?" I asked Tam, on the ninth night, huddled under a lean-to while sleet ticked against the canvas. "What if the last four circles fill in while we're still walking?"

"Then we walk faster."

"That's not a plan, Tam."

"It's the only part of the plan I'm certain of." He fed a stick to the fire. "Look. I stood post outside his chambers for three years. I've seen him tired, drunk, grieving, furious. This is none of those. This is a man being *edited*. Slowly. From inside." He looked at me across the flames. "You hear the dead. I watched the nearly-dead. Same trade, different ledgers. And everything I saw said: not yet gone. Slower than it wants to be. Something in him is still fighting the stitching."

"Something in him," I repeated. "Or someone."

Tam's face went carefully blank, which was how I knew I'd said something true. Because there was a question underneath our journey that neither of us had spoken: the queen. Isolde, who watched her husband sleep with fear she'd filed under love. Who noticed the edits before anyone. Whose letters to her husband's physicians had been intercepted by whom, exactly?

Maren's map had an opinion. The map always had opinions. On the tenth morning, the ink along the southern border thickened and darkened around a single word I hadn't seen before, small and deliberate, in Maren's hand:

*Isolde.*

Not a route. Not a warning. Just the name, placed on the map like a pin in cloth, saying: *here. Start here.*

"She knows," Maren said through the knife, when I pressed her. "She has known longer than anyone. She reads his sleep-talk the way you read relics. She hasn't acted because acting alone gets queens widowed twice — once by the thing, once by the council that prefers the thing. She needs hands she can trust." A pause, pointed as a chisel. "Hands that carve, Freya. Hands like yours. Hands like your mother's."

I wrapped the knife in wool and didn't speak to Maren for the rest of that day. Some inheritances need to stay folded for a while before you can wear them.

* * *

On the eleventh night, Tam finally told me.

Not all of it. But enough. We'd made the ridge above the river valley, and the capital's glow was visible far to the northeast — a smudge of orange on the horizon like a bruise — and he stood looking at it with his arms folded and said, without preamble:

"He asked me to stay. In the room. After the third night I saw his eyes change."

I didn't move. Didn't breathe, much.

"There's a look a man gets when he's losing an argument with himself. I'd seen it in deserters, in men before execution. The king had it at breakfast, over eggs, talking about drainage in the lower city. Perfectly ordinary sentence. Wrong eyes." Tam's jaw worked. "I reported it. Wrote it up properly, through channels, because I was a good soldier and good soldiers use channels. Two days later my captain told me the report never existed, and that if I valued my pension I'd find that it had never existed *in me* either." He laughed, short and bitter. "So I deserted. Ran from the thing I'd sworn to protect, because staying meant pretending I hadn't seen it. Eleven years of service, ended in one corridor."

"You don't have to—"

"I know what I don't have to do, Freya." But his hand found mine in the dark, rough and cold and certain, and stayed there. "I'm telling you because you'll meet him soon. The king. And you'll look in his eyes, and you'll want to run, and I need you to know that I ran already and it didn't help anyone. So we don't run. Whatever we see. Agreed?"

"Agreed," I said, and my voice came out steadier than my hands.

Below us, the river ran north toward the capital, carrying ice and silt and the reflected smear of the city's lights. Four turns left. Maybe three and a half, if you counted honestly, and I had always counted honestly; it was the one virtue the ledgers had left in me.

* * *

We came down out of the hills on the twelfth day, onto the flats, where the road widened and the traffic thickened and every face became a possible hunter. Tam taught me to walk like a merchant's wife. I taught him to carve a plain travel-charm — badly; his hands were built for sword-grips, not gravers — because a bone-carver draws less attention on this road than a big man with a soldier's shoulders and a deserter's eyes.

And on the evening of the twelfth day, resting in the shadow of a waystone, I unrolled the map one more time and found that Maren had added something new.

The thirteenth circle — the empty one — had changed. It was no longer simply an outline. Around its rim, tiny as lice, Maren had inked words, circling and circling, a sentence bent into a ring so that it had no beginning and no end. I held the map close to the last of the light and read it, and read it again, and felt the ground tilt gently under everything I thought I understood.

*The binding closes from the crown,* the ring said. *But crowns are carved. Ask who holds the mold.*

Ask who holds the mold.

My mother's workshop. Her marks, her tools, her hands — the hands that had trained mine without either of us knowing why. Somewhere in the world, the original mold of that crown still existed, and whoever held it could unmake what had been made, or remake it better, or —

Or finish it. On the thirteenth turn. With everyone gathered in one room.

I looked up at Tam, and he was already watching me, and whatever he saw in my face made him reach for his sword-belt out of pure habit.

"Bad news?" he asked.

"Worse," I said. "Specific news." I rolled the map tight. "Someone's waiting for us in the capital, Tam. And I think I know whose hands they're using."

Far down the road, where the light failed, a rider had stopped at the waystone behind us. Watching. Not following. Waiting — the way the crown-thing waited, the way the thirteenth circle waited, patient as ink.

They turned their horse toward us.
