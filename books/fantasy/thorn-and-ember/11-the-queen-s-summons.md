## 11. The Queen's Summons

I had been waiting at the waystone for three hours, and in that time I had learned several things about myself.

First: that a queen can sit on a cold stone in the dark wearing a servant's cloak and discover that she has no idea how servants stay warm. Second: that my husband's seal, pressed into wax by my own hand, is a more effective weapon than any sword in the palace armory — because it opens roads the way nothing else does, and closes mouths even faster. And third, the one I kept turning over like a coin I suspected was clipped: that I had rehearsed this conversation forty times on the ride north, and every rehearsal had ended with me saying something dignified.

Dignity was not going to get me what I wanted. I had finally understood that much. Dignity was what the crown wanted from me. It was what the council wanted, what the protocol masters wanted, what every portrait painter who had ever smoothed my jaw into marble wanted. Dignity was the costume they dressed me in so that I would stand very still while everything I loved was quietly rearranged around me.

So when the two travelers came up the road — a broad-shouldered man with a deserter's careful walk and a woman with ink-stained fingers and a knife at her hip that seemed to bother the air around it — I stood up out of the dark and said the thing I had decided to say instead of the dignified thing.

"I need you to save my husband, and I don't care what it costs, and if you tell anyone I begged I will have you both flayed."

The woman stopped walking. The man's hand went to his sword, which told me he was the sort of guard who had actually guarded things, rather than the sort who wore the uniform to dinners.

"Your Majesty," the woman said slowly. Not a question. She looked at my face in the waystone's thin light, and I watched her decide not to pretend. Good. I was tired of pretending; it was the one luxury I'd had too much of.

"You know me."

"I've seen your portrait in the guild hall," she said. "It doesn't do you justice." A pause. "Or perhaps it does. You look tired."

"I haven't slept properly in four months," I said. "My husband talks in his sleep, and I have been sitting up listening to him talk, because it is the only time he tells the truth anymore."

That landed. I saw it land — a small flinch, quickly mastered, in the woman called Freya Almeida. I had done my research. Everyone in this story had done their research; it was the only honest labor any of us performed.

* * *

Let me go back, because I am told that stories require it, and because there are things about those four months that I have said aloud to no one.

Aldric began talking in his sleep in the spring, shortly after the twelfth anniversary of his coronation. That is not how I would have begun the story once. Once I would have begun with the war councils, or the harvest shortfalls, or the delegation from the salt provinces that arrived angry and left angrier. Those were the official troubles, the ones with documents. But documents were never the trouble. The trouble was smaller and closer and it slept beside me every night and sometimes, in the small hours, it sat up in bed with its eyes open and its breathing wrong and spoke in a voice that was almost Aldric's but pitched slightly too high, the way a musician plays a familiar song a half-step sharp — close enough that your ear accepts it before your mind objects.

The first night it happened I thought it was a nightmare. Kings have nightmares; it comes with the ledgers.

The second night I noticed he was answering questions.

No one was asking them. The room was empty except for me, and I certainly hadn't asked anything. But he lay there with his eyes closed and his mouth moving, and the cadence of it was unmistakable — the pause, the tilt of the head, the little deferential cough he used before disagreeing with someone. He was in conversation. He was being *polite*, in his sleep, to something that did not cast enough presence in the room for me to feel it, and I lay very still and listened, and what I felt was not fear exactly. It was the feeling of standing outside your own house and watching a light move through rooms where no one should be.

I started keeping notes. This is what I am actually good at, whatever the ballads say about queens: I keep records. I wrote down everything he said, in a cipher of my own devising, in a book I hid inside the hollowed-out psalter on my bedside table, because no one has ever suspected a queen of hiding secrets in scripture. We are presumed pious. It is one of our few reliable protections.

The sleep-talk came in fragments. Some of it was nonsense — lists, mostly. Names and numbers, recited in that too-high voice with the patience of an accountant. But some nights it was worse. Some nights it was *Aldric*, the real one, or what was left of him, surfacing like a drowning man breaking water for one gasp before going under again. On those nights he said things like:

*I don't remember signing that.*

*Who was in the room? Why can't I remember who was in the room?*

*Isolde. Isolde, if I forget you, count the bells. Count the bells and I'll find my way back.*

And then, twice, only twice, in a voice that was not his at all:

*He asks after you. Isn't that sweet. He wants to know if you've noticed.*

I burned the page with that one in it. Not because writing it down made it more real, but because I could not bear the thought of some future historian reading it and thinking: *she knew, and she stayed.* As though staying were cowardice. As though leaving the house with the strange light in its windows would save anyone inside it.

Here is what I concluded, and I concluded it the way I conclude everything, alone, at a desk, with cold tea: something was wearing my husband. Not possessing him — the priests' word, and useless, because possession implies a demon and demands an exorcism and both of those come with rituals and witnesses and a paper trail. This was quieter than that. This was a tenancy. Something had moved into my husband the way a squatter moves into a great house — politely at first, using the side entrances, keeping the fires low — and it was gradually rearranging the furniture, and it had been doing so for twelve years, and everyone, including me, had been admiring the new arrangements without asking who had made them.

Because here was the terrible part, the part I could not say to the council, could not say to my confessor, could barely say to myself: the kingdom had never been better governed. Under the sleeping king, the wars had wound down. The granaries filled. The guilds prospered. Whatever wore him was *competent*. It was patient and far-sighted and it made decisions Aldric would never have had the stomach for, and the realm flourished, and I lay awake at night next to my flourishing husband and understood that I was the only person in three kingdoms who wanted the old failure back.

Love against duty, the storybooks call it, and they always mean the same arrangement: the lover wants the wrong thing, and duty stands in the doorway with its arms crossed. No one ever writes the version where duty is the seducer. Where duty says: *he is happy, the realm is happy, you are happy, why break the spell for the sake of a man who wasn't even good at being king?* Duty whispered that to me for months. Duty sounded very reasonable. Duty sounded, in fact, exactly like the voice in my husband's sleep.

That was how I knew it had to be refused. Anything that reasonable, that invested in my contentment, was not on my side.

So: I read the sleep-talk. I followed the fragments. And when a disgraced palace guard named Tam Vess deserted his post six weeks ago — after, I learned, filing a report about the king's eyes that was ordered destroyed and its author reassigned to a border fort he never reached — I began to understand that other people had seen the light in the windows too. And when my agents traced Vess to the north road, traveling with a bone-carver carrying a map sealed with the mark of a cartographer dead eleven years, I did the one thing left that duty forbade and love required.

I put on a servant's cloak, took my husband's seal, and rode out to beg.

* * *

Which brings us back to the waystone, and the woman looking at me with the particular expression of someone deciding how much truth a stranger can carry.

"You've read his sleep-talk," she said. Not a question either. She struck me as a woman who dealt in records herself; we recognized the trade in each other.

"Four months of it," I said. "In cipher. Would you like to hear the worst of it?"

"No," said Tam Vess, at the same moment she said, "Yes."

They looked at each other. Something passed between them — an argument conducted entirely in eyebrows, the kind of fluency that takes either years or weeks depending on how honestly two people have been lying to each other. I envied it. I had not had an eyebrow-conversation with anyone in a decade.

"The worst of it," I said, because I had ridden three hours in the cold and I was going to spend it, "is that he counts bells. In his sleep. When the other voice gets loud, he counts the palace bells, over and over, like a man holding a rope. And last month—" My voice did something I did not authorize it to do. I let it. There was no one here to perform for. "Last month he counted them wrong. He got to nine and then he said, in that voice that isn't his, *there aren't any more bells, my love. You can stop counting now.*"

Silence on the road. Somewhere behind us, an owl asked its idiot question and got no answer.

"And you think we can help him," Freya Almeida said.

"I think you're carrying a map drawn by a dead woman that charts the thirteenth turn of his reign," I said. "I think my husband's eyes changed color the week after his coronation and a guard was silenced for noticing. I think the Vellum Guild — which trades, I remind you, in *impossible things*, a phrase that should have been shut down by its sheer audacity — has spent twelve years and a fortune keeping certain accounts balanced. And I think that whatever is happening to Aldric happens on the thirteenth moon-turn, which is in four turns' time, which means that the woman standing in front of me holding a carving knife that hums is either my husband's last chance or the reason I will be a widow by spring." I pulled my cloak tighter. "So yes. I think you can help him. I think you may be the only ones who can. And I think you're going to try, because you didn't kill me just now, and people who intend to walk away from a dying king don't hesitate the way you hesitated."

"She's right," Tam said to Freya. "You hesitated."

"I hesitated because she insulted my knife," Freya said. Then, to me: "It doesn't hum. It *speaks*. There's a difference, and the difference matters, and if we're going to do this, Your Majesty, you should understand what you're allying yourself with." She held up the knife, and I confess I leaned away from it, because the air around the blade moved the way air moves above a candle — a shimmer, a suggestion of warmth where there was none. "There's a dead cartographer in this steel. Her name was Maren. She drew your husband's reign like a disease chart, turn by turn, and she died getting that chart out of the capital. Everything I know, I know because the dead won't leave me alone. Including, probably, soon, your husband. If he dies before the thirteenth turn, he'll be in here too. With the rest of them."

I looked at the knife for a long moment. A queen learns to hear what isn't said; it is most of the job. What wasn't said, in that flat bitter recitation, was: *and I am so tired, and I have lost everything to this, and I am still walking north.*

"Then we want the same thing," I said.

"We want versions of it," she said. "You want your husband back. Maren wants the crown broken. The guild wants—" She stopped. Her jaw tightened in a way that told me the guild wanted something, and that the wanting had teeth marks in it.

"The guild," I said carefully, "has been very generous to the crown these past twelve years."

"I know," she said. "I kept their books."

Ah. There it was. I had wondered, riding north, what kind of woman carries a dead woman's map toward a living king, and the answer was: a bookkeeper. Someone who had spent years making the impossible balance on paper and had finally found a column that wouldn't sum. I almost pitied her. I say *almost* because pity between women in our position is a currency best hoarded; we'd likely both need it later.

"Then you'll understand," I said, "when I tell you that I cannot go back to the capital openly. If the guild knows I've met you, they will know what I suspect, and the moment they know what I suspect, Aldric stops counting bells and starts—" I stopped myself. Some sentences you don't finish even in the dark, even to strangers, because finishing them makes them load-bearing.

"Starts forgetting you entirely," Freya said quietly. "Yes. We've read the map. We know how it works. Each turn, it takes more of him. By the thirteenth, there's nothing left to take."

"Then we have four turns," I said. "And I have one request before we go anywhere."

Tam's hand hadn't left his sword-belt since I'd stood up. "Name it."

"When we reach the capital — and we will reach it, because I have a seal that opens gates and a face that opens doors faster — you do not treat me as a queen. You treat me as a wife. Can you do that? Because the queen cannot be seen to conspire against her own consort's government. The queen must smile at the council and pour the wine and wear the marble face. But the wife—" I heard my own voice drop, and I let it, because the cloak was off now, all of them, the cloaks were off. "The wife can stand in a bedroom at three in the morning with a notebook full of cipher and listen to the man she married count bells against the dark. And the wife is done listening. The wife intends to *act*."

Freya Almeida looked at me for a long moment. Then she did something no one had done in eleven years of my reign: she laughed — short, startled, real.

"Corvane always said royalty was just grief with better tailoring," she said. "He'd have liked you. That's practically a threat."

"Your mentor?"

"My mentor." Her hand closed around the knife. "And possibly my enemy. Possibly yours. Possibly everyone's. Which brings us to the actual problem, Your Majesty, which is that we're being followed, and I don't think it's the guild."

We turned together. Down the road, where the light failed, the rider who had waited at the waystone behind us had turned their horse toward us and was coming on at an unhurried trot — unhurried the way winter is unhurried, the way debt is unhurried. And as it came close enough for the last grey light to find it, I saw the rider's livery, and my stomach performed a slow, dignified revolution.

Palace colors. My husband's household guard — or what served in place of it.

"Not the guild," I breathed. "Then who—"

"I am," said the rider, pulling up, throwing back her hood, and I knew the face under it, because I had promoted her myself two years ago, because she was the finest officer in the household guard, because I had trusted her with everything except the truth. Captain Sera Vail looked down at the three of us, and her expression was the worst thing I had seen in four months of bad nights: not menace. *Relief.*

"Your Majesty," she said. "Thank the gods. I've been trying to reach you for a week. They watch you day and night now, and I couldn't get near you, so I followed the only thread they couldn't cut." She nodded at Freya's satchel, at the rolled map inside it. "The king sent me."

The road went very quiet.

"In his sleep?" Freya asked.

Captain Vail shook her head, and her voice, when it came, was steady in the way of someone who has decided to be steady or die of it.

"No, mistress carver. Awake. Yesterday morning, at his dressing, while the chamberlain droned on about the Night of Bells — he looked straight at me, and his eyes were *his*, both of them, brown as riverwater, and he said: *Vail, find my wife. Tell her the bells still work. Tell her I'm still counting.*"
