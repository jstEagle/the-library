## 12. Corvane's Version

They did not drug me. That was the first insult.

I woke in a room I knew — the counting room above the guild's salt cellar, where I had spent eleven years balancing impossibilities against each other until they came out even. My own chair. My own desk. Someone had even set out my good lamp, the one with the whale-oil wick that burned without smoke. A cup of tea sat at my elbow, still steaming.

"You always did like it too sweet," Corvane said from the shadows by the window. "I had them use three sugars. Forgive me if I've let it go cold."

My wrists were bound to the arms of my chair with vellum strips. Not rope — vellum, cured and inked with script I recognized as my own hand from years ago, ledger-entries repurposed into restraints. The guild wasted nothing, least of all its traitors' work.

"Corvane." My voice came out steadier than I felt. "You've redecorated."

"I've tidied. There's a difference." He stepped into the lamplight, and I saw how much the last weeks had cost him. Guildmaster Corvane had always been a man built like a well-kept ledger — everything in columns, nothing wasted, nothing bleeding over the margins. Now there were smudges under his eyes and his hands, those precise hands that could mend a torn charter so no auditor would ever find the seam, trembled slightly when he folded them behind his back.

He was afraid. That frightened me more than the bindings did.

"You killed a courier," I said.

"The courier was dying when he reached your dock. A knife through the lung does that. I merely failed to save him, which is a different sin and a smaller one." He pulled the second chair — Tam's old chair, though Tam had never existed when that chair last had an occupant — and sat across from me, close enough that our knees nearly touched. "You burned my ledgers, Freya."

"Our ledgers."

"No." He said it gently, the way you'd correct a child who'd miscounted. "Mine. You kept them beautifully, but they were never yours. That is not an accusation. It is simply the shape of things. You were the finest instrument this guild has ever produced, and instruments do not own the music."

"And here I thought I was family."

"You are." Something moved behind his eyes — not quite pain, but its accountant. "That is why we're having tea instead of what Ondine suggested."

* * *

He let the silence sit for a while. Corvane had taught me that silence was a tool like any other: it cost nothing, weighed nothing, and broke most people before any question needed asking. I had learned it at his elbow. I refused to be broken by my own inheritance twice in one lifetime.

So I looked around the room instead. My room. The shelf where I kept the tokens of dead sellers whose voices had hummed through my knife — empty now, of course; I'd taken Maren's map and run, but someone had cleared the rest. On the wall, the framed first page of my first ledger, the one Corvane had hung there himself, ink still wet, saying: *A record honestly kept is the only honest thing a merchant ever makes.*

"You took down nothing," I observed. "You're keeping my shrine dusted."

"I'm hoping you'll come back and need it."

"That's either touching or sinister."

"It's usually both." He leaned forward. "Tell me what the cartographer told you. All of it. Not because I don't know — I want to know what she emphasized. Dead women editorialize."

"Maren," I said, and watched him flinch at the name. Good. Let him flinch. "She told me about the crown. About the binding. Thirteen moon-turns, and whatever wears Aldric's face stops borrowing it and owns it outright. Four turns left, Corvane. Maybe fewer now. I lost time being carried here in a crate."

"A crate," he said, "with air holes. And a cushion. You were unconscious for two days on a river barge, not rotting in a hold. I signed for the cushion personally."

"You're very proud of your atrocities' upholstery."

"My atrocities." He laughed, short and dry, with no humor in it anywhere. "Yes. Let's talk about my atrocities. That is, in fact, why you're here."

* * *

What he did then surprised me. He didn't argue. He opened a ledger.

It was an old one — older than me, older than my mother, the spine cracked and re-sewn twice. He laid it on the desk between us and turned pages with the reverence other men reserved for scripture. "Do you know what the kingdom looked like twenty-two years ago?" he asked.

"Stable. Prosperous. I've seen the commemorative coins."

"You've seen the coins minted afterward, yes." His finger stopped on a page dense with entries in a cramped, furious hand. "Here. Grain riots in the south, three provinces. Here — the eastern lords arming their household troops beyond legal limits. Here — the western fleet refusing to answer royal summons. Here, here, and here — assassinations. Not of the king. Of tax collectors, magistrates, anyone who represented the crown's reach. Eleven in one summer. The kingdom wasn't on the edge of civil war, Freya. It was in the antechamber, waiting to be announced."

"I know my history."

"You know the version printed after. This—" he tapped the page, "—is the version invoiced. Every entry a payment, a shipment, a delivery. Do you see what the Vellum Guild actually traded in? Not impossible things. Plausible ones. We sold stability to people who couldn't afford to buy it openly. When the crown needed grain that didn't exist, we found grain. When it needed a lord's death to look like illness, we found the illness. And when it needed a king who would not break—"

He turned the page, and there it was. An entry in my mother's hand.

I had known it was coming. Maren had told me. But knowing a thing and seeing your dead mother's handwriting describing it are different animals entirely, and the second one bites.

*One (1) binding vessel, coronet form,* she had written. *Materials per attached schedule. Delivery upon completion of thirteenth proof. Note: subject's tolerance exceeds projection. Recommend maintenance schedule of annual adjustment by principal carver.*

Principal carver. Her. And after her —

"After her, you," Corvane said quietly. "You didn't know that part yet, did you. I can see it land. Yes, Freya. Every year, on the anniversary of the coronation, a package arrived at this guild. Bone blanks, cut to specification. And every year, you carved them — small charms, decorative pieces, you thought — and every year they went to the palace and were fitted into the crown's inner band. You have maintained that binding for nine years. Your hands have been in that king's head since you were nineteen years old."

The tea had gone cold. I stared at it anyway because looking at Corvane was not survivable just then.

"I didn't know," I said. Which sounded exactly as useless as it was.

"No. You didn't. I made certain of it, because a carver who knows what she's carving starts asking questions, and questions are how guilds die." He sat back. "Now ask the important one."

I hated him for making me say it. I asked it anyway. "Why?"

"Because the alternative was fire." He said it without drama, which somehow made it worse. "Twenty-two years ago I stood where you stand now, holding the same choice, and I ran the numbers. Civil war in a kingdom of four million souls. Best estimates — and I employed the best estimators money could buy — put the dead at six hundred thousand in the first five years, before plague and famine finished the arithmetic. Six hundred thousand, Freya. Against one man. One man's continuity of self, weighed against six hundred thousand continuities of everything. I chose. I have never pretended otherwise, and I have never slept poorly over it, and if that makes me a monster then monsters should be given ledgers, because we keep excellent records."

"You sacrificed him."

"I stabilized a nation. The king agreed."

That stopped me. "He what?"

"He agreed." Corvane's voice softened into something almost gentle. "Aldric was thirty years old, childless, brotherless, and watching his kingdom eat itself. He came to us. Do you understand? We didn't sneak into his bedchamber with a crown. He walked into this guild, sat in that chair you're sitting in, and asked what we could do. The binding doesn't take the unwilling — that's not mercy, it's mechanics. A resisted binding frays. It required his consent, freely given, and he gave it with full knowledge of the terms. He read the contract, Freya. He initialed every page. I still have it."

"You're lying."

"Am I?" He reached into his coat and produced a document, folded in thirds, sealed with the royal signet. He set it on the desk but did not open it. "You may read it. Or you may continue believing the story where I'm a villain and you're innocent, whichever is more comfortable. But comfort was never what this guild sold."

* * *

I read it.

I wish I could tell you I found a flaw — a coerced signature, a clause hidden in the marginalia, some technicality my eleven years of ledger-keeping would have caught. I found none. The hand was Aldric's — younger, steadier, but his. The terms were laid out in plain language, which is the cruelest way to lay out terrible terms: the binding vessel, the thirteen-turn deepening, the gradual transfer of will, the dissolution of the original self upon completion. Signed. Witnessed. Initialed on every page, in the manner of a man who wanted no ambiguity left for anyone to exploit later.

Including a clause in the final paragraph that Corvane had not mentioned:

*Should the bearer, at any point prior to the thirteenth turn, clearly and repeatedly express revocation of consent, the guild is bound to attempt unbinding, at whatever cost.*

"Attempt," I said.

"Unbinding has never been attempted," Corvane said. "Because until three weeks ago, no one believed it necessary. The king has been content. Content, Freya. He rules well. The provinces are quiet. The granaries are full. Whatever wears his face governs with his values, because it wears his memories, and the kingdom has never been healthier. So I ask you — I am genuinely asking, not rhetorically — what exactly is it that you're trying to save?"

And there it was. The seduction. Not of power, not of safety — of reasonableness. He had built his argument the way I built a ledger: every entry documented, every figure cross-checked, the whole thing balanced so perfectly that disagreeing felt like bad arithmetic rather than bad ethics.

I thought about Tam, who had seen the king's eyes change and been ordered silenced for noticing. I thought about the village of Bone and Ash, where Ondine had cut the dead away from their voices and called it peace. I thought about Maren, dead these twenty years, spending her ghost's last strength to move ink across a map because someone had to say: *this was wrong, and I helped, and it must stop.*

But mostly I thought about the clause. *Clearly and repeatedly express revocation.* Because I had heard something, hadn't I? In the palace ossuary, in the whispering dark, among ten thousand patient bones — I had heard the dead speak my name like a key, and beneath their voices, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, something else. A rhythm. A man's breathing, uneven, fighting its own sleep. Hours of his reign he could not account for. Waking nightly with dread he mistook for indigestion.

A man losing the argument with his own reflection does not sign contracts willingly. A man signs them once, at thirty, terrified, and spends the rest of his erasure trying to remember why.

"He's changed his mind," I said.

"You can't know that."

"I can hear it." I lifted my chin. "That's the thing about my gift, Corvane — the one you never understood, for all your ledgers. Bones don't lie. They don't editorialize, they don't negotiate, they don't offer cushions with air holes. And the bone at the center of that crown — the keystone piece, the one my mother carved and I have maintained for nine years — it's been screaming for months. Not the binding. Him. What's left of him. He wants out."

Corvane was quiet for a long moment. Then he did the last thing I expected.

He nodded.

"I believe you," he said. "I've believed you since the courier died saying 'thirteen turns.' Why do you think I brought you here instead of letting Ondine have you?" He rose and walked to the window, and when he spoke again his voice had the careful flatness of a man reading figures aloud. "Here is my version, then, since you've heard everyone else's. The binding is fraying — not because it was evil, but because it was built in haste by brilliant, frightened people, and maintained by a girl who didn't know what she was maintaining. In four turns it fails. Not completes — fails. The thing inside him isn't a patient tenant anymore; it's a squatter planning arson. When it takes him fully, it won't rule with his values. It will rule with his armies. And I am an old man who made one bargain twenty-two years ago and has spent every year since paying its interest, and I am telling you, Freya, with nothing left to gain: I cannot fix it alone. But you —"

He turned. His eyes were wet, and I believed the tears, and I hated myself for believing them.

"— you were trained for this without knowing it. Your mother built the lock. You hold the key's shape in your hands. Help me perfect it — rebuild the binding clean, consensual, reversible. Give the king back his door. Don't shatter the crown and loose whatever's been feeding in there for twenty-two years onto a kingdom that remembers what civil war costs. Mend it. You're the only mender alive."

It was a good argument. God help me, it was a good argument, and he meant it, and that was the worst part — that I stood in my own chair in my own room, bound in my own ink, being offered the chance to save everyone by the man who raised me, and some treacherous part of me wanted to say yes just to make the numbers come out even again.

I looked at the contract on the desk. At the clause he'd hoped I wouldn't weigh too heavily.

*Clearly and repeatedly express revocation.*

"Here's my version," I said. "You'll unbind me from this chair. You'll give me back my knife and my map. And you'll open the guild's archives to me — everything on the binding, every proof, every maintenance record — because if I'm going to mend anything, I need to see the whole lock. Those are my terms."

Hope flickered across his face, quick and naked. "And the crown?"

"The crown gets mended or it gets broken," I said. "Whichever sets him free. That clause is in your contract too, Guildmaster. You just haven't read it lately."

For a moment — one long, teetering moment — I thought he would agree. I saw him weigh it, the old accountant weighing the only asset he had left against the only debt he'd never audited.

Then the door opened, and Ondine stepped through it, and behind her, in the corridor, I heard the sound of vellum strips being cut from someone else's wrists.

"They've taken Tam," she said, and her voice was gentle as a scalpel. "Both of you, in the archive, by dawn. Or neither of you sees it at all."
