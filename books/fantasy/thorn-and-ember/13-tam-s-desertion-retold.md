## 13. Tam's Desertion, Retold

They took my sword and my boots but left me my belt, which told me everything I needed to know about the Vellum Guild's competence with prisoners. A man can hang himself with a belt. He can pick a lock with the buckle. He can do a great many things with a belt that have nothing to do with holding up trousers, and the fact that nobody thought to take mine said either that they were amateurs or that they wanted me alive only until they didn't.

I was still working out which when they threw Freya into my cell.

Not threw, exactly. They guided her in with a firmness that stopped short of cruelty, the way you'd handle something valuable that had misbehaved. She landed on her feet anyway — she always lands on her feet, it's one of the things about her — and looked around the cell the way an accountant looks at a fraudulent ledger: cataloguing, unimpressed, already planning corrections.

"Tam." Her voice was flat. "You're here."

"You sound surprised."

"I'm not surprised. I'm *annoyed*. There's a difference." She sat down against the opposite wall, as far from me as the cell allowed, which was perhaps six feet of damp stone and a bucket neither of us acknowledged. "I had a plan."

"We all had plans. Mine involved not being captured."

"And how did that go?"

"It went fine right up until it didn't."

She almost smiled. Almost. It was there and gone like a fish under ice, and then her face went back to that careful blankness she wears the way other people wear cloaks. We sat in silence for a while, listening to the prison breathe. The cells beneath the Guildhall were older than the guild itself — some forgotten noble's wine cellar, repurposed — and the stones sweated cold like they were embarrassed about something.

"They didn't search you properly," she said finally.

"No?"

"Your boot knife is in your sock."

"That's where I keep it."

"In your *sock*, Tam."

"It's called concealment. Look it up."

She shook her head, but something in her shoulders came down half an inch. That's the thing about Freya Almeida that took me too long to understand: she doesn't relax. She recalibrates. Every conversation is an audit, every silence an entry in some ledger only she can read. When she'd decided I wasn't the guild's creature sent to worm confidences out of her, she filed me somewhere safer, and I felt the reclassification happen like weather changing.

"So," she said. "Corvane's version."

"Of what?"

"Of everything. Of why the crown exists, why the king needs binding, why sacrificing one man for ten thousand is arithmetic instead of murder." She pulled her knees up. "He gave me the whole sermon. And the terrible thing, Tam, the genuinely terrible thing, is that it's a good sermon. I stood there wanting to believe him because believing him would mean I hadn't spent my whole life serving monsters. Just men who make hard choices. There's a difference between those two things, isn't there? Tell me there's a difference."

"There's a difference," I said.

"How would you know?"

"Because I used to be a man who made hard choices. And then I became something else, and I remember exactly what it cost to cross over." I leaned my head back against the stone. "You want to know why I deserted? You've never asked, you know. Weeks on the road together and you never once asked."

"I assumed you'd tell me if you wanted to."

"That's the most Freya thing anyone has ever said to me."

* * *

Here is what I told her, and I'll tell it now the way I told it then, without decoration, because decoration is how liars survive and I was tired of surviving that way.

Three years in the King's Guard. Three years of standing at doors and looking impressive and eating better than I deserved. I was good at it. That's not bragging; guarding is mostly a talent for boredom, and I have a genius for boredom. You stand at a door for eight hours and you learn the sounds of a palace the way a shepherd learns sheep — which footstep is the chamberlain's, which creak means the cats are fighting, which silence means someone is doing something they shouldn't.

King Aldric was a good man. I want that on the record before anything else, because history is going to be written by people who never smelled the tallow smoke in his hallways. He remembered names. Not just lords' names — scullery boys', grooms'. He asked after my mother once, having heard her mentioned, and when I told him she'd died he apologized for asking, which no king has ever done to any guard in the history of kingdoms. He carried the weight of the crown like a man carries a wounded friend: awkwardly, stubbornly, refusing to put it down.

The thirteenth moon-turn of his reign was coming. Nobody marked it. Why would they? Reigns are counted in years.

It started small. The king began losing hours.

I noticed it before the queen did, because noticing is my job and my affliction. He'd dismiss his council at dusk and then, come morning, ask why no decisions had been made about the grain tariffs. He signed documents twice — same signature, same hand, hours apart, no memory of the second. Once I found him standing in the portrait gallery at three in the morning, perfectly still, staring at his grandfather's likeness, and when I spoke his name he turned around with an expression I will see when I close my eyes for the rest of my life, however long that turns out to be.

It wasn't confusion. Confusion I could have handled. Confusion is human. This was *recalibration* — a fraction of a heartbeat where his face was nothing at all, a blank slate being written upon, and then the king surfaced again, smiling, saying something mild about insomnia, and the smile fit his face the way a borrowed coat fits: correctly, and not at all.

I reported it. That's the part Corvane will have left out, so let me say it clearly: I did my duty. I wrote it down in the incident log, discreet language, *His Majesty appears fatigued, irregular sleep patterns observed*, and I delivered it to the captain of the guard, who delivered it upward, and for six days nothing happened, and on the seventh day I was summoned to a room I'd never been in before, below the armory, where a courteous old man with ink-stained fingers offered me wine.

Guildmaster Corvane.

I didn't know who he was then. I know now that the Vellum Guild has its fingers in more pies than a baker with a death wish, including, apparently, royal pies. He was kind. That's what I need you to understand, Freya, because you grew up inside his kindness and maybe you've gone numb to its texture: the man is genuinely kind. He listened to my report. He thanked me for my vigilance. He said the crown was aware of certain... stresses upon His Majesty, medical matters, delicate ones, and that the wisest service I could render was to amend my log.

"To say what?" I asked him.

"To say nothing. To report that further observation revealed ordinary fatigue. To let the matter rest."

"And if I don't?"

And here's where a smarter man would tell you he saw the threat coming, that he braced himself, that honor held firm. What actually happened was that Corvane sighed — a real sigh, tired, almost paternal — and said, "Then we will have to conclude that the stress of palace service has affected your judgment, Ser Tam, and arrange a leave of absence from which guards do not always return. Your mother raised you alone, I believe. After your father. In Saltmere. It would be a shame if grief found her before her time."

They knew my mother's name. My dead mother's name, in a guild ledger somewhere, filed between impossible things.

I amended the log. God forgive me, I amended the log. I wrote *ordinary fatigue* in my own handwriting and I hated myself with a purity of hatred I hadn't known I was capable of, and I went back to my door, and I stood there, and I watched.

Because that's the thing about a man with a genius for boredom. I kept watching.

Over the following weeks I watched the king lose more hours. I watched the queen start sleeping in the antechamber and telling everyone it was the heat. I watched new faces arrive — quiet men with calloused hands and no livery, who walked the corridors at night measuring things, doorways and windowsills, like carpenters. I watched a delivery of white bone enter the palace through the armory gate at midnight, crates of it, packed in straw, and I watched it go up to the king's chambers and come down again as nothing visible at all.

I understood eventually that whatever was happening to Aldric wasn't illness. Illness doesn't get installed.

And I understood something else, the night I finally ran. The king summoned me — me specifically, out of forty guards — to walk with him in the garden past midnight. He talked about nothing for a while. Weather, harvests, the scandalous price of falcons. And then he stopped by the fountain and gripped my arm hard enough to bruise and said, in a voice that was entirely, desperately his own:

"Vess. If ever I seem to forget you — if ever I look at you and don't know your name — promise me you'll remember it for both of us. Promise me somebody will be keeping count."

That was the last thing Aldric said to me as a free man. Maybe the last thing he ever said as himself at all.

I reported it again — the garden, the words, everything — and this time I didn't write it in any log. I went straight to the queen's private secretary, because if the guild owned the guard, maybe they hadn't gotten to her yet. The secretary listened very carefully, thanked me very graciously, and had me arrested within the hour. For desertion-in-planning, they called it. Treason-adjacent. A charge designed to end quietly.

I broke out of that cell the same way I'll break out of this one, and I ran, and I've been running since, carrying the knowledge that the only man who ever asked after my mother is being eaten alive in a tower and I told the world he was *tired*.

* * *

Freya didn't speak for a long time after I finished. The cell had gotten darker; somewhere above us, a bell counted an hour neither of us cared about.

"You amended the log," she said at last. Not accusing. Testing the weight of it, the way she tests bone.

"I did."

"And then you kept watching."

"I did."

"Those are both true at the same time."

"That's the whole problem with being a person, Freya. Everything true is true at the same time."

She laughed — short, surprised out of her, immediately regretted. Then, quieter: "My mother carved the mold for that crown. Maren showed me. The marks match mine. Corvane trained me to maintain it, or finish it, or — he never quite said which, and I think the not-saying was the point." Her hands were in her lap, and she was looking at them like they belonged to a stranger who'd been writing checks against her account. "So you were the guard who lied in a log, and I was the carver who kept the ledgers of a guild that sells impossibilities, and we both sat inside the machine and called it loyalty."

"Called it a paycheck, in my case."

"Don't ruin my moment of moral clarity with accuracy."

But she was smiling, actually smiling now, and the cell got less cold, or I imagined it did, which amounts to the same thing in a place like this. There's a particular intimacy in confession between two people who've each been holding their breath for years. You exhale together and the room changes.

"Why didn't you ever ask me?" I said. "About the desertion. All those weeks."

"Because I heard the dead talk about you."

That stopped me. "Come again?"

"The charms I carve hum with voices. Sellers, couriers, the occasional ghost with opinions about road conditions. Someone along the north road mentioned a deserter who paid for a ferry with a royal signet ring and then apologized to the ferryman for the state of the realm." She shrugged, elaborate and fake-casual. "I decided anyone who apologizes to ferrymen on behalf of kingdoms probably has a reason."

"A ghost vouched for me."

"Technically a dead toll-collector."

"My mother would be proud. She always said I'd go far, though I don't think she meant 'far from the law.'"

Silence again, but the companionable kind now, the kind that fills up rather than empties out. I found myself studying her in the gloom — the sharp line of her jaw, the ink stain on her knuckle she'd never managed to fully scrub, the way she sat like someone ready to stand. I'd been traveling beside this woman for weeks and I realized I'd been doing it the way you lean into a wind: not deciding to, just discovering you can't stand up straight without it.

"Freya."

"Don't," she said softly.

"You don't even know what—"

"I know exactly what. We're in a cell. One of us may die tomorrow, possibly both, and people say things in cells that the daylight has to carry afterward, and I don't know if I can carry—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Started again, smaller. "Say it anyway. If you mean it. I'd rather have it and be frightened than not have it and wonder."

So I said it. Quietly, the way you admit to a crime: "I think I love you. Have for a while. Since the village with the silent charms, maybe, when you cried for strangers' dead and pretended it was fog in your eyes. Possibly since the ferry. I'm bad at knowing when things started. I'm only sure they did."

She was quiet long enough that I started composing retractions. Then:

"I audit everything," she said. "Voices, ledgers, people. I trust numbers because they can't lie to me. But I've been keeping a private column, these past weeks, and every entry in it is you, and I can't balance it, and for the first time in my life I don't want to." A breath. "There. Logged."

"Logged."

"Don't make it weird, Vess."

"Wouldn't dream of it."

We sat with it, the admission, letting it be small and enormous at once. Somewhere in the walls water dripped with metronome patience. My sock-knife waited. And I was thinking — I admit it, I was already thinking about belts and buckles and the acoustics of corridor patrols — when footsteps came down the stair, unhurried, familiar.

A key turned. The door opened on Guildmaster Corvane, lantern-lit, courteous, flanked by two enforcers whose faces I remembered from the palace corridors at night.

"Ser Vess," he said warmly. "And Freya. I do apologize for the accommodations. I find that important conversations go better underground." He stepped inside, and behind him, in the corridor, I heard something I hadn't heard since the night I fled: the dry whisper of many bones being carried on many trays, moving toward the deep levels of the Guildhall.

"Tomorrow," Corvane said, "you'll both accompany me to the ossuary. There are old friends of yours down there, Freya. And as for you, Ser Vess—" he smiled, patient as arithmetic "—I understand you're a man who keeps count. Come and see what we've been counting."

The door closed. The lock turned.

Freya's hand found mine in the dark, brief and hard, and I felt the knife in her sleeve that the enforcers had somehow failed to find — or had been allowed not to find.

"Belt buckle," she breathed. "Left side. Tell me you still have yours."

I did. And above us, faint through the stone, the bones began to murmur.
