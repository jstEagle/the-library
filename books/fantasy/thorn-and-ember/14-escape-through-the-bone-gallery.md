## 14. Escape Through the Bone Gallery

The lock on our cell was iron, and iron is honest work for a locksmith and useless work for a bone-carver. But the palace, in its infinite wisdom, had given us a bench of carved oak, and oak grows around things — nails, hinges, and once, in a chapel I'd visited as a girl, the finger bones of a saint who'd been buried standing up because no one could afford to lay him down.

I pried the nail out with my knife and held it in my palm until it stopped being a nail.

"You're doing the thing," Tam said from the far wall. "The thing where you go somewhere I can't follow."

"I'm asking a favor."

"Of a nail."

"Of whoever died holding it." The wood remembered hands. Wood always remembers; it just doesn't gossip the way bone does. This nail had been driven by a joiner named Aldous, who'd been proud of his joints and lonely in his marriage, and who would have been delighted — genuinely delighted — to be useful one more time. "Aldous," I said, "the door hinge is two feet to your left. Be kind to it."

The nail bent itself into a hook shape, slid through the gap under the door, walked up the wood like an insect, and turned the simple latch from inside.

Tam stared at me. He'd seen me talk to relics before, on the road, but there is a difference between watching a woman murmur at a charm and watching furniture commit treason.

"Aldous," I said, retrieving my nail. "He built doors for forty years. He says most of them deserved better than what people did behind them."

"Is he — does he mind? Being used?"

"He's bored, Tam. He's been bored since 1189." I put my ear to the door. Two guards, by their breathing — one young and chewing something, one older and pretending not to be asleep. Corvane hadn't wasted his best people on us. Why would he? We were the trap's bait, not its prize. "Stay behind me. And whatever you hear down there, don't apologize to anyone. It slows them down."

"Down where?"

"The ossuary." I'd felt it the moment they brought us into this wing — a pull in my teeth, low and vast, like standing near the sea. Every old palace kept its dead somewhere, and the Vellum Guild had helped furnish this one for generations. Bones sold as relics, bones bought back as favors, bones stored against future need. Hundreds of them. Thousands.

All of them silent, because Ondine had passed through the capital last spring, and all of them *there*, and I was done being polite about the difference between hearing the dead and freeing them.

* * *

The corridor outside was torchlit and empty in the way corridors are only empty right before they aren't. We went left, away from the stairs, following the pull in my teeth, past storerooms smelling of tallow and dust, until we reached a door banded in bronze and marked with the guild's seal — the open ledger, the quill crossed over it like a sword.

"They gave the crypts to your guild?" Tam whispered.

"We gave ourselves the crypts. Kings are grateful for strange things when you save them from stranger ones." I set my hand flat on the bronze. Beneath it, faint as breath on a winter window, something stirred. Not a voice yet. A turning-over in sleep. "Corvane keeps his inventory here. Everything the guild has ever silenced and shelved."

"That's monstrous."

"That's bookkeeping," I said, and hated how easily the word came, how many years I'd said exactly that and meant nothing by it. "Come on."

The lock was guild-work, which meant it wasn't a lock at all but a courtesy — a token-slot that wanted a carved bone key. I didn't have one. I had my knife, and my knife had Maren in it, and Maren had spent her death learning every trick the guild ever cut into bone.

*Ask it who it thinks it's locking out,* she said, dry as chalk dust.

I pressed the knife's edge to the slot and let her speak through the steel — not words, exactly, but the *shape* of authority, the particular cold signature of a master-carver's mark. The lock hesitated. Locks are made by carvers, and carvers are vain, and vanity recognizes its betters even posthumously.

It opened.

Beyond was a stair going down into air so still it felt held, the way you hold your breath passing a sickroom. And then the Bone Gallery opened around us, and I forgot, briefly, how to walk.

It wasn't a crypt. Crypts are humble. This was a library. Niches rose three stories into the dark, each one fitted with shelves of labeled bone — femurs filed under parish, skulls sorted by decade, small boxes of hand-bones catalogued in Corvane's own cramped script. Candles burned in niches at intervals without flickering, because there was no air moving anywhere in this place, because nothing in here had needed to breathe for a long time.

And it was quiet. Not empty-quiet. *Silenced*-quiet. I could feel them the way you feel a word stuck behind your teeth — thousands of presences pressing against a door that wouldn't open, that had been nailed shut from the outside by someone who called it mercy.

"Oh," Tam said softly. "Oh, this is — how many?"

I ran my fingers along the nearest shelf. Labels. Dates. Causes of silence, in guild shorthand: *voluntary, contracted, adjudicated.* Adjudicated. As if the dead had stood trial.

"Enough," I said. "Tam. When I start, it's going to be loud, and it's going to be strange, and some of what comes out of these bones is going to be ugly. People don't die tidy. Do you understand me?"

"What do I do?"

"Guard the stairs. And keep count of the candles. When they start going out, Corvane knows we're here."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about silencing the dead: it isn't removal. Nothing is ever removed. Ondine's work — the guild's work, my mother's work, my work, if I was honest, because I'd carved maintenance tokens for twenty years without asking what they maintained — Ondine's work was a *seam*. A stitch of carved bone laid across the voice, neat as tailoring. And every seam can be picked.

I took down the nearest skull — a woman, sixty or so, jaw wired shut with silver thread — and I set my knife against the little bone charm fused to her temple, the silencer's stitch, and I cut.

Not flesh. Never flesh. I cut the seam.

The sound that came out of her was not a scream. It was a name. Her own, spoken like a woman remembering where she'd left it — *Marget, Marget Alsen, I am Marget Alsen* — and then everything else, forty years of everything else, pouring out into the dark: her husband's cough, the price of bread, the flood year, her daughter's laugh, her own mother's voice saying *come in from the rain, Marget, you'll catch your death,* and she had, she had caught it, and she had *so much to say—*

The next niche. The next seam. My knife moved like it had been waiting its whole life for this work, and maybe it had, maybe every carving I'd ever done was rehearsal for the picking-apart, the way a lockpick is a key turned inside out.

"Freya," Tam said from the stairs, and his voice had gone strange.

I looked up. The candles were guttering — not going out, but bending, all of them, toward me, as if the whole gallery were inhaling. And along the shelves, in the niches, in the labeled dark, the dead were waking in sequence, a wave rolling outward from Marget Alsen's skull, voices catching and joining and rising, hundreds of them, thousands, and underneath all of it, threading through it, one sound repeated:

*Freya. Freya Almeida.*

Not pleading. Not accusing, though some of them had every right. Saying it the way you say a password. The way you say *key.*

Because that's what I was. That's what I had always been, and I had spent my life thinking the gift was a wound when it was a door, and doors don't apologize for what walks through them.

"Freya Almeida," said a voice near the ceiling, an old man's, amused. "She's come. She's finally come to let us out."

"Freya," said another, closer, a child's, and I flinched, and the child said, gently, "No — don't be sorry. You came. That's the whole of it. You came."

My eyes were burning. There was work to do. I cut the next seam, and the next, working down the row while the gallery filled with the noise of the unburied living — arguments resuming mid-sentence after decades, greetings shouted across the aisles, someone singing, badly, a harvest song — and above it all my name, my name, my name, passed hand to hand like bread.

Then the first candle went out.

* * *

"Freya!" Tam again, sharp now. "Boots on the stair. A lot of boots."

"How many is a lot?"

"All of them."

I looked down the endless aisle of niches, at the thousands of seams still intact, and made the arithmetic of cowardice and discarded it. No time to free them all. Time to free enough.

"Marget!" I called. "Marget Alsen!"

"I'm here, love." She sounded, impossibly, like someone settling into a chair.

"Can you reach the others? Can you show them the seam?"

A pause. Then, rippling outward through the dark, dry laughter — the laughter of the dead discovering a joke at the living's expense. *Show them.* As if the stitch were complicated. As if they hadn't all lain here feeling it, every day, knowing precisely what had been done to them and by whom and with which tools.

The sound that followed was not chaos, though the enforcers coming down the stairs would remember it as chaos for the rest of their lives. It was organization. It was ten thousand voices arriving at a single decision, and the decision was *out.*

Niches began opening themselves. Bones moved — not walking, nothing so theatrical, but shifting, tumbling, a skull rolling from its shelf to crack against the floor and keep talking, unhurt, unbothered, furious and joyful at once. Hand-bones skittered across flagstones like crabs. The enforcers reached the bottom of the stair into a gallery that had decided, collectively, to become a weather event.

I grabbed Tam's arm. "Now. While they're busy apologizing to the floor."

We ran up the aisle opposite the guards, through falling candle-smoke and rising song, and the dead made way for us — actually made way, bones drawing aside, a corridor clearing through the storm of themselves — and everywhere I passed they said my name, softly now, a tide going out, and I understood that they weren't calling me. They were *remembering* me forward. Marking the path. A woman named Marget Alsen, who had waited thirty years in a labeled box to say her own name, shouted the last thing I heard as we reached the stair:

"Freya Almeida! Tell them what we cost! Tell them we were *people!*"

"I will," I said, and meant it as a vow, and the gallery roared its answer behind us, and we climbed.

* * *

We came out of the ossuary into a corridor full of smoke and shouting, and I allowed myself four steps of pure triumphant running before the world reminded me that victories have invoices.

They came from the cross-corridor — six enforcers, guild-trained, and at their center a figure in grey whose face I knew better than my own reflection, because I'd spent twenty years studying it for approval.

Ondine.

She wasn't surprised. That was the worst of it. She stood in the torchlight with her hands folded like a woman at a funeral she'd personally arranged, and she looked at me — at the knife in my hand, at the bone dust on my sleeves, at the sound still bleeding faintly up through the floorboards beneath our feet — and she said, with genuine sorrow:

"You've woken them."

"Every one I could reach."

"And how many will sleep again because of tonight?" She shook her head slowly. "Freya. Sister. I taught you the seam-cut myself, second-year. Did you think I never wondered what was underneath it?"

Behind her, down the corridor, the palace bells began to ring — wrong, arrhythmic, an alarm pattern — and Ondine raised one hand and her enforcers stopped, because she hadn't finished speaking, and Ondine never left anything unfinished.

"I have a proposal," she said. "You're going to want to hear it before you run."

Tam's hand found mine in the dark. Behind us, below us, the dead sang.

I did not run.
