## 15. Ondine's Bargain

The ossuary of the palace of Aldric smelled of cold stone and older grief. I had woken every soul on those shelves not an hour past, and they were still murmuring — a low tide of voices, names, half-finished prayers, the small domestic complaints of the dead. *It's drafty in here. Who moved my femur? I was promised a bell.*

And into that tide walked Sister Ondine, calm as a woman entering her own kitchen.

I knew her before she said anything. You spend enough years around bone and you learn to read a person by how the dead react to them. The shelves near her went quiet as she passed, one alcove at a time, like candles being pinched out. Not frightened quiet. *Relieved* quiet. That was worse.

"Freya Almeida." She said my name the way Corvane did — with the particular warmth of someone who has already decided how you'll be useful. "You've made rather a mess."

"Working on a bigger one." I kept the knife loose in my hand. Maren's knife. Maren hummed along its spine, uneasy, which told me everything Maren always told me: run.

Tam was somewhere behind me with the queen's guard, holding the corridor we'd broken through. I could hear him shouting, which meant he was alive, which meant I couldn't go help him without turning my back on the thing walking toward me between the shelves of the dead.

So I didn't turn around.

"You're Ondine," I said. "Corvane's other clever girl."

"His only clever girl, once you ran." She stopped perhaps ten feet away, hands folded, and I got my first good look at her. Younger than me by a few years. Plain, patient face, the kind you'd trust with your purse or your confession. Her carving kit hung at her hip in oiled leather, each tool in its loop, nothing wasted. A craftsman's kit. My stomach recognized it before I did. It was nearly identical to mine.

Bone-charmer's tools. Silence-maker's tools.

"The village," I said. "Bone and Ash. Every charm on every neck, cut dead. That was you."

"That was mercy." She said it simply, without heat, and that was the horror of it. No cruelty in her at all. "Do you know what I heard when I came there? Three hundred years of the dead complaining. Begging. Bargaining. One old man had been asking his granddaughter to forgive him for sixty years, over and over, and she'd been refusing to hear him for sixty years, and neither of them could stop. Neither of them could *rest*. So I cut them loose." She touched a shelf gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping child. "They don't suffer anymore. They don't *want* anymore. Isn't wanting the wound? Isn't listening how it stays open?"

"They're people," I said.

"They were." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and I hated it. "You still use the present tense. That's Corvane's training. He teaches his girls to keep accounts, and accounts need living debtors."

"And what does the crown teach you?"

Something flickered behind her eyes then — the first honest thing in her face. "That it's almost finished," she said softly. "And that when it is, no one will have to listen to any of this ever again. Every grave in the kingdom, silent. Every relic mute. Grief with an end to it, instead of a voice." She stepped closer. The dead around us went quieter still, drawing in like tide from a shore. "That's why I'm here, Freya. Not to kill you. To make you an offer. Corvane wanted you captured; I'd rather you were *converted*. You're better than he thinks you are. Better than he deserves."

"Flattery from a silencer," I said. "Careful. I'll start thinking you want something."

"I do." She drew a breath, and for a moment — just a moment — she looked young. Tired. Like a girl who had heard too much for too long and found only one door out of the room. "Help me silence the crown-thing. Permanently. Not unbind it — *unbind* is just moving the prisoner to another cell, and you know it. Your whole plan is to take it into yourself. I've seen what that does. I've done the arithmetic." Her voice dropped. "Take the binding apart instead. Unmake the vessel. And while your knife is in it — silence it. Silence all of it. The crown-thing, the voices in the bones, the whole groaning choir. End it everywhere. No more couriers dying with maps in their teeth. No more dead sellers humming through ledgers. No more little girls hearing their mothers' ghosts ask them for things they can't give."

My hands went cold around the knife.

She knew. Of course she knew. Corvane had raised us both out of the same clay; he'd known exactly which lever to pull for each of us. For Tam, shame. For Isolde, love. For Ondine, exhaustion. And for me —

For me, silence. The one thing I had never dared want out loud.

"You think I haven't imagined it?" My voice came out rougher than I meant. "Every day since I was nine years old. A room where nobody talks. Do you know what the dead sound like, Ondine? They don't sound like choirs. They sound like a market at closing time. Everyone shouting over everyone else, forever, about things that stopped mattering a hundred years ago. I have balanced guild accounts to the sound of six generations of merchants arguing about a shipment that rotted before I was born." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Yes. I want quiet. I would trade both hands for quiet."

"Then—"

"But." I held up the knife. Maren hummed against my palm, steady now, certain. "But the dead aren't the noise, Ondine. They're the *record*. Everything anyone ever was, still saying its piece, still owed its hearing. You don't end suffering by cutting the tongues out of the archive. You just end the testimony." I thought of Bone and Ash, those three hundred necks hung with dumb charms, the villagers' faces — relieved, some of them. Grateful, even. And underneath the gratitude, a terrible lightness, like people who had set down something they were carrying and couldn't remember what it was. Couldn't remember whom they'd loved enough to carry it. "You took their grief away. Fine. You also took their dead. And in ten years their children won't know there was ever anything to take."

"Grief isn't sacred," Ondine said. "It's just pain with good publicity."

"So is mercy." I let the point of the knife drift toward her. "Here's my counter-offer. Stand aside. Help me unbind the king. When it's done, if you still want a world without voices, build your own silence and live in it. Leave everyone else's ears alone."

"No." She said it without anger. That was the worst part — she declined me the way you decline a second helping, politely, finally. "Because your way leaves the crown-thing's power loose in the world, and loose power finds vessels. Always has. Always will. Silence is the only seal that holds." She began unbuckling the flap of her tool roll, unhurried. "I'm sorry, Freya. Truly. You're the first person I've met who understands the problem. That makes killing you feel like amputation."

"Feel free to keep talking," I said. "It gives me time to pick my ground."

* * *

We fought among the shelves because there was nowhere else to fight. The ossuary was a long vaulted gallery, alcove after alcove, niches stacked to the ceiling, every bone sorted and labeled in a dead archivist's careful hand. Narrow aisles. Bad footing. The dead pressed in on every side — awake now, thanks to me, chattering, watching, offering commentary.

*Left!* shrieked somebody's grandmother as Ondine's paring knife came for my throat, and I dropped, and it whispered past where my carotid had ambitions.

"Thank you," I gasped.

"Don't mention it," said the grandmother, who had been dead since the reign of Aldric's grandfather and considered this the most interesting century of her afterlife.

Ondine fought the way she carved: economical, patient, no motion wasted, every strike a finished sentence. I fought the way I fled: badly, improvisationally, with a great deal of luck and an unfair amount of help. Because that was the difference between us, and it opened up between us like a fault line even as her blade rang off Maren's spine.

She worked alone in the silence she made.

I had three hundred dead screaming directions.

"She feints high!" — *duck* — "Her left knee's bad, old break!" — *circle right* — "Behind you, girl, BEHIND—" I spun and caught her wrist with my elbow, awkward, graceless, enough. We broke apart, breathing hard, two craftswomen doing violence with workshop tools, and I thought absurdly: *this is what Corvane made. This is his masterpiece. Two daughters with identical kits, trying to carve each other.*

"Why?" I demanded, backing up the aisle. "Why does it matter so much to you? What did you hear that broke you?"

For a moment she didn't answer. Then, circling me past a shelf of skulls, she said: "A boy. In my first year at the guild. His mother died and left a message in her knucklebone, and Corvane gave it to me to transcribe, as practice." Her voice stayed level. Her knife did not. "'Tell Ondine the porridge is ready.'" She laughed, one dry note. "He was five. He drowned in a well. He'd been waiting forty years to tell his mother breakfast was on. And she was standing right there in the room with me, and I had to decide whether to tell her, knowing she'd spend the rest of her life listening to a five-year-old say four words, over and over, because that's all a five-year-old has."

"What did you do?"

"I told her." Something cracked in her voice, very slightly, like ice under weight. "And she thanked me. And then she came back every week for eleven years, until she died, and her last words were 'is he still there,' and he *was*, he's probably *still there*, still saying it, and it was the cruelest thing I ever did and I did it out of *kindness*." She lunged. "Silence is kinder! Silence would have spared them BOTH!"

I caught her blade on Maren's haft and the dead roared — not in fear but in fury, hundreds of voices at once, the whole archive rising off its shelves:

*WE ARE STILL HERE*

The sound hit Ondine like weather. She staggered, and I saw it — saw the thing under her conviction, the truth she'd built her mercy on top of and refused to look down at: she wasn't deaf to them. She'd never been deaf. Silencers aren't people who can't hear. They're people who can't stop, and found one way to make it stop.

"You're one of us," I breathed. "You hear them. All the time. Even silenced ones. Especially silenced ones — because cutting a voice doesn't kill it, does it? It just stops it from being *answered*. They're all still in there, Ondine. Under your charms. Still saying 'the porridge is ready.' Forever. And you can hear every one."

Her face went white. "Shut up."

"That's your secret. That's why you need the whole world silent. Not for them." I advanced now, and the dead advanced with me, a tide at my back. "For *you*. Because if even one voice keeps speaking anywhere in creation, you'll have to keep listening."

"*Shut UP—*"

She came at me wild, all her economy spent, and I did the only thing I could think of. I drove Maren's point into the shelf beside her head — into the bone of a skull older than the kingdom — and I poured everything into it, every trick my mother's hands had taught me without my knowing, and I spoke the dead man's name.

The whole alcove woke at once.

Not a murmur. A *voice* — a baritone like a landslide, the voice of a man who had commanded garrisons, filling the gallery, shaking dust from the vaults. Ondine reeled as though struck. Behind her blindfold of sound, I could have killed her.

I put the knife down instead.

"Look at them," I said, when she could breathe again. She was on her knees between the shelves, hands over her ears, and the great voice had gentled now, become what it probably was in life: a tired officer reading a muster roll. Around us the whole ossuary had gone soft — not silent, never silent, but *low*, hundreds of voices settled into something like evening. "This is what they are, Ondine. Not torture. Not mercy. Just — everyone, still here, saying their piece. You can't carve that away. You can only stop your own ears, and call the stopping kindness."

She knelt there a long moment. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious and, beneath the fury, lost.

"You'll fail," she said hoarsely. "The binding will take you, or the crown-thing will, and the world will get worse, and I will be right, and I will hate being right."

"Probably," I agreed. "But grief with an ending isn't peace, it's just a shorter book. I choose the long one."

She gathered her tools slowly, buckled the roll, stood. At the mouth of the gallery she paused, not turning around.

"Corvane is in the throne room. He's moved the ritual forward — the bells are a decoy, the binding completes tonight, not at the thirteenth turn." She said it flatly, a tradeswoman settling accounts. "I could have lied to you. Consider whether that means I've changed, or whether I just want to watch you lose honestly." And she walked out of the ossuary into the dark, leaving the dead murmuring behind her like surf.

I stood alone among the shelves, bleeding from a dozen shallow cuts, holding a knife full of ghost.

"Well," said the old grandmother. "Are you going to stand there, or are you going to save the king? Your young man's shouting has gotten much more urgent."

I ran. And behind me, faint under the alarm bells beginning to ring, I heard the dead of the ossuary do something no ossuary had ever done.

They sang me out.
