## 16. The Mother's Hands

Maren did not lead us so much as *pull* us, the way a current pulls a leaf. Tam and I walked the north fork out of the hills while my carving knife tugged in my grip like a hound on a leash, and every time I slowed to catch my breath, the blade grew warm against my palm, insistent, almost rude.

"She was a cartographer," I said for the third time that morning. "You were a cartographer. Why would you know where my mother kept her workshop?"

The knife answered through my bones rather than my ears — Maren had never bothered much with sound once he'd learned he didn't need it. Words arrived the way memories do: fully formed, slightly accusatory.

*Because she carved me a map of the palace ossuary when I was alive and could still walk, and because I spent forty years dead learning where everything ends up, and nothing ends up lost, Freya Almeida. Things end up hidden by people who loved them.*

Tam walked behind me with his hand resting on his sword hilt, which was his way of holding someone's hand. "Is it talking again?" he asked.

"It's always talking."

"Is it saying anything useful?"

"It's saying my mother hid things from people who loved her."

"That tracks," Tam said. "My mother hid her drinking from my father, who loved her very badly."

I might have laughed if my stomach weren't doing what it was doing. We crested the ridge below the old quarry road, and there it was: a cottage built into the hillside itself, turf on the roof, one chimney breathing no smoke at all, windows like closed eyes. It should have looked abandoned. Instead it looked *waiting* — the particular patience of a place that has been told someone is coming.

*Your mother bought this the year you were born,* Maren said. *Under another name. She came here four times a year. She called it maintenance.*

"Maintenance," I repeated aloud, because some words need air to become real.

"What kind of maintenance?" Tam asked.

"I don't know yet." My hands knew. That was the horror gathering at the base of my skull even before we reached the door. My hands had begun to sweat in a specific pattern, the way they did before difficult work, as if they'd been here before.

The door wasn't locked. Of course it wasn't locked. You don't lock a workshop you expect your daughter to inherit.

* * *

Inside, dust hung in the slanting light like a held breath, and I understood immediately that no one had disturbed this room since the day my mother last left it. Tools on their pegs. A whetstone worn into a smile-shaped hollow. A kettle on the cold stove, waiting for water that never came.

And on the workbench in the center of the room, under a cloth of good linen that someone had folded with care:

A crown.

Not *the* crown. A mold of one — bone-white plaster cast in two halves, the inner surface carved with channels and grooves so fine they looked like frost ferns. I lifted the cloth and the smell hit me first: bone dust and resin and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed tomb, which I suppose is exactly what it was.

*Turn it over,* Maren said, gentler now. *Look at the maker's marks.*

I turned it over. And the floor of my whole life tilted.

There, along the inner band, were the carving marks. Every carver has them — the tiny signatures of habit, the angle at which your wrist rests, the depth your hand trusts without asking permission. Corvane used to say he could identify any guild carver blindfolded by running a thumb along their work. He'd identified mine when I was nine years old and proud of it.

These marks matched mine exactly. Not similar. Not the same school or tradition. *Exact.* The same slight leftward drift on curved cuts. The same hesitation notch at the end of long strokes, where my hand always paused to check the grain. Marks I had been told all my life were mine alone.

"I don't understand," I said, and my voice came out very small in that dusty room.

*You do,* Maren said. *You simply haven't said it yet.*

"My mother—" My throat closed around it. "My mother trained my hands. Before the guild. Before Corvane found me. She gave me exercises. Grain-reading drills. Repetition cuts. Ten thousand repetitions of the same stroke until the hand forgets it ever did anything else." I set the mold down carefully, the way you set down something that might be listening. "She wasn't teaching me to carve."

*No.*

"She was calibrating me."

*Yes.*

Tam had gone still by the door. He was a man who understood being shaped for a purpose you hadn't chosen; I watched him recognize the shape of mine. "Freya—"

"The crown needs maintenance." I heard myself say it from a great distance, calm as a ledger entry. "Bindings decay. Channels clog. Someone has to re-cut the grooves every few years, someone whose hand matches the original maker's, because a mismatched cut would sever the binding instead of feeding it. She couldn't come back forever. So she built the replacement." I looked down at my own hands, those familiar, competent, trusted hands. "She built me. Corvane didn't find a talented orphan. He found the tool arriving at the workshop."

The silence afterward had texture. Dust settled. Somewhere under the floor, water moved through stone with the patience of centuries.

*I'm sorry,* Maren said, and for the first time since I'd known him — since he'd died bleeding into a courier's coat — he sounded like he meant it past mere politeness. *I found out too late to tell her no. Your mother believed, Freya. That's the part that took me longest to forgive. She believed the crown was mercy. One man bound, thousands spared a war. She did the arithmetic everyone does, and she put herself on the side of the ledger where the cost goes.*

"And you?"

*I did the same arithmetic. I drew the maps that moved the pieces. We are all somebody's steady hand until we look down and see what we're holding.*

I sat down on my mother's stool. It fit me. Of course it fit me. I pressed my palms flat against the workbench and felt — not voices, not exactly, but the residue of work, the way a church holds the shape of its prayers. This bench had held the crown-mold through hundreds of hours of careful cutting. Some of those hours had been mine, in miniature, rehearsed in a guild classroom without knowing why the exercises mattered.

"She visited four times a year," I said. "Maintenance visits. And she never once came home and told me. Never taught me anything true. She just... drilled my hands and left."

*Or she couldn't,* Maren said. *Or telling you made it yours, and she wanted it to be hers alone for as long as she could carry it. I've been dead a long time, girl. I've learned that the living lie mostly to buy time, not to cause harm. Time is the only gift most of us can afford.*

"Then time ran out."

*Time always runs out. The question is what you were doing with it when it did.*

* * *

Tam lit the stove. It seemed important to him, somehow — a small domestic rebellion against a room full of ghosts. He found wood stacked by the hearth, dry after twenty years, and soon the kettle was doing the thing kettles do, and the room smelled faintly of iron and warmth instead of tombs.

I searched the workshop properly then, because grief is no excuse for bad procedure, and procedure was the only inheritance I trusted anymore. Drawers of gravers and scorers. Jars of bone dust labeled in my mother's tight hand. And beneath the floorboard under the stool — because there is always a floorboard under the stool, Maren guided me to it with the smugness of the eternally dead — a journal.

Her handwriting. Dates stretching back thirty years. Maintenance logs.

*Third visit this year. Channels at the second and ninth grooves have narrowed. Re-cut both. The king spoke to me today. He asked my name. I told him it was raining.*

I read that line three times. Then I read the next entries, and the next, years of them, and the pattern emerged with terrible clarity: each visit, the grooves needed less repair but the *notes* got shorter. Drier. As though whatever she was maintaining was slowly maintaining her back.

*The binding strengthens each moon-turn. Thirteen turns to full seat. After seating, no further maintenance required. After seating, I will be free of it.*

After seating. The thirteenth turn. The number scrawled on a dying man's lips. The countdown Maren's map had shown us — four turns remaining when we started, fewer now.

But here was the thing that stopped my heart mid-beat, the thing I read four times with my hands flat on the page as if I could hold the truth down by force:

*If unbound before the thirteenth turn, the vessel requires a new anchor. The binding does not die. It moves. Counter-carving alone will loose it into whatever hands are nearest. God forgive me, I designed it that way. A binding that dies is a binding that can be murdered. A binding that moves must be negotiated with.*

I sat back. The kettle screamed. Tam leapt for it, cursed, burned his thumb, swore more creatively.

"Found something," I said.

"You've got your reading face. The one worse than your carving face."

"The crown can be unbound before the thirteenth turn." I turned the journal toward him. "But it doesn't die. It jumps. Into whoever's closest. Which means when I counter-carve that thing off Aldric's head, unless someone plans to volunteer—"

"We'll figure that out," Tam said, too fast.

"We'll *what?*"

"We'll figure it out. Later. With everyone. Because if I know you, and I have known you through ambushes, silencers, and one genuinely haunted ferry crossing, you've already decided to take it yourself, and I want it noted for the record that I object, and that we're discussing it later, with witnesses."

Something in my chest cracked — not painfully. The opposite. Like ice giving way to moving water. I looked at this disgraced, loyal, impossible man standing in my mother's kitchen holding a kettle he'd just scalded himself on, objecting to sacrifices I hadn't proposed yet, and I thought: *oh.* Oh, no. Oh, this is inconvenient.

"Noted for the record," I managed.

"Good." He poured tea into two cups he'd found, mismatched, chipped. My mother's cups. I drank from my mother's cup and it tasted like iron and steam and being alive despite everything.

*Freya,* Maren said quietly. *There's one more thing. Look at the last page.*

I flipped to the back of the journal. A single entry, undated, the handwriting shakier than anything before it:

*To whoever finds this — if it is my Freya, then I am sorry, and I am proud, and I am sorry again, in that order. The mold is yours. So is the choice I never gave you. You can maintain the binding, as I did. Or you can break it, knowing what breaking costs. Or — and I only learned this at the end, too late to use it — a carver whose hand matches the mold can do a third thing. Not maintain. Not break. Rewrite. Change what the binding wants. It would take everything you have. It would take the hands themselves. But it would not move the crown-thing into anyone. It would unmake its hunger at the source.*

*A third option, daughter. I built you for the first one. Choose the third.*

I closed the journal. My hands were shaking, and then, strangely, they weren't. Because for the first time since a dying courier had pressed a map into my arms, the shape of the thing ahead resolved into something I recognized.

Not a trap. Not a sacrifice. A *cut*. The hardest cut of my life, the kind you make once, with the grain, all the way through — but a cut, and cutting is the only thing I have ever truly known how to do.

They made me a tool. Fine. Tools are for using, and no one — not Corvane, not my mother, not the thing wearing a crown thirteen turns deep — gets to decide what for.

Outside, far to the south, bells began to ring. Not the Night of Bells — not yet, days yet — but practice bells, rehearsal bells, the city warming up its throat.

Tam cocked his head. "That's early for them to start."

"No," I said, standing, taking up knife and journal and mold. "That's right on schedule. They know how little time is left. So do I."

One turn remained. And I finally knew what my hands were for.
