## 17. One Turn Remaining

There is a particular kind of quiet that comes before a thing goes badly wrong. I learned it in the palace guard. It sounds like the hush after a bell stops ringing — not peace, just the air holding its breath to see what you'll do next.

The Night of Bells was made of that quiet, stretched across a whole city.

From the widow's rooftop where we crouched, Vellharbor spread out below us like a spilled jewelry box. Every bell tower in the capital had been lit from within — great paper lanterns hoisted into the belfries so that the towers glowed like candles in their own wax. Thousands of people filled the streets below, singing the old rounds, wearing masks of kings and queens and saints, drinking enough spiced wine to float a barge. Once a year, the Night of Bells let the city pretend it was happy. The bells themselves wouldn't ring until midnight, when the king would appear on the high balcony and strike the Great Bell with a hammer older than the dynasty, and everyone would cheer, and no one would look too closely at his face.

Except this year, the king wasn't going to appear on any balcony.

This year, Corvane had moved the binding ritual to the throne room, and we were going to walk into it like fools who hadn't read the invitation carefully.

"You're smiling," Freya said beside me. She didn't look at me. She was watching the palace, her chin on her knees, her carving knife turning over and over in her fingers the way it did when she was nervous — which was always, lately, and which she thought nobody noticed.

"I'm not smiling."

"Your voice smiles when you lie. It's very annoying."

"That's a strange complaint from a woman who lies for a living."

"I balance ledgers," she said. "Balancing isn't lying. Balancing is making two wrong numbers agree."

I laughed before I could stop myself, and the laugh came out too loud in the rooftop dark, and somewhere below a drunk saint looked up suspiciously. Freya's mouth twitched. That was the whole of our courtship, more or less: insults traded in whispers on rooftops while the world ended slowly around us. There are worse ways. I've seen most of them.

Isolde knelt at the roof's far edge, still as a drawn bow. She'd traded her crown for a servant's grey cloak weeks ago, but she'd never quite managed to trade away the way she held herself. Even kneeling behind a chimney pot, she looked like the building belonged to her. Which, technically, it did.

"The servants' gate rotates at the third quarter-bell," she said. "The captain of the night watch owes me his life and hates me for it. He'll be looking elsewhere." She paused. "He'll also be expecting this to be a trap. So will everyone else. Corvane has known we were coming since the moment Ondine walked away from that ossuary and reported back."

"We assumed as much," Freya said.

"No. *I* assumed it. You two assumed we might get lucky." Isolde turned then, and in the lantern-light her eyes were the flat grey of a winter sea. "I want to say it plainly, once, so none of us can pretend later. We are walking into a room built to kill us. The only reason to go anyway is that the alternative is my husband dying on the thirteenth turn while the city sings. If anyone here has a better plan, now is the hour."

Nobody said anything. Maren's map lay between us, its ink crawling slow as insects, the dead cartographer's annotations shifting by lamplight: *throne room, north stair, the old gallery beneath — they've moved it, they've moved everything.* Four turns ago there had been time for cleverness. Now there was one turn left, and cleverness had packed its bags.

"One turn remaining," Freya said quietly. She said it the way other people say prayers.

I looked at her — really looked, the way I'd been trying not to for weeks because looking felt like admitting something — and I thought about the first time I'd ever done an act of conscience. It had gone badly. I'd seen the king's eyes change in the middle of a council session, seen something ancient and patient look out through a man I'd sworn to die for, and I'd done the correct thing: I'd reported it. And the crown's answer had been an order to report to the silencing cells, and my answer to *that* had been to desert in the night like a thief, and I'd been running from both answers ever since. Conscience, in my experience, was just cowardice with better posture.

But sitting on that rooftop, listening to the city sing below us, I felt something settle in my chest. Not courage. Courage is a word soldiers use for adrenaline they survived. This was quieter than that. It was the understanding that I had already run once, and running hadn't saved me, and there was exactly one way left to find out whether the other choice fit any better.

"If I don't come out of this," I said, "someone should know my real name isn't Tam Vess."

Freya looked at me sharply. Isolde didn't blink.

"It's Tomas Vessarion," I went on, because apparently I'd decided to spend my possible last hour confessing. "My father was a net-mender in Saltrow. I lied my way into the guard at sixteen because the palace had hot food. Everything else about me is roughly accurate, which I've always considered a moral achievement."

"Tomas," Freya said, testing it. Then, wryly: "It doesn't suit you."

"No," I agreed. "Tam suits me fine. But if there's a stone anywhere, put the true one down. My mother would appreciate it."

"She'd appreciate you surviving," Freya said.

"My mother," I said, "has never once appreciated anything I've done. It's one of her finest qualities. Keeps a man humble."

Isolde made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth. "Third quarter-bell," she said. "Save your confessions for the throne room. Corvane loves those."

* * *

The servants' gate worked exactly as Isolde promised, which frightened me more than a fight would have. A gate that opens too easily is a mouth.

We went through in ones and twos, cloaks grey, faces down, moving with the kitchen traffic — the Night of Bells fed half the city, and the under-halls of the palace were a river of trays and steam and shouting cooks who had no time to look at anyone's face. For a hundred paces we were nothing. Invisible. I remembered being a guardsman and how thoroughly I'd ignored servants, and I blessed every lazy shift I'd ever stood.

Then the river of kitchens thinned, and we climbed the north stair, and the invisibility wore off like cheap paint.

The upper corridors were empty. Not quiet — empty, which is different. The Night of Bells filled every corner of the palace with music and light except these halls, where the sconces burned low and the shadows pooled like standing water. Someone had cleared them. Someone wanted a clean road from the stair to the throne room, the way you clear a road for a funeral.

Maren's map had gone hot in Freya's hands — not warm, *hot*, the ink boiling, the dead cartographer's handwriting scrawling itself faster and faster along the margins. Freya held it against her chest like a child carrying a cat that wanted down.

"He's agitated," she murmured. "Maren. He keeps writing the same thing."

"What thing?"

"*Beneath. Beneath. They kept the old door.*" She frowned. "There's a gallery under the throne room. An ossuary. The palace keeps its honored dead there — kings' bones, heroes, that sort of thing."

"I know it," Isolde said. "Every coronation processes through it. Aldric hated it. He said it smelled like being reminded." Her voice caught slightly on his name, and she mastered it the way she mastered everything, quickly and without witnesses.

We reached the antechamber before the throne room — the Hall of Bells proper, where the Great Bell hung in its frame of black oak, big as a house, silent, waiting for midnight and a king who wasn't coming. And there, at last, we found people.

Enforcers. A dozen of them, guild-grey, standing in loose formation around the bell's frame with the bored patience of men who have been told to wait for something. Not searching. Not patrolling. Waiting.

"They're not guarding the door," Freya whispered. "They're guarding the *bell*."

"Why guard a bell?"

"Because it's not a bell anymore." She unrolled the map fully, and I saw what the ink had become: a diagram of the Great Bell itself, sectioned like an anatomist's drawing, and threaded through every section, lines of script in a hand that shook even in death. Maren had known. Maren had died knowing. *They poured the binding into the bronze,* the dead hand wrote. *The crown is only the key. The bell is the lock. When it rings at midnight —*

Freya stopped reading aloud. Her face had gone the color of tallow.

"When it rings at midnight," Isolde finished, flat, "the whole city hears it. Every soul in Vellharbor will be standing in the streets when the binding completes." She closed her eyes briefly. "Not a ritual. A coronation. He means to bind the king in front of ten thousand witnesses and call it a miracle."

"And Corvane knows we're here," I said. "Which means this isn't his defense. This is his *invitation*. He wants us to come through that door."

"Then he shouldn't have made the door so obvious," Freya said, and there was something new in her voice — not fear, or not only fear. Anger, banked and steady, the kind that carves. "Maren says there's another way. Beneath. The old ossuary gallery connects to the throne room through the kings' crypt. Corvane moved the ritual to the throne room, but he can't move the bones. The dead stay where they're laid." She looked up at me, and in the low light her eyes had the same glow as the map's ink, faint and greenish, and I understood suddenly why people crossed the street when bone-carvers passed. "And the dead, Tam, are on our side tonight. What's left of them."

We went down.

* * *

The ossuary beneath the palace was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature. It was the cold of a place that has been holding its breath for centuries. Niches lined the walls floor to vault, thousands of them, each holding a skull or a stacked arrangement of long bones, each labeled in gilt letters: names, dates, deeds. *HERE LIES ALDRIC THE THIRD, WHO HELD THE BRIDGE AT SORROWS FORD.* *HERE LIES MAERLIN, QUEEN-REGENT, WHO CHOSE ONCE AND CHOSE WELL.* Generations of the dynasty, arranged in the dark like books on shelves, waiting to be read.

Isolde lifted her lamp and walked slowly down the center aisle, reading the names of her husband's ancestors, and I watched her lips move and wondered what she was asking them.

Freya, meanwhile, had stopped walking entirely.

She stood with her head tilted, the way she did when she listened — really listened — and her expression was one I'd never seen on her before. Not pain. Not the flinch she usually wore when the dead spoke too loud. Something closer to recognition.

"They know I'm here," she whispered. "All of them. They've been waiting down here for—" She pressed a hand flat against a niche wall, against the stone between two skulls, and her voice cracked. "Centuries. Nobody down here has been carved. Nobody's heard them since they were laid to rest. They're not angry. They're just—"

She didn't finish. She didn't need to. I knew what it was to wait unheard. I'd done it for two years.

"Tomas." She used the true name deliberately, and it landed differently coming from her — heavier, warmer. "When we go up through the crypt door, Corvane will be waiting. He'll have enforcers, maybe Ondine, maybe worse. Isolde and I have a job to do with the crown. Which means someone has to hold the door."

"Ah," I said. "The classic."

"The classic," she agreed, and her mouth did the twitch again, but her eyes didn't join it. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you how it is, because you taught me that on the road — that liars survive longer when somebody tells them the truth. So: the truth. You may not walk out of that doorway. I need you to stand in it anyway."

I looked at her for a long moment. Above us, muffled through feet of stone, the city sang. Somewhere beyond the crypt door, a man I had loved and betrayed and failed was about to be erased forever unless two women and a deserter did something insane about it. My second act of conscience. My father the net-mender used to say a torn net either gets mended or it catches nothing ever again, and there's no third option, boy, so stop crying about the hole.

"I have one condition," I said.

"Name it."

"When this is over — if I'm alive, if we're all alive — you carve me something. A little charm. Nothing fancy. And you tell me honestly whether it hums."

Freya went very still. "You want to hear a dead person."

"I want to hear *you*, someday, when you're dead. Preferably a very long time from now, complaining about me to whoever's within earshot. I figure if your gift survives tonight, that's the only version of you I'll ever get to keep." I shrugged, aiming for wry and mostly hitting it. "Sentimental, I know. Blame the wine fumes drifting down from the festival."

"Tomas Vessarion," she said softly, "if you hold that door, I will carve you the loudest damn charm in the history of bone."

"Deal." I drew my sword — the guard-issue blade I'd carried out of the palace two years ago, still sharp, still honest steel — and turned to face the crypt door, the plain black oak that separated the sleeping kings from the throne room above. From Corvane. From whatever waited up there wearing my king's face.

Behind me, I heard Freya press her palm to the niche wall one last time, and I heard her say, barely above breath: "*Wake up. All of you. Follow me.*"

And the ossuary — every shelf, every skull, every gilt-lettered name in the dark — began, very faintly, to sing.

The crypt door swung open onto torchlight and the smell of hot bronze, and Corvane's voice drifted down the stair like oil poured on water:

"Come in, Tam. I've been saving you a seat since the night you ran."

I gripped my sword and started up toward my second act of conscience, hoping — for the first time in two years — that this one would stick.
