## 19. Unbinding

The throne room of King Aldric had been built to impress the poor and intimidate the ambitious, and it did neither for me anymore. I'd seen its bones by now — literally, thanks to Maren's map and my own gift — and what I knew was this: beneath the marble and the gilding and the banners of a house that hadn't won a war in two generations, the palace was an ossuary with pretensions. The dead lay stacked under the flagstones like cordwood, catalogued and forgotten, and every one of them was awake tonight because I had asked them to be.

The bells were still ringing. Corvane had moved the ritual here, into the open heart of the palace, on the Night of Bells, when no one would think twice about sound that shook the windows. Clever old man. I had loved him once, which is not the same as having been wrong to love him, but it is adjacent.

The crown sat on Aldric's head at the top of the dais, and the thing inside the crown sat inside Aldric, and both of them looked down at me with his grey eyes and none of his kindness.

"Freya Almeida," it said, in a voice like my mother's reading aloud. "Come up. Let's finish your education."

"Not yet," I said, and drew my knife.

* * *

Let me explain the counter-carving, because it deserves explanation, being the only genuinely original work I have ever done and therefore the one thing I'd hate to have misunderstood by posterity.

A binding vessel — and I learned this from Maren's ghost, who had spent her death apologizing for teaching it to my mother in the first place — is carved so that every groove runs *inward*. Grief in, memory in, will in. A spiral that tightens. The crown on Aldric's head was a masterwork of inwardness: eleven years of a king's self, wound round and round and drawn down into the gold like thread onto a spindle, leaving just enough of him on the surface to wave at crowds and sign decrees.

You cannot unmake such a thing by breaking it. Break it and everything inside spills out at once, unanchored, screaming, into whatever's nearest. What's nearest, if you're standing where I was standing, is you.

So instead you carve *outward*. You take the spiral and you reverse it, groove by groove, line by line, and you give all of it somewhere to go. The problem — and there is always a problem; the guild taught me that problems are the only reliable currency — is that the somewhere has to be a vessel of sufficient capacity.

Maren had been very quiet when we worked out what that meant.

"You," she'd said finally, through the knife, in the tone of someone confirming a diagnosis they already suspected. "It has to be you."

"I'd worked that out," I said. "Thank you."

"I could lie to you. Ghosts do it constantly. It's practically our national pastime."

"Don't you dare." And she hadn't. That's the thing about Maren I'll carry longest, longer even than the rest: she told me true when lying would have cost her nothing.

So. The counter-carving. My hands. My knife. The crown. And me, at the bottom of the reversed spiral, catching everything that came down.

Tam had said, before we went in, "There has to be another way."

"There isn't."

"Then I'm saying something stupid now so I don't regret not saying it later." He'd taken my face in both hands — callused palms, guard's grip, gentle anyway — and said, "Whatever happens up there, you come back down. That's an order from a man who deserted once and knows how it's done."

"I've never followed an order in my life."

"I know," he said. "It's why I'm here."

Isolde had said nothing at all. She'd adjusted my collar, the way you do for someone going out into weather, and kissed my forehead like a queen conferring a knighthood, and gone to stand beside Tam with her sword drawn and her grief folded up small enough to fit in a pocket. Queens are good at pockets. It's most of the job.

Now Tam stood at the base of the dais with his back to the great doors, and Isolde stood at his left shoulder, and between them they held the width of the room against everything Corvane had.

Which was a great deal. Enforcers in guild grey, six of them through each door and more coming, blades out, faces set in the particular blankness of people who have been paid to stop asking questions. I knew three of them by name. One had shown me how to hold a chisel when I was nine. He wouldn't look at me. Good. I didn't want him to; I wanted him to keep his eyes on Tam, who was doing something showy and stupid with a pike he'd taken off a wall, buying seconds the way Tam bought everything — loudly, and on credit.

And I put my knife to the crown.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the dead, and I say this as their bookkeeper: they are not few.

Living, you go days without thinking of them. Dying, you become one of a crowd so vast that numbers stop meaning anything — generations stacked on generations, back and back, to the first woman who looked at a fire and thought *mine*. Every village. Every plague year. Every war. Every child who didn't make it through a winter. They are all still *there*, in the way water is there under a desert, waiting for someone to dig.

The crown-thing had dug. Eleven years, it had been digging — drinking the dead of the whole kingdom through the binding, hoarding them in the spiral the way Corvane hoarded ledgers. It wasn't a parasite wearing a king. It was a library wearing a king. And when my knife touched the first groove and began to turn it —

— it opened the doors.

Every voice. At once.

I have spent my life hearing the dead one at a time, politely, through tokens, the way you hear a neighbor through a wall. This was the wall coming down and the ocean behind it. Ten thousand voices speaking into me simultaneously — not words, mostly, but the pressure of words, the *want* to be heard, grief and grievance and love and boredom and terror, a grandmother's lullaby and a soldier's last curse and forty years of a fisherman complaining about the weather, all of it pouring down the reversed spiral, pouring into me, because I had made myself the vessel, because that was the design, because Maren had told me true.

My knees went. The knife nearly left my hand. Somewhere very far away, steel was ringing on steel, and Isolde was shouting a command in a voice trained to be obeyed, and none of it reached me. There was no room. I was full of the drowned and the buried and the burned, and they were not malicious, that's the terrible part — they were just *there*, all of them, wanting what the dead always want, which is one moment of attention, and I was giving ten thousand moments per second until my skull felt like a bell that had been struck by God.

*Stop,* I thought. *Stop, stop, stop.*

They couldn't. That was the point of the crown. They'd been held so long, wound so tight, that release was not something they could modulate. I had pulled a cork from a sea.

And in the middle of it — this I remember clearly, this I will remember however long or short my remembering lasts — in the very center of the roar, one voice, close, dry, familiar:

*Freya. Listen.*

Not *stop listening.* Not *pull away.* Maren, dead these thirty years, pressed into a carving knife, saying the one thing I least wanted to hear.

*I can't—*

*You can. This is the work. Your mother flinched from exactly this, and look what her flinching built. Listen, girl. All of it. Find the pattern.*

Find the pattern. Ledger-work. Of course. Of course it came back to ledgers. I am P. M.—no. I am Freya Almeida, and I balance accounts, and an account is only chaos that hasn't been read properly yet.

So I listened.

I let the flood come and I stopped fighting it and I did the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than burning my ledgers, harder than leaving home, harder than loving anyone: I opened myself completely to ten thousand voices of pain and *paid attention*. Grief became data. Terror became entries in a column. Name after name after name poured through me and I gave each one its instant of hearing and moved to the next, the way you row a boat — one stroke, then the next, then the next, because if you think about the whole sea you drown.

And under the noise, the pattern. The spiral. Groove by groove I could feel it now from the inside, feel where the binding ran tightest, where Aldric's own will had been bent double and hammered flat. There — the fourth ring, where his wedding day had been folded in. There — the ninth, where the face of a man he'd executed had been used as a knot. The thing had built itself out of his worst moments, tightened the spiral wherever he was weakest, and I could feel every joint of the construction because I could feel *him* underneath, faint as a heartbeat under a blanket, still insisting, still Aldric, still stubbornly himself after eleven years of being worn.

He was humming. Under everything, under the flood, the king was humming the same four notes over and over, some song from his childhood, keeping time with himself so he wouldn't forget his own rhythm. It was the bravest thing I have ever heard, and I have heard dying men joke.

I carved toward it.

Groove by groove, ring by ring, I turned the spiral outward, and the voices came down the new channel and into me, and it hurt — it hurt the way birth hurts, the way drowning hurts, the way truth hurts — but hurt is information too, and I had stopped mistaking it for a reason to stop.

* * *

Outside my skull, the world was losing.

I know this because parts of it kept intruding. A scream that might have been Tam. The crash of a body against stone. Isolde's voice, ragged now, no longer commanding but bargaining: *"Corvane, please—"*

And Corvane's voice, calm as a ledger-line: "Hold them at the stairs. Don't harm the queen. She's worth more alive." Then, closer, directly to me, pitched under the roar the way you speak to someone mid-seizure: "Freya. Child. Stop. You're killing yourself and you haven't even reached the hard rings."

I kept carving.

"You were the best of them," he said, and his voice did something I'd never heard it do — it wavered. "Do you know why I chose you? Not the gift. Plenty had the gift. Because you never flinched from what the bones said. Even at eight years old, you wrote down the ugly truths in your neat little hand. I thought — I truly believed — a mind like yours could be trusted with the arithmetic of kingdoms."

"The arithmetic," I managed, through teeth, through the flood, "is wrong."

"One man against civil war. One man against ten thousand dead. Show me the sum that favors him."

I couldn't answer. Partly because I was elbow-deep in the seventh ring of a king's stolen soul, and partly because the honest answer was ugly: for years, I'd have agreed with him. For years I'd balanced exactly those sums in the guild's ledgers and called it discretion.

But I'd sat in a silent village since then. I'd watched a woman wear bone charms cut smooth of every voice and call it mercy. I'd heard a king hum four notes to keep himself alive. The sum wasn't one man against thousands. The sum was *everyone*, counted honestly, including the dead — and the dead, I was learning, groove by groove, were not a resource. They were us, later.

"Corvane," I said, and found — this surprised me — that I could speak gently. "You should have asked me. Twelve years ago, you should have sat me down and said, *child, we did a monstrous thing for a good reason, help me decide what to do about it.* I'd have stayed. I'd have helped. That's the tragedy of you. You never once trusted the person you raised."

Silence, from him. From everywhere, almost — the fight at the doors had collapsed into gasping and the scrape of boots, and Isolde was dragging something, someone, and Tam—

Tam was suddenly beside the dais, bloodied, grinning like a man who has decided the odds are a formality, and he put his back against mine and said, "Still here. Keep going. I'll watch the stairs."

"You're bleeding."

"I'm aware. It's very motivating." A pause, and then, quieter, in the space between two waves of the dead: "Whatever you're hearing in there — I hope someone's telling you the truth for once."

Someone was. Ten thousand someones. And the truth was a pattern, and the pattern had a shape, and the shape was almost finished turning outward, and the last ring — the innermost, the oldest, carved by my mother's hands in a workshop that smelled of tallow and pine — was the tightest knot in the whole design, because it had been cut with love, and love cuts deepest when it's afraid.

I set my knife to my mother's final groove, and the crown-thing spoke through Aldric's mouth one more time, and this time it did not use my mother's voice. It used mine.

"Last chance," it said, with my inflections, my dryness, my tired eyes. "Break the spiral wrong and everything in here comes out unanchored. Him. Them. Me. Do you know what I am, Freya? I'm every grief this kingdom refused to carry. Unbound, I don't vanish. I spread. You'll trade one bound king for a kingdom of hollowed ones."

"That's not a threat," I said. "That's a ledger entry. And it's wrong." I turned the blade. "Because grief isn't a thing you contain. It's a thing you *carry*. Watch."

And I cut through my mother's last line, and the spiral turned fully outward, and the crown came apart in my hands like frost in sunlight —

— and everything it held came down the channel, all of it, the whole drowned kingdom, into me.

I heard the bells stop.

That was the last thing, before the white: distant bells faltering mid-peal, one note hanging unfinished in the air, and ten thousand voices rising through me like water finding its level, and somewhere a man's heartbeat steadying under a blanket, and Tam shouting my name in a voice I'd remember even if I remembered nothing else.

Then the white took the throne room, and the dais, and the fight, and the fear.

I listened. Right up until listening was the only thing left of me —

— and then even that began to go, voice by voice, entry by entry, the ledger closing itself page by page, and the last thing written in it was a debt, unpaid, owed by a waking king to a woman already forgetting the sound of his gratitude.
