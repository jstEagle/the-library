## 21. A Debt Owed

There is a particular humiliation in being a king who cannot remember his own reign.

I discovered the full extent of it on the morning after my restoration, when I woke in my own bed with a headache like a cathedral bell and asked Isolde what year it was. She told me. I asked her what I had done with the spring. She looked at me the way one looks at a man standing at the edge of a cliff — kindly, and with one hand ready to grab his collar — and said, "You signed the grain treaties."

"I remember the grain treaties," I said, and then, because honesty had become a habit somewhere in the last four moon-turns without my permission, "I remember signing them. I don't remember deciding to sign them. There's a difference, and the difference is the whole of me."

She sat down on the edge of the bed and took my hand, which she has done perhaps forty times in eleven years of marriage, and I could count them because I keep accounts of such things the way misers keep coins. "Then we'll rebuild the ledger," she said. "Together. Page by page."

That is what I married her for, incidentally. Not the alliances her father brought, not the face that launched three diplomatic incidents. The ledgers. A woman who hears *we will rebuild* where other people hear *you poor broken thing.*

* * *

The trouble with rebuilding a ledger is learning how much was stolen from it.

Chancellor Brecht came to me that first week with the honest faces of a man delivering bad news he has rehearsed in front of a mirror. He laid out the Vellum Guild's accounts — or what passed for accounts, though they were written in a cipher Corvane had designed himself, because apparently even a kingdom's bookkeeping must have secrets — and explained, in the tone men use for arithmetic that has gone wrong, exactly what the guild had been trading in all these years.

Impossible things. That is not my phrase. It was theirs. It was in their own charter, inked by some guildmaster two centuries dead who presumably thought it charming: *the trade in impossible things.* Relics that remembered their owners. Charms carved from the bones of the dead so the dead could go on advising their grandchildren about weather and marriage and grudges. And above all — above and beneath everything — bindings.

"You signed the charter renewals," Brecht said gently. "Every five years. Your father signed them before you."

"Did I," I said. It was not a question but I made it one anyway, because there are only so many ways to ask a man *was I present for my own life?*

"The guild's argument," Brecht went on, "was stability. The binding kept you—" he caught himself, which I appreciated, since most men would not have bothered catching themselves, "—kept the crown steady through the succession crisis. Through the western famine. Through the border war. One man's sleep against ten thousand waking lives. They will make that argument again, sire. They are already making it. Half the court thinks Corvane was a patriot."

"And the other half?"

"The other half remembers what your eyes looked like last winter, and does not say so aloud."

I thanked him. He left. I sat in my study with the guild's ciphered ledgers spread before me and felt something I can only describe as homesickness for a house I had never lived in — grief for the king who had occupied my body while I slept inside him, who had done things in my name that my hands remembered doing. My hands remembered. That was the worst of it. I would reach for a pen and find the grip wrong, too tight, the way a stranger holds a tool.

* * *

It took me nine days to learn where Freya Almeida had gone.

Nine days, because the palace staff had developed an institutional allergy to saying her name in my presence, and because Tam Vess — restored to his post, promoted twice over my signature, which he informed me he would be framing rather than hanging — had decided that the woman who saved my life deserved better than to become a court curiosity.

"She's not here," he told me, when I finally cornered him in the guard barracks and asked him directly. "And before you ask where she is, I'm going to tell you that she'd hate being found, and then I'm going to tell you anyway, because you're the king and I've learned what happens when I decide what the king shouldn't know." He rubbed his jaw. His conscience, I gathered, had been through a war recently and still limped. "Coastal village. Saltmere, past the marshes. She carves charms now. Plain ones. For fishermen's children mostly."

"Plain ones," I repeated.

"That's what Ondine did to the village before—" He stopped. Recalibrated. "What happened at the ossuary took her hearing, Majesty. All of it. The voices she carried her whole life. She gave them up to pull you out of that crown." He met my eyes, and I saw that he was choosing every word the way a sapper chooses footing. "So when you go to thank her — and you're going to go, I know that look, I've stood guard outside that look — understand that she cannot hear a word of it. Whatever you feel, whatever you owe, it lands in silence. You should know that before you dress for it."

I dismissed him, and then I sat with what he'd said, and then I laughed — alone, badly, the laugh of a man who has just understood the shape of a joke played on him by God or fate or whichever clerk handles these filings.

Of course. Of course it was this. The woman who heard everything — the dead, the relics, the whispers in bone and ivory and the marrow of the world — had bought my life with her ears, and now the debt I owed her was payable only in words, and words were precisely the currency she could no longer collect.

Somewhere, I felt certain, Maren the cartographer was laughing too, wherever dead mapmakers go.

* * *

I went as myself, which is to say I went badly disguised.

A king traveling incognito is a fiction maintained entirely by the politeness of everyone around him. I wore a merchant's coat and rode an unremarkable horse and was recognized within the first hour by a toll-keeper, a shepherd, and a child who shouted "it's the king!" with the unfiltered delight of someone who has not yet learned that royalty prefers its miracles deniable. By Saltmere, half the village knew I was coming, and the other half pretended not to, which is the more sophisticated courtesy.

Freya's workshop stood at the end of the shingle beach, a low stone building with salt-crusted windows and a door that opened before I knocked.

She had known, then. Of course she had known — no, wait. She hadn't. That was the thing. She couldn't have known; the dead who would have warned her were silent now, all of them, forever. She had simply seen the horse, or heard the child shouting, or watched the village pretend very hard not to look at me. Ordinary sight. Ordinary hearing. The plain senses of a plain woman in a plain doorway, and I realized with a lurch that I had never actually seen her plainly before. In the throne room, in the aftermath, in the stories the court told — she had always been wrapped in the terrible gift, the bone-carver who heard the dead. Now she was just a woman with sea-wind in her hair and carving dust on her apron, squinting at me in daylight.

"Majesty," she said. Her voice was rougher than I remembered. Disuse will do that, or maybe use of a different kind. "Tam said you'd come eventually. He said you were stubborn about debts."

"He would know. He deserted once over one."

Something moved through her face — not quite a smile, more the memory of where smiles were kept — and she stepped back to let me in.

The workshop smelled of salt and bone dust and lamp oil. Racks of finished charms lined the walls: small carved fish, knots, moons, hands. Nothing ornate. Nothing that hummed. I knew enough now to know what I was looking at — or rather, what I wasn't. Every piece silent. Every piece just bone.

She set out tea without asking whether I wanted it, which struck me as either great confidence or great indifference, and possibly both.

"I've come to thank you," I said, when the cups were poured, and immediately heard how it sounded — a king announcing gratitude like a proclamation. I tried again. "I mean — I don't know how to do this. There's no protocol for it. Believe me, I checked. Brecht looked. Four hundred years of court records and not one entry for *what do you say to the woman who died for you and lived.*"

"You don't have to say anything," Freya said. She turned her cup slowly on its saucer. "That's rather the point. I can't hear it. Not the words — those I hear fine, my ears work, thank you — but the rest. What's underneath words. I used to hear what's underneath everything." She looked up at me, and her eyes were steady and tired and utterly unsentimental. "You could stand there swearing eternal fealty until your voice gave out and I'd get exactly what any deaf woman gets. The shapes of your mouth. So please don't perform it. It wastes both our afternoons."

"All right," I said. "Then I'll say it plainly and let it land where it lands. You saved my life. You gave up the only thing that ever made you feel less alone to do it. And I will spend the rest of my reign trying to deserve it, knowing full well that deserving isn't repaying and that nothing I do will ever balance the column." I paused. "I was a bookkeeper's son before I was a prince. Unbalanced columns haunt me."

For a moment — a real moment, unperformed, the kind I had learned to treasure the way starving men treasure bread — she almost smiled. "My mother balanced columns too," she said. "In her way." And then the moment closed, because her mother's way had included forging the thing that ate me, and some wounds do not stay bandaged for tea.

We drank our tea in a silence that was not comfortable but was honest, which is rarer and worth more.

* * *

Before I left, I walked the beach with her, because she said the workshop got stuffy and I suspect she wanted me out of it before I started rearranging her shelves with royal helpfulness.

The tide was going out. Gulls argued over something in the wrack. Down the shore, Tam Vess was mending nets with a fisherman's family, having apparently appointed himself the village's part-time guard, part-time gossip, and full-time excuse to stay near Freya without saying so — a strategy I recognized, being a practitioner of it with my own queen these twenty years.

"I abolished the guild," I told her. "Yesterday. The trade in impossible things, all of it. The charter's burned, the workshops sealed, the ledgers turned over to the crown archives where anyone may read them. Corvane's cipher took Brecht's clerks six weeks to break and what they found in it made three of them resign." I watched her profile. "I know what you'll say. That some of it was good. The charms, the voices, the comfort — that families relied on it, that the dead weren't monsters, that silencing isn't the same as saving. Ondine already made the argument, at sword-point, and she made it well."

"Did she win?"

"No. But she made me lose sleep, which I suppose is the tax on any argument worth having." I picked up a flat stone and skipped it — badly; kings skip stones the way they ride horses, with more conviction than skill. "Here's why I did it anyway. The guild's founding idea was that impossibility could be managed. Bottled, priced, distributed. That you could put the dead's voices into tokens and sell them at fair market rate and nothing would ever go wrong as long as the accounting was careful." I looked at her. "You were their best accountant, Freya. Their most careful hand. And the careful accounting ended with a thing wearing my face and your mother's fingerprints in its mold. If diligence couldn't hold it, nothing could. Some doors need to be locked, not managed."

She was quiet for a long time. The tide sucked at the shingle.

"My mother thought she was managing it too," she said finally. "Maren showed me the marks in the original mold. They were mine. Or mine-to-be. She trained me for maintenance without ever telling me what I'd maintain." She bent, picked up a piece of driftwood, turned it over in her fingers the way I imagined she once turned bones, listening for something that no longer spoke. "I spent my whole life thinking my gift was mine. It was an apprenticeship. I was a tool being kept sharp." She set the driftwood down. "So yes. Burn the charter. Lock the door. Just—" and here her voice did something complicated, "—just leave the fishermen their charms. The plain ones. Let them carve whatever they like for their children, with no voices in it and no price on it. Silence given freely is different from silence imposed. Even I can hear that much."

"It shall be law by autumn," I said. "Carving for love, exempt from guild, tax, and crown interference. I'll write it myself. My own hand. Which, incidentally, grips pens strangely now — too tight, like a stranger's — so forgive the handwriting."

That earned me the smile. The real one, brief as a gull's shadow, gone before I could be certain I'd seen it.

* * *

At the door of the workshop, when I mounted my horse to leave, I made my mistake.

I had planned not to. Tam had briefed me, Isolde had coached me, I had rehearsed against the mirror of my own vanity for the entire ride south. Do not bow too deeply — it makes it ceremony. Do not offer gold — it makes it purchase. Do not promise visits — it makes it patronage. Say goodbye like a man leaving a friend's house, because that is what it is.

But at the last moment, swinging up into the saddle, I heard myself say: "If you ever need anything—"

"Don't," Freya said. Not cruelly. Wearyly, the way you'd wave off a fly you've grown fond of. "Please. Don't finish that sentence. Because here's the truth of it, Majesty, and you may carry it home as payment if you like: there is nothing you can give me. Not gold, not titles, not favor. You want to repay me and you can't, and every offer you make is going to remind me of what I paid. So the kindest thing you can do — the only thing — is go be a good king and let me carve my fish." She folded her arms, sea wind pulling at her hair. "Debts like ours don't get paid. They get honored. Honor yours by governing. I'll honor mine by living quietly and liking it."

I sat there on my horse like an idiot, holding the unfinished sentence in my mouth where it tasted of ash and gratitude, and finally I nodded.

"Fishermen's children," I said. "Exempt by autumn."

"By midsummer, if you're half the king she thinks you are." She tipped her chin toward the beach, toward Tam, who was pretending very hard to be absorbed in a net. "He talks about you like a brother he's furious with. Go be worthy of it."

I turned the horse. I rode north along the shingle with the village watching me go and pretending not to, and I did not look back, because looking back would have been for me and not for her, and I had promised myself I would stop taking from this woman even the small coin of her attention.

* * *

Isolde met me in the courtyard, which she never does, which meant news.

She read my face before I dismounted — twenty years of practice — and whatever she saw made her take my arm instead of offering the report first. "Tell me later," she said. "Or don't. Was it—"

"It was what debts are," I said. "Unpayable. Honored anyway. She fed me tea and corrected my policy and sent me home to work." I looked at her, my queen, my ledger-keeper, my wife who mistakes fear for love no longer, if she ever truly did. "She also reminded me of something I intend to have framed beside Tam's promotion orders."

"What's that?"

"That the best thanks you can give someone is becoming worth the cost of them." I swung down. "Now. What news has you meeting kings in courtyards?"

Isolde hesitated. Isolde never hesitates. When she spoke, her voice had the careful flatness she saves for reports containing words like *plague* and *treason.*

"A rider from the capital outran us an hour ago," she said. "From the archive clerks breaking Corvane's final cipher — the one sealed inside his personal strongbox, the layer beneath the layers. Sire, there was a second project. Before the crown. Older. The guild didn't invent binding at Aldric's coronation." She gripped my arm tighter. "They learned it from something. And according to Corvane's own notes, that something was never destroyed. It was only ever *leased.*"

Behind us, far down the coast, faint as a gull's cry — or perhaps only the wind through the courtyard arches, or perhaps only a tired king's imagination reaching for what it had lost —

a bell rang that no one had rung.
