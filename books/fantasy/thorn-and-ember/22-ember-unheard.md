## 22. Ember, Unheard

The village is called Gullwrack, which tells you everything you need to know about the local imagination: there are gulls, and there is wreckage, and someone long ago decided that naming the place twice would be excessive. It sits at the end of a road nobody maintains, on a coast the maps mostly give up on, and in the eleven months since Tam Vess and I limped into it — him with a shoulder that clicks when it rains, me with ears full of beautiful nothing — I have come to think of it as the first honest thing I ever chose.

I carve charms now. Plain ones.

That sentence deserves some unpacking, so let's sit with it. For twenty years I carved bone the way other people pray: compulsively, desperately, and with the growing suspicion that somebody on the other end was listening. Every piece I finished hummed. A rib token from the Vellum Guild's ledger days might murmur the seller's name, or the price he'd sold his grandmother's finger-bone for, or simply weep in a key only I could hear. My whole life was conducted in that hum. The dead were my ledgers, my confidants, my constant and uninvited dinner guests.

Then I took a binding meant for a king into my own bones, shattered a crown-thing older than the dynasty, and went deaf to all of it forever.

You'd think I'd regret it more. Some mornings I do — I'll say this plainly, because Ondine was right about one thing, damn her merciful heart: silence is not peace. Silence is just silence. When I wake before dawn in our little house above the net-shed and the world doesn't murmur, there's a moment — half a heartbeat, no longer — where I reach for the voices the way you reach for a banister that isn't there anymore. And then I remember why they're gone, and whose face I saw when the last of them left, and I get up and put the kettle on, and that is that.

But here's what I didn't expect. I still know how to listen to bone. Not hear — *know*. My hands remember what my ears have lost. When I shape a charm for old Marta's grandchild, who was born early and cries like the tide owes him money, my knife finds the grain of the sheep femur and follows it, and something in the shaping settles the child the way a mother's voice settles a child, even if neither of us can say why. The villagers call them luck-charms. They're not. They're just honest work done by hands that spent too long doing dishonest work, trying to make amends one small bone at a time.

Tam says I'm being hard on myself. Tam mends nets beside me most mornings and has developed a whole philosophy of self-forgiveness that he preaches at me like weather. He also once deserted the royal guard because his conscience outvoted his oath, so I take his sermons with a grain of salt the size of his hypocrisy.

Still. He's here. That counts for more than salt.

* * *

This morning there's fog off the water, the good kind, the kind that makes the village feel like the inside of a held breath. I've set up my board on the porch of the net-shed because the light is grey and even, best for seeing the grain. My tools are laid out in their old guild order — scribe, gouge, burnisher, the paring knife — though only the last of them came with me out of the fire. The others I made myself, from antler and whalebone, and they're better. There's a lesson in that I refuse to examine too closely.

Beside me, Tam works a torn trammel net through his fingers, coaxing knots back into service. He's got rope-burn scars across both palms now and wears them the way other men wear medals. His hair is going grey at the temples, which delights me beyond all reason, and he pretends not to know it delights me, which delights me further.

"You've got your judge-face on," he says without looking up.

"I don't have a judge-face."

"Freya." He holds up a knotted section of net like an exhibit. "You looked at my repair-work like it owed you rent."

"It does. You've crossed those two lines again. Third time this week."

"They're *sister lines*—"

"They're a crime against cordage." I lean over and unpick his crossing with two fingers, and he lets me, grumbling, the way he always lets me, which is its own kind of vow after everything. "There. Now it'll hold a fish instead of merely offending one."

"You know," he says, resettling the net across his knees, "a year ago you were unmaking a god-crown in a burning throne room, and now your greatest passion is my knot hygiene."

"A year ago I couldn't hear myself think. This is an improvement."

He laughs. Out here, with the fog swallowing the harbor and the gulls arguing about something dead on the tideline, his laugh sounds enormous. It fills up space the voices used to fill. I don't tell him that. He knows it anyway; that's the trouble and the treasure of the man.

Marta's granddaughter comes down the lane then, six years old and built entirely of elbows and demands, holding out a broken charm on a leather cord. The sheep-bone fish I carved her at midwinter has snapped clean at the tail.

"It died," she announces.

"Fish do that."

"Mama says you can fix it."

"Mama is correct, which must be exhausting for her." I hold out my hand. She surrenders the fish, watches me turn it over with the gravity of a magistrate inspecting evidence.

"Did it hurt?" she asks suddenly. "When you fixed the king?"

The question lands in the quiet the way a stone lands in a well. I keep my hands moving, fitting the tail-bone back to its shoulder. "A bit," I say. "Most things worth fixing cost something to fix."

"Was it worth it?"

Out of the mouths of elbow-children. I look at her — at the fierce ordinary aliveness of her, a child who will grow up never knowing what almost wore her king, never hearing the word *Vellum* as anything but paper — and I find, to my genuine surprise, that the answer comes easy.

"Yes," I say. "Ask me again in fifty years, though. I'll have better perspective."

She accepts this, takes her mended fish, and thunders off toward the shore, and Tam looks at me over the net with an expression I've learned to read fluently: pride wearing the disguise of teasing.

"Fifty years," he says. "Bold of you to assume I'll still be putting up with you."

"You'll still be crossing sister lines. Someone has to supervise."

* * *

Afternoon brings the post-boat, which comes fortnightly if the weather forgives it, less often if it doesn't. Today it forgives. Old Breck the skipper climbs the ladder from the quay carrying a parcel wrapped in oilcloth, and I know before he reaches me that it's from the capital, because the capital is the only place that wraps anything in oilcloth.

"Brought this special," Breck says, handing it over. "Fellow met me at the dock personally. Tall sort. Looked at me like I was a document he was auditing. Said, 'Tell her the debt stands.'"

I don't ask which fellow. There's only one tall auditing sort who'd send word to Gullwrack.

Inside the oilcloth: a letter, sealed with the royal arms, and beneath it a smaller thing wrapped in wool. I open the letter first. The hand is Aldric's own, careful and slightly old-fashioned, the penmanship of a man who learned to write under tutors who believed handwriting was a moral discipline.

*Mistress Almeida,*

*Another season turns. The kingdom is quieter than it has been in thirteen years, though I am learning that quiet, like silence, is not the same as peace, and must be tended daily or it curdles.*

*The Guild is dissolved. You will have heard. What I could not dissolve was the habit of owing. So: the crown's remnant sits in the vault below the throne room, and every year on the night of bells I go down and look at it, and every year I come back up and try to govern in a manner worthy of what you paid. It is a poor repayment. I know that. But you cannot hear me say thank you, and I cannot carve, and so this letter is the only currency left to us both.*

*I am told the fishing is good on your coast. I confess I envy you. Kings are not permitted envy, so consider this intelligence gathered illegally.*

*The debt stands. It will stand past my death, and I have instructed my heir accordingly, though God help her, she thinks I'm being sentimental.*

*Yours in obligation and (illegally) in envy,*
*Aldric*

Underneath, in different ink, smaller, clearly added by another hand: *He dictated the envy part to himself and then laughed about it for an hour. — I.*

I sit with the letter a while. The gulls carry on. Somewhere behind me, Tam has stopped pretending to mend nets.

And then I unwrap the smaller bundle, and I have to set it down on the boards between us and look at it for a long moment before I trust my hands to be steady.

It's a carving. Small, palm-sized, whale-ivory, unfinished — deliberately, obviously unfinished, one edge still rough, waiting for a carver's decision. And I know the hand that shaped it. I know it the way you know your own reflection: the long sweeping stroke, the way the grain is followed rather than fought.

My mother's marks. From the workshop Maren led me to beneath the palace, where the original crown-mold waited in the dark with my whole inheritance coiled around it. The guards catalogued everything after Corvane fell. Most of it burned. Someone saved this.

"He had it sent to the only person alive who could finish it properly," Tam says quietly. "Or bury it. His words were 'she'll know which.'"

"Did he actually say that?"

"No. But he had the look of a man who'd thought it."

I pick it up. The ivory is cool and patient. My thumb finds the unfinished edge, and my hands — my traitor, redeemed, magnificent hands — already know three ways to resolve it, and each way is wrong, and each way is mine to choose, and that is the entire point of the gift. He hasn't sent me a relic. He's sent me a decision.

I wrap it back in the wool. Not today. Maybe not this year. Some doors you leave shut until you're sure you want what's behind them.

But I keep it on the shelf above the hearth, next to the jar of buttons and the chipped blue cup Tam refuses to replace, and every morning I look at it, and every morning it stays quiet, and every morning that gets a little easier to be grateful for.

* * *

Evening. The fog has burned off into a sky the color of hammered tin, and the village smells of woodsmoke and fish guts and salt, which is to say it smells like home, though it took my nose a shamefully long time to agree.

We eat on the porch because the house is too warm with the stove going. Bread, smoked mackerel, the last of Marta's pickled onions, which I defend from Tam with the ferocity of a woman who has survived worse than onion theft. Afterward he goes back to the nets because he's the kind of man who can't leave a task half-mended, and I stay with my carving board, working through the commission pile: three charms for Breck's crew, a birth-token for the Hollis baby due at spring tide, a plain memorial bead for old Sef's widow, who asked for it "without any of your listening nonsense, girl," and got my most sincere promise.

It's full dark when I finish the last of Breck's charms — a simple protective hook, sheep bone, nothing in it but skill and good intentions. I turn it in my fingers, checking the edges, the balance, the burnish.

And it hums.

Once. Faintly. A single note, low and brief, like a string plucked in a far room, like a voice clearing its throat at the edge of audibility, like — and I know how mad this sounds, I have had eleven months to construct alternative explanations and none of them survive contact with the truth — like something dead, or nearly dead, or barely born into whatever comes after, saying *here. I am here. Do you hear me?*

My whole body goes still. The note fades. The night resumes its ordinary sounds: surf, wind, Tam's steady breathing over the nets, the gulls settling in the rocks.

Eleven months of silence, and one note, arriving unasked, unearned, unexplained. Not the flood of voices that broke me. Not the crown's chorus. Just — a knock. A door, somewhere very far away, standing ajar.

Ondine would say it's a flaw in the silencing, a thread she missed, a debt the universe hasn't finished collecting. Corvane — if any part of him survived his fall, which I genuinely don't care to investigate — would say it's an opportunity, and I'd laugh in his face. Maren, wherever the dead cartographer drifts now that I can't follow, would probably just say *well?*

Well.

Here is what I know. I chose this silence. I paid for it with the only coin I had, and I'd pay again, and I am not going to spend my remaining years flinching at doorways or pressing shells to my ears like a lovesick girl. Whatever hums in the bone, it can knock all it likes. I am not obliged to answer. That, too, is a freedom I bought.

But I am not going to pretend I didn't hear it, either. That would be its own kind of dishonesty, and I did my decades of dishonest work.

So I do neither. I don't press the charm to my ear. I don't throw it in the sea. I set it gently on the corner of the board, apart from the others, facing the water — and I smile, because it seems the dead haven't entirely given up on me, and I find, to my considerable surprise, that I haven't given up on them either.

Tam looks over. "That one different?"

"Maybe."

"Going to tell me?"

"Eventually." I blow out the lamp, leaving the charm in the starlight. "It's only knocked once. Let's see if it knocks again."

Out past the breakwater, the sea keeps its own counsel, and the small bone sits quiet in the dark, humming nothing, promising nothing, waiting — patient as the dead, hopeful as the living — for morning.
