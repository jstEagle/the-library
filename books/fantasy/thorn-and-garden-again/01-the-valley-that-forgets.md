## 1. The Valley That Forgets

The storm came to Rafael Sterling every morning at dawn, and every morning Rafael strangled it in its sleep.

It began as a taste — copper and ozone on the back of the tongue, like biting a coin left out in lightning weather. Then a prickling along the forearms, beneath the skin, where twenty years ago the gift had lived loud and unhidden. Rafael lay still beneath their quilt in the small stone house at the valley's northern bend and breathed the way Grandmother had taught them before she died: four counts in, seven counts held, eight counts out. *The storm is water,* she used to say, her knotted hands folded over her cane. *And water can be poured thin. Pour yourself thin, child. Pour yourself into the ground and let it drink you down.*

So Rafael poured.

They felt it drain out of them through the soles of their feet, through the floorboards, into the packed earth of the Hollow, where magic went to die. That was the whole truth of this valley, the reason exiles like the Sterlings had been sent here two generations past: the Hollow drank power the way sand drank rain. Nothing gifted could live here. Nothing gifted could even cross its borders without going quiet, dimmed, muffled to a whisper of itself. The old kings had meant it as a prison. The Sterlings had learned to call it mercy.

By the time the first light cleared the eastern ridge, Rafael was only Rafael. A woman of forty-three with grey coming in early at her temples, callused hands, and a faint ache behind her eyes that never quite left — the toll of the strangling, paid daily for two decades. She rose, dressed in wool the color of river stones, and put the kettle on.

Through the window above her washbasin she could see the border stones.

There were nine of them visible from the village of Netherhollow, standing in a broken crescent along the southern rim where the valley opened toward the low country. Grey menhirs taller than a house, older than any kingdom, carved with spirals no one had ever managed to read. They did not glow or hum or do anything so dramatic as the ballads claimed warding stones should. They simply stood, patient as gravestones, and everything beyond them was the world, and everything inside them was the Hollow, and Rafael had not crossed that line since she was twenty-three years old.

Twenty years. She had watched seasons wheel around those stones like horses around a maypole. She had watched children be born in Netherhollow who did not know her real name — they knew her as Rafe the tinker, Rafe who fixed roofs and sharpened scythes and asked no questions. She had watched her own reflection change from a young person crackling with barely leashed thunder to this: someone careful. Someone quiet. Someone who checked the sky each morning not for beauty but for evidence.

*Pour yourself thin.* She could almost hear it now, over the whistle of the kettle. *No one hunts a shadow.*

Rafael poured tea instead of storm, and began the day.

* * *

The roof of Widow Ashka's cottage leaked at the northwest corner, and had leaked for three autumns running, because Widow Ashka was ninety-one and stubborn as a fence post and refused to admit that climbing ladders was beyond her now. So it was Rafe the tinker who climbed the ladder that morning, slate tiles balanced in a leather satchel across her back, while the widow stood below with her arms crossed and offered commentary.

"You've set that one crooked."

"It's seated fine, Ashka."

"My eyes are ninety years old, girl, but they aren't *blind*."

Rafael shifted the tile a half-finger's width to humor her, though it had been true the first time. Below, the village was waking around them the way it always woke: smoke from chimneys, the smell of Bram Hale's bread already drifting from his ovens, geese complaining near the millpond. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary smells. The ordinary music of a place that had forgotten how to want anything extraordinary, which was precisely what made it safe.

"Storm last night," Ashka said, conversationally. "You hear it? Out past the stones. Rolled around the rim all night like a dog circling a door it can't get through."

"I heard something." Rafael drove a nail home with two measured taps. The truth was she had heard more than something. The truth was she had lain awake until nearly midnight feeling that distant thunder move along the ridge, and had felt it the way a man missing a leg feels rain coming in his knee — a deep, phantom answering ache, her drowned gift stirring in its sleep and pressing up against the walls she'd built around it. Storms called to storms. Even dead ones. Even buried ones.

"Strange weather lately," Ashka went on. "My joints say worse is coming. My joints said so before the flood year too, and nobody listened then either."

"Your joints are wiser than the king's council," Rafael said mildly, and the widow cackled.

But when Rafael came down the ladder an hour later, work finished, palms dusty, she stood a moment in Ashka's garden looking south — past the terraced fields, past the millpond gone bright as hammered silver under the morning sun, all the way to the border stones — and let herself admit what the night's thunder had been telling her.

Something was gathering out there. And it wasn't only weather.

* * *

Midday found her at the forge-end of Tam Broadbelt's smithy, trading labor for a new hinge. This was ritual too — everything in Rafael's life was ritual, she'd long since stopped pretending otherwise — the same faces, the same tasks, the same jokes worn smooth as river pebbles from handling. Tam complained about his apprentices. Rafael complained about damp rot. Neither of them ever said anything that mattered, and Rafael loved them for it with a fierce, unspoken tenderness that sometimes frightened her with its size. Twenty years, and these people were the shape her life had grown around, like a tree growing against a fence.

She was quenching the hinge in the trough when the sound started.

At first it was only a tremor in the water, concentric rings shivering outward from the basin's edge. Then a hum in the iron rakes hung on the wall. Then a whine building overhead, high and strange, and Tam stuck his head out the door and said, "What in the name of — Rafe. *Rafe.* Come look at this."

She came. She looked. And the fragile peace of twenty years cracked straight down the middle like lake ice in spring.

An airship was crossing the valley.

Not over it — that would have been impossible; the winds above the Hollow were chaos, and everyone knew the royal mail ships gave the valley a wide berth, skirting the rim rather than tempting the turbulence. But this one was not skirting anything. It came wallowing up out of the south, low over the border stones themselves, close enough that Rafael could see the rivets on its hull and the banner streaming from its stern: gold crown on green field. It moved slowly, ponderously, a whale in a fish pond, and it was *broadcasting*. A herald's voice rolled out of brass horns mounted along its rail, amplified by some engine-glamour, filling the whole bowl of the valley until it seemed to come from everywhere at once, from the stones, from the sky, from inside Rafael's own skull.

"BY ORDER OF HIS MAJESTY KING ALDRIC THE SECOND OF THAT NAME, HEIR OF THE STERLING LINE, SOVEREIGN OF THE THORNMARK AND THE LOW COUNTRIES AND ALL LANDS BOUND BY CROWN LAW—"

Tam was staring up open-mouthed, soot-black face tipped back. Down the lane, doors were opening. People were coming out of houses and off porches, wiping hands on aprons, shading their eyes. The whole village turning skyward like flowers after a sun they'd never seen.

"—LET IT BE KNOWN THROUGHOUT THE REALM THAT A GREAT SICKNESS WALKS AMONG US. A ROT OF THE BLOOD, CALLED THE GIFT, WHICH CORRUPTS ALL IT TOUCHES AND FEEDS UPON THE COMMON GOOD. LET NO HOUSE SHELTER IT. LET NO VILLAGE HIDE IT. LET EVERY LOYAL SUBJECT REPORT TO THE CROWN ANY PERSON WHO SHOWS SIGNS OF THE TAINT — WHOSE EYES STORM WHEN ANGERED, WHOSE HANDS SPARK, WHOSE WORDS BEND OTHER MINDS, WHOSE SHADOW MOVES AGAINST THE LIGHT."

The voice paused. Somewhere in the crowd by the well, a child began to cry, and was hushed.

"THE CROWN HEREBY DECLARES THE HUNT FOR THE GIFTED OPEN IN ALL PROVINCES. THOSE WHO COME FORWARD WILL BE TAKEN INTO ROYAL CARE, FOR THEIR OWN PRESERVATION. THOSE WHO ARE FOUND WILL BE TAKEN ALSO. THOSE WHO HARBOR THEM—" the voice did not finish the sentence, and somehow the silence was worse than any threat could have been. It let the crowd imagine. It let Rafael imagine.

The airship wallowed on northward, following the valley rim, trailing its proclamation from one end of the Hollow to the other, making sure. Making certain that no corner of the kingdom — even the valley that forgot, even the grave where magic went to die — would fail to receive the word.

Rafael stood very still among her neighbors and kept her face arranged into the same baffled, frightened expression she saw on theirs. Inside, behind the arrangement, her mind was moving fast and cold, cataloguing, calculating, an old habit snapping awake like a dog that had pretended sleep for twenty years.

*Gifted.* They hadn't used that word in the proclamations of her childhood. Back then, when the purges had flared and guttered like bad lamps, the crown had spoken of *aberrants*, of *the touched*, of *those afflicted*. But *gifted* — that was the word the gifted used for themselves. That was the word whispered in exile kitchens, in the letters Grandmother burned after reading. The crown had stolen their own word and turned it into a warrant.

And there was something else. Something in the cadence of the herald's speech that snagged in her ear like a fishhook. *A rot of the blood which corrupts all it touches and feeds upon the common good.* Feeds. Not threatens. Not harms. *Feeds.* You chose that verb for a parasite, not a plague. You chose it when you wanted people to think of something alive, something hungry, something with a mouth.

Grandmother's stories stirred in her memory, the ones told late at night with the shutters closed, about what waited in the capital. About the old king's garden that healed, and the thing it had become.

"Filth," spat Tam beside her, loud enough for the neighbors to hear, nodding up at the receding ship. "Hunting folk like animals. What's next — hunting anyone whose grandmother sneezed wrong?"

Murmurs of agreement, uneasy, performative. Everyone agreeing loudly because agreement was safe. Rafael nodded too, and said, "Shameful," in the right tone, and hated how easily the lie came after twenty years of practice.

The airship dwindled to a speck against the western ridge and vanished. The valley settled back into its afternoon noises — hammers, geese, wind in the barley. Everything looked the same as it had an hour ago.

Nothing looked the same at all.

* * *

That evening Rafael walked to the border stones.

She told herself it was to think, and partly that was true. But her feet took the shepherd's path south without consulting her, down through the terraces and along the millstream, through stands of birch going gold at the edges, and out onto the high moor where the land opened and the nine great menhirs stood against the sunset like the fingers of a buried hand.

Up close they were always colder than the air around them. She pressed her palm flat against the nearest one, as she had done perhaps a thousand times over two decades, and felt nothing — no hum, no whisper, just stone and lichen and the faint warmth leaving her own hand. On the far side, beyond the crescent, the world fell away into the lowlands: dark woods, a thread of road, tiny lights beginning to kindle in distant farmhouses. The whole breathing kingdom, spread out below her like a banquet she was not invited to.

She had come here the day she arrived, aged twenty-three, hollow-eyed and hunted, her father's death still raw, her mother's fate unknown, her gift screaming inside her like a gull locked in a cellar. She had pressed her palm against this same stone and felt the valley reach up and take the screaming away, and she had wept with gratitude, and she had promised herself: *never again. Never again will I be seen.*

For twenty years she had kept that promise. She had poured herself thin every dawn. She had fixed roofs and sharpened scythes and laughed at Tam's jokes and become, truly become, Rafe the tinker — so thoroughly that some mornings she woke and had to search for several seconds before finding Rafael Sterling underneath, the way you grope under a bed for a thing you're no longer sure you own.

And now a voice out of the sky had spoken the word *gifted* over her home, and somewhere behind that voice was whatever had made the storms gather wrong along the ridgeline for a week, and somewhere beyond the horizon a hunt was opening like a flower made of teeth.

Her palm still rested on the stone. Slowly, deliberately, she curled her fingers into a fist and knocked once — a soft knock, knuckle on granite, half greeting, half farewell to a vow.

Because she understood, standing there in the failing light with the wind coming up cold from the lowlands, that the arithmetic had changed. Twenty years of invisibility had rested on one assumption: that the world outside would stay outside. That the hunt would pass the Hollow by, as plagues and wars and kings' whims always had, because the Hollow was worthless — no roads led in, no magic survived within, nothing here for a crown to want.

But the herald had crossed the stones. The herald had come *in*. The rules of her sanctuary were being rewritten in brass and engine-glamour, and if the crown was willing to fly its ships over the valley that forgets, then the valley had stopped being invisible too.

And there was the other thing. The thing she had been refusing to look at since midday, which now, alone on the moor with the first stars pricking through, she finally turned over and examined.

*A rot which feeds.* Green-veined skin, Grandmother had murmured once, describing the court of the old king. Roots under the skin, smiling and serene. If the sickness was spreading — if refugees were going to come stumbling over the moor ahead of it, the way refugees always came ahead of every fire the kingdom lit — then the Hollow's borders would not stop what was coming. Magic died at the stones. But hunger didn't need magic. Hunger just needed an open door.

Far off to the southeast, out past the crescent of stones, down in the dark of the lowlands, a light flickered where no farmhouse should be. Then another. Then a third, strung along the thread of the old road like lanterns carried by walking hands.

Coming north. Coming toward the stones.

Rafael Sterling stood a moment longer with her fist against the cold granite of the border, listening to the wind carry up faint sounds from the darkness — voices, many voices, and among them a thin, high note that might have been a child crying.

Then she turned and ran for the village, to wake Tam, to wake the reeve, to stand in the square and say words she had spent twenty years perfecting the art of never saying — because tomorrow, ready or not, the world was coming through the stones, and it would be knocking at her door.
