## 2. Refugees at the Stones

The first of them came on a morning so still that Rafael heard the cart wheels from half a mile off, groaning up the switchback road like something in pain.

They had been repairing the east fence when the sound started—Rafael and old Tam Broadfoot, who had lost most of his hearing to forty years of forge-smoke but could still hear a cart before anyone, as though the mountain spoke to him through his boots. He straightened, pressed one palm flat against the fence post, and looked at Rafael with an expression that needed no words.

"Travelers," Rafael said.

"Travelers don't come." Tam said it simply, the way a man states the weather. "Nobody comes to the Hollow unless they're lost or running."

The valley had rules about visitors, unwritten and ancient as the stones themselves. The border stones—twelve of them, gray and lichen-crusted, set in a broken arc where the pass narrowed—did not merely mark the edge of the Hollow. They marked the edge of *possible*. Magic died at those stones. It was why Rafael's grandmother had chosen this place, or so the family stories went: a valley where a storm-caller's gift went quiet as a held breath, where no scrying could reach, no hunter's charm could sing, no crown's divination could taste the shape of what slept inside a person's blood. The Hollow forgot you. That was its mercy and its whole design.

Rafael had spent twenty years learning to be forgotten.

It was not a life most would envy. Every dawn began with the dampening—a ritual of breath and small denials, pressing the storm down into the cellar of the self, feeding it just enough air to keep it alive without letting it thunder. Rafael did it while kneading bread, while hauling water, while mending nets down at the tarn, so that the practice wore into the bones like any other chore. Some mornings the storm pushed back. Some mornings it rained indoors. But mostly, mostly, Rafael Sterling was nobody: a quiet neighbor with strong shoulders and a talent for roofs, who fixed Tam's shingles every autumn and never once let lightning answer their temper.

Twenty years. And now there were cart wheels on the road.

* * *

There were nine of them in the first group. Rafael counted from behind Tam's woodshed, because counting was something you did before deciding anything else—who, how many, how armed, how frightened. Nine travelers out of one cart and two wagons, drawn by mules too thin for the work they'd been asked to do. A woman with a baby slung against her chest. Two men who moved like brothers even if they didn't look alike. An old man who walked with a stick he clearly hadn't owned long enough to trust. The rest were faces without stories yet, gray with dust and the particular exhaustion of people who had been running longer than they could remember.

They stopped at the border stones the way everyone stopped at them—not knowing why, only feeling it. A threshold in the skin. The woman with the baby put her hand on the nearest stone and shuddered, then laughed, a small cracked sound.

"It's quiet here," Rafael heard her say. "Do you feel it? It's *quiet*."

And Rafael, crouched in the shadow of the woodshed with sawdust in their hair, felt the truth of that land on their own shoulders like a weight: these people had crossed half a kingdom chasing silence, and the silence was real, and it was not enough.

Because Rafael could see it now. Up close, in the flat gray light of morning, there was no mistaking it.

The old man with the borrowed stick had green in him.

Not paint, not dye, not moss. Veins. They ran up beneath the skin of his wrist like rivers seen from a great height, branching, delicate, wrong—the color of new leaves, of spring sap, of something growing where nothing should grow. As Rafael watched, the old man shifted his grip on the stick, and the green pulsed. Gently. Rhythmically. Like a heartbeat that wasn't his.

Rafael knew that green. Knew it from a childhood spent listening to stories told in low voices after the lamps were blown out, stories their mother told with the door barred even in summer. *The Verdancy*, she called it. The green rot. The king's garden gone to weed. In the stories it was a fairy-tale horror, the kind of thing you used to frighten children away from the capital: *behave, or the Verdancy will find you, will root in you, will smile your smile for you.* Rafael had assumed—had *needed* to assume—that it was embellishment. Twenty years of safety had made a mythologist of everyone in the Hollow. Distance turned all monsters into stories.

But here it was, growing in an old man's arm on the far side of twelve gray stones, and it was not a story, and it had followed these people all the way to the edge of the one place magic could not go.

* * *

By midday there were thirty of them camped in the meadow below the stones, and by evening, sixty. Word had traveled faster than carts. Of course it had—the proclamation crackled from every passing airship now, the royal seal stamped across the sky in letters of smoke and light: *BY ORDER OF KING ALDRIC, ALL PERSONS OF UNGOVERNED GIFT SHALL PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR ASSESSMENT.* Assessment. Rafael had learned, over twenty years of careful listening, what the crown meant when it used soft words. Assessment meant ledgers. Ledgers meant lists. Lists meant hands.

And the refugees came anyway, because the Hollow was the one place the list could not follow—or so they believed.

Rafael worked the crowd's edges all afternoon, doing the small useful things that let a person watch without seeming to watch. Carrying water. Helping raise a tarp against the coming rain. Listening. That was the real labor: listening, sorting fear from rumor, rumor from the terrible grains of truth.

"The hunt's not a hunt," said one of the brothers, a wiry man named Colm who had been a ferryman on the Sere until the airships came. He said it to anyone who would listen, and most people wouldn't, because the alternative to his theory was unbearable. "You don't hunt what you want alive. You *harvest* it. My cousin's girl was taken in Vellford last spring. Assessed, they said. She came back three months later smiling, and her arms—" he made a gesture along his own forearm, and couldn't finish it.

"Green," whispered the woman with the baby. Her name was Isolde; Rafael had learned that much. "Her arms were green."

"And she smiled," Colm said. "That's the part nobody tells you. She smiled the whole time."

Rafael carried water and said nothing and felt each sentence settle into their chest like stones dropped into a well.

Because here was the arithmetic Rafael had never let themselves do, and which the day forced upon them regardless: the proclamation had gone out eight days ago. Eight days, and already sixty souls huddled below the border stones, already the roads behind them were emptying, already somewhere a child was being taken smiling. And where had Rafael been for those eight days? Here. Behind the stones. Dampening the storm. Tending fences. Being safe.

Safe *because* the storm was hidden. Safe because twenty years of invisibility had kept the crown's eye from ever turning toward the Hollow at all—and now the eye had turned, and the people flooding toward the valley were the ones who had run out of places to hide, the ones whose gifts were small enough to be caught, loud enough to be noticed, and not nearly strong enough to matter to anything except the thing in the king's blood that wanted feeding.

*Harvest,* Colm had said. And Rafael thought: *and I am the field they haven't found yet.*

* * *

Night came down cold, and Rafael climbed to the ridge above the meadow, as much to be alone with the guilt as to watch.

From up here the campfires made a scatter of orange constellations below the border stones, and beyond them, far to the north, the sky over the lowlands glowed faintly—not with city-light, Rafael realized, but with something greener. A bloom of color on the horizon like aurora, like the memory of spring, pulsing slow and patient.

The Verdancy was visible from here. From the safety of the stones, Rafael could see the harvest happening, the way you can see a distant fire and know, with sick certainty, whose house is burning.

"You've been up here since sundown."

Maren's voice. The herbalist had come up the ridge path without a sound, which was her way; she moved through the world like someone the world had agreed not to notice, and Rafael had always privately suspected she understood more than she let on about the cost of that. She settled onto the rock beside them, wrapped her shawl tighter, and followed their gaze north to the green glow.

"That's it, isn't it," she said. Not a question. "What your grandmother's stories were about."

"My grandmother's stories were about everything," Rafael said. "Most of them were true. That's the problem."

They sat in silence awhile. Below, a child laughed—one of the refugee children, chasing fireflies between the tents, and the sound rose up the ridge bright and unguarded, and it hurt worse than anything the day had produced.

"They're going to keep coming," Maren said finally. "Word travels. By winter there'll be hundreds. Maybe thousands." She paused. "And the stones won't stop what's in them, will they. The green." She nodded toward the meadow, where Rafael knew—at least three more of the newcomers bore the veins, though none of them knew yet, or none would say. "Magic can't cross. But corruption isn't magic. It doesn't need permission."

"No," Rafael said quietly. "I don't think it does."

"So the sanctuary has a leak in it." Maren said it without drama, the way she diagnosed a fever. "And we're sitting inside it telling ourselves we're dry."

Rafael looked at the green pulse on the horizon. Looked at the fires below. Felt the storm shift in its cellar, restless, pressing upward against twenty years of discipline, and wondered—for the first time in two decades—not whether they could keep it down, but whether they should.

"I grew up thinking the exile was a punishment," Rafael said. "My grandmother's shame. Our family's penance. Hide the gift, bury the name, live small, and maybe the world forgets you." A breath. "But sitting here watching them come—Maren, I think we weren't exiled. I think we were *spared*. And I have never once asked what that spared us *from*, or what it cost everyone who didn't get to hide."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was gentler than Rafael had ever heard it. "Guilt's a poor fuel," she said. "Burns dirty. But it burns." She rose, brushing moss from her skirt. "Come down tomorrow. Talk to them. You count things better than the rest of us—you'll see what needs seeing."

She left Rafael alone with the stars and the green horizon and the sleeping camp below the stones.

* * *

Near midnight, Rafael went down.

Not to the fires—they weren't ready for conversations, for names and stories that would make the guilt heavier—but to the border stones themselves. Twelve gray sentinels in the moonlight, lichen-crusted, older than the kingdom that hunted on their far side. Rafael laid a palm against the nearest one, as the woman Isolde had done that morning, and felt the familiar deadness. The stones drank magic like sand drank water. On this side, Rafael was only a person. It was the closest thing to peace they knew.

Then, through the stone, faint as a pulse in a wrist, they felt it.

Green.

Faint, threadlike, patient—something on the other side of the boundary, pressed up against the stones the way roots press against the wall of a cellar, testing, tasting, waiting for a crack. It wasn't in the camp. It wasn't in the refugees' veins, not yet, not really. It was in the *ground itself*, on the far side of the line where the world forgot, and it had begun, very softly, very slowly, to grow toward home.

Rafael snatched their hand back. Their heart slammed against their ribs, and deep in the cellar of themselves, the storm woke fully for the first time in months—rising, roaring, demanding—and for one terrible, clarifying second, Rafael let it rise. Just to feel it. Just to know it was still theirs.

Lightning flickered inside their closed fist, blue-white between the fingers, and the meadow below went silent, every firelit face turning toward the ridge where a stranger stood glowing in the dark.

Isolde saw. The brothers saw. Half the camp saw, before Rafael killed the light and stood shaking in the sudden blackness.

And far to the north, on the horizon, the green glow pulsed once—brighter, attentive, like an eye opening.

It had seen them too.
