## 3. What Grandmother Buried

The rain came that night as if it had been waiting for permission.

Rafael felt it before they heard it—a pressure behind the sternum, the old familiar tug of water gathering itself in the clouds beyond the ridgeline. For twenty years they had practiced not feeling it. Twenty years of swallowing each storm whole, of letting their gift pool unspent in the marrow like floodwater behind a dam, and still, on nights like this, it knocked. Politely at first. Then less politely.

They lay on their back on the narrow bed in the loft above Maren's borrowed cottage—no, not borrowed; nothing in the Hollow was ever given, only lent by silence—and watched the rafters while the first drops struck the shingles. The sound was ordinary. That was the point of the Hollow: everything here was ordinary, deliberately, stubbornly so. The valley forgot things. That was its nature and its mercy. Magic thinned to nothing at the border stones, and what could not live without power simply... faded. Rumors softened into weather. Names blurred. A person could be anyone here, which meant a person could be no one, which was precisely what Rafael Sterling had spent two decades learning how to be.

But the proclamation had cracked something.

*The crown will hunt the gifted,* the airship's voice had said, crackling across the valley like a god clearing its throat. *All who carry the old blood will be found. All who carry it will be rooted.* And then, worse, softer, almost tender: *Do not be afraid. Rooting is a mercy.*

Rafael turned the words over again in the dark, the way one worries a loose tooth. *Rooted.* The word had meant nothing to them until the refugees came, until they had seen the green threads lacing up a child's forearm like veins of malachite, until they remembered—the way memory ambushes you when your guard is down—that Grandmother Elowen used to say *rooted* the way other people said *drowned.* Quietly. With her hand pressed flat against her mouth.

Grandmother Elowen, who had died nine years ago with her secrets intact and her garden immaculate.

Rafael sat up. The rain thickened against the roof, and beneath it, faint but insistent, they felt the storm out past the ridge gathering weight, asking to be called down. They breathed through it. In through the nose, four counts. Out through the mouth, eight. Dampen, dampen, dampen. It was a discipline like any other, prayer or plowing, and it had kept them alive for twenty years.

It did not keep them from thinking about the house at the end of the lane.

* * *

Elowen Sterling's cottage stood empty these days, but Rafael kept the key around their neck on a leather cord, worn smooth as a river stone, and let themselves in once a season to air the rooms and chase the mice from the pantry. The villagers thought it was sentiment. Rafael let them think so. Sentiment was harmless. Sentiment was exactly the kind of thing a hollowed-out nobody would do.

The truth was that Rafael had never been able to make themselves go through the trunk in the back room.

Tonight, with the proclamation still ringing in their ears and green-veined refugees sleeping in Maren's barn, sentiment would not suffice. Rafael lit the lamp, set it on the worktable amid Grandmother's dried bundles of feverfew and yarrow, and knelt before the cedar trunk the way one kneels before a grave they've been avoiding.

The lock was long broken. The lid came up with a groan.

On top lay the ordinary archaeology of an old woman's life: winter cloaks smelling of camphor, a christening gown gone yellow, letters bundled with twine—correspondence from cousins in the low country, invoices from seed merchants, all of it mundane, all of it carefully arranged to look like the whole of a life. Rafael worked down through the layers with growing frustration, because some part of them had known, even kneeling there, that this was camouflage. Grandmother Elowen had been many things—sharp-tongued, tireless, terrifyingly competent with a trowel—but she had never once in her life done anything without intention.

Beneath the linens, beneath a false bottom of slats that lifted free with a fingernail's leverage, the trunk stopped being a trunk and became a reliquary.

A book bound in green leather gone black at the corners. A ring of braided silver set with a stone like frozen sap. And a letter, sealed with wax pressed by a signet Rafael had never seen: a stylized flower whose petals were also, if you looked too long, a crown.

Rafael's hands were steady—they were always steady; storms taught you that—but their breath was not.

They broke the seal.

---

*Rafael.*

*If you are reading this, then the world has finally come knocking, and I am sorry for it, and I am sorry I am not there to answer the door with you. I have left this letter where only desperation would find it, because I hoped—I prayed, though the Hollow does not favor prayer—that you would grow old never needing it.*

*You know the story we tell. You have told it yourself a hundred times, in the schoolroom voice we all use here: that the Sterlings were exiled for pride, for storm-calling, for the arrogance of a family that thought the sky belonged to them. It is a good story. It is even mostly true. But it is the wrong half of the truth, and the wrong half of the truth is more dangerous than a lie, because a lie you can learn to distrust.*

*So sit down, child, wherever you are, and light whatever lamp you have, because I am going to give you the other half.*

*We were never only storm-callers. Storm-calling is the loud inheritance, the visible one, the one that gets families burned. But the Sterlings carried a second gift, older and quieter, and it is the reason for everything that has happened to us since.*

*Long ago—we were called Fairweather then, before the crown renamed us, another theft I'll come to—we were the keepers of the Gardenwright's craft. Not growing tricks. Not hedge-magic. I mean the true thing: the art of speaking to land the way a mother speaks to a feverish child, of coaxing blight from soil, of teaching exhausted ground to breathe again. When the Sundering Famine came, when three harvests failed and the rivers ran thin as regret, it was our line the villages sent for. We walked the dead fields barefoot. We sang the roots awake. There are songs about it still, in places far enough from the capital that songs are allowed.*

*And the king knew it. Aldric the First—he named himself after no one, he was the first Aldric, remember that—came to my grandmother's grandmother in the worst year of the famine and asked, not commanded, asked, for our help. He knelt in the mud. He wept, they say, genuinely. And we gave him what he asked for: a cutting of the living heart of the Gardenwright's craft, grown from generations of our own tending, a spirit of healing rooted in a single plant, self-sustaining, meant to be planted in the royal gardens so that no kingdom would ever starve again.*

*He planted it. And then he did the thing we should have foreseen, because we were healers and healers are fools about hearts: he fed it on his own bloodline.*

*A healing spirit needs to draw from somewhere, Rafael. From soil, from rain, from the patient goodwill of the ones who tend it. Aldric gave it himself instead—bound it to his heirs, generation after generation, so that it grew strong on royal vitality the way ivy grows strong on a wall. And a spirit fed on one family's hunger for permanence does not stay a healer. It becomes what its keeper is. Ours learned to mend. His learned to hold.*

*That is what the Verdancy is. Not a plague, not a curse out of nowhere. It is our gift, stolen, starved, and bent. Every green vein on every rooted wrist is a thread of the Gardenwright's craft, twisted from mending into binding. The crown hunts the gifted now because the Verdancy hungers for gifted magic specifically—it was made from it, and it is trying to remake itself from the only substance it remembers. Your storm, if they ever catch you, would not be executed. It would be eaten.*

*I know what you're doing right now, because I did it myself at your age. You are looking for the flaw in the telling. Exile, you'll say—the Sterlings weren't exiled for hiding a garden secret. No. Listen. When my grandmother discovered what the Verdancy had become—when she saw the first rooted courtiers smiling their terrible contentment—she went to the palace and demanded the return of the cutting. She was refused. She went back with kin and craft and tried to take it, and the attempt failed, and the price of the failure was the exile of our whole line. The official record calls it sedition. The true record is simpler: we tried to steal back our own stolen heart, and lost.*

*Which brings me to the shame I have carried longest, and the reason for this letter's last instruction.*

*Your father did not die in the border skirmishes, whatever the valley has decided to remember. He went east, alone, to try again what my grandmother tried. He believed—God forgive us both, we raised him romantic—that a direct descendant of the original givers could walk into the Verdancy's heart and simply take it back, the way you'd take back a borrowed tool. He was strong, Rafael. Stronger than me. He had the storm in him like a cathedral has wind.*

*The Verdancy took him in an afternoon.*

*I say this not to wound you. I say it because you must understand what you are holding. This is not a story about a wicked king and a wronged family. It is a story about a wounded thing. The Verdancy was born to heal and has been forced to bind for four hundred years, fed on fear and appetite and the desperate love of kings for their own continuity. It does not know what it was. I truly believe it does not know. And it cannot be destroyed—my grandmother proved that much, and paid in exile for the lesson—because you cannot kill a spirit that lives in ten thousand rooted bodies. Cut it out of the crown and it dies everywhere at once, taking everyone it has rooted with it. That is not victory. That is arithmetic written in children.*

*There is another way. It is in the book beneath this letter—the Gardenwright's own hand, or near enough—and it is not a kind way either. Read it anyway. Read all of it, even the parts that ask for more than you think you have. Especially those parts.*

*I have spent my whole life keeping you small, and I am not sorry, and I am sorry. Smallness kept you alive. But smallness was never going to be enough forever, because the Verdancy grows, and kingdoms rot outward from the throne, and someday—not today, maybe, but someday—the knock will come, and someone of our blood will have to open the door.*

*I only wanted it to cost you less than it cost your father. I wanted you to have the choice he never got: to see the whole of the inheritance before deciding what to do with it.*

*Shame says: this happened to us. Responsibility says: this happened because of us, and therefore can end because of us. Choose responsibility, Rafael. It is heavier. It is the only one that sets anything down.*

*With everything I had, which was less than you deserved,*

*Your grandmother,*
*Elowen Sterling Fairweather,*
*who buried the garden and kept the thorn.*

---

The lamp had burned low. Rain still worked at the roof, patient as erosion, and somewhere below the floorboards the house settled into its night sounds, indifferent to the fact that Rafael had not moved in a very long time.

They read the letter twice more. Then they opened the book.

The pages were dense, illustrated in ink that had faded to the color of dried stems, diagrams of root-systems and binding-runes and—here, near the middle, marked with a pressed flower gone translucent with age—a rite drawn out in careful steps, each stage annotated in Elowen's cramped, furious hand. *Unbinding.* Not destruction. Not transfer to another unwilling vessel. An unbinding: the returning of a captive spirit to the open ground, with a note beside it that made Rafael's chest tighten:

*Requires an anchor of sufficient depth. The spirit must have somewhere to rest while it learns to be itself again. Storm-depth, perhaps. Perhaps nothing else.*

Rafael closed the book slowly and held it against their chest, and outside, the storm they had been refusing all evening gathered itself against the ridge and pressed, gently, insistently, against the borders of their discipline like a hand against a door.

*Anchor of sufficient depth.*

Their grandmother had known. Of course she had known. She had written the arithmetic herself and left it in a trunk for a desperate day, hoping the numbers would never need to be read.

Rafael blew out the lamp. In the darkness, the rain intensified, and the gift rose in them like a drowned thing surfacing, and for the first time in twenty years they did not immediately push it down. They just sat with it, breathing, feeling the enormous patient weight of everything they had spent a lifetime refusing.

Then, through the rain, a sound from the direction of the border stones: shouting. Many voices. And beneath the voices, threading through the dark like a smell of smoke before any fire is seen, the green-sweet scent of cut stems rising off the valley's well-water.

Sanctuary, Rafael understood suddenly, had never been a place.

It had only ever been a head start—and theirs was finished.
