## 4. The Green in the Well

Maren had spent thirty years learning the language of water, and on the morning the valley betrayed its own promise, it was the water that told her first.

She knelt at the well behind the tanner's cottage with her cupping glass and her bundle of testing herbs, the way she did every third day since the refugees began arriving — because a healer who does not check her sources is a poisoner with good intentions, her mother used to say. The well rope was slick. That was the first wrongness. The Hollow's wells ran cold and mineral-bright even in high summer, their ropes dry as old bone within an arm's length of the waterline.

She drew the bucket up and looked into it, and looked again.

A film lay across the water's surface. Not scum — she knew scum, the grey-green rot of stagnant cisterns, the oily rainbow of tannery runoff. This was finer than that. A lacework, almost delicate, like frost seen from underneath, except that it was green. Not the green of algae or duckweed but a deeper, more deliberate green, the green of something *grown* rather than accumulated, veined through with threads paler than the rest, branching like the finest work of a silversmith who had gone mad and taken up botany.

Maren dipped two fingers in before she could stop herself. Habit. Thirty years of habit: touch the water, taste the air, read what the world was saying.

The water was warm.

She snatched her hand back and pressed it against her apron as though she could wipe the knowledge off along with the wet. Wells in the Hollow were snowmelt-cold in every season. She had tested them herself, dozens of them, when the first refugees came over the ridge with their hollow eyes and their terrible stories — tested for rot, for fever-marks, for anything the desperate might carry. Clean. All of them clean. The border stones kept magic out, everyone said so, and whatever this Verdancy was, it was magic, wasn't it? It had to be. Everything the refugees described — the rooted villages, the smiling servitude, the king with something living behind his eyes — all of it was magic gone wrong, and magic stopped at the stones like rain against a roof.

But corruption, said a small cold voice in the back of her mind, the voice that sounded like her mother, is not magic. Rot is not magic. Blight is not magic. A seed does not need to be a sorcerer to grow.

She fetched a lantern, though it was full morning, and held it over the well mouth. The light sank down the stone shaft and found the walls, and Maren felt her stomach turn slowly over, like a boat coming about.

Green threaded the mortar between the stones. Fine as spider silk, pale as new birch leaves, running downward into the dark in delicate branching lines — roots. They were roots. Something below, in the aquifer itself, in the deep shared water that fed half the eastern farms, had put out roots into the well shafts of the Hollow, and it had done it quietly, patiently, without disturbing so much as a pebble.

Magic cannot follow here, she thought. But seeds are not magic. Seeds are just hunger wearing a coat.

* * *

By noon she had walked the eastern farms, and by late afternoon she wished she hadn't.

The Tarrow well: laced. The millrace where it pooled below the wheel: filmed, faintly, the pattern so fine you'd miss it if you weren't looking for the specific geometry of it — those branching veins, always the same branching veins, like a signature written in a hand too small to read. The irrigation channel behind Dellis's barley field carried a haze of green that dissolved when she stirred it and reassembled when the water stilled, patient as anything that has all the time in the world.

Dellis himself stood at his fence watching her test his water, arms crossed, face folded into the particular stubbornness of a man who has decided in advance not to believe bad news.

"It's pollen," he said. "Spring pollen off the birches. Washes into everything."

"In autumn?" Maren asked, and held up the cupping glass so he could see the sample. In the glass, the green had begun to move. Not drift — *move*, the way a sleeping thing moves, slow contractions, a rhythm. "Pollen doesn't have a pulse, Dellis."

He looked. She watched him look, watched the certainty drain out of his shoulders and leave him standing there slightly stooped, suddenly older. "The stones," he said. It wasn't an argument. It was a prayer. "The border stones keep it out. They've kept everything out for two hundred years."

"The stones keep out magic." Maren corked the glass and tucked it away. Her hands were steady; she'd trained them to be, years ago, for the sake of frightened patients, and she was grateful to them now. "This isn't magic. This is agriculture. Somebody planted something, and it's growing, and it doesn't care what our grandfathers carved into rocks."

"Then what do we do?"

It was the question she'd been carrying since dawn, and she still didn't have an answer worth the breath it took to say. Boil the water? You can boil rot out of a wound, but the thing in the aquifer was below everything, feeding on everything, and she had no idea whether fire touched it at all. Seal the wells? The valley had no other water. Leave?

Leave. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. These people had opened their doors. Half the households on the eastern slope had given up beds, cellars, winter stores, for strangers who came stumbling over the ridge with green-veined skin and stories that curdled the milk of the imagination. And now the thing they'd fled from was seeping up through the ground beneath the hearths that sheltered them, and Maren could already see how it would go — the way these things always went, in every village she'd ever lived in. First the fear. Then the pointing fingers. *They brought it. The refugees brought it.* And then doors closing, and then worse.

"We do nothing rash," she said aloud, because someone had to say something and no one else was saying it. "We tell people. Quietly, tonight, house to house — no shouting, no panic. We boil drinking water regardless. And we find Rafael Sterling."

Dellis frowned. "The quiet one? What's Sterling got to do with water?"

"Nothing," Maren said. "But Sterling's grandmother wrote the stories the refugees tell, if you listen close enough. The garden. the Verdancy. Where it came from. And I want to know what we're growing before I decide whether to fight it or run from it."

* * *

She found Rafael at dusk, on the low roof of Widow Cett's cottage, re-laying a line of flashing that had lifted in the last storm. Of course she did. Half the valley could have told her where to find Sterling without thinking: fixing something. Shingles, hinges, fences, tempers. Twenty years of being useful in a hundred small unremarkable ways, and never once — Maren realized it now, counting back — never once crossing the border stones, never once doing anything that might draw a second glance. She had thought it was modesty. Standing in Cett's yard with her sample glass in her pocket, watching the lean figure work with careful, competent hands, Maren understood it had been hiding. The most thorough hiding she had ever seen, hidden inside plain sight, inside helpfulness.

"Sterling," she called. "I need your ears and your grandmother's letters."

Rafael came down the ladder without hurry, wiping hands on a rag, and Maren watched the face settle into its usual pleasant blankness — the face of a person who has practiced having nothing remarkable about them. "Widow Cett's roof will hold another season. What letters?"

"The ones about the garden." Maren held up the cupping glass, uncorked it, let the dusk-light fall into it. The green moved in its slow pulse, serene, unhurried. "Because this is in six wells east of here, it's in the millrace, and it's in the aquifer under half the valley, and every refugee who's crossed our border in the last month has been drinking water like it for weeks before they arrived. So either you tell me what your family knows about the Verdancy, or I start knocking on doors and asking louder questions."

Something flickered behind the blankness. Not guilt exactly. Recognition — the look of a person hearing a knock they've been dreading for twenty years.

"You'd better come inside," Rafael said.

* * *

The Sterling cottage smelled of lamp oil and dried herbs and, faintly, of paper — old paper, the sweet dusty smell of things kept long past their usefulness because throwing them away would mean admitting they mattered. Rafael lit two lamps instead of one, which told Maren more than any words, and then took down a box from the top shelf of the cupboard, a box that had clearly not been opened in years, judging by the felt of dust disturbed along its lid seam.

Inside: papers. A journal bound in cracked green leather. And letters, bundled with string, in a hand Maren recognized from the refugee camp — because old Sef, the graybeard from the marches, had shown her a scrap once, a page he'd carried out of a burning archive, and sworn the handwriting belonged to the last Gardenwright.

"This is hers," Maren said. "Your grandmother's."

"My grandmother's," Rafael agreed quietly. "And before you ask — yes. I've known what the Verdancy is since I was a child. I've known what my family did. Or what was done with what my family made." The lamp flame bent in a draft, and the shadows moved on Rafael's face like something surfacing. "I also knew it couldn't cross the stones. Everyone knew that. If it's in your wells, Maren, then the stones aren't keeping out what matters, and I don't—" A pause. The rag in Rafael's hands was being wrung slowly, unconsciously, twisted tight as a rope. "I don't know what happens next. That's the truth of it. I've spent twenty years making sure nothing happened next."

"Then let me tell you what happens next," Maren said, and heard her own voice go hard, harder than she'd meant, thirty years of triage and lost patients rising up behind the words. "What happens next is that in a week, maybe two, the children start showing veins. I've seen it in the refugees — Sef's granddaughter, the little Ferrow girl. Green threads under the skin at the wrists first, then the throat. And they get *calm*, Sterling. That's the horror of it. They stop crying, stop fighting, stop wanting anything, and their parents weep over them and the children smile. That is what happens next, in the valley that took my patients in when nowhere else would, and I will not stand in this kitchen discussing boxes of old letters while it climbs up our well shafts."

"So we run." Rafael said it flatly, and Maren heard what lived under the flatness: not cowardice. Weariness. Twenty years of weariness, worn smooth as river stone. "Take the healthy ones, take the children who aren't showing yet, cross the stones and keep moving until—"

"Until what? Until we find another valley with another set of stones, and wait for the water to catch us there?" Maren spread her hands. "Sterling, I'm a herbalist. I know plants. And everything you've just shown me in that box says this thing isn't a plague. It's a *crop*. Someone planted it, someone harvests it, and it feeds on gifted folk — that's what your own grandmother's letters say, isn't it? Running doesn't starve a crop. Running just spreads the field."

"And staying feeds it!" For the first time Rafael's voice cracked open, and what was underneath was not calm at all. "You think I don't know what's in those letters? My grandmother's gift was stolen and twisted into that thing. Every person it roots, every child it eats alive from the inside — that's my family's harvest, Maren. Mine. I have spent my whole life being small so that nothing like me would ever feed it, and now it's in the *wells*, and you're asking me to fight it, and I am telling you that everything I am is exactly what it wants, and you have no idea what that costs to say."

The kitchen went very quiet. The lamps burned. Somewhere outside, a nightbird called, ordinary and indifferent.

"I know more than you think," Maren said finally, gentler. "I watched my sister root, in the marches, three years ago. She smiled at me through the window of a house I couldn't enter and told me she'd never been happier, and her wrists were green to the elbow." She let that sit. "So believe me when I say I have no love for this thing and no illusions about it. But I also know this: the refugees who came here carrying it — some of them are still themselves. Fighting it. Holding it off somehow, in their own bodies, day after day. Which means it can be resisted. Which means it can perhaps be *starved*. And which means, Sterling, that somewhere in your grandmother's letters — in the original garden, in the thing before it was broken — there may be the shape of an answer. Because you don't cure a wound by amputating the patient. You cure it by finding what the wound was *for*."

Rafael looked down at the green leather journal, and Maren watched a war move across that carefully blank face — fear against duty, twenty years of hiding against the sudden unbearable visibility of need. When Rafael spoke again, the voice was low and stripped bare.

"If I open that book — if I go looking for what my grandmother buried — there's no closing it again. You understand that. Whatever I've been, whoever I've pretended to be in this valley, it ends at that cover."

"Good," Maren said. "Because it ended anyway this morning, at the bottom of a well."

For a long moment Rafael didn't move. Then, with hands that were not quite steady, the string was cut, and the green leather journal fell open on the table between them, and the lamplight lay across pages of cramped, urgent script — and across a pressed sprig of leaves, still green after forty years, still impossibly, deliberately green.

Rafael went white.

"Maren," Sterling said, very quietly. "This sprig. It's from the original garden. My grandmother pressed it the day before the king took it from her." A pause, and the words came out like stones dropped down a well. "It's still alive."

Outside, far off toward the border stones, a bell began to ring — the alarm, the old alarm that hadn't sounded in a generation — and both of them turned toward the door as boots thundered past in the lane, voices shouting the news up the darkening street:

*Riders on the ridge! Crownhunters on the ridge!*
