## 5. Crownhunters on the Ridge

The morning the crownhunters came, Rafael was on Maren's roof, replacing slates that had slipped in the autumn gales, and so they saw the riders before anyone else did.

Three figures on the ridge road, small as beetles against the grey sky, moving at a pace that had nothing of travelers about it. Travelers ambled. Travelers stopped to rest their horses, to argue over maps, to curse the ruts. These three rode the way water rides downhill—steady, purposeful, drawn toward something. Toward the valley.

Rafael went still on the rooftop, a slate half-lifted in their hands, and watched.

The Hollow lay below them in its cup of hills, terraced fields gone gold with late harvest, smoke rising straight from forty chimneys into windless air. Twenty years Rafael had lived inside this bowl of quiet, and in all that time they had learned its rhythms the way a blind person learns a room: by touch, by echo, by the absence of anything that might betray them. The valley forgot. That was its gift, or its curse—the border stones drank magic down like thirsty ground drinks rain, and within these hills Rafael's storm-gift was a whisper under water, a thing felt rather than heard. Here they were nobody. A roofer. A neighbor. The quiet Sterling who kept to themselves and never, ever crossed the stones.

But the riders were crossing toward the ridge road, and the ridge road led down.

"Rafael." Maren's voice from below, sharp with the particular irritation she reserved for slow work. "Are you nailing those slates or courting them?"

Rafael looked down at her—broad, brown-faced, herbalist's hands stained green to the wrist—and found they couldn't speak. Not yet. They lifted one finger instead, pointed at the ridge.

Maren squinted. Her face changed slowly, the way weather changes, irritation giving way to something colder and more careful. She had come over the stones herself, two years ago, carrying nothing but a satchel of seeds and a sister's memory. She knew what purposeful riding meant.

"That'll be for the proclamation," she said quietly. "Airship came through three weeks back. Crown's hunting the gifted now, isn't it. Said they'd search every hollow and highland in the kingdom."

"They said that," Rafael agreed. Their mouth had gone dry. "They also said the hunt would be *merciful*. I remember the wording."

"Mercy from Aldric's crown." Maren spat over the fence with the precision of long practice. "I've seen mercy. It looks like my sister's hands, after."

Rafael climbed down without answering, because there was nothing in them to answer with—only an old, cold arithmetic beginning to run beneath their ribs. Three riders. One valley. And somewhere in this valley, hidden for twenty years behind dampened breath and careful hands, the last storm-caller of the Sterling line.

They had always known, in the way one knows a debt will someday come due, that the peace could not hold forever. Knowing a thing and standing in its arrival are different animals entirely.

* * *

By midday the whole valley knew. Word ran through the Hollow faster than rain off slate: riders at the top of the pass, wearing the king's colors under travel cloaks, asking questions at the border stones. Asking about storms. Asking whether anyone here remembered lightning where no clouds were, thunder from clear skies, the smell of ozone on a still day.

Rafael sat in their own kitchen and listened to Old Bram enumerate it all, pacing the length of the room with his bad leg dragging, while Tam and the Odell twins crowded the doorway and Maren stood at the window with her arms folded tight.

"—asked Piet at the mill whether he'd ever seen anything strange, sky-wise," Bram said. "Piet told them he'd once seen a cow struck dead by lightning on a cloudless day, thirty years back, before any of us came here. And you know what the tall one did? Wrote it down. Wrote it down in a little black book, like a woman noting what she needs from market."

"They're building a picture," Maren said. "Piece by piece. That's how hunters work. They don't need proof—they need a shape, and then they go looking for whatever fits it."

"A shape," Tam said. He was young, nineteen, all elbows and fear he hadn't learned to hide. "A shape of what?"

"Of a storm-caller," Rafael said.

The room went quiet. Outside, a crow called twice and thought better of it.

"You don't know that's why—" Tam began.

"I know exactly why." Rafael kept their voice level, which cost them something; underneath the levelness, their heart was going like a fist on a door. "Twenty years ago, when my family was exiled, the old king's men burned our name off every record in the capital. But names burn slower than people remember. If the crown is hunting the gifted—if the Verdancy has the throne now, if it's *harvesting*—then there's one gift it wants above all others. Storm-calling is the loudest magic there is. You can hide a healer. You can hide a root-singer, a charm-worker, a hedge-witch. You cannot hide someone who calls lightning out of a blue sky. Not forever. Not if the hunters are patient."

"And are they patient?" Bram asked.

Rafael thought of the little black book. Of the riders coming down the ridge road the way water comes downhill.

"Yes," they said. "That's the trouble."

Maren turned from the window. "Then you should go deeper in. Up past the far terraces, where the stones are thickest. Nobody lives up there, but nobody hunts up there either, not with the mist the way it gets. Wait them out. Hunters get bored. Hunters move on."

"And if they don't?" Tam asked. "What if they start pulling people out of their houses? What then, Maren? We just let them?"

"What would you have us do?" she snapped, and Rafael saw it land on him like a slap, because Tam was young enough to still believe courage was a plan. "There are forty of us who can lift a hoe and maybe eight who can swing a sword, and those three ride for a king whose garden grows out of season and eats people. You want to fight that with hoes?"

"I want us to do *something*—"

"*Something* is what got my sister rooted," Maren said, quieter now, which was worse. "Something is what the crown counts on."

Rafael listened to them circle each other and felt the strange double life of the moment: part of them standing in this kitchen, part of them already climbing the far terraces in the dark, already counting the days until the riders grew bored or the winter closed the passes. Part of them, too—a part they didn't look at directly—already hearing the storm that slept beneath their skin stir in its sleep, restless, as if it smelled rain coming.

Because that was the worst of it, the thing Rafael admitted to no one: the gift didn't sleep peacefully. Twenty years of dampening it, holding it down the way you'd hold a drowning animal underwater—it had made the gift hungry. Wild. On nights when the pressure built, Rafael walked the border stones alone and felt the thing inside them press against the silence like a hand pressed against glass. Someday, they'd always known, the glass would crack. They'd only hoped to be elsewhere when it did.

"Bram," Rafael said. "How many days' food could we carry up to the far terraces without it being noticed?"

Bram stopped pacing. Looked at them for a long moment with his rheumy old eyes, and Rafael watched understanding arrive in him—not surprise, not quite. Bram had been in the valley twenty years too. Bram had heard the stories.

"Three days' worth, quiet-like," he said. "Four if we're greedy."

"Then start tonight," Rafael said. "And Maren—" They hesitated. Every instinct they owned said *say nothing, reveal nothing, be stone.* But the kitchen was full of faces, and the riders had books, and patience cuts both ways. "If they ask about me. About the Sterling who keeps to themselves. Tell them whatever you need to tell them. My safety isn't worth this valley."

"No one's saying your name," Maren said flatly. "Not while I'm breathing."

"That may stop being your choice."

"Then it'll be a short conversation," she said, and turned back to the window, and Rafael understood that this, too, was a kind of love—the fierce, ungracious kind, the kind that plants itself between a person and danger and refuses to be moved.

* * *

It happened on the third evening.

Rafael was in the lower field, past the last of the harvest-stooks, gathering the stray sheaves before frost took them. The light was failing in that particular amber way autumn evenings have, all honey and long shadows, and the valley was quiet in the deep, settled way of places that believe themselves safe. Too settled, some traitor corner of Rafael's mind whispered. Too quiet, the way a forest goes quiet before something moves through it.

They heard the screaming first.

Not words—just sound, high and ragged, from the direction of the mill path. Then hooves. Then a second scream, younger, cut short, and Rafael was running before their mind finished the sentence *that's Wren's mother, that's the Odell girl*, their body knowing what their thoughts were still assembling: the riders had not waited. The riders had come down into the valley at dusk, when the fields were empty and the doors were open, and they had taken someone.

The mill path bent around the old alder stand, and Rafael came around it at a dead run and stopped so hard they nearly fell.

Two of the riders were there in the fading light, dismounted, and between them—held by both arms, feet barely touching the ground—was the Odell girl, Sela, fifteen years old and thin as a whip, her face white as flour. Old Piet was on his knees in the mud of the path with a hunter's boot planted between his shoulder blades, his arm bent wrong behind him. The third rider stood apart, holding the horses, and this one was a woman—tall, armored under a plain dark cloak, her hair cropped close, her face lit by the last of the daylight in a way that showed no cruelty and no kindness either. Just attention. Terrible, thorough attention.

"We know she's marked," the tall one was saying. Her voice carried without effort, pitched like a bell rung softly. "Green in the veins at the wrists. You've been hiding it. Rooted folk feel no pain, farmer—she hasn't cried out once since we took her. Hasn't struggled. Look at her eyes."

Sela's eyes. Rafael looked, and their stomach dropped through the floor of the world. The girl's gaze was fixed on the middle distance, calm, faintly smiling, the serenity of the rooted—the placid, dreaming contentment that Maren had described with such loathing—and beneath the collar of her dress, at her wrists, the veins ran faintly green as moss on north-facing stone.

"She wandered in over the stones two months back," Piet gasped, from the mud. "Refugee. We didn't—we didn't know—"

"No," the woman agreed. "You didn't. That's rather the point of valleys like this, isn't it. Places that forget." She crouched, unhurried, and took Sela's chin in a gauntleted hand, tilting the girl's face to the dying light. "The crown thanks you for your hospitality. The rooted belong to the Verdancy now. She'll be cared for in the capital. Better than you could—"

The world broke open.

Later, Rafael would try to piece together the order of it and fail. What remained in memory was not sequence but sensation: the sight of Sela's smiling eyes; the knowledge—sudden, total, physical—that those eyes would be emptied, that the girl behind them was already being eaten alive and no one, no one had come for her; and beneath that, older and vaster, the glass cracking.

The storm came up through them like a drowned thing surfacing after twenty years underwater.

There was no decision in it. That was the horror Rafael would carry afterward—not that they chose, but that they didn't. Pressure first, everywhere, ears popping, the air itself going taut as a drumhead. The alder leaves above the path shivered though there was no wind. The hair rose along Rafael's arms and they felt the sky *notice* them—felt the whole grey ceiling of the evening turn its attention downward like an enormous eye opening—and then the light came.

It did not fall from clouds. There were no clouds. It simply was, a white-blue column of fury striking the ground ten feet from the hunters, splitting the turf, hurling mud and burning grass in a fan, and the thunder arrived a heartbeat later, not rolling but *detonating*, slamming through Rafael's chest and out again, and the horses screamed and tore free, and the hunter holding Piet staggered back with his hands over his ears, and the tall woman—

The tall woman did not run. She turned, slowly, mud smoking on her cloak, and looked across the crater at Rafael standing in the field's edge with their arms out and their hair lifting in a wind that belonged to no weather, and her expression was the most frightening thing Rafael had ever seen: recognition. Calm, professional, absolute.

"There you are," she said.

The storm wanted more. That was the truth of it, the shame of it: even as Rafael clawed the gift back down—even as they crushed the pressure closed and sealed the glass over the drowning thing again, shaking, tasting copper and ozone—the storm strained toward the hunters like a dog toward meat. Somewhere in the roaring of it, Rafael had felt the exact shape of what it would take to kill all three of them, had felt how *easy*, and that knowledge lodged in their chest like a swallowed coal.

Sela crumpled when the rooted trance shattered—Rafael saw it happen, saw the green flicker gutter as the lightning's raw wash scoured the valley air, saw the girl's eyes flood with ordinary terror and pain and confusion, fifteen years old and suddenly *herself* again—and Piet rolled free of the stunned hunter and dragged her behind the alders, and Rafael stood in the smoking grass and made their arms come down.

Silence rushed back in like water into a wound. Their ears rang. Their knees nearly buckled.

They had shown themself. In front of witnesses, in front of the book, in front of the tall woman with the terrible attentive face—they had done the one thing twenty years had been built to prevent, and they had done it without choosing, and the worst part, the unspeakable part, was that some starved corner of them felt *relief*. Twenty years of holding a drowning animal underwater, and for four seconds it had breathed. Four seconds of sky and fury and being, finally, unmistakably, *seen*—and Rafael had wept with terror and something adjacent to joy, and hated themself for both.

The tall woman walked toward them. Unhurried. Her companions were regrouping by the horses, one of them bleeding from the ear, neither drawing steel, and Rafael understood dimly that they were following her lead, and that her lead was this: to cross the torn ground step by step and stop six paces away and study Rafael the way scholars study texts.

"Storm-caller," she said. Not a question.

Rafael said nothing. Their voice had gone somewhere; they weren't sure they'd get it back.

"The last one," the woman continued. "The records say the Sterlings were exterminated to a soul. Records are often wrong. Records are written by people who want the world tidy." She tilted her head. "My name is Serah Vane. I'm the one who decides, in the next few minutes, whether this valley burns."

Behind her, in the alder shade, Piet was whispering to Sela, holding her face in his ruined hands, calling her name. Maren's voice was in Rafael's memory—*no one's saying your name, not while I'm breathing*—and the smell of scorched grass was in their nose, and the storm was coiled again beneath the glass, sullen and awake now, awake in a way it had never been before, pressing its hand against the inside of their ribs.

"You saved her," Vane said, and there was something unreadable moving under the words. "You know that, don't you. Whatever else happens tonight. Whatever you're afraid of. You cracked her loose from the root with that strike—scoured it right out of her, at least for now. I've watched the Verdancy take children for six years, storm-caller, and tonight is the first time I've ever seen anyone take one back."

She held Rafael's gaze, and Rafael searched her face for the trap in it and could not find one, and that frightened them more than the lightning had.

"So decide quickly," Vane said. "Because the man beside you is writing everything down, the sun is setting, and I have orders regarding the last storm-caller that I am finding, tonight, unusually difficult to follow."

She waited. The valley held its breath around them, forty chimneys' worth of smoke rising straight up into the darkening sky, and Rafael stood in the burnt grass between their hiding place and the world, seen at last, and understood that whatever they said next, nothing in the Hollow would ever forget again.
