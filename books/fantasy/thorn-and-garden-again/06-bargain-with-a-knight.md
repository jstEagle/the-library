## 6. Bargain with a Knight

The rope around Rafael Sterling's wrists was good hemp, waxed against rain, and Serah Vane had tied it herself with the particular care of a woman who did not trust her own hands to stay steady.

They knelt in the mud of the valley road, all three of them—the storm-caller and the two refugees who had run when Vane's patrol crested the ridge. The refugees she had let go. A wordless flick of two fingers, and her soldiers had looked elsewhere while the pair scrambled into the tree line. But the storm-caller she had taken, because orders were orders, and because the sky above the Hollow had split open twenty minutes ago with a fury that no dampened, hidden gift should have been able to produce.

*The last storm-caller,* the Verdancy's whisperers in the capital had called them. *Find them. Root them.*

Vane crouched so that her eyes were level with Rafael's. Up close, the fugitive was younger than the reports suggested—thirty at most, though there was something in their face that made age hard to read, the way weather makes stone hard to date. Mud streaked one cheekbone. Their breathing was controlled, deliberate, the breathing of someone who had practiced calm until it became architecture.

"You know who I am," Vane said.

"I know what you are." Rafael's voice was low and even. "A crownhunter."

"Serah Serah Vane. Fourth Lance of the King's Hunt." She recited it flatly, the way one reads an indictment. "And you're going to tell me your name, though I already know it. Rafael Sterling. Grandchild of Elowen Sterling, who walked out of this valley once and never came back." She paused. "You knew her?"

Something moved behind Rafael's eyes—grief, or its fossil. "She died four years ago. In that house there." They tipped their chin toward a crooked cottage down the slope, smoke still rising from its chimney as if the world were ordinary.

"Then you'll have found her letter by now. The one she hid for you." Vane watched the flinch travel through them like a current through wire. Yes. They'd found it. Everyone always found the letters; the dead were sentimental that way. "Good. That saves time."

"Time for what?"

"For me to decide," Vane said, "whether I'm about to be the worst thing that ever happened to you, or merely the second worst."

Behind her, her soldiers waited by the treeline—Corporal Ashe with his crossbow slung but loaded, young Pellin picking at the bark of an alder, both of them studiously not looking at the prisoner because looking made it harder. Vane had chosen them for exactly that quality: the ability to not look. It was a skill the Hunt selected for now, though nobody wrote it down.

She stood and walked a few paces away, giving herself room to think, giving Rafael the dignity of not being watched while they tested the knots they would never escape.

The valley was beautiful. She hated that it was beautiful. The Hollow sat cupped in green hills like water in a palm, terraced fields climbing the slopes, a river braiding silver through the middle of it, roofs of slate and thatch arranged along the banks with the comfortable disorder of centuries. No spires. No banners. No out-of-season roses forcing themselves through cobblestone. Just a place where people had agreed, collectively and for twenty years, to be small.

Magic died at the border stones. Every child in the kingdom learned it as a cautionary rhyme: *the Hollow keeps, the Hollow sleeps.* The gifted who fled here shed their gifts like snakeskin at the boundary and lived out their lives as ordinary farmers, which was either mercy or amputation depending on whom you asked. Vane had asked, once, years ago, a gray-haired woman at a market stall who had crossed over in the first exodus. The woman had held up her hands—plain hands, work-worn—and said, *I don't miss it. I miss knowing what I was.*

Vane understood that distinction better than most. She had spent eleven years in the King's Hunt learning the difference between what she did and what she was, and the gap between those things had lately become wide enough to fall into.

"Serah." Corporal Ashe's voice, carefully neutral. "Rider coming down from the ridge. Green sash."

Her stomach dropped through the mud. *Green sash.* Not a hunter—a rootwarden, one of the Verdancy's own, the ones who wore the king's colors but answered to something older wearing the king's face.

She turned. The rider was still distant, a speck descending the switchback trail, but the green sash was unmistakable even at that range, and so was the way the rider sat—not guiding the horse so much as agreeing with it, that uncanny smoothness the rooted had, bodies moving like kelp in a current.

"They sent a warden," Pellin said quietly, coming up beside her. "For one storm-caller? We had it handled."

"We didn't have anything handled. We had a capture." Vane kept her voice low. "There's a difference, and the warden is here to make sure we learn it."

She glanced back at Rafael. The storm-caller had heard everything—of course they had; the wind carried sound strangely in the Hollow, another of its small mercies—and their face had gone the color of old tallow. They knew what a rootwarden meant. Everyone in the borderlands knew, now. The stories came down the refugee roads like floodwater: villages that smiled too much, children with green threading under their skin like veins in marble, whole towns that stopped grieving, stopped arguing, stopped being people, and called it peace.

*Root them,* the whisperers had said. *Do not damage the vessel.*

Vane had delivered vessels before. She thought about it often, in the hour before dawn, which was when thoughts like hers chose to arrive.

She walked back and crouched again.

"Here is what happens next," she said. "In about ten minutes, Warden Ossel reaches us. He will look at you the way a man looks at a purse he intends to empty. He will put his hand on your sternum, and something green will come out of his hand, and it will go into you, and after that you will still be standing there, and you will still have your name, but you will not be in there anymore. Do you understand me?"

"I understand," Rafael said. Their voice hadn't shaken. Vane gave them credit for it. "And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I've seen it done to a nine-year-old girl in the palace cells, and she asked me afterward if her mother had come for her yet, and her mother had been dead two years, and the thing wearing her face smiled when it said it." Vane heard her own voice go flat and hard, armor over the wound. "I'm telling you because I would rather cut off my own sword arm than put my hand on your chest and do that. And because I have a proposal that might spare us both."

Rafael studied her. Vane let them. She had a face built for being studied—plain, scarred along the jaw from a lance drill gone wrong, eyes that people described as tired even when she wasn't. Honesty was the only currency she had left, and she spent it deliberately.

"A truce," she said. "You come with me willingly. Not to the warden—to me. My escort, my custody, my report. I take you to the capital alive and unrooted, and I keep you that way as long as I am able. In exchange, you don't fight. You don't call the storm. And when we reach the palace, whatever happens—" she hesitated on the edge of the lie, then stepped back from it, because Rafael would smell a lie the way horses smelled wolves— "whatever happens, happens. I can't promise you'll walk free. Anyone who promises you that is selling you something. But I can promise you'll walk in as yourself, and every day I can buy you is a day the Verdancy doesn't have you."

"And if I refuse?"

Vane looked up the ridge, where the warden's speck had grown to a shape, and the shape had grown to a man on a gray mare, and the man's smile was already visible, wide and serene as a painted saint.

"Then I tie you properly, hand you to Ossel, and spend the rest of my life knowing exactly what I did." She said it without flinching, because flinching would have been worse than useless. "But there's more, and you're not going to like it. If you refuse—if you run, if you fight, if you give Ossel any reason to think this valley is harboring resistance—the order is fire. All of it. Every roof, every field, every person who ever crossed those stones hoping to disappear. The crown has wanted an excuse to burn the Hollow for years. The hunt finally gave them one. Your little lightning show on the ridge today was heard in three provinces, Sterling. There are men in the capital drawing maps right now."

"That wasn't—" Rafael stopped. Swallowed. Started again, quieter. "I didn't mean to. It's been dead in me for twenty years. It just—it woke up."

"I know," Vane said. "I watched it happen. I also watched you throw it sideways, away from the refugees, when you could have thrown it at us. Don't think I missed that."

The wind moved through the valley, carrying woodsmoke and the green smell of cut hay, and somewhere downslope a dog barked at nothing, and all of it—the whole living ordinary valley—hung in the balance of the next thirty seconds, balanced on the face of a person kneeling in the mud with their wrists bound.

"Why?" Rafael asked. Not *why me*. Vane noticed that. Not *why should I trust you*. Just *why*, naked, the real question underneath all the others. "Why does a crownhunter care what happens to me?"

Because of a girl named Liss, Vane thought. Because of a girl named Liss with brown hair and a talent for making bread rise without yeast, whose cell was three doors from mine, who used to sing through the wall at night until the rooting took the singing out of her, and who looked at me the morning after and smiled with her sister's smile and asked me if I was happy, serah, are you happy, everyone here is so happy—

"Because I was ordered to bring back vessels," Vane said aloud, "and I brought back eleven, and I remember all their faces, and the Verdancy counts on men like me forgetting. Forgetting is how it wins. It doesn't break people. It makes them content, and contentment forgets." She stood, knees cracking, and offered Rafael her forearm—not help, exactly, but the nearest thing to honesty her body knew how to give. "So. Willingly, with me, and I fight for you as far as my rank can reach. Or the warden, and the fire after. Choose."

Rafael looked past her, up the switchback, at the warden closing the distance with his terrible gentle smile. Then at the valley—the roofs, the river, the neighbor's house with its chimney smoking, some ordinary soul inside it unaware that their life had just been weighed. Vane saw the exact moment the arithmetic finished, saw it land in Rafael's shoulders like a yoke.

"If I go with you," Rafael said slowly, "the warden still has to report something. He won't accept *the serah handled it*."

"He'll accept it if I make him." Vane reached up and unclasped the heavy signet from her belt—the fourth-lance seal, grey steel, worn smooth by three generations of Vanes—and pressed it into Rafael's bound hands. "Swear on this. On the Hunt's law, the old law, before the whisperers rewrote it: *a willing vessel surrendered to a knight's custody may not be rooted without the knight's consent.* It's in the codex. Nobody's invoked it in six years, because nobody's been willing. But it's there, in ink, and Ossel is a creature of rules the way a leech is a creature of blood. He'll hate it. He'll honor it."

Rafael turned the signet over in their fingers. Their hands were shaking now—finally, honestly shaking—and Vane felt something twist in her chest that she refused to name, because naming it would make it harder to do what came next.

"My grandmother's letter said the Sterlings' magic was stolen," Rafael said quietly. "That it was meant to heal, and a king bent it into a chain. If I go with you—if I walk into that palace—I'm walking into the place where it happened. Where it's *happening*."

"Yes."

"And you'll get me as close to the throne as my chains allow?"

Vane blinked. Of all the answers she had braced for—begging, bargaining, cursing—that one landed like a stone in still water. "That's not what I offered."

"It's what I'm accepting." Rafael closed their fingers around the signet and lifted their bound wrists. "Willingly, Serah Vane. Under your law. Take me to the king."

Down the switchback, the warden's horse clopped onto the valley road, and Ossel's voice floated ahead of him, warm as honey left too long in the sun: *"Well met, well met! What a lovely little valley. What lovely, lucky people."*

Vane hauled Rafael to their feet, took a breath that tasted of smoke and hay and the end of twenty years of peace, and turned to meet him.

Whatever came of this bargain—hers, Rafael's, the valley's—they would all three of them pay for it in the capital. She only prayed the coin would hold.

* * *

Ossel dismounted like syrup pouring, unhurried, delighted, and his eyes went straight past Vane to Rafael with the frank hunger of a man at a banquet.

"There you are," the warden breathed, spreading his green-sashed arms. "The last storm-caller. Oh, you are *precious*."

"He's claimed," Vane said, stepping into his line of sight. "Under the old codex. Willing vessel, knight's custody, article nine." She held out her open palm, where the fourth-lance seal sat heavy as a verdict. "Witnessed and sealed, Warden. Touch him and you touch the law."

Ossel's smile stayed perfectly, terribly intact.

But his eyes, for just a moment, went cold as pond ice—and Vane knew, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life reading men across drawn blades, that she had not ended the fight.

She had only moved it to different ground.
