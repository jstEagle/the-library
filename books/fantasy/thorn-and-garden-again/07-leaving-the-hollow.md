## 7. Leaving the Hollow

The border stones did not look like much. That was the first cruelty of them.

Rafael had walked the line of them a hundred times in twenty years—gray, lichen-crusted monoliths sunk into the earth like old teeth, spaced along the ridgeline where the valley met the world. Shepherds used them to scratch their initials. Children dared each other to touch them. No one remembered, anymore, that they were not markers but walls. No one but Rafael, who had spent two decades learning the shape of the cage by pressing against its bars.

They stood at the northernmost stone now, at dawn, with frost silvering the grass and Serah Vane waiting three paces behind with her arms crossed and her patience worn thin as river ice.

"You can still refuse," Vane said. "I'll report that the valley was empty. I've lied to the crown before. It's a skill."

"I know." Rafael's breath came out white. "You lied beautifully when you told your hunters there was nothing worth burning here."

"There was something worth burning here. There is." Vane's jaw tightened. "That's why we're doing this instead."

Rafael turned back to the stone. Up close, it hummed—or rather, it *didn't*, and the absence of its hum was the loudest thing in the valley. For twenty years Rafael had felt the silence of this place the way a deaf man feels a quiet room: as pressure, as shape, as a constant low ache where sound should be. The storm-gift had not been taken from them. It had only been muffled, folded down small, buried alive beneath the skin like a seed under winter soil.

Maren had stood at the valley's edge yesterday and told them: *Magic cannot follow you past those stones, Sterling. But corruption can. Think about which one you'd rather carry.*

Rafael thought about Wren—the child they hadn't met yet, the rooted children Vane described with a voice gone flat and careful, the way people speak of things too large for grief. They thought about the green veins spreading through the well water, up the valley's throat, into the place that was supposed to forget.

"Tell me again what happens," Rafael said.

"When you cross?" Vane shrugged, a soldier's shrug, all armor over uncertainty. "I don't know. No one's ever crossed *into* the world from here carrying what you're carrying. The stories say the gift comes back. The stories also say the Hollow swallows anyone who tries to leave, so I'd take the stories lightly."

"And if I lose control?"

"Then I'll talk you down." A pause. "Or I'll put a bolt in you before you level us both. Whichever comes more naturally in the moment."

"That's very reassuring."

"I'm a very reassuring woman." But her hand had drifted, without seeming to notice, toward the crossbow slung across her back, and Rafael chose not to mention it.

* * *

They had said their goodbyes the night before, and Rafael was grateful now that it was done—that they didn't have to look back down the valley at the smoke of Maren's chimney, at the roof they'd patched every autumn for old Tam Weatherby, at the whole small geography of a life built carefully out of smallness. Saying goodbye once was hard enough. Saying it twice would have broken something.

But the body remembers, and Rafael's body kept turning toward the valley the way a tongue returns to a missing tooth.

Twenty years. Twenty years of dampening the storms, of feeling thunder build behind their ribs on hot summer nights and swallowing it down until their teeth ached. Twenty years of never letting lightning find them, of standing in doorways during storms with their hands flat against the wood, grounding themselves, whispering *not here, not here, not here*. Twenty years of being half a person and calling the half a life.

And underneath all of it, always, the knowledge—half shame, half terror—that some part of them had *liked* the storm. Even muffled. Even starving. Some part of them had pressed its ear to the prison wall and listened to the weather like music.

"Rafael." Vane's voice, quieter now. "Whatever happens on the other side of that stone—it isn't a test. You don't have to prove anything to me."

"Yes, I do," Rafael said. "Just not to you."

They stepped forward and laid both palms against the border stone.

It was cold. Colder than stone should be in autumn, cold as deep water, and beneath the cold Rafael felt the old warding—grandmother's work, or her mother's, generations of Sterlings braiding their power into the valley's bones to keep the world out and the family in. It recognized them. That was the terrible thing. It recognized them the way a lock recognizes its key, and it began, gently, almost tenderly, to let go.

"No," Rafael breathed—and then corrected themselves, because *no* wasn't true either. "Yes. Yes, do it."

The stone drank their permission like rain into drought-cracked ground.

* * *

Crossing took four steps.

On the third step, the storm-gift surfaced.

There was no gentleness in it. No slow return, no waking like a sleeper stretching. It came up the way a drowned thing surfaces—bursting, gasping, blind with need—twenty years of suffocation ending in one convulsive inhalation, and Rafael went to their knees in the wet grass with their mouth open around a scream that came out as light.

The sky answered.

Clouds that had been gray and orderly all morning boiled suddenly dark, churning inward from every horizon as if the whole atmosphere had been waiting, patient as a spider, for exactly this moment. Pressure dropped so fast Rafael's ears popped. Static crawled over their skin, lifted their hair, crackled blue-white between their fingers where they gripped the earth—and the earth *gripped back*, because they could feel everything now, everything, the water table singing below, the iron in the ridge stones humming, the vast slow respiration of the atmosphere itself turning toward them like a great face turning to regard a long-lost child.

*Home,* the storm said, in no words at all. *Home, home, home.*

And God help them, Rafael wept. Kneeling in the frost with lightning walking the clouds above in slow luminous circles, they wept—not from fear, though the fear was there, enormous, but from the sheer unbearable *rightness* of it. This was what they had been. This was the thing the valley had amputated, and now the limb grew back all at once, nerve and bone and blood, and it hurt worse than any wound because it felt like being *reminded*.

They could taste the coming rain. They could feel the charge differential between cloud and ground, could count the heartbeats until discharge, could reach out with a thought and pull the lightning down like drawing a thread through cloth. It would be easy. It would be *glorious*. Somewhere below the ridge a hillside waited, pines and granite and twenty years of their own restraint, and every cell in their body screamed to unmake it, to finally, *finally* stop holding back—

"RAFAEL!"

Vane's voice. Thin, distant, cutting through like a knife through fog.

"Look at me. Not the sky. *Me.*"

Rafael couldn't. Looking at Vane meant looking away from the storm, and the storm was home, the storm was everything, the storm was twenty years of hunger finally fed—

A hand closed on their shoulder. Warm. Human. Grounded in a way the sky would never be.

Vane had crossed to them—crossed the stones herself, into whatever this was, with her crossbow still slung and unused—and she knelt in the grass close enough that Rafael could see the frost melting off her cloak in the heat of the static, could see her eyes, steady and brown and utterly without awe.

"You want to know what I see?" she said, conversational, as if they were discussing rations. "I see a woman about to make the same mistake my sister made."

The name landed like cold water. Rafael's focus stuttered.

"She burned bright too," Vane went on, low and even, her grip tightening. "Everyone told her how special she was, how powerful, how the world had never seen anything like her. And she believed it, because why wouldn't she? Power tells you it loves you, Sterling. Power whispers *you were made for this* right up until it eats the parts of you that aren't useful." Her voice cracked, just slightly, just once. "So you look at me. Right now. And you tell me who you are when the storm goes quiet."

The question hung there. Lightning circled overhead, patient, beautiful, hungry.

Who was Rafael when the storm went quiet? Someone who patched roofs. Someone who kept their neighbor's accounts straight and sat with dying people so they weren't alone. Someone who had spent twenty years being *small* and had hated it and had chosen it anyway, every single day, because smallness kept people safe.

Someone who was more than the loudest thing inside them.

"I'm—" Their voice came out ruined, scraped raw. "I'm Rafael. I'm Rafael *Sterling*."

"And what do Sterlings do?"

"We fix things." A shuddering breath. "We don't break them."

"Then put it down," Vane said softly. "Put it down, and pick it up again later, when you're holding it instead of it holding you."

* * *

Coming down took longer than going up.

Rafael learned that quickly: the gift gave itself back all at once, but surrendering control of it was a negotiation, a long exhale drawn out over minutes while the clouds reluctantly loosened their grip and the pressure eased and the lightning withdrew into the high gray ceiling like a tide going out. They did it by breathing. By counting. By keeping their eyes fixed on Vane's face and naming, silently, ordinary things—*her cloak is wet, the grass is frosted, my hands are shaking*—until the extraordinary receded to arm's length.

When it was done, the hillside still stood. The pines still stood. The world remained, miraculously, unmade.

Rafael collapsed backward into the wet grass and lay there, wrung out, laughing in a way that was dangerously close to sobbing.

"I nearly killed us," they said to the sky.

"You nearly killed the hillside. We were never in danger." Vane settled onto the ground beside them, uncrossing her legs with a soldier's economy. "Though I'll admit the crossbow felt useless. Like bringing a spoon to a flood."

"Why did you cross the stones?" Rafael asked, turning their head to look at her. "You didn't have to. Whatever that was—you walked into it."

Vane was quiet for a moment. Above them the clouds continued their slow dissolution, shedding the last of the storm's darkness back into ordinary morning.

"My sister used to say the gifted were the luckiest people alive," she said finally. "Carrying a miracle around inside them. I used to be jealous." She plucked a blade of frost-stiffened grass and snapped it. "Then I watched what it cost her. What it cost everyone near her. And I stopped being jealous, and started being angry, and then the crown started hunting children and I got angry in a different direction." She looked at Rafael sideways. "I crossed the stones because if you'd leveled that hillside, you'd never have forgiven yourself. And someone has to keep you alive long enough to finish this."

"That's the most sentimental thing a crownhunter has ever said to me."

"I'll deny it under oath."

Rafael laughed—a real laugh, ragged and surprised out of them—and then pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. Because beneath the laughter, beneath the exhaustion, the other truth was already growing, cold and patient as the frost.

They had crossed the stones. They had their gift back, whole and roaring, and it was everything they had dreamed of for twenty years and nothing like what they had dreamed of, because dreams don't include the way it *wants*, the way it reaches through your hands toward destruction and calls it freedom.

And ahead of them lay the road to the capital. The gilded city. The king with the drowned man behind his eyes. The Verdancy, feeding, rooting, wearing contentment's face.

Rafael sat up. The storm-gift stirred beneath their skin—not raging now, but awake, attentive, a great animal lying watchful at the base of their skull. It would never sleep again. They understood that. From this morning forward, every hour of every day, they would be holding it, and it would be holding them, and the balance between those two facts would be the whole work of their life.

"Which way to the capital?" they asked.

Vane rose, shouldered her crossbow, and pointed east, where the road dropped away from the ridge into country Rafael had never seen—green and rolling and threaded, far off, with a faint shimmer of gold that might have been morning light.

Might have been.

"Three weeks' walk," Vane said. "Less, if the weather's kind."

Rafael looked at the sky—at *their* sky now, responsive, listening—and allowed themselves a thin smile.

"The weather will be kind," they said. "I'll see to it personally."

And together they walked down off the ridge, leaving the Hollow behind them for the first time in twenty years—while far to the east, in a palace of out-of-season gardens, something ancient and starving lifted its many-rooted attention and turned, slowly, toward the storm that was coming for it.
