## 8. The Road of Rooted Things

The road out of the mountains was wider than Rafael remembered roads being.

That was the first wrongness, though they didn't know it yet. Twenty years in the Hollow had shrunk their sense of the world to goat tracks and footpaths, to the packed earth between the border stones and Maren's well. So when the cart road unspooled before them—broad enough for four wagons abreast, lined with waymarkers carved with the king's thorned crown—Rafael thought only: *the world is bigger than I let myself believe.*

They would learn, over the next three days, that the world was also smaller. Smaller and quieter and smiling.

* * *

Serah Vane rode ahead with her visor up, which was itself a kind of confession. A crownhunter with her face bared was a woman off duty; a woman off duty could not be reported by a passing patrol as escorting her prisoner improperly. She had explained this to Rafael on the first morning in a voice like a ledger being balanced: *"You walk beside me, not behind me. You wear no bonds where folk can see. If anyone asks, you're my cousin from the hill country, simple-minded since a fever."*

*"Simple-minded,"* Rafael had repeated.

*"Can you look vacant?"*

*"I've had twenty years of practice looking like nothing at all."*

Something had flickered across Vane's face then—not quite sympathy, not quite its opposite—and she had turned her horse forward and said nothing else for an hour.

Now, on the third day, she reined in at the crest of a low rise and did not move for a long moment. Rafael came up beside her and followed her gaze down into the valley below, where the village of Harrow's Bend sat along its river like a thing placed rather than grown.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing. Smoke rose straight and unhurried from chimneys; laundry hung in rows, white as teeth; the fields were green to the furrows' edge without a single weed daring to show itself. And the people—

The people were all outside. Every one of them. Standing in their dooryards and doorways and lanes, faces turned up toward the road, toward the two riders on the ridge, and they were *smiling*. Not waving. Not calling out. Just smiling, uniformly, patiently, the way flowers smile at the sun without any expectation that the sun will smile back.

"Vane," Rafael said quietly. "Why are they all looking at us?"

"Because we're here." Vane's voice had gone flat, the voice she used for facts too heavy to carry any other way. "Keep riding. Don't stop. Don't meet anyone's eyes longer than courtesy allows."

"They're just standing there."

"Yes."

"How long have they been standing there?"

Vane nudged her horse forward. "Since spring, if the reports are true. The whole village. They work the fields. They eat. They sleep, presumably. They tend each other when they're sick." She paused. "They sing, sometimes. All together. At dusk. The patrols say you can hear it a mile off, and it's the worst sound in the kingdom."

"Why?"

"Because it's happy," Vane said. "Completely, perfectly happy. And there's nothing underneath it."

* * *

They rode through Harrow's Bend because the road went through Harrow's Bend, and there was no other road.

Rafael kept their eyes forward as instructed, but eyes are treacherous things, and theirs kept sliding sideways. A woman stood at a spinning wheel set out on her porch, though the wheel was still. Her hands rested in her lap, one over the other, and she smiled at Rafael with such open, uncomplicated warmth that Rafael's body responded before their mind could intervene—a smile rising to answer hers, an instinct older than thought, the animal courtesy of one human meeting another.

Rafael strangled it before it reached their mouth. Their heart hammered afterward, as if they'd narrowly avoided stepping off a cliff.

A man was repairing a fence. His hammer rose and fell, rose and fell, in perfect rhythm, and he smiled at his work, and there was something in the rhythm that made Rafael's skin crawl—the absence of variation, of hesitation, of the small human stumbles that make labor laboring. He drove nails the way rain falls. He drove nails the way a machine does what it is.

And then, near the village well, Rafael saw the child.

She couldn't have been more than six. She sat on the well's stone lip, swinging her legs, and she was watching a butterfly with an expression of mild, pleasant attention—but her eyes didn't follow it. The butterfly looped and darted and landed on her knee, and her gaze stayed fixed on the middle distance, on nothing, and she smiled, and her legs swung, and Rafael felt something in their chest crack sideways.

Because Rafael knew what children looked like when they watched butterflies. They had been that child once, in another valley, before the hiding began. Children chased the thing they watched. Children *reached*.

This child had been reached into, and whatever reached her had left the reaching part of her hollow, and filled the hollow with contentment, because contentment was cheaper than joy and lasted longer.

"Vane." Rafael's voice came out cracked. "The girl. By the well."

"I see her."

"Is she—"

"Rooted." Vane said the word the way a surgeon names a tumor. "Whole village, most likely. Keep your face still. They notice distress. It—" she hesitated, and Rafael heard, for the first time since the ridge, something almost like fear in her, "—it interests them."

Ahead, the villagers nearest the road had begun, very slowly, to turn. Not to follow—nothing so aggressive as following. They simply rotated, with the smooth unhurried patience of sunflowers, keeping their faces oriented toward the riders as the riders passed through them. Smiling. Dozens of smiles. Rafael counted eleven before they made themselves stop counting.

And beneath the hoofbeats, faint but unmistakable, someone had begun to hum.

* * *

They made camp that night two miles beyond the village, on a bald hill where Vane insisted on building no fire. Rafael sat wrapped in a blanket, watching the dark line of Harrow's Bend below, where a single warm glow marked the tavern or the temple or wherever rooted people gathered in the evenings, and tried to make their hands stop shaking.

"It feeds," Rafael said finally. "Back at the stones, the refugees—the ones with the green in their veins—I asked Maren what it wanted, and nobody knew. But today I understood. It doesn't want land. It doesn't want bodies. Those are just..." They searched for the word. "Pots. It wants what's inside the pots."

"Magic," Vane said. She was cleaning her sword with the methodical care of someone who needed her hands busy. "Yes."

"But those people aren't gifted. Harrow's Bend is farmers and millers and a wheelwright. I didn't feel anything from them—no gift-sense, nothing. So why root them?"

"Because rooting isn't the harvest. It's the *field*." Vane set the whetstone down. "I've escorted rooted villages before. Transferred prisoners through them. You learn things, watching. The rooted don't dream—or rather, they dream in common, all stitched into the same quiet. And every gifted person who passes through, every storm-caller or flame-wright or root-speaker, feels it pull at them. Like a hook testing the water." She looked up. "The Verdancy plants a village the way a fisherman sinks a weir. Then it waits for the fish to swim through."

Rafael thought of the child at the well. Of the humming. "And when a gifted person swims through?"

"Then it roots them too, if it can. And a rooted gifted person—" Vane's jaw worked. "A rooted gifted person gives everything. Forever. Their gift doesn't die, it gets *drunk*. Slowly, over years. The entity spreads it through the whole web. One hedge-witch rooted in a village like that can feed a hundred miles of field." She paused. "There's a reason the hunt exists, Sterling. The Verdancy doesn't just tolerate the crownhunters. It *directs* us. Every gifted person we drag to the capital goes into the garden under the palace, and the garden drinks them, and the fields spread, and the harvest grows."

"The hunt isn't a purge," Rafael said slowly. The horror of it settled over them like cold water. "It's a *roundup*."

"Now you understand why I stopped sleeping well."

Rafael pulled the blanket tighter. Below, the glow in Harrow's Bend pulsed gently, once, twice, like a slow heartbeat, and Rafael realized with a lurch that they could *feel* it now—faintly, distantly, the way you feel a drum through a floor. A vast, patient attention, spread thin across the countryside, tasting the air.

Tasting *them*.

"Vane. Can it sense me?"

"It sensed you the moment you crossed the border stones, I'd guess. Your gift roared back loud enough to level a hillside." She said it without accusation, but the words landed anyway. "It knows a storm-caller is on the road to the capital. Which means it *wants* you to arrive. Which means—" she met Rafael's eyes in the dark, "—we're not sneaking anywhere. We're walking down its throat. I need you to understand that before tomorrow."

Rafael lay back against their pack and stared at the stars, which seemed very far away and very indifferent, and listened to the pulse from the valley, patient as tide.

"Then we'd better make sure it chokes on us," they said.

Vane almost smiled. Almost.

* * *

The second village was worse, because by then Rafael knew what to look for.

Milletford sat at a crossroads, and unlike Harrow's Bend it was not entirely rooted—or it hadn't been. They saw it in the first minute: a knot of perhaps twenty people huddled in the square around a cart loaded with belongings, loading it with the frantic, stumbling speed of the unrooted, while all around them the rest of the town went about its business in that terrible serene rhythm, smiling, humming, occasionally pausing to watch the fugitives pack with mild, friendly interest.

An old woman was arguing with a rooted man—her son, by the family resemblance carved in grief. He stood in his doorway, smiling, while she clutched his sleeve.

"Milo. Milo, come with us. Please. Whatever it is, whatever's got you, we'll get it out of you, there are people in the capital who—"

"I'm fine, Mother." His voice was warm honey. "Everything's fine here. Why would you leave? Everything you need is here."

"You haven't slept in your own bed in a month—you stand in the yard all night, all of you, facing the east—"

"The light comes from the east," Milo said pleasantly. "You should stay. There's room. There's always room."

Rafael might have ridden past. Might have kept their face still and their hands on the reins, as instructed, as survival demanded. But the old woman's hands were shaking on her son's sleeve, and Rafael had spent twenty years learning exactly how much a person could bear to watch without acting, and the answer, they discovered, was less than this.

They swung down from the horse.

"Sterling—" Vane's warning hiss.

Rafael crossed the square to the old woman and took her other arm. Up close, Milo's smile was worse than it had been at a distance; there were fine green threads visible at his temples, beneath the skin, delicate as frost ferns, branching down his neck and disappearing under his collar.

"Ma'am," Rafael said quietly. "Take your people and go. Now, while it's day. Don't stop until you're past the mountains."

"And leave him?" Her voice broke. "He's my *son*."

"He's still in there," Rafael said. "What's wearing him is telling you the truth, in its way—everything you need *is* here. It needs him. And it needs you to stay, because grief is a door." They glanced at Milo's serene face, and made themselves say the rest. "But right now, staying won't save him. Going might. There are people working on how to cut these threads. Go where it's cold and high and the water runs fast. It doesn't like fast water."

That last part was invention. Rafael knew it was invention. But the old woman's spine straightened, and hope is a lever that moves even when the ground beneath it is false, and she nodded, and called her people to the cart, and they rumbled out of Milletford westward while Milo watched them go, smiling, waving, waving, waving, until the road bent and took him from view.

When Rafael turned back, Vane had dismounted and stood beside their horse, and her expression was unreadable in the way of fortifications.

"That was stupid," she said.

"Yes."

"It felt its attention on you. The whole square. Did you feel it?"

Rafael had. A pressure like the moment before lightning, except soft, curious, almost *tender*—a vast green attention turning its face toward them the way the sunflowers had turned theirs. And threaded through the pressure, unmistakable now that they knew to listen for it, hunger. Not greedy hunger. Patient hunger. The hunger of a thing that has starved so long it has forgotten hunger is not peace.

"It pulled at my gift," Rafael said. "Just a little. Like a hand testing a door handle."

"And you gave it your name and address by walking over there." Vane mounted. "Next time, ride past. Whatever's left of the person will forgive you. The dead always forgive the living—it's the living who can't manage it."

Rafael climbed back into the saddle, and for a long time neither of them spoke, and the road carried them east through country that grew greener and greener, lush past all season or reason, hedgerows heavy with blossom that should have finished months ago, orchards fruiting and flowering at once, the whole land wearing abundance like a mask worn slightly too long.

* * *

On the fifth evening, camped in a hollow beech wood where the roots ran silver and the Verdancy's presence was a constant low hum beneath the soil, Rafael finally asked the question that had been growing in them since Harrow's Bend.

"Vane. When it roots a gifted person—it drinks their gift. You said that. But what does it drink it *for*? What does a healing spirit want with storm-magic? What does it build with it?"

Vane was silent a long moment, turning her sword belt buckle over and over in her fingers.

"I escorted a prisoner once," she said. "A weather-reader, an old man, taken from the coast. Rooted in transit—he just... went calm, halfway to the capital, and started smiling, and there was nothing to be done. I rode beside him for three days. On the last night he talked in his sleep. Not words, exactly. More like... weather. Storms described in a human voice. And the fields for fifty miles around bloomed that week. Out of season. Everything at once." She looked up. "It doesn't build, Sterling. It *remembers*. It takes a gift and unwinds it back into raw magic, the way you'd unravel a sweater, and it wears the wool. It's been starving for two hundred years, and every gift it drinks is a meal it never stops being hungry after."

"A healing spirit," Rafael murmured. "Grandmother wrote that it was meant to heal the land. Meant to pour magic *into* the soil, not drink it out of people."

"Then somewhere along the way, someone taught it that people were the soil."

Rafael lay awake long after Vane's breathing deepened, listening to the hum in the earth, feeling the faint tug against their gift like a hooked line testing deep water, and thought about their grandmother's letter hidden against their ribs, and about the child at the well whose eyes wouldn't follow the butterfly, and about Milo waving from his doorway, waving, waving, certain that everything anyone needed was already here.

*Two hundred years of starvation,* they thought. *Someone stole its inheritance too.*

The thought should have been pity. It arrived instead as dread, because pity was dangerous, pity was a door, and Rafael could feel, even now, the entity's attention resting on them from a hundred miles in every direction, gentle as moss, patient as roots, *pleased*.

In the morning they crested the last hills, and there it was: the capital, spread across its river plain in the dawn haze, white walls and gilded spires and gardens blazing with impossible color, beautiful as a promise.

Every garden in it was blooming out of season.

And on the wind, faint but unmistakable, came singing—thousands of voices, in perfect, contented unison, welcoming them home.
