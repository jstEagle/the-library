## 9. The Gilded City

The road crested the last hill an hour past dawn, and Rafael saw the capital for the first time.

It rose out of a morning mist like something dreamed rather than built—white towers veined with gold, terraced gardens cascading down the palace hill in ribbons of impossible color, and above it all, the airships drifting on their slow circuits like patient birds of prey. Even at this distance Rafael could hear it: the faint hum of the city, thousands of voices braided into a single low note that never quite resolved.

"Beautiful, isn't it," Vane said from the driver's bench. She had not looked back at them once since they'd left the valley. "Everyone says so. Everyone says it and means it, and no one asks why the roses bloom in Frostmonth."

Rafael said nothing. Their wrists were bound—not tightly; Vane had seen to that with a knight's efficiency and something else, something almost apologetic—but bound all the same. The chains were iron, dull and cold, and where they touched Rafael's skin they felt less like restraint than like a hand pressed over a mouth.

Because the storm was *loud* now.

Twenty years in the Hollow had taught Rafael to live with silence, to think of their gift as a river dammed behind stone, a pressure held at bay by will and habit and the valley's dead air. But outside the border stones, the gift had come back like a drowned thing surfacing, and it had not quieted since. It moved under their skin with every mile closer to the city—a restlessness, a hunger, thunder building behind the ribs. The sky overhead was clear blue for thirty miles in every direction, and Rafael could feel every drop of water in the air, every charge gathering along the distant front, the whole atmosphere waiting on them like a dog waiting on a whistle.

*Not here*, Rafael told it. *Not yet.*

"You'll want to remember your part," Vane said. "You are my prisoner. Storm-caller, taken on the northern ridge after a struggle. I am bringing you to the Verdancy's court because the crown has promised me—" she paused, and Rafael heard the old bitterness move through her voice like a stone through a current, "—because the crown has promised me things. You do not speak unless spoken to. You do not look at anyone's eyes longer than courtesy allows. And if anyone offers you fruit, or wine, or water from a silver pitcher, you refuse."

"Why?"

"Because rooted things feed what they root." She flicked the reins. "Eat nothing you haven't seen cut. Trust nothing you haven't watched made. In this city even kindness is a vector."

The road descended. The mist parted. And the gates of Aurelian—the Gilded City, crown of King Aldric's line—opened before them like a mouth.

* * *

Rafael had imagined cities before, in the long evenings of the Hollow: noise and smoke and jostling crowds, the beautiful ugly sprawl of human want. What they had not imagined was *quiet*.

The streets were full. Markets lined the avenues, stalls heaped with bread and silk and copperwork, carts rolling, children running—and yet the sound was wrong. No shouting. No laughter sharp enough to startle. The vendors called out their wares in pleasant, level voices, like men reading from the same book. The pedestrians moved with a fluid unhurried grace, parting around each other without collision, without irritation, without ever quite touching.

And the green.

It was everywhere. Ivy sheeted the tenement walls in patterns too regular to be wild. Window boxes spilled roses out of season—Frostmonth roses, fat and red as wounds. Trees stood along the boulevards with leaves so glossy they seemed lacquered, and beneath every tree the ground was soft with moss that grew in perfect circles, perfect spirals, perfect *patterns*. Rafael looked too long at one spiral and felt their eye begin to slide down it, following the curve inward, inward, toward some calm dark center—

They bit the inside of their cheek hard enough to taste blood. The spiral let them go.

"Don't stare at anything that grows," Vane murmured, without turning her head. "And don't touch."

A woman passed close to the wagon, carrying a basket of early strawberries. She smiled at Rafael—warmly, genuinely, with every tooth—and her smile did not reach any further than her teeth. Her forearms, where the sleeve fell back, were traced with fine green lines, delicate as frost-fern, running up beneath the cloth.

"She's happy," Rafael said quietly.

"She's *content*," Vane corrected. "There's a difference. Contentment doesn't ask questions. My sister used to say—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, and gave the reins a small cruel jerk that had nothing to do with the horses. "Keep your hood up."

But Rafael kept looking, because looking was the only weapon they had. They catalogued the city the way they'd once catalogued weather: the direction of the green veins (always upward, always toward the palace hill), the density of the rooting (thicker near the gardens, thinner in the tanner's quarter where the stink of lye hung heavy—even the Verdancy, it seemed, disliked certain smells), the faces of the unrooted (fewer than Rafael expected, and moving differently—quick, hunched, eyes down, the way people walked in the Hollow when the heralds' airships passed).

There were more of them than there should have been. Unrooted people, threading through the serene crowds like minnows through still water. A boy selling matches who met Rafael's eyes for half a second too long. An old man washing a doorstep who scrubbed the same clean stone in a rhythm that was unmistakably, deliberately, *bored*. People pretending. People performing contentment for the rooted neighbors around them.

An underground. Ovid's people—or people like them, scattered through the gilded streets like seeds under snow.

Rafael let one thread of their storm-gift slip loose, fine as spider-silk, and brushed it against the match-boy's shoulder as the wagon rolled past. Just a whisper of charge, static and static alone, nothing a crownhunter could name. The boy flinched—then went very still, then very carefully did not turn around.

But his hand, setting down his matches, tapped the crate twice. Pause. Twice again.

Rafael's heart hammered against the iron chains. *Someone heard.*

* * *

The palace hill swallowed the wagon in shadow as they climbed, and the beauty turned ravenous.

Gardens tiered the slope in descending arcs, and every one of them bloomed against the season's law. Winter jasmine and midsummer peonies shared beds with autumn crocuses; cherry trees heavy with blossom stood beside apple trees equally heavy with ripe fruit, pollen and perfume mingling into a sweetness so thick it sat on the tongue. Bees moved among the flowers in unhurried spirals. Everything grew toward everything else. Nothing choked anything else out. There was no competition in these gardens, no struggle, no winter—only abundance arranged into perfect, suffocating harmony.

It should have been lovely. Rafael had spent twenty years dreaming about gardens, about the family inheritance they'd been told was shame and theft. And standing here, chained in a cart amid this riotous plenty, they understood for the first time what the stories had been trying to tell them: that a garden which cannot die is not alive. That growth without season is a kind of hunger. That the Verdancy had taken the shape of Eden because Eden was the most convincing lie a parasite could wear.

"It shows you what you want," Vane said. Rafael startled; they hadn't spoken aloud. "The gardens. Early on, when the rooting first spread, people used to swear the flowers smelled like their childhoods. Like their mothers' kitchens. Like whatever they'd lost." Her gauntlets creaked on the reins. "It baits the trap with sweetness. Remember that when you're standing in front of the throne and it starts being gentle with you. It is *always* gentle right before it roots someone."

The gates of the outer court opened onto marble. Guards in white and gold took Vane's papers, took the horses, took Rafael roughly by the arms—and here Vane played her part flawlessly, snapping at them that the prisoner was to be handled gently, that a storm-caller bruised was worth half a storm-caller, that she would answer for the condition of her captive personally to whoever asked. Her hand, gripping Rafael's arm, pressed once—twice—pause—twice.

Rafael nearly laughed at the audacity of it. The knight had learned the signal in a single afternoon, watching a match-boy through a spyglass she'd pretended not to hold.

They passed through colonnades where fountains ran green-tinted and sweet-smelling, through halls where servants glided with downcast eyes and seraphic calm, deeper and deeper into the palace, and with every step Rafael felt the pressure build—not the storm this time, but something else, something *listening*. The air itself had weight here. The light through the windows fell too golden, too kind. Somewhere far below, beneath the marble and the throne room and twenty feet of royal masonry, something vast turned over in its sleep and noticed them.

*Storm-caller*, the weight seemed to say, tasting the word. *At last.*

Rafael clenched their jaw and thought of the Hollow. Of Maren's herb garden. Of Wren, somewhere in these halls, a child being slowly eaten. They put their grief and fury behind a wall of gray stillness and let the guards march them on.

* * *

The cell they gave Rafael was not a dungeon.

That was the worst of it. It was a *room*—a beautiful one, high in a tower, with a wide arched window overlooking the gardens, a bed with clean linens, a washbasin, fresh bread and cheese and a jug of water on the table. The door locked from the outside, but there was no grate, no straw, no darkness. Comfortable captivity. Courteous captivity. Captivity that wanted very badly to be liked.

Rafael did not eat. They paced instead, mapping the room, testing the window—it was barred, but the bars were set in old mortar, and mortar crumbles, given time and vibration. Beneath the floor they could feel, faint and rhythmic, a pulse. Slow as a sleeping heart. The Verdancy's heartbeat, rising up through the foundations of the palace, through the whole city, through every green-veined wrist and placid smiling face.

They sat on the bed and closed their eyes and reached.

This was the dangerous part. Reaching out with the storm inside a city that hunted storms was like striking flint in a powder store. But Rafael had spent twenty years learning to hold lightning on a fingertip, to spend a whisper where others would spend a shout, and they needed the underground more than they feared the risk.

They sent the thread out thin, thinner, thinnest—a filament of charge sliding down through the tower stones, through walls, seeking the rhythm the match-boy had tapped. Two beats. Pause. Two.

For a long time there was nothing but the pulse below, vast and slow and patient, and Rafael felt like a swimmer treading water above something enormous.

Then—faint, from somewhere in the warren of servant corridors below—two taps answered. Pause. Two.

Rafael exhaled. And began to talk, in the only language that couldn't be overheard by ears or by roots: pulses of static, tiny weathers, a code written in pressure and charge. *Prisoner. Tower. Willing. Who speaks for Ovid?*

The reply came slower, more cautiously, threaded through the palace's bones like water through limestone. And it came in fragments, from different hands—a match-seller's crackle, a laundress's hum of static, something older and steadier that might have been Ovid himself or someone trained by him—until the message assembled itself in Rafael's mind like storm-light stitching across a dark field:

*We know why you came. The child lives. The heart-root lies beneath the throne. When the court sleeps, we come to you. Do not eat. Do not sleep deeply. Above all—*

The message stuttered, broke, resumed from another quarter, urgent now:

*—do not let it learn your name. It knows the Sterling blood. It has been waiting for a Sterling for a hundred years.*

Outside the window, the out-of-season sun poured gold across the blooming gardens, and far below, in a courtyard, servants paused in their work to smile up at Rafael's tower—all together, all at once, all wearing the same warm empty smile.

The pulse beneath the floor quickened, just slightly. Glad, somehow. Welcoming.

Rafael pressed their palm flat against the cold stone and held on to the storm, and waited for night.
