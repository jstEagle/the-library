## 10. Audience with the Hollow King

They dressed him in chains that were not chains.

A chamberlain with green-threaded cuffs at his wrists—ornamental, Rafael noted, purely ornamental—fastened a collar of pale silver around Rafael's throat and pronounced it a *restraint of courtesy*, as though courtesy were something you could forge and buckle on. It weighed almost nothing. That was the point of it, Rafael suspected. The weight was meant to live in the mind.

The corridor to the throne room stretched ahead like a throat swallowed whole by a garden. Vines climbed the marble pillars in spirals too regular to be natural, each leaf turned toward them as they passed, the way sunflowers turn toward the sun—or the way an audience turns toward a door when someone important enters. The air smelled of honey and wet earth and, beneath it, faintly, of iron. Of blood kept very clean.

Rafael walked between two guards whose faces wore the same serene half-smile Rafael had seen on the rooted villagers along the road. But these smiles were thinner. Stretched over something struggling.

*He's in there,* Rafael thought, for perhaps the hundredth time since the gates. *Somewhere under all that green, he's still in there.*

Vane walked three paces behind, in her formal grays, her face arranged into the blank competence of a knight delivering a prisoner. Only Rafael, who had spent twenty years learning to read what people hid, caught the way her thumb pressed white against the seam of her sword belt. *Hold,* that pressure said. *Hold, hold.*

The doors opened without being touched.

* * *

The throne room of Aldric's court was beautiful the way a preserved specimen is beautiful. Light fell through windows of colored glass in long amber columns, and where it touched the floor, small flowers bloomed—out of season, out of soil, out of any sense of decency, pushing up between the flagstones in carpets of white and violet. Courtiers stood in loose clusters, murmuring, and their murmuring had a strange harmonized quality, like bees in a wall. No one coughed. No one shifted their weight. No one, Rafael realized with a creeping coldness, blinked at anything like a human rhythm.

And on the throne, King Aldric.

He was younger than his portraits. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the crown: not gold but living wood, a circlet of intertwined branches that had grown—*grown*—into his hair, its roots visible at his temples like pale veins climbing toward his eyes. His robes were green at the hem where the fabric had begun, gently, patiently, to sprout.

But it was his eyes that stopped Rafael's breath.

They were green, both of them, a deep and lovely green—and behind that green, far back, like a man at the bottom of a well looking up through water, something else moved. Something terrified. As Rafael crossed the floor under the eyes of the court, that drowned thing surfaced for one instant, and Rafael saw it plainly: a man screaming without a mouth, pounding without fists against the inside of his own skull.

Then the water closed over him, and the king smiled.

"Rafael Sterling." The voice was warm, resonant, pitched perfectly to fill the hall. "Storm-caller. Last of your line. You honor us."

"I didn't come to honor anyone," Rafael said. Their voice sounded small in all that harmonized silence. They made it smaller on purpose; a storm that pretends to be a breeze is left unwatched. "I came because Serah Vane offered me terms, and I accepted them. I'm told the crown keeps its bargains."

"The crown keeps everything," said the king, and laughed, and the court laughed with him—a single sound, thirty throats in unison, and Rafael's skin crawled from nape to heel. "That is rather the point of a crown."

* * *

They were brought close, close enough to see the fine grain of the wooden crown, the tiny white buds opening along its rim like a procession of teeth. Up close, the smell of honey was stronger, and underneath the honey, something older and sadder—the smell of a garden gone to seed, of compost and rain and things quietly rotting into nourishment for other things.

"You are wondering," the king said, tilting his head with a birdlike curiosity, "what I am."

"I know what you're not," Rafael said. "You're not Aldric."

"Aldric." The name was tasted, rolled, found wanting. "Aldric is a boy who liked dogs and hated lessons. Aldric cried when his tutor struck his knuckles. Aldric has been asleep for eleven years, Sterling, and he sleeps so *soundly* now. Would you wake a sleeping man to show him what his kingdom has become? What kindness is there in that?"

Behind the green, the drowning man surged upward—so suddenly that Rafael flinched. For half a heartbeat the king's face changed: the smile sagged, the eyes went wide and wet and horribly ordinary, brown, just brown, a tired man's eyes. His lips shaped a word. No sound came. But Rafael had grown up reading mouths across crowded market squares, guarding secrets in silence, and the shape was unmistakable.

*Run.*

Then the green rose in him like floodwater and smoothed his face flat again, and the warm voice resumed as though nothing had interrupted it.

"You are afraid," the Verdancy said through him. "Good. Fear is honest. I have had enough of serenity; I make it, I wear it, I am *drowned* in it—" A pause, a flicker, as if the entity had caught itself using a word it did not intend. "I am surrounded by it. But you—you came up the road burning. Your gift crackles off you even now, leashed and furious. Do you know how long it has been since anything in this room was *furious*?"

"Let him go," Rafael said. "Let Aldric go, and take me instead. Isn't that what you want? A vessel. An anchor. I've seen what you do with gifted children—I've seen Wren." They hadn't meant to say the name. It tore out of them anyway, raw-edged, and somewhere in the harmonized court a single murmur rippled and died.

Something moved behind the king's eyes. Not the drowning man this time—something colder, more curious.

"Wren," the Verdancy repeated. "Yes. A small root, a tender one. We drink slowly from children; they last longer. You disapprove."

"I don't disapprove," Rafael said, and heard their own voice drop into the low register that used to bring clouds scraping over the Hollow's ridgeline. "I *refuse* it. There's a difference, and the difference is about to matter to you."

The court had gone utterly still. Even the flowers on the floor seemed to lean in.

"Threats," the king mused. "From a collared guest. How refreshing." He rose from the throne—unhurried, graceful, the wooden crown creaking softly as it grew another hair's breadth into his scalp—and descended the dais steps to stand before Rafael. This close, Rafael could see that his skin, beneath its healthy color, was faintly mapped with green, the way paper shows the watermark only when held to light. "Do you know why we hunted your kind, Sterling? Not cruelty. Cruelty is so *crude*. We hunted because the gifted burn. Every gift is a fire, and fires consume their fuel, and when a gifted one burns out, the land burns with them. We have seen it. We have *caused* it, in our ignorance. We offer instead a garden. Stillness. Rootedness. No gift will ever gut you from the inside again. No child of yours will ever be exiled for the crime of weather in their blood."

"That's not peace," Rafael said. "That's a jar with holes in the lid."

"And yet the moths come willingly." The king smiled again, and this time there was no warmth in it at all, only patience older than the dynasty wearing a young man's mouth. "You came willingly. Through the gate, down the road, past every warning. Into my hall, wearing my courtesy-collar. Tell me, storm-caller—if I am a prison, why do your feet keep walking deeper into it?"

Because Vane's sister. Because Wren. Because a man at the bottom of a well shaping *run* with lips that would never be allowed to scream. Because twenty years of hiding had taught Rafael exactly what it cost to survive by disappearing, and they would not watch an entire kingdom learn that lesson in green.

"Because I'm here to bargain," Rafael said aloud.

* * *

"Ah." The Verdancy drew the syllable out like thread from a spool. "Bargain. Yes. We hoped you would. Violence is so wasteful, and you are—we will say it plainly, since plainness seems to be your dialect—you are the finest vessel we have ever seen. Twenty years of compression. A storm folded into a valley until it grew dense as a star. Do you know what you could hold, Rafael Sterling? What you could *be*?"

It gestured, and the court parted, and Rafael saw what lay beyond the throne: a window, floor to ceiling, overlooking the capital. From this height the city looked like any city—rooftops, spires, smoke, the glitter of the river. But threaded through it all, visible only because Rafael now knew to look, ran the green. Gardens on every roof. Vines in every gutter. And moving through the streets, people—thousands, tens of thousands—walking with that same unhurried serenity, tending blooms that should not bloom, smiling smiles that belonged to something else.

"All of this," the Verdancy said softly. "Rooted. Fed. Healed. No famine, no blight, no burning. And beside the throne—an anchor worthy of us. Not consumed. *Partnered.* Your storm, given purpose. Your name, cleared. Your family's exile, undone by the very heir they fled from. The Sterlings could come home, Rafael. All of them. The valley could open its roads and no one would ever need to hide again."

And there it was. The hook, baited with everything Rafael had ever wanted, dangled in front of them with a patience that had outlasted kings.

Rafael felt it catch. They would have been lying to themselves to pretend otherwise. *Home. Unhidden. The valley's borders open, the border stones just stones.* Twenty years of dampened skies and careful hands and neighbors who must never, never know—and here was a voice offering to make the hiding unnecessary, to make Rafael not merely safe but *crowned*.

For three full breaths, they let themselves want it.

Then they looked at the king's eyes.

At the bottom of the green, the drowning man was watching. He had surfaced again—surfaced, Rafael understood suddenly, because the Verdancy was distracted, savoring its offer, its grip slackening the way a fist relaxes in sleep. And the man in the well was shaking his head. Slowly. Deliberately. With the last strength of a man who had been drowning for eleven years and still, God help him, refused to let anyone else be pulled under in his place.

*No,* said the shape of his mouth. *Don't.*

Pity hit Rafael like a physical blow—pity and revulsion tangled so tightly together they could not be told apart, grief for the boy who liked dogs and hated lessons, nausea at the thing wearing his growth-ringed crown. *This is what acceptance looks like,* Rafael thought. *This is the jar with the holes in the lid, from the inside. Eleven years of smiling while something drinks you.*

"No," Rafael said.

The word dropped into the harmonized silence like a stone into still water.

"We decline," Rafael said, louder, letting their voice carry to every serene face in the hall, to every vine-wrapped pillar, to the roots in the walls and the seeds in the wells and whatever dark taproot ran beneath the throne room floor. "We decline your partnership, your pardon, and your garden. And we're going to tell you something you may not know, because whoever starved you into this never bothered to tell you either: you weren't made for this. You were made to *heal*. And there is a rite—there is always a rite—to give you back."

The change was instantaneous.

The warmth drained out of the throne room like water from a cracked basin. Every flower on the floor closed at once. The courtiers' smiles did not vanish—they *tightened*, becoming fixed, becoming masks over masks, and the harmonized breathing of thirty people hitched into a single ragged intake of breath.

The king's face, when it turned back to Rafael, was still smiling. But the smile had gone rigid as bark, and the green in his eyes had darkened to the color of pond water, and beneath it the drowning man was no longer visible at all—which frightened Rafael more than any rage could have.

"There is no giving back," the Verdancy said, and now the voice had layers in it, many voices braided, some of them very old. "There is no return. There is only the garden, and what serves it, and what feeds it."

"Then you've forgotten what you are," Rafael said. "And I haven't. My grandmother made sure of that."

The silence that followed had weight, and texture, and roots.

It was Vane who broke it—stepping forward, armor clinking, her voice clipped and professional and pitched just loud enough to carry: "Your Majesty. The prisoner grows feverish. With your leave, I'll return them to confinement before they harm themselves."

A long moment. The king studied Rafael with those pond-dark eyes, and Rafael stared back, heart slamming, storm pressing against twenty years of walls, and thought: *he heard me. Whatever he is, however deep he's buried—he heard me.*

"Yes," the king said at last, pleasantly. "Confinement. See that our guest is... comfortable. We will want to continue this conversation." The smile widened by a fraction. "Roots grow best in the dark, after all."

* * *

The cell they took Rafael to was clean, warm, and windowless, which told Rafael everything: the Verdancy did not consider them a prisoner to punish but a cutting to cultivate.

The door shut. The footsteps receded. And in the silence, Rafael pressed their palm flat against the stone wall and felt it—faint, unmistakable, threading up through the palace foundations like a pulse through a wrist.

Green. Growing. *Listening.*

They had come to bargain and left having declared war. They had seen the king and found a hostage. And somewhere below the throne room floor, the thing they had promised to free had begun, very patiently, to plan how to keep them.

Rafael sat down in the dark, put their back against the listening wall, and began, silently, to count the hours until nightfall—because Ovid had told them once, in a whisper on the road, that the underground's root-singers could hear through the Verdancy's own network, if one knew how to sing into it.

And Rafael had a message to plant.
