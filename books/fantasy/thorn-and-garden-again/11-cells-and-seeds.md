## 11. Cells and Seeds

The cell was not what Rafael expected of a palace.

They had imagined stone and iron, the kind of dungeon that lived in every border-town ballad—dripping walls, rats with ambition. What they got instead was a room of pale green marble, warm as a hand, with a narrow bed draped in soft wool and a window of thick glass through which the palace gardens glowed even now, at midnight, in colors no honest season would allow. Roses in January. Lilies burning gold under a moonless sky. Everything fed. Everything blooming. Nothing resting.

Rafael had been awake for two days, or perhaps three—the hours blurred here, syrup-slow—and they had spent all of them pressing their palm to that glass, feeling for the storm.

It was still there. That was the miracle they kept re-proving, like a tongue returning to a chipped tooth. Beyond the valley's border stones, the gift did not die; it *woke*. It sat in Rafael's chest like a second heart, heavy and eager, and when they breathed too deeply it stirred, and the lamps in the corridor guttered, and somewhere below, roots in the garden soil shifted toward them like faces turning at a sound.

Which was, Rafael understood now, exactly the problem.

The Verdancy knew they were here. He had felt it from the moment Serah Vane marched them through the gilded gates—a vast, slow attention settling over the palace like weather, like a hand laid flat against the back of the neck. The king had received them with courteous horror in his eyes and something else behind his eyes, something patient and green and old, and it had said, through Aldric's smiling mouth: *You are very far from home, little storm.*

Then they had been escorted down, and down again, past levels where the marble gave way to older stone, into a cell designed not to hold a prisoner but to hold a *presence*. The walls were laced with veins of copper. The air tasted faintly of sap. And every few hours, no matter how carefully Rafael curled their gift inward, the copper warmed, and the veins brightened, and Rafael knew the thing beneath the throne was tasting the air of them.

So they were not surprised, on the third night—or the fourth—when the wall opposite the bed opened.

It didn't bang open. It *bloomed*, seams of mortar softening like wax, petals of stone folding outward, and through the gap came a child.

A girl, maybe nine years old, barefoot on the cold floor, wearing a plain grey smock. Her hair was dark and badly cut, hacked short in the way hair gets cut when someone fears what hides in it. She stood very still, watching Rafael with eyes too old for her face, and Rafael saw—in the low lamplight, unmistakable—the green.

It traced her like ivy on a fence post. Thin emerald lines ran up from her bare feet, over her ankles, vanishing beneath the smock, reappearing at her wrists, one delicate vein crawling along her jaw to just beneath her left eye, close enough that she must see it, always, at the edge of her vision.

"Wren," the girl said, before Rafael could speak. "I'm Wren. They said you'd come. They said—" Her voice cracked and steadied, the way children's voices do when they've practiced being brave alone. "They said you were a storm-caller. Are you really?"

"I'm really," Rafael said quietly.

Wren's whole face changed. It was like watching a lamp lit in a shuttered room. "I thought the heralds made you up. I thought—" She stopped herself, jaw tightening, an adult gesture in a child's face. "Can I sit? The roots get restless if I stand too long near doors."

"Sit," Rafael said, and moved aside on the bed, and Wren crossed the cell with a strange careful gait, placing each foot flat, deliberate, like someone walking on a frozen pond. When she settled beside Rafael, Rafael felt it—not touch, but *attention*, a faint green warmth leaning toward them through the child's skin, curious, sniffing at the storm.

"You're rooted," Rafael said. Not a question. They had seen it at the border stones, on the refugees; they had learned the word from Maren's furious mouth.

"Since I was six." Wren said it matter-of-factly, reciting. "My village gave me up themselves. There was a proclamation, after the hunt started. Anyone with the green could bring the child to a crown warden and get bread and seed-grain for winter." She picked at a thread on the smock. "We were starving anyway. My mother cried the whole way to the warden's wagon and then stopped crying all at once, right at the gate, and I think that was worse."

Rafael's throat tightened. Twenty years in the Hollow, safe behind stones that killed magic, and this was what the safety cost—this child, traded for grain, carrying a parasite in her blood because her gift had been the only harvestable thing her family owned.

"Do they hurt you?" Rafael asked. "The wardens. Here."

"No." Wren frowned, genuinely puzzled, which was somehow worse than fear. "That's the strange part. Everyone thinks it's terrible, being brought here, but it isn't. It's *warm*. The green is warm, and it talks to you, and it never lets you be hungry or scared. It takes things away, but it wraps the taking up so pretty you almost say thank you." She looked up at Rafael, and for one unguarded moment the too-old eyes went young and terrified. "That's how I know it's bad. Because I *almost* say thank you. Every day I almost do."

*Compassion first,* Rafael thought, hearing Maren's voice across the miles—Maren, who had argued in the well-shadow that they should fight the corruption, burn it out, damn the cost. But sitting beside this small body with its gentle infestation, Rafael found that hatred had nowhere to put its feet.

"What does it want from you?" Rafael asked.

"To be full," Wren said simply. "It's always so hungry. That's why they bring it the gifted. Ordinary people, it can only root in a little—it goes to sleep in them, mostly. But gifts are like..." She searched for the shape of the idea, hands moving. "Like wells. Deep ones. It drinks deep from wells." Her gaze dropped. "Mine's not very deep yet. That's why it's slow with me. The others—the big ones—they go fast."

"How many others?"

"A lot." Wren's voice went small. "There are rooms below. I hear them singing sometimes. Not songs—just... notes. Like bottles blowing in wind. That's what's left of their gifts. Bottles with wind in them."

Rafael closed their eyes. In the dark behind their lids, the storm shifted restlessly, and the copper in the walls warmed in answer, attentive as a dog at a door.

*Little storm,* the green had said through the king's mouth. *How deep your well runs.*

* * *

Sleep took Rafael eventually, sitting upright, Wren curled at the foot of the bed like a cat who had decided the danger was tolerable. And in sleep, the green came in.

Not as a dream at first. As a smell—wet loam, crushed leaves, rain on hot stone—and then as light filtered green-gold through leaves far above, and Rafael was standing in a garden that did not exist anywhere on the earth anymore.

They knew it instantly, the way you know a face in a portrait you've only heard described. This was the Gardenwright's garden, the grandmother's stories made solid: terraces climbing a hillside, every plant blooming in honest season, bees working in the ordinary daylight, a stream running clear over brown stones. No excess. No gaudiness. Just abundance held in balance by patient hands. At the center, where a fountain should be, stood a tree with silver bark, and around the tree the air itself seemed kinder.

And beneath the tree, woven into the light and the loam and the slow pulse of every living root, was a presence.

It was not monstrous. That was the thing that broke Rafael's heart before they even understood why. It was *small*, in the way of beloved things—no larger than its garden, no grander than its purpose. It moved through the flowerbeds like a breeze that had learned tenderness, coaxing a wilted stem upright, warming frost off a peach blossom, humming without sound. It had been born of the healing-magic, Rafael realized; it *was* the healing-magic, given breath by generations of Gardenwrights who poured their care into the land until the land learned to care back.

It noticed Rafael the way the sun notices a cloud passing—warmly, without alarm. It showed them things, wordlessly, the way a garden shows a gardener:

A woman with strong brown hands—Rafael's blood sang recognizing the line, the Sterling jaw, the stubborn brow—kneeling in the dirt, laughing, teaching the spirit a new herb's name.

A king arriving at the garden gates. Young. Hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food. The first Sterling king, before the crown had eaten him hollow, standing among the flowers with covetous eyes, saying: *This should serve the kingdom. All of it. Everywhere. Why should one valley keep such a treasure?*

The woman refusing. The woman standing between the king and the tree with her hands open and empty and immovable.

The king coming back with soldiers. The binding-craft—he had his own craft, the crown's craft, the magic of chains and oaths—and Rafael watched, sick, as the king wrapped the spirit in it, not killing it (it could not be killed; it was woven into every root of the land) but *turning* it. Redirecting it. Teaching the healing thing to heal on command, then to grow on command, then to grip on command, then to *hold*, and hold, and hold—

The garden torn up by the roots and replanted in a palace courtyard, in soil it hated, under orders it could not refuse. The spirit's song bent from a lullaby into a leash. And generation after generation of kings learning to use it: to bind rivals, to quiet rebellions, to root the gifted and drink them dry, feeding the entity's starvation while deepening its hunger, until the healing thing forgot it had ever healed anything freely, and became only appetite wearing a gardener's gloves.

The vision shuddered. The garden darkened at its edges, rot creeping in from the frame of the memory like fire eating a photograph, and Rafael felt the spirit feel it—the endless gnawing emptiness, the hunger that grew with every feeding, the terrible confusion of a creature built to give, forced only to take.

*I don't remember,* the green said, in no voice at all, everywhere at once. *I don't remember what I was for.*

Rafael woke gasping, hand pressed to their sternum, tears on their face they had no memory of starting.

Wren sat up instantly, alert, frightened. "You dreamed it," she whispered. "Didn't you. You dreamed the garden."

"You've seen it too?"

"All the time." Wren hugged her knees. "When it drinks, it leaks. Little bits fall out and land in my head. A lady laughing. A mean king. A tree with silver bark." She hesitated. "Sometimes it cries. Did you know it cries? Big things aren't supposed to cry."

"It's not a big thing," Rafael said, and their voice came out rough. "It's a small thing that was made enormous. That's different. That's worse."

Wren considered this with the gravity of a scholar. Then she scooted closer and leaned against Rafael's arm, careful of the green veins on her wrist, and asked the question Rafael had been dreading since the wall bloomed open:

"If it used to be good—really good, before—can it be good again?"

Outside the glass, the false gardens blazed in their out-of-season glory, every bloom a command obeyed, nothing resting, nothing wild, nothing free. Rafael looked at them and thought of a spirit starved for twenty generations, of a king drowning inside his own smiling face, of a child who almost said thank you every day.

"I don't know," Rafael said honestly. "But I intend to find out."

Wren nodded slowly, as if this were the answer she'd crossed the palace to collect. Then her head tilted, listening to something far below—those bottled notes, those rooms of wind—and her face went white.

"It knows you're awake," she said. "It's coming up. Rafael—it's coming to *look* at you."

Beneath the floor, deep beneath the throne, something vast turned over in its sleep, and every copper vein in the cell walls began, softly, to glow.
