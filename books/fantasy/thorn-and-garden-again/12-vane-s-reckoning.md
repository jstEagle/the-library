## 12. Vane's Reckoning

The order came at dusk, sealed in green wax that was not wax at all.

Serah Vane stood in the antechamber of the palace's eastern wing and turned the scroll over in her gloved hands, watching the seal pulse faintly, like a heartbeat trapped under skin. She had carried a hundred royal orders in her twenty years of service—orders to burn, to arrest, to escort, to bury—and every one of them had arrived in plain red wax that smelled of tallow and honest bureaucracy. This one smelled of crushed leaves and something sweeter beneath it, something like overripe fruit left too long in the sun.

*Root the prisoner,* the script said, in letters that seemed to grow even as she read them, tendrils of ink curling off the page like living things. *The storm-caller resists passive grafting. Proceed by force. The crown requires their depth.*

She had known it would come. She had felt it coming for days, the way one feels weather behind the mountains—a pressure in the old scar tissue along her ribs, a taste of copper at the back of her throat. But knowing did not soften the moment when she broke the seal and read her own name written in a thing's handwriting.

*Serah Vane. Knight-Commander, Third Order of the Crownhunt. You are directed to—*

She stopped reading. She knew what it would say. She had watched the rooting masters work often enough to recite the procedure in her sleep: the patient bound to a chair grown from the floor itself, the gardener-priest's hands sinking into flesh as if flesh were loam, the seed pressed home above the heart. And then the screaming, which lasted exactly as long as it took for the Verdancy to find the gift and wrap itself around it, and then the silence, which was worse than any scream she had ever heard, because after it the person was still there—breathing, blinking, smiling—but they were not *there*, they were a house with all the furniture rearranged by someone who had never lived in it.

Her sister had screamed for four minutes.

Vane closed her hand around the scroll until the green wax cracked, and the smell of it filled the room, sweet and rotten, and somewhere below her feet, through six floors of marble and gilded stone, she imagined she heard the palace breathe.

"You've received your orders."

The voice came from the doorway, and Vane did not turn to it right away. She knew the speaker by his footsteps—the soft, deliberate tread of a man who never hurried because nothing was ever allowed to escape him. Master Corvess, first of the rooting priests, robed in white so clean it hurt to look at, his hands folded before him like a man arriving at prayer.

"I have," she said.

"And?"

"And I will attend to it." She turned then, and gave him the flat, obedient face she had spent two decades perfecting, the face of a soldier whose doubts had been drilled out of her long ago. It was a good face. It had survived inspections by the old king himself.

Corvess studied her a moment too long. His eyes were pale as celery water, and they had the particular stillness of eyes that no longer blinked entirely on their own. "The storm-caller is strong," he said. "Stronger than anything we've grafted since the queen. Their depth will feed the garden for a generation." He smiled, and it was almost a human smile, which made it infinitely worse. "You should feel honored, Knight-Commander. You're bringing in the harvest of a lifetime."

"Every harvest is a lifetime, master, to whatever you cut down."

Something flickered across his face—annoyance, or perhaps amusement; with the rooted ones it was impossible to tell where the man ended and the thing began. He inclined his head. "Dawn, then. The cells have already been prepared." He paused at the door. "Do not be late, Serah. The garden dislikes waiting."

When he was gone, Vane stood alone in the antechamber while the lamps burned low and the scroll in her fist slowly uncurled, its edges sprouting fine white filaments that reached for her fingers like hungry roots. She dropped it on the stone floor and ground it under her heel until the green light inside it guttered out.

Then she went to find her sword, and her sister's ring, and the last scrap of courage she had buried twelve years ago beneath her oaths.

* * *

The cells beneath the eastern wing were not built of stone.

That was the first thing anyone noticed, though the palace's architects had done their best to disguise it: walls of pale, seamless material, warm to the touch, veined faintly with green, breathing—if one pressed an ear against them—with the slow rhythm of some vast sleeping body. The Verdancy had grown these cells itself, in the palace's deep years, and everything within them served it: the doors that sealed like bark over wounds, the chairs that grew up out of the floors to hold the unwilling, the lamps that glowed with the soft phosphorescence of foxfire rather than honest flame.

Rafael Sterling sat in one of those chairs when Vane came down the stairs, and they were awake—of course they were awake, the rooted ones never let their prisoners sleep more than the entity permitted—and their eyes tracked Vane from the moment her boot touched the bottom step.

"So," the storm-caller said. Voice rough but level. They looked terrible—three days in the cell, wrists marked where the growing chair had held them, a bruise blooming along one jaw from the capture on the ridge—but their eyes were still their own, and that alone made them the most remarkable thing Vane had seen in years. "It's dawn somewhere, I suppose."

"It's past midnight." Vane stopped three paces away, close enough to see the fine tremor in the prisoner's hands, far enough that the chair's roots could not reach her. Two guards flanked the door behind her. She had chosen them herself: Dace and Merrin, the only two soldiers in her company whose smiles still came crooked, whose laughter still startled them sometimes, as if it surprised them to remember how. Unrooted. For now. "I'm sorry about the hour. What I have to say won't keep."

Rafael's gaze flicked to the guards, then back. "Is this the part where you tell me it's nothing personal? Because I'll save you the speech. I've heard it from better liars than you, Serah Vane. On the ridge, you hesitated. Liars don't hesitate."

"No," Vane agreed quietly. "They don't." She drew her sword.

The prisoner didn't flinch, but the air in the cell changed—Vane felt it, the way one feels the pressure drop before a summer storm, a static crawling up her arms, raising the hairs there. The lamp-flames bent toward Rafael like grass in wind. Behind her, she heard Dace shift his weight, uncertain.

"Orders came tonight," Vane said, turning the blade so its edge caught the foxfire light. "From the crown itself. Green seal." She watched understanding move across the prisoner's face like a cloud shadow. "They want you rooted by force. By morning. And they want me—" her voice caught, just slightly, on the words, and she hated herself for it, hated the weakness, hated the twelve years of discipline that could not quite strangle it, "—they want me to do the cutting."

The static in the air thickened. Somewhere overhead, faintly, through six floors of palace, came a sound like a great green lung drawing breath.

"So this is the part," Rafael said slowly, "where you decide whether you're a knife or a person."

"That depends," Vane said, "on whether you can run."

For a long moment, neither of them moved. Then Rafael laughed—a short, disbelieving sound, half a sob—and the static broke like a fever breaking, and the lamps straightened, and Vane sheathed her sword and drew instead a small iron key, black with age, that she had carried against her heart for twelve years without once knowing why until tonight.

"The guards outside are mine," she said, working the key into the lock of the growing-chair. "Dace, Merrin—they'll hold the stair. There's a drainage gallery beneath the western foundation, older than the palace, older than the Verdancy. It comes up beyond the wall, in the tanner's quarter." The roots released Rafael's wrists with a sound like tearing cloth. The storm-caller stood, swayed, caught themselves on the chair's arm. Up close Vane could see the exhaustion carved into their face, the hunger, the stubbornness sitting behind all of it like a lamp in a window. "There are people waiting for you there. People who know what the Verdancy is. People who think it can be unmade—not destroyed. Unbound."

"Unbound." Rafael tested the word like a tooth. "And who are these people?"

"Smugglers of souls. Heretics. The last root-singers in the kingdom." Vane pulled the prisoner's arm across her shoulders and took their weight without being asked, the way she had once taken another's weight, another lifetime ago. "Their leader is a man named Ovid, and if he offers to sing for you, let him. It doesn't hurt. That's more than I can promise about anything else that happens tonight."

They had reached the stairs when Rafael spoke again, low, their breath warm against Vane's ear.

"Why?" Simple. Direct. No sentiment in it. "You had a career. An honorable grave, someday, with your name spelled right. Why throw it away for someone you hunted?"

Vane climbed three steps before she answered. When she did, her voice was steady, which cost her more than screaming would have.

"Because twelve years ago I carried my sister to one of those chairs myself. I told her it wouldn't hurt. I believed it, because believing it was easier than the alternative." The stairwell door loomed ahead, barred from outside; Dace was lifting the bar. "Liora was nineteen. She could make flowers bloom in winter. That was all. Flowers, in winter. They took her anyway, because the garden is always hungry, and I stood at the door and watched her smile afterward with someone else's smile, and I have spent every day since learning the difference between obedience and honor." She pushed the door open onto cold night air. "I am not carrying you to a chair, storm-caller. I am carrying you out. Try to keep up."

* * *

The drainage gallery smelled of old water and older stone, and it was the first place in the capital where Vane had ever seen mold grow freely, unchecked, ordinary—life doing its ugly, unmanaged business without the Verdancy's permission. She found the sight strangely comforting.

They went by torchlight, Rafael limping beside her, Dace and Merrin bringing up the rear with the nervous energy of men who had crossed a line they could never cross back. Twice, patrols passed overhead—Vane heard their boots through gaps in the vaulting, and each time she pressed herself and her burden into the shadows and held her breath and listened to the rooted guards walk by, humming tunelessly, in perfect unison, songs they had never learned.

"They're everywhere," Rafael whispered, after the second patrol. "In the walls. In the people. How does anything here stay itself?"

"It doesn't," Vane said. "That's the point of it."

The gallery ended at a rusted ladder and a hatch crusted with a century of river silt, and beyond the hatch was the tanner's quarter at its stinkiest hour, and beyond that, down alleys that Vane had memorized from a smuggler's map she'd bought with her sister's ring, a door.

It looked like nothing—a cellar entrance behind a chandler's shop, boards nailed across it at careless angles. But when Merrin knocked the pattern Vane had taught him—two, pause, three, pause, one—the boards swung inward on silent hinges, and lamplight spilled out, and a voice like dry leaves said:

"Knight-Commander Vane. And the storm-caller, delivered to our very doorstep. My grandmother told stories about nights like this." A figure stepped into the light: a small man, sixty-odd, bald as an egg, with a network of fine silver scars tracing his throat like the strings of an invisible instrument. His eyes were quick and kind and terribly tired. "Ovid," he said, by way of everything. "You'd both better come down. We haven't much time, and less room, and the song takes a while to explain."

The cellar beneath the chandler's shop was larger than it had any right to be—older walls behind newer ones, a warren of chambers dug down and outward over generations. And it was full. Vane counted thirty before she stopped counting: the unrooted gifted of the capital, the ones who had slipped through the harvests, hidden in plain sight among the smiling crowds. A woman whose skin shimmered faintly like fish-scale. Twin boys holding hands, sparks jumping between their fingers. An old soldier with frost on his breath in high summer.

And children. Three of them, asleep on pallets in the corner, guarded by a woman with a cudgel and the posture of someone who had used it recently. One of the children stirred as they passed—a small girl, seven or eight maybe, brown-skinned, hollow-eyed, with a thin green thread visible at her throat like a vein of ore in pale stone.

Rafael stopped dead.

"She's rooted," the storm-caller breathed. "Partially. She's—"

"Being eaten," Ovid said simply. "Slowly. The Verdancy found her in the spring harvests. Wren's gift is small—she sings things alive, little things, wounded birds, wilted herbs—but depth isn't about size, as you of all people should understand. The entity likes her. It savors her." He gestured them onward, deeper, and his voice dropped. "We've slowed it. Root-song can do that—hold the threads loose. But slowing is not saving. Every day, a little more of her goes under, and every day, a little less of her comes back."

They gathered in the deepest chamber, around a table of raw wood on which lay objects that made Vane's chest tighten: her sister's world, laid out by strangers. Bone needles. A bowl of river-clay. Sheets of vellum covered in notation unlike any music she knew—circular, branching, like the rings of a tree seen from above. And at the center, under glass, a single dried flower that could not possibly still be alive, and yet was, its petals moving almost imperceptibly, opening and closing like a slow breath.

"What is this?" Rafael asked, staring at the flower.

"The Gardenwright's rite," Ovid said. He sat, and folded his scarred hands, and looked up at the storm-caller with an expression Vane recognized—hope, the dangerous kind, the kind that kills. "Or what remains of it. The magic your family kept, before the first king stole it and twisted it into his garden of chains. The Verdancy was born healing. It has spent nine hundred years being starved, and beaten, and taught to devour, but underneath—underneath, it remembers what it was made to do." His eyes glittered. "It can be reminded. Not destroyed—that way lies a graveyard; every rooted soul in this city dies with it, and there are forty thousand of them. But unbound. Released from the crown. Given back to the land, whole, the way it was meant to be." He leaned forward. "The rite needs three things, storm-caller. A singer who knows the old words—I am the last, and I am old, and my voice is going. A shield strong enough to hold the gates while we work—and your knight-commander has already sworn to that, whether she knows it or not." A glance at Vane, who did not contradict him, because she couldn't. "And an anchor. A vessel of sufficient depth to hold the Verdancy while it changes, to give it something to cling to besides its hunger. Someone willing to stand at the center of it and offer up their own gift as the price of everyone else's."

Silence took the room. In the corner, the sleeping children breathed. The impossible flower opened and closed.

"And if the anchor refuses?" Vane asked quietly. "If no one stands at the center?"

"Then in a year, maybe two, there is no Hollow anywhere in this kingdom," Ovid said. "No valley the magic dies at. No border stones. Only garden, everywhere, forever, and everyone in it smiling." He spread his hands. "That is the arithmetic. I won't pretend it's gentle."

Vane looked at Rafael then—at the storm-caller standing in torchlight among strangers, exhausted and hunted and free for exactly one night, being asked to pay for freedom with the only self they had ever known. She thought of Liora, who had never been given a choice at all. She thought of the green seal cracking under her heel.

"We need time," Rafael said at last, and their voice was hoarse, and steady, and Vane heard in it the first distant rumble of a storm deciding which way to break. "How long to prepare this rite?"

Ovid smiled, and it was not entirely a comforting sight.

"Three days," he said. "Which is convenient, because by my count, the palace will notice your empty cell by morning—and when it does, Knight-Commander, it will send everything it has. So we had best hope your sword arm is as good as your conscience." He rose, and the lamplight made his shadow climb the ancient walls like a rising tide. "Welcome to the underground. Rest while you can. Tomorrow, we teach you the song."

Above them, muffled through earth and brick, the city hummed its contented, root-fed lullaby—and somewhere far beneath the throne room, something vast turned in its sleep, and began, softly, to listen.
