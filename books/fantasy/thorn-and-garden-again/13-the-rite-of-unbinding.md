## 13. The Rite of Unbinding

Ovid had sung for the dead before he had ever sung for the living.

That was how the gift had come to him, or perhaps how he had come to it—crouched in the root-cellar beneath his mother's house in Marrowfen, singing to a row of bodies wrapped in linen while the Verdancy's green crept up through the floorboards like frost made of light. His mother had been among them. He could not remember which bundle was hers anymore, and that forgetting was its own kind of wound, one he carried the way other men carried scars: quietly, and always touching it.

The root-song had kept the Verdancy out of him. That was all anyone needed to know about Ovid, though of course no one ever asked, because asking would mean admitting what lived beneath the capital's gilded streets, in the dark, in the wet, in the places where the city's beauty did not bother to reach.

He stood now at the center of the Undercroft—the old cistern hall three levels below the palace gardens, where the vaulted ceiling wept mineral tears and the walls were furred with pale moss that glowed faintly, the only light they permitted themselves—and waited for the last of them to arrive.

They came in ones and twos, as they always did. A woman with river-gift hands that trembled when she thought no one watched. Two brothers who shared a single ember between them, cupped in clay, passed back and forth like communion. An old man whose gift was so small—a warmth in his palms, nothing more—that the Verdancy had never bothered to root him, and who came anyway, every night, because small gifts deserved guarding too.

And at the edge of them, apart from them, sat the storm-caller.

Rafael Sterling. Ovid had known the name since childhood, when it was still spoken as a warning in his mother's kitchen—*the Sterlings, the exiled ones, the reason the crown fears us*—and now here the name was, sitting on a wet stone bench with their coat still damp from the tunnels, watching him with eyes the color of weather about to happen.

Beside Rafael, Serah Vane stood with her arms crossed and her sword still belted at her hip, which had caused an argument lasting most of an hour when they'd first arrived. *No steel in the Undercroft,* Ovid had said. *She stays armed or she doesn't stay,* Vane had said. In the end they had compromised: Vane stayed, armed, and Ovid pretended not to notice the way her hand never actually rested near the hilt. She was not there to draw it. She was there because of a sister, and because some debts are paid in rooms like this one.

And there was Wren.

The child sat closest to the moss-light, because Wren liked light now, craved it, leaned toward any glow the way a plant leans toward the sun—which was exactly the problem, wasn't it, exactly the horror of it, dressed up in a child's small hunger. The green veins had reached Wren's collarbones. Ovid could see them pulsing faintly in the moss-glow, slow as breathing, patient as roots.

He had spent eleven years learning everything the old songs knew about the thing in the king's blood. Tonight he would have to say aloud what he had learned. Tonight he would have to do arithmetic.

Ovid hated arithmetic. Songs were honest; numbers lied by pretending they didn't.

* * *

"Before I teach you the rite," Ovid began, and his voice carried in the cistern the way voices do in places built to hold water, round and resonant, "I need you to understand what the rite is. Not what you hope it is. Not what the stories say. What it is."

He let the silence sit. He had learned that from the dead, too—they were excellent teachers of patience.

"The Verdancy is not a disease," he said. "I know that's what the crownhunters call it. I know that's what the rooted call it, when they can still call anything. It is not a sickness and it is not a parasite, not truly, though it behaves like both. It is a spirit. A garden-spirit. Old beyond old, older than the kingdom, older than the first stone of this city." He touched the wall beside him, laid his palm flat against the cold stone as if greeting something. "It was called the Gardenwright once. It healed land. That was all it did. Blighted fields came green under its attention. Poisoned rivers ran clean. It gave itself away endlessly, because giving was its nature."

"And then a Sterling stole it," Vane said flatly.

"A Sterling *was given* it," Ovid corrected, gently, because this mattered, because the difference between theft and gift was the whole hinge on which tonight would turn. "Your grandmother's line held it in trust. And the first Aldric—he was not born a king, whatever the histories claim; he was a warlord with a crown hammered out of a shield—took it into himself. Bound it to his bloodline. Thought he could own a spirit the way one owns a horse." Ovid shook his head slowly. "You cannot own a thing whose nature is to give. You can only starve it. So he starved it. Every heir after him fed it less and took more, until the healing curdled into hunger, and the hunger learned to feed itself, and what remained—" he spread his hands, "—is what walks around wearing your king's face."

Rafael had gone very still. Ovid had seen grief take people that way before, the stillness of someone listening to a story they already half-knew and had hoped never to hear confirmed.

"You've felt it," Ovid said to them. "In the cells. Through the child."

"I felt it starving," Rafael said. Their voice was low, roughened. "It's not hungry the way a wolf is hungry. It's hungry the way—" They stopped. Started again. "The way a well is empty. There's just nothing there, and everything pouring into the nothing, and the nothing doesn't even know how to stop being nothing."

"Yes," Ovid said softly. "That is the truest description I have heard in eleven years, and I hate that it comes from a stranger."

Wren looked up at that. "I don't feel empty," the child said. "I feel full. That's the worst part. It feels like being loved."

No one answered. There was nothing to answer with. Ovid had heard rooted children say versions of it before, in the early days when the underground had tried to save them, and every version had broken someone.

"That," he said finally, "is why we are here. Because the rite exists. Because there is a way. And because the way has a price, and I will not sing one note of it until every soul in this room understands the price in full."

* * *

He drew the rite's shape on the wet floor with chalk he kept for exactly this purpose—had kept it for eleven years, waiting, hoping never to use it. Three circles. One within another within another.

"The outer circle is severance," he said. "The song of cutting. If we sing it complete, with enough gifted voices joined, the Verdancy dies. Not sleeps. Dies. Torn out of every root it has grown, burned out of the royal bloodline, ended." He looked around the circle of faces. "And every person it has rooted dies with it. Because the rooting is not a leash, friends. It is a graft. The spirit's life runs through them the way sap runs through a branch. Cut the trunk and every branch falls."

"How many?" Vane asked. Her voice was soldier's-voice, clipped, but Ovid saw her knuckles whiten against her arms. She had asked the question she already feared.

"Half the city," Ovid said. "Perhaps more. The rooted are everywhere—in the guard, in the kitchens, in the nurseries. The Verdancy has had twenty years of Aldric's reign to spread, and longer than that in the old kings' quiet experiments. We estimate nine thousand souls in the capital alone. Across the kingdom—" he closed his eyes briefly, "—we stopped counting. The villages on your road, Sterling. The smiling ones. Whole towns, grafted entire."

"Nine thousand," the river-woman whispered. Her trembling hands had gone still, which somehow was worse.

"And the child," the old man with the warm palms said, though everyone had been thinking it. Everyone had been looking, sidelong, at Wren.

"And Wren," Ovid agreed. "Yes. Severance kills the rooted. All of them. Including the innocent, including the children, including every soul the Verdancy took without asking." He met Rafael's eyes. "Including, I suspect, your King Aldric—who is more root than man by now, though there is a man in there. I have felt him. Frightened. Drowning. Calling for help in a voice too small for anyone to hear."

"He's not innocent," Vane said. "Whatever's left of him in there, he let it happen. He fed it."

"Perhaps," Ovid said. "But I have not yet found the man qualified to decide how much guilt a drowning person carries. Have you?"

Vane said nothing. But her jaw worked, and Ovid marked it, and moved on, because mercy delayed was not mercy denied.

* * *

"The middle circle," he said, tapping the second ring of chalk, "is anchoring. This is the harder teaching. The Verdancy cannot simply be released—it must be *held*. Given a new vessel, a willing one, who takes the spirit's hunger into themselves and becomes its anchor. Its root-tree. Its—" he searched for the word and found the old one, the one his mother had used, "—its garden-bed. The anchored spirit feeds on the vessel's gift instead of on the gifted. It takes and takes, as it always has, but it takes from one willing source rather than ten thousand unwilling hosts. And the rooted live. The grafts survive, sustained, gradually loosening over years, until one day the branches can stand without the trunk."

"For how long?" Rafael asked. "How long does the anchor hold it?"

Ovid was quiet a moment. "Forever," he said. "Or until the anchor dies. There is no release written into the rite. The first Gardenwrights bound themselves seasonally—one year, then another took up the burden—but those rites are lost. What survives is the permanent binding. The old songs are clear on this point, and I have checked them until my eyes bled." He paused. "And there is more."

"There's always more," Vane muttered.

"The anchor must surrender their gift. Entirely. Permanently. The Verdancy consumes magic—it cannot be anchored in a vessel that keeps drawing power, or it will simply consume the vessel from inside, and then it will be loose again, hungrier. So the rite requires the anchor to give up the gift as payment. All of it. The well is emptied to fill the garden." He turned to Rafael, and he made himself say it plainly, because plainly was the only kindness left in the room. "A storm-caller's depth, Sterling. That is what the mathematics demand. The Verdancy has grown vast off twenty generations of kings. A small gift would anchor it for a season and then fail, and it would burst free ravenous. Only a deep well can hold it. Only a storm-caller. Which means the rite asks you to become ordinary. To spend the rest of your life hollow where the storm used to live, holding a starving god in the emptiness, forever."

The cistern dripped. Somewhere far above, muffled through three floors of stone, the city went on being beautiful and wrong.

Rafael looked down at their own hands. Ovid watched them turn the hands over, palm and back, as if checking whether the storm were visible, whether it showed in the skin. Twenty years dampened, he had heard—twenty years in a valley where magic died at the borders, twenty years of strangling their own nature to survive. And now the world proposed to finish the job permanently, and call it salvation.

"You're asking me to choose between nine thousand strangers and myself," Rafael said.

"No," Ovid said. "I'm telling you what the choice is. The choosing is yours. That distinction is the whole of my office." He straightened. "I am a root-singer, not a judge. I keep the songs alive so that when the moment comes, someone can sing them. I do not decide who pays."

"Someone has to decide," Vane said.

"Yes," Ovid agreed. "And it will be the one who pays. That is the only moral shape this rite has left. A forced anchor fails within days—we tried, once, eleven years ago, with a volunteer who was not deep enough and not entirely willing. Her name was Sefa. The Verdancy ate her gift in four days and her mind in six, and when it burst free of her it took the east quarter's water supply with it, and forty-one people rooted in a single night." His voice did not waver, but his hands, he noticed, had curled into fists at his sides. "So. No forcing. No sacrificing the unwilling. The rite knows the difference. The spirit knows the difference. Willingness is not sentiment—it is structural. It is load-bearing. Do you understand me? The binding holds *because* the anchor chooses it. Remove the choice and the whole architecture collapses."

* * *

Wren raised a hand. Small, thin, the green veins bright at the wrist. The gesture was so schoolroom-ordinary that it hurt.

"What if nobody anchors it?" the child asked. "What if everybody just says no?"

"Then we attempt severance," Ovid said, "and we lose half the city, and you."

Wren considered this with the terrible seriousness of children, who have not yet learned to look away from hard things. "And if somebody anchors it, they lose everything forever, and I get to maybe be okay someday."

"Yes."

"That's not fair."

"No," Ovid said. "It is not fair. Fairness is not among the tools available to us. We have songs, chalk, and bad choices. That is the inventory." He crouched down to the child's level, knees cracking, and spoke gently. "Listen to me, Wren. Whatever is decided, no one will make you watch it be done. And no one will decide it *for* another. Those are the rules of this room. The rooted taught me that rule by breaking it."

Wren nodded slowly. Then, with the merciless clarity only children and drunks possess: "The storm-person is going to do it. Aren't they. They've got the look."

"Wren," Rafael started—

"They've got the look," Wren repeated, pointing a small finger, green-veined, certain. "My ma had it. When she decided things. It's the look of somebody doing sums they hate."

Ovid looked at Rafael Sterling then—at the exile, the hidden one, the last storm-caller, sitting in the dark with a child's finger aimed at their heart—and saw the sums finishing behind their eyes. Grief and resolve arriving together, the way weather arrives, the way storms arrive, all at once and from every direction.

He had sung for the dead before he sang for the living. He had hoped, these eleven years in the dark, that he might die without ever singing the middle circle. Hopes, he had learned, were just debts you owed the future.

"The rite must be sung at the heart-root," he said, rising, addressing them all now, his voice filling the cistern like water filling a cup. "Beneath the throne room. Where the first binding was made. And it must be sung soon—the Verdancy felt us the moment Sterling crossed the city gates, and it is not patient, and it is not stupid. It will move against us. Perhaps tomorrow. Perhaps tonight." He picked up the chalk one final time and drew, beside the three circles, a fourth mark: a simple vertical line, the oldest symbol in the root-songs. The stroke that meant *a life given*, and also, in the older dialects, *a seed planted*.

"So rest," he told them. "Eat. Hold each other. And you, Sterling—" he met those weather-colored eyes, "—come with me. There is a verse of the anchoring song that must be taught mouth to ear, singer to anchor, and no other way. If you are going to do what I think you are going to do, you should at least learn what it will sound like, when the storm goes out of you."

Rafael stood. For a moment they swayed, as if the decision had weight, as if it pressed down on the shoulders like a change in the air before thunder.

"All right," Rafael Sterling said quietly. "Teach me the song."

And Ovid, who had carried the middle circle for eleven years like a stone in his chest, felt the stone shift at last—not lighter, never lighter, but moving, becoming music, becoming the beginning of the end or the end of the beginning—and led the storm-caller deeper into the dark to learn the price of nine thousand lives.
