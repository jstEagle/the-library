## 14. Storm Over the Palace

The warning came as a taste.

Rafael was halfway down the servants' stair, Ovid's map folded against their ribs, when the air changed. Not the way weather changes—weather had manners, a slow courtesy of pressure and cloud. This was different. This was the palace itself turning its attention, the way a sleeping man turns his face toward a sound in the dark.

Green. The taste was green, sweet and cloying, like sap bleeding from a cut branch. It coated Rafael's tongue and settled into the back of their throat, and beneath it they felt something vast shift its weight—a root-system the size of a city flexing underground, in the walls, in the water, in the veins of every smiling servant who had passed them without seeing.

*It knows,* Rafael thought. *Ovid's rite hasn't even begun, and it knows.*

They pressed themselves into an alcove between a tapestry and a marble saint whose gilded hands had been worn dull by centuries of touching. Below, through the balustrade's carved spindles, the throne room's antechamber stretched out in candlelit splendor—and it was filling with guards.

Not marching. That was the horror of it. They simply *arrived*, drifting in from side corridors like leaves drawn toward a drain, spears in hand, faces smooth and pleasant and utterly empty. Rafael counted thirty before the numbers stopped meaning anything. Forty. Fifty. They arranged themselves in ranks without a word of command, without a shouted order, and stood at perfect attention, and smiled.

Every one of them wore the green. Faint threads beneath the skin of their throats, their wrists, climbing toward their eyes like ivy seeking a window.

"Rooted," Rafael breathed. "All of them."

* * *

They found Vane where the plan said she would be—crouched in the shadow of the armory arch, sword across her knees, whetstone forgotten in her hand. Her eyes were fixed on the same gathering of guards, and her face had gone the color of old tallow.

"They were my soldiers," she said quietly, without looking up. "Some of them. I trained half those men. Corven there—third rank, left side—he used to cheat at cards and give his winnings to the orphan kitchens." Her jaw worked. "He's smiling at a wall."

"Vane—"

"The Verdancy pulled them all in one breath. One breath, Sterling. It didn't have to hunt them down or fight them. It just... reached, and they were gone." She finally turned, and her eyes were wet and furious. "That's what's waiting for us if we fail tonight. That's what's waiting for *you*, if you let it touch you."

"It already touched me once," Rafael said. "In the audience hall. It offered me a place beside the throne." They crouched beside her, keeping their voice low. "It didn't root me because it wants me willing. It wants me *ripe*. Which means right now, while it's still trying to seduce me instead of take me—we have a door that won't be open tomorrow."

Vane stared at them for a long moment. Then she laughed, short and bitter, and slid her sword home.

"You sound like my sister." She stood. "She also thought she could out-talk a monster."

"What happened to your sister is exactly why we're doing this."

"I know." Vane rolled her shoulders, and the knight came back into her spine, the grief folding itself away somewhere behind her breastplate where it lived. "Ovid's in the undercroft with Wren. The rite needs an hour to prepare. We need to hold the guard off for an hour, and there are—" she glanced back at the antechamber "—a hundred and twenty of them, and they don't feel pain, don't tire, and don't stop."

"And I'm the only person in this palace who can call lightning."

"Yes." Vane's mouth twisted. "Which brings me to the part of the plan I hate."

* * *

The part she hated was this: Rafael had to stand in the open.

The great gallery ran the length of the palace's eastern wing—a corridor of white marble and tall windows, hung with portraits of dead kings, wide enough for six guards abreast. It was the only route from the barracks to the undercroft stairs. If the rooted guard reached the undercroft, they would reach Ovid and Wren, and everything would end with the quiet efficiency of roots strangling a seedling.

So Rafael walked to the center of the gallery, alone, and waited.

For twenty years they had done the opposite of this. Twenty years of smallness, of dampened skies, of pulling the storm down inside themselves until it was a pebble in the chest, a stone swallowed so deep it barely ached. They had learned to walk through the Hollow like a held breath. They had learned to be *less*.

Now Rafael stood on marble under a hundred candles, and let go of the pebble.

It did not come gently. Nothing about it was gentle. It came like a dam failing, like a lung bursting open after drowning, and Rafael heard themselves make a sound—not a scream, not quite a laugh—as the storm roared up through twenty years of suppression and filled every corner of their body at once. Their hair lifted. The candle flames bent sideways, all of them, in unison, toward the windows. Static crawled along the marble under their bare feet and the portraits of dead kings seemed to lean away from them on their walls.

*Oh,* Rafael thought, dizzy with it. *Oh, I forgot. I forgot what I am.*

The first rank of guards came around the corner at a walk—unhurried, pleasant, spears leveled—and Rafael raised one hand, and the world answered.

Lightning did not strike the ceiling. It struck *through* it, down through slate and timber and the storm-dark sky above, and it took the gallery's iron chandelier and turned it into a falling sun. The blast threw the front rank of guards off their feet; the windows blew inward in a glittering blizzard of glass; thunder filled the corridor so completely that Rafael felt it in their teeth, their bones, the hollows of their eyes.

And underneath the terror of it—underneath the screaming and the smoke and the guards scrambling up from the shattered marble—was joy. Bright, filthy, glorious joy. Rafael laughed again, wilder this time, and pulled the storm into their other hand, and the wind came when they called it: a howling wall of it that swept down the gallery and bowled the second rank over like ninepins, sent spears clattering, tore a portrait of King Aldric's grandfather clean off its hook.

*This is what they exiled us for,* Rafael thought, watching three grown soldiers fly backward from a gesture. *This is what I buried. And it fits me like my own skin.*

A spear took them across the shoulder—shallow, glancing—but the pain barely registered, because a guard had gotten close, close enough to see the green threads pulsing in his neck, close enough that Rafael could see he was still *smiling* as he struck. Rafael hit him with a bolt at a range of four feet and the man flew ten feet and did not get up, and Rafael looked at the crumpled body and the joy stuttered.

*Is he alive? Is he—*

There was no time. More were coming. There were always more coming, ranks and ranks of them, pouring out of the dark corridors with their pleasant faces and their patient, terrible obedience, and Rafael called the storm again and again and again, and each time it answered faster, easier, *happier*, as if it had been starving too and could not believe the feast.

Rain came through the broken windows now, horizontal and hard. The gallery flickered blue-white, thunder rolling continuous as drumfire. Rafael stood in the middle of it with their arms spread and their heart hammering like a trapped bird, and they were magnificent, they were terrible, they were everything the heralds' proclamations had promised and worse—

—and then a bolt went wide.

It was nothing. A fraction of a degree of aim, a heartbeat of distraction, a guard who moved unexpectedly. The lightning missed the soldier charging at Rafael's flank and struck the wall instead, and the wall was stone, and stone does not burn—but the tapestry next to it did. The tapestry, and then the paneling, and then the gallery's ancient rafters above, flames racing along dry wood that two hundred years of candles had seasoned like kindling.

Rafael watched the fire catch with a strange, floating detachment, and understood, all at once and completely, what they had been playing with.

*I can't aim it.* The thought arrived cold and clear beneath the roar. *Twenty years of dampening it, and I never learned to aim it. I've been swinging a sword with my eyes closed.*

A guard screamed—truly screamed, and the sound was human, terrified, *rooted or not human*—as fire spread along the ceiling toward the barracks stair. Toward the servants' quarters. Toward the wing where Wren was being kept, where the child's cell sat directly beneath the oldest timber in the palace.

The exhilaration curdled. Rafael felt it turn in their chest like milk spoiling, the joy going gray and heavy, and suddenly the storm in their hands was not a homecoming but a thing with teeth, a thing that had never once cared what Rafael wanted, a thing that would burn a palace full of rooted innocents and a dying child and laugh in thunder while it did it.

"STOP," Rafael said aloud, to the storm, to themselves, and dragged it down.

It fought them. God, how it fought them—twenty years of hunger rising up in mutiny, lightning still crawling along their arms looking for permission, the wind shrieking through the broken windows as if betrayed. Rafael clenched their fists and *pushed*, the way they'd pushed every morning of their childhood, but a thousand times harder, wrestling the storm back down their throat like swallowing a river. Their nose bled. Their vision went white at the edges. Somewhere in the roaring they heard themselves sobbing, once, briefly, furiously.

Then it was down. Caged. Beating against the inside of their ribs like a fist on a door.

The gallery burned. Rain fell through the ruined roof onto smoking marble. Guards lay scattered among the glass and the wreckage—some groaning, some still, too many still—and the fire crept toward the east wing with the patience of a thing that had all night.

* * *

Vane found Rafael on their knees in a puddle, blood running freely from their nose, hands pressed flat against the cold marble as if holding the floor down.

"The east wing," Rafael rasped. "I set the east wing on fire. Wren's cell is under the east wing."

"Buckets are already moving—the kitchen staff, some of the unrooted servants Ovid's people woke." Vane hauled them upright by the arm, and her grip was iron and her voice, surprisingly, was not unkind. "You held the gallery. A hundred and twenty guards, and you held it alone. No army in this kingdom could have done it."

"No army in this kingdom would have burned the building down around the people it was protecting." Rafael wiped their face with a shaking hand and looked at the wreckage—at the bodies, at the fire, at the rain—and felt the storm pulse hungrily against its cage, wanting back out, wanting *more*. "Vane, I enjoyed it. Do you understand? Every second of it, right up until the tapestry caught. I stood there throwing lightning like a god and I was *happy*."

Vane was quiet for a moment. Through the broken windows, the storm Rafael had summoned was still breaking over the capital—rain lashing the gilded roofs, lightning walking the clouds in slow, distant strides, a private apocalypse with nowhere to spend itself.

"My sister said something like that, near the end," Vane said at last. "After they rooted her. She told me the Verdancy only ever offers people what they already want." She turned Rafael by the shoulder, forcing them to meet her eyes. "The question isn't whether you're dangerous, Sterling. You are. Tonight proved it better than any proclamation. The question is what you do in the next hour, knowing it."

Rafael looked down at their own hands. Ordinary-looking hands, rain-wet and trembling, still faintly traced with static that crawled and faded, crawled and faded, like something pacing its cell.

Below them, beneath the burning wing and the flooded gallery and a hundred feet of palace stone, Ovid was singing the first notes of the binding-song. Rafael felt it through the soles of their feet—a vibration, a thread of the old garden-magic waking in the deep places, calling to the Verdancy's heart-root like a bell calling a beast to heel.

The entity felt it too. Rafael knew that with absolute certainty, because the green taste in their throat suddenly thickened, and every rooted guard left standing in the palace turned their head at once—toward the undercroft stairs, toward the heart, toward Wren—with the same pleasant, vacant smile.

"They know," Rafael said. "Vane—they know where the rite is. All of them, all at once."

"Then we're done being careful." Vane drew her sword, and the blade caught the firelight, and she grinned like a woman stepping off a cliff. "Come on, storm-caller. Let's go meet the heart."
