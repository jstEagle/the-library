## 15. What the Garden Remembers

The heart-root pulsed beneath Rafael's palm like a second heartbeat, slow and vast, the rhythm of something that had been breathing since before their grandmother was born.

They had descended alone. That had been the bargain Ovid insisted on—*one vessel, one touch, no one else to anchor you back if you drown*—and Vane had argued until her voice went hoarse and then gone silent in a way that frightened Rafael more than shouting would have. She stood at the top of the root-stair now, he knew, hand on her sword hilt, watching the green light rise out of the dark like heat-shimmer off summer stone.

Rafael knelt in the chamber beneath the throne room, where the roots of the Verdancy had grown so old and thick they had become architecture—arches of living wood, walls veined with sap that glowed faint gold-green, the air thick with the smell of loam and rain and something sweeter underneath, something like the inside of a ripe pear. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. That was the horror of it, or had been, before Rafael pressed their hand flat against the great root's skin and felt—not malice, not hunger, not any of the things they had braced themselves to feel—but *grief*, old and bottomless as an aquifer.

Then the world folded like a page being turned.

* * *

Rafael stood in a garden.

Not the palace gardens they had walked through on the way down, all gilded trellises and flowers blooming out of season with that uncanny, forced perfection. This garden was older. Wilder. The paths were packed earth worn soft by bare feet, and the plants grew in the tangled abundance of things loved rather than controlled—beans climbing through the corn, marigolds standing guard over the tomatoes, a fig tree leaning its branches over a stone bench worn smooth by generations of sitting. The light was late-afternoon gold, the kind that made everything look remembered even while it was happening.

A woman knelt at the edge of a vegetable bed, her hands buried to the wrist in dark soil, and Rafael's heart stopped and started again, because he knew her. Not from portraits—the Sterlings had been scrubbed from every wall in the kingdom—but from the one faded daguerreotype his mother kept hidden in a false bottom of her sewing box, wrapped in oilcloth like contraband. Grandmother Elowen. Younger here than Rafael had ever seen her, her black hair braided crown-wise, dirt on her cheek, laughing at something outside the frame.

"Come here, little storm," she said, without looking up, and Rafael's whole body went cold.

But she wasn't talking to them. A child ran into view—a girl of six or seven, gap-toothed, dragging a watering can nearly her own size—and Rafael realized with a lurch that this was not memory-as-witness. This was memory-as-inhabitant. He looked down at his own hands and found them small, brown, five years old. He was inside his grandmother's childhood. The Verdancy was showing him what it remembered, and what it remembered first was *her*.

"You have to talk to them," the child-Elowen said gravely, kneeling beside the bean rows. "They get lonely when you only sing."

"I do not only sing," said another voice, warm as banked coals, coming from everywhere and nowhere—from the soil itself, from the leaves, from the light. "I listen. There is a difference."

"Then what are they saying right now?"

A pause, and Rafael felt it—the attention of something enormous turning gently, like a whale shifting in deep water, toward a row of beans.

"They are saying," the voice said, amused, "that your brother picked the biggest one yesterday and did not tell you."

"I KNEW IT—"

Elowen scrambled up, and the vision blurred with motion, and Rafael laughed—a wet, broken sound—because he could feel it through the child's body, through the borrowed nerves of his grandmother at five years old: the hum under everything. The presence in the garden. Not a parasite. Not a tool. A *companion*. The Gardenwright inheritance was not a spell you cast. It was a friendship you kept. Generations of Sterlings had grown up talking to the spirit of the land the way other children talked to imaginary friends, except this friend answered, and healed blight with a sigh, and coaxed harvests from stony ground, and asked for nothing in return but company.

*This is what we were,* Rafael thought, grief rising in him like floodwater. *Before we were exiles. Before we were hunted. We were gardeners who talked to the world.*

* * *

The scene changed the way weather changes—no fold this time, just a slow darkening, gold going to grey.

The same garden, older. Elowen was a young woman now, and she was not alone. A man stood at the gate, and Rafael knew him too, though no image of him survived anywhere: King Aldric the First, the Sterling king, the founder of the line. He was younger than the statues made him look, broad-shouldered, with his family's dark hair and a smile that did not reach his eyes. Rafael recognized those eyes. He had seen them flicker behind the current king's face, terrified, drowning.

"The land is dying in the east," the king said. "Drought, blight, the rivers running thin. My people starve while your valley blooms." He stepped through the gate uninvited, and Rafael watched his grandmother's body tense—the spirit's hum faltering, wary, like a dog hearing a stranger on the road. "You Sterlings sit on the greatest gift in the kingdom and call it a hobby."

"It isn't mine to give," Elowen said quietly. "It isn't anyone's. It's a *relationship*. You can't take a relationship."

"Everything can be taken," the king said, "if you take the right end of it."

And Rafael wanted to scream at her—*run, grandmother, run*—but the vision held her still, held them both, because this was memory and memory does not bend for the wishes of the people walking through it.

The king came back. That was the thing the histories never recorded, Rafael thought, watching it happen across three visits, four, six—the vision compressing years into a handful of scenes like a hand crushing autumn leaves. The king came back with scholars, with instruments, with questions dressed as courtesies. He mapped the garden. He measured the hum. And Elowen, trusting, proud of her inheritance for perhaps the first time in her careful life, *answered him*, because no one had ever taught the Sterlings that their gift was something to hide. Hiding was a lesson the family learned later, written in exile.

On the last visit, the king brought soldiers.

There was no battle. That was the worst part. The spirit rose up to meet them—Rafael felt it, felt the whole valley's worth of gathered presence gather itself like a storm front, ready to defend the garden and the woman in it—and the king simply smiled and produced the thing he had spent two years building from Elowen's own careless answers: a lattice of iron and salt and cut stone, inscribed with words the spirit had taught *Elowen's ancestors in trust*, binding-words, listening-words, turned inside out into cage-words.

The spirit screamed. Rafael screamed with it, because he was still small, still five years old somewhere underneath, and the sound that came out of the garden when the lattice closed was the sound of a person being buried alive. The soldiers flinched. Even the king flinched, just for a moment, before his face hardened into the expression Rafael had seen a hundred times in mirrors during the worst years of his childhood—the expression of someone deciding that a terrible thing is necessary, and therefore no longer terrible.

"What are you doing?" Elowen was on her knees in the ruined bean rows, hands full of soil that had gone suddenly, terribly silent. "What are you DOING—it trusted you—it TRUSTED—"

"And now it will trust me," the king said. "A gift that heals whoever it pleases is a curiosity. A gift that heals only where I command it—that is a *throne*."

He took it. Rafael watched him take it, watched the lattice sink into the earth like a blade, watched the spirit's vast wounded presence shrink and twist and compress itself into something portable, something the king could carry away in a reliquary of black glass. The garden did not die. That was almost worse. The garden lived, because the spirit would not let it die, poured everything it had left into keeping the plants alive around the wound of its own theft—but the hum was gone. The answering voice was gone. The beans grew and no one told Rafael's grandmother which ones were ready.

They left the valley within the year. Exiled by the king who had stolen from them, so no one would ever look too closely at where his miracle had come from. Rafael understood now, with a clarity that felt like a knife going in, why his grandmother had never spoken of it except in fragments, why the family stories were all thorn and no flower. She hadn't lost a gift. She had lost a *friend*, and been forbidden even to mourn it aloud, because mourning would have raised the question of what, exactly, the crown was carrying in that black glass.

* * *

Then the visions darkened further, and Rafael understood they were now seeing what the spirit itself had seen, from inside the reliquary, from inside the crown, through two hundred years of the kings who wore it.

It was starving.

That was the truth at the center of everything, the truth the Verdancy hid behind its creeping vines and its placid rooted villages and its smiling hollow-eyed servants: it was starving, and it had been starving since the day of the lattice. The Gardenwright's spirit had not been built to rule. It had been built to *exchange*—to give healing and receive companionship, to pour itself into the land and be replenished by the love of the people who tended it. The kings gave it nothing. They commanded it, squeezed it, used it to bless their harvests and curse their enemies, and it shrank, and shrank, and the shrinking hurt, and after enough generations of hurt, something in it broke sideways.

The first rooting happened under Aldric the Third, Rafael saw—a kitchen boy whose small gift of warming bread had caught the king's attention, and the king, irritated, had ordered the spirit to *quiet* the boy. And the spirit, mad with hunger, had done something it had never done before: instead of pouring its power outward, it had poured itself *inward*, into the boy, wrapping his little gift in its own substance the way a starving animal curls around food. The boy had gone calm. Serene. Smiling. The king had been delighted.

The spirit had been horrified. Rafael felt that horror, felt it trying to let go and finding it could not, because the boy's gift was *warm* and the spirit was so cold, because two hundred years of starvation had bent its nature until feeding and harming had become the same gesture. Every gifted person it rooted, it rooted half in hunger and half in despair, and every time it swore to stop, and every time the hunger won, and the centuries stacked up like fallen timber until there was no path back through them that the spirit could see.

*They think I am a monster,* it had whispered once, in the dark of the crown, to no one, to itself. *Let me be a monster. Monsters are fed.*

Rafael came out of the vision weeping.

He knelt in the root-chamber with tears running freely down his face, his hand still pressed to the heart-root, and everything he had carried into the dark—twenty years of fear, the hunt, the refugees with green veins, Maren's poisoned well, Vane's hard eyes, Wren's fading light—all of it rearranged itself around this new center the way iron filings leap into a new pattern around a moved magnet. He had come down here prepared to hate the thing he must unbind. He had rehearsed hatred the way a soldier rehearses a charge.

He could not hate it. Hatred required an enemy, and what he had touched was not an enemy. It was a dog that had been kicked for two hundred years until it bit, chained in a yard of its own making, still—God help it, still—wagging at the sound of a kind voice. Somewhere beneath the corruption, beneath the rooted villages and the hollow king, the garden-spirit that had laughed with a five-year-old girl about stolen beans was still in there. Starving. Waiting. Remembering the shape of love without remembering how to ask for it.

"Oh," Rafael said aloud, to the dark, to the root, to the buried friend of everyone he had ever descended from. "Oh, you poor thing. What did they *do* to you?"

The heart-root pulsed under his palm, and this time the pulse stuttered—startled, he thought, that anyone had ever asked it that question. Green-gold light rippled out through the chamber walls, and far above, muffled through fathoms of living wood, Rafael heard the throne room erupt into chaos: shouts, the ring of steel, Vane's voice bellowing his name.

The Verdancy had felt him touch its memories. And whatever remained of the king inside it had felt it too—and was, even now, reaching for him through the root, not to trap him, but with something that felt desperately, impossibly like hope.
