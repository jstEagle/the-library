## 16. The Choice of Anchors

The underground's meeting hall had once been a cistern, and it still remembered water. Rafael felt it in the walls—a damp memory seeping through the old stone, a ghost of coolness that no fire could quite burn away. They sat on a bench that had been a flood-marker once, its back carved with lines and dates: *the high water of this year, the drowning of that one.* Now the lines marked something else. Ovid had chalked them over with the rite's arithmetic.

*One vessel. One gift surrendered. One anchor, held for a lifetime.*

Rafael read the words for the hundredth time since dawn and felt nothing like understanding. Understanding was a door they kept approaching and finding locked from the other side.

Around them, the cistern breathed with the quiet industry of the unrooted. A woman with silver-threaded hands—her gift was mending, Rafael had learned; she could knit broken things whole, though the Verdancy had begun to root in her left arm and now the fingers curled like fern-fronds when she wasn't watching them—stirred a pot of barley over a brazier. Children slept in a heap of blankets near the far wall, their breathing synchronized in the way of children who had learned to sleep in shifts. Wren was not among them. Wren was two chambers away, behind a door of salt-packed oak, sleeping under Ovid's binding-song so the green in her veins would creep slower through the dark hours.

Two days since the dream-vision. Two days since Rafael had walked inside the Verdancy's heart-root and seen what it had been before the kings got hold of it: a garden-spirit, bright as morning, made to heal. Starved into hunger. Twisted into thirst. *Not evil,* Rafael had told the others, voice cracking in the middle of the word. *Wounded.*

Ovid had nodded slowly, the way a man nods when someone finally says the thing he has been carrying alone for thirty years. Then he had drawn the chalk lines on the bench-back, and explained the rest.

* * *

"The rite is simple," Ovid said now, settling onto the bench beside Rafael. He was an old man built like a root himself—gnarled, patient, grown around obstacles. His voice, when he sang, could make stone listen. When he spoke, it was dry as autumn leaves. "Simple the way dying is simple. One motion. No taking it back."

"Say it again," Rafael said. "All of it. I want to hear where I'm wrong."

"You're not wrong anywhere. That's the cruelty of it." Ovid folded his hands. "The Verdancy cannot be killed without killing everything rooted in it. You've seen the city. Half the capital carries its mark. The children in the palace cells. Your Wren. Tear out the heart-root and every vein of green goes necrotic in the same hour. I watched it happen once, in a village that tried to burn it out of their fields. Forty-one people. They smiled all the way to the end—the entity didn't understand they were dying until they were dead."

Rafael's stomach turned. They had seen the smiling. Whole villages on the road south, faces smooth as pond-water, tending gardens that bloomed out of season, waving at travelers with hands that moved like things on strings. Contentment as a mask over consumption.

"So we don't kill it," Rafael said. "We unbind it. Return it to what it was."

"We return it to what it was *made* to be. But a spirit that has starved for three hundred years cannot simply be pointed at the land and released. It would take root in the nearest hungry thing and begin again. It needs—" Ovid searched for the word, and Rafael knew it was coming, had known since midnight, had lain awake feeling it approach like weather. "It needs an anchor. A living vessel strong enough to hold it. To carry it the way a riverbed carries a river. Not to cage it. To *shape* it. To give its hunger banks to flow between until it remembers how to be water instead of flood."

"And the vessel?"

"Must have depth." Ovid's eyes, pale as birch bark, held steady. "The Verdancy feeds on gifted magic. That is its nature now—it has eaten nothing else for centuries. A vessel without a gift gives it nothing to reshape. It would consume the vessel in a day and move on. But a vessel whose gift runs deep enough—deep enough to feed it while teaching it, deep enough to survive being drunk from—" He stopped. Started again, gentler. "You know what I'm describing, storm-caller. Say it yourself. It will sit easier if you say it."

Rafael looked at the chalk lines. *One vessel. One gift surrendered.*

"A storm-caller," they said. Their voice came out flat, strange, like someone else's read aloud from a page. "Me."

* * *

Afterward Rafael climbed the cistern stairs because sitting still had become unbearable, and Vane fell into step beside them without asking, which was its own kind of mercy. The knight had a way of accompanying people that asked nothing. She walked like a woman who had decided long ago that questions were a luxury of peacetime.

They emerged through a broken grate into the root-cellar of an abandoned dyer's shop, and from there up a ladder, through a trapdoor, onto the shop's flat roof. The capital spread below them in the blue hour before full dark, and it was beautiful, and it was wrong. Gardens bloomed on every balcony—roses in the frost month, wisteria heavy as snowfall. Lamplighters moved through the streets with unhurried grace. Somewhere a choir was singing, and every voice was perfectly, terribly in tune.

"Vane." Rafael wrapped their arms around their knees. "When you were a knight of the crown. Did you ever imagine you'd end up sitting on a roof with a storm-caller, planning treason against a garden?"

"Never imagined the garden part." Vane settled cross-legged beside them, sword across her thighs out of habit rather than need. "I imagined worse company."

That startled a laugh out of Rafael—short, cracked, but real. Below them the choir swelled. The Verdancy's serenity lay over the city like a second sky.

"They told you already," Vane said. Not a question. "Ovid. About the anchor."

"He didn't have to tell me. I did the arithmetic myself, somewhere around the third time he said *depth.*" Rafael stared at their own hands. Ordinary-looking hands. Hands that had spent twenty years learning to be ordinary. "There's exactly one person in this city—or this kingdom—with a gift deep enough to hold it. And I've known it was me since the moment I understood what anchoring meant. Do you know what the worst part is? Some part of me is *relieved.*"

Vane turned her head. She didn't speak. She had a soldier's patience for silence, and she spent it generously.

"Twenty years I've been holding this thing down," Rafael said. "Dampening it. Sitting on it like a lid on a boiling pot. Every day in the Hollow, I woke up and my first thought was *how do I keep it quiet today.* My first thought every morning for twenty years was about the storm. Not my neighbors. Not the roof I was fixing. The storm." Their throat tightened. "And now someone tells me: give it away. All of it. Forever. Be ordinary. And some small, shameful part of me thinks—*finally. Finally I can put it down.*"

"There's nothing shameful in wanting to set down a weight," Vane said. "I carried a sword for the crown for eleven years. The day I understood what it was doing—what *I* was doing—I wanted nothing more than to drop it in a river. Wanting to stop isn't surrender. It's just tiredness telling the truth."

"But that's the trap, don't you see?" Rafael's voice rose, and somewhere below a lamplighter paused, head tilting at the sky, listening to weather that hadn't come. Rafael dragged the volume back down. "If I do this because I'm *tired*, then I'm not choosing the anchor. I'm choosing escape. And the Verdancy deserves better than that. Wren deserves better than that. If I'm going to hand it my whole self, I have to be sure I'm giving, not fleeing." They pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "And I can't be sure. I can't find the line between the two. I've looked for it for two days and it isn't there."

Vane was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had the roughness it got only when she talked about her sister.

"When they took Lira," she said, "she fought. Of course she fought—she was fourteen and her gift was small, just a little warmth-magic, candle-flame stuff, but she fought them like a wildcat. And afterward, in the cells, when the rooting started, she stopped fighting. That was the part I couldn't forgive myself for. Not that I failed her. That she *stopped.* Because the Verdancy makes it feel like peace. It takes the fighting out of you and calls the emptiness contentment." She looked at Rafael squarely. "So here's how I tell the difference, when I can't find the line. Peace makes you larger. Emptiness makes you smaller. Whatever you choose—ask yourself which one you'll grow into."

* * *

Rafael went down to the salt-oak door alone, past midnight, when the cistern had gone quiet except for the brazier's tick and the mender-woman's slow breathing at her post.

They didn't go in. They sat with their back against the door, shoulder blades against wood that smelled of brine and old prayer, and listened to Wren breathe on the other side. Small breaths. Steady. A child asleep, dreaming whatever children dream when there is a forest drinking at their blood.

*I gave up storms for twenty years,* Rafael thought. *But giving up isn't the same as giving away. I never gave it away. I just buried it alive and called the grave a valley.*

They tried to remember who they had been before the Hollow. Before the exile. They had been seven years old when the family fled, seven years old when the sky over their grandmother's house had split open because Rafael had cried too hard and the clouds had answered. Everything after that was suppression. Dampening. Careful invisibility. The storm-gift was the first thing anyone had ever known them by—the reason for the exile, the reason for the hiding, the shape of every fear their parents carried—and so it had become, without their consent, the whole of who they were. *Storm-caller.* Even here, even among friends, that was the name in everyone's mouth.

And underneath the name? Who was underneath?

Rafael reached for an answer and found, unsettlingly, that the answer was not empty. There were things in them that had nothing to do with lightning. The patience of roof-mending. The particular attention they paid to strangers' hands, reading fear in knuckles. The stubbornness. The terrible soft-heartedness that had let refugees across the border stones knowing it might cost everything. Maren's forgiveness, still warm in their chest like a coal. Twenty years of small ordinary kindnesses performed with ordinary hands—and those years belonged to them, not to the storm. The storm had been silent the whole time. The kindness hadn't.

*Maybe that's who I am underneath,* Rafael thought. *Maybe the storm was always just weather passing over something steadier.*

But then—honestly, because they had promised themselves honesty even here, especially here—the other truth rose up too. The joy of it. That night on the ridge, when the gift had flared free and the lightning had come down like an answer to a question Rafael had been asking all their life. The intoxication of it. The feeling, crossing the border stones, of a drowned thing surfacing—terrible and *theirs*. Power as homecoming. However much they had resented the burden, some root-deep part of them had sung when the sky answered. To give that up forever was not setting down a weight. It was amputation. It was choosing to be a smaller shape for the rest of their life, and hoping Vane was wrong about which choice made you smaller.

Both things were true. That was the discovery that broke them open, sitting against the salt-oak door in the dark: both things were true at once. They wanted to be rid of it and they could not bear to lose it. They were more than the storm and the storm was in everything they were. Any choice made from only one of those truths would be a lie wearing courage's clothes.

So they would have to choose from both.

Rafael laid their palm flat against the door. Beneath the wood, faint as a pulse through a wrist, they felt it—the green thread of the Verdancy in Wren's veins, feeding, patient, growing. Tomorrow or the day after, Ovid would say the time had come. The heart-root beneath the throne room would not wait forever; already the palace guard stood too straight, smiled too readily, and Serah Vane reported that the king's summons had gone out in the Verdancy's own handwriting, elegant and green.

*One vessel. One gift surrendered.*

"I know," Rafael whispered—to Wren, to the spirit starving beneath the palace, to the storm coiled in their own chest like a sleeping animal that trusted them. "I hear it. All of it."

And the storm stirred—not flaring, not resisting, just turning over in its sleep, the way a creature does when it knows its keeper is leaving the room, and presses itself briefly against the closing door.

Rafael stood. Their legs were stiff and their eyes were raw and somewhere above them the false spring bloomed on under a sky that had forgotten winter. They climbed back toward the cistern, toward Ovid and the chalk lines, and with every step the answer assembled itself—not as certainty, certainty was a luxury no one in this city could afford—but as resolve, which was certainty's poorer, tougher cousin, and had carried people further.

At the top of the stairs, Ovid was waiting, lantern in hand, as if he had heard the decision coming up through the stone.

"It's time?" Rafael asked.

Ovid's face was grave and kind. "The heart-root has begun to move," he said. "Tonight."
