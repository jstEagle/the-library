## 17. Root and Ruin

The bells began at dusk, and Serah Vane knew before the first toll faded what they meant.

She stood on the palace steps with a stolen officer's cloak around her shoulders and Rafael's absence like a stone in her throat. Somewhere below, beneath the throne room, in the dark the Verdancy had grown for itself, her friend—she could admit it now, friend, after everything—was descending into the thing's heart. And above, the city was waking to the fact that something had gone wrong in its gilded dream.

Not waking. That was too kind. The rooted were *shifting*, the way a sleeper shifts when a hand touches their shoulder. She could see it from the steps: figures pausing mid-stride in the lantern-lit streets, heads tilting as one toward the palace, faces serene and terrible in their unanimity. Ten thousand people turning like compass needles.

"They know," said Ovid beside her. The root-singer's voice was hoarse from hours of preparation, their hands stained green to the wrist from grinding the old resins. "The heart felt him coming. It's pulling everyone it holds to stand between us and the doors."

"Then we hold anyway." Vane drew her sword—not the ceremonial blade the crownhunters carried, but her own, the one she'd buried under a floorboard in Aldervale for six years because looking at it meant remembering her sister. It came up singing. "How long do you need?"

Ovid looked at the sky, where storm-light was already gathering—Rafael's doing, or the sky answering him, Vane no longer knew the difference. "The binding-song has three movements. The first two I can sing alone. The third needs the anchor inside the heart, pulling against me from within." A pause. "If he pulls wrong, or if I falter, the song becomes an invocation instead. We won't unbind it. We'll give it everything."

"Then don't falter."

"I'm sixty years old and my knees are bad," Ovid said, with the ghost of a smile. "But my grandmother sang this song to a living garden, so perhaps it remembers me."

Vane left them there and went down into the streets.

* * *

They came in silence, which was worse than shouting.

Vane had held gates before—in the border wars, at Cael Ford, in places whose names had been scrubbed from maps since. She had held them against screaming hordes and disciplined columns, and she had learned that noise was manageable; noise was just fear given voice. But the rooted did not scream. They walked out of the side streets and down the great avenue by the hundreds, lanterns swinging in their unhurried hands, and their faces were the faces of people going to a festival. Smiling. All of them smiling.

"Fall back," she told the underground fighters along the barricade of overturned carts. "Blades only. No killing blows unless you must—they're not choosing this. None of them chose it."

A young man with a crossbow shook. "My mother's out there. Third row. She's smiling at me, Captain."

"I know." Vane's own chest was a closed fist. "I know. Hold."

The first wave reached the carts and simply... pushed. No weapons. They laid their hands on the wood and shoved, gentle as movers, gentle as gardeners rearranging a trellis, and the barricade groaned and slid, and behind their mildness came more of them, and more, a tide with patient hands.

Vane stepped onto the barricade's spine and raised her sword flat across her body—the old parley stance, though there would be no parley here—and shouted over the sea of upturned faces:

"You are being used! Whatever is in you, whatever whispers that this feels good, that this feels like rest—it is eating you while you smile! Fight it! If you have anything left that is yours, fight it!"

For one heartbeat—one—one woman near the front faltered. Her smile flickered like a lamp in wind. Her eyes found Vane's, and in them was something small and drowning and *hers*, and her mouth shaped a word.

*Run.*

Then the green rose in her veins like a tide coming in, and the smile smoothed back over her face, and the press resumed.

Vane's vision blurred. She blinked it away and turned to the line. "Lock shields. We hold until the song ends or we fall. Those are the only endings tonight."

* * *

In the crypt beneath the throne room, Wren was losing.

The child sat in the circle of salt-and-ash that Ovid had drawn days ago, guarding the descent tunnel with the only weapon they had: themselves. Because Wren's gift was small—a warmth-gift, candle-flame small—but it was *theirs*, still theirs, even with the Verdancy threaded through their blood like ivy through a fence, and the entity wanted it finished. Wanted the last loose root pulled tight.

So it pulled.

Wren felt it as cold, spreading up from the soles of their feet. The warmth in their chest guttered like a candle in a doorway. And the voice came with it, patient as water wearing stone:

*You are tired, little ember. You have been tired for so long. Let go. Resting is not dying. Resting is just—*

"No." Wren's voice cracked. They were eleven years old and their hands were shaking, but they pressed both palms flat against the cold floor and pushed their small flame down into the stone, warming it, claiming it, marking the circle as *mine*. "This is mine. My mam gave it to me. She said—" Their breath hitched. "She said gifts aren't taken, they're kept. So I'm keeping it."

*Your mam is smiling somewhere right now,* the voice murmured, almost tender. *Wouldn't you like to smile too?*

And oh, that was the trap, that was the hook in the fish's mouth—because Wren *did* want it, some shameful sliver of them wanted the peace, the softness, the end of being afraid. They wanted it the way a drowning person wants the sweet gray water.

But then they thought of Rafael Sterling walking down into the dark on their behalf. Of a stranger who'd known them four days and was giving up the storm in his blood so Wren could keep a candle.

Wren gritted their teeth and fed the flame.

"Cold," they whispered to the darkness, to the roots writhing pale at the tunnel mouth. "You can't grow in a warm house. Everybody knows that."

The roots recoiled, hissing like water on iron. And Wren laughed—small and fierce and eleven—and burned brighter.

* * *

Above, the second movement of the binding-song began, and the night changed shape.

Ovid stood at the top of the palace steps, arms raised, and what came out of them was not music so much as weather—the sound the land itself might make if the land could grieve. People on the barricade wept without knowing why. Rooted soldiers slowed, their smiles twitching, something ancient in their marrow remembering another song, a kinder one, a garden-song from before the theft.

And the sky answered.

The storm Rafael had called as cover now boiled over the capital like a lid lifting off a pot. Lightning walked the clouds in long silver strides. Rain came down in sheets, hissing on lanterns, running green off the rooftops as the Verdancy's surface-roots drowned and died in the wash.

Vane, hip against the barricade, cutting the straps of a grappling hook some rooted guardsman had thrown, looked up at that sky and thought: *Hold on, storm-caller. Whatever you're finding down there—hold on.*

* * *

Rafael descended.

The tunnel beneath the throne room was not dug. It was *grown*—walls of pale woven root, smooth as bone, lit from within by a faint green pulse that quickened as he went deeper, quickened like a heartbeat recognizing footsteps. His storm-gift roared in him the whole way down, twenty years of dammed thunder straining at its banks, and he let it build, because Ovid needed it built, because the third movement required an anchor who could pull against a god.

But he was afraid. He would not lie to himself about that. He was afraid not of dying but of *becoming*—of touching the heart-root and finding, as he'd found in the dream-vision, that he pitied it. Pity was a dangerous thing to carry into a battle of wills. Pity opened doors.

At the bottom, the tunnel opened into the chamber he had seen with his eyes shut: the heart.

It was a garden.

That was the horror of it, and the truth of it, and he stood on the threshold and wept a little, because of course it was. Of course. What else would a stolen healing-spirit build at the center of its hunger, after two hundred years of being forced to be a crown? Rows of white flowers bloomed in soil that had never seen sun. Pale trees arched overhead, their branches heavy with fruit that gleamed like pearls. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. It was beautiful the way a cage built by a bird is beautiful—all instinct and longing and no sky.

At the center stood King Aldric.

Or the shape of him. He hung among the roots like a man caught in amber, eyes open, and when they fixed on Rafael they were lucid—lucid and drowning and pleading.

"*Sterling,*" the king rasped, and the roots tightened around his throat, and the whole garden shuddered. "*He knows you're here. He'll pull the city down on your friends. You have to—*"

The voice changed. Deepened. Became a chord of voices, the king's and ten thousand others braided into one vast calm:

**YOU BROUGHT THE STORM TO MY GARDEN, LITTLE THIEF'S CHILD.**

"Not a thief's child," Rafael said. His voice echoed strangely among the white rows. "A gardener's. That's the difference you never understood. My family didn't steal the gift. Your king did. We've been carrying your cure for two hundred years, and you've been hunting your own medicine through the streets."

The garden went very still.

**THE MEDICINE BURNS,** the Verdancy said, and for one unguarded moment, beneath the vastness, Rafael heard something else—something small and starving and two centuries lonely. **ALL FIRE CONSUMES. I HAVE BEEN PATIENT. I HAVE KEPT THEM SAFE FROM THEIR OWN FLAMES.**

"You've kept them in jars," Rafael said softly. He stepped forward, into the first row of white flowers, and felt the roots rise around his ankles like curious serpents. He let them. "I'm not here to burn you. I'm here to finish what my grandmother couldn't. There's a third ending, old one. Not destruction. Not dominion. *Release.*"

**RELEASE IS DEATH.**

"Release is *garden*." Rafael raised his hands, and the storm came down through him at last—through root and bone and two hundred years of exile—filling the chamber with light and pressure and the smell of rain on hot stone. "You were made to heal a kingdom. Heal it. But you'll need somewhere to put all that hunger, all that patience, all that terrible love. You'll need an anchor."

The roots went taut. Every flower turned toward him.

**AND WHAT ANCHOR DO YOU OFFER?**

Rafael smiled, and it hurt, and it was the truest thing he had ever done.

"The storm," he said. "Me. Come and see."

* * *

On the steps, Ovid's voice tore like cloth.

The third movement was beginning, and it required two singers, and only half the song stood at the top of the stairs. Below, the barricade was bending. Vane's shield line was a fistful of bleeding people holding back a thousand gentle hands, and Wren's candle was guttering far underground, and the sky was breaking open over all of them—

—and in the heart-garden, Rafael Sterling spread his arms and offered the storm, and the Verdancy, starving, *reached*.

The last thing Vane saw before the light took everything was Ovid's face, wet and singing, turned toward the palace like a sunflower toward a violent dawn.

The song had begun.
