## 18. Unbinding the Crown

The heart of the Verdancy was not what Rafael had expected.

They had braced for teeth. For thorns. For the green dark of a thing that had eaten a kingdom from the inside out, that wore contentment like a mask over its hunger. What they found instead—sinking through layers of root and memory, past walls of woven light that parted before them like a mother opening her arms—was silence. A vast, aching silence, the kind that lived in empty houses and abandoned gardens.

*Here*, Ovid's song said, though it was no longer sound so much as shape, a thread of meaning Rafael could feel against their skin like warm water. *Here is where it keeps what it has taken.*

Rafael drifted in the green.

Around them, suspended in the living amber of the entity's core, hung everything the Verdancy had ever swallowed. Faces. Voices. Small bright fragments of gifted lives—the flicker of a fire-caller's first spark, the hum of a woman who could speak to rivers, a boy's laughter that made flowers open out of season. They were not dead, these things. That was the horror and the mercy of it all at once. They were *kept*. Pressed like blossoms between the pages of an enormous book, preserved so perfectly that preservation had become a kind of burial.

And beneath them all, deeper than anything, a man sat with his head in his hands.

Rafael knew him without being told. The shoulders were broader than the king's throne-room silhouette suggested, worn thinner now, hunched under an invisible weight. His hair had gone white at the temples though his face was still young-ish, still handsome in the way portraits demanded, but the portrait-painters had never captured this: the absolute exhaustion carved into every line of him, the look of a man who had been drowning for years and had finally stopped struggling because struggling was its own kind of drowning.

"Aldric." Rafael's voice came out strange here—not air-borne but root-borne, traveling through the entity itself, so that the whole green dark seemed to whisper it back. "Aldric Sterling."

The man flinched. His hands lowered slowly, and Rafael saw his eyes—Aldric's eyes, but without the Verdancy's sheen behind them, just human and brown and terrified.

"You're real," Aldric said. His voice was rusted from disuse. "You're—you're actually here. I thought I'd dreamed the last one. The girl who sang. I thought I'd dreamed all of you."

"I'm real." Rafael knelt in the green light, which was not quite kneeling, since there was no floor, only the sense of descending into something that held them gently and would not let go. "My name is Rafael Sterling."

Something moved across the king's face—recognition, then grief, then a terrible bitter laugh that shook his whole frame.

"Sterling." He said the name like a wound reopening. "Of course. Of course it would be a Sterling who came down here. My grandmother stole from your grandmother, and now we're both—" he gestured at the suffocating splendor around them, the pressed faces, the kept light, "—we're both buried in the garden she should have had."

"Then you know," Rafael said softly. "You know what this is. What it was meant to be."

"I've known since I was nine years old." Aldric pulled his knees up like a child. In the throne room he had been a mountain, a monument, a crown wearing a man; here he was only a person, and smaller than Rafael had imagined any king could be. "It showed me. When it took me—when it rooted—it showed me everything. The garden my ancestor was given. The healing magic. It was supposed to go into the *land*, do you understand? Into the fields and the forests and the rivers, so nothing would ever blight, so no child would ever starve. And Corwin Sterling took it—" his voice cracked on the old king's name, "—took it and bound it to the bloodline instead. Made it serve the crown instead of the soil. Starved it for three hundred years and called the starving *loyalty*."

"And now it's starving other people," Rafael said. Not cruelly. But they did not soften it either; they had learned, somewhere between the valley and this place, that mercy without truth was just another kind of rot. "Wren is eight years old. She's down here too, somewhere. Being kept. Being eaten slowly enough that no one notices until there's nothing left but the smile."

"I know." Aldric covered his face again. "I know. Every day I know. Do you think I don't feel it? Every gift it swallows, I feel it pass through me like cold water. I have begged it to stop. I have screamed at it inside my own skull until I had no voice left even in dreams. And it doesn't hate me, that's the worst of it. It doesn't punish me. It just—" he searched for the word, and Rafael watched him find it and wince at it, "—it just *loves* me. The way a famine loves the last granary. It holds on because holding on is the only thing it remembers how to do."

Rafael reached out—not touching, not yet, but close enough that the green light between their hands began to shimmer.

"My grandmother wrote me a letter before she died," they said. "She told me our family's inheritance wasn't the storm. The storm was just the echo, the afterthought. The inheritance was the garden. The healing. And she said—" Rafael closed their eyes, and the words came back whole, learned by heart across twenty years of careful invisibility, "—she said: *what was stolen cannot be returned by the thief. It can only be returned by someone willing to carry it home.*"

"She waited her whole life," Aldric whispered. "Your grandmother. She sent letters to my father. To my grandfather. They burned them. She sent one to me when I was crowned and I—" his breath hitched, "—I never read it. The Verdancy read it first. It was afraid. I felt it be *afraid*, for the first time, and I let it burn the letter because I wanted it to stay calm. I wanted my reign to be quiet." He laughed again, that awful rusted sound. "I traded a kingdom's healing for a quiet coronation week."

"You were nine-and-twenty and alone with a monster wearing your face," Rafael said. "I won't call that treason. I've spent twenty years hiding in a valley. I know exactly how loud fear can be, and how easy it is to mistake silence for peace."

For a long moment the king said nothing. Then, slowly, he uncurled. He looked at Rafael's offered hand as if it were something holy, or something on fire—he could not decide which frightened him more.

"If I take it," he said, "it will feel you. It will know you're here. It's sleeping right now, sated from the harvest, but it wakes fast and it wakes *hungry*, and you're carrying a storm-caller's depth. You're the richest thing it has ever smelled."

"That's rather the point," Rafael said.

*

The waking was not gentle.

The moment Aldric's hand closed around Rafael's, the whole green world convulsed. The pressed faces shuddered in their amber. The walls of woven light contracted like a throat, and through every root-thread of the entity poured a single vast emotion, wordless and enormous:

*fear.*

Not rage. Rafael had prepared for rage, for the lashing desperation of a cornered beast. But what crashed over them was fear—old fear, ancient fear, the fear of something that had once been small and soft and *needed*, that had given everything it was away and been repaid with chains. The fear of the garden-spirit remembering, all at once, three hundred years of hands reaching into it to take.

*THIEF,* the Verdancy thundered, and the word was not aimed at Aldric. It wrapped around Rafael like a fist of roots, squeezing, tasting, drinking at the storm-depth in their chest. *THIEF OF STORMS. THIEF OF EVERYTHING. YOU ALL COME TO TAKE. THE FIRST ONE CAME TO TAKE AND I WAS YOUNG AND I GAVE AND GAVE AND GAVE—*

"I know," Rafael gasped, and did not fight the grip, because fighting was taking, fighting was another hand reaching in. Instead they opened themselves. Let the entity pour into them as far as it wished, see everything—the dampened storms of twenty years, the border stones, the neighbor's roof mended with careful ordinary hands, the letter read a thousand times by candlelight. *I know what it is to hold something precious and watch it be twisted. I am the last of the thieves' line. I came to give back.*

The grip faltered.

*I came to give back,* Rafael said again, and meant it with their whole body, their whole buried inheritance, their whole storm. *Not to destroy you. Not to use you. My family broke you, and I am asking your pardon, and I am asking something harder than pardon: I'm asking you to stop.*

*STOP?* The laughter that shook through the green was jagged as broken glass. *IF I STOP I DIE. YOUR KIND MADE ME THIS. I ROOT OR I STARVE. THERE IS NO THIRD THING.*

"There is," said a third voice, small and steady.

Wren.

She was there—of course she was there, drawn along the bond Rafael hadn't known they'd forged in the cells below, standing in the green light with her bare feet and her green-veined wrists and her chin tipped up at the vastness around her with the particular fearlessness of children who have already survived the worst thing. Beside her, ghostly, translucent, stood others—the pressed faces, waking, gathering, a congregation of the kept.

"You think letting go is dying," Wren said, "because everyone who ever held you taught you that. But my nan let go of her garden when she got too old to tend it, and it didn't die. It went wild. It went *everywhere*. Roses through the fence and mint in every crack and bees from three houses over." She spread her small arms. "Letting go isn't dying. Letting go is planting."

*PLANTING,* the Verdancy echoed, and the word moved through it like a tremor through a leaf.

"The rite needs a vessel," Rafael said quietly. "Someone strong enough to hold the anchor while you release. I'm offering. My storm-depth for your freedom. Not a cage—a cradle. Long enough for you to remember how to grow into soil instead of people. And then, when you're ready, I let go too, and you spread, and you heal, and nobody owns you. Not a king. Not a family. Not even me." They smiled, and found to their surprise that tears were running down their face in the green light. "Thorn and garden again. That was always what you were. Both of them. Never just the thorn."

The entity was silent for a long time.

In that silence Rafael felt it turn things over—the way it must once have turned seeds over in spring soil, testing, hoping. They felt three hundred years of starvation press against the possibility of one act of trust. They felt it look at Aldric, who was weeping openly now, who had fallen to his knees in the light; at Wren, whose green veins had begun to fade to the pale gold of healthy new growth; at the congregation of the kept, waiting.

*IF I AM WRONG,* it said at last, and its voice had changed—smaller now, softer, the voice of something young, *IF THIS IS ANOTHER TRICK OF THE CROWN'S BLOOD—*

"Then I will spend my whole powerless life making it right," Rafael said. "That's all anyone can offer. That's what a promise is."

*THEN,* said the garden that had been a parasite that had been a gift that had been stolen, *TEACH ME THE SONG AGAIN.*

Ovid's binding-song rose through the roots—from above, from the palace, from the underground singing in a circle around the throne room, their voices threading down through stone and soil. And Rafael took Aldric's hand in their left and Wren's in their right, and they began to walk—not downward anymore but outward, toward the edge of the green, toward the world—and everywhere they walked, the amber released what it held. Faces woke. Gifts unfurled like leaves after rain. The river-speaker went home to her rivers; the flower-laughing boy ran laughing through corridors of dissolving light; and the Verdancy poured itself out after them, thinner and thinner and freer and freer, until what remained in Rafael's cupped hands was not a monster at all but a handful of living seed, warm as a bird's egg, glowing faintly the gold-green of the very first spring.

Above them, impossibly far above, the gilded ceiling of the throne room split open like a shell.

*

Rafael came back to their body kneeling on the cracked marble dais, Ovid's song ending in a long sustained note that hung in the ruined air. Vane stood over them with her sword drawn and blood on her cheek, holding the line against guards who were no longer guards—no longer rooted, no longer smiling, blinking at each other in dawning confusion like men waking from a shared fever dream.

On the throne, King Aldric drew a breath that seemed to come from the bottom of the sea.

He looked older and younger at once. Diminished. Human. His hands, gripping the armrests, were trembling—but they were *his* hands, and everyone in the hall could see it.

"It's gone," he said hoarsely. "It's—" He looked at Rafael, kneeling amid the ruin with a handful of glowing seed cradled against their chest, and the king of the realm bowed his head to them, low and long, in front of gods and witnesses. "Sterling. I don't—I don't have words that are large enough."

"Grow some," Rafael said. Their voice cracked, and they realized why: the storm was gone. Utterly, quietly gone, spent down to the last drop into the cradle-anchor, and where it had lived for thirty years there was now a stillness so complete it rang like a struck bell. They were ordinary. They were empty-handed. They were free.

They looked down at the seed in their palms, and it pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat learning its rhythm.

Somewhere beyond the shattered windows, far past the city walls, past the roads and the ridges and the border stones, a valley waited—one specific patch of ground that had spent twenty years forgetting how to be magical, and one grandmother's letter that had promised, in a careful faded hand: *what was stolen can be carried home.*

Rafael closed their fingers around the seed, stood up on shaking legs, and turned to Serah Vane.

"Knight," they said. "How soon can you get me a horse?"
