## 19. After the Storm

Serah Vane had stood on battlefields where the dead lay in rows like felled timber, and she had thought she knew what the aftermath of a war looked like. She had been wrong. The aftermath of a possession was stranger than any slaughter—quieter, gentler, and somehow more terrible to walk through.

The city woke in stages.

First came the sound: a low murmur rising from ten thousand throats at once, like a forest exhaling after a long held breath. Serah stood on the palace steps as dawn broke grey and gold over the capital, and she heard it roll outward through the streets—the sound of people remembering themselves. A woman near the fountain stopped mid-smile, her face crumpling as though a mask had cracked away all at once. A man dropped the broom he'd been sweeping with for what might have been days and sat down hard on the cobbles, staring at his own hands as if they belonged to someone else.

Then came the weeping. Not the wailing of grief, exactly—or not only that—but the raw, unguarded crying of people who had not been permitted to feel anything at all for months. The Verdancy had worn contentment like a coat over its captives, and now that the coat was stripped away, everything underneath poured out at once: terror, relief, rage, confusion, and beneath it all a vast and simple grief for the time they had lost.

Serah descended the steps into it because someone had to. Her armor was scorched down one side where the rooted guard had turned on her at the gates; her sword arm ached to the shoulder; there was a bruise blooming purple along her jaw where a gardener—a *gardener*, a soft-bellied man who smelled of compost—had struck her with a shovel with the strength of something far older than himself. But she walked, and the crowd parted around her, and some of them reached out to touch her pauldron as she passed, the way farmers touch a post that has held through a storm.

"Is it over?" an old woman asked her, gripping her wrist with shocking strength. "Is it truly over? I dreamed I was happy. I dreamed it for so long, and I couldn't wake."

"It's over," Serah said, and found her voice did not break, though it wanted to. "You're awake now."

The woman looked at her a long moment, searching her face for the lie, and then released her and turned to help the man with the broom, and Serah kept walking, because there were miles of city behind those two and every mile would need the same words.

* * *

By midday, the scale of it became clear, and the scale was the miracle.

Ovid's root-singers moved through the districts in their patched robes, singing the old rites into wells and doorways and the roots of street trees, easing the last threads of the Verdancy out of flesh and soil. And everywhere they went, the rooted woke—and lived. That was the thing Serah could not stop checking, the number she made Ovid give her three times before she believed it: no deaths. Not among the rooted. Whatever Rafael Sterling had done inside the heart of the thing beneath the throne room, they had done it without breaking a single life entangled in its roots. Half the city had been bound to that heart, and half the city still breathed.

"Because it wasn't killed," Ovid told her, sitting on a well's edge with his hands wrapped around a cup of barley tea he never drank. He was an old man with a voice like wind through wheat, and he looked more tired than any soldier Serah had ever seen. "It was *released*. There is a difference wider than the sea. Kill a parasite and you kill what it feeds on too, if the feeding has gone deep enough. But this—" he gestured at the waking streets, at a child chasing a dog who had clearly been standing motionless in the square for hours, "—this was a wound being closed. Slowly. Gently. By someone who understood that the monster was also the patient."

"Rafael," Serah said.

"Rafael." Ovid nodded. "And the king. Don't forget him. It takes two hands to unclench a fist." He sipped the tea at last, grimacing at its coldness. "I sang the binding-song until my throat bled, girl, and I will tell you honestly: I did not believe it would work. I believed we would die in that hall and call it a mercy. What happened instead was—" he searched for the word, "—*gardening*. The gentlest violence I have ever witnessed."

Serah looked east, toward the palace gardens, where the out-of-season blooms were doing something none of the botanists could explain. They weren't dying. They weren't running wild either. They were simply... settling, folding back into season like birds returning to a roost, the roses closing their impossible summer petals and letting winter have them. The Verdancy, unbound from the crown, had gone into the land itself—the way it should have gone a hundred years ago, the way a stolen inheritance should always have flowed. She had seen a farmer that morning kneel in a field outside the walls and press his palm to soil that steamed faintly green, and weep without knowing why.

Hope, she thought. This was what hope looked like when it arrived after too long an absence: it looked like confusion. It looked like people not trusting their own joy.

* * *

King Aldric received the city's first delegation three days later, and Serah stood guard at the door of the audience chamber because she could not make herself stand anywhere else.

He was diminished—that was the word everyone used, whispered in corridors, though no one quite meant it as insult. The man who sat the throne now was thinner, greyer, older by decades than the serene golden figure who had presided over the hunt. His hands trembled when he lifted them. His eyes, which had glowed faintly green even in the deepest night for as long as anyone could remember, were just eyes now: brown, bloodshot, human, and haunted by everything they had watched from inside their own skull.

He had abdicated nothing yet. But everyone in the palace knew it was coming, because Aldric had spent the first day of his freedom doing something no Sterling king had ever done: apologizing. To the families of the taken children. To the refugees driven across the kingdom. To the gifted who had hidden in cellars and hollows while his crown hunted them. He received them in ones and twos, and he knelt to them, and the court chroniclers wrote it all down with shaking hands because there was no precedent in any book for a king on his knees.

Today the delegation was different. Today it was the underground—Ovid's people, the unrooted gifted who had survived in the city's cracks—and with them, escorted up from the valley roads only that morning, a handful of weathered folk from the Hollow, Maren the herbalist at their head with her green-veined forearm bare and unashamed.

Serah watched from her post as Aldric rose from the throne and came down the dais steps to meet them, and she heard him say, in a voice roughened by weeping and disuse:

"The crown took your inheritance and made it a chain. I cannot return what my grandfather stole, but I can return what remains. The garden-magic belongs to the land and to its keepers—not to kings. And kings will no longer decide alone who is dangerous and who is safe. There will be a council. Gifted and ungifted alike. Root-singer and commoner. I am told I will not enjoy sharing power." A ragged, disbelieving laugh moved through the chamber. "Good. I have enjoyed power for thirty years, and look what it grew in me."

Maren stepped forward, and Serah saw the herbalist study the king the way she studied a sick patient—with neither hatred nor mercy, only assessment. "Words are cheap, majesty," she said. "Soil isn't. Send seeds, not decrees. Let us see what grows."

Aldric bowed his head. "Seeds," he agreed. "And hands to plant them. Mine, if you'll have them."

* * *

The medal ceremony was scheduled for the following week, and Serah Vane decided, somewhere between the third fitting for ceremonial dress and the second draft of the herald's proclamation honoring her, that she would rather be flayed.

It wasn't false, exactly—what they meant to honor. She had held the gates. She had freed the prisoner she'd been ordered to root. She had turned her sword against the crown she'd served for twenty years, and twenty years of service bought a person a great deal of credibility when they finally broke with it, and the council-in-formation needed credible voices badly. She understood all of that. She understood, too, that refusing would read as ingratitude, or worse, as guilt.

But she had seen the cells beneath the palace. She had seen what waited there for captured children before Rafael's storm tore the doors open—Wren small and grey-eyed in the dark, the green climbing her arms like ivy claiming a wall. And Serah had served that system faithfully, escorting prisoners, filing reports, telling herself each time that the crown must know best, that the hunt was regrettable but necessary, that the serenity in the citizens' faces was peace rather than theft. Twenty years of looking directly at it and calling it duty.

She could not stand on a dais and accept a medal for the single day she had finally acted like what she'd claimed to be.

She went to find Rafael on the evening before the ceremony, down in the palace gardens where the restored magic pooled thickest, and found them sitting on the low wall beside a bed of ordinary, properly seasonal crocuses, watching Wren chase fireflies with both hands full of light.

"You're leaving," Rafael said, without turning around. Storm-callers, Serah was learning, heard things in the air before they were spoken. Or perhaps they had simply once been like that themselves—she was still learning what Rafael was now, this quiet, watchful person where the storm had been.

"I'm leaving," Serah confirmed. She leaned on the wall beside them. "There's a garrison on the southern coast that's been requesting a drill-master for years. Nobody wants the posting. It's far from the capital, far from the council, far from"—she gestured vaguely at the palace, the proclamations, the whole glittering apparatus of gratitude—"all this."

"Exile by choice," Rafael said. "I know the shape of it. I lived in it for twenty years."

"That's what frightens me," Serah admitted. It came out plainer than she intended, the way truths did around this person. "That I'll build myself another Hollow. That hiding will start to feel like virtue again."

Rafael considered that, turning a crocus petal between their fingers. "Then don't hide," they said finally. "Teach. Drill your soldiers differently. Write letters to the council when they do something stupid—they'll do plenty, new councils always do. Exile isn't the same as absence, Serah. I learned that too late to spare myself a lot of grief. You can leave a place and still belong to what happens there."

Below them, Wren caught a firefly, cupped it gently, and let it go, shrieking with laughter at her own mercy. The sound rang off the garden walls, bright and absurd and utterly ordinary, and Serah felt something in her chest unknot slightly—the same feeling she'd had watching the city wake, hope arriving like a stranger she didn't trust yet.

"They'll want to give you a medal too," she said.

"I know." Rafael smiled, thin and wry. "I'm going to ask them to plant a tree instead. Something with thorns. For honesty's sake."

* * *

On the morning of the ceremony, Serah left before dawn.

She went first to the cells—not the ruined ones below the old wing, but the new holding rooms the council had ordered built, airy and windowed, already standing empty because there was no hunt anymore, no list, no harvest. She stood in the doorway a moment anyway, checking, the way she checked every morning and suspected she would check for years. Empty. Good. She locked nothing, because there was nothing to lock, and climbed the stairs into the cold gold light of a capital learning to breathe.

At the gates, a young clerk from the herald's office was waiting with the medal case and a face full of dread at having been assigned to intercept her. "Lady Vane—the procession forms within the hour, if you would just—"

"I've left a letter for the council," Serah said. "In it I decline the honor, recommend three names worthier than mine, and offer my services to the southern garrison, effective immediately." She swung her travel pack onto her shoulder. "Tell them the truce I made in the Hollow stands. Tell them I'll answer letters."

"And—forgive me, my lady—but what shall I tell the chroniclers? They'll want a reason. For the histories."

Serah looked past him, down the long avenue where the city was waking again—truly waking this time, vendors shouting prices, children running late for lessons, a root-singer's song drifting faintly from a rooftop garden where green things grew in honest season. Somewhere beyond the walls, a storm was building over the western hills; she could feel the pressure change against her old soldier's instincts, the world gathering itself for weather.

"Tell them a knight looked at what she'd defended," Serah said, "and chose to go learn how to defend something better."

She walked out through the gates without ceremony, past the guards who saluted her and the citizens who didn't recognize her at all, and took the south road as the sun cleared the rooftops. Behind her the capital gleamed, wounded and mending. Ahead the road ran long and empty toward the coast, toward whatever ordinary, difficult, unblessed work waited there.

Her sister had loved the sea. She hadn't thought about that in years. She found she was thinking about it now, walking, and that the thinking hurt less than it used to—which was, she supposed, the most anyone could hope for after a storm: not to be unbroken, but to be able to carry the breaking without it carrying you.

Far behind her, over the palace, the first true spring clouds gathered, and somewhere in the valley beyond the mountains, she imagined, a former storm-caller was watching them come, and smiling.
