## 20. Ordinary Hands

The silence came first in the mornings.

For twenty years Rafael had woken to the storm the way other people woke to birdsong—a low pressure behind the ribs, a taste of iron on the tongue, the distant mutter of a sky that answered when called. Even dampened, even buried beneath two decades of careful discipline, it had been there. A second heartbeat. A companion they had hated and hidden and, if they were honest with themselves in the small hours, loved.

Now there was nothing.

Rafael would open their eyes in the borrowed room above the root-singers' hall—Ovid's people had insisted, *you will not convalesce in a cell, storm-caller*—and lie still, listening inward for that familiar rumble the way one listens for surf through fog. And there was only quiet. The particular, terrible quiet of a house after the family has gone.

They had thought they understood what surrender meant. They had stood in the Verdancy's heart-root with Aldric's hand clasped in theirs, both of them weeping into the green dark, and they had said the words Ovid taught them, and they had felt the gift pour out of them like water from a broken jar—not torn away, not stolen, but *given*, which was supposed to make all the difference. They had watched the storm settle into the great wounded spirit below the throne room, anchoring it, feeding it gentleness instead of hunger, and they had thought: *this is the price, and I have paid it, and now everyone lives.*

No one had told them about the mornings.

* * *

On the ninth day after the unbinding, Rafael went down to the kitchen of the hall because their hands needed something to do. That was the other thing no one warned you about: the restlessness. Twenty years of holding a storm in check had built muscles of attention they didn't know how to unclench. Without the constant work of dampening, of shaping, of *holding back*, their body kept reaching for a task that no longer existed, fingers curling around air.

The kitchen was warm and smelled of barley and rosemary. Maren was there.

She had come to the capital with the first wagons of aid from the Hollow—the valley had opened its roads at last, and its herbalists had come south in numbers, because half the city's rooted were waking into bodies that had been sat in too long, minds that had been smiled smooth, and someone had to teach them how to be frightened again without being broken by it. Maren had not forgiven Rafael yet. She had said so plainly, standing on the palace steps amid the ash and the trampled flowerbeds: *I'm glad you did it. I'm not glad you took twenty years deciding.* But she had come anyway, and she worked beside them in silence most days, and Rafael had learned that this, too, was a kind of forgiveness—the kind that showed up with clean bandages.

"Sit," Maren said, not looking up from the mortar she was grinding. "You're hovering again."

"I'm helping."

"You're hovering with good intentions. Sit."

Rafael sat. There was a basket of dried yarrow on the table, stems to be stripped, and they set to it because their hands were begging for occupation. Strip, sort, pile. Strip, sort, pile. Outside the window, the city went on with its strange, tender business of recovery—scaffolding on the burned wing of the barracks, children chasing each other through a garden that no longer bloomed out of season but bloomed honestly, late and patchy and real.

The silence where the storm lived ached. It ached the way a missing tooth aches, the tongue returning again and again to the gap.

"Do you know what I miss?" Rafael said, before they could stop themselves. "It isn't the power. It's not even the—" They gestured vaguely at their own chest. "The feeling of it. It's the *listening*. I listened to weather my whole life. Every morning I'd wake up and I'd know things. Where the rain was gathering over the western hills. How the wind felt coming down off the mountains, whether it was carrying cold or just moving air around. It was like having a hundred far-off friends who murmured to each other all night long." Their voice caught. They stripped another yarrow stem with more force than necessary. "And now it's just me in here. Just my own small weather."

Maren's mortar stopped grinding.

"I spent twenty years hating it," Rafael went on, quieter. "Hating the gift. It got us exiled. It got my parents' names struck from every record in three duchies. I hid it so well I forgot there was anything underneath it to hide. And then I gave it away, and I thought—I don't know what I thought. That I'd feel lighter. Free." They laughed, and it came out wrong. "I feel amputated, Maren. I keep reaching for something that isn't there, and every time I reach, I remember exactly what I lost, and I remember that I *chose* it, and I can't even be angry properly because it was the right thing to do."

Maren set down the mortar. She looked at them for a long moment with those flat, assessing eyes that had catalogued ten thousand patients and suffered no fools among them.

"Yes," she said. "That's grief."

"It's not—"

"It is. You've lost something that was part of you, and you chose the loss, and choice doesn't make grief smaller. It only makes it lonelier, because everyone expects you to be at peace." She pushed the basket toward them. "Strip your yarrow. Grief likes busy hands. And when Wren comes—and Wren will come, that child has been asking after you since dawn—you will tell her the truth, whatever she asks, because that child has been lied to by everyone who ever claimed to love her, and she'll know if you do it too."

* * *

Wren arrived in the early afternoon, as predicted, moving slowly but moving under her own power, which two weeks ago had seemed an impossible luxury. The Verdancy had been deep in her—rooted along her spine, threaded through the place where her gift lived, drinking her dry year by year in the name of keeping her safe. When the entity released its grip, it had taken most of what it had stolen with it, and Wren had woken in the infirmary hollow-eyed and furious and nine years old, mourning a magic she could barely describe because she'd never been allowed to use it.

Her gift had been small, she said. A warmth-gift. She could make tea stay hot. She could keep a sleeping brother from shivering. She could, once, on a dare, make a whole room feel like summer.

She talked about it the way Rafael talked about the listening. Small things. Far-off friends.

Today she came into the kitchen with a blanket around her shoulders despite the mild day, climbed onto the bench across from Rafael, and stared at them with the direct, unnerving frankness of children and cats.

"The garden's growing," she said.

"What garden?"

"The one you planted. Under the palace. Ovid showed me. It's coming up through the flagstones where the heart-root used to be, and it's *green*, proper green, not Verdancy-green, and the rooted people go sit in it when they feel bad." She tucked her chin onto her knees. "Serah says it's yours. What you gave. That the storm you gave up turned into a garden."

"Not exactly," Rafael said. "The storm anchored the spirit. The spirit remembered how to be a garden. I just... held the door open."

Wren considered this. Then, with the merciless precision of the very young: "Do you miss it?"

There it was. Rafael's hands went still over the yarrow.

"Yes," they said.

"Was it worth it?"

"Wren—"

"You're doing the thing grown-ups do." Her voice cracked, and beneath the crack Rafael heard something older and rawer: a child who had spent years being managed, soothed, smiled at by a parasite wearing adult faces. "Everyone does it. They say 'it doesn't matter' or 'you wouldn't understand' or 'ask me when you're older.' Serah does it. Ovid does it less, but he does it. I was eaten alive by a thing in the king's basement and nobody told me what was happening until it was happening, and now I want one true answer from somebody, and you—" her chin trembled, and she locked it down with visible effort, "—you're the only person who ever gave the true thing up *on purpose*, so I figured maybe you'd know."

The kitchen was very quiet. Maren had slipped out at some point; Rafael hadn't heard the door.

Rafael set down the yarrow. They moved around the table and sat on the bench beside Wren, close enough that the blanket brushed their arm.

"Yes," they said. "I miss it. Every morning. I wake up and listen for it and it's not there, and some days that feels like drowning in slow motion. Some days I'd give almost anything to feel rain coming from fifty miles away again." Wren's eyes were huge and wet and unblinking. "And yes. It was worth it. Both things are true at the same time, and nobody tells you that either—that you can grieve something and still know you'd do it again."

"Why?"

"Because someone had to."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the beginning of one." Rafael looked at their own hands—ordinary hands, calloused from roof-mending and herb-stripping, hands that had once held lightning and now held dried yarrow. "When I was in the heart-root, I saw what the Verdancy really was. Not a monster. A wounded thing. A healing spirit that got starved for centuries and started eating people because eating was the only way it knew to survive. And I saw that it couldn't stop on its own, and it couldn't be killed without killing everyone it had rooted in—including you. So the choices were: let it keep going, destroy it and destroy you and half this city with it, or find it a new anchor. Someone willing. Someone whose gift ran deep enough to hold it."

"And that was you."

"That was me. And here's the part I need you to hear, Wren, because I think you already suspect it." They turned to face her fully. "I didn't do it because I was noble. I did it partly because I was tired—tired of hiding, tired of the gift that cost my family everything. Partly because I was angry at it, if you can believe that. Angry at the storm for getting us exiled. Giving it up felt like winning an argument I'd been losing for twenty years." A breath. "But underneath all of that, underneath the tiredness and the anger and yes, the nobility too, there was one simple truth: I was the only one who could, and you were the one who'd die if I didn't. When the arithmetic is written in a child's life, you don't get to keep your thunder."

Wren was silent for a while. Her fingers picked at the blanket's fringe.

"But you still miss it," she said finally.

"Every day. Probably forever. That's what giving something up *means*, Wren—it means the missing doesn't stop just because the choice was right." Rafael smiled, small and crooked. "But here's the other thing. Here's what I'm learning, badly, slowly, some days worse than others. I spent my whole life thinking the storm was me. First I thought it was the worst thing about me, then I thought it was the best thing about me, and both times I was wrong. The storm was something I *had*. It wasn't something I *was*. I was the one who mended Mrs. Calloway's roof. I was the one who sat with dying refugees at the border stones so they wouldn't be alone. I was the one who held Aldric's hand in the dark and told him he was still a man. None of that needed lightning. All of it needed me."

"And now you're just... ordinary."

"Now I'm just myself," Rafael corrected gently. "Which turns out to be harder and better than being extraordinary. Ask me again in ten years and I might even believe it."

Wren leaned sideways until her head found Rafael's shoulder. She was light, too light still, a bird-boned child rebuilt from ruin. "Does it hurt? The missing?"

"Like a toothache of the soul."

"My warmth's gone," Wren whispered. "I put my hand on the teapot this morning and nothing happened and I cried into it. Maren pretended not to see." A wet laugh. "Tea's still cold."

"Cold tea is a scandal," Rafael agreed solemnly. "We should form a society. Victims of our own sacrifices, meeting weekly to complain."

Wren giggled—actually giggled, a rusty sound like a gate opening after years shut—and then sobered, and asked the question Rafael had been waiting for, the one underneath all the others:

"Will it ever come back? The missing going away, I mean. Or the gift. Either one."

Rafael thought about the garden growing up through the palace flagstones. Thought about the spirit down there in the dark, learning gentleness again, fed now not by stolen magic but by a surrendered storm that had become, against all expectation, a source of quiet nourishment rather than appetite. Thought about the border stones of the Hollow, and the cutting of restored garden-magic wrapped in damp cloth in their pack, waiting to be carried home.

"No," they said honestly. "The gift won't come back. Anchors don't get refunds." They squeezed Wren's shoulder. "But the missing? The missing changes. One day you'll reach for the warmth and it won't ache—it'll just be a memory, and memories can be carried without bleeding. And one day after that, you'll discover you've grown other things in the space it left. Patience, maybe. Or a way with words. Or the ability to sit with someone else's grief because you know its shape." They tapped Wren's chest lightly, over her heart. "Gardens grow in ruins, little bird. I have it on excellent authority."

* * *

They stayed in the kitchen until the light went amber, stripping yarrow side by side, Wren chattering about the garden and the rooted lady from the bakery who made bread for the whole street now that she was free to choose it, and Rafael answering in the easy rhythm of two people who had survived the same war from different trenches.

Near dusk, footsteps on the stairs—Vane's tread, unmistakable, a soldier's walk even in civilian boots. She appeared in the doorway with her travel cloak already fastened and a look on her face that Rafael had learned to read as *trouble, politely delivered.*

"Storm-caller." She nodded at Wren. "Little bird. Sorry to interrupt the domestic bliss." She held up a folded paper, sealed with plain wax. "Courier came from the valley this afternoon. Your friend Maren's colleagues—the ones who stayed behind. The border stones are doing something strange. Glowing, they say. Humming. And the refugees camped outside them are reporting that the mist that's kept the valley hidden for two centuries is..." She hesitated, choosing the word with care. "*Thinning.*"

Rafael's hands went still.

"There's more," Vane said. "The message asks for you by name. Says whoever carries the garden's blood should come home." A pause, and something almost gentle crossed the knight's scarred face. "Says the valley wants to learn how to stop forgetting."

Outside the window, the last light caught the young garden growing through the palace stones, green and patient and alive. Rafael looked down at their own ordinary hands—empty of thunder, full of yarrow dust—and felt, for the first time in nine days, not the ache of the gap but the pull of the road.

"Pack your things, Wren," they said quietly. "We're going home."
