## 21. Planting Season

The road home was longer than the road away.

Rafael had known it would be. Roads always stretched when you carried something fragile in your hands, and Rafael carried a cutting—a green shoot no longer than a forearm, its roots wrapped in damp linen and river-clay, cradled against their chest like a sleeping child. It had been given to them by Ovid on the palace steps, three days after the world changed, pressed into their palms with both of the root-singer's scarred hands.

"It remembers how to grow," Ovid had said. "It only needs somewhere that wants it."

Rafael had thought immediately—had not been able to stop thinking—of the Hollow.

Now the valley's rim rose ahead of them, blue-green in the late afternoon light, and Rafael stood at the crest of the ridge with the cutting against their heart and felt something in their chest loosen for the first time in twenty years. Below, the Hollow lay exactly as they had left it: the crooked line of border stones threading down the slope like an old scar, the fields beyond them golding into autumn, the smoke of evening fires rising straight and unhurried from chimneys that had never once had to fear what came over the hills.

Home. The word had always tasted strange to Rafael, half sweetness and half ash. Today it simply tasted true.

They began to walk down toward the stones.

* * *

The valley had not been idle while Rafael was gone.

That was the first thing they understood as they descended—the Hollow was *fuller* than they had ever known it. Tents clustered along the near fields where once there had been only pasture. New frames of houses rose at the edges of Bramblewick, their timbers pale and unweathered. Children Rafael didn't recognize chased each other through the stubble, shrieking, and one of them—a small girl with green still faintly visible in the webbing of her fingers, fading now, day by day, since the Unbinding—stopped mid-game to stare at the stranger coming down the road with a bundle in her arms.

Rafael knew what they must look like. Thin. Weathered. Their hair gone iron at the temples where the storm had lived and then departed, leaving gray in its wake like frost after a hard night. They wore a traveler's coat and carried nothing but the cutting and a satchel, and they walked with the careful gait of someone who had learned, long ago, to take up as little of the world as possible.

But the girl didn't run. She shaded her eyes, squinted, and then her whole face opened like a window thrown wide.

"You're the storm-caller," she called out, loud enough to turn every head in the field. "You're the one who freed the king!"

So much, Rafael thought wryly, for arriving quietly.

By the time they reached the first cluster of tents, a small crowd had gathered—refugees mostly, the ones who had come to the valley seeking sanctuary and found instead a home that had finally decided to stop hiding. An old man with rooted-veins gone silver at his wrists clasped Rafael's hand in both of his and wouldn't let go. A woman pressed a heel of fresh bread into their satchel without asking. Someone had already sent a boy running toward the village, and Rafael could see figures beginning to gather at Bramblewick's edge, pointing.

"I'm not—" Rafael started, and stopped, because the old man was looking at them with such naked hope that the words died.

"You're not what?" he asked gently. "Not a hero? No. Heroes are stories. You're a person who did a hard thing when it needed doing. That's rarer."

Rafael looked down at their own hands—ordinary hands now, calloused and human, empty of thunder—and found, to their surprise, that they could accept this. "I came home," they said simply. "That's all I came to do."

"Then welcome," said the old man, "home."

* * *

Maren was waiting at the border stones.

Of course she was. Rafael saw her from fifty yards off, standing beside the great moss-eaten monolith that marked the valley's edge, arms crossed, herbalist's apron still dusted with dried yarrow, her expression arranged into something that was trying very hard to be stern and not entirely succeeding.

Rafael stopped a few paces away. The space between them held twenty years of small kindnesses and one large wound—the day Rafael had refused to flee with her, the day Maren had called them a coward who hid behind other people's courage, the day she had walked back into the valley alone while Rafael stayed behind the stones and told themselves it was wisdom.

"You look terrible," Maren said.

"You should see the other side of the war."

"I did. I saw it in my well water." Her voice cracked on the words, just slightly. She looked down at the ground between them, at the border stone, at anything but Rafael's face. "I owe you an apology."

"No, you don't."

"Don't tell me what I owe, Rafael Sterling. I've spent two decades keeping accounts in this valley; I'll know when one is settled." She lifted her chin. "I called you a coward. I said you'd let us all rot behind these stones rather than risk your own skin. And then you walked out of here, gave up the only thing you had left of yourself, and pulled a poisoned crown off a drowning king so that children like Wren could grow up whole." Her eyes were bright and furious and wet. "I was wrong about you. I have been wrong about you for a very long time, and I am sorry."

Rafael set down the satchel. Slowly, deliberately, they crossed the remaining distance and took Maren's hands—the same way Ovid had once taken theirs, palm to palm, gift to gift, though neither of them had any gift left between them now except the ordinary kind.

"You were right to be angry," Rafael said. "I *was* hiding. For twenty years I told myself the valley needed me quiet, but the truth is I needed me quiet. I needed to be safe more than I needed to be brave. If you hadn't shamed me, I might have stayed behind these stones until the Verdancy seeped through the groundwater and we all smiled ourselves to death." They squeezed her hands. "Your anger was the first crack in the wall, Maren. Don't apologize for building it. Just help me tear the rest down."

Maren laughed—a wet, broken sound—and pulled them into a hug that smelled of yarrow and woodsmoke and home.

When she stepped back, her gaze fell on the bundle in Rafael's arms, and her breath caught. "Is that—"

"A cutting. From the garden at the palace's heart. The garden as it was meant to be, before the first king bent it into a chain." Rafael unwound the top layer of linen, letting her see the shoot: slender, vivid, its leaves catching the low sun like stained glass. "Ovid says it will grow anywhere it's welcomed. He says—" Rafael hesitated, because saying it aloud made it real, made it a promise with teeth. "He says the valley doesn't have to forget anymore."

* * *

They planted it together, beyond the stones.

Not inside the Hollow—that had been Rafael's first instinct, to bring the healing magic home to the place that had sheltered them—but beyond, on the open hillside where the border stones ran, in the exact spot where the valley's magic thinned and died. Because that was the point. That had always been the point.

"The stones were a wound," Rafael explained, kneeling in the turned earth while Maren worked the soil loose beside them with a herbalist's sure hands. "My grandmother wrote about it, in the end. The Hollow wasn't made to protect magic. It was made to *amputate* it—to cut the valley off from the land's living current so the Sterlings' inheritance couldn't be traced, couldn't be reclaimed. We didn't live in a sanctuary. We lived in a scar." They pressed the cutting's roots gently into the dark earth. "Scars can heal. But only if you stop pretending they're not there."

The soil went back around the roots handful by handful. Maren fetched water from her own well—the same well that had once run green with corruption, clean now for weeks, sweet again—and poured it slow and careful around the young stem. A little crowd had gathered, as crowds do, drawn by curiosity and the particular gravity of watching something begin. Wren was there, taller than Rafael remembered, her laugh restored, her gift flickering quietly in her fingertips like a candle relit. The old man with the silvered veins. Dozens of others, refugees and valley-folk alike, standing shoulder to shoulder with no line drawn between them.

And the cutting grew.

Not fast—not miraculously, nothing so vulgar as spectacle—but steadily, visibly, the way a spring does when the frost finally breaks. A new leaf unfurled while they watched. Then another. Its stem thickened, darkened, put out a second branch, a third, and where its shadow fell across the hillside grass, the grass itself seemed to stand a little straighter, a little greener, as if remembering something it had been told to forget.

And through the border stones—through the old dead line where the valley's magic had been severed for generations—Rafael felt something move.

Not power. Not their storm; that was gone, gone forever, and they had made their peace with the silence where it used to sing. This was subtler. A warmth. A current, faint as the first thaw-wind of March, sliding past the stones and into the Hollow for the first time in twenty years, touching the fields, the roofs, the wells, the people. Rafael watched Maren feel it too—saw her hand go to her chest, saw her eyes widen, saw tears spill freely down a face that had long ago decided crying was a luxury.

"It's coming through," she whispered. "Rafael—it's coming *through*."

"That's the idea." Rafael sat back on their heels, dirt to the elbows, utterly content. "The valley kept us safe by forgetting. Now it gets to keep us safe by remembering. There's a difference, and it matters."

Wren crouched beside the sapling, close enough that her breath stirred its leaves, and Rafael saw the girl's gift answer it—a faint green shimmer rising to meet the garden-magic, recognizing kin. "Will it keep growing?"

"As long as anyone tends it." Rafael looked around at the gathered faces—at the refugees who would become neighbors, at the valley-born who would learn to share what they had guarded so jealously, at the border stones that were, even now, becoming merely stones. "That's the thing about gardens. They're not a possession. They're a promise. Somebody has to keep showing up."

"Then we'll show up," Wren said, with the absolute certainty available only to children and the recently saved.

* * *

Spring came early that year, or perhaps it simply came honestly for the first time in living memory.

By the time the roads were dry enough for carts, the Hollow had changed its mind about itself. The council that formed in Bramblewick's meeting hall—valley-born and refugee seated side by side, Maren's voice among the loudest—voted to pull down the warning signs along the southern approach. *TURN BACK. THE VALLEY FORGETS STRANGERS.* Rafael helped carry the posts to the carpenter's yard to be split into fence rails. It felt right, somehow, that the signs of exclusion should end up holding up somebody's garden boundary.

New houses went up along the eastern slope, built by rooted and unrooted hands together, and if the timber sometimes grew a little straighter where Wren helped with the joinery, nobody minded. The school reopened with triple its old enrollment. Travelers came over the hills now instead of fleeing from them—peddlers, pilgrims, the occasional nervous official from the capital bearing letters under Aldric's diminished seal, come to see for themselves whether the rumors were true: that there was a valley where the gifted and the plain-lived had stopped being afraid of each other.

Rafael taught them, when they asked. Not magic—they had none to teach, and had discovered they didn't miss teaching it—but the history. The true history, thorns included: the theft, the exile, the hunt, the hunger of a wounded spirit starved into a monster by men who wanted its power and not its purpose. The children listened with the fierce attention of the young hearing a story that has their own names woven into it.

"And then what happened?" a small boy asked one afternoon, when Rafael reached the part about the cutting on the hillside.

"Then people showed up," Rafael said. "Day after day. Year after year. That's the secret ending of every good story, if you want to know. Nobody wins anything once. They just keep arriving."

Outside the schoolhouse window, the garden-tree on the hillside had grown tall enough to see from the village—a young oak now, impossibly, its canopy wide and generous, visible from every field in the Hollow. A landmark. A promise kept. On windy days its leaves made a sound like distant rain, and the children swore you could hear singing in it if you stood beneath it long enough.

Rafael could hear it too. It sounded, they thought, like their grandmother's voice, and like a spirit finally fed, and like a valley learning its own name again.

They walked home in the golden light, past the border stones that guarded nothing now except memory, and paused—as they always did—at the place where the garden's roots had crossed the old dead line. Grass grew thick there, unnaturally green, threaded with tiny white flowers that bloomed in every season. Rafael crouched and pressed one palm flat against the earth.

Warmth rose to meet them. Patient. Alive. Waiting, the way good soil waits—not for power, not for glory, but for seed.

And far to the east, past the hills, past the rebuilt capital, a rumor was already moving along the roads with the spring carts: word of other valleys, other scars, other places where the old severances still ached in the land. Word that a certain former storm-caller had experience with borders, and forgiveness, and things that grew where they shouldn't.

Rafael stood, brushed the dirt from their knees, and smiled at the horizon.

There was planting yet to do.
