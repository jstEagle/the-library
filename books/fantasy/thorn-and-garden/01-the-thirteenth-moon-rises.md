## 1. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The snow came down the way it always did in Thornfell — sideways, mean, and full of opinion about your business. Oscar Kavanagh pulled her collar up against it and told herself, for the hundredth time that winter, that she could have been anywhere else. Anywhere with a hearth and a roof that didn't whistle. But the roads didn't patrol themselves, and the Kavanaghs had walked them for four generations without once asking why.

She carried a lantern low, so its light wouldn't spill past the bend where the smuggler track cut through the pines. Old habit. The men who used these roads paid her family in salt, lamp oil, and silence, and silence meant never showing more light than you needed to. Her grandmother's voice lived in her ear on nights like this: *A keeper walks dark, Oscar-girl. Light is a thing you lend, not a thing you own.*

The thirteenth moon of the year was rising over the Thornfell peaks, thin as a paring of bone. There was no thirteenth moon, not properly speaking — the almanacs ended at twelve, and the thirteenth was only what happened when the calendar ran out of room and winter kept going anyway. But her grandmother had counted it, every year, and marked it on the gatehouse lintel with a smear of chalk. "The year's hidden month," she'd say. "The door knows things the calendar doesn't." Oscar had chalked the lintel herself at dusk, feeling foolish, feeling watched.

The track descended toward the valley in long switchbacks, and at each turn the wind found some new seam in her coat to slide into. By the time she reached the lower road her fingers had gone wooden inside her gloves, and she was thinking mostly of the kettle waiting at home when she saw it: a smudge on the white of the gatehouse steps, too small to be a man, too upright to be a deer.

Her heart did something complicated.

Nobody came to the gatehouse. That was the whole of it, the whole truth of her life distilled into one clean rule — nobody came to the gatehouse. Smugglers passed below on the valley road; they didn't climb. Soldiers kept to the fort at Greywater, two days east. Pilgrims had stopped coming before her grandmother's time, if they'd ever come at all. The gatehouse stood at the end of a road everyone else had forgotten, a grey stone fist closed around a greyer stone door, and for thirty years Oscar had been the only living thing that touched it.

She set the lantern down behind a boulder and approached with the knife loose in her hand, not drawn but ready, the way her grandmother had taught her — *a blade shown is a debt promised.* The smudge resolved into a child. Small, maybe seven years old, sitting on the bottom step with their back against the door itself, as if they'd knocked and then simply run out of the strength to wait for an answer.

Frost had gotten into the child's eyelashes. Their lips were the color of slate, and their hands were bare, which was the detail that undid Oscar's caution — bare hands, blue-knuckled, pressed flat against the stone of the threshold like the child had been trying to warm themselves on the door and failed even at that.

"Hey," Oscar said. "Hey. Can you hear me?"

The child's eyes opened. They were dark and enormous and wrong in a way Oscar couldn't name — not the wrongness of sickness, but the wrongness of something seen through water. The child looked at her, looked at the lantern-light leaking around the boulder, looked back at her, and spoke.

The words were nothing. That was Oscar's first thought: the child is fevered, the words are nothing, sounds shaped like speech but emptied of meaning. Except — and here her body reacted before her mind could stop it, a cold sliding sensation down the back of her skull, like a finger tracing an old scar — except they weren't nothing. They were almost something. The cadence rose and fell in a pattern her blood recognized before her mind caught up, a lilt like a rocking, a lilt like —

Like her grandmother's voice, singing in the kitchen, in the language she'd called *nothing, just nonsense, old women's noise*, the syllables she'd hummed over bread dough and dying fires:

*Orra veth, orra veth, sul marren i daleth.*

The child said something very close to it. Close enough that Oscar's mouth went dry.

"That's —" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again, slower, kneeling in the snow so they were eye to eye. "Where did you learn that?"

The child answered at length. It was clearly an answer — there was pleading in it, and something formal, rehearsed, like a lesson drilled by someone desperate for the pupil to remember. Then the child shuddered, all at once, the great involuntary shudder of a small body losing its war with the cold, and Oscar made the decision she would spend the rest of her life either explaining or defending.

Duty said turn the child away. Duty was a word she'd grown up eating with every meal: keep the door shut, keep the roads walked, keep yourself small and unremarkable so no one ever asks what it is you keep. Thirty years of duty fit her like her own skin. And duty said, plainly, that whatever this child was, whoever sent them, whatever tongue they spoke — the answer was a blanket, a night's shelter in the woodshed, and directions downhill at first light, given firmly, given without warmth.

But her blood said open the door.

Not the gate — she wasn't mad, she'd never touch the gate — but the gatehouse door, the ordinary oak-and-iron door of the house she'd lived in her whole life, the one that stood three feet from the sealed stone gate it guarded. Her blood said open it, and her hands were already moving, hauling the child up out of the snow, and the strangest part, the part she would lie awake turning over later, was that the child came willingly. Came as if the door were expected. As if the child had been told, by whoever drilled those strange syllables into them, exactly whose house this was.

Inside, the gatehouse was one room and a loft, smelling of peat smoke and lamp oil and the dried herbs Oscar hung from the rafters every autumn. She got the fire up from its banked coals, stripped the child's soaked outer clothes — threadbare, fine-made once, embroidered along the hems with patterns she'd only ever seen in the margins of her grandmother's oldest journal — and wrapped them in her own spare blanket by the fire. The child let all of it happen with the eerie patience of the half-frozen, watching her with those dark water-seeing eyes.

Then, warming, color creeping back into their face like dawn coming up over the valley, the child pointed at the far wall.

At the lintel above the inner doorway — the one her grandmother had chalked every thirteenth moon, the one Oscar had smeared fresh that very dusk. The child said a short phrase, quiet and certain, and though Oscar couldn't parse a single word of it, she understood it completely, the way you understand a look or a held breath. It was a greeting. It was *this house, this house, I know this house.*

"You can't know this house," Oscar said. Her voice came out rough. "Nobody knows this house."

The child reached into the breast of their frozen shirt and drew out something on a cord: a key. Small, iron, older than any iron had a right to look, black with handling and polished bright along one edge as if it had been carried against a heartbeat for a very long time. The child held it out to her across the blanket-wrapped space between them, and the firelight moved along the polished edge, and Oscar felt the shape of it in her chest before her eyes finished confirming it — because she owned its twin. It hung on a nail in her bedroom, under the floorboard, wrapped in oilcloth, and her grandmother had put it there and said only: *When they come asking, and they will come asking, you'll know.*

"I don't understand," Oscar whispered, and hated how young her voice sounded. "Who are you? Who sent you?"

The child answered in that impossible, almost-familiar tongue, and this time they sang it — two lines only, wobbling, off-key, a child's voice frayed by cold and fear — and Oscar sat down hard on the hearthstone because she knew the song. Knew the tune the way you know your own pulse. It was the lullaby. Her grandmother's nonsense lullaby, note for note, except that here, in this child's mouth, it wasn't nonsense at all. Here it had words, real ones, and the last of them, repeated soft as an apology, was a name:

*Vael.*

Outside, the thirteenth moon cleared the shoulder of the mountain, and its light came through the frost-furred window and lay across the floorboards in a long pale stripe — pointing, Oscar realized with her heart slamming slow and heavy as a bell, straight at the sealed stone gate. And somewhere down in the valley, faint but unmistakable, dogs began to bark.

* * *

Later — after broth, after sleep took the child mid-sentence, mid-question, their small hand still curled around the iron key on its cord — Oscar knelt at the window with the lantern shuttered to a coin of light and watched the valley road.

Three riders. Torchlight swinging. Moving fast for the weather, moving with purpose, and even at this distance she could see they weren't smugglers. Smugglers rode like the dark owed them money. These rode like the dark worked for them.

They'd reach the foot of the switchbacks by midnight. They'd find her tracks in the snow, and the small ones over hers, and they'd follow both up to her door.

Oscar looked back at the sleeping child, at the frost still melting in the wool blanket, at the key rising and falling on its cord with each small breath. Her grandmother's voice came to her one more time, unhurried, unbearably clear, as if she'd been saving it for exactly this night:

*A keeper walks dark, Oscar-girl. But sometimes the dark sends someone to your door, and then, my love, you find out what the keeping was for.*

Oscar banked the fire, took down her knife and her heaviest coat, and went to stand in her own doorway, facing down the mountain, waiting to see what the thirteenth moon would bring her.
