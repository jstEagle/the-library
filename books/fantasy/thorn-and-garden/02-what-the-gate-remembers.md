## 2. What the Gate Remembers

The child slept for two days in the gatehouse's back room, beneath the quilt my grandmother had sewn from coat scraps and stubbornness. I sat up with her the way my mother once sat up with fevers, feeding her broth spoonful by spoonful, and every time she surfaced toward waking she said words in that old tongue—soft, rounded words that fell against my ear like river stones worn smooth.

I knew them. That was the wrongness of it. I knew them the way you know a song you've never heard sung, only hummed.

*Alenna veth,* she murmured on the second night, eyes half-open, unfocused. *Alenna veth, mara.*

Come home, little bird. Come home.

My grandmother used to sing it while she stirred the pot, while she mended netting, while she walked the wall at dusk with her lantern swinging. I'd always assumed it was some mountain nonsense, a lullaby made of nothing. Now a half-frozen child lay in my bed speaking it as if it were the only language left in her mouth, and my hands shook so badly I spilled the broth across the blanket.

* * *

The journals lived in an iron chest under the floorboards of the front room, wrapped in oilcloth and wax-sealed, one for each keeper going back further than the Kavanagh name went back to anything else. We were told they existed. We were never told to read them. *When it's time,* my grandmother would say, tapping the floor twice with her heel, *you'll know.* I had spent twenty-six years not knowing.

I knew now.

I built the fire high and laid out nine journals on the table, the oldest swollen with damp, its leather gone soft as bark. The early ones were written in cipher—a shifting cipher, each page keyed by a phrase, and it took me until well past midnight to understand the key wasn't a word but a song. The lullabies. Each journal opened to whichever verse matched the moon-month of its writing. My family hadn't just sung to their children. They'd been passing down their own keys all along, hidden in cradlesongs, and no one outside the gatehouse line could ever have found them.

That was when I understood we had never been simple wardens of a door. We had been keepers of a memory someone very powerful wanted dead.

The oldest journal began: *To whoever keeps this watch after me. The gate does not lead into Thornfell. Thornfell leads through the gate.*

Vael. The word appeared first as a smudge, then everywhere—*the country of Vael, its fields, its orchards, its hundred bells.* A country erased. Not conquered, not ruined: unmade. There had been a treaty—the Journal of my great-great-great-grandmother Aldith called it the Treaty of Quiet—and the treaty had not taken land or lives. It had taken *remembering*. Every map redrawn, every record burned or rewritten, every tongue ordered forgotten, every road renamed so thoroughly that even the names of the roads forgot what they had led to. Vael did not fall. Vael was *edited*, and my family's gate was the last unedited sentence in the whole world.

And we were not jailers. Page after page said so in different hands across two centuries. *We do not lock Vael away. We keep the door so Vael can be found again.* The sealing had been done at the request of Vael's own last queen, whose name the journals wrote as *Seren of the Ninth Bell*, because a country that cannot be remembered cannot be hunted. The forgetting was armor. The gate was the scabbard. And the Kavanaghs held the hilt.

*We are wardens,* the journals insisted, over and over, in handwriting growing more urgent as the generations passed, *we are wardens, we are not jailers, do not let them make you a jailer.*

* * *

*The tall woman feeds me broth and her face does the thing faces do when they are frightened but trying to be kind. Her tongue is broken. She has maybe forty words of ours, worn like coins rubbed smooth—enough to buy bread, not enough to buy understanding.*

*I speak anyway, because speaking is how I stay Wren and not just a cold child in a strange house. I tell her my name is Wren of the Ninth Bell, which is a true thing even though there is no ninth bell anymore. There is no bell at all anymore. There is no anything anymore, except me, walking out of the white pass where the snow doesn't remember either.*

*They told me the gatehouse would have a keeper. They told me a keeper would open the door. But this woman looks at the door like it's a wound, and I am starting to be afraid that everything they promised me about the way home was also something that got forgotten.*

* * *

By the fourth day Wren could sit at the table wrapped in the quilt, watching me work through the journals, and I discovered something that undid me a little: she could help. Her language was the key to the cipher's later sections—the journals from the last hundred years had grown lazy, mixing cipher with plain script and phrases of living Vaelish, and where my grandmother's lullabies gave me the frame, Wren gave me the meaning. She would point at a word, frown, say it aloud in her round river-stone voice, and something in my blood would turn over and recognize it.

*Thornfell* meant, in Vaelish, *the hedge of thorns*. Our mountains weren't named after themselves. They were named after the gate.

On the fifth evening I found the last entry in my mother's hand, dated three days before the fever took her. It was short.

*The heir will come before the thirteenth moon turns. Keep her alive. Keep yourself kind. The crown will come asking, and when they ask you what the gate is, tell them the truth: it is a promise. Promises are heavier than doors. — M.*

I read it four times. Then I heard the hooves.

* * *

Three riders came up the smuggler road at dusk, wearing no colors, which in Thornfell meant crown colors. The one in front was a broad woman with iron-grey hair cropped close and a voice like a slammed drawer. She didn't dismount.

"You'll be Oscar Kavanagh."

"I will."

"Captain Ilse Marchand. Crown survey." She let that word, *survey*, hang in the cold air like a blade waiting to be dropped. "There's reports of a child come through the pass. Unregistered. Speaking an unknown language." Her eyes went past my shoulder, cataloguing the gatehouse, the gate behind it, the frost-silvered thorn hedge that ran along the wall like a scar. "You'll have seen nothing. Remote posts see nothing. That's what remote posts are for."

"She's here," I said, "and she's sick, and she's mine to look after until she isn't."

Something flickered across Marchand's face—not surprise exactly. Recalculation. "The crown has an interest in that gate, Keeper. An old interest. You may have heard your family held certain... obligations."

"My family kept a garden," I said, because my mother's journal had taught me that much: never show them the hilt. "A walled one. Nothing grows in it. It needs tending, is all."

"A garden," Marchand repeated, flat. Then, softer, in the tone of a person laying a card face-down rather than face-up: "Orders change, Keeper. In spring there'll be engineers on this road. What stands behind your wall won't stand past midsummer. I'd think about which side of that wall you want to be standing on." She turned her horse. At the edge of the dark she paused. "The thirteenth moon turns in nineteen days."

They rode down into the valley, and their hoofbeats took a long time fading, as if the mountain itself were listening after them.

Wren stood in the doorway behind me, small inside the big quilt, watching the dark where the riders had gone. When she spoke, her voice was steady in a way no child's should have to be.

"Marath vell," she said. Nineteen days. She had counted too.

Then she looked up at me, and said the other thing, the thing I had already read in six generations of journals and had been trying all week not to hear:

"Open the door. Take me home."

Outside, over the thorn hedge, the moon rose—one night older, eighteen nights remaining—and it looked, for the first time in my life, less like a moon than like a bell being slowly rung.
