## 3. A Country with No Door Out

The deep roads smelled of cold iron and old smoke, the way they always did this far under Thornfell. Oscar walked with one hand on Wren's shoulder and the other on her grandmother's journal, bound shut with a smuggler's wax-seal, and she counted the lanterns ahead of her like a rosary. Seven. Then five. The tunnels narrowed, and the dark came in close as wool.

"You're certain about Doss," she said.

Tam Doss had sold her lamp oil and salt pork for eleven years, haggling so hard over every copper that she'd once watched him weep over a broken scale. He was not the sort of man who sheltered fugitives.

"He's certain about *me*," Wren said. Her Common had grown fluent in six weeks, quick as frost spreading across a window, but when she was tired the old language crept back into it, vowels softening like river stones. "He knew my grandmother's name. Before I said it."

That was the thing about Vael that Oscar was still learning: it wasn't gone. It had been folded up small, tucked into the seams of things—a name stitched inside a coat lining, a song hummed by a man who claimed he'd never learned it, a child who walked out of a blizzard speaking a dead tongue at a sealed gate. The treaty hadn't killed Vael. It had only taught everyone to look away.

The lantern light ahead swung wildly, and then Tam Doss filled the tunnel, broad and grey-bearded, holding a lamp in one fist and a knife in the other.

"Kavanagh." His eyes went to Wren and something moved across his face—recognition, or grief wearing its coat. "You brought her down here. Into the deep roads. With Marchand's riders crawling the passes like lice."

"They'd have found us at the gatehouse."

"Aye." He lowered the knife slowly, the way a man lowers a thing he's held too long. "Well. Come in, then. Gods forgive me. Come in."

* * *

Captain Ilse Marchand had burned through four informants in three weeks, and none of them had lied to her, which was the infuriating part.

She stood in the gatehouse now while her sergeant pried up floorboards, and she let the silence do its work. The old woman's cottage was clean as a chapel. A kettle hung cold. Two cups sat drying by the basin.

"Two cups," Sergeant Bray said.

"I can count, Sergeant."

Marchand crossed to the hearth. The mantel held no portraits, no keepsakes—only a line of small iron keys, rusted nearly to lace, arranged oldest to newest like a family tree made of metal. She did not touch them. Touching things told you less than watching what people flinched away from.

The file on her desk in the capital said: *Oscar Kavanagh, gatekeeper, no known associates beyond smuggling contacts of the lowest order.* The file was wrong. Files were always wrong about people who had chosen to be invisible; they mistook invisibility for emptiness. But Marchand had spent twenty years learning that the quiet ones kept the deepest cellars.

"The girl spoke Vaelish," Bray said. "The scholars confirmed it. Which is impossible."

"Nothing is impossible, Sergeant. It's merely unlicensed." She turned toward the door that dominated the far wall—the famous Gate of Thornfell, black oak banded in grey iron, older than the kingdom that claimed it. No lock. No handle. Only a keyhole shaped like nothing Marchand had ever seen, a spiral wound inward like a shell. "And where there's a language, there are speakers. And where there are speakers—"

"—there's a country."

"There's a country." She said it flatly, because saying it any other way would mean admitting what the crown had ordered her to prevent: that Vael had never actually died. It had only been *unwitnessed*. And unwitnessed things, given a witness, came back. "Find them. Both of them. And send word to the engineers—I want sappers ready by the next moon turn. If the gate can't be guarded, it will be stone."

* * *

Doss's hall ran thirty feet underground, lit by lamps turned low, and it was fuller than Oscar had ever seen it. Smugglers. Every one of them. Men and women whose names she knew from ledgers and handshakes, whose goods had passed her gate for a decade, sitting shoulder to shoulder with their hats off, looking at Wren like men look at a sunrise they'd stopped believing in.

An old woman named Sefa sang something low while the stew went round, and Oscar's blood went strange, because it was her grandmother's lullaby—not quite, but close enough that the two songs leaned toward each other like family at a funeral.

Wren was crying without noise, the way children cry when they've practiced being unheard.

"That's the mourning verse for the fall of the walls," she whispered. "My nurse sang it. Nobody outside Vael knows it. *Nobody* knows it."

"They knew," Oscar said. "They just weren't allowed to remember out loud."

It was near midnight when the lamps guttered all at once, though there was no draft, and the temperature dropped the way it drops when a door opens somewhere it shouldn't.

They came through the walls—or through the memory of walls. Figures in the grey livery of a country that existed on no map, faces like faces seen underwater. The ghost-wardens of Vael. The smugglers didn't scream. They bowed their heads, every one, as if remembering an etiquette buried deeper than fear.

The foremost warden looked at Oscar, and its voice was wind through a keyhole.

*Keeper's daughter. The gate has dreamed of you your whole life. Show us the right.*

Oscar's hand closed around the key she'd worn since childhood—her grandmother's key, warm against her sternum, heavier than iron had any right to be.

"How?" she asked aloud, and her voice cracked on the smallness of it. "How do I show anything? I don't even know what I am."

*Say what your blood says,* the warden answered. *Not what your orders say.*

And Oscar understood, then, that the test had already begun years ago—that every night she'd stood at the gate feeling it hum under her palm like a sleeping animal, every morning she'd checked a seal she could never explain, she had been answering. The question was only whether her answer would hold in the open air.

"I keep the door," she said slowly. "Not because I was told to. Because someone on the other side might need it kept *openable*." She lifted her chin. "I'm not Vael's jailer. I'm not the crown's jailer. I'm the keeper of a threshold, and thresholds belong to both sides."

The lamps flared. The wardens' faces, for just a moment, looked almost like living faces—tired, hopeful, human. Then the cold withdrew, and Sefa was singing again, and Doss was pouring whiskey with hands that shook.

Wren took Oscar's hand. "They'll come to the gate now," she said. "All of them. The ones who were waiting."

"Waiting for what?"

"For someone to prove the door still opens from our side too."

* * *

They reached the gatehouse at dusk on the third day, and Captain Ilse Marchand was already there.

She stood before the gate with forty soldiers and two sappers' carts of black powder, and when Oscar came up the mountain path with Wren's hand in hers, Marchand didn't draw a sword. She simply stepped aside, clearing the sightline to the door, and said:

"Gatekeeper. Step away from the door. By order of the Crown, this gate will be destroyed at dawn."

"It's not yours to destroy."

"It's not *anyone's*, Kavanagh. That's rather the point. A door to nowhere is a wound. A door to a country that legally does not exist is treason with hinges." Her voice was steady, almost gentle. "Give me the key. You'll be treated fairly. The girl goes to the capital, learns some useful trade, forgets all this. Everyone lives. Isn't that what you people want? Quiet?"

Oscar looked at the gate. Under her feet, through the frozen ground, she felt it humming—the same note it had hummed her whole life, except now she knew it was a voice, and now she knew it was saying *soon*.

Behind Marchand, the sky was doing something wrong. Clouds circling the peak like water finding a drain. Frost climbing the soldiers' spear-hafts in patterns like written script. The ghost-wardens coming, and behind the ghost-wardens, faint as a memory of music, something else—the sound of a country remembering itself.

"Dawn's a long way off," Oscar said, and put the key in the lock.

"No!" Marchand's sword cleared its scabbard—but she hesitated, one heartbeat, and in that heartbeat Wren ran forward and pressed both palms flat against the oak, and began to speak in the old tongue, fast and pleading, a child asking a door to be a door.

The spiral keyhole drank the key like a throat swallowing.

The gate groaned—a sound like tectonic grief—and the iron bands glowed cherry-red, and Oscar felt her whole life pivot on a hinge older than kingdoms, and she thought, absurdly, of her grandmother checking the seal every morning, and understood at last that the old woman had not been keeping the world *out*.

She had been holding it *shut with love*, until someone worth letting in arrived.

The locks turned. All thirteen of them, one for each moon.

The gate swung inward onto a light that had no business existing, and out of that light walked figures in grey livery—solid now, breathing, alive—and the first of them knelt before Wren and said, in a tongue Oscar almost understood, words that needed no translation:

*The heir comes home.*

Marchand stood with her sword half-raised, forty soldiers at her back, and watched a country walk out of a doorway, and Oscar saw the exact moment the captain realized that her orders had been written for a war against nothing—and that nothing had just become everything.

"Captain," Oscar said quietly. "I believe you wanted to negotiate."
