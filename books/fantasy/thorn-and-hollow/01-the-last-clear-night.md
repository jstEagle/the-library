## 1. The Last Clear Night

There are three rules to moving contraband through the Thornfell passes, and I learned all of them before I learned to read.

One: the mountain is always smarter than you. Two: the crown's soldiers are dumber than the mountain, which is the only reason the trade survives. Three: never look back at what you're leaving behind, because the road ahead deserves your whole attention, and grief is a heavy thing to carry uphill.

I was thinking about rule three when I heard the bell.

Not a real bell. A wind-bell, one of dozens the Guild of the Hollow Road hangs in the high passes—little hammered-copper things that sing when anything heavier than a marmot disturbs the air around them. Smugglers hate them. Soldiers curse them. The guild swears by them, and the guild has been swearing by them for longer than anyone can count, back past the oldest ledgers, back into the part of our history where the ink gives up and the stories start.

The bell sang twice. Then it went quiet, which was worse.

"Hold," I said, and my hand came up, and behind me eleven people and fourteen mules stopped on a shelf of switchback no wider than a coffin laid sideways. Below us the valley fell away in black folds stitched with river-silver. Above us—

Above us were the stars. All of them. Every star the old maps named and a hundred the maps never bothered with, crowded so thick over Thornfell that the dark between them looked like the exception rather than the rule. On nights like this you could navigate by the sky alone, no lantern, no risk, the whole heavens turned into a road-sign just for us. Old Sefa Ironhale used to say the stars were the first smugglers, slipping light past heaven's customs every night since before there were roads to walk. She said it like a joke. Sefa says everything like a joke. It's how she gets away with telling the truth.

I stood on that narrow shelf and counted the bells' silence against my heartbeat. One. Two. Three.

Then the third bell along the ridge sang—a different note, higher, urgent—and I exhaled through my teeth.

"Goat," I announced. "Big one, coming down from the north scree." I turned and found Tam Rusk grinning at me out of the dark, his face a pale smear under that ridiculous fur hat he refuses to replace even though half of it is more moth than fur. "You owe me a copper, Rusk."

"I owe you nothing. That could've been a soldier."

"A soldier would've made four bells and a smell. You know what the passes smell like after soldiers? Boiled wool and fear."

"Two coppers says you can't tell goat from goat-herder at this distance."

"I don't take bets I win in my sleep." I started forward again, and the line followed, mules complaining in their low, patient way, boots finding stone by feel. This was the part of the job I loved best—the part nobody pays for. The reading of the mountain. The way twelve years of walking these passes lives in your ankles now instead of your head, so you don't decide where to step, you simply know, the way you know your own name.

My name, for the record, is Mara Delacroix, and according to Oren Vey, master of the Guild of the Hollow Road, I am "the finest guide the passes have produced in forty years, which given the state of the last forty years is less a compliment than an indictment."

Oren talks like that. He's the reason I can read, and also the reason I'm sarcastic about it.

* * *

The shipment itself was nothing worth dying for, which is to say it was exactly the kind of thing we usually die for. Salt pork, iron nails, a crate of fever-tincture bound for Greyford where the coughing sickness had settled in for the winter like a relative who won't leave, and—wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of the fourth mule's panniers, because even charity rides better with a little sin underneath it—six bolts of lowland silk. The silk belonged to a merchant in Harrowgate whose name I'd spell wrong if I tried. The rest belonged to the people of the high valleys, who have paid taxes to a crown they've never seen a soldier of, and who have learned not to expect much back except paperwork.

The guild takes a cut of everything that moves. Ten percent, same as always. And here is the thing about that ten percent that the crown men never understand when they call us thieves: it doesn't go into anybody's pocket. It goes into the hall. Into the roof that doesn't leak anymore, mostly. Into the ledger-room where Oren keeps two hundred years of records in a fireproofed cellar, written in a cipher he taught me when I was nine because, he said, "someone young should know where the bodies are buried, and everyone else who knew is either dead or Sefa."

It goes into the pension pot for guides who come down off the mountain too broken to climb again. It goes into bread for the widow Kess up in Hollowmere, who fed every runaway the guild ever sheltered and never once asked why they were running. It goes into the ward-stones.

That last one took me years to notice. Every season, a portion of the guild's cut vanished into a line item Oren called *maintenance, border*. Nobody asked what borders needed maintaining. Borders are lines on maps. Lines on maps don't rust.

But I'm getting ahead of myself, and rule three applies to thoughts as well as footsteps. Never look back. Keep walking.

We made the saddle below Crag's Tooth by moonset, and that's where the weather found us—or almost found us. I felt it first as a pressure in my bad ear, the one I cracked falling through the ice-bridge at Widow's Step three winters back. A stillness. A held breath. I stopped, tasted the air, and called the halt for camp in the lee of the tooth itself, tucked under its shoulder like a child under a parent's arm.

Tam squinted at the sky, then at me. "Clear night. Best one all month. Why are we stopping?"

"Because the mountain's holding its breath, and I don't care to be standing in the open when it exhales."

"You and your ear."

"My ear has saved this company more coin than your hat has cost it, and your hat has cost it plenty." But I let him build the fire small, behind the wind-break, because cold nights make argumentative men stupid and stupid men freeze. There's a fourth rule, actually. I keep saying there are three, but there are always more rules; the mountain writes new ones faster than anyone can memorize them. Rule four: a guide who won't stop for weather is a guide whose people die pretty.

We ate salt pork and hardbread and the last of the dried plums, and I sat with my back against stone and did the thing I do at the end of every leg of every run, the thing nobody taught me but that I've done since I was small enough to sleep in a pannier: I counted.

Eleven souls. Fourteen mules, all sound. Cargo intact. Debt column: the guild owes the tanner in Harrowgate sixty crowns, overdue; I owe the guild—I stopped counting my own debt years ago, because Oren keeps the figure himself and shows it to me every midwinter with the expression of a man presenting a bill he intends never to collect. "Sentiment," he calls it, on the ledger, in his own hand. Line item: sentiment. The man puts mercy in the accounts and calls it bookkeeping.

And above it all, the stars, wheeling slow and indifferent and generous. I lay back against my pack and looked at them until my eyes watered from the cold, and I thought—not for the first time—that if the world ever gave me one night like this to keep, no debts, no deadlines, no frozen pass between me and breakfast, I might finally learn to be content.

The world, as I say, has never once been generous. But it was generous that night, and I didn't know yet to be afraid of that.

* * *

We came down into the high valley at dawn, and Greyford met us with smoke and silence.

Greyford is a town the way a stubborn stump is a tree—half its timber gone, still standing anyway. The coughing sickness had emptied half its lanes. We delivered the tincture to a healer named Bren who counted vials twice and thanked me once, which in Greyford counts as effusive. The widow whose son carried our letters down-valley fed us barley soup that tasted mostly of salt and gratitude. And everywhere, on doorposts and lintels and the lintel-beam of the shrine, I saw the same mark chalked fresh: a circle with a line through it. The old sign for *warded*. Grandmothers were chalking it over doors that hadn't been marked in living memory.

"Superstition's back," Tam said, nodding at one. "Half the town's wearing iron nails on cords. Saw a boy with a horseshoe around his neck like a saint's medal."

"People are frightened. Sick towns reach for old signs."

"It's more'n that." He lowered his voice, which was unlike him; Tam's volume settings are *boisterous* and *catastrophic*. "Talk in the tavern last night, while you were settling accounts. The old folk are saying the stones have gone quiet."

"What stones?"

"The border stones. The ward-stones, Mara. The ones on the pass above Thornfell village. My gran used to say you could put your palm flat on them and feel them humming, like bees in a wall. Says her mother said it too, and her mother's mother. The shepherds up top say—" he hesitated, and I watched a man who fears nothing except debt collectors genuinely hesitate— "the shepherds say they haven't hummed since summer's end."

I thought about the line item. *Maintenance, border.* I thought about how Oren had sent three separate crews up toward the high stones this autumn, quietly, in ones and twos, the way you send crews when something needs watching and you'd rather not discuss it. I'd guided two of those crews myself. I'd stood beside old stones taller than I am, carved with spirals worn nearly smooth, and I'd felt—something. A faint vibration under my palm, so subtle I'd told myself it was water moving deep in the rock. Everyone knows Thornfell's roots are full of water. Everyone knows the mountain hums, if you listen long enough. Mountains do that.

Don't they?

"Mara?" Tam was watching me. "You've gone strange."

"I'm always strange. It's load-bearing." I swung up onto the lead mule. "Come on. We're due at the hall by tomorrow dusk, and if the master wants extra hands on the high route this season, I want first pick of the work."

"Why?"

Because I wanted to put my hand on one of those stones again and pay proper attention this time. Because a guild that maintains borders nobody remembers needing maintained is a guild with a secret, and I have spent my whole life inside that secret's walls without once asking what the walls were for. Because rule five—and here I'll admit the rules are endless—is that a guide who stops asking questions stops being a guide and becomes cargo.

"No reason," I told him. "Ride."

Behind us Greyford's smoke rose straight up into a sky already filling with evening stars, every one of them burning steady, bright, untroubled, as if the whole heavens were leaning in to watch what happened next.

I remember that. Remember it better than anything else from those days: how many stars there were, and how completely I trusted them to stay.
