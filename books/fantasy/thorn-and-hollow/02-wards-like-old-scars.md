## 2. Wards Like Old Scars

Oren Vey woke me before dawn by the simple method of standing over my bunk and breathing until I surrendered.

"You're coming up the border with me today," he said.

I squinted at him through the gloom of the dormitory loft. The guildhall smelled of last night's stew and the tallow candles someone always forgot to snuff. Somewhere below, Tam was already arguing with the ledgers, which meant it was early even by guild standards. "Coming up the border," I repeated. "That's a new way of saying *you're behind on your debts, girl, and the roads are closed till the thaw*."

"The roads are never closed." He tossed something at me—my boots, one after the other, both landing where my face had been a moment earlier. "And your debts are between you and the ledger-keeper, who is Tam, who will forgive them eventually because he's soft. Get dressed. Wear the good coat."

"The good coat is for deliveries."

"Today it's for maintenance."

I'd been with the Guild of the Hollow Road eleven years by then, and in all that time Oren Vey had never once used the word *maintenance*. We were smugglers. We moved what needed moving—salt when the crown taxed it, medicine when the crown hoarded it, letters from lovers and traitors, occasionally both in the same envelope. Maintenance was a word for priests and masons and men with nothing better to do than oil hinges.

But he was already thumping down the ladder, so I got dressed.

The good coat was grey wool with a hood that actually kept rain off, a luxury I'd traded two crates of Greyford cheese to acquire, and I resented being made to wear it for anything less than profit. I came down into the main hall to find Oren waiting with a pack far too large for a day trip and an expression I couldn't read. Oren had three expressions: grumpy, grumpier, and the one he wore at funerals. This was the third one.

"We're not doing maintenance," I said.

"We are absolutely doing maintenance." He handed me half the pack weight without asking. "You're just going to wish we weren't."

Outside, the frost had come early and hard. The yard stones were silvered, our breath hung like ghosts arguing about whose turn it was to haunt, and the mountains above us stood white against a sky still crowded with stars—the last clear night, though nobody knew that yet, least of all me. I remember looking up at them while I buckled the straps. Remember thinking they looked close enough to steal from. That's the smuggler's eye for you: everything is inventory, including heaven.

Sefa Ironhale met us at the gate with her own pack and a face like a slammed door.

"Why her?" Sefa asked.

"Because she asked the right question last month," Oren said, "and because you've done this walk four hundred times and I need someone who'll see it fresh."

"Fresh eyes see wrong things."

"Sometimes that's the point."

Sefa grunted, which from Sefa was a full paragraph of assent, and we went up.

*

There are two ways out of Thornfell vale: the cart road that switchbacks east toward the lowlands, and the Hollow Road itself, which goes nowhere anyone admits to going. We took neither. We took the goat track north along the river gorge, climbing through pines that creaked under their new load of ice, and by full light we were high enough that the valley below looked like something stitched—a patchwork of roofs and smoke, small and holdable in one hand.

Oren didn't talk as we climbed. That should have warned me. Oren talked constantly when he was comfortable—a running commentary on weather, tariffs, the moral failings of the Lowmarsh toll-collectors—and went silent only when his mind was somewhere he didn't want company. So I matched his silence, and Sefa provided hers free of charge, and the three of us walked up into the mountains like mourners who hadn't agreed yet on who had died.

Near midday, the track crested a shoulder of rock and there it was: the first ward-stone.

I'd seen it before, of course. Everyone in the guild had. It stood maybe chest-high, a pillar of grey stone shot through with veins of something darker, set on the ridge where the Thornfell passes began their long descent toward the lowland plains. Travelers' superstition called them the Old Man's Fingers—one every few miles along the whole border range, dozens of them, worn smooth by centuries of weather. Shepherds touched them for luck. Children dared each other to spit on them. Nobody, in eleven years of hauling contraband past them, had ever told me they mattered.

Oren knelt at its base, unpacked a leather roll, and got to work like a man sharpening a knife.

What he did, I can only describe sideways. There were grooves in the stone's base, channels worn deep and deliberate, and he packed them with a paste from a clay jar—grey, smelling of iron and pine resin and underneath it something else, something cold, like the air off a glacier. Then he took a rod of black metal from the roll, laid it across the grooves, and hummed.

Actually hummed. Three notes, low, more felt than heard. And the ward-stone answered.

I don't know how else to put it. The dark veins in the rock brightened—not glowing, exactly; more like the way a bruise warms when pressed—and a sound rose out of the ground itself, a sustained tone beneath hearing, the note a mountain would sing if it could be bothered. My teeth ached with it. My back teeth. The hairs on my arms lifted, and not from cold.

"What," I said.

"Maintenance," said Oren.

"Don't you dare."

He sat back on his heels and looked up at me, and his funeral expression had gotten worse. "Sit down, Mara."

I sat down.

For a long moment none of us said anything. The stone sang its subterranean note. Below us the valley smoked peacefully. Above us the stars' daytime ghosts hung invisible in the blue.

"How many stones are there?" I finally asked, because it seemed safer than the other forty questions.

"Sixty-one on the Thornfell line. More on the ranges east and west, but those aren't ours." Oren began repacking his tools with the fussy precision of a man buying time. "We maintain ours. Have since before the guild had a name."

"We're smugglers."

"We're *smugglers*," he agreed. "That's the coat we wear so nobody asks what's underneath. Think about it, girl. What does a smuggling guild actually need? Roads nobody watches. Routes that stay open through any season. Silence from the towns along the way, silence from the garrisons, silence from the tax-men. Now ask yourself—how does a handful of illiterate quarry-runners in the worst mountains in the world manage all that for six hundred years?"

"Because we're good?"

"Because the routes were built for this." He patted the stone beside him like an old dog. "Every pass we use, every tunnel, every blind trail—laid out by the same hands that raised these stones. The smuggling is the cover story. It has been the cover story since before there was a crown to smuggle against. The Guild of the Hollow Road exists to keep these wards fed and humming, and we have kept that secret so long we've forgotten we're keeping it."

The word *fed* snagged in me. "Fed. Like—it eats?"

"It drinks," Sefa said, speaking for the first time in hours. Her voice was flat as slate. "The paste. Iron, ash, resin, and blood-meal from the spring slaughter. Six parts, old recipe. You'll learn to mix it if Oren has his way, and you'll hate the smell by winter."

"You've known," I said to her. "All this time."

"Thirty years." She shrugged. "It's not the kind of thing you tell a new recruit. New recruits leave."

"And I'm not new anymore."

"You asked why the eastern route never ices over," Sefa said. "In your second year. Nobody asks that. Everybody just uses it and thanks their luck. But you asked."

I remembered. A warm road through a killing winter, and everyone shrugging, and me filing the question away because that's what you do when the answers would cost more than the curiosity. I'd thought the answer was luck. Or trade secrets. Or the particular blessing of mountains that liked us.

Mountains don't like anybody. Mountains have to be paid.

"Why now?" I asked Oren. "Why show me? Why not let me go on hauling salt and being happy?"

"Two reasons." He held up fingers. "One: I'm sixty-eight years old, my knees are gravel, and Sefa's no spring kid herself. Somebody younger has to learn the routes to the stones or the knowledge dies with us, and then—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "Then the stones starve."

"And two?"

He looked out along the ridge, where the next ward-stone stood a mile off, a grey finger against the snow. When he spoke again his voice had dropped into the register he used for bad news delivered gently, which was somehow worse than shouting.

"Two: they've gotten harder to feed. Every year, a little harder. The paste that lasted a season when I was young lasts three months now. The notes I have to hum—I had a teacher who barely had to breathe to raise that song. Something on the other end of these stones is pulling harder than it used to, and either it's growing hungry or we're growing weak, and I've spent thirty years hoping it was the second one." He turned and fixed me with those pale, watery, terrifyingly steady eyes. "I don't think it's the second one anymore."

The wind came up the gorge and hit us all three at once, sharp with snow-smell, and I pulled the good coat tighter around myself and hated it suddenly, hated the comfort of it.

"So what's on the other end?" I asked. "Of the stones."

Oren and Sefa exchanged a look. It was brief, but I caught it, and it was the look of two people deciding how much truth a person can carry before the carrying breaks them.

"That," Oren said, "is a longer walk than this one. Come on. We've got five more stones before dark."

*

We worked the rest of the line through the afternoon. I watched, and then I helped, and then Oren had me humming the notes myself—badly, flat, my voice cracking on the second tone until Sefa corrected my breathing with a shove between the shoulders—and each stone woke the same way, that deep subterranean answer rolling up through my boots like a heartbeat belonging to somebody enormous. By the fourth stone I'd stopped flinching. By the fifth I noticed something I probably shouldn't have noticed, which is that the stones weren't identical. They leaned. All sixty-one of them, I later learned, lean slightly inward, toward the heart of the range, like a crowd of people bending over something on the ground.

Like guards bending over a prisoner. Or mourners over a grave. Depending on the day, I've settled on both.

We camped that night in a shepherd's hut with a cracked roof, and Oren boiled tea over a fire he built with the economy of a man who'd done it ten thousand times, and I sat wrapped in the good coat watching the stars wheel overhead—thick, reckless, beautiful, more stars than the lowlands ever saw—and tried to fit the day inside the life I already had.

It wouldn't fit. That was the trouble. Eleven years of certainty, and one afternoon of humming at rocks had cracked it like river ice.

"Oren," I said. "The routes. The tunnels. All those years I thought we were clever—"

"We were clever," he said. "Cleverness is real. It's just not the whole story. Nothing is ever the whole story, girl. That's the first lesson of a long life: every wall you trust has another wall behind it, and the second wall was built by people who knew about the first."

"Who built the first wall?"

He sipped his tea. Steam fogged his beard. "Ask me again when you can hum the second verse."

"There's a second verse?"

"There's always a second verse." He lay back against his pack. "Sleep. Tomorrow we take the western fork, and tomorrow you learn what the guild actually owes, and why."

"Owes whom?"

But he was already closing his eyes, and Sefa had taken first watch with her crossbow across her knees like a woman expecting company, and the fire ticked and settled, and I lay awake a long while listening to the mountain.

That's the thing I want to set down properly, because people ask me afterward—survivors, historians, the odd crown clerk with more courage than sense—and they always expect me to describe some dramatic omen. A rumble. A voice. Lights under the snow.

There was none of that. Just the ordinary night-sounds of a high pass: wind, ice settling, the hut timbers complaining. Except that underneath it all, faint as a word spoken in another room, the ward-stones a mile down the ridge were still singing. They never stop, Oren told me later. They only fall silent once.

And that night, lying between sleep and waking, I could have sworn the note changed. Deepened. Slowed. The way a sleeper's breathing changes when the dream turns bad.

I told myself it was the wind. I was very good at telling myself things, back then. I'd had eleven years of practice.

In the morning we took the western fork, and Oren Vey told me what the guild owed, and why—and by the time he finished, the stars were nine days from going out forever, and neither of us knew it, and the mountain under our feet had begun, at last and very slowly, to turn over in its sleep.
