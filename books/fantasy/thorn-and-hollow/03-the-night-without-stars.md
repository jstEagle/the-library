## 3. The Night Without Stars

I'd like to tell you I knew something was wrong before it happened. That the smuggler's instinct in my blood—the one that's kept me alive through two betrayals and one avalanche already—whispered a warning hours ahead, and I acted on it like the heroes in Oren Vey's dusty old books.

It didn't. I was counting money.

That's not even a euphemism. I was literally sitting at the long table in the guildhall's back room with a stub of candle and Tam Rusk's abacus, tallying what the Greyford run had earned us after tolls, bribes, and the mule we'd lost to a crevasse named—by Sefa, with great affection—"Biter." Forty-one crowns, twelve shillings, and a coin I suspected was counterfeit but which had such a pretty face on it I couldn't bring myself to melt down. The guild's debts outnumbered its crowns by roughly the ratio of stars in the sky to guild members, which on that particular night was about to become an ironic comparison.

The hall above me was loud. That should have been my first clue. The Hollow Road's people were celebrating the successful crossing—Sefa had brought down a full shipment of lowland salt and highland wool without losing so much as a crate to frost or tax collectors—and guild celebrations were rare enough lately that they went at them like starving men at a funeral feast. Boots on floorboards. Brann Oakfist attempting a song and being loudly discouraged. Someone had found honey wine, which meant someone had traded something they shouldn't have, which meant by tomorrow morning there would be recriminations.

Tonight, though. Tonight the rafters shook with it, and I sat below with the ledger because somebody always has to, and in the Guild of the Hollow Road, that somebody had been me since I was fifteen years old and discovered I could add faster than anyone who wasn't too drunk to check my work.

"You're missing it," Tam said from the doorway.

He leaned there with a cup in hand and that easy smile he wore like other men wore cloaks—put on when leaving the house, adjusted to suit the weather. Twenty years old to my nineteen, broad-shouldered, dark-haired, with the kind of face that made customs officers forget to ask questions. My oldest friend. Practically my brother. Which is why I didn't look up from the ledger when I answered him, because if I looked up I'd be persuaded, and the ledger wouldn't balance itself.

"I'm missing debt," I said. "It'll keep."

"The wine won't."

"Then drink your share and mine. I'll take it in coin."

He came in anyway—he always came in anyway—and hooked a chair out from under the table with his boot and sat across from me, close enough that his knee knocked mine under the boards, the way he'd done since we were children sharing a bench in the guild's schoolroom while Sefa taught us knots and maps and which mountain plants you could eat and which ones would have you seeing your dead grandmother for three days.

"You know what Oren says," Tam said, twirling his cup. "A guild that only counts backward never moves forward."

"Oren also says a smuggler who quotes the guildmaster is angling for something."

His smile widened. "Am I?"

"You're always angling. It's your best quality." I set down the quill and finally gave him the look he wanted. "Out with it, Rusk. What are you selling?"

"Nothing. Everything." He glanced toward the ceiling, where the noise of the party thumped and swayed like a heartbeat. "It's just—I was up on the ridge today. Checking the north ward-stone, like Oren asked." Something flickered behind his eyes, quick as a fish turning in shallow water. "It hums strange lately. Did you know that? Lower than it used to."

"They all hum strange. They're older than the kingdom. Oren says they've been singing the same song since before there were roads to smuggle on."

"Lower," Tam said again, and held my gaze, and for a moment I saw something underneath the easy smile that I couldn't name—not guilt, I decided later; I decided many things later, all of them wrong—but intensity, the look of a man watching a door he intends to walk through. "Doesn't that strike you as odd? A song getting quieter. Like a singer running out of breath."

"Tam." I reached over and pressed the back of my hand against his forehead, theatrical, checking for fever. "You've been drinking Oren's history books again. Come drink something honest instead."

He laughed, and the flicker was gone, buried back under the smile where I would not find it again until it was far, far too late. He stood, drained his cup, and pointed the empty at me with mock severity.

"One dance. Then back to your numbers. If the world ends tonight, Delacroix, I refuse to let it catch you hunched over a ledger like a gargoyle with a grudge."

"It won't end tonight," I said. "Nothing ever happens here except winter and taxes."

Reader, note the date of that remark. I intend to be reminded of it for the rest of my life.

* * *

I did go up eventually. Ledger closed, candle pinched out between finger and thumb, and I climbed the narrow stair into the noise and heat and light of the guildhall proper, and if I tell you that I paused at the top of those stairs—if I tell you I stood there a moment longer than necessary, looking at all of them, every rough face and scarred hand, Brann red-faced and singing now that everyone had given up discouraging him, Sefa Ironhale in her corner nursing ale and pretending she wasn't smiling, old Oren Vey holding court by the fire with a book he wasn't reading and young apprentices hanging off his sleeves—I want you to understand that I wasn't thinking anything sentimental. I was a smuggler taking inventory. That's all. Counting what I had, the way you count coin, so you know exactly how much you can afford to lose.

Forty-one crowns, twelve shillings, one counterfeit. Two dozen souls, give or take the ones out on the road.

I took the cup Brann thrust at me, danced one dance with Tam and half of another with the twins from the Greyford line who cheated at everything including dancing, and stepped outside into the cold because the room had gotten too warm and I'd learned early that warmth is a thing you should enjoy in moderation, like trust.

The night was the kind they write bad poetry about. The sky over Thornfell was clear as polished glass, and the stars were out in their thousands, sharp and white and thick along the spine of heaven, so many that the dark between them seemed almost blue. The peaks stood black against them, the high passes silvered with snow, and the border range ran north like the jagged edge of a broken plate. Somewhere up on the ridgeline, invisible from the yard, the nearest ward-stone was humming its ancient low hum, and beneath our feet, though I didn't know it yet, the mountain held its breath around whatever it had been holding for a thousand years.

I remember standing there with my cup going cold, doing sums without meaning to. What forty-two crowns buys. How long the winter stores last. Whether the crown's new tariff would strangle the Greyford route by spring. Ordinary arithmetic. A life measured in small survivable numbers.

And then the humming stopped.

I don't know how I heard it stop from the yard, a hundred yards downhill from the nearest stone. I shouldn't have been able to. But you live your whole childhood under a sound, a sound older than the guild, older than the kingdom, a sound so constant it stops being sound and becomes silence's opposite, the floor everything stands on—and when it goes, you feel it the way you feel a floor vanish. In your stomach first. Then everywhere.

The yard went quiet around me. Not gradually. All at once, like a held note cut clean. Inside the hall, the music stumbled on for another few bars, laughter still rolling, and out in the yard a dozen smugglers stood frozen with cups halfway to lips, every face tilted upward, every person wearing the same expression I could feel on my own: the look of a dog that hears a whistle no one else does.

"What was that?" Brann, from the doorway, suddenly sober-sounding.

Nobody answered, because nobody knew, and then the stars began to go out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded over—there wasn't a cloud from Thornfell to the coast, I'd stake my life on the weather if not my honesty. They went out the way candles go out when you pinch them: here, and then not, with nothing in between. The brightest ones first, or maybe I only noticed the bright ones first—Vasha's Lantern, the Shepherd's Knot, the little tight cluster we called the Dice Cup that Sefa used to navigate the high passes. Snuffed. Then whole constellations, whole reaches of sky, swallowed in patches like ink dropped into water. And the dark that came down in their place wasn't ordinary dark. Night-dark is a blanket; this was a lid. It pressed. It had weight. I felt it settle on my shoulders like a hand.

Behind me, inside, someone screamed, and the music stopped for good.

I did what I always do, which is the thing I'm most ashamed of and most proud of, sometimes in the same breath: I took charge. Terror disguised as competence—that's the Galloway family crest, if the Delacroix family had a crest, which we don't, because families like ours don't get heraldry, we get ledgers.

"Inside, all of you," I said, and my voice came out steady, which surprised me, because inside I was a child again hearing the floorboards creak in a house that should be empty. "Brann, bar the doors. Get every lamp, every candle, every lantern lit—everything we own, all of it, now. Sefa—"

But Sefa was already beside me. She'd come out of the hall silent as she does everything, this grey-haired slab of a woman who'd carried me out of a burning waystation when I was six, and she stood squinting up at the dying sky with her jaw working like she was chewing on something too tough to swallow.

"That's not weather," she said.

"No," I said.

"That's not anything." She turned, and I saw real fear in her face for the third time in my nineteen years, and each of the previous times had involved fire. "Get Oren. Get him now. He'll know. He has to know."

The ground moved.

Not an earthquake—or not only an earthquake. It was deeper than that, slower, a single vast muscular shift beneath the mountain's roots, like something enormous rolling over in its sleep. The yard lurched. The well-house shed its roof tiles in a rattling cascade. From somewhere in the high passes came a sound I will hear until I die: a deep, grinding boom, then another, then a long rolling thunder that wasn't thunder, as the snowpack on the northern shoulders of Thornfell let go all at once.

"Avalanches," Sefa said. "All of them. Every pass at once." She grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise. "Mara. The routes. Every route we have just closed."

Later—much later, when I understood what the wards had been for and what their death meant—I would learn that the passes hadn't merely been blocked. They'd been *held*, all these centuries, propped open by the same old song that kept the stars burning over the borderlands and kept whatever slept beneath the mountain sleeping. The guild hadn't been maintaining stones. We'd been holding up the sky, badly, without knowing it, and when the last stone failed, the sky fell in on us from both directions: stars above, mountains around, and something below beginning to stretch limbs it hadn't stretched in a thousand years.

None of that did I know in the yard. In the yard I knew only that the world's oldest silence had fallen, the stars were dying overhead like a city going dark quarter by quarter, and the man whose job it was to explain impossible things was asleep by his fire with a book in his lap.

I ran for him anyway. Through the doors, through the chaos—the hall lit now with two dozen flames, everyone shouting, the twins crying, Tam pushing through the crowd toward me with his face bloodless white and his easy smile gone as if it had never existed—and up the stairs two at a time to Oren Vey's study, where I burst in without knocking for the first time in my life.

He was already awake. Already standing at his window, staring north, and in the lamplight his face was terrible to see—not afraid, which would have been bearable, but *grieved*, the way you grieve at a funeral for someone you always knew you'd bury someday.

"Mara," he said, without turning. His voice was very calm. Calm the way ice is calm. "How long have the ward-stones hummed for you? Since childhood?"

"S-since always. Why does that matter? Oren, the stars—"

"Because they stopped for me thirty years ago," he said, "on a night much like this one, and I told myself I had imagined it. Go downstairs. Tell them to light everything and touch nothing and say nothing to the lowland caravans. Can you do that?"

"Yes."

"And Mara—" Now he turned, and he took my face in his two old hands, the way he had when I was small and feverish, and his eyes were wet and fierce and more frightened than any avalanche could make them. "Whatever happens tonight, whatever you see or hear or dream in the nights after—come to me first. Before Tam. Before anyone. Promise me."

"Oren, what's happening? What did the stones do?"

He closed his eyes. Beneath us, faint as a heartbeat, fainter than it had ever been, the mountain shifted again—and this time, in the ringing quiet after, I would have sworn on the counterfeit coin in my pocket that I felt something answer it. Felt, deep under the guildhall's foundations, under the rock and the roots and the thousand years of our ignorance, something vast turn its attention upward.

Toward the place where the humming used to be.

Toward us.

"Promise me," Oren said again.

"I promise," I said. And downstairs, in the hall full of firelight and fear, with the stars gone out and the passes sealed and my family gathering around me like sheep in a storm, I kept my eyes open for Tam Rusk—and could not find him anywhere at all.
