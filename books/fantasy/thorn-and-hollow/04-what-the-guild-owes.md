## 4. What the Guild Owes

I have kept a great many secrets in my time. Some of them were even mine.

Most of them belonged to other people—dead people, mostly, who had pressed them into my hands the way you press a hot iron into someone's grip and say *hold this*, because they're tired of holding it themselves. That is what inheritance means in the Hollow Road: not land, not coin, not a name on a deed. It means being the one still standing when the truth needs somewhere to live.

The night the stars went out, I sat in the guildhall at Thornfell with forty frightened smugglers looking at me as though I had personally arranged the sky, and I knew that the oldest secret of all had finally come due.

* * *

You should understand something about Oren Vey before I go further, or nothing I say will make sense. I am not a brave man. I have never been a brave man. Bravery is Mara Delacroix walking into a blizzard with a knife in her boot and a smile like an apology she doesn't mean to make. Bravery is Sefa Ironhale holding a rope bridge steady for three hours in a crosswind because a boy from Greyford was on the far side of it. What I am is a man who learned early that if you stand very still and look older than you are, people mistake paralysis for wisdom.

It has worked for sixty years. I recommend it to no one.

The hall was lit by every lamp we owned, which was itself a small confession—we did not own many lamps, because lamps cost candle-tallow and tallow costs silver, and silver in the Hollow Road goes to boots and bridges and bribes. Forty faces in lamplight. Tam Rusk near the door with his jaw set the way it sets when he has already decided something and is waiting for the rest of us to catch up. Old Marta counting heads by touch because her eyes went years ago. And at the front, arms crossed, snow still melting off her shoulders onto my good floorboards, Mara Delacroix, twenty-four years old and asking questions with her whole body.

"Again," she said. "From the beginning."

"Mara—"

"You said the ward-stones failed. All of them. At once." She held up her hands, palms out—I noticed the red marks across them and filed it away, the way you file away things about people you love when there isn't time to ask. "Stones don't do that. Stones fail one at a time, over decades, and we fix them. You taught me that. So either every stone in two hundred miles decided to die tonight out of solidarity, or somebody killed them, or—" she stopped, and I watched her arrive at the third option and flinch from it, "—or they weren't stones doing the work in the first place."

There it is. That mind. I have spent half her life being grateful for it and the other half dreading the day it turned its attention on me.

"Sefa," I said instead, because cowardice is a habit like any other. "Tell them about the maintenance."

Sefa Ironhale stood up from the bench by the hearth with the sound of a landslide deciding to be polite about it. She is older than me, which she reminds me of monthly, and she has hands like tree roots and a face that suggests God made her from leftover mountain and never came back for the scraps. She looked around the hall at those forty faces and I saw her decide, the way I had decided an hour before, that the secret was done keeping itself.

"The routes," she said. "You all think the smuggling routes are why the guild exists. You think we're thieves with a pension plan." A few uncomfortable laughs. She let them die. "The routes are why the guild *survives*. They're not why it exists."

She told them then. Not everything—she told them what the runners need to know, which is the same thing I would have told them, because we had rehearsed this, she and I, over thirty years of quiet suppers, rehearsing for a night neither of us believed would come. The guild maintains the border wards. Every guildmaster since the founding has sent crews along the passes twice a year to tend the old stones—not repairing them, exactly. Feeding them. There are words, and there is blood, and there are songs that aren't songs, and none of it is magic in the way the lowlands understand magic, with sparks and theatrics. It is closer to farming. You tend a thing that grows whether you tend it or not, and you pray your tending matters.

"Why?" someone asked—Dellin, one of the Greyford runners, young enough that his voice still cracked on hard questions. "Why does a smuggling guild keep border wards? Whose border? Against what?"

And that was the question, wasn't it. That had always been the question, and for three hundred years the answer had been *none of your business, and also please don't ask again*.

I stood up. My knees objected. My knees object to most things now; we have an arrangement where I ignore them.

"Sit down, all of you," I said, "because I'm only going to tell this once, and afterward some of you are going to want to leave, and I'd rather you heard all of it first."

* * *

Here is the history, then, as it was given to me by the woman who trained me, who had it from the man who trained her, back through twenty-two generations to the founders—and I will tell it plainly, because plainness is all I have to offer tonight.

Before Thornfell had a name, before there was a lowlands worth the word, there was a war. Not a war of armies—the histories the crown keeps don't even remember it—but a war between peoples, the ones who lived in the valleys and the ones who came up out of the deep places under the mountains, and like most wars it ended not with victory but with exhaustion, and like most exhausted wars it ended with a crime dressed up as a peace.

The thing beneath Thornfell—you will forgive me, but I have never been able to call it anything grander than *the thing beneath Thornfell*, because the old records call it seven names and each one is a prayer or a curse depending on which side wrote it down—that thing was not destroyed. It could not be destroyed. It was *sealed*. Buried under the roots of the mountain behind wards cut into living stone, and the people who did the sealing knew that wards fade. Everything fades. Stone forgets. So they founded an order to remember for it.

Not an army. An order of road-keepers, masons, couriers. People who could move through the passes in any season, unnoticed, unremarkable. Who could tend the stones twice a year for centuries without anyone asking why. Who could pass the duty down hand to hand, generation to generation, wrapped in enough mundane trade—smuggling, cartage, ferrying—that no king would ever bother to look twice at us.

"We're not a guild of smugglers," I told them. "We never were. We are a guild of gravekeepers who took up smuggling so we could afford the shovels."

The silence after that lasted long enough for me to hear the wind working at the shutters, which it does in a particular way in winter, a long low note like a finger run round the rim of a glass. Some of them were staring at their own hands, as if the work they'd done all these years might have left a mark they'd missed.

Marta, blind old Marta, spoke first, from her corner. "My grandmother used to sing the feeding-song. I thought it was a lullaby." She said it without accusation, which somehow was worse than accusation. "She sang it while she baked bread. I sang it to my daughter."

"It was always meant to be sung to children," Sefa said quietly. "That's how it survives."

"And the wards failed anyway," Tam said. His voice was level, careful, and I have known Tam Rusk since he was nine years old stealing apples off my windowsill, and I know what his level voice costs him. "Three hundred years of tending and singing, and last night every stone died at once. So either the order failed—"

"Or it was helped," Mara finished.

I looked at her. She was looking at the floor. I got the distinct and terrible impression that she had already thought several steps past where the rest of the room was standing, and that one of those steps pointed inward, at the people in this room, and I was not yet ready for anyone to take it.

"The stones can be broken deliberately," I admitted. "It takes knowledge. The knowledge lives here, in this hall, and in perhaps four other places in the world."

"So it's one of us." Dellin said it flatly, the way you say a diagnosis.

"It might be no one," I said, and even as the words left my mouth I heard how thin they sounded, a coat of paint over rot. "The seal is old. Old things end. That has always been the fear—that one morning we'd simply find ourselves obsolete, the last keepers of an empty grave."

"But you don't believe that," Mara said.

"No," I said. "I don't."

Because here is the part I had not yet told them, the part that had brought me down to the hall in the first place, moving slow on bad knees, carrying the weight of the guild's whole inheritance in a satchel I had not opened in thirty years.

When the stars went out, the key woke up.

* * *

The key is not a key. I say this to everyone eventually, and it helps no one, so consider yourself warned. It is a piece of the seal itself—a fragment of the original binding, cut free by the founders and carried south by the courier line, generation to generation, so that if the seal ever weakened there would exist in the world one object that could reach the heart of the binding and mend it. Or close it. The old texts disagree, in the way old texts do, and the disagreement is not academic, and I will come to that.

It looks like nothing. A lump of grey stone the size of a fist, veined with something darker, warm to the touch the way a sleeping animal is warm. It has been passed down the courier line for three hundred years, master to heir, and for three hundred years it has done precisely nothing, which is the best quality you can ask for in a relic. My predecessor gave it to me in this same hall, in winter, with the same wind at the shutters, and said the same thing I was about to say: *I'm sorry, and thank you, and don't open it until you must.*

Thirty years I've kept it under the floor of my quarters under a loose stone under a rug nobody liked. Thirty years of sleeping above it and telling myself it was just a rock, just an inheritance, just a story.

Last night, when the stars went out, it began to hum.

Not loudly. That's the horror of it—it wasn't dramatic. It sounded like a kettle thinking about boiling. Like a hymn remembered from childhood, half-heard through a wall. I lay in my bed listening to it and felt thirty years of comfortable lies slide off me like snow off a roof, and I got up, and I dug it out, and I carried it down here in a satchel, and it has not stopped humming since.

Mara heard it before I opened the satchel. Of course she did. Her head came up like a hound's, and her eyes went straight to the bag on the table as if it had called her name.

"That," she said. "That's what I felt on the pass. Before the stars went out. Under my feet, through the ice—a sound like—" She stopped. Rubbed her palms together absently, and I saw the red marks again, and this time I asked.

"Your hands."

"They're fine."

"Mara."

"Frostburn, maybe. From the shipment." But she wouldn't show me, and I let it go, because the room was full and the hour was late and I have learned to pick which battles to lose in front of an audience. I noted it. I put it with the other things I was noting about her—the way she'd gone still when Sefa described the sealing, the way her breathing changed when the satchel hummed—and I told myself I'd worry about it tomorrow.

Tomorrow is a country I have been planning to visit my whole life.

* * *

I told them the rest of it standing beside the satchel, one hand resting on it like you'd rest a hand on a sick animal's flank. The seal is failing. The stars going out is not a symptom of the waking—it is a symptom of the wards dying, and the wards dying is the mountain's fever breaking, and what comes next comes fast. Avalanches sealing the high passes: that was the mountain closing its own doors, or the old magic thrashing in its sleep. The cold coming down out of the north out of season: that was the deep places exhaling after three hundred years of held breath. And underneath it all, faint as a heartbeat through a floor, the humming, growing.

"What happens if it wakes?" That was Tam again. He hadn't moved from the door. He'd barely moved all night, and I thought: he knows something. I thought it and dismissed it, because that is what I do, because I am a man who has spent sixty years mistaking caution for virtue. "Fully wakes. What are we actually talking about?"

"I don't know," I said, and it was the truest sentence I spoke all night. "The founders' records describe a war. The lowlands lost the last one, and the lowlands had gods on their side then. Best case, we get another age of dark and cold and things moving in the passes. Worst case—" I shrugged, an old man's shrug, inadequate to the task. "Worst case the histories stop being written."

Silence again. Then Tam pushed off the doorframe and said, calm as a merchant quoting price: "Then we sell it."

The room turned toward him.

"Not the power. The *secret*. The key, the knowledge, the warning—all of it, to the crown, tonight, before the mountain finishes waking. Let them raise armies. Let them empty the treasury on wards and soldiers. We're forty smugglers and a blind woman. We cannot fight whatever's under there, and we cannot outrun it, and we sure as hell cannot bury it again—we don't even know how, Oren, do we? Do any of us alive know how?"

He was right. That's the thing about Tam Rusk; he is usually right, and it has never once saved him. No one alive knew the rite of sealing. Sefa knew fragments. I knew less. The full working died with the founders, or rather it didn't die, it was *lost*, which is worse, because dead things stay put and lost things turn up in the wrong hands.

"The crown let the wards decay," Sefa said heavily. "You think they didn't know? You think three hundred years of our tithes and our quiet went unnoticed? They knew, boy. They decided we'd do the work and they'd spend the money elsewhere. Selling them the key is handing a drunk the family silver."

"Then sell it high," Tam said, and there were murmurs—there were actual murmurs of agreement, and I watched the guild fracture along a seam I had always known was there and always hoped was decorative.

But that argument belongs to another chapter of this miserable history, and I am an old man with limited ink, so let me move to the part that matters. The part that happened after, when the arguing had burned down to embers and people drifted into knots of quiet conversation, relearning each other's faces in the light of what they now knew. I caught Mara's eye across the hall and tilted my head toward my quarters, and she came, because she always comes when the guild calls, and that is precisely the problem.

* * *

In my quarters, with the door shut and the satchel on the table between us, I finally opened it.

The key sat in its wrapping of oilcloth like a heart in a chest, grey and veined and humming, and up close the sound was worse—words almost forming inside it, a voice remembering how to speak after three hundred years of disuse. Mara leaned over it and her breath fogged, though the room was warm, and the red marks on her palms had spread since the hall, faint traceries like frost ferns climbing a window.

"When," I said.

"When what?"

"Don't. When."

She looked at her hands as if noticing them for the first time. "On the pass," she said finally. "Right before the stars went out. I touched the ice where the sound was coming through, and it—" She flexed her fingers. "It didn't hurt. It felt like being *recognized*. Like something down there went through every face it knew and stopped on mine."

The key's humming deepened. I swear to you it did—I am not a fanciful man, but I swear the pitch bent toward her, a dog turning its head at a whistle.

"Well," I said, when I could speak steadily. "That settles one question."

"What question?"

I had rehearsed this speech for thirty years, the way Sefa and I rehearsed everything. It had been eloquent, in rehearsal. It had moved me to tears once, alone, at midnight, practicing to an empty chair. What came out instead was an old man's voice, cracked and plain:

"The courier line ends with me. I have no heir. I have a bad heart and worse knees, and I cannot carry that thing south through what's coming, and even if I could, the key answers to whoever it *recognizes*, and it appears to have made its choice." I turned the satchel toward her. "I'm sorry. And thank you. And don't open it until you must."

Mara stared at the stone. Then at me. Then at the stone again, and I watched her do the arithmetic—the debt to the guild, the love of it, the fury at it, all the ledgers she keeps behind those sharp grey eyes—and I saw her arrive at *yes* before she said it, the way you watch weather arrive before the rain.

"You're giving me the worst job in the world," she said.

"I'm giving you the only job in the world. There's a difference. Most jobs you can quit."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll carry it myself and die somewhere stupid in the passes, and the key will sit in a snowdrift humming until the mountain eats the world, and honestly, Mara, I'd deserve it for asking."

That got the ghost of a smile out of her, which was more than I deserved. She reached out and put her hand flat on the key—on the fragment, on three hundred years of inheritance, on the last hope of the lowlands sitting on a scarred table in a smuggler's bedroom—and the humming steadied. Settled. Went quiet the way a frightened animal goes quiet when someone finally picks it up.

"Oh," she said softly. "Oh, it's *scared*."

And that, more than anything, is what I want recorded in whatever history survives this: not that the thing beneath Thornfell was a monster, nor a god, nor any of the seven names the founders gave it. But that when a smuggler's daughter touched the key, the first thing she understood about the ancient terror buried under the world was that it was afraid.

She closed her fingers around it and lifted it out, and it weighed nothing in her hand. Inheritance is like that. It weighs nothing right up until it weighs everything.

"When do I leave?" she asked.

"Tomorrow. Before dawn, before the arguments start again—Tam will want the key, others will want it buried deeper, and I'm too old to win both fights. Take the southern tunnels. Sefa knows the way and she'll go with you, over her objections, which she'll voice at length." I hesitated, and then, because I owed her this much honesty at least: "Mara. One more thing. About the wards failing."

She waited, key in hand, frost-marks crawling gently up her wrists.

"Someone broke those stones," I said. "Deliberately. With knowledge only this guild possesses. Which means that somewhere between here and wherever the truth is, there is a traitor wearing a face we all love." I looked at her—really looked, memorizing her, the way you memorize a house you're leaving. "Trust no one completely. Not even me. *Especially* not me; I've lied to you longest and best."

Outside, the wind found a new note against the shutters, lower than before, and beneath it—if you held your breath, if you were old and listening the way I have spent a lifetime learning to listen—you could hear the mountain shifting its weight, like something enormous rolling over in a bed too small for it.

Mara pocketed the key and squared her shoulders, and I recognized the look on her face, because I had seen it before, on the face of every keeper who ever handed this burden down: the look of someone stepping forward to hold a door closed against the dark, knowing the door was already opening, hoping only to choose what comes through.

"Dawn, then," she said.

"Dawn," I agreed.

And in the dark under the mountain, patient as stone, the humming went on.
