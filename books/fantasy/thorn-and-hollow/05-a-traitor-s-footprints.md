## 5. A Traitor's Footprints

The morning after the stars went out, the guildhall smelled of tallow smoke and fear, which is to say it smelled like every other bad morning of my life, only thicker.

I'd slept two hours on a bench in the map room with my boots on and the sealing key wrapped in oilcloth against my ribs like a second heartbeat. Oren had given it to me at dusk—pressed it into my hands with the gravity of a man handing over a live coal—and then the whole world had gone dark and none of us had slept since, really. Sleep is a luxury for people whose mountains aren't breathing.

"Footprints," Sefa Ironhale said, by way of good morning.

She was crouched in the doorway of the ward-vault, the little stone room off the east corridor where we kept the maintenance tools nobody had ever explained to me before two days ago. She's a big woman, Sefa, built like a door that has decided never to open again, and when she crouches she does it the way other people kneel in church: total commitment.

"Whose footprints?" I asked.

"That," she said, "is the interesting part."

* * *

Here is what I knew by the time the sun should have risen but didn't, because the sky was a lid of featureless grey, as if some god had thrown a sheet over the furniture:

The wards hadn't failed. They'd been failed.

There's a difference, and the difference was written in ash. Each ward-stone along the border ridge had a feed-channel—a groove cut into the mountain itself, running from the deep galleries up to the stones, carrying whatever old power the guild had been feeding them for six hundred years. Oren showed me one, pried open with a crowbar while his hands shook. The channel wasn't empty because the power ran out. It was empty because someone had packed it with salt and grave-iron and sealed it with wax stamped with our own guild-mark.

"Somebody used our seal," Oren said. He said it very quietly, the way you'd say *somebody burned down my house*, which was more or less what he meant. "Somebody with access to the vault. Somebody who knew the maintenance schedule."

"Sixty people have access to the vault," I said.

"Fifty-eight now." He didn't look at me when he said it. "Two are dead on the high pass. Avalanches don't care about grief."

Fifty-eight suspects, then, minus the ones I'd stake my life on, minus Oren himself, minus Sefa, who was currently glaring at the wax seal like she could interrogate it. That still left a number too large to hold in your head without your hands starting to shake.

Tam Rusk found us there. Of course he did. Tam has a nose for trouble the way some men have a nose for rain, except Tam doesn't come in out of it—he stands in it with his arms crossed, waiting for it to explain itself.

"Well," he said, looking at the opened channel, the salt, the iron, "that's tidy work."

"Tidy," Sefa repeated, flat as a frozen lake.

"Whoever did it cared enough to do it properly." Tam crouched beside the channel, and I watched his face do something complicated—something between admiration and horror, arriving at neither. "You don't pack a feed-channel with grave-iron unless you know exactly what you're starving."

"You know a lot about it," Sefa said.

"I know a lot about everything. It's my worst quality." He stood, dusted his knees, and looked at Oren. "So. What's the plan, Guildmaster? Because I've been listening to the hall all night, and here's what I heard: fifty-eight people pretending they didn't hear the mountain move. Half of them want to run south. The other half want to run south faster."

"The plan," Oren said, "is that Mara carries the key to the crown at Cael Harrow, and we discover whether the crown remembers its debts."

And Tam laughed.

I want to be fair to him. I've had three days since to be fair to him, and I've managed it maybe twice. But in that moment, with the sky gone blind and the mountain muttering under our feet, his laugh was the ugliest sound in the world.

"Oh, we're telling the crown," he said. "That's rich. That's genuinely—" He stopped himself, pressed his knuckles to his mouth, started over. "Oren. The crown let the wards rot. You told us yourself—the royal stipend stopped coming eighty years ago. The last king's surveyors mapped our routes for tax purposes and never once asked why smugglers needed access to the deep galleries. The crown doesn't remember its debts because the crown never knew it had any, and if we walk into Cael Harrow with this key and this story, we're not creditors. We're witnesses. And you know what happens to witnesses."

"They get paid," I said.

"They get buried," Tam said. "Or they get used. Which, granted, is sometimes the same thing."

* * *

We argued about it in the map room, which is where the guild argues about everything, possibly because the maps make even our worst ideas look like they're going somewhere.

I won't give you the whole fight. Fights in families are mostly repetition with better insults. But the shape of it went like this:

Sefa wanted to reseal the wards ourselves, or die trying, and she said so in four words, which is her preferred rhetorical form. "Fix it. Or bury me."

Old Wenna, who keeps our ledgers and our grudges in the same book, wanted to take the key and vanish into the lowlands and let Thornfell keep its own secrets. "It slept six hundred years," she said. "It can sleep another six hundred while we decide what to do."

"It isn't sleeping," Oren said. "It's waking. There's a difference, and the difference is going to eat us."

And Tam stood at the end of the table with his hands flat on the map of the Hollow Road—all those tunnels we'd carried contraband through, silk and salt and stolen relics, the whole crooked circulatory system of our lives—and made the argument I'd been dreading, because I could feel it working on me even as I hated it.

"Sell it," he said. "Not hide it. Not hoard it. Sell it. We go to the crown not as beggars but as merchants, because that's what we are, that's what we've always been, and we name our price. Amnesty for every soul in this hall. Land. Coin. Grain for the winter that's coming, because look outside, Mara—look at the sky. The famine in the lowlands is already real, and it hasn't even started snowing yet."

"The crown will take the key and hang us for knowing," I said.

"Then we sell it somewhere else. Cassian Vole wants it. His people want it. Half the border villages that got moved when the wards were raised, their grandchildren are still angry, and anger keeps better than grain." He spread his hands. "Everyone wants what's under the mountain, Mara. Everyone but us. Why should we be the only ones who don't get to choose?"

"Because choosing is how it gets out," Sefa said.

"Because it's a cage," Wenna said, "and we're the jailers' kids, and jailers' kids always think the prison is theirs."

That silenced the room for a moment. Wenna has that gift. She says the true thing in the worst possible tone and everyone needs a minute.

But it was Tam I couldn't stop watching. Because he wasn't angry. That was the thing I kept circling back to, the splinter working under my skin. Tam Rusk, who once fought two river-pirates over an insult to my cooking, who taught me to read star-charts on the flat roof of this same hall, who has never in twenty years let me walk into danger alone—he stood there calm as a man reading a menu, arguing for selling our oldest secret to whoever paid best, and he looked *reasonable*. He looked like the sensible one. And the sensible one was wrong. I was almost sure he was wrong. Almost.

"How did you sleep?" I asked him, later. After. When the others had gone and the candles were drowning in their own wax.

He looked at me across the map table. "Badly. Same as everyone."

"You weren't in the hall when the stars went out."

Something flickered behind his eyes—fast, gone before I could name it. "I was checking the north route. Someone had to see if the passes were passable."

"Were they?"

"Avalanches took the high road. All of them. Simultaneous." He smiled, and it was his ordinary smile, warm and crooked, the one that had gotten us out of a hundred customs houses. "Someone was very busy that night, wasn't they?"

"Yes," I said. "Someone was."

I didn't say what I was thinking, which was: *the avalanches came after the wards fell, Tam. The mountain shrugged when the leash slipped. Unless someone set the charges first.* I didn't say it because saying it would make it real, and because I loved him, and because both of those facts were starting to feel like the same problem.

* * *

I went down to the ward-vault alone that evening, because I'm a fool who thinks solitude clarifies things, and stood among the tools of a trade I'd inherited without being told.

The vault is small. Six feet by eight, stone walls, a rack of crowbars and wax pots and brushes, and a shelf of ledger-books going back further than the crown of Cael Harrow itself. I pulled the newest one—maintenance logs, decades of them, entries in Oren's careful hand and his predecessor's and hers before him. Feed-channel inspections. Wax renewals. The quiet, patient housekeeping of a secret.

The last entry before the failure was dated nine days ago. Channel seven, cleared and resealed. Signed with the inspector's mark.

I know every mark in this guild. I've forged half of them.

The mark on that entry belonged to Tam Rusk.

Now—here's the thing. Here's the thing I turned over all night like a stone with something alive under it. Nine days ago, channel seven was due for inspection. Tam *was* assigned channel seven; I'd seen the roster myself. A man doing his honest work signs his honest mark. The sabotage channels—the ones packed with salt and iron—those had been done longer ago, months ago, done slowly and carefully, and signed with marks too, and I spent until dawn comparing them, and every single sabotaged channel bore Tam's signature.

Every one. Which meant either Tam Rusk had gutted the wards that held the world together, or somebody wanted me—or Oren, or Sefa—to believe he had.

Both possibilities broke something. I sat on the cold floor of that little vault with the ledger open on my knees and understood that whichever way the truth fell, the guild I loved was already dead. Either our brother had betrayed us, or someone had arranged things so that we could never trust each other again, and either way the wound was the same wound. Trust doesn't care whose knife made it.

I closed the book. I put it back exactly where it had been, spine aligned, because even in the middle of a betrayal you keep the house tidy—that's how the guild raised me.

Then I went to find Tam, and he wasn't in the hall, and he wasn't in the dormitory, and his bunk was stripped, and his kit was gone, and there was a note on his pillow in handwriting I'd have known anywhere, handwriting I'd trusted with my life a dozen times:

*Mara. Don't follow me south. Whatever they tell you about me, remember I was the one who never lied to you. —T.*

Which was, of course, a lie, or a truth, or both, and I stood there holding it while the starless dawn came grey through the window, and behind me Sefa said, "Tell me you didn't know," and I realized I was holding the ledger too, and that my face had already answered her question.

"He signed the sabotage logs," I said. "All of them."

Sefa looked at me for a long moment. Then she picked up her axe from where it leaned against the wall, and she said the four words I'd been dreading worse than the dark:

"Then we go now."

South, she meant. Down the Hollow Road, into the deep tunnels, toward the waking thing and the key burning against my ribs. Because if Tam was racing for the heart of the mountain—or racing to sell it—every hour we spent grieving was an hour he gained.

I took one last look around the guildhall. The benches, the maps, the roof where Tam taught me the names of stars that no longer existed. Fifty-six people upstairs pretending to sleep. A family with a knife in it, and no way to tell which hand held the handle.

"Lock the vault," I told Sefa. "Nobody goes near the galleries. And Sefa—if anyone asks where we've gone—"

"Nobody will ask," she said. "They'll be too busy asking each other questions with no good answers."

She was right, as usual. I shouldered my pack, settled the key against my ribs, and followed her down into the dark of the Hollow Road, chasing the footprints of the best friend I ever had, praying to gods I no longer believed in that I'd catch him before the mountain did.
