## 6. Down the Hollow Road

The key was smaller than it had any right to be.

I'd expected—after Oren's speech, after the way the old man's hands shook when he pressed it into mine—something grander. A sword. A crown. At minimum a very large book with warnings on every page. Instead it was a rod of black iron no longer than my forearm, cold enough to burn through my glove, and heavier than its size explained. It sat in the bottom of my pack like a stone with opinions.

"You'll want to stop checking on it," Sefa said.

"I'm not checking on it."

"You've opened that flap four times since we left the guildhall." She didn't look back at me. She never looked back at me; Sefa Ironhale walked forward the way other people breathed, automatically and without apology. "It doesn't get lighter from looking at it. Believe me. I've tried."

The tunnels behind the guildhall's wine cellar were older than the guildhall, older than the town, older, if you believed Sefa, than most of the lies people told about Thornfell. The Guild of the Hollow Road had kept them secret for six hundred years—not because of what they led to, we'd all assumed, but because smugglers who can't be followed can't be hanged. That was the story I'd grown up on. The roads were ours because we'd dug them, and we'd dug them because kings are thieves with better tailoring.

It turned out we'd dug them to keep something asleep. Every mile of tunnel I'd ever loved was a stitch in a bandage over a wound the size of a mountain.

So. That was a thing.

* * *

We went down through the Cold Gallery first, where the air tasted like the inside of a coin, and then past the Weeping Stair, where water had carved the steps into something like organ pipes. Sefa carried two lanterns and a third wrapped in oilcloth for emergencies, which in Sefa's vocabulary meant anything short of her own death. She'd been down here before. That much became obvious within the hour, and it should have been obvious to me years ago, and the fact that it wasn't made me angry in a slow, grinding way, like a millstone turning somewhere behind my ribs.

"How many times?" I asked.

"Times what?"

"Sefa."

She considered lying. I watched her consider it—the set of her shoulders, the way her jaw worked around whatever she almost said. Then she grunted, which from Sefa was a confession with witnesses.

"More than once," she said. "Less than a hundred."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one you're getting tonight, girl. Ask me again when there's more light and less mountain between us and the sky." She lifted her lantern higher. The flame leaned toward the dark ahead of us, eager as a dog on a scent. "Watch your footing. The floor gets dishonest from here."

The floor did get dishonest. The flagstones gave way to raw rock, then to rock that had been *worked*—cut in long, sweeping curves, as though the tunnel had been carved by water with a sense of architecture. Old carvings surfaced in the walls now and then: spirals, mostly, and lines of script in no alphabet I knew, worn shallow by centuries of draft. My fingers found one without my permission. The groove was warm.

Everything else down here was cold enough to ache. The groove was warm as skin.

I took my hand back and told myself I hadn't noticed.

* * *

We walked until my legs forgot there was such a thing as up. Sefa counted hours by a little brass watch she consulted like a priest consulting scripture, and at hour nine she called a halt in a chamber where the ceiling rose into darkness and someone, long ago, had built a hearth into the living rock. There were iron rings in the wall for tethering animals that hadn't been seen down here in generations. There was a stack of firewood, dry as bone, that nobody had any business finding this deep.

"Guild caches," Sefa said, before I could ask. "Every thirty miles. You think the Hollow Road ran itself?"

"I thought a lot of things."

"Aye." She struck flint to steel, and sparks caught, and the small fire threw our shadows huge against the curved walls. "That's the trouble with thinking. It's all built on top of something you don't know yet. Same as everything else."

I ate hardtack and dried plums and pretended both were food. The key sat against my spine where the pack rode, and I swear I could feel it—not heat exactly, more like attention. The way you feel eyes on the back of your neck in an empty street. I shifted so it wasn't touching me directly. It didn't help. Attention isn't a thing you can shrug off your shoulder blades.

"Tell me about him," I said. "Cassian Vole. Oren wouldn't say much. Just 'exiled war-priest' and 'don't let him touch the key,' which is the kind of instruction that makes a person curious."

Sefa fed a stick to the fire. Her face went orange and shadowed, half and half, which felt about right for everything she was likely to tell me.

"Cassian Vole was chaplain to the Third Border Legion, back when the crown still garrisoned Thornfell properly. Good soldier-priest. Believed in his god the way some men believe in gravity—totally, and without needing it proven daily." She turned the stick. "Then the legion got orders to put down the Ashfast rising—you won't have heard of it, the crown burned the records—and Vole looked at what he was asked to do and said no. Loudly. In front of everyone. They broke him out of the priesthood for it, cast him out of the order, and he walked into the mountains with a hundred followers and a grievance the size of a cathedral."

"The Ashfast rising. Those were the people the wards displaced." I'd read the phrase in Oren's papers, in a hand so old the ink had gone brown. *Displaced.* Such a tidy word for what must have been done.

"Some of them. Their grandchildren, anyway. The wards pushed the old settlements off the borderlands three hundred years back—flooded valleys, blighted fields, the usual quiet cruelty. Cassian preaches that the buried power belongs to them. That it was stolen and caged, and that opening it is justice." Sefa's mouth twisted. "He might even believe it. That's the dangerous part. A man who lies knows he's lying, and a man who knows he's lying can be caught. A man who believes—" she shook her head. "You can't argue a man out of a thing he never argued himself into."

"And he's digging toward it."

"He's been digging toward it for twenty years. Slowly. Carefully. He's mad, but he's patient mad, which is the worst kind." She looked at me across the fire, and her eyes were flat grey and very old. "And now the wards are down, and he doesn't have to dig anymore. He just has to walk."

* * *

We slept in shifts. I took second watch, sitting with my back against the warm carving—which I had decided, firmly, not to think about—and watching the fire eat itself down. Somewhere far above us, avalanches were sealing the passes. Somewhere farther, stars were missing from the sky like teeth pulled from a smile. Down here there was only rock and dark and the sound of Sefa breathing in the next chamber, steady as a tide.

The key hummed.

Not loudly. Not even really a sound—more a vibration I felt through the stone at my back, through my ribs, through the bones of my feet where they touched the floor. Like standing too close to a bell that hasn't been rung yet but remembers how.

"Stop it," I whispered, and felt immediately stupid, and felt immediately worse than stupid, because the humming paused. As if listening. As if it had heard.

I did not sleep after that. I sat with my knees drawn up and my arms around them and I thought about Tam Rusk, who had argued in the guildhall for selling the secret to the crown, whose footprints had been found in the ward-vault where no guild member's should have been, and I thought about how his voice had cracked when he said *the lowlands are starving, Mara,* and how part of me—that treacherous, reasonable part—had wanted to agree with him. And I thought about a war-priest I'd never met, digging toward a thing under the mountain because he believed it belonged to his people, and how part of me wanted to agree with *him.*

Everyone wanted the same door open. They just disagreed about who should walk through it first.

Only me and Sefa and a dead man's instructions stood on the side of keeping it shut. It seemed like thin odds for the world.

* * *

Hour eleven, by Sefa's watch, we reached the Fork of Names.

That's what the old maps called it—the ones Oren had burned after showing me, one time each, holding the pages over a candle the whole while as if the maps themselves might betray us. Three galleries split away from a vaulted junction: the Low Road running south toward Greyford, the Deep Road angling down and east toward the roots of the mountain, and a third passage, unmarked, that Sefa looked at for a long moment and then deliberately did not look at again.

"We take the Low Road," she said. "South. Fast. Out of the mountain entirely by tomorrow night, and then it's open country to Greyford."

"And the Deep Road goes—"

"Nowhere you need to see."

"Which is another way of saying toward it."

Sefa sighed the sigh of a woman who has raised children and smugglers and saw little difference between the trades. "Aye. Toward it. The heart-chamber's at the end of the Deep Road, or near enough, and before you ask—no, we're not going down there. Oren said carry the key south, and south is where we carry it, and the fewer times you stand over that thing's grave, the fewer chances it has to whisper up through the dirt at you."

"It already whispers."

She stopped with her boot half-lifted. Turned. Looked at me the way she looked at cargo manifests that didn't add up.

"When?" she said.

"In the night. Through the stone. Not words. More like—" I hunted for it. "Like hearing someone hum a song you used to know. You don't remember the words, but your mouth wants to move."

Sefa set her boot down slowly. She came close, took my chin in one calloused hand, and turned my face to the lantern light, examining me for frostbite, fever, madness—I couldn't tell which she feared most. Whatever she saw, she didn't like it.

"Palm," she said.

"What?"

"Show me your palms."

I showed her. She turned them over, thumbs pressing hard into the meat of them, hunting for marks. Found none. Some tension went out of her shoulders—not all of it. Some of it stayed, the way some cold stays in a room after the fire's lit.

"All right," she said. "All right. It's reaching because the wards are down and it's waking and you're carrying the one thing in the world shaped like a keyhole to its door. That's expected. That's in the old accounts." She released my chin. "But you tell me the moment it becomes words, Mara Delacroix. Not tomorrow. Not when it's convenient. The moment. Because there's a difference between a thing knocking on a door and a thing picking the lock, and by the time you notice the difference, it's usually too late to matter."

"And if it does become words?"

"Then I knock you unconscious and carry you and the key separately, and we both hate each other for a decade, and we live." She picked up her lantern. "Come on. South road. And girl—"

"Mm?"

"Don't talk to it. Even to tell it to stop. Especially to tell it to stop. Everything you say to a thing like that is a thread, and it pulls threads."

* * *

The Low Road was wider, older, prouder than the passages behind us. Vaulted ceilings. Flagstones fitted so tight a knife blade couldn't find the seams. Every fifty paces, a niche held the stub of a lamp bracket, and some of the niches held offerings—old ones, ancient ones: copper coins gone green, a child's clay bird, a ring of braided hair fused to black. People had come down here to leave gifts for something. For a long time. For longer than the guild had existed.

"For safe passage," Sefa said, catching me looking. "That's what the lowland folk believed lived under the mountain. A road-god. A keeper of ways. They'd send offerings down the old shafts and hope their carts didn't break an axle."

"And what actually lives under the mountain?"

"Ask me again in a year, if we're both alive, and I'll have had time to decide how honest to be."

We made good distance. Too good. By hour fourteen I'd stopped cataloguing my aches and started trusting them, the way you trust a familiar pain, and the rhythm of walking had gone dreamlike—step, breath, lantern-sway, step—when Sefa's fist came up and I walked into her pack.

She doused her lantern. We stood in absolute dark, and I learned that absolute dark underground is a different animal from night. Night has edges. This had none. It pressed on my eyes like wool.

"Listen," she breathed.

At first: nothing. Our own breathing. The tick of cooling lantern glass. Then, beneath all of it, arriving through the soles of my boots rather than my ears—a rhythm. Regular. Distant. A knocking, or a digging, or a drum.

*Tok. Tok. Tok.*

Iron on stone. Patient as erosion.

"Vole?" I whispered.

"Or his people. Or—" Sefa's hand found my arm in the dark and gripped hard. "Or something answering him. Move. Quiet. Off the main gallery—there's a maintenance crawl twenty paces back. We go around."

We went around. The crawl was exactly what it sounded like: a gap in the wall barely tall enough for a kneeling woman, floored with grit that bit through my trousers, roofed with stone that I could feel with the top of my skull like a held breath. We crawled. My pack scraped the ceiling and the key inside it sang—one clear, thin note, silent to the ear and deafening to the spine—and I clamped both hands over my mouth to keep from making any sound at all, because every instinct I owned suddenly insisted that the thing digging in the dark ahead of us had heard.

The knocking stopped.

In the silence that followed, I heard something worse: voices. Faint, warped by stone, but voices—several, murmuring, and one rising above the rest in cadence that no smuggler uses. It rose and fell like liturgy. Like a psalm. Like a man reading scripture to a congregation of shovels.

Then, closer than the voices, a new sound: a scrape, a shift, the groan of a prop being set. They were coming along the Low Road. Toward us. Behind us lay the junction and the way we'd come; ahead lay them; below us lay nothing but crawl and dark.

Sefa's hand closed on my wrist in the blackness. Two squeezes. Hold. One squeeze. Move. It was the old guild signal, older than either of us, taught to every child raised on the Hollow Road, and I clung to it like a rope over a cliff, because it meant the guild was still real, still here, still hers and mine, even if everything else it had ever told me was a lie dressed as a family trade.

We crawled. The liturgy faded behind us, then swelled again—closer? No. Parallel. They'd taken a branch gallery. They were digging *around* something, converging on something, and it took me a hundred body-lengths of crawling to understand what, and when I understood it I stopped breathing for three full seconds.

They weren't following the Low Road.

They were following *us.* Or rather—they were following the key. The same warmth I'd felt in the carved spirals, the same hum I'd felt through the floor. Cassian Vole didn't need scouts or maps. He had a compass, and I was carrying it on my back, and every step I took south drew him a line straight to the door he'd spent twenty years trying to open.

I squeezed Sefa's wrist twice. Hold. Then I did the bravest and stupidest thing I've done in a life well-stocked with both: I slid the pack off my shoulders, in the pitch dark, in a crawlspace wide as a coffin, and I passed it backward along my body to her.

Her grip on it told me she understood. Take it. Carry it apart from me. Break the line.

We moved again, faster, and the knocking resumed somewhere behind us—furious now, urgent, a tempo like a heartbeat climbing toward panic—and I crawled through the dark under the roots of the mountain with empty shoulders and burning arms and one thought repeating with every step:

*It knows where I am. It has always known where I am.*

Ahead, faint as memory, the crawl sloped downward.

South, Sefa had said. The Low Road runs south.

This was not south.
