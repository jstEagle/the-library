## 7. The Priest of Ash

I want it recorded—somewhere, by someone with better handwriting—that Sefa Ironhale snores like a rockslide having opinions.

We'd made camp in a side gallery off the Hollow Road, a pocket of the tunnel where the air still moved and the dark was the honest kind: total, but at least predictable. Two days below ground. My feet had filed a formal complaint. The key rode against my sternum on its cord, warm as a sleeping bird, and I'd stopped asking myself whether that was normal because the answer kept being *no* and I didn't like the follow-up questions.

"Sleep," Sefa had said, which is veteran for *sleep or I'll make you regret your life choices*. She took first watch. She always takes first watch. I think she doesn't trust anyone else's eyes, including mine, and after five days of knowing her I couldn't even argue.

So I slept, and dreamed—not of the buried thing, thank every star that used to exist—but of Oren Vey's hands on the ward-stone, teaching me the maintenance rite when I was nine. *You don't fight the stone's hum, girl. You join it.* Then the hum changed key, mid-dream, into something like a voice clearing its throat, and I woke because my body has excellent instincts and terrible timing.

The gallery was full of ash-light.

Not firelight. Ash-light—the grey-white glow of burned things still burning somewhere out of sight, reflected down the tunnels the way dawn comes down a valley. It moved along the walls in slow pulses, and in that light stood men and women in robes the colour of old snow, and every one of them held a censer swinging smoke that smelled of juniper and scorched iron.

And at their center, seated on my bedroll like he owned it and possibly the mountain too, was a man reading a small black book by the light of his own hand—which, I noted with the calm of someone dissociating professionally, appeared to be producing a faint grey luminescence from the fingertips.

"Well," I said, because I am famous for my composure under pressure. "That's my blanket."

"Mara Delacroix." He didn't look up from the book. His voice was low and even, a voice built for empty churches and long silences. "Thief of the Guild of the Hollow Road. Carrier of the key of Vhal-Morra. Daughter of nobody, heir to everything." He turned a page. "I've been looking forward to this conversation."

"Sefa," I said.

"Is asleep." He gestured, almost gently, and I saw why: a rope of woven ash-grey cord lay across Sefa's chest, rising and falling with her snoring. Whatever it was, it wasn't waking up. "She fought four of my people before they touched her. I respect that. So she sleeps until we're done talking. As will you, if you're sensible."

Four of his people. Before they touched her. I filed that away under *reasons Sefa Ironhale terrifies me*, a file already thick enough to stop a crossbow bolt.

"You're Vole," I said. "Cassian Vole. The exiled priest."

"The exile is their word." He finally looked up, and I'll give him this: he had the kind of face you'd believe in a painting of a saint, all hollows and patience, except for the eyes, which were the flat grey of ice over deep water. "Exiled implies I left something behind worth staying for."

"You left a war," Sefa said groggily, from the floor.

Everyone froze. Including me. Including, visibly, Cassian Vole.

Sefa sat up. The ash-rope slid off her like she'd been wearing it as a scarf for decoration. She looked around the gallery, counted censers, counted priests, did whatever arithmetic veterans do, and sighed the sigh of a woman whose nap has been ruined by amateurs.

"Sixteen of you," she said. "Two exits. One of us awake." She looked at me. "Mara. This is the priest?"

"This is the priest."

"The one who digs toward the heart-chamber with a hundred followers and a stolen reliquary?"

"That's the one."

"Hm." She reached for her boots. "I expected uglier."

* * *

Here is what I knew about Cassian Vole before that night, which was everything the guild's informants could scrape together and not much more:

He had been a war-priest of the Crown's Third Vigil, which is a polite phrase for a man who blessed siege engines. When the famine in the lowlands curdled into riots three winters back, Vole preached a sermon blaming the crown's neglect—and then, when his superiors ordered him to bless the soldiers who broke the riot instead, he'd turned his blessing around and consecrated the rioters' makeshift weapons. For that they stripped his orders, burned his name from the rolls, and put a price on him that would keep me in comfortable retirement.

He went north. He gathered the desperate. And somewhere in the last year he'd learned what the guild knew—or guessed it—and started digging toward Thornfell's roots with the fervor of a man who believes salvation has an address.

I had imagined a fanatic. Fanatics I know how to handle. You let them talk, you nod in the wrong places, you wait for the crack in the voice where the belief thins and the cruelty shows through, and then you run through the crack.

Cassian Vole did not crack. That was the problem.

* * *

They gave us tea.

I want to stress that part, because it undid me more than the ropes would have. His people brewed actual tea over a little spirit-lamp, in tin cups, and handed them to Sefa and me like we were guests at a funeral wake, and Cassian sat across from us on a supply crate with his little black book closed on his knee, and said:

"Ask me anything. Before I ask you anything. It's only fair—you've been lied to longer than I have."

"We haven't been lied to," I said.

"No?" He opened the book—not to read, just to rest his hand on it, the way some men touch a weapon. "Then tell me what the wards are for."

"Keeping things out," I said. "Border defense. Old kingdom magic, pre-Compact. Everyone knows—"

"Everyone knows what the crown taught them to know." His voice never rose. That was the unnerving thing; it stayed level as a frozen lake no matter what walked across it. "I spent two years in the archives at Carreth Vale before they expelled me. Do you know what I found? A tithe ledger from the founding era. Every border province paid a levy—not coin. People. Sent north into the passes every seventh year, 'for the mountains' keeping.' The records stop. No arrival logs. No return. Three thousand souls over sixty years, Mara Delacroix, marched into Thornfell and erased from history so cleanly that even the guild that maintains the stones forgot—or was made to forget—why."

The tea was hot in my hands and I suddenly couldn't feel it.

"That's a story," Sefa said. Her voice was careful. Too careful. I'd heard her lie to customs officers with less care than that.

"It's a ledger," Vole said. "Numbers don't preach. They just sit there, being true." He leaned forward. "Your guildmaster knows. Ask him why the sealing rite requires names. Ask him whose names are carved on the underside of the heart-chamber's door. Ask him—" and here, for the first time, something moved in his face, some grief-shaped thing swimming up under the ice, "—ask him what the wards actually do, and who they were built to hold, and whether the thing beneath the mountain was ever a monster at all, or just the last prisoner of a crime everyone agreed to stop calling one."

Silence. The censers swung. Somewhere far above, through miles of stone, the starless night pressed down like a thumb on a bruise.

"The power's waking anyway," I said, because someone had to say something and my mouth had always been faster than my judgment. "Whatever it is. Your digging helped that along, didn't it? If it wakes angry—"

"It wakes because it was wronged," Vole said simply. "Everything wronged does. You know this. You grew up among smugglers; you know what a debt is. The lowlands owe a debt three thousand people deep, plus interest compounded over centuries of winter." He spread his hands. "I'm not trying to unleash a catastrophe, Delacroix. I'm trying to pay one."

"And if paying it drowns everyone downstream?"

"Then the debt was always going to come due." He looked at me with those lake-water eyes, and there was no madness in them, no hunger, nothing I could brace against. Just certainty, worn smooth as a river stone. "The question was only ever whether we'd meet it honestly or be found hiding when it arrived. Your guild chose hiding for six hundred years. I'm choosing honesty."

"You're choosing digging," Sefa said. "With explosives. Toward a sealed power. That's not honesty, priest, that's archaeology with a death wish."

"I prefer *excavation with intent*."

Despite everything—despite the ropes, the ambush, the fact that his people had taken us while we slept—I felt the corner of my mouth twitch. traitorous muscle. He noticed. Of course he noticed.

"You find me funny," he said.

"I find you *reasonable*," I said, and hated how it came out. "That's worse. Cruel fanatics I can hate. Reasonable ones get inside."

Something flickered across his face—not triumph, exactly. Recognition. The look of a man who has just confirmed a diagnosis.

"Good," he said quietly. "Then you'll understand when I tell you I'm not going to kill you, or take the key, or stop you going south."

Sefa and I looked at each other.

"Run that past me again," she said.

"The key opens the seal from outside. From above. But the true binding—" he tapped the black book, "—was performed from within, by the bound themselves, willingly, at the end. The original rite. The one the guild forgot and the crown suppressed and the archives barely remember. I don't need your key, Delacroix. I need what you'll choose to do with it." He stood, unhurried, and the ash-light made his shadow climb the gallery wall like a second, taller priest. "Take her south," he told his people, meaning me. "Feed them first. If the guild's courier dies of bad cooking before she makes her choice, the choice means nothing."

"You're letting us go," I said flatly. "Just like that."

"I'm letting you go *curious*." He picked up his book. "Hate me if it helps. But carry the question south with the key. Whose mountain is this? Who paid, and who collected? And when the thing beneath asks you for justice—" he paused at the edge of the ash-light, half in shadow, and for one unguarded moment he looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with digging, "—it will ask *you*, courier. Not me. Not the guild. Not the crown. The key chooses who hears it. I've seen its mark on you since the pass. You've been hearing it for days, haven't you? In dreams?"

My silence answered him. My silence, and probably my face, and probably the way my hand crept up without permission to touch the cord at my throat.

He nodded once, like a physician confirming the obvious, and walked into the dark, and his people folded around him like smoke closing over a doused flame, and then it was just Sefa and me and two cups of tea cooling in a gallery full of the smell of juniper and burnt iron.

* * *

We didn't speak for an hour. We walked. South, deeper, following the Road's old markers—guild chisel-marks, a century of them, my whole childhood written in stone—and the key beat against my sternum like a second heart that had opinions.

It was Sefa who finally stopped, in a widening where the Road crossed an underground stream, and said: "He was telling the truth."

"Don't."

"Some of it. Maybe most of it. I've heard forty years of liars, girl. That man believed every word, and belief leaves a different sound than lies." She crouched by the stream, splashed water on her face. "Which doesn't mean he's right. Believed things can be wrong. But it means we can't just—walk past it."

"The tithe ledger could be forged. Could be misread. Could be—"

"Mara." She looked up at me, water dripping off her chin, and in the key's faint warmth-light her face was older than the mountain. "I've been guild thirty years. You think I never wondered why the maintenance rite needed *names*? Why we whispered them? Why the old master flinched every seventh year, like a calendar lived under his skin?" She wiped her face roughly. "I wondered. I just never asked. Asking felt like pulling a thread that held up the whole coat. And it's cold in the lowlands, girl. You need your coat."

The stream talked to itself in the dark. The key warmed against my skin, patient as a held breath.

"He wants me to doubt," I said at last. "That's the weapon. Oren warned me—'he'll offer you truth, girl, because truth cuts deeper than any blade he owns.'"

"Oren say anything else?"

"He said the key chooses who hears it." I laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "He said I've been hearing it for days. And the worst part, Sefa—the worst part isn't that he's right about the dreams. It's that when he talked about debts, about the lowlands owing something—" I pressed my palm flat against the key through my shirt, warm, warm, *too* warm, "—some part of me agreed. Some part that remembers every winter Greyford buried children. Some part that thinks: what if the famine isn't weather? What if it's *interest*?"

Sefa stood. She was quiet a long moment, and when she spoke her voice had gone soft, which from Sefa Ironhale is more alarming than shouting.

"When we get south," she said, "you ask Oren. Straight out. No riddles, no ceremony. You make him answer for every name and every seventh year. And whatever he says—" she gripped my shoulder, iron fingers, iron woman, "—we decide together. Not the priest. Not the guild. Not the thing in your dreams. Us. Understood?"

"Understood," I said.

We walked on. And behind us, faint, from galleries we'd already left, I could swear I heard singing—low, level, certain, a hymn with no joy in it and no hatred either, just the sound of people marching toward what they believed was owed them.

And ahead, south, where the Road bent toward the valleys and the world of refugees and frozen rivers and a crown that had let the wards rot—

Ahead, the key pulsed. Once. Twice.

Like something knocking politely at a door I hadn't known I was keeping.

"Girl," Sefa said quietly, watching my face. "What did it just say?"

I told her the truth, because after Cassian Vole, lying felt like a courtesy I could no longer afford:

"It asked me whose side I'm on."
