## 8. Refuge at Greyford

I had been a healer in Greyford for eleven years, and in that time I had learned three things with absolute certainty: fever breaks or it doesn't, children lie about how they got hurt, and the world does not end quietly.

The night the stars went out, all three of those certainties held true at once, which is more than most bad nights can say.

* * *

The dark came down like a lid.

I was sitting up with old Marta Brenner's lungs — a losing argument I'd been having for two winters — when the light through her shutters simply stopped. Not dimmed. Stopped. I remember thinking the shutter had blown closed, and getting up to check, and standing in Marta's doorway like an idiot while she wheezed behind me and the sky outside was black from horizon to horizon. No moon. No stars. Nothing. As if someone had thrown a cloth over the whole bowl of heaven and tucked in the edges.

Marta asked what was wrong. I told her nothing. That was the first lie of many I'd tell that winter, and honestly one of the kinder ones.

By morning, half of Greyford had decided it was an eclipse, a quarter had decided it was punishment, and the remainder — my people, the sensible ones — had decided it was weather and gotten on with chopping wood. I admired them. I even envied them. But I'm a healer; I don't get to believe things are fine just because believing would be convenient. I look at symptoms. And the symptom list, as it accumulated over the following days, read like something out of a plague-year ledger written by a drunk:

Rivers freezing mid-current, solid as road-stone, in a valley where the Greywater hadn't iced before midwinter in living memory.

Crops dying in the fields overnight — not frost-bitten, *dead*, black and brittle as if the life had been drawn out through the roots.

Birds falling. Not flying south. Falling.

And cold. A cold that didn't behave like cold. Cold that came in through walls that should have held it out, that found the gap under every door, that sat in the marrow of you no matter how many layers you piled on.

"You're frowning," said Tam's grandmother — no, wait, that's not right, let me start again. "You're frowning," said Widow Ashka, who was ninety if she was a day and had survived three husbands, two floods, and one unfortunate incident involving a bull, "and frowning never once fixed anything."

"Frowning is diagnostic," I told her, wrapping her hands in wool. "It means I'm thinking."

"Thinking's overrated. My second husband thought constantly. Died of it." She flexed her fingers inside the wraps and squinted at me with her one good eye. "You felt it too, didn't you. The night the sky went out."

I didn't answer, which was answer enough. Because yes. Under the terror of it, under the animal scream of a world gone blind, there had been something else — a feeling in the ground, in the soles of my feet, like a great bell struck somewhere far below and still ringing. The whole valley had felt it. Half of them wouldn't admit it. The other half were already calling it the voice of whatever god they'd stopped praying to years ago.

"It's coming from the mountains," Ashka said. "North. Whatever it is." She patted my arm with a hand like a bundle of kindling. "You'll want to be ready. You always are. It's your worst quality."

* * *

They started arriving on the fourth day.

Refugees, I mean. From the high valleys, from the passes, from the little huddled villages that clung to Thornfell's skirts like burrs. They came down the frozen riverbed on sledges and on foot, and they came in worse shape than any winter migration I'd seen, because this wasn't migration. Migration has a plan. This was flight.

I turned the temple into a hospital by noon and the granary into a ward by evening, and by the time the lanterns needed lighting — though God knew why we bothered, the dark outside was total either way — I had forty-one patients and two assistants and a headache that felt structural.

Frostbite, mostly. Exposure. Broken bones from the ice. A baby with a fever who scared me more than everything else combined, because babies don't lie about how they feel, and hers said the cold was inside her.

It was somewhere past midnight — although midnight had become a technicality, since the sky couldn't tell us anymore — when the smuggler walked in.

I knew she was a smuggler the way you know rain is coming: the boots, the way she moved like the floor might owe her money, the sword worn easy rather than proud. She was lean and dark-eyed and had the particular expression of someone carrying something heavy who wants very much for you not to ask about it. Behind her came a grey-haired woman built like a door — a soldier's build, a veteran's scars — and between them they half-carried, half-dragged a boy of maybe fifteen whose left foot was a ruin of black frostbite.

"He put it in the river," the smuggler said, without preamble, without hello, as if we were mid-conversation. "Crossing the ice. It cracked. He went in to his knee. Three days ago."

"Three days," I repeated.

"We were moving fast. There wasn't—"

"Three *days*." I was already kneeling by the boy, peeling back the filthy wrapping he'd bound it with. The smell hit me. I'd smelled it enough times to name it without looking: dead flesh going sour. The toes were beyond saving. Possibly the foot. Possibly more than the foot, if the rot had climbed.

"There wasn't time," the smuggler said again, and there was something raw under the flatness of it. Guilt. Good, I thought, uncharitably. Guilt means there's a conscience in there somewhere to work with.

"Name?" I asked the boy.

"T-Torren," he chattered.

"Torren. Look at me. Not at her, at me." He looked. His eyes were glassy with fever but he held them on mine, and I gave him the truth straight, because I've found frightened people can carry almost anything if you hand it to them level. "Your foot is badly hurt. Some of it is already gone. I'm going to do everything I can to save the rest, but I need you to hold still and trust me, and I need you to fight. Can you fight?"

He nodded. Small, but real.

"Good." I stood and turned to the smuggler. "You. Boiling water, that cauldron, now. Door-woman—" the veteran raised an eyebrow at the title, and I decided I liked her immediately, "—there's a still behind the temple, third house, blue shutters, tell them Ilse sent me and they'll give you the good spirits. All of it. Go."

And to my lasting surprise, they went. No argument, no haggling, none of the territorial nonsense soldiers and smugglers both like to perform. The smuggler just rolled up her sleeves and worked the bellows, and the veteran marched out into the killing dark like it was a chore she'd done before, and I took off the boy's boot and began the ugly work.

I won't describe the amputation. Not because it was terrible — it was, they always are — but because the terrible part isn't the part that matters. What matters is that Torren screamed, and then stopped screaming, and then slept, alive, with eleven toes instead of ten and one fewer foot, and the smuggler sat beside him until dawn mopping his brow with water she'd boiled herself, and never once said a word about being anywhere else.

Her name was Mara Delacroix. I learned it at dawn, when I finally made her stop and drink something.

"Mara," I said. "That's a lowlands name."

"Born there. Raised elsewhere."

"Elsewhere being?"

"The north." She drank. Her hands were steady but her eyes had the hollowed look of someone who hadn't slept in a week, and I recognized it because I see it in mirrors. "You've got a lot of people here."

"And more coming. You brought news from the passes, I assume. Since you came *from* the passes, which nobody does anymore unless they're fleeing or insane."

"Both, probably." She set down the cup. For a moment I thought she'd give me nothing — smugglers are professional givers-of-nothing, I've dealt with their kind through Greyford for years, half our medicine comes through their roads and half our trouble too — and then something in her face shifted, some decision made and unmade and made again. "The wards failed. On the border. All of them, at once. The stars didn't go out on their own. Something happened in the mountains, and it's not finished happening."

I waited for the rest.

"That's it?" I said. "That's the whole report? 'Something happened'?"

"I don't know more than that." Which was a lie. I could see it was a lie; I've spent twenty years watching faces for the flicker that says *there's more and I'm not saying it*. But I also saw why she lied, or thought I did — because the truth, whatever it was, was a load meant for bigger shoulders than a snowbound town full of refugees.

So I let it go. Then. I want that on the record. I *let it go*. Like a fool.

* * *

Over the next six days, Greyford taught me what the abstract version of the end of the world looks like when you shave off the poetry.

It looks like ration lines. It looks like the miller weeping because the millrace froze solid and no amount of prying would crack it, and the grain stores measured in weeks instead of seasons. It looks like a mother asking you — me, specifically, because I'm the one with the ledger of who's strong enough — whether her father could survive the walk south to her sister's in Lowmark, and me having to say no, and watching her decide to leave him anyway, and understanding it, and hating everyone involved including myself.

It looks like Sefa Ironhale — the door-woman, the veteran — standing at the edge of town each morning facing north, utterly still, like a compass needle that had found its pole and didn't like the reading.

And it looks like Mara Delacroix working herself half to death in my wards, hauling water, splitting wood, holding down panicked men twice her size during the seizures the deep cold brought, and all the while carrying that invisible heavy thing, and all the while lying beautifully with her eyes whenever anyone asked what was coming out of the mountains.

On the sixth day, the answers arrived on their own, the way answers do.

* * *

They came at dusk — such dusk as we had, the sun a bruise behind the southern hills — a fresh wave of refugees, and among them a man with a broken arm who talked too much, the way men with broken arms sometimes do, pain loosening their tongues. He'd come down from the high valley near the old guild roads.

"Saw them," he told the room, between my tugs at his splint. "Saw the guild folk running. Guildhall's done, they say. Soldiers on the passes, crown men everywhere, hunting somebody. And the priest — the ash-priest, Cassian Vole, him and his white-robes — they're digging. Up at the mountain gate. Digging into the mountain itself, with fire and engines, like they mean to open it."

"Open what?" someone asked.

"How should I know? Something buried. Something the guild was hiding, maybe. Maybe the thing that killed the stars." He winced as I tied off the splint. "Gentle, woman."

"Gentle costs extra," I said, and got the laugh I wanted, because a room that laughs holds together another day.

But I was watching Mara. Everyone was pretending not to watch Mara, and everyone was watching Mara. She had gone very still by the window, and the look on her face was the look I'd seen on her the first night, the guilt-look, except deeper now, calcified. The word *guildhall* had landed on her like a stone on glass.

Later — much later, in the temple, among the sleeping rows — I found her sitting with Torren, who was feverish but fighting, exactly as instructed. She was telling him a story, low, some smuggler's tale about a bridge that only existed on Tuesdays. He was smiling. I stood in the dark and watched her do it, and I understood suddenly and completely that this woman was going to walk back north into whatever was waiting there, and that she'd take the guilt with her like luggage, and that she'd never ask anyone's permission or forgiveness.

"You could stay," I said.

She didn't jump. I'll give her that; she heard me coming and pretended she hadn't, which is its own kind of courtesy. "Stay where? Here?"

"Here. Greyford needs hands. Good ones. Yours are good." I sat down across from her. "You've been useful, Delacroix. Don't fish for compliments, I'll deny them later. But useful. And this—" I gestured at the dark, the cold, the rows of breath and misery, "—this is only going to get worse. Whatever's in the mountain, it's killing my town from a hundred miles away. People are going to die of it all winter, ordinary people, and no one will ever write a song about a single one of them. You could help. Here. Where it counts in units of actual human beings."

She was quiet a long moment. Torren had drifted off. Somewhere outside, the wind found the gap under the temple door and moaned through it, low and vast, and I swear on my grandmother's grave it sounded less like wind every day and more like the bell I'd felt under my feet that first night.

"It doesn't stop here," Mara said finally. "Whatever it is. The cold, the dark. It doesn't respect town limits, healer. I've seen what's under the mountain. Or — I've seen the door of it. And I know who's trying to open it, and I know what happens if they do, and staying here would just mean watching it arrive on schedule." She looked at me, and her eyes in the lantern-light were tired and fierce and honest for the first time since she'd walked in. "I have to go back. I have a thing to do and it's the only thing that helps, even if I can't prove it, even if it sounds mad."

"The key," I said.

Her whole body went rigid. "Who told you—"

"Nobody told me anything. You talk in your sleep. Badly. You said 'the key' four times last night and 'Oren' twice, and you cried once, which I mention only to embarrass you." I leaned forward. "I'm a healer, Delacroix. People lie to me professionally, and I've gotten good at the truth underneath. You're carrying something that matters, north, into a war between a priest, a crown, and whatever your guild was guarding. And you're going to do it with one grumpy veteran and a boy with nine toes."

"Ten. We saved five."

"Don't interrupt. My point is, you're going to die of arithmetic. Two against everyone is bad math." I stood, and pulled my pack from under the bench — the one I'd packed three days ago, in the dark, after the man with the broken arm mentioned the digging, because apparently I'd known before I let myself know — and dropped it in her lap. "Which is why you'll be taking a healer."

She stared at the pack. Then at me. "You don't know what you're signing up for."

"No. Neither do you. Difference is, I've spent twenty years learning that not-knowing is not a reason to sit down." I ticked it off on my fingers. "I can't fight, I complain constantly, and I steal medical supplies from the rich, which you'll approve of. But I can keep your people alive, I know these mountains better than any crown soldier, and every soul in this valley knows me, which means doors open and lies get believed. Also," I added, because fair was fair, "you saved Torren's life getting him here in time. Barely. Another day and I'd have taken the leg. So consider us even, and consider me coming."

For a moment she just looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic herself — the same instinct that makes smugglers count exits — weighing me, finding the cost, finding it acceptable.

"Sefa will object," she said.

"Sefa can object to my face. I've been yelled at by dying men; a veteran will be a rest cure."

Something cracked in her face then — not a break, a thaw, the smallest possible thaw — and she almost smiled. "Why?" she asked. "Really. Why come?"

And I looked north, at the wall of perfect black where the mountains should have been, at the place where the bell-sound lived, and I told her the truth, which was:

"Because I've spent my whole life patching what breaks after it breaks. And something up there is breaking the whole world, slowly, and I am *tired*, Delacroix, of bandages. I want to get to the wound."

Outside, the wind moaned through the door-gap, long and low and patient, like something below the ice turning over in its sleep.

Mara picked up my pack and slung it over her shoulder.

"We leave at first light," she said. "If we can find first light."
