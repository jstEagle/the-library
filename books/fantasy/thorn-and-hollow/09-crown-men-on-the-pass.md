## 9. Crown Men on the Pass

The official road out of Greyford was the first honest road I'd walked in years, and I hated it immediately.

Honest roads are wide. Honest roads are graded and graveled and marked with crown-stones every league, each one stamped with a sunburst that's supposed to make you feel watched over. What they actually do is tell anyone who's looking exactly where you are. Smugglers learn to read roads like faces, and this one had the flat, open expression of a man about to lie to you.

"Could cut north through the fells," Sefa said beside me. She said it quietly, which for Sefa was practically a whisper. "Add two days."

"We don't have two days." I kept my eyes on the pass ahead, where the road climbed between shoulders of black rock still wearing last week's snow. "The key gets heavier every time I sleep. Don't ask me how I know that. Ask it."

Ilse, walking behind us with her healer's pack and her permanent expression of someone smelling something unpleasant, snorted. "It talks to you in your sleep and you're carrying it *toward* the mountain. Remarkable judgment all around."

"It doesn't talk. It—" I flexed my fingers inside my gloves. The frost-burns had faded to pink scars that ached when the wind came off the peaks. "It hums. Like a ward-stone the night before it fails."

Nobody had an answer to that, so we walked. Behind us Greyford's smoke thinned into the grey sky, and ahead of us the pass opened its throat.

I want to say I saw them coming. I want to tell you I read the road like a face and knew. But the truth is they were already there when we rounded the shoulder of the fell—twelve riders in crown blue, drawn up across the road with the patient confidence of men who have never once been told no by anything smaller than an army.

* * *

Their captain rode forward alone. Young, but not green—young the way a blade is young, all edge and no rust. His armor was good steel, his horse was better than any horse has a right to be on a mountain road, and his smile was the kind they teach you in officers' school: warm as a ledger.

"Mara Delacroix." He said it like he'd been practicing. "Captain Aldous Ren. Fourth Crown Lancers, detached to the Border Restoration Commission."

"That's a lot of words," I said. "None of them explain why twelve lancers are blocking a public road."

"Public roads exist because the crown maintains them." He spread his hands, magnanimous. "And we're here to offer you protection. You've had a difficult journey, Mistress Delacroix. Starless nights. Frozen passes. Bandits, priests, worse. The crown extends its hand."

"And what does the crown want in return for its hand?"

"The item you carry south from Thornfell. The guildmaster's key." He said it easily, the way you'd ask to borrow a cup of sugar. "Hand it over, and you'll have escort, provisions, royal pardons for—well." His eyes flicked over our company, cataloguing: a one-armed veteran smuggler, a valley healer with mud on her skirts, three of Oren's people who'd come down from the guildhall with faces like closed doors. "For whatever histories might need pardoning. The Commission is not interested in the past. Only in stability."

Behind me, Sefa shifted her weight. I didn't look at her. Looking at Sefa when she shifted her weight was how you got trampled.

Here's the thing about being raised by smugglers: you learn early that when a man offers you something for free, the price is just hiding somewhere you haven't checked yet. So I smiled back at Captain Ren, warm as a ledger myself, and asked the question I already knew the answer to.

"The Border Restoration Commission. When was it founded?"

His smile held. "Three years ago."

"And the wards along the border—the old stones, the ones your commission is named after restoring. How long has the crown known about them?"

The pause was small. A half-breath. But I've spent my life reading pauses the way priests read scripture, and that one had whole chapters in it.

"I'm sure I couldn't speak to—"

"You could," I said. "You absolutely could. Because here's what I know, Captain. Those wards were maintained in secret for four hundred years by a smuggling guild too poor to afford boots for all its children. And the crown—the crown that built this lovely road, that posts these lovely stones, that collects taxes from every valley under those wards' shadow—the crown either never noticed four hundred years of secret maintenance, which makes it blind, or it noticed and did nothing, which makes it something else entirely." I let that sit. "Which is it?"

Ren's smile finally cracked—not into anger, but into honesty, which was somehow worse. "The crown knew," he said quietly. "The archives knew. There were always more urgent fires. Famine, war, succession. The wards held. They'd always held. Why spend gold on a wall that never falls?"

"Because walls fall," Ilse said behind me, sharp as a suture. "That's rather the entire lesson of the last month, isn't it?"

"And now the crown sends twelve lancers to collect the key," I went on, "from the one person carrying it toward the only place it can be used. Not toward the capital. Toward the mountain. Funny thing, that. Almost like the Commission doesn't want the key restored to crown custody at all. Almost like somebody wants it somewhere it can't ever be used again—or somewhere it can."

Something moved behind Ren's eyes. Calculation, or conscience; from that distance, same shape.

"Mistress Delacroix. I'm offering you the best outcome available to you. Escort. Pardon. A pension, if you want one. Refuse, and I'm required to take the key by authority of the Crown Writ, and I'd very much prefer not to hurt anyone today."

"There it is," I said. "There's the price. It was hiding right where I expected."

I looked back at my people. Sefa had her axe loose in its loop, casual as breathing. Ilse had put herself bodily in front of little Fen, one of Oren's runners, who was fifteen and white as the snowpack. Old Marta had her knife out and was smiling, which from Marta meant the opposite of what it usually means.

Every institution I'd ever feared was lined up against us: the crown, the law, the writ, the road itself. And every one of them had known. Had known about the wards, known about the thing beneath Thornfell, known for generations, and had spent those generations letting a guild of thieves do the watching for free.

"You know what the funny part is, Captain?" I said. "I spent thirty years assuming the law was coming for me someday. Built my whole life around dodging it. And now the law finally shows up, and it wants the one thing I'd burn before I handed over." I pulled my gloves off. My scarred palms were cold in the mountain air. "So no. We keep walking. You can write that in your report however you like."

Ren sighed. He actually sighed, like a man disappointed in a business partner. "Mistress Delacroix. Please don't make this difficult."

"Difficult," Sefa repeated, and laughed once, low. "Girl's been making things difficult since she was six. It's her one gift."

Then the leftmost rider made the mistake.

He was the youngest of them—I saw that later, in the ugly accounting afterward—and when Sefa's hand drifted near her axe he panicked, or he was eager, or his officer's careful theater simply broke against the reality of a one-armed woman who'd buried gods. He drew. He shouted something about resisting arrest. And he put a crossbow bolt into the space where Sefa's chest had been a half-second earlier, because Sefa Ironhale does not die to crossbows, she dies to old age if the world is very lucky, and it wasn't.

What happened next took eleven seconds. I counted, afterward, because counting was the only way I could hold it.

Sefa's axe took the rider's weapon arm off at the elbow. Marta's knife opened the throat of the lancer wheeling behind her. Fen—fifteen-year-old Fen, who I'd promised Oren I'd keep safe—drove a smuggler's pick into a horse's flank and brought the whole animal and its rider down screaming. Ilse dragged Fen backward by the collar, swearing in three dialects. I got the key's case off my shoulder and swung it like the blunt instrument it suddenly was, and caught a lance thrust aside, and then Ren was in front of me with his sword drawn and his face gone from ledger-warm to stone.

"Last chance," he said.

"You keep saying that like it's worked on someone."

We fought. I won't dress it up. I've fought customs men and rival crews and one memorable bear, and none of it was glorious; fighting is mostly terror and arithmetic. Ren was better than me with a blade—he was better than most people—but I've never fought fair in my life, and while he was managing my sword I put my boot on the ice-slick crown-stone behind him and let gravity file its complaint. He went down hard. I put my sword-point at the gap in his gorget and stood there shaking, and he lay there looking up at me with snow melting into his eyes.

"Do it," he said. "Or move. One or the other."

Around us, the skirmish had burned itself out. Two lancers dead, three wounded, their horses scattered up the road. On our side: Marta bleeding from a scalp wound, Fen crying without making any sound, Sefa standing over the boy whose arm she'd taken, her chest heaving.

"Bind the wounds," I heard myself say. "All of them. Theirs too."

Ilse stared at me. "*Theirs?*"

"They're not the enemy, Ils. They're the crown's hands. Cut off the hands, fine. You don't leave them to freeze." I crouched over Ren. "Your Commission sent twelve men to take a key it doesn't understand from the only person trying to save the country it governs. Somebody above you decided the mountain staying shut matters less than the mountain staying *theirs*. Go home. Tell them we're off the roads. Tell them whatever keeps you alive."

He searched my face for a long moment. Whatever he found there made him nod, slowly, like a man signing something.

"You knew," he said. It wasn't a question. "About what's under there. That's why you're going *toward* it."

"Four hundred years of secrets, Captain. The guild kept theirs. Your masters kept theirs. Mine are just heavier." I stood, sheathed my sword, and picked the key back up. It hummed against my hip like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. "Get your people off this pass before dark. Things walk up here at night now. You'll want a fire, and you'll want it big."

* * *

We left the road at the next switchback, where an old smugglers' trail dropped off the crown-way like a secret leaving a mouth. Sefa led, because Sefa knew these cuts from before the crown paved over half of them, and the rest of us followed single-file down into a country of scree and pine and silence.

Fen cried properly, finally, once we'd stopped. Ilse held him and glared at me over his head, daring me to comment. I didn't. There was nothing to say that wasn't either a lie or a wound.

"He was fifteen," Ilse said eventually, when Fen had gone quiet against her shoulder. "The soldier. The one whose arm—"

"I know."

"Do you? Because you gave orders like a general and then walked away like a courier, and I'm trying to decide which one of those is the mask."

I stopped walking. Below us, the trail bent through the pines, and beyond the pines the lowlands spread out grey and starving under a sky that hadn't shown a star in six weeks. Somewhere down there, Tam Rusk was doing whatever Tam Rusk did now. Somewhere behind us, the guildhall I'd grown up in was holding its breath around an empty chair.

"Both," I said. "They're both masks. Underneath there's a girl from the Hollow Road who just learned the crown knew about the wards the whole time and let them rot because it was cheaper. She's furious. She's so furious she could burn the capital down. And she can't, because the key hums and the winter deepens and there are maybe eight people in the world she'd trust with her life, and one of them is fifteen and just learned what blood smells like off a sword." I started walking again. "So yes, Ils. Both masks. Same face underneath."

She caught up in three strides. For a while neither of us spoke, just boots on scree and wind in the pines.

"The captain let you go," she said at last. "Really. He had riders left. He could have pressed."

"He could have."

"Why?"

I turned the question over, and found the answer sitting in my stomach like a swallowed stone. "Because he wanted us to run," I said. "Because somewhere in that pretty armor is a man who read the same archives I'm guessing he wasn't supposed to read, and he'd rather the key reach the mountain than reach his own Commission. He just couldn't say it out loud." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "That's the joke, Ils. The only honest man I've met in a month was the one pointing a sword at me."

Ahead, Sefa raised her fist. Halt. She was staring down-trail at something, and her whole body had gone stiff in a way I'd learned to dread.

"What is it?" I whispered.

She pointed. Far below, at the tree line where the trail met the valley, a thread of smoke rose—thin, deliberate, black against the grey. Not a chimney's smoke. A signal fire. And arranged around it, small with distance but unmistakable, figures were waiting on the trail. Not soldiers. No blue, no horses.

One of them raised an arm and waved, slow and friendly, the way you wave at family.

Sefa spat. "That's guild-sign," she growled. "Old sign. Pre-Oren." Her jaw worked. "That's a parley call, girl. Somebody down there knows the signs, and knows we'd know 'em too."

I looked at the waving figure, and the humming key against my hip, and the long dark falling up the valley behind us, and felt the splinter Tam had lodged in my heart shift another inch.

"Well," I said. "Let's go see what family wants."
