## 10. Tam's Bargain

I have always been good at waiting. It's the smuggler's first virtue, really—patience dressed up as laziness. You wait for the patrol to pass. You wait for the moon to cloud over. You wait three years in a lowland town eating famine bread and watching children's ribs count themselves through their shirts, and then you stop waiting, because patience is only a virtue when there is something worth waiting *for*.

So let me tell you how I stopped.

Her name was Wren. She was seven. She was not my daughter—I want that on the record, carved somewhere, because I know what Mara will say when she hears this story, and she will say *you did it for a child you barely knew*, and that will be true, and it will also be beside the point. Wren was the daughter of the woman who kept the boarding house where I lodged in Millbrook, down in the lowlands, where the grain had failed three years running and the crown's relief wagons came twice a season and left half-empty because the roads north were "unsafe." Unsafe. The roads were safe as scripture. The roads had been safe for four hundred years because my guild kept them so, quietly, at night, with oil and chalk and songs nobody sang anymore.

Wren died in the second week of Frostmoon, two winters ago. Not dramatically. No fever, no accident. She just got smaller and smaller until one morning her mother couldn't wake her, and I stood in the doorway of that little room with my hat in my hands like a man at a funeral, which is what I was, and I thought: *the wards are humming a hundred miles north of here, and nobody up there is hungry.*

That was the splinter. Everything after was just me pushing it deeper.

* * *

You should understand something about the Hollow Road before you judge me—and you will judge me, everyone does eventually, it's the one trade that never fails. The Guild wasn't a family because we loved each other. We did love each other, God knows, in the way soldiers love each other, which is to say fiercely and inconveniently and mostly without saying it. But the guild was a family the way a wall is a family: every stone holding up every other stone, and nobody asking what the wall was *for*.

Oren Vey knew what it was for. Oren has known since he was younger than Mara is now, and he carried that knowledge the way he carried everything—silently, with bad posture, and a flask he pretended we didn't know about. When I came back from the lowlands that spring and asked him, straight out, over cards, "What are the ward-stones actually keeping in?" he looked at me for a long moment and said, "Tam. Deal the cards."

That was answer enough. That was the whole confession, if you knew how to read him.

So I read him. And then I read the archives, because I'd spent twenty years learning locks and I'd never once tried the lock on our own history. The guildhall cellar had a door behind the wine racks—of course it did, we're smugglers, we put doors behind everything—and behind that door were ledgers older than the kingdom. Ledgers written by men who called themselves road-wardens and meant *prisoners' guards*. Ledgers that described, in careful clerical hands, what slept under Thornfell. Not a demon. Not a god. Something older than both categories, something the first wardens had found already wounded and already furious, and instead of healing it or killing it—they couldn't do either—they had buried it and built their whole civilization on top of the grave and called the grave a border.

And the thing under the mountain, the ledgers said, in one marginal note by some warden who'd gotten careless with his pen: *it dreams of green.*

It dreamed of green. Of growing things. Of warmth moving north and frost retreating and fields that didn't lie fallow under a cold that never quite broke. Do you see? Do you see why I couldn't just deal the cards and drink my flask and die comfortable in a warm hall while the lowlands starved in sight of all that buried spring?

No. Of course you don't. Mara won't either. That's the tragedy of loving someone—you know exactly how they'll refuse to understand you.

* * *

Here is what I actually did, though. This part matters. I want it recorded precisely, because Cassian Vole will tell it differently, and Cassian Vole was not there.

I didn't break the wards. Breaking them would have freed the thing outright, and whatever else I am, I read enough of those ledgers to be afraid of it properly. What I did was find the maintenance schedule—the great secret the guild had kept for centuries, the little pilgrimage Oren made every autumn with his chalk and his consecrated oil—and I skipped one stone. Just one. A minor node in the eastern chain, a stone no bigger than a well-cap, one whose turn in the rotation wouldn't come due for another year. I figured the wards would thin. I figured the thing beneath would stir, and dream harder, and its dreaming would leak. Warmth. Green-thought. Enough to nudge the seasons, maybe. Enough to make the lowlands bloom again without ever waking anything fully.

I told myself it was surgery. Careful, measured, reversible. I even left myself a way to undo it—I memorized the rite, copied the old formulas, kept the notes hidden in the lining of my coat like a coward's insurance policy, which is exactly what it was.

What I didn't account for was the other stones failing. Age, Oren called it later, white-faced, standing in the snow among dead ward-stones while the stars went out overhead like a lamp being carried out of the room. Four hundred years of maintenance and the last generation of us had been going through the motions, oiling stones whose hearts had cracked decades ago, singing words nobody remembered the meaning of. My one skipped stone wasn't the knife. It was the finger that pushed against a door already rotting off its hinges.

The stars went out. The mountain shifted. Avalanches sealed the passes, and somewhere far below us something vast rolled over in its sleep and began, slowly, to open its eyes.

And I stood in the guildhall yard with forty terrified people around me, and Mara looking at the empty sky with an expression I'd never seen on her face—not fear, worse than fear, *betrayal*, though she didn't yet know by whom—and I said nothing. I helped barricade the gates. I volunteered for the scouting runs. I smiled at Sefa Ironhale and let her call me "boy" the way she always does, and every hour I didn't confess, the confession got heavier, until carrying it felt like carrying a second Tam around inside my coat.

Then Oren gave Mara the key. I watched it happen from the doorway—watched him put four hundred years of guilt into the hands of a girl he'd fished out of a canal when she was nine, watched Mara take it the way she takes everything, jaw set, eyes wet and furious about being wet—and I understood that I had run out of time for patience.

Because Oren wasn't sending her south to fix things. He was sending her south to *seal* them. To bury the dream of green again for another four hundred years, or another four thousand, and come home and never speak of it, and let Wren's mother bury another child, and another after that, forever, because the alternative frightened us.

I left that night. Left a note, even, because I'm not a complete monster—it said *gone ahead, don't follow, trust me*. Which, in hindsight, from Mara's perspective, reads less like reassurance and more like a taunt. I've had time to think about the note. I regret the note.

* * *

Which brings us to tonight, and the ruined chapel above Greyford, and the moment I finally stopped hiding from the only person whose opinion of me I couldn't survive losing.

She came alone. Of course she came alone—that's the maddening thing about Mara Delacroix, she treats danger like a personal invitation. I heard her boots on the frozen gravel ten minutes before she reached the door, because I wanted her to hear me waiting, because ambushes are for enemies and I needed this to be something else, even if neither of us could name what.

"You look terrible," she said, from the doorway. Snow in her hair. The key hanging at her throat on its cord, glowing faintly, the way it does now, the way it started doing after the stars went out. Like a coal that hadn't decided whether to go out.

"You look like Oren," I said. "That's worse."

"Don't." One word, flat as a blade laid on a table. "Don't do the thing where you're charming until I forget why I'm angry. I've watched you do it to customs officers for ten years. I'm not a customs officer, Tam."

"No," I agreed. "You're the person they sent to clean up after me, and you don't even know it yet."

And then I told her. All of it. Wren, and the ledgers, and the skipped stone, and the surgery-that-became-a-wound. I told it badly—I'd rehearsed it a hundred times on the road and it all fell apart in the actual telling, the way confessions do, coming out sideways and defensive. She listened the way she listens, which is to say completely, which is the cruelest thing about her, because she doesn't interrupt to save herself the pain of hearing you.

When I finished, the silence lasted long enough for the wind to change direction.

"Wren," she said finally. "The girl in Millbrook."

"You remember her."

"I remember you talking about her for a month. I remember you sending money south that winter and lying that it was gambling winnings." Her voice did something complicated. "I thought you'd taken a mistress."

"A mistress would have been cheaper."

"Tam." She stepped closer, into the light of my little fire, and I saw she'd been crying recently and had done it efficiently, the way she does everything. "You killed the sky."

"I thinned a ward. The wards were already dying—"

"You *killed the sky*. Every star, gone. The passes sealed with people on the wrong side of them. Greyford starving in the dark right now, a valley away. Whatever you meant to do, that's what you *did*." She held up a hand before I could speak, and I shut my mouth, because she'd earned the gesture and we both knew it. "But here's what I can't figure out, and I've been figuring it since Sefa pulled your note out of the ash bucket. If you wanted the thing free, why leave me a warning? Why write *trust me*?"

"Because I don't want it free," I said. "I want it *heard*. There's a difference. The ledgers call it a prisoner. Cassian calls it a god. I've stood close enough to feel it, Mara, through the key you're wearing—it's not either of those things. It's something that was hurt and buried and told to be quiet for four thousand years, and everything it does, every cold snap, every nightmare leaking into the world, is it *asking to be let out*. And we keep answering by building the wall higher."

"So your plan was to knock a hole in the wall and hope it asked nicely."

"My plan was to make the guild negotiate. With the crown, with the thing itself, with anyone. Because the alternative—" I gestured south, toward the lowlands I'd walked through, the empty granaries, the frozen rivers, the graves getting dug in ground too hard to dig. "The alternative is what's happening right now. Oren's answer to every hard question is a locked door. I loved him like a father and his answer is a locked door, and people are dying on the other side of it."

"And yours is an unlocked one," she said. "With a monster behind it."

"With something that *dreams of green* behind it. Yes. Yes, fine, call it a monster, but Mara—at least my monster might say yes."

She was quiet again. And here is the part I need to set down honestly, because I promised myself I would not flatter myself in this account: for a moment, she considered it. I saw it happen. I've spent my life reading marks across a card table, and I know consideration when I see it—the slight shift of weight, the eyes going distant, the mouth that stops performing. She looked at the key at her throat and she thought about opening the door instead of locking it, and she thought about Wren, and about Greyford, and about every winter that would come after this one if we simply reburied the problem along with our consciences.

"I can't," she said at last. And it wasn't the refusal that mattered—it was the sound of her voice, which wasn't certain. Which was a woman choosing a side of a scale she couldn't actually see balanced. "Not like this. Not because a starving man's ghost argued me into it in a ruin. If that thing comes out, it comes out because we understood each other, not because we pried the lid off. Those aren't the same, Tam. You of all people should know the difference between a door opened and a door broken."

"That's a distinction for philosophers," I said. "Out here it's just weather."

"Then I'll be a philosopher in the weather." She pulled her cloak tight. "Come with me. Back to Sefa and Ilse. We'll figure it out together—all of it, the wards, the thing, the famine, all of it, out loud, with everyone, because that's the part you never tried, Tam. You did this alone because you knew we'd say no. So say yes now, in front of me, and mean it."

And God help me, I almost did. That's the truth. I stood in that ruined chapel with the fire dying and the girl I'd taught to pick locks and cheat at cards looking at me like I was still worth saving, and I wanted to say yes so badly my teeth ached.

But I'd seen the camp in the valley below. Cassian's people, two hundred strong, digging at the mountain gate with faith and fire-picks, and among them folk from the lowlands who'd walked north through the dark because they'd heard—Cassian makes sure they hear—that the answer to the famine lay under Thornfell. If I went back to Mara's little company, I abandoned them to a fanatic. If I stayed, I abandoned her.

There was no version of this where I didn't abandon somebody. That's what I should have understood three years ago in Millbrook, and I'm only learning it now, in the worst classroom in the world.

"I can't," I said. Her own words, handed back. "Not yet. There are people down in that valley who followed me here believing I'd get them to the mountain, and Cassian will work them to death or feed them to his god if I don't stand between. You go seal your door, Mara. But when you get down to the heart-chamber—and you will, you're too stubborn to die in a corridor—remember what I told you. It dreams of green. Ask it what it wants before you decide what it deserves."

She stood there a long moment, firelight and starless dark fighting over her face.

"You're going to make me do this the hard way," she said.

"I'm going to make you do it the *right* way. Hard is just what right feels like from the inside."

"That's the most Tam Rusk sentence ever spoken, and I hate you." But her voice cracked on *hate*, just slightly, just enough, and we both pretended it hadn't, because pretending is also a kind of love, in the guild, in the family, in the walls we build between the things we can't afford to feel.

She left. I watched her go down the frozen track toward Greyford, small and straight-backed and carrying four hundred years on a cord around her neck, and I turned south toward Cassian's camp, toward the two hundred souls who thought I was their salvation, toward the lie I'd have to keep living because the truth had gotten everyone I loved killed or exiled or worse.

The splinter's still in. I know that. She refused me and I'm glad she refused me and her refusal changed nothing and changed everything, because now when I imagine the door under the mountain opening, I imagine her face on the other side of it, and I can't decide if that makes me more careful or more dangerous.

I was still deciding when the horns started.

Down in the valley, Cassian's camp lit up with torches—not the ordinary evening fires but signal-flares, red ones, the war-priest's own color—and I heard shouting carry up through the frozen air, and then, fainter, from the direction of Greyford, answering horns. Mara's horns. Someone had found her company, or she'd been seen leaving my meeting, or Cassian had simply grown tired of waiting outside the mountain gate while the key walked around loose in the world wearing a cord.

Either way, the choice I'd been putting off had just been made for me, the way choices always are.

I started running.
