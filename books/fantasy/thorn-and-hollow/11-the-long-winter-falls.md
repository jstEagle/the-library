## 11. The Long Winter Falls

I have been cold in ways most people never get the chance to learn. I've slept in snowbanks and called it strategy. I've crossed the Grey Saddle with frost in my beard—when I still had one—and come down the other side with two fewer toes and no complaints worth making. Cold is an old acquaintance of mine. We don't like each other, but we know the etiquette.

What came down off Thornfell on the third night without stars was not cold.

It was a *decision*.

* * *

We'd made Greyford's outskirts by then—or rather, we'd made the hills above it, because Mara took one look at the valley and said, "No," in that voice she uses when her heart is doing arithmetic her mouth refuses to finish.

The town was there. Smoke from a few chimneys, thin as apology. But the river—the Whitlow, which I've seen run brown and loud and rude through every spring of my long, regrettable life—was frozen solid. Not at the edges. Not in slow creeping fingers from the banks. Frozen mid-current, like a man struck dead mid-sentence. The water hadn't stopped. It had been *stopped*, the way you'd stop a horse by putting your hand flat against its chest.

And the fields. Gods, the fields. Barley should have been knee-high stubble by harvest's end; instead every stalk stood white and glassy, bent under its own weight of ice, and when the wind moved through them they didn't rustle. They *rang*. Thousands of little crystalline bells tolling over a dead crop.

Ilse stood beside me on the ridge and said nothing for a long while. She's a healer; she reads land the way she reads bodies. Finally: "That's not dying. Dying looks different. That's—" She hunted for the word and hated all of them. "That's embalmed."

Mara had her back to us, arms crossed, watching the valley like it owed her money. Which, in fairness, everything did lately.

"We can't go down," she said. "Not yet. If the crown men are anywhere, they're where the food is." A pause. "There's a watchtower up the north fork. Old guild waystation. Sefa?"

"Kestrel's Rest," I said, because she was waiting on me and because saying the name out loud felt like proof the world still contained things I knew. "Built three hundred years back, abandoned two hundred. Walls are good stone. There'll be a hearth, if nobody's stripped the flue."

"And if somebody has?"

"Then we burn furniture and I'll complain about it the entire time. That's the arrangement."

She almost smiled. Almost. These days Mara Delacroix kept her smiles like a miser keeps coin, and I couldn't blame her. Every time she spent one lately, something in the world went out.

* * *

Kestrel's Rest was exactly as I remembered it, which is to say: ruined, but ruinously stubborn about it. Round tower, walls thicker than my waist is deep, roof half-collapsed into a nest of slate and pigeon filth and history. The hearth was intact. The flue drew. And there was even furniture to burn, so my complaining schedule stayed full.

Mara set watches. Ilse inventoried what remained of our food with the grim efficiency of a woman counting pulse beats. The girl—Wren, one of the refugees who'd attached themselves to us at Greyford's edge before Mara turned them gently away, all but this one, who simply would not be turned, a quality I respect enormously in the young and find insufferable in everyone else—Wren stacked wood and asked questions. That child asked questions the way rivers ask questions of stone. Constantly. Erosively.

"Why did the stars go out?" was tonight's. Directed at me, because I have the face people direct things at. I've been told it's a face that invites confession. I've spent sixty years trying to sand that feature off and failed.

"Ask the mountain," I said.

"The mountain can't talk."

"No," I agreed. "But something under it can, and I'd thank it to keep its opinions to itself."

Wren chewed on that. Kids do that now—chew on things instead of crying. The starless dark has aged every child in these valleys forty years in a fortnight.

The cold pressed in against the tower like a creditor at a door. We had fire enough, barely, but the wind found every seam, and beyond our little ring of warmth the night had gone strange. Frost climbed the *inside* of the windows. When Wren breathed near the wall, her breath didn't fog—it crystallized, tiny feathers of ice drifting down, beautiful and wrong as a funeral wreath made of glass.

Mara sat apart, turning the key over in her hands. The sealing key. Bronze-dark, older than any nation I could name, and warm—always warm, which had stopped seeming remarkable to her around the same time she stopped sleeping properly. She stared into it like it was a well and she was checking for water.

"Don't listen too hard," I told her. It came out gruffer than I intended, which for me is roughly normal volume.

Her head came up sharp. "To what?"

"To anything that talks in your own voice. Nothing honest needs that trick." I fed the fire another chair leg. "Sleep. I'll take middle watch."

"You've been up since before dawn."

"I'm old. Sleep and wakefulness stopped meaning much to me years ago. Now I just experience various flavors of tired."

She didn't sleep. Of course she didn't. She sat there holding that key like a hand she wasn't sure she wanted to hold hers, and I watched her, and I thought: *soon*. Because the tower was quiet and the night was long and some debts don't wait for courage. They wait for silence. And Kestrel's Rest had nothing else.

* * *

It was deep watch when I finally spoke, or tried to, and found the words had grown roots somewhere below my ribs.

Mara noticed. She notices everything except how much people love her, which is the specific blindness of children raised useful instead of raised cherished. The Guild of the Hollow Road raised her the first way and called it the second, and I was one of the hands that did the raising, and I carry that particular stone comfortably; it fits the shape of all my others.

"Sefa," she said. "You're staring at the fire like it owes you an explanation."

"It does. Most fires do, if you've been paying attention long enough." I pulled my cloak tighter. "How old do you think I am, girl?"

"Older than Oren. Older than the guildhall's east wing." A ghost of her old grin. "Older than lying, probably."

"Closer than you know." I held my hands to the flames. They ached in the old places, the deep places, the places where frostbit flesh remembers itself forever. "Sixty-eight years I am, by the count I keep, which may be wrong, because I lost a decade once and never bothered to go looking for it. And I want you to understand something before I say what I'm going to say next. What I did, I did believing it was right. I have never once believed otherwise. That's either a mercy or the whole of the problem, and I've had sixty years to decide, and I still don't know."

Mara went very still. Stillness is her weapon and her shelter both.

"I helped bury it," I said. "The thing under Thornfell. The power the wards hold down. I was there when the last seal was laid, and I carried stones, and I sang the binding songs, and I watched a woman I loved walk into the heart-chamber and not walk out again."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind worried at the broken roof like a dog at a bone.

* * *

You'll want the story straight, so let me give it straight, the way I gave it to her, with the fire burning low and Wren asleep under Ilse's coat and Mara's eyes on me like twin lanterns.

The wards are older than the Guild. Older than the crown. Older than the border they guard, which was drawn across maps by men who thought mountains were furniture. When the first wards were raised, there were people living in the high valleys—people the histories call the Hollow Folk, though that's not what they called themselves. Their name for their own country translates, roughly, as *the warm place between*. They kept something there. Or it kept them—I never learned which, and I suspect the distinction mattered more to them than to anyone who came after.

The lowland kingdoms wanted the passes. The passes meant trade, and trade meant power, and power has always known exactly what it wants and never once asked politely. So the ward-masons came, and the wars came, and when the wars ended the Hollow Folk were scattered like seed in wind, and the thing beneath the mountain—which had loved them, or been loved by them, or both—grieved.

A grieving power under a mountain is not a metaphor. It's weather. It's earthquakes. It's a winter that doesn't end until someone makes it end.

So the masons built the wards. Not to cage it—that's the lie written in the official accounts. You cannot cage grief; you can only bury it, and buried grief does what buried anything does. It waits. It grows patient. It learns the shape of the coffin and dreams of doors.

The sealing needed anchors. Living ones. The rite binds the seal to lives freely given, because freely given is the only knot that holds. Nine anchors, in my day. I was the youngest. Twenty-two, and sure of everything, the way you are at twenty-two, which is to say completely wrong in every direction at once.

"I don't remember agreeing," I told Mara. "That's the part I need you to hear. They tell you, and you nod, and you mean it—but meaning isn't understanding. Understanding comes later, and it comes alone, and it comes forever."

Nine anchors. The seal drank us slowly, the way a tree drinks a fencepost. One by one, over the years, the others dimmed. Not died—*dimmed*. Went grey and quiet and forgetful, their lives bleeding backward out of them until there wasn't enough left to hold the knot. Maren forgot her daughters' faces and smiled at strangers calling them by name. Old Hollis walked into the Whitlow one spring morning, calm as Sunday, and the river carried him off and I swear to you the current was singing.

By the time I was fifty, I was the last anchor living. And here is the ugly truth I've kept behind my teeth for eighteen years, the truth that made me leave the high country and join a smuggling guild and drink more than any doctor would endorse:

*I ran.*

Not far. Just far enough. Down out of the high passes, down to Thornfell's shadow-lands, into a guild of liars and thieves who never asked where I'd come from. I told myself distance didn't matter, that the rite held regardless. Mostly true. Mostly. But every year the seal weakened, and every year I felt it weaken—in my bones, in the ache of my frost-old hands—and every year I chose not to go back up the mountain and stand at the heart-chamber and offer what remained of me. I chose roads instead. I chose brandy and bad company and the small daily heroics of moving contraband past fools. I chose to grow old.

And the guild—Oren's guild, my guild—became wardens without knowing it, maintaining the stones because tradition said so, and I let them stay ignorant, because telling them meant telling them about me, and I was done being an anchor. I wanted to be a person. Do you understand? I wanted one life that belonged to me.

"So the wards failing," Mara said slowly, "wasn't just neglect."

"The wards were woven through nine lives, girl. Eight of them spent. Mine was the last thread, and I'd been unraveling myself for years—every mile I put between me and that mountain was a loose stitch." I looked at my hands. "Oren knew some of it. He knew I was old guild, older than old. He never knew how old. If he had—" My voice caught on something. I let it. "If he had, he'd have marched me up that mountain at knifepoint, and he'd have been right to, and I'd have hated him forever, and the wards might still be standing."

* * *

Mara didn't speak for a long time. When she did, it wasn't absolution, which I hadn't earned, and wasn't condemnation, which I hadn't feared. It was worse. It was *understanding*.

"You're telling me the price," she said.

"I'm telling you the invoice. The price comes later, and it's always higher than quoted." I leaned forward. "Listen to me now, because I'll only say this once and I'll deny it in the morning if you repeat it. That key you're carrying—it will offer you a way to fix everything. That's what such things do. The power under Thornfell isn't evil. Evil is simple, and it is not simple. It's wounded and enormous and it *loves*, in its way, the way a flood loves a valley. And when it speaks to you—and it will speak to you, it's already started, I've watched you listening to your own hands—it will sound reasonable. It will sound like mercy. It will offer to give back everything this winter is taking, and it will cost exactly what everything costs."

"How do you know it's already talking to me?"

"Because you hold it the way I used to hold my own wrist. Checking for a pulse that isn't yours." I sat back. "I'm not telling you to throw it away. Throwing it away is how Cassian wins, and how Tam wins, and how every fool who thinks destruction is a plan wins. I'm telling you what it costs, so when the bill arrives you can't say nobody warned you. Grief is instruction, girl. Mine taught me one lesson well: *decide what you'll pay before you're standing in the shop.*"

Wren stirred in her sleep and murmured something—a name, maybe, or a question. Ilse shifted her coat over the child without waking, the automatic tenderness of the practiced healer, and I thought: there it is. That's the other instruction grief gives, if you let it. Not just *know the price*. Know what's worth it.

* * *

Near dawn, the cold broke its own records.

The temperature dropped so fast the tower walls cracked—a long rifle-shot report that had us all up with knives drawn. Frost bloomed across the inside of the hearth and strangled our fire mid-flame. And through the window slits we saw it: the darkness itself thickening, a blackness with weight, pressing down on the valleys like a lid on a pot.

And beneath our feet—faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat heard through a floor—

Something turned over in its sleep.

Mara stood in the dead hearth-light with the key in her fist, and it was glowing now, faint and gold, pulsing in time with that vast slow rhythm below. Her face was lit from underneath, and I saw on it the beginning of the dream, the seduction, the *reasonable voice*.

"Sefa," she whispered. "It knows I'm coming."

"Aye," I said. "It's known since the night the stars went out. It's been waiting for you longer than you've been alive, girl—it waited for me first, and I disappointed it, and it's very patient about second chances." I picked up my axe. "So we'd best decide what we're bringing it besides ourselves. Dawn's coming. Eat something. Then we go down the north fork—and pray whatever gods are still answering that the crown men are slower than the cold."

Behind us, in the frozen hearth, one ember refused to die. Small, stubborn, orange as defiance.

I took it as an omen. I've taken worse advice from less reliable sources.
