## 12. Voices Under the Ice

The watchtower had a name once. Sefa said it was called Vane's Rest, after some long-dead warden who'd supposedly held the pass alone against three hundred raiders for a night and a day. Now it was mostly a hole in the sky with ambitions of being a wall, and we were sheltering in its basement like mice under a dying man's floorboards.

I want to tell you I dreamed of nothing that first night. That would be tidy. That would be the kind of thing you'd put in a ballad—*and Mara slept the sleep of the just, untroubled by the dark.* But ballads are written by people who weren't there, and I was there, and what happened was this: I dreamed of the guildhall kitchen, and Oren Vey was alive, and he was complaining about my knife-work the way he always did, and for the length of that dream I was so happy I could have wept blood.

Then he turned around, and his eyes were the color of deep ice, and he said, in a voice that was his voice and also not his voice at all:

*You left us.*

I woke with frost on my eyelashes.

Inside. In a tower with a fire going. Frost on my *eyelashes*, and the air around me cold enough to crack teeth, while Ilse slept ten feet away breathing steam like everyone else in the room was warm and I was a window left open in winter.

I told myself it was the draft. Towers have drafts. This tower had more holes than a smuggler's alibi, and cold pooled where it pleased, and there was no reason whatsoever to look down at my own hands and see what I saw.

But I looked anyway. Because I'm a professional.

The key was in my fist.

Now, understand—I don't sleep holding the key. Nobody sleeps holding the key. Sefa had drilled that into me somewhere on the Hollow Road, back when the tunnels still felt like tunnels instead of a throat: *"It goes in the wrap-cloth, girl, and the wrap-cloth goes against your spine, and if I ever catch you cradling it like a babe I'll take it off you and carry it myself."* The thing hummed faintly through layers of wool and oilcloth, a vibration you felt in your molars more than heard, and every one of us had learned to hate that hum the way you learn to hate a toothache.

Somehow, in my sleep, I'd unwrapped it. Unbuckled the strap, unwound the cloth, and closed my bare hand around a piece of worked stone older than any kingdom on the map. And my palm—the palm pressed flat against it—was burned. Not heat-burned. *Cold*-burned, white and waxy across the heel of my hand, the kind of mark you get from gripping iron in a killing frost.

The key wasn't cold. It never was. It sat in my hand at exactly the temperature of skin, which is somehow worse, because it means it's *paying attention.*

I wrapped it up. Buckled the strap. Sat in the dark with my heart going like a drum in a retreat and listened to my family breathe—Sefa's slow rasp, Ilse's muttering, the two Greyford boys who'd attached themselves to us somewhere past the ford—and I did not wake anyone.

Because here is the thing about telling people your nightmares: they start looking at you differently. And here is the other thing, the thing I didn't let myself think until much later: part of me didn't *want* to stop dreaming. Part of me wanted to know how the dream ended.

That's the hook of it, you understand. That's how it gets in. Not through the gate, but through the wound.

* * *

The second dream came the next night, and it didn't bother with Oren.

It gave me Tam instead.

Not Tam as I'd last seen him—gaunt, fever-eyed, standing in the snow outside Greyford with his heresy hanging off him like wet clothes—but Tam from before, Tam at sixteen, sitting on the loading dock behind the guildhall splitting an apple with me, both of us laughing at something I can't even remember now except that it made my ribs hurt. The dream-Tam handed me half the apple and said, "Remember when it was simple?"

And I said—it was a dream, you say things in dreams—"Nothing was ever simple."

"True," he said, and smiled, and his eyes went ice-colored, all of them, every face in the dockyard turning toward me with those pale drowned eyes, dozens of them, the whole remembered guild wearing the same gaze like a uniform. "But it could be again. You know what I offered. You know it stands. The famine ends. The roads reopen. Your dead live—not as they were, no one lives as they were, but *live*, Mara, warm and arguing and complaining about your knife-work. All you have to do is turn the key."

"I don't even know what it turns," I said, and hated how reasonable my own voice sounded, how much it sounded like negotiation.

"Neither does a lockpick," said every mouth in the dockyard at once, "and yet doors open."

I woke up standing.

Standing in the middle of the ruined ground floor of Vane's Rest, barefoot in snow that had blown in through the broken roof, key in hand, wrap-cloth trailing behind me like a tail. The stars were still gone—of course they were; the sky above the broken rafters was a black lid, seamless, pressing down—and my breath hung around me in clouds, and my palms were burned worse now, both of them, matching marks like I'd grabbed two hot irons out of a forge, except the forge was winter itself.

Ilse found me there. I heard her come down the stairs and I did the only clever thing available to me, which was to sit down quickly among the rubble and pretend I'd been sleeping sitting up, which even half-asleep she would not believe, because Ilse believes nothing, it's her defining virtue.

"You walk in your sleep now," she said.

"New skill." I kept my hands tucked under my arms. "I'm full of talents."

"Mara." She crouched in front of me. She had that healer's way of looking at you, like she could count your pulse by watching your throat. "Show me your hands."

"They're fine."

"They're burned. I've been watching you favor them since yesterday. Show me your hands or I'll wake Sefa, and you *really* don't want Sefa asking questions at this hour."

So I showed her. Because Ilse I could manage. Ilse would treat the symptom and not ask about the disease, because that was our arrangement, unspoken but ironclad: she patched us, we didn't make her regret it.

She turned my palms over in hers and her lips thinned to a line. "Frostburn. From inside. There's no exposure pattern like this—no wind-chafe, no blistering. It's like the cold came *out* of you." She looked up. "What are you holding at night?"

"Nothing."

"Liar. Beautifully done, but a liar." She reached into her kit, spread a salve that smelled of pine tar and something sharper, and worked it into the burns without another word. It was kindness performed as surgery, and I could have kissed her for the silence of it.

She said one thing, wrapping my hands in linen. "Whatever's talking to you—and something is, don't waste breath denying it, I've seen possession-adjacent before, in the plague camps south of the Sorrows—it wants you tired. Everything that hunts wants you tired first. So eat, and sleep in shifts, and if you find yourself standing in the snow again, you *wake someone*. Swear it."

"I swear," I said, and meant it right up until the moment meaning it stopped being enough.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about being hunted by a god-shaped absence: it doesn't chase. It *waits*. It has mountains of patience—literally, in this case—and it knows something I've known my whole crooked life, which is that the surest way to make a person do a thing is to make them think it was their idea.

The dreams got better. That's the obscene part. They got *good.*

The third night I dreamed of the high passes in summer, real summer, the kind we used to get before the wards failed, wildflowers coming up between the switchbacks and the whole world smelling of pine and sun-warmed stone. And the passes were open. Every one of them. And the guild was walking them—Oren grumbling at the head of the column, Sefa singing something filthy, young runners I'd trained myself, and the mule-trains loaded with honest cargo for once, cloth and salt and medicine, and along every mile of road stood ward-stones humming their old note, tended, loved, *ours*.

And the voice—which by then I recognized, though it wore whatever voice it liked, Oren's, Tam's, once horribly my own mother's, a woman I barely remembered—said:

*This is what I remember. This is what I was, before they buried me. I was the warmth in the stone. I was why the passes never froze. Ask the old one—ask Sefa Ironhale what the winters were like when I walked free. Ask her what they took.*

And here's the splinter, the one that lodged so deep I'm carrying it still: I *did* wonder. Because Sefa talked about the old winters sometimes, in the way old soldiers talk about old wars, and there was something in her voice—not grief exactly. Guilt's cousin. Something that lived next door to guilt and borrowed its fire.

The fourth night, the voice stopped offering. It started asking.

*What will you do when they ask you to stay?* it said, in my own voice, sitting across from me in the dream-kitchen of a guildhall that no longer existed except as ash and memory. *When the binding needs an anchor and Sefa volunteers, because she always volunteers, because she's been paying a debt for sixty years and calls it duty? What will you do when the price comes due and it's not yours to pay, and the only coin you hold is me?*

"That's not how it works," I said in the dream. "The binding takes what it takes."

*Everything takes what it can,* said my own mouth, smiling with someone else's patience. *Your crown. your guild. Your priest with his ash and his certainties. Everyone who ever touched power told themselves the taking was necessary. I am only honest about it. Turn the key, and no one pays but the ones who buried me, and they're mostly dust. Is that not mercy? Is that not justice? Cassian preaches justice. Tam starves for it. Even you—you steal from the rich and call it balance. We are alike, you and I. We both keep accounts.*

I woke with frost furring the inside of the tent, my breath crystallizing into actual flakes, little six-pointed stars falling upward out of my mouth, and both hands locked around the key through the wrap-cloth, through the strap, through the leather of the belt itself, fingers clenched hard enough that Ilse later had to pry them loose one at a time, murmuring, the way you'd calm a horse in a thunderstorm.

I hadn't sworn to wake anyone. I hadn't. But I lay there shaking in the grey pre-dawn, listening to the tower groan around us, and I understood—with the clarity that only comes at the bottom of a hole—that I was losing. Not dramatically, not with horns and battle. Losing the way a road loses itself under snow: gradually, then completely, then all at once you're standing somewhere you never chose to stand.

* * *

I should tell you about the morning I almost told Sefa. Because it matters, and because I failed, and failures are the load-bearing walls of any honest account.

We'd been at Vane's Rest four days. The cold had stopped being weather and become a fact of geology. The river in the valley below had frozen mid-current—we'd seen it, a river arrested like a caught gesture, water standing in white waves where it had been moving—and the Greyford boys had stopped asking when spring was coming, which is the saddest sentence I know how to write.

Sefa and I were splitting firewood, because Sefa believed idle hands invited despair and she'd been proving it for sixty years. My bandaged palms made me useless at the axe, so I held the rounds and she swung, and between strokes she talked the way she only talked when her hands were busy—about the old roads, the old guilds, names of people dead so long they'd gone mythic.

"Sefa," I said. "When the power was sealed. Before. What were the winters like?"

The axe stopped mid-swing. She lowered it slowly, and for a moment she looked every year of her age and several she hadn't earned yet.

"Cold," she said. "But an honest cold. Snow that fell and melted. Rivers that froze at the edges and ran in the middle." She set the axe down. "After the sealing, the winters got longer. A week, then two. Everyone said it was the mountain grieving. The founders wrote it down, first hundred years of records—*the cold deepens, the cold deepens*—like they were keeping score of a debt." She looked at me straight. "Why are you asking?"

"No reason."

"Mara Delacroix." Sixty years of command in four words. "You've been sleeping wrong for days. You flinch when the key hums. Your hands are burned in a pattern no frost ever made. And you're asking me about the sealing like you're checking someone's homework." She stepped closer. "Is it talking to you?"

And I swear to every vanished star, I opened my mouth to say yes. To give her the whole rotten account—the dreams, the offers, the standing in the snow, the voice wearing Oren's face and my mother's and mine. It was all lined up behind my teeth.

And then I saw what would happen. I saw it with terrible clarity: Sefa would take the key off me—she'd *have* to, she'd told me she would—and either she'd carry it herself, sixty-year-old bones marching back down into the dark toward the thing that had already taken her whole life once, or she'd decide I was compromised, and a compromised courier on a mission with no backup is a problem with one traditional solution. Either way, I'd bought us four days of trust and spent them in a single sentence.

Or maybe that's a lie. Maybe that's what I told myself. Maybe the truth is smaller and uglier: maybe the voice was right, and some part of me wanted to hear how the dream ended, and telling Sefa would end it, and I wasn't ready to be done with Oren's voice, even a stolen one. Even a trap.

"No," I said. "It's not talking. I've just been dreaming strange. Everyone has. The whole sky's gone out—dreams are bound to go strange."

Sefa looked at me for a long time. Long enough for the wind to fill the silence twice over. Then she picked the axe back up.

"When you're ready to tell the truth," she said, splitting the round in one clean stroke, "I'll be here. I'm always here. It's my whole cursed character." Another stroke. "But Mara—if that thing in the mountain learns your name through a key, you tell me *that day*. Not the day after. Not when it's convenient. That day. Promise me."

"I promise," I said.

Liar. Beautifully done, as Ilse would say.

* * *

The fifth night, the voice tried something new, and this is the part I've told no one, not Ilse, not Sefa, not the blank page I'm writing this on ahead of its time.

It stopped pretending to be anyone.

It came as the dark itself—as the starless sky pressing down through the broken roof, vast and patient and almost tender, and it said, in no voice at all, in the way that deep places speak, directly into the marrow:

*You think I offer corruption. I offer arithmetic. Your crown let the wards rot because maintaining them cost money and the mountain never voted. Your guild smuggled over the graves of the people I belonged to and called it survival. Your priest burns villages to save souls. Your friend broke the world to feed it. Everyone you love is compromised, child. Everyone but you—and you are only uncompromised because no one has yet given you anything worth compromising for.*

*I am giving it now.*

*Turn the key. End the winter. Save them all. And when they ask what it cost, tell them the truth no one else in this story will say aloud: that it cost nothing but a lie. The lie that burying things makes them safe.*

I woke at dawn with snow falling *inside* the tower, a soft column of it drifting down through the rafters onto the exact spot where I lay, settling on my blanket, my hair, my outstretched hands—hands that had unwrapped the key again, that were holding it up, both palms burned white now to the wrist, holding it up toward the empty sky like an offering.

Like a question waiting for a yes.

I put it away. I wrapped it, buckled it, strapped it down, and I sat there while the impossible snow melted on my eyelashes, and I made a list, because lists are how I pray. One: I cannot sleep near the others anymore. Two: I cannot sleep far from the others. Three: I have perhaps a night, maybe two, before the yes comes up my arm and out of my mouth. Four—

Four was interrupted, because that was the morning the scouts came down from the ridge at a run, breathless, half-frozen, with news that rearranged everything: riders on the south road. Crown banners. And with them, walking at the front of the column like he owned the frost itself, a tall figure in ash-grey robes whom the Greyford boys took one look at and started praying against.

Cassian Vole had found us.

And some traitorous corner of my frost-burned heart thought: *good. Maybe he'll know how to answer it.*
