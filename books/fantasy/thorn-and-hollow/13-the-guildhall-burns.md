## 13. The Guildhall Burns

The news came on a mule.

That's the thing about catastrophe nobody tells you: it doesn't arrive on wings of fire or in the mouths of prophets. It arrives on a mule, carried by a boy with frostbitten ears who smells of wet wool and terror, and it waits until you've just finished eating because the gods have a sense of humor and it's a bad one.

We were three days out of Greyford, camped in the lee of a granite shoulder the old road maps called the Widow's Tooth, when the boy stumbled into our firelight. Sefa had him wrapped in her cloak before he'd finished his first sentence, which was mostly teeth chattering. But he got the rest of it out eventually, word by frozen word, while I stood there holding a tin cup of tea I would never drink.

Soldiers. Crown men, flying the king's colors, marching up the Hollow Road with Cassian Vole's ash-marked faithful walking beside them like they'd been invited to the same wedding. They reached Thornfell two nights past. The guildhall—

The boy said *burned*. He said it twice, because the first time my face apparently didn't do anything at all, and people repeat themselves when your face doesn't do anything. It's a kindness, usually. That night it felt like being hit twice with the same hammer.

"Oren Vey?" I heard myself ask. My voice sounded like someone else's, someone competent, someone standing very far away from this fire. "The guildmaster. Did he—"

"They say he wouldn't leave." The boy's eyes went everywhere except mine. "They say he stood in the door of the hall and read them the old charter. Out loud. All of it, even the parts in Old Lowthian that nobody speaks anymore. And then—" He swallowed. "Then they say the roof came down."

*They say.* The favorite phrase of people who weren't there. I've built half my life on *they say*, hauled contraband on the strength of it, dodged toll-men and worse because *they say* the pass was closed. But I never once thought I'd be standing in the snow learning about Oren Vey's death from a stranger's secondhand sentence.

I set down the cup. Very carefully, the way you do when you're not sure what your hands are planning, and don't want them getting any ideas.

"Mara," Ilse said quietly. She'd come to stand beside me somewhere in the last minute without making a sound, which is a healer's trick I've decided is slightly unnatural. "Sit down."

"I'm fine."

"You're white as the snowfield and your hands are shaking."

"My hands are cold. It's winter. There's a great deal of it about."

She didn't argue. Ilse never argues; she just looks at you with those flat grey eyes until your lie gets embarrassed and goes home. I sat down. The tea was terrible. I drank all of it anyway, because Oren Vey taught me never to waste good tea, and even terrible tea deserved better than the ground, and because if I stopped moving for one moment I was going to feel the size of the hole where the guild used to be, and I wasn't ready. I might never be ready. Readiness is for people with time.

Sefa sat across the fire sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening. She'd been doing it since the boy arrived. The blade was already keen enough to split a hair, but she drew the whetstone along it in long slow strokes, over and over, and I understood suddenly that she wasn't tending the knife. She was keeping her hands busy for exactly the same reason I'd drunk the terrible tea.

"The soldiers came up the south road," she said at last, not looking at me. "Past Highmoor. That means they crossed the Wardens' Ford."

"So?"

"So the Wardens' Ford has been watched by guild eyes for two hundred years. Somebody told them it was clear." The whetstone paused. "Somebody inside."

Tam. The name rose in me like bile, and I made myself look at it instead of swallowing it down. Tam Rusk, who'd shared my bread for fifteen years, who'd argued with his whole warm stubborn heart that we should sell the secret to the crown and save the lowlands from starving. Tam, whose footprints I'd found in the ward-vault, whose logic had lodged in me like a splinter I still couldn't work loose. Maybe he hadn't meant this. Maybe he'd opened a door expecting a negotiation and the crown had walked through it with torches.

Maybe. But Oren was dead either way, and dead doesn't distinguish between malice and good intentions gone wrong. Dead just takes the whole account and closes it.

"We go back," I said.

Sefa's whetstone stopped entirely. "Back? Girl, there's nothing back but ash and crown patrols."

"There's the ward-stones. There's the deep vault. There's whatever records Oren kept that the fire didn't eat, if anything survived, and Oren kept everything, he kept receipts from thirty years ago, he kept a letter of complaint from 1789 about a shipment of turnips—" My voice cracked on *turnips*, of all the stupid things, and I hated myself for it, and I kept going anyway because the alternative was stopping. "And there are survivors. If anyone got out, they'll make for the fallback points. The Widow's Tooth is one of them. The others need telling."

"And the soldiers?"

"Avoided," I said. "It's what we do."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief, at least not the kind that comes with smoke attached: it makes you brilliant and stupid at the same time. For four days I planned our route back through the passes with a clarity I haven't managed before or since — every patrol pattern, every blind ravine, every frozen waterfall you could climb behind. And the entire time, underneath the brilliance, some animal part of me was screaming that I was walking toward a burned building because a building can't be dead unless I see it, and buildings aren't people, Mara, and you know exactly whose death you're actually trying to unmake.

Oren Vey found me in a snowdrift when I was nine years old, stowaway on a guild cart, half-frozen, biting the hand that pulled me out because trust was a language I hadn't learned yet. He didn't scold me. He fed me stew, and when I'd finished he said, "You bite like someone who's been hungry a long time. We can work with that." Then he put me to scrubbing pots, and when I'd scrubbed them all he put me to counting inventory, and when I caught the tally error he'd planted on purpose he looked at me over his spectacles and said, "Huh," in a tone I would spend the next twenty years trying to hear again.

He never said he was proud of me. Guildmasters don't, I think. It's in the charter somewhere, probably, right between the sections on toll arbitration and turnip complaints. But every time I brought a shipment through clean, every time I talked us out of trouble or fought us out of worse, there was that *huh*, and I banked them like coins, and I thought there'd always be more.

Now there wouldn't. There was a fixed number of *huh*s in the world and I'd spent them all without knowing, and no amount of route-planning brilliance was going to mint new ones.

We reached the high valley above Thornfell on the fifth day, and I smelled it before I saw it.

Woodsmoke you expect in winter; every hearth makes it. This was different. This was the smell of a lot of wood burning at once, and pitch, and something under it — wool, maybe, or parchment, or the particular char of old oak that's been standing for centuries soaking up lamp oil and candle wax and the accumulated years of a hundred generations. The guildhall had stood since before the kingdom had its current name. I know because Oren told me, twice a year, whether I wanted to know or not.

We came over the ridge at dusk, and there it was.

Or there it wasn't. The hall itself was a blackened skeleton, ribs of timber against a bruised sky, snow falling gently into the ruin as if the sky were trying to apologize. The ward-stones around the perimeter — the ones we could reach — were shattered. Not toppled: shattered, pounded to gravel, each fragment chalked with the ash-hand sigil of Cassian's faith. The great barn was gutted. The smithy's roof had caved. And in the yard, driven into the frozen mud in a neat row, were eleven stakes.

Empty stakes. Whatever they'd been for, they'd been used, and then the bodies had been taken down, or burned, or both. Around the base of each stake the snow was stained in ways I made myself look at, because Oren Vey had taught me that the things you refuse to look at are the things that kill you later.

"Eleven," Sefa said behind me. Her voice was gravel dragged over iron. "He kept eleven names on the active roll."

I knew. I'd helped him update it at midwinter. Eleven names, eleven stakes. The arithmetic was simple and it went through me like a blade finding the gap in armor.

Ilse put her hand on my shoulder. I shrugged it off, then immediately wished I hadn't, then didn't take it back because taking it back would mean admitting I needed it, and I needed to not need anything for approximately the next hour.

"Spread out," I said. "Vault first. Then the fallback caches. If anyone survived, they'll have left signs. You know the marks."

They spread out. I went to the vault alone, which was tactically idiotic and emotionally mandatory.

* * *

The vault door was bronze, older than the hall above it, and the soldiers had clearly tried to breach it and failed — the stone around it was scarred with chisel marks and one long gouge that might have been a battering ram's parting insult. Oren's wards still held, the last loyal servants of a dead house. I spoke the guild-phrase — the real one, the one that wasn't in any charter, the one that was just Oren's mother's name because he was sentimental under all that granite — and the door swung open onto darkness that smelled of dust and ink and safety.

I lit a lamp with hands that shook, and I looked at what the fire had spared.

Shelves. Ledgers. The ward-maintenance logs going back six hundred years in Oren's crabbed hand and his predecessors' hands before him. The map cabinet. And on the central table, laid out square and deliberate, three objects: the sealing key's case, empty, because the key was with me and had been since the night the stars went out; a letter sealed with black wax; and Oren's signet ring, the one he'd worn every day of my life, sitting on top of the letter like a full stop.

He'd known. Standing in his burning hall, reading his charter at men with torches, he had known how it ended — and sometime before, maybe days before, maybe the moment the stars vanished, he had come down here and left me an inheritance.

I broke the seal. His handwriting, steady as ever, though the last lines wobbled in a way that told me he'd written fast:

*Mara. If you're reading this, I'm not, which I expect will annoy you both.*

*The guild was never the hall. I should have told you that years ago instead of letting you love the stones. Stones burn. What we are is the road, and the road is the people on it, and people scatter and regather like starlings. Do the gathering. Don't ask permission — the ones who'd give it are dead and the ones who wouldn't are the enemy.*

*The key is yours now, whatever it costs. I'm sorry for that. I chose you because you're the least compromised and the most stubborn, and I won't pretend those weren't the same reason.*

*One more thing. If Tam Rusk had any hand in this, remember that he loved this guild too, wrong as he loved it. Hate what he did. Try — try, girl, I know it's hard — not to hate him. Men who hate themselves too thoroughly stop being able to fix anything.*

*Scrub the pots. Count the inventory. Huh.*

I read it three times. On the third reading I discovered I was sitting on the floor of a six-hundred-year-old vault with a dead man's ring in my fist, crying in a way I hadn't cried since I was nine years old in a snowdrift, and I let it happen, because there was no one to see, and because Oren had earned every drop of it and more.

Then I wiped my face, put the ring on my thumb — it fit nowhere else — took the ledgers that mattered, and went back up into the snow to find my people.

* * *

They came in through the night, one and two and three. Doss, the forger, soot-streaked and missing an eyebrow. Young Pimm and his sister Wren, who'd been running a cache delivery and thus owed their lives to being elsewhere. Old Marette from the toll-house, carrying nothing but a knife and a grudge. By midnight there were nine of us around a fire in the ruined smithy, nine out of eleven, and I made myself say the arithmetic out loud because the dead deserve their count.

"Two unaccounted," I said. "Bren and Hallam. Search parties at first light."

Nobody spoke. The fire crackled. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the broken ribs of the hall and made a sound almost like voices, almost like the old songs sung off-key by a house that was dying.

"Mara." Sefa, from across the fire. "What are we?"

And there it was. The question I'd been circling all night like a moth around a flame it wanted to kill. Because the honest answer was *nothing* — the guild was a charter and a hall and a roll of eleven names, and the charter was ash, the hall was ash, and the roll was nine living and two lost and one dead man's ring on my thumb. The guild was over. Every institution I'd ever feared or loved had failed or betrayed or burned, and here we sat in the ruins of the only home I'd ever had, and the world expected us to do the decent thing and disperse.

I looked around the fire. Doss with his missing eyebrow. Pimm and Wren, nineteen and seventeen, orphaned to everything but each other. Marette, seventy if she was a day, jaw set like a woman daring the universe to try something. Ilse, who'd known the guild for three weeks and stayed anyway. Sefa, who'd buried the power once already and carried the cost of it in her bones.

Not a guild. Something else. Something that hadn't been named yet, which meant something that could still become anything.

"The guild is dead," I said. "I want that said plainly, once, so we don't spend the next year pretending otherwise. The charter, the hall, the roads, the old order — burned, broken, finished. Oren Vey is dead. Bren and Hallam may be. The crown razed our home with a priest's blessing, and the kingdom will write it down as the suppression of a smuggling ring, and no one will ever be punished, because that's what kingdoms do."

Wren flinched. Pimm put his arm around her. I kept going, because softening it would have been the cruelest thing I could do.

"But Oren left me a letter, and in it he said the guild was never the stones. It was the road, and the road is the people. So here's what I say. I say we are not the Guild of the Hollow Road anymore. I say we are the people who choose to stay — stay in these mountains, stay with each other, stay with the truth the crown wants burned and buried. I say we finish what Oren died guarding. I say we take the key south, we seal what's waking beneath Thornfell, and then—" and here my voice did something I didn't authorize, went low and hard and glad, "—then we make them write *us* into the law. Not amnesty begged for. Terms dictated. A history acknowledged. Justice outside their law until their law learns to hold it."

Silence. Long enough that I heard my own heartbeat and started composing apologies.

Then Marette spat into the fire — her version of a signature — and said, "About bloody time somebody claimed the job. Vey dithered forty years."

Doss laughed, a raw bark. Sefa just nodded, once, the way mountains nod. Ilse caught my eye across the flames and gave me the smallest tilt of her head, which from Ilse was a coronation.

Pimm raised his hand like we were in school. "What do we call ourselves? If we're not the guild."

I looked at the black ribs of the hall against the stars — the few stars that had crept back, thin and changed and stubborn, the way we were.

"Thorn and hollow," I said. "The thorn keeps. The hollow holds. We're what grows in the wound and doesn't die of it."

Marette snorted. "Terrible name."

"It'll grow on you."

Everything after that was logistics, and logistics I could do all night. Caches to raid, routes to map, the south road and the deep tunnels and the question of Cassian Vole, who had burned my home and was even now digging toward the heart of the mountain with a fanatic's patience. We planned until the fire burned low, and when the others finally slept I sat alone with Oren's ring on my thumb and the key against my breastbone, and I felt it — faint, patient, cold as the space between stars — the buried thing noticing me again. Dreaming toward me. *Come down,* it seemed to murmur through the metal. *I know what you've lost. I can give it back.*

"No, you can't," I whispered. "But thank you for asking."

And far to the south, where the passes dropped toward the lowlands, a light was moving on the night road. Torches. Many torches, moving in formation, coming north along the Hollow Road toward Greyford and everything between it and the mountain.

Crown men, or Cassian's, or both. It hardly mattered anymore. I woke Sefa with my boot, gently, the way she'd taught me.

"On your feet, Ironhale," I said. "Company's coming. Let's go introduce ourselves."
