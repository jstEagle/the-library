## 14. Allies of No Law

I had stitched wounds in Greyford for eleven years, and in all that time I had learned exactly two universal truths: people bleed the same whether they're saints or sinners, and nobody—*nobody*—follows a plan they don't understand.

Mara Delacroix understood this without being told. Which was, I suppose, the first thing that made me believe she might actually manage what she was attempting.

We'd come down out of the high country half-frozen and half-starved, Sefa Ironhale limping on a knee she refused to admit was ruined, Mara carrying a key that hummed against her hip like a hornet in a jar. And we'd found Greyford not empty, as I'd half-feared, but *full*—fuller than it had any right to be. The starless dark had pushed people downhill like meltwater, and meltwater pools in the low places. Refugees from three valleys crowded the town's granary, its chapel, its tannery—anywhere with walls and a roof. They were cold, frightened, hungry, and angry, and I want you to understand that those four things together make the most dangerous fuel in the world.

Into that tinderbox walked my smuggler with her dead guild's secrets and her frost-burned palms hidden inside her gloves, and asked them to follow her up a mountain.

Gods help me, I helped her do it.

* * *

It began, as most ruinous things do, with a speech she didn't want to give.

"They won't listen to me," she said, pacing the back room of what passed for an inn these days—a room that smelled of tallow smoke and wet wool. "I'm nobody to them. A smuggler. Worse, a smuggler who got caught."

"You didn't get caught," Sefa rumbled from the corner, where she sat working a whetstone along a blade that had seen more winters than either of us. "You got intercepted by crown soldiers, fought your way clear, and came out the other side. In my experience that's called a reputation."

"In your experience everything's called a reputation."

"Because it usually is." She tested the edge with her thumb, unbothered. "These people watched their stars go out. They've spent weeks listening to the ice groan on the peaks like something walking. They don't want comfort, girl. Comfort died the night the sky did. They want someone to point at and say *her*. Someone who knows what's under the mountain and isn't lying about it."

"And if I'm not sure I know?"

"Then lie convincingly," Sefa said, "the way you're doing right now, and stop wasting my whetstone."

I snorted despite myself. Mara shot us both a look of profound betrayal, which was fair—I'd known her barely two months and had already developed the specific affection of a healer for a patient who refuses to stay in bed. She stopped pacing. She looked at the map spread on the table between us: Thornfell's southern shoulder, the passes sealed by avalanche, the deep tunnels marked in Oren Vey's fading ink, and at the center of it all, that small circle he'd drawn before the soldiers burned him out of his own hall—the heart-chamber. The thing beneath.

"The wards are gone," she said quietly. "The old seal is failing. Whatever's buried under that mountain has been asleep for centuries, and it's waking up cold and furious, and every week we wait the winter gets worse because it's *leaking*. That's what the cold is. It's not weather. It's grief coming off something that was buried alive."

"And your plan," I said, because someone had to say it aloud, "is to walk back down into the thing's own throat and seal it from the inside, where the last sealing failed."

"When you put it that way—"

"I always put it that way. It's my job to put things the way they are." I crossed my arms. "You'll need fighters. You'll need people who can dig and haul and hold a tunnel mouth against whatever Cassian Vole has mustering up there. You'll need food for a month underground. And you'll need every soul in that granary to decide, tonight, that you're worth dying behind."

Silence. Even the whetstone stopped.

"So," Mara said at last, "no pressure."

"No pressure," Sefa agreed gravely. "Just history."

* * *

She gave the speech in the granary that evening, standing on grain sacks so the crowd could see her over each other's shoulders, and I stood at the edge of it watching faces, because that was my contribution—reading the room the way I read a fever chart.

There were maybe two hundred souls packed into that space. Farmers whose fields had frozen black. Miners from the eastern shafts, still wearing the pale scars of old cave-ins. A knot of deserters from the crown garrison at Harrow Ford—six men who'd walked away when their officers started confiscating refugee grain as "requisition," and who flinched whenever anyone moved too fast near them. And scattered through it all, the smugglers. Mara's people, or the remains of them: hollow-eyed survivors of the guildhall fire, carrying burns and grudges in equal measure, looking at her like sailors looking at a lighthouse that had gone dark and then, impossibly, flickered again.

Mara didn't start with the mountain. I'll give her credit; she'd listened to me after all.

"My name is Mara Delacroix," she said. "Some of you have heard it. Most of you heard it wrong." A ripple—not quite laughter, but adjacent to it. "For twelve years I ran contraband through the Thornfell passes. Silk, salt, medicine, occasionally people who needed moving quietly. I broke the law for a living, and I was good at it, and the Guild of the Hollow Road fed me and raised me and taught me every stone in those mountains. Three weeks ago the crown burned our hall to the ground and put our guildmaster's head on a spear at the pass road. So when I tell you I'm not here on behalf of any law, I mean it in the most literal sense available."

That landed. I watched it land—in the deserters especially, who had stopped flinching and started leaning forward.

"Here is what I know, and I'll say all of it, because I'm done with institutions that hand out truth one spoonful at a time." And she told them. Not the sanitized version—the whole ugly architecture of it. The guild founded to guard, not smuggle. The ward-stones maintained in secret for six hundred years. The thing beneath Thornfell, buried by people who called themselves its betters, sealed away rather than reckoned with. The wards failing. The stars going out. The crown knowing about the wards—knowing—and letting them rot because maintenance was expensive and the mountain had been quiet for generations.

"Somebody in my own family opened the door," she went on, and her voice cracked on the word *family*, just once, and I think that crack bought her more than anything else she said. "He thought freeing it would end the famine. He wasn't stupid, and he wasn't evil. He was desperate, and desperation is how every disaster in history has gotten its start. The power under that mountain is offering the same bargain to everyone now—to me, to you, to the priest digging toward it with an army of the faithful. End the winter, it says. End the hunger. Just let me out first."

A farmer near the front—a big man with frost-split hands, Doss, I knew him from the clinic—called out: "And why shouldn't we take that bargain? What's so terrible under there that we freeze politely instead?"

Mara looked at him for a long moment. I braced for the evasion.

"Because it doesn't end the winter," she said. "It *becomes* the winter. What's buried under Thornfell isn't a god or a demon. It's older than both categories, and it's been locked in the dark so long that its idea of mercy is putting everything to sleep. Forever. The cold isn't its attack. The cold is its *gift*, already arriving, and the only reason any of us are still warm is that the last seal is holding by threads." She held up her hands, pulled off her gloves, showed the crowd the frost-burned palms. Gasps went around the room like wind through wheat. "This is what it does to me through a key and three miles of rock. Imagine what it offers when you're standing in front of it."

Doss sat down slowly.

"So no, I can't promise you spring," Mara finished. "I can't promise you the crown will pardon you or the harvest will come or the stars will ever come back the way they were. I can promise you this: nobody is going to make this decision for you again. Not the guild, not the crown, not a war-priest, not a starving man with a knife and a theory. For six hundred years the people under this mountain were buried by folks who never asked them. I'm asking." She swept her gaze across all of them, and I swear to you, that woman who couldn't look me in the eye when I changed her dressings held two hundred strangers' stares like she'd been born to it. "Whoever wants to stand watch over this mountain with me—not for me, *with* me—stay in this room when the rest go out. Whoever wants to take their chances south, the road's open, and no one here will think less of you. I'll think less of you if you pretend you weren't afraid. Fear's the honest part."

They filed out. And then they didn't, all of them—maybe forty stayed, milling in the lamplight, awkward as cattle, glancing at each other to see who else had lost their minds.

Forty. Out of two hundred.

Sefa leaned over to me and murmured, "In my day a good muster was one in five. That's nearly double."

"In your day," I whispered back, "people hadn't watched the sky die."

* * *

What followed was three days of the strangest army-making in the history of armies, and I was in the thick of it because Mara took me aside on the second morning and said, "I need you running the sick line and the supply ledger, which means I need you telling people what to do, which means congratulations, Lieutenant."

"I didn't agree to a rank."

"You agreed to stay. Rank came attached. Read your contract."

"There is no contract."

"Then it's a very generous arrangement."

I want it noted that I resisted. I want it further noted that I lost, immediately, because she was right and we both knew it. Forty people marching into a mountain in winter need someone deciding who eats first, whose cough is a cold and whose is the lung-rot, whose frostbite toe comes off and whose keeps. I'd done triage in famine years. I could do triage in rebellion. The difference, I discovered, was mostly paperwork.

Sefa took the fighting side of the ledger—she drilled the deserters and the smugglers together in the town square, and the sight of that scarred old woman bellowing at farm boys until they held a shield wall correctly became, within a day, the closest thing our company had to entertainment. She paired them deliberately: a deserter with a smuggler, a miner with a refugee, stitching strangers into pairs the way I'd stitch skin. "Nobody fights for a cause," I heard her tell them, pacing the ranks. "Causes are too far away. You fight for the fool next to you, so get to know the fool."

By the second evening the fools were sharing their rations and their names. By the third, two of them were engaged, which I diagnosed as premature but declined to treat.

And Mara worked the room. That's the only phrase for it. She sat with the miners and learned which tunnels under Thornfell's shoulder were sound. She sat with the deserters and extracted, piece by careful piece, what the crown garrisons were doing—not just at Harrow Ford but everywhere—and her face when she learned the crown was already sending surveyors up the southern passes, hunting for the same heart-chamber, was a study in a woman crossing an item off her list of remaining illusions. She sat with the smugglers and gave them back something I don't think they'd had since the guildhall burned: orders. Purpose. A job that wasn't just surviving.

On the third night I found her on the inn's back step, staring up at the blank sky, and I sat down beside her because my feet hurt and the bench was there, and I told myself the other reasons later.

"Do you know what I keep thinking?" she said, without preamble, because we'd passed the stage of preambles somewhere around the icefall. "Oren used to say the guild wasn't a family, it was a *road*—a thing people traveled together and maintained and argued over. I thought that was him being cold. Now I think it was him being kind. Families you can lose. Roads, you rebuild."

"We're rebuilding, then."

"We're building something that never existed before. Smugglers and deserters and farmers, sworn to no crown, no guild, no god. Justice outside any law." She laughed, short and unamused. "Do you know how that sounds? Like the opening chapter of a tragedy. Every ballad starts with a band of outlaws and ends with them hanging."

"Every ballad also ends with somebody singing it," I said. "Which means somebody survived to remember. Ballads are written by the leftovers, Mara. Aim to be a leftover."

She looked at me then, really looked, and something in her face eased by a degree I'd learned to measure in patients. "Why did you come with us? Really. Greyford needed a healer. You could have stayed."

"Greyford will need a healer long after we're dust, and it'll have one. I trained Brinnet for two years precisely so I'd be replaceable." I pulled my coat tighter. "You want the real answer. Fine. I spent eleven years binding wounds that the world kept reopening. Famine, taxes, soldiers, cold—same wound, different knife. And when the stars went out I realized I could spend the rest of my life sewing shut a hole in the bottom of the boat, or I could go find the hole." I shrugged, embarrassed by my own honesty. "Also you were insufferable and I wanted to supervise you."

"That's the truest thing anyone's said to me in weeks."

"It's the only thing anyone's said to you in weeks that wasn't strategy. You're welcome."

* * *

The muster formed at dawn on the fourth day, in the grey light under the starless sky, and I'll record it plainly because plainness is what it deserved.

Forty-one of us, counting the three of us and a fourteen-year-old named Wick who lied about his age so badly that even Sefa gave up correcting him. Two carts of provisions, a forge-cart for tools, rope enough to hang a regiment. The miners had brought hammers and knowledge. The deserters had brought arms they technically stole, which made them smugglers now too, and the look on their sergeant's face when Mara pointed this out—"Congratulations, you've been promoted out of the law"—was worth the whole miserable campaign ahead.

Mara stood up on the lead cart and didn't make a speech. She'd made her speech; instead she just said, "We go up the shepherd track to the old gate, then down through the galleries to the heart-chamber. We seal what's waking, from inside, where it counts. Some of us may not come back up. Everyone is allowed to turn around at any point, forever, no questions." Then she paused and added, almost as an afterthought, in a voice that carried anyway:

"But nobody goes alone. That's the whole rule. That's the entire law of this company. Nobody goes alone."

And forty ragged, frightened, stubborn people—smugglers and deserters and farmers and one extremely reluctant lieutenant—heard that rule and straightened up like it was a coronation.

We were three hours out of Greyford, climbing into the white silence below Thornfell's shoulder, when Sefa, ranging ahead as outrider, came back down the trail at a pace her bad knee should have forbidden. Her face told me before her words did.

"Smoke," she said. "At the old gate. Fresh. A lot of it." She looked at Mara. "And on the rocks above the pass—signal fires, relaying. Somebody's watching the approaches." She spat into the snow. "Vole's not digging toward the heart anymore, girl. He's dug in *on top of it*. He's holding the door."

Behind us, Greyford's chimneys smoked peacefully in the valley, unaware. Ahead, the mountain wore its ring of fires like a wound crowned with ash.

Mara put her hand flat against the key at her hip—it had begun humming louder, I noticed, the closer we climbed, like something on the far side of a wall had noticed us noticing it—and smiled the smile I had learned, in two short months, to dread and admire in equal proportion.

"Good," she said. "Saves us the walk."
