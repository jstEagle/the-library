## 15. The Frozen Gate

The mountain gate had a name once. Something carved in old Guild script on the lintel—*Vael Duren*, maybe, or *Vael Duran*, the letters worn to suggestions. Sefa called it the Frozen Gate because of the icefall that hung above it like a chandelier nobody with sense would light. Cassian Vole's people called it nothing at all. They'd pitched their camp beneath it and built fires under a thousand tons of hanging glacier, which told me everything I needed to know about how much they valued their own skins.

Not much. That was the whole point of them.

We lay on the ridge above the pass, forty-one of us against what Ilse counted as sixty-odd, and watched their cookfires gutter in the wind.

"Sixty," she said again, like repetition might improve the arithmetic. "And us with two crossbows, one hammer, and a healer who's better at making people comfortable about dying than not dying."

"You're selling yourself short," Sefa said. "You're also good at insults."

"I'm aware." Ilse pulled her scarf up over her nose. "I want it noted before we die that I objected."

"Noted," I said. "Duly."

Below us, the camp moved with the grim orderliness of the converted. Cassian ran a tight operation—I'd give him that much. Watch rotations, wood stacks, a shrine of ash and bone at the center where his people knelt in shifts. They were digging at the gate itself, prying at the old seal-iron with crowbars and faith in equal measure, and every so often the mountain answered with a groan that I felt in my back teeth.

"They'll breach it in a day," Sefa said. "Maybe two. The iron's old. The rite behind it is older, and rites don't hold without hands to tend them."

"Then we don't fight through," I said. "We talk first."

Sefa turned her head slowly, the way she did when she wanted me to know she thought I was an idiot but respected my commitment to it. "Talk. To the man who strung up two of our scouts last month."

"He let us go once."

"He *let* us go. That's worse. Men who kill you are finished with you. Men who let you go are curious."

"I know." I settled my chin on my arms and looked down at the fires. "But he stood there in the dark and told me the truth about the wards, or at least his version of it, and he didn't have to. And half those people down there aren't fanatics. They're farmers. Deserters. Lowland folk whose children starved last winter while the crown sat on its hands. You don't kill your way through people like that. Not if you want anything left standing after."

"And if talking fails?"

"Then we fight," I said. "Badly, probably. But we fight."

Ilse snorted. Sefa just grunted, which from her was a benediction.

* * *

We came down at dusk, when the light goes blue and honest and everyone looks older than they are. No banners. No war cries. Just forty-one people walking out of the snow with their hands visible and their weapons slung, which in my experience frightens soldiers more than a charge does. A charge, at least, tells you what to do.

Their pickets spotted us early—they weren't amateurs—and by the time we reached the edge of the camp we were ringed by spears and the flat, hungry stares of the faithful. Cassian's people wore ash on their faces, streaked over the eyes like tears that had learned discipline. Up close they smelled of smoke and unwashed wool and, underneath it, something sweeter and wronger. The mountain's breath, leaking through the gate. I knew that smell from my dreams. My palms started itching inside my gloves, and I made a note to never tell anyone that.

Cassian came out of the command tent wiping grease off his hands, unhurried, like we were expected guests. He looked thinner than at our last meeting. The exile's leanness had gone from hard to hollow, and the fire in him burned brighter for it, the way a lamp burns when the oil runs low.

"Mara Delacroix," he said. "Twice now you've walked into my camp. Either you're brave or the Guild taught you nothing at all."

"The Guild taught me plenty," I said. "Mostly how to walk into places I shouldn't be. It's the family trade."

Behind him, his people murmured. Somewhere in that murmur was Tam—I felt it before I saw him, a specific ache at the base of my skull, the way you feel weather coming. He stood at the edge of the shrine with his hood back and his face doing something complicated. We didn't look at each other. It was the politest thing either of us had ever done.

"You've come to argue," Cassian said.

"I've come to be honest. Arguing's just honesty with the volume up."

That got a sound out of his line—not laughter, exactly, but the ghost of it, quickly strangled. Good. Dead audiences can't defect.

"Last time we spoke," I said, pitching my voice to carry, "you told me the wards were built on theft. That the power under this mountain belonged to the people who lived here before Thornfell existed, and the founders caged it and called it peace. You're right."

The murmur stopped dead. Cassian's eyebrows rose a fraction—the first genuine expression I'd seen on him.

"You're right," I said again, louder. "I've seen the deep galleries. I've read the murals. The sealing wasn't justice. It wasn't even strategy, mostly. It was fear wearing armor. Our guild kept the locks oiled for six hundred years and told itself stories about protecting the lowlands, and maybe some of it was true, but the foundation of it all was: *this was theirs, and we took it.*"

"Mara," Sefa said quietly, beside me. A warning. Or a prayer. With Sefa it was hard to say.

"No, let her," Cassian said softly. His eyes had gone bright. "Please. This is the most interesting thing to happen at this gate in weeks."

"So here's where we differ," I went on. "You think the answer to an old crime is to break the cage. Let it out. Let it take what it's owed. And I understand why. Gods know I've wanted to burn things for less. But I've heard it speak, Cassian. Through the key, in my sleep. And it isn't a liberator waiting for its chains. It's a wound that's forgotten it was ever anything else. It doesn't want justice. It wants *everything*—the lowlands, the winter, the stars, all of it gone, and us gone with it, and it would call that balance. Your people starved. Mine too. Freeing this thing won't feed a single one of them. It will just make sure nobody starves again because there's nobody left."

"You presume to know its mind?"

"I presume to have held its key while it whispered to me every night for a month." I pulled off my glove and held up my palm. The frost-burn scars had faded to silver, but silver reads well in dusk light. "It offered me my guildhall back. Oren alive. Every debt forgiven, every road open. All I had to do was turn the key. Do you know what that offer costs a person? Do you know how many nights I spent arguing with it? So no, I don't presume. I *know*. And I'm telling your people, not you—" I turned, slow, meeting eyes along the spear-line, "—because you deserve to hear it from someone who isn't asking you to die for their certainty. Not him. Not the crown. Not me. I'm asking you to walk away. That's all. Walk away, and live, and help us build something that actually feeds people. There's a valley full of refugees at Greyford doing exactly that. Nobody's kneeling. Nobody's burning. The soup's terrible, but there's enough of it."

Silence. Wind off the icefall, ticking and groaning. Somewhere a rope creaked against a tent pole.

"You have a smooth tongue," Cassian said at last. "Oren Vey's training. He always did prefer words to iron. Look where it got him."

Something went cold in my chest. "What did you say?"

His smile was gentle, which was the cruelest thing about him. "You haven't heard? Ah. Mara. The guildhall burned three nights ago. Crown men and my overzealous brothers, working at cross purposes, as armies do. I'm told the old man died at his post. At the ward-stone, actually. Hands on it. Very fitting."

The world went narrow and loud and very far away, all at once. I heard Ilse's breath catch. I heard Sefa make a sound I'd never heard her make—a small, cracked thing, quickly buried. And I heard myself, from somewhere outside my body, say:

"Then there's nothing left to talk about."

"No," Cassian agreed, almost kindly. "There isn't."

He raised his hand.

* * *

Here's what nobody tells you about battles at a frozen gate: the ice decides more than the generals do.

Cassian's line came on in good order, ash-faced and silent, and it might have gone badly for us—we were outnumbered, outflanked, and running hot on grief—but the mountain intervened. Whether it was the digging, or the key in my pack waking up, or just the glacier finally tiring of being patient, the icefall let go.

Not all of it. One shoulder of it, a house-sized wedge that calved off with a crack like the sky breaking and came down on the eastern end of the camp. It missed everyone, mostly—it wasn't aimed, the mountain doesn't aim—but it buried the shrine, split the command tent in half, and turned disciplined spearmen into a stampede of individuals. In the white chaos, Sefa's voice cut like a whip: "*Left flank! Now!*"

What followed wasn't a battle so much as an argument conducted with elbows. We didn't try to hold ground; we pushed through, toward the gate, toward the breach point, and Cassian's people—farmers and deserters, remember, not killers—found that driving steel into people who'd walked in with open hands was harder than the sermons promised. Some fought anyway. Ilse broke a man's arm with a splint board and then set it properly, shouting apologies the whole time, which I think sums her up entirely.

I lost sight of Cassian in the snow-churn. I lost sight of everything. Then a hand caught my collar and spun me around, and it was Tam, face bare, eyes wet, axe nowhere in his grip.

"Mara—"

"Don't." I shoved him back. "Don't you *dare* say anything to me right now. Oren is dead. Because of wards you opened. Because of a door *you* unlocked for these people."

"I know." His voice broke on it. "I know. I'm not asking forgiveness. I'm asking you to look—" He grabbed my shoulders and turned me, and I saw it: along the western edge of the rout, a dozen of Cassian's people had stopped fighting. Had dropped their weapons, actually, and were standing in a knot around a grey-bearded woman with ash still on her face, and she was looking at me across the battlefield with an expression I couldn't name until I could: recognition. The look of someone hearing their own doubt spoken aloud by a stranger.

"They heard you," Tam said. "Before the fighting. What you said about the whispering. About the offer. Half of them have been having the dreams too, Mara. He tells them it's the voice of the dispossessed calling them home. But they dream of teeth. They've just never had anywhere else to put it." His grip tightened. "Let them come with us. Please. It's the only thing I can give you that's worth anything."

I wanted to hit him. I want to be honest about that, since honesty was apparently the theme of the evening. I wanted to hit him until the arithmetic changed, until Oren breathed again, until the guildhall stood unburnt against the starless sky. Instead I looked at the grey-bearded woman, and the knot of the uncertain around her, and I did the thing the Guild had actually taught me, the real lesson under all the lockpicking:

I gave them a reason to follow.

"You lot!" I shouted, and my voice carried the way it does in cold air, too far, too clear. "Greyford valley, three days south! There's food, there's work, there's no kneeling required! If you're done trading your children's future for a dead god's promises, walk south and ask for Delacroix's company! We'll take anyone who can carry a shovel or grow a turnip!"

The grey-bearded woman spat in the snow—an old gesture, a binding one, an oath in the oldest currency—and turned south. Eleven followed her. Then fourteen. Cassian screamed something from the heart of the battle, a word in a language I didn't know, and two of the fourteen flinched and turned back.

Twelve stayed walking. I counted them the way you count anything you can't quite believe.

* * *

We took the gate at full dark.

Not by force—by *speed*, which is force's smarter cousin. While Cassian rallied his core, his true believers, the ones past persuasion, Sefa led a squad up the goat track she swore she'd climbed during the reign of the last king but one, and we hit the breach crew from above. The seal-iron was already half-prised, black veins crawling through it where the old rite failed, and the air around it hummed with that sweet wrong smell so thick I could taste it on my fillings.

I put my hand on the iron. The key in my pack sang—one note, low, like a struck bell underwater—and the veins stopped crawling. Held. For now.

Below us, Cassian stood in the ruins of his camp with perhaps twenty faithful left, staring up at the gate, at me, at the twelve defectors dwindling south through the snow. He didn't rage. That was the worst part. He looked at me with something terribly like respect, and he mouthed words I couldn't hear but understood anyway:

*This isn't over. It's just beginning.*

"Come on," Sefa said, gripping my shoulder. Her voice was rough as gravel and twice as heavy. "Grief later. Gate now. The seal needs tending tonight, and I'm the only one left alive who knows the rite."

I looked back once—at the fires, at the shrine buried under its house of ice, at the place where Oren Vey had died with his hands on a stone, keeping faith with a sin. Then I turned to the gate, and the dark beyond it, and the long descent into the mountain that waited for us in the morning.

"Tomorrow we go down," I said to what remained of my company—to smugglers and healers and deserters and twelve ex-fanatics with shovels. "Tonight, we grieve. Tomorrow, we finish what six hundred years of liars started."

The key hummed against my back, warm as a sleeping animal.

It sounded, I thought, almost pleased.
