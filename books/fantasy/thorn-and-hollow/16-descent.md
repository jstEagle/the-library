## 16. Descent

The mountain swallowed us the way a debt collector swallows excuses—completely, and without any interest in our comfort.

I'd been in the deep tunnels before. Every smuggler who worked Thornfell had. There were three routes through the roots of the range that the Guild had kept secret for longer than anyone could remember, and I'd run all of them with contraband salt, untaxed brandy, and once—a story for another night—a live snow leopard in a crate that complained the entire way. The tunnels were familiar the way an old scar is familiar. You know where it is. You know what it cost.

But we weren't taking the smugglers' roads this time. We were going down past them, past the deepest marker Sefa had ever cut, into galleries that hadn't seen lamplight since before the Guild had a name.

"Cheerful place," Ilse said behind me, her voice flat in the way it got when she was frightened and refusing to spend anything on it. Her healer's kit rattled faintly at her hip with every step. "Remind me why we didn't take the nice surface road like normal pilgrims."

"Because the surface is full of Cassian's people, crown soldiers, and weather that wants us dead," I said. "Down here it's just the dark."

"Just the dark," she repeated. "Wonderful. I feel better already."

Sefa walked point with a lantern in one hand and a war-hammer across her back, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who had done this before—which, I reminded myself, she literally had. Sixty years ago, if her confession in the watchtower was true, and I'd stopped doubting it somewhere around the third night of frost-burned dreams. She'd helped bury the thing beneath Thornfell. She knew these galleries the way I knew the back alleys of Greyford: by scar tissue.

"Keep your hands on the guide-rope," she called back without turning. "The floor lies to you down here. Ice over stone looks like stone until it isn't."

We moved. Behind me came the rest of what remained of my company—thirty-one souls when we'd left Greyford, twenty-six now after the fight at Cassian's camp. Smugglers, deserters, two of Ilse's refugee boys who'd refused to stay behind, and old Bren with his crossbow and his endless opinions about everyone's footwork. My people. My family, or the closest thing to one the world had left me after the guildhall burned.

Every step down, I counted them. I don't know when I started doing that. Somewhere after the camp, maybe. You count your people the way a miser counts coin—not because you doubt the number, but because you can't bear to be surprised by it.

* * *

The murals started about four hours in.

At first I took them for water stains—dark sweeps on the tunnel walls, catching our lantern light wrong. Then Sefa stopped, held her light high, and went very still in a way that made my stomach drop before I understood why.

"Mara," she said. "Come look at this."

The wall was carved. Not painted—carved, deep into the living rock, and then filled with something that still gleamed faintly, some mineral ground fine as flour mixed with... I didn't want to guess what. The figures stood out pale against the dark stone like bones against soil.

A procession of people. Hundreds of them, rendered small and huddled, moving away from a mountain rendered huge. Above the mountain, a figure with its arms spread wide—not triumphant, I realized after a long moment. Bound. Chains ran from its wrists into the rock, and the chains were carved so they seemed to come from the people themselves, from their joined hands, their linked arms. Below, in a script older than any I knew, words marched in tight columns.

"Ilse," I said. "You read Old Lowland?"

"Some." She stepped up beside me, and I heard her breath catch. "It's a record. A... testimony, maybe. 'We did not conquer it. We did not defeat it.'" Her finger traced under the columns. "'We feared it, and we bound it, and we called the binding peace.'"

Nobody said anything for a while. Lantern flames are loud when you're listening to silence.

"Keep going," Sefa said quietly.

So we did, and the murals kept coming, gallery after gallery, and they told the story in stone the way Oren used to tell stories in smoke: slowly, and with the worst parts saved for last. The people in the carvings settled the lowlands. They built. They prospered. And in every panel, at the edge, always—the mountain, and the chained figure, growing smaller with each generation as the carvers' courage grew and their guilt shrank. By the later galleries the figure wasn't chained anymore. It was just a shape in the dark, barely worth carving. An afterthought. A bedtime story.

"They forgot," Bren said. His voice was strange, stripped of its usual bluster. "Generations on, they just... forgot what they'd done."

"No," Sefa said. "They didn't forget. They *tidied*. There's a difference, and it's worse." She touched one of the last panels, where robed figures laid ward-stones along the mountain's flanks. "This is the founding of the Guild. This is us. We weren't guards. We were jailers who got comfortable enough to call ourselves shepherds."

I thought of Oren Vey's hands on the ward-stones, humming along the border, patient as a man tending a garden. All those years of maintenance. All those centuries of quiet pride. *We keep the passes safe,* the Guild said, and meant it, and never asked safe for whom.

"The priest isn't entirely mad," I said, and hated how it sounded out loud.

"He's not mad at all," Sefa said. "That's the problem with him. Mad men you can outrun. Honest ones you have to answer."

Ilse snorted softly. "He answers questions with fire."

"Aye. But he asks good ones." I kept walking. "Let's hope the thing down there asks worse."

* * *

We made camp in a wide gallery where a spring had frozen mid-fall—a curtain of ice hanging from a crack in the ceiling, blue-white and glowing faintly with the same light that lived in the key against my chest. I'd taken to sleeping with it wrapped in wool inside my shirt, because when it touched bare skin I dreamed, and the dreams had a voice now, and the voice knew my name.

That night I dreamed anyway. Wool, apparently, was a suggestion rather than a wall.

*Mara Delacroix,* it said, in a tone like snow settling. *You walk the old road. You read the old walls. Tell me, jailer's daughter—when you looked at the chains, whose hands did you see?*

"Mine," I said, in the dream, in the dark. "Mine. That's why I'm here."

*Here to bind again. Here to carve me smaller.* Something moved in the dream that might have been laughter, if glaciers laughed. *You are kinder than your ancestors. You at least intend to say goodbye.*

I woke with frost on my eyelashes and my palms burning cold, and I lay there watching the ice-curtain glow and thought about how easy it would be to stop walking. To sit down in this beautiful blue light and let the mountain keep me. Nobody would ever know I'd quit.

Then Bren rolled over in his sleep and muttered something insulting about Ilse's footwork, and the ordinary ugliness of it made me laugh silently into my collar, and I got up, and I counted my people—twenty-six—and we went down.

* * *

On the second day below the murals, the tunnels changed character. The neat galleries gave way to raw fissures, places where the mountain had shifted over centuries, and the air grew warmer instead of colder, which Sefa said was wrong, which Sefa said was very wrong, which was becoming a refrain I could have done without.

Then Tam's footprints appeared.

I knew them the way you know anything you grew up with—by the drag of his left heel, the habit he'd never broken from an old ankle injury I'd helped him bandage when we were nineteen and stupid and laughing about it. The prints came out of a side fissure and merged with ours, heading down.

"He's ahead of us," Sefa said. "Half a day, maybe less. He's traveling light and he knows these marks better than I do—he was Oren's other student."

"He was my friend," I said.

"That too." She crouched over the prints. "Mara. He's not running from us. He's racing us. There's a difference, and it matters. A man running from you watches his back trail. A man racing you doesn't care if you follow. He only cares about arriving first."

I stared down the dark throat of the tunnel, at the faint warmth rising out of it like breath.

"Tam," I said to the dark, "you absolute idiot," and it came out more like a prayer than an insult, because that's what Tam Rusk had always been to me—an idiot I loved, and a splinter I couldn't dig out. He'd opened the wards believing he could end the famine. He'd burned half the lowlands' future to warm its present. And his logic still lodged in me like a hook: *the wards were sin, Mara. You saw the walls. Who are we to keep the lock turned?*

"We move faster," I said. "Bren, take six and push the pace. Ilse, keep the stragglers close. Sefa—"

"I know," she said. "Point. Same as ever." But she paused, and put her hand briefly on my shoulder, heavy as a roof beam. "Whatever's at the bottom, girl—we decide together. That's the whole point of having people. Remember it when he's standing there asking you to decide alone."

* * *

The icefall found us on the third day.

We heard it before we saw it—a groan rolling up through the rock, deep and structural, the sound a mountain makes when it shifts its weight. Then a crack like the sky breaking, and Sefa screaming *"RUN"* in a voice I'd only heard once before, at the guildhall, the day it burned.

We ran. The tunnel sloped steeply, the ice-curtain glow gone, replaced by the key's light bleeding through my shirt and the desperate bobbing of lanterns. Behind us the groan became a roar, and I understood with the clarity of pure animal terror that the mountain wasn't falling—it was *moving*, the buried thing turning in its sleep, and we were fleas on a shifting blanket.

The fissure forked. Left passage narrow, right passage wider, and no time—

"LEFT!" Sefa bellowed. "Left, all of you!"

Most of us went left. I was near the front, hauling Ilse by the wrist, and I felt the moment the mountain decided: a sound like the world's largest door closing, a rush of displaced air that snuffed every lantern at once, and then darkness absolute and the roar cutting off as though a hand had closed over the mountain's mouth.

When we relit the lanterns—hands shaking, Ilse swearing with a fluency that would have impressed the deserters—we stood before a wall of ice. Not a wall. A cliff face of it, filling the passage floor to ceiling, blue-veined and thick beyond guessing, still steaming faintly with fresh cold. Behind it, muffled to almost nothing, voices. Knocking. Bren's voice, unmistakable even through a glacier, shouting names.

"Bren!" I hammered the ice with my fist. "*BREN!*"

"MARA!" A pause. Muffled, distorted, but alive. "Eleven of us! We're whole! Ilse! Is Ilse—"

"I'm here!" Ilse pressed her palms to the ice. "I'm here, you great loud man, I'm here!"

I counted the faces around me. Fifteen. Fifteen plus eleven. Twenty-six. Everyone alive. The relief hit me so hard I had to lean against the ice to stand up.

Then I heard Sefa beside me, very quiet: "The right-hand passage comes out above the heart-chamber. It's the fast way. The left loops around—two days, maybe three, through the old galleries." She set her palm flat against the ice, and her face did something complicated. "We can't cut through this. Not with tools, not with time. And Tam has neither."

Through the ice, Bren's voice again, thinner: "Mara! What do we do?"

And there it was. The question, arriving exactly as promised, exactly as Sefa had warned it would, asking me to decide alone.

I looked at the ice. Eleven people behind it, trusting me. Fifteen around me, waiting on me. Two days lost if we looped—two days during which Tam Rusk, dragging his left heel, carrying whatever he carried, would reach the heart-chamber of a waking power and do God-knew-what with God-knew-what intentions, honest ones, good ones, the most dangerous kind.

Or we split. Fifteen go on fast; eleven find their own way around and catch up when they can. Except the right-hand passage above the heart-chamber was exactly where Cassian's people would be massing—I'd seen the besieged camp at the gate, and this deep, his fanatics would thicken like sediment. The fifteen would walk into the worst of it. The eleven would walk the long safe way and arrive late to whatever was left.

Neither choice was a choice. Both were sacrifices wearing different coats.

"Mara." Sefa's voice, low. "Decide."

I put my forehead against the ice. It was warm on my skin, which was wrong, which was the mountain breathing, which was everything down here being wrong, and I thought: *Oren, you old liar, you should have sent someone who didn't love so many people.*

"Bren," I called through the ice. "Can you hear me?"

"Loud and clear, boss."

"You have eleven. You have the long galleries. Take the left loop, mark your turns with chalk, and do not—" my voice cracked, and I hated it, and I let it, "—do *not* be heroes. No detours. No rescues. You arrive late and you arrive alive, and if we're not at the heart-chamber when you get there, you seal the entrance behind you and you take Ilse's route south and you *live*, do you hear me? That's an order. That's the last order I'll ever give you if you make me regret it."

Silence through the ice. Then Bren, rougher than I'd ever heard him: "Understood, boss." A pause. "Mara. Whatever's down there. Make it count."

"Always do," I lied, and turned away before my face could betray me, because they could hear my voice through a glacier but they didn't need to hear it break.

"Fifteen on," I said. "Fast passage. Sefa, lead. Ilse—" I caught her eye, and she was already looking at me, and there was no fear in her face, only that hard bright compassion of hers, the kind that signs itself up for things. "Stay close to me."

"Wasn't planning on wandering," she said.

We went. Down the right-hand fork, into air that grew warmer with every step, past rock that sweated and gleamed, toward the heart-chamber and whatever waited there—Tam, Cassian, the chained shape from the murals grown vast again in my mind's eye.

Behind us, faint as a heartbeat through miles of stone, I could almost believe I heard eleven sets of footsteps keeping pace.

Ahead, in the warm dark, something was singing.

It knew my name.
