## 20. Terms With the Crown

The crown sent its envoy to Greyford, which told me two things: first, that they were afraid to make me come to them, and second, that someone down in the capital had finally read the old maps and understood what Greyford sat between. You don't station a negotiation at a crossroads unless you're worried about who else might be watching.

They arrived on the ninth day after we came down from the mountain, when the false spring was still holding—thin sunlight, snow going grey at the edges, the whole valley smelling of wet stone and thaw. Nine riders under the sun-and-stag banner, and at their head a woman I'd have picked out as the real authority even without the sash: mid-fifties, silver at her temples, riding like she'd learned on campaign rather than in a paddock.

"That's Chancellor Vess," Ilse said beside me. She'd come out to stand in the mud of the road with the rest of us, arms crossed against the cold. "She negotiated the grain treaties during the famine years. They don't send her for small matters."

"They don't send her at all," Sefa would have said, but Sefa was still behind us in the mountain, being an anchor, and so it fell to me to notice what she would have noticed: nine riders is not an honor guard. Nine riders is a delegation that expects to walk back out again. Crowns only bring armies to negotiations they intend to win by other means.

"Then let's hear what small matter brings the Chancellor's own tongue to a mud street in Greyford," I said, and went out to meet her.

* * *

We held it in the temple—not because anyone present was devout, but because it had the biggest room and the thickest walls, and because there is a certain irony in conducting crown business under a roof built by people who believed no king could reach them here. The pews were gone; the refugees had burned most of the wood before the watchtower winter ended. What remained was stone floors, stone walls, and forty-odd of my people arranged along the sides with the studied casualness of smugglers who have been asked to look unarmed.

Vess took it all in without comment. She was good. I'll grant her that. She looked at the scarred faces, the mismatched weapons, the way Ilse stood slightly ahead of the Greyford townsfolk and Sefa's empty place had been given a chair anyway—someone had set it at the table with a mug of tea nobody would drink—and she adjusted whatever speech she'd rehearsed on the ride north.

"Mara Delacroix." She said it like a ledger entry. "Charged by the Crown of the Lowlands with—" and here she paused, and I watched her decide, deliberately, to abandon the formal phrasing— "with finding out what actually happened this winter, and making sure it doesn't happen again."

"Efficient," I said. "The last three sets of men you sent found out what happens when you point soldiers at people who've just buried a god. How's Captain Aldery? Still learning to write with his left hand?"

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, quickly banked. "Captain Aldery misjudged his orders. So did the men who burned your guildhall. The Crown does not deny these things happened. It wishes to discuss terms."

"There's a word I like." I leaned back. "Let's discuss terms, Chancellor. But first, a question, because I find negotiations go better when everyone's honest about the shape of the table." I put the key on the stone between us. Just set it there, plain as a loaf of bread. It hummed faintly, the way it always did now, warm as a sleeping animal. "Do you know what this is?"

Her eyes went to it and stayed there a beat too long. "I know it was reported missing from Thornfell by agents who are no longer reporting anything."

"Your agents knew about the wards." I kept my voice level, almost gentle, because I wanted her to hear how gently I could say a damning thing. "Your archives have the maintenance schedules. Your treasury has been paying a 'border survey stipend' to the Guild of the Hollow Road for four hundred years—that's us, Chancellor, that's the money we thought was smuggling profit, that's why the routes never made economic sense. Somebody in your government has known since before your grandmother was born that the roads were cover, the tolls were tribute, and the guild was a garrison pretending to be thieves. And every year, the stipend got smaller, and fewer ward-stones got replaced, and nobody asked why, because the mountains stayed quiet and quiet mountains don't appear in budgets."

Silence. Behind me, I heard Tam shift—he'd insisted on being present, and I'd allowed it, partly because he deserved to hear it and partly because I wanted the crown to see him sitting among us, pardoned-but-not-forgiven, proof that I keep my people close even when they've earned a knife in the dark.

"The Crown inherited the arrangement," Vess said carefully. "Documents decay. Institutional memory—"

"Decays fastest exactly where it's inconvenient," I finished. "Yes. I've heard that excuse from better liars than you, Chancellor, though none better dressed." I let her sit with it a moment. Then I took the key back, because there's a difference between showing a card and surrendering one, and Sefa taught me that difference at cost. "So. Terms. Here are mine, and I'll say them plainly, because I'm a smuggler by trade and plain dealing is all I know."

I counted them off on my fingers, and Ilse, bless her sharp tongue, had quill and parchment ready before I started, because she'd guessed I'd want witnesses.

"One. Amnesty. Full amnesty, written amnesty, for every soul in this company—for smugglers, deserters, the refugees who fought at the frozen gate, and Tam Rusk, whose crimes I will describe myself, in my own words, so that no clerk can inflate or soften them. Amnesty means pardoned, fed, housed, and left alone. Not pardoned and quietly disappeared. If one of mine vanishes into a crown dungeon inside a year, I will consider the treaty broken, and I want you to understand what breaking it looks like now." I turned my palm up. Frost-scarred, both hands, from the nights the key talked to me. "You've seen what's under those mountains. So have I. Up close. I am the only person alive the binding answers to, Chancellor, and I'd very much like to remain reasonable."

"Two. Greyford eats. Grain from the royal stores, seed stock for spring planting, timber and masons for the houses the cold took, and healers' supplies—Ilse has a list longer than your arm and more detailed than your ledgers. This isn't charity. Half the lowlands' grain moved through guild roads that are closed forever now; the crown needs working towns on the southern passes more than Greyford needs your charity. We're negotiating a supply chain, not begging."

"Three." And here I paused, because this was the one that mattered, the one Oren had died without knowing he'd won. "The truth goes in the law. The full history—the wards, the sealing, the people the old kings displaced to build them, the guild's true founding, all of it—written into the crown chronicles and taught in the law-schools. Not the sanitized version where brave knights imprisoned a monster. The version where the monster was a wounded thing older than the kingdom and the heroes were smugglers doing maintenance work for four centuries on a stipend that shrank every reign. My people die in that story if it stays buried. Their names get written right, or nothing else we discuss today is worth the parchment."

* * *

Vess was quiet a long time. To her credit, she didn't waste breath protesting the stipend revelation—she'd have verified it before she ever mounted her horse; chancellorship selects for that.

"You ask a great deal," she said finally.

"I ask exactly what the crown owes and has failed to pay," I said. "That's not great. That's arithmetic."

"And what does the Crown receive? Beyond, I gather, your continued reasonableness."

"Continued reasonableness is worth more than you think—it's the only thing standing between your kingdom and a renegotiation of the binding conducted by whoever digs fastest." I let that land. "But fine. Commerce. Here's what you get. One: the passes stay open—new roads, honest ones, patrolled by my people under crown commission. Smugglers make the best border-guards, Chancellor; we know every trick because we invented most of them. Two: the key stays bound to me and to the mountain, and its maintenance continues, and the knowledge of it gets passed to people I choose—which means the crown gets continuity instead of another four hundred years of decayed documents. Three: you get the lowlands through next winter without famine, because Greyford fed and the passes moving means the grain moves."

"And the guild?"

"The guild is dead." I said it steadily, though something in my chest pulled at the words like a scab. "Burned in its hall with its master. What rises here is something else—unnamed yet, unhidden, on your books instead of under them. You'll hate it less than you fear it, Chancellor. That's been my experience with honest neighbors."

She studied me for a long moment, this mud-street smuggler with frost-burned hands sitting in a burnt-out temple dictating terms to the Lowlands. I watched her do the arithmetic I'd already done: the alternative to me was Cassian's scattered faithful, or Tam's logic in some hungrier head, or simply nothing—no binding, no maintenance, and the mountain waking on its own schedule. She was too good at her work not to reach the total.

"The amnesty list will require review," she said at last. "Names, dates, crimes."

"Ilse has them all written. Cross-referenced. In duplicate." Ilse didn't look up from her parchment, but her mouth twitched.

"The Greyford aid can move within the month."

"It moves within the fortnight, or the town eats bark before your convoys arrive and I negotiate with whoever's angrier."

"A fortnight," she agreed. "And the histories... the Crown Chronicle office will resist. They resist everything."

"Then tell them the alternative is that I write it myself and sell copies in every market square in the lowlands, illustrated, with the parts about crown negligence in bold type. I have distribution channels, Chancellor. Old habits."

For the first time all day, Vess smiled—a thin, genuine, tired thing, the smile of a woman who had expected bandits and found a counterpart. "You would, wouldn't you."

"On good parchment. With woodcuts."

She extended her hand across the stone. I took it. Her grip was dry and strong and she didn't flinch at the frost scars, which I noted and filed away as the single most respectful thing any crown official has ever done in my presence.

* * *

They rode out at dusk, and I stood in the street with Ilse and Tam and watched the nine riders shrink into the grey distance.

"You realize," Ilse said, "that you just became an institution."

"Don't say that word to me."

"Somebody has to maintain the binding after you. Somebody has to hold the crown to the treaty. Somebody has to teach the next generation which stones hum and which ones scream." She shrugged. "Institutions are just families that survived long enough to write things down. You'd know—you wrote ours down today."

Tam said nothing for a while. When he spoke, his voice was rough. "You put my name in the amnesty. You described my crimes yourself."

"Someone had to say them true. A clerk would have made you either a monster or a martyr, and you're neither. You're a man who broke a thing he loved because he loved something else more. The law needed to hear it plain so it could forgive you accurately." I glanced at him. "Forgiveness is ongoing work, Tam. The amnesty buys you the time to do it. It doesn't do it for you."

He nodded slowly, looking east toward the pass, toward the mountain where Sefa held her post and the murals ran down into the dark. "Oren would have hated this," he said. "All this daylight. All this... paperwork."

"Oren spent fifty years keeping secrets that were eating him alive," I said. "He'd have hated it for a season. Then he'd have slept better than he ever did." I turned back toward the temple, where lamplight was starting in the windows, where forty-odd survivors were waiting to hear whether they were criminals or citizens. "Come on. Both of you. We should tell them before the rumor does."

And we went in together—but at the door, I stopped, because the key had grown warm against my ribs in a way I hadn't felt since the heart-chamber. Not warning. Not seduction. Something else.

Almost like approval.

Or almost like attention.

I couldn't tell the difference yet. And that, I thought as I stepped inside to face my family, was a question for tomorrow—and possibly for the mountain.
