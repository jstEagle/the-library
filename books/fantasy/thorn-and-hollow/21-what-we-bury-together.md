## 21. What We Bury Together

The first lie of spring came on a Tuesday.

I know it was a Tuesday because Ilse had started keeping a calendar again, chalked on the doorframe of the new hall in Greyford, and she'd marked the day with a small furious tick as if the season owed her money. The light came up gold over the eastern ridge, warm on the face, smelling of wet earth and thaw, and half the town walked out into the square without their coats, laughing like fools, like people who had forgotten what a trick felt like.

By noon the frost was back. By dusk the false dawn had curdled into the same grey starless nothing we'd lived under since the binding, and the coats went back on, and nobody laughed anymore, but nobody wept either. That was the shape of the year: hope arriving early, staying briefly, leaving a receipt.

I stood in the square with my hands in my pockets and watched them all take it, and I thought — not for the first time, and I suspect not for the last — *you did this.*

Not the frost. Not the winter. But the reason for it, or part of the reason, or enough of the reason that when Mara looked at me across a crowded room her eyes did a thing I can only describe as arithmetic. She wasn't counting what I'd cost her. She was counting what I still owed, and whether the ledger would ever close.

It wouldn't. I'd stopped expecting it to somewhere around the heart-chamber, when I put my knife toward Cassian Vole instead of toward her, and discovered that one good act does not launder a hundred bad ones. Forgiveness, it turns out, is not a door that opens. It's a road you walk, and the other person decides daily whether you're still allowed on it.

Mara decided daily. To her credit, she never pretended otherwise.

* * *

We were building a guild that wasn't a guild, which is the sort of sentence Oren Vey would have hated, may he rest however dead men rest when soldiers burn their halls down around them.

The old Guild of the Hollow Road had survived three centuries on a single principle: *hide.* Hide the roads, hide the routes, hide the ward-stones, hide the truth, hide behind smugglers' grins and forged manifests while the real work hummed beneath your boots. Secrecy was our spine. It was also, as it turned out, our rot. You cannot hide a duty for four hundred years without eventually forgetting it's a duty at all. You start thinking of it as property. And property, as any smuggler will tell you, exists to be sold.

So the new thing had no name yet, which everyone agreed was healthier than giving it a bad one. It had a hall — the old tithe-barn at the edge of Greyford, roof patched, floor swept, a hearth big enough to warm thirty people who mostly didn't like each other yet. It had a roster: deserters from the crown garrison, three of Cassian's defected faithful who kept flinching at loud noises, a dozen Greyford farmers with strong backs and stronger grievances, and whatever remained of the Hollow Road's scattered survivors, trickling in through the passes one and two at a time, each of them carrying the same expression — a smuggler arriving at a fence post where his whole world used to be.

And it had Mara Delacroix, who had never asked to lead anything larger than a mule train and now led this, badly some days, brilliantly on others, and always with the key on a cord around her neck, resting against her sternum like a second heartbeat.

"It should have a name," said Ilse, who ran the roster because she ran everything, being constitutionally incapable of letting a list go untended. "People follow names."

"People follow food," said Brannic, the tallest of the deserters, who had once been a sergeant and now carried timber because Mara had told him rank was a debt here, not a wage. "Name it after we've eaten."

"We're naming it after we've *earned* it," Mara said, from the head of the trestle table, where she sat with maps spread out and her boots, as ever, on the bench like a woman in her own kitchen rather than a woman deciding the fate of the lowlands. "Names are promises. We don't make those yet."

She caught me looking at her and her eyes did the arithmetic thing, quick, almost gentle, and then she moved on to the next item, which was seed grain, which was always the next item, because the false dawns kept killing the plantings and Greyford's stores were a rumor of themselves.

I'll say this for her: she gave me work. Hard work, visible work, work done in front of people. She could have buried me in the tunnels with Sefa. She could have sent me south and never spoken to me again, and no one in the company would have called it cruelty. Instead she handed me a sledge, a cart of raw stone, and the worst job in the lowlands, and I understood it for exactly what it was.

Penance you choose is bearable. Penance assigned by someone who has the right to assign it — that's the only kind that means anything.

* * *

Here is what the job was.

When the binding closed, the old wards died with it. Every ward-stone along the Thornfell border — the ones the guild had maintained in secret for centuries, humming their quiet song to keep the buried thing asleep — had gone dark and cold, ordinary rock now, monuments to an order that no longer existed. But the binding hadn't destroyed the principle. It had changed it. The power beneath the mountain was no longer caged; it was *bound*, willingly, bound to the mountain itself through Sefa Ironhale, who stayed below as its living anchor and who had smiled at me when she volunteered, the way old soldiers smile at young ones going off to wars they've already survived once.

Sefa, who had helped bury the thing a lifetime ago. Sefa, who taught me to read a pass in the dark. Sefa, whose goodbye I hadn't been present for and didn't deserve to be, and whose absence sat in the company like a chair kept empty at table.

The new stones — Mara called them seeds, and the name stuck because Mara's names always stuck — were different. Small, fist-sized, cut from the deep galleries' living rock, each one keyed to the covenant rather than the cage. They didn't imprison anything. They *listened*. Planted along the old roads, sunk into the earth at crossroads and shrines and the thresholds of towns, they would hum faintly when the bound power stirred, and sing louder if it ever strained against its promise, and carry word down the lines faster than any rider. A nervous system instead of a wall. An honesty instead of a secret.

And someone had to plant them. Alone, mostly, on the dead roads, in the cold, in country where every village knew the guild's face and half of them had lost someone to the famine the starless winter worsened.

Someone. Me.

"You realize," I told Mara the night she handed me the first cartload, "that you've invented a punishment worse than hanging."

"I know," she said. She was sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, which was how she talked about hard things. "That's why it's you."

"You could send anyone."

"Anyone would think it was labor." She set the knife down and looked at me straight, and there was no arithmetic in it then, just the flat honest weight of her. "You'll know it's amends. That's the point, Tam. I'm not punishing you. I'm trusting you with the proof that you understand what you broke. Don't make me wrong."

I didn't sleep much that night. In the morning I took the cart north.

* * *

The first stone went into the ground at Hollowford Cross, where the old road forked toward the pass, in soil so cold it rang under the mattock. I dug the hole myself, knelt in it, and set the seed-stone upright the way Sefa had shown me to set trail-markers a decade ago — *deep enough that frost won't heave it, true enough that a stranger could find it, plain enough that it needs no explaining.*

Then I did the rite. There wasn't much of a rite. Mara had written it on a scrap of paper and made me memorize it, and it was less than twenty words, most of which were an apology. You laid your palm on the stone and you said: *We know what was done here. We do not ask it to sleep. We ask it to be heard.* And then you waited.

Nothing happened, the first time. Of course nothing happened. I sat back on my heels in the freezing mud feeling like a man who'd prayed to a locked door.

On the fourth stone — up past the shrine at Coldwell, where the refugees had burned offerings all winter — something happened. A hum. Faint, below hearing more than in it, rising through my knees and my palms and the fillings of my teeth, and the stone warmed under my hand like a bird coming back to life, and for one absurd moment I felt *acknowledged*, which is a ridiculous thing to feel from a rock, except that it wasn't a rock, not entirely, not anymore, and neither, I suppose, was I.

I sat there until dark. I don't fully know why. Partly it was the strangeness of being forgiven by geology. Partly it was that the hum sounded, very faintly, like Sefa's voice, and I wasn't ready to come down from that.

Partly — and this is the part I've only admitted to the page — it was that kneeling in the dirt saying *we know what was done here* was the first time in my life the words had been true when they came out of my mouth. I'd spent years as the guild's smooth talker, the one who could sell a lie to the man it was stolen from. Every apology I'd ever given before this one had been currency. This one cost.

* * *

Ilse found me at the ninth stone.

I'd planted it at the edge of Greyford's common field, close enough to town that children could watch, and they did — a ring of them, mittened and solemn, treating me like a man burying treasure, which in a sense I was. Ilse stood at the back with her arms crossed and her breath steaming, waiting with the patience of a woman who has stitched wounds shut and knows better than to grab at the patient.

"They're saying you talk to them," she said, when the kids had been shooed home.

"The stones?"

"The stones, the mountain, the thing under it. Brannic swears he heard you apologizing to a hole in the ground."

"I was. It's in the liturgy."

"Mm." She crouched beside the fresh-planted stone, studied it with a healer's eye, and touched two fingers to its surface the way she'd take a pulse. Whatever she felt made her eyebrows move. "It likes you," she said, sounding annoyed about it.

"That makes one of us."

"Don't do that." Her voice didn't rise. It never rose; it just got sharper, like a blade drawn slowly. "Don't do the self-pitying smuggler routine at me, Tam Rusk. I've watched you dig nine holes in frozen earth and I've watched Mara count every single one of them like coins, and I've watched you accept both without whining, which is more than I expected, so hear this while I'm in a good mood." She tapped the stone. "This matters. What you're doing matters. The town watches you plant these and they see a man doing the hardest work there is, which isn't the digging. And when the famine comes back — and it will, the false dawns see to that — these stones are going to be the difference between panic and warning. So stop talking like a man serving a sentence and start talking like a man doing a job, because the children are learning which one you are."

She stood, brushed off her knees, and paused.

"For what it's worth," she added, not looking at me, "forgiveness isn't a gift. Anyone who tells you it's a gift is selling something. It's a practice. Like stitching. You do it daily or the wound opens." A beat. "She knows that. She's practicing. So are you. That's the whole of it."

She walked back toward town. I knelt there a while longer with my hand on the warm stone, and if a grown man wept briefly into frozen mud at a crossroads, well. The children had gone home. The stone kept my secret, or the mountain did, and both of them had practice.

* * *

By the time the cart was empty I'd planted nineteen stones from Hollowford to the foot of the pass, and the roads had begun, very quietly, to change.

Not visibly. The passes stayed sealed — the binding had drained the old magic out of them for good, and the smugglers' hidden ways were closed forever, collapsed or simply gone dim, routes that had existed for centuries fading like footprints in snow. That was the price, and Mara paid it without blinking, though I saw her stand at the mouth of the Hollow Road's great gate for a long time the day they confirmed it, one hand flat on the stone, saying nothing, mourning a thing that had been her whole childhood.

But along the open roads, the low roads, the roads ordinary people traveled — those had begun to hum. Travelers reported it: a warmth in the ground at certain crossings, a sense of being *watched over* rather than watched, a strange courage on the starless nights, as if the dark had edges now. The stones listened, and the bound power answered through them, and the answer, filtered through covenant instead of cage, came out as something almost like companionship.

The villages noticed. Villages notice everything; it's how they survive. And what they noticed was that the guild's old ghost — the smuggler, the liar, the man who'd helped break the sky — was walking their roads planting singing rocks and apologizing to holes in the ground, and that Mara Delacroix's new unnamed thing had sent him, openly, with no escort and no disguise.

Oren Vey would have fainted. Hiding had been the spine. And here was the replacement spine, built out in daylight where anyone could look at it, and it held.

* * *

Mara met me at the twentieth site herself, at the threshold of the high pass, where the last stone of the line would look up the mountain toward the place where Sefa kept her long watch.

She arrived alone, which surprised me, wrapped in a grey cloak with frost in her hair, and she stood watching while I dug. Neither of us spoke until the stone was set and my palm was on it and the hum rose up through the frozen ground like a note held under water.

"Heard you had a visitor," she said.

"Cassian?" My stomach dropped. He was alive — broken, burned, half-mad since the heart-chamber, but alive, and fanatics mend the way rivers find the sea.

"No. Worse." She almost smiled. "A crown herald. From the south. Riding under a white banner, wanting to know why songs are spreading through the lowlands about singing stones, and who exactly is planting them, and whether the crown ought to be worried."

"And what did you tell them?"

"That the stones are ours, the roads are open, the covenant is public record, and the crown is welcome to send someone to kneel in the mud and apologize too, if they'd like to learn how it's done." She crouched, laid her own hand on the fresh stone, and I watched the hum answer her differently than it answered me — deeper, older, the way a dog greets its master versus its master's difficult friend. "They'll come anyway. Chastened crowns stay chastened exactly as long as it takes them to feel strong again. We bought a year with the treaty, maybe two. Then they'll want the key, or the stones, or the mountain, or all three."

"So we keep planting."

"So we keep planting." She stood, and looked up the pass, into the dark where the stars weren't. One year, nearly, since the night they went out. "You know what tomorrow is."

I did. The anniversary. The company had been circling it for a week the way people circle a grave.

"She'd have hated a ceremony," I said.

"Sefa? She'd have demanded one and heckled it." Mara pulled her cloak tighter. "Come up with us tomorrow. All the way to the gate. You've earned the walk."

"Mara." I made myself say it plainly, no smuggler's varnish. "I haven't earned anything. That's rather the point of me."

She considered that with the seriousness she gave everything, then shook her head.

"You've got it backwards, Tam. Earning's not a gate you pass through. It's the walking." She turned downhill, then paused. "Nineteen stones. Twenty tomorrow. Keep walking."

I knelt back down, laid my palm on the stone one more time, and felt the hum climb through me toward the mountain — toward the bound and listening dark, toward the empty chair at the table far below, toward whatever Sefa had become down there in the deep galleries, anchor and keeper and the bravest coward I ever loved.

And far above us, at the very top of the black sky, so faint I'd have sworn I imagined it if the stone under my hand hadn't warmed like a held breath —

— a single star flickered.

Once. Twice. Like something opening one eye after a long sleep, checking whether the world below had finally learned its lesson.

I stood very still in the frozen mud, heart hammering, and did not call out, and did not run, and did not stop smiling for a long, long time.

Tomorrow, I thought. Tomorrow we walk up and tell her.

The star waited, patient as a promise, and began — very slightly — to grow.
