## 22. First Star Over Thornfell

A year ago I would have told you that the only good reason to climb a mountain at dawn was that someone was paying you to do it, and paying badly.

Now here I am, unpaid by anyone but my own stubborn heart, leading six people up a pass that used to be a smuggler's road and is about to become something else entirely. The morning is cold enough to bite through wool and pride alike, which in Thornfell counts as a mild day. Above us, threaded through a sky scrubbed clean as new glass, hangs a single star — pale, patient, and utterly wrong for daylight, which is to say exactly on time.

We named it before we agreed on anything else about the new order. Ilse wanted something clinical: *The Anomaly*, she said, because naming things grandly is how cults get started. Sefa — before she stayed behind — had lobbied hard for *The Old Woman*, on the grounds that it watched everything and approved of nothing. In the end Tam won by default, because while we were arguing he simply started calling it *the First Star* in his reports and the name stuck like burrs.

Tam is good at names sticking. It's one of the many things I have decided to forgive him for, mostly.

"Delacroix." Ilse's voice carries up the switchback without her having raised it, the way it does when she wants you to know she's said your name twice already. "You're doing the thing."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you stare at the sky like it owes you money."

"It did owe me money," I say. "For most of a year, the sky owed me an entire firmament. I'm just checking on the repayment schedule."

She snorts. Below her, the rest of the company picks their way over scree that a year ago was buried under forty feet of avalanche and ice. The binding took the passes' magic when it closed — that was the price, ours to pay and paid — but it turns out mountains don't need magic to be climbed. They just need stubbornness and decent boots, both of which Thornfell has never lacked.

The company is small. That's deliberate. Six of us, no banners, no law but what we carry in our own hands: me; Ilse, who healed half of Greyford through the worst winter in living memory and complains about it with the consistency of a dripping roof; Bren and Kesh, twins out of the lowland deserters, who can read a trail the way priests read scripture and trust it about as much; old Wren Halloway, who claims to be seventy and moves like fifty and lies about everything else so consistently we've stopped keeping score; and at the rear, walking light as guilt, Tam Rusk.

Traitor. Saboteur. Brother. He answers to all three, though only I use the first two anymore, and only when I want to watch him flinch. It keeps us honest.

We're not climbing to hide anything. That's the difference between this year and every year before it. We're climbing because the ward-stones need tending, and tending them in daylight, on open roads, under a star everyone in the valley can see — that *is* the work now. Oren Vey spent his whole life guarding a secret that was eating the Guild alive from the inside, and I spent mine learning the routes that kept the secret fed. I've retired from secrets. They're bad employers.

The key rides against my chest where it always has, wrapped in oiled silk, warm as a held bird. It doesn't whisper anymore. That took months — the dreams stopped sometime around midwinter, the frost-burned palms healed by spring — but it still warms when I'm near the stones, and once, only once, high on the Greyford ridge, it pulsed slow and deep, like something enormous turning over in its sleep and finding itself comfortable.

I choose to read that as approval. I've earned the right to a little optimism. I bought it at full price.

* * *

The first ward-stone stands where the treeline gives up, a grey shoulder of granite taller than Wren and older than any nation that claims this border. When I was a girl running contraband past it, I thought it was just rock — one more featureless monolith the mountains grow like warts. Now I know better. Now I put my bare hand flat against it, the way Sefa taught me in the tunnels when we were both younger and the world was simpler and lying to ourselves less expensively, and I wait.

The hum comes up through my palm bones. Faint. Steady. Alive.

"There she is," Wren says softly. She's beside me without having seemed to cross the intervening ground, which is her particular gift. "Singing again."

"Singing quietly."

"She's had a long year. So have you. Quiet's allowed."

I keep my hand there longer than the rite strictly requires. Somewhere beneath my feet, down past galleries of murals that show exile painted as triumph, past the icefall that split our company and nearly ended it, past the heart-chamber where I offered acknowledgment instead of a cage and got, in return, a changed sky — somewhere down there, something vast and wounded is bound to this mountain not as a prisoner but as a resident, and once a season I climb up to knock politely on its door.

It never answers in words. But the stone hums, and the star holds, and the rivers run, and last autumn Greyford brought in a barley harvest that made grown farmers weep into their beer. Acknowledgment, it turns out, goes a long way. Who knew. Certainly not the kings and guildmasters who spent nine hundred years preferring chains.

"You're brooding," Ilse says, arriving at my elbow with her medical bag and her expression of professional disapproval. "Give me your hands."

"They're fine."

"Frost-burned palms scar poorly when their owner refuses to moisturize. Hands."

I give her the hands. She inspects the old burns, prods a callus, pronounces me "adequately maintained, which for you is a festival," and slathers something on me that smells like pine tar and regret. Around us the others are working — Bren and Kesh clearing debris from the stone's base, Wren unpacking the seed-satchels.

Ah, yes. The seeds.

That's Tam's contribution, and I'll admit it grudgingly even though he'll never hear me do it. Ward-stone seeds: cuttings from the living granite, packed in ash and mountain moss, planted along the old roads where the Guild's network once ran. They grow slowly — a hand's breadth a year, Wren says — but they grow, and each one hums when it takes root, and in fifty years there will be a lattice of them across the lowlands, singing to each other and to the thing below, a covenant written in stone instead of silence.

Oren would have hated it. Openly tended wards? Recorded history? A guild charter that says, in plain ink, *we sealed something here, and it was wrong of us, and we stay to make it right*? He'd have called it suicide. He'd also, I think, after a long pull of whiskey and a longer silence, have called it necessary. He died before he could decide. Cassian Vole's fire made the decision for him, along with the guildhall and most of my childhood.

I don't hate Cassian anymore. That surprised me most of all. His conviction was real even if his methods were ash, and some of his followers plant seeds alongside us now, and one of them — a quiet woman named Odile who once held a knife to my throat at the frozen gate — taught Kesh the old displaced-tongue blessing for new stone. The original sin of the wards was real. Exile dressed up as protection. All we've done differently is admit it out loud and ask instead of compel. Apparently that was the whole trick. Nine hundred years of blood and winter, and the whole trick was *asking*.

"Delacroix." Tam, at the trail's edge, looking back down-valley. "Riders."

My hand finds the knife at my hip out of pure habit before my brain catches up and reminds me that riders on this road are almost certainly ours. Almost certainly. Habit keeps you alive; paranoia keeps you company. I've learned to tell them apart, mostly.

Four horses, moving easy. Royal blue and grey — crown colors, but the faded, practical ones of the border survey corps, not the parade regalia I remember from the pass ambush a year ago, when soldiers offered protection with drawn steel and I learned that the crown had known about the wards all along and let them rot anyway. These days the crown sends clerks where it once sent blades. Terms With the Crown, we called that chapter of our lives privately. Amnesty for the company. Aid for Greyford, delivered and audited by Ilse personally, which is the only audit in the kingdom that has never found a discrepancy, because nobody has the nerve. And the Guild's true history written into law — the wards, the sealing, the exile of the mountain's first peoples, all of it, in the archives where any literate citizen can read what was done in their name.

Justice outside the law became reform within it. On my terms. I still marvel sometimes that they agreed. Then I remember the winter, the frozen rivers, the dead crops, and I stop marveling. Fear is a wonderful negotiator. Mercy, it turns out, is a better one, but fear gets you to the table.

The lead rider dismounts at the base of the switchback. Young, windburned, carrying a document case and the careful posture of someone delivering news they've been coached not to apologize for.

"Mara Delacroix?" she calls up.

"No one by that name," I call back, pleasantly.

She blinks. Behind me, Wren makes a noise that might be a laugh or might be a cough. The woman consults her case, visibly reorganizing. "Then — no one by that name. You're requested. The Chancellor's office. There's been—" she hesitates, and the hesitation is what puts the cold back in my spine, because rehearsed couriers don't hesitate, "—there's been a development. At the mountain."

Ilse is suddenly very still beside me. Tam has come up the trail without seeming to move, the way he does, and his eyes meet mine with a year's worth of shared catastrophe compressed into a single look.

"Define development," I say.

"The survey team at the lower gallery," the courier says. "They were taking readings off the murals. The depth soundings came back wrong. The chamber—" she swallows. "The chamber measures deeper than it did last month. As if something down there has been digging."

The key against my breastbone pulses once. Slow. Deep. Not alarmed, exactly.

*Interested.*

* * *

We stand the courier a hot meal at the waystation below the pass — Ilse's rule: no one delivers bad news on an empty stomach, it sours the digestion and the messenger both — and by the time we're done, the four of us who matter have gathered at the window looking up at the peak, and the single star above it, burning pale and steady through the brightening afternoon.

"Digs could be natural," Kesh offers. "Settling. Meltwater channels."

"In winter?" Bren says. "Nothing's melting."

"Ice quakes shift chambers all the time."

"Not *upward*," Wren says quietly, and nobody argues, because Wren has been underground more years than the rest of us combined and knows what shifting stone sounds like when it's merely geology versus when it's ambition.

Tam speaks last, which is his habit and his penance both. "It bound willingly. You felt it. I stood in that chamber, Mara, I saw what you saw. It chose the mountain."

"It chose," I agree. "Choosing isn't the same as being content forever. Ask any married couple." I touch the silk-wrapped warmth at my chest. "Or maybe the binding is holding fine and the readings are wrong. Or maybe it's making room. For what, I don't know. Yet."

"For us?" Ilse says. Not fearful. Clinical. Which is worse.

"For someone," I say. "Down is patient. Down has all the time ever minted. The question isn't whether it's moving. The question is whether it's moving toward something or away from something, and either way—" I turn from the window, and find that I'm smiling, which surprises me, until I realize it isn't surprise at all. This is the job now. This is what I traded the roads for, the name, the easy silver and the shadowed passes. Not peace. Vigilance. Watchfulness with good boots. "Either way, we go down and ask. Politely. Like neighbors."

"Six of us?" Bren asks.

"Six of us. Small companies move fast and lie well." I'm already repacking the satchel, hands remembering the old rhythms, the weight of a road kit assembled for descent instead of ascent. "Kesh, chart the lower galleries from Wren's old maps tonight. Bren, horses and cold-weather gear by dawn. Tam—"

He waits. A year ago he'd have argued, charmed, maneuvered. Now he just waits, and there's something in that patience that forgives him a little more than yesterday did.

"Come with me to the heart-chamber," I say. "If it's waking toward something, I want your eyes there. You opened the door once. Maybe you'll see it opening again before the rest of us do."

Something moves across his face — gratitude, or grief, or the place where those share a wall. "Always," he says.

Outside, the light is going gold and long, and the First Star is rising over Thornfell ahead of schedule, early and eager, watching us the way Sefa used to watch us — approving of nothing, missing nothing. Somewhere below the stone, below the murals of exile, below the icefall and the galleries and the chamber where mercy proved harder than any blade, something old stirs and digs and waits to be asked what it wants.

I shoulder the pack. My family gathers around me — healer, traitor, twins, liar, all of them volunteers, all of them stayers, none of them bound by anything but choosing.

"Well," I say. "Let's go see what our neighbor built."

The star holds. We descend.
