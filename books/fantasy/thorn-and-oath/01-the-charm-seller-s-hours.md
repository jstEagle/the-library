## 1. The Charm Seller's Hours

The trick to surviving Vell was to be useful in exactly one way and invisible in every other.

Noor Brightwater had learned that lesson at eleven years old, from a woman named Maren who sold boiled chestnuts three stalls down and had once, briefly, been a witch herself before the Purges taught everyone better manners. *Be dull,* Maren had said, folding a chestnut into Noor's palm like it was a coin. *Dull people die of old age. Interesting people die of interesting things.* Then she'd coughed blood into her apron for two winters and died of something perfectly ordinary, which Noor chose to consider a victory.

So Noor was dull. Noor was *aggressively* dull. Noor Brightwater, charm-seller, second stall past the fountain on Cordwainer's Row, purveyor of luck-knots, fever-strings, and little braided wristbands that were supposed to keep your sweetheart faithful — though honestly, if you needed a bracelet for that, the problem wasn't magical.

The luck-knots were the best sellers. They took real skill: red cord, seven loops, a particular tension in the final pull that most people couldn't get right no matter how many times they watched. Customers liked to watch Noor's fingers work, quick and sure, and they said things like *you've got clever hands* and *my grandmother made these, before the Ban*, and they never noticed that when Noor finished the seventh loop, their fingertips flickered. Just barely. A warmth under the skin, a shimmer like heat off summer cobbles, gone before anyone could blink.

Noor blinked first. Every time. It was practically liturgical.

"Two knots," said a woman at the stall, slapping down copper. "One for my son's apprenticeship, one for my roof. It leaks."

"The knot doesn't fix roofs," Noor said. Standard disclaimer. Warden-approved language. "It fixes luck. Your roofer will suddenly have an opening in his schedule."

"And my son?"

"Your son will suddenly be the sort of apprentice masters fight over." Noor's hands moved as they spoke, cord looping through cord, muscle-memory older than the law that outlawed it. "Assuming he deserves it. The knots are honest that way."

The woman snorted but bought both anyway. Everyone knew the knots were just knots. That was the beauty of them — deniable, harmless, legal. A charm-seller in Vell sold the *idea* of magic, carefully packaged so it couldn't possibly work, because anything that might actually work got you a hood over your head and a cell beneath the Wardenhall.

Which was why Noor kept the real thing buried deep, deep down, wrapped in seven layers of not-thinking-about-it.

* * *

The night market ran dusk to midnight, lantern-hour to curfew-bell, and it smelled like fried onions and tallow smoke and wet stone. Vell was built in layers — old city under new city under older city still — and the market lived in the seam between the newest layer and the one beneath, where the cobblestones didn't quite match and doorways opened onto stairs that went down instead of up. Officially the night market was licensed. Unofficially, everyone knew half its vendors paid rent to people who didn't exist on any ledger, and the other half paid rent to Tam Voss, who owned the building and pretended she was only a landlady and not, historically, one of the more successful smugglers to ever run the Emberhold road.

Tam collected Noor's stall fee every eighth day and never asked questions, which was worth more than the coin.

"You're low on fever-strings," Tam said tonight, appearing at the stall's edge like she'd always been there, the way large weathered women appear when they've decided to appear. She was sixty-something, built like a dock crane, with grey hair cut short and hands scarred from forty years of rope-work. "Girl across the row has flux. Whole tenement'll have it by week's end."

"I know. I'm making more tomorrow."

"Make them tonight." Tam dropped the coins in the dish without counting them, which was either trust or an insult, depending. "Flux doesn't wait for business hours."

"Fever-strings are just thread and rosemary," Noor said mildly. "You know that."

"Mm," said Tam, in a tone that suggested she knew several things, none of which she intended to say out loud, and left.

Noor looked after her for a moment longer than necessary. This was another rule of survival in Vell: never let anyone see you look after them longer than necessary. Looking-after was a kind of knowing, and knowing was a debt, and debts between two people who were both hiding something could come due in ugly ways.

But Tam was already gone, absorbed into the crowd, and Noor let their shoulders drop a half-inch and turned back to the stall.

* * *

The Wardens came by twice before midnight, which was average.

They always came the same way: two of them, grey-and-white cloaks, boots striking the cobbles in unison like they practiced it, which they probably did. The tall one — Halvard, Noor had privately named him years ago, though never aloud — would walk the row slow, letting his gaze drag across each stall like a hand checking pockets. His partner, younger, round-faced, perpetually chewing something, would stop at whichever vendor looked most nervous and chat. Friendly. Almost kind. That was the trap. The nervous ones talked too much, volunteered names, mentioned that the woman at the candle stall had been seen buying saltpeter, and then someone's cousin would disappear for a fortnight.

Noor had perfected the posture for Warden visits: slightly bored, slightly tired, hands visible, eyes down over current work. Not servile — servile drew attention. Not hostile — hostility drew attention. Bored. Boredom was invisible.

Halvard stopped at the stall anyway. He always did. Not because he suspected anything — Noor was nearly certain he suspected nothing — but because stopping at the charm-seller's stall was part of his route the way pissing against a wall was part of a dog's. Routine. Territory.

"Evening, Brightwater."

"Warden." Noor kept looping cord. "Quiet night."

"They're all quiet nights until they're not." He picked up a luck-knot, turned it over in his gloved fingers. He did this every visit. He never bought one. "Business good?"

"Roof leaks and apprenticeships. There's always a leak somewhere."

Halvard smiled the way a wall smiles. He set the knot down precisely where he'd found it, aligned with the others, which was somehow worse than if he'd tossed it. "Curfew bell's at midnight. Don't be the last one packing up."

"Never am."

He moved on. Noor exhaled slowly through the nose and discovered their fingertips were flickering again — a soft amber glow bleeding up through the skin of both hands, reacting to the fear-adrenaline like iron to a lodestone. They pressed their palms flat against the underside of the stall table, against the old wood, and willed it down. *Not here. Not now. Not ever, ideally.*

The glow guttered. Died. Noor's heart took a few extra beats to follow it.

That was the thing nobody warned you about having magic in a city that had outlawed it: it wasn't like hiding a weapon. Weapons stayed quiet unless you reached for them. Magic leaked. It responded to fear, want, anger, grief — to everything, really, except the one thing you needed from it, which was calm. Noor had spent twenty-six years learning to be calm the way other people learned languages, total immersion, day and night, until calm stopped being a skill and became a personality.

Some nights Noor wondered who they'd have been if Vell had been the kind of city where a child could light a candle with a thought and get clapped on the head instead of hanged.

Most nights Noor made sure not to wonder. Wondering was a door, and doors opened from the inside.

* * *

Midnight approached the way it always did in Vell: gradually, then all at once, the crowd thinning and the lanterns being shuttered and the vendors folding their goods with the brisk economy of people who had somewhere to be that wasn't here. Noor packed the luck-knots into their wooden case, coiled the spare cord, stacked the fever-string bundles — short again, damn Tam and her accurate eyes — and latched the case just as the curfew bell began its long bronze tolling.

Seven strokes. On the fifth, Noor was alone on Cordwainer's Row. On the sixth, the last of the stragglers had vanished down the under-stairs. On the seventh —

On the seventh stroke, someone groaned.

Noor froze, latch half-turned. The sound came from the shadowed mouth of the alley behind the candle-stall, that narrow slot between buildings where the market's refuse went and, occasionally, people who'd drunk too much. Drunk was fine. Drunk was common. You rolled them onto their side so they didn't choke and you went home.

But this voice, when it came again, wasn't drunk. It was *burnt*. There was no other word for it — a sound like air pulled across char, wet and ruined, and underneath it something worse: a whisper, cracked and desperate, shaping words.

"—the fire is going out—"

Noor stood very still in the empty market, case in hand, and told themselves to walk away. This was what the Wardens were for. This was what the city guard was for. Strangers who collapsed in alleys making strange pronouncements were officially Not Noor's Problem, and unofficially — unofficially they were the kind of interesting that Maren had warned about, the kind that died of interesting things.

The man crawled into the lantern-light.

He was wrong. That was the first coherent thought Noor managed. He was *wrong*, the way a burned house is wrong — familiar shapes all present, all melted. His cloak was scorched through in patterns that didn't look accidental; they looked deliberate, sigil-like, spirals of char radiating from his chest. His hands were worst of all. Burned to the wrist, blackened, and yet — Noor's stomach dropped — yet faintly glowing at the fingertips. Amber. The same amber that lived, hidden, under Noor's own skin.

The man looked up. His eyes found Noor's across twelve feet of empty cobbles, and they were clear, lucid, terrified, and burning-gold.

"You're a keeper's get," he rasped. "I can smell the ward-blood. Listen — listen — there's no time, they're coming, I led them away but they'll circle back—" He fumbled at his cloak, at something bound inside it, dragging it free with hands that shouldn't have worked. "The fire is going out. The seal is failing. It has to pass to — it was always meant to pass to—"

He pressed it into reach: a scroll, heavy paper, sealed with a blob of dark red wax stamped with a mark Noor had seen exactly once before, carved above their grandmother's cellar door, and never once understood.

"Take it," the man whispered. "Hide it. Don't burn it — it won't burn, I tried, God help me, I tried—"

Boots. Many boots, far off but closing, the specific cadence of a patrol moving with purpose.

The man's face changed — terror folding itself shut, replaced by something almost like relief. "They can't take what they can't find," he said, louder now, and Noor realized with horror that he meant to be found. He pushed himself up against the candle-stall, arranged himself to be discovered, and looked at Noor one last time.

"Emberhold," he breathed. "Before the last ember dies. *Please.*"

Then he raised his ruined voice and shouted — "HERE! IT'S HERE! THE MAP IS HERE!" — at the top of what he had left, which was enough.

Noor ran.

They didn't decide to run; their body decided, twenty-six years of survival instinct executing a single unanimous vote, and Noor was three stalls away and dropping down the under-stairs into the old city with the scroll crushed against their chest before the first lantern flared to life overhead. Behind and above them: shouting, the crash of a stall going over, a Warden's voice barking orders, and then — cutting through everything, brief and terrible — a sound Noor had never heard outside of stories.

A fire that burned with no fuel. A wet, hungry crackle. The smell of char rolling down the stairwell after them like a tide.

Noor ran until the stairs ended, until they burst out into the Warrens' lowest street where the lamps burned green with old swamp-gas and nobody asked anybody anything, and there they stopped, bent double, lungs sawing, heart slamming, the wax-sealed scroll pressed so hard against their ribs it would leave a bruise.

Around them, the night went on. A woman argued with a fishmonger. Children chased a cat. Someone played a fiddle badly two streets over.

Above, muffled by five layers of city, the shouting faded. The fire-crackle stopped. And Noor Brightwater, charm-seller, aggressively dull, invisible in every way that mattered, stood in the green lamplight holding a dead man's map and understanding — with the terrible clarity that arrives only when it's least wanted — that their life had just stopped being survivable in exactly one way.

Somewhere in their chest, beneath the fear, beneath the grief, their fingertips had begun, very softly, to glow.
