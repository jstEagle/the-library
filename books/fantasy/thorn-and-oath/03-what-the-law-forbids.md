## 3. What the Law Forbids

The dead man's name was Oren Vail, and he had owed money to everyone in the Saltrow district.

Kess learned this before dawn, standing over the body in the Warden morgue while the coroner's assistant tried not to look at the burns. They were wrong, those burns. She'd seen fire deaths before—stove accidents, lamp spills, the occasional arson that the Wardens were actually paid to investigate. This was none of those. The scorching started at his hands and climbed his arms like ivy, as if the fire had come out of him rather than onto him. His fingertips were charred to black glass.

"Opium accident," said the assistant, a nervous young man named Pell who filed reports the way other men crossed streets: quickly, without looking up.

"Opium doesn't burn you from the inside out."

"Captain Hargrave wants it filed as an opium accident."

Kess looked at Pell. Pell looked at his ledger with tremendous concentration. She had been a Warden for eleven years and an ex-Warden for four months, and she still hadn't learned how to stop being the person people said *the captain wants it filed* to. Technically she wasn't even supposed to be in the morgue. Technically her credentials had been revoked in a ceremony so brief it hadn't even bothered with a speech—just a clerk, a stamp, and a door held open with someone's boot.

"Fine," she said. "File it however keeps you off Hargrave's list. I'm going to talk to his friends."

"He didn't have friends."

"Everyone has friends. They're just usually the kind who charge by the hour."

* * *

Saltrow at midmorning smelled of fish brine and cheap tallow, and Kess moved through it in plain clothes—a gray coat, no insignia, hair tied back like any dockworker's widow. Four months ago she'd have walked this street and parted it. Wardens got respect in Saltrow, or at least fear, which spent the same. Now she was just another tired woman asking questions, and the difference itched like a healing scar.

Oren Vail's "friends" turned out to be three people: a fence named Doss who operated out of a chandlery, a laudanum cook called Mother Quist who operated out of everywhere, and a woman at the boarding house on Tanner's Lane who had shared his bed for two winters and knew him better than anyone alive, which is to say she knew almost nothing true about him.

Doss first. He received her in the back of the chandlery among crates of tallow candles, and when she mentioned Vail's name he went smooth-faced in the way of men who'd survived interrogations by pretending to be furniture.

"I don't know any Vails."

"You sold him a courier's satchel last month. Waterproofed. You complained to Mother Quist that he overpaid because he was frightened and frightened men don't haggle." Kess leaned against the crates. "I'm not a Warden anymore, Doss. I can't arrest you. But I also can't be reported to, which means whatever you tell me goes exactly nowhere unless I decide otherwise. That makes me the safest person you'll ever confess to."

He studied her. She let him. Cynicism was its own currency; people trusted it more than badges.

"He came into money," Doss said finally. "Or thought he did. Said he'd found work as a courier for someone out east. Paid double what couriers get, triple if he never spoke of it. Then three weeks ago he stopped being frightened and started being something worse."

"Worse how?"

"Certain." Doss spat into a sawdust bucket. "Frightened men drink. Certain men make plans. He asked me about land west of the mountains. Asked who could forge travel papers good enough to pass the Emberhold checkpoints." His eyes flicked up. "Nobody travels to the Emberhold, mistress. That's not a place. That's a hole in the map where a place used to be."

"What was he carrying?"

"If I told you that, I'd be guessing. If you told me what you think he was carrying, we'd both be guessing together, and guessing isn't a crime yet."

It would be soon, Kess thought. The Assembly had been tightening the magic statutes all year—broader definitions, harsher penalties, new categories of contraband with names like *arcane instruments* and *residual enchantments*, which meant, in practice, anything the Wardens couldn't explain and therefore feared. She'd watched the law grow teeth for years from inside the jaw. Now, from outside, she could see the whole animal: a thing built to protect people that had learned to feed on them instead.

She'd believed in it once. That was the part that shamed her—not the disgrace, but how long she'd been loyal after the belief died.

* * *

Mother Quist received her clients in a different rented room every week, which made her easy to find for anyone willing to follow the smell of boiling poppy through half of Saltrow. She was sixty and built like a rolled mattress, and she poured Kess tea without being asked, which in her trade was either hospitality or a test.

"Oren," she said, settling into a chair across from her. "Poor lamb. He used to sit right there and shake."

"From the poppy?"

"No. From the dreams." She stirred her own cup without drinking. "He told me once—he was deep in it, mind you, not responsible for his mouth—that there was a fire under the world and somebody had let it go out too far. Said it like a man reciting scripture he didn't believe. And then he laughed and cried at the same time, and I've cooked for enough dying soldiers to know what that sounds like."

"A fire under the world."

"That's what I said."

Kess turned her cup slowly. Everyone knew the stories—the Dragon War, the Sealing, the great sacrifice that ended magic's age and gave the crown its founding myth. Children learned it in primer school: the brave Brightwater line and their allies bound the dragonfire forever, and the world grew safe and dull and lawful. The Wardens' oath referenced it. Half the statutes on the books existed because of it. Magic was banned because magic had nearly ended the world, and everyone agreed this was simply history, the way everyone agrees the sun rises.

But Kess had served eight years on the western circuits. She'd seen wards older than the crown still holding mountain passes, still purifying wells, still quietly doing their work in villages where the Wardens pretended not to look. If the sealing had ended magic, someone had forgotten to tell the magic.

And now a burned man with charred fingertips had run through the city screaming about a fire going out.

"What else did he say?"

"That he'd seen it." Mother Quist set down her cup. "Not dreamed it. Seen it. Said the seal was cracking and the cracks were spreading, and when it went, everything hidden would come out at once—and most of what's hidden should stay hidden, but some of it—" she shrugged, vast shoulders rising and falling— "some of it was ours to begin with. Taken away. He said the ban wasn't protection, it was theft. Then he apologized for talking nonsense and asked me for something to sleep."

"Did he mention anyone? A name? A place?"

"He talked about a market. Night market, down by the river gates. Said there was someone there who'd know what to do." Her eyes were sharp suddenly, sharper than her trade required. "You're not a Warden. I can tell because you listen. So I'll give you advice for free: whoever's at that night market, they're small. Little charms, little luck-knots, nothing that hurts anybody. And if your old masters find them first, they won't ask questions, they'll just do what the statute says. You remember what the statute says."

Kess remembered. The statute said death, and the statute had recently been amended to define *death* more efficiently.

* * *

The woman on Tanner's Lane gave her one thing worth having, though it took an hour of listening to grief before Kess recognized it.

"He kept a journal," the woman said, red-eyed, holding a slim book wrapped in oilcloth. "Burned pages out of it every few days. Superstitious habit, he said. But he left the last one in, and I'm not burning anything of his. I'm not burning anything of his ever again."

The journal's earlier entries were mundane—debts, weather, complaints about Doss's prices. But the final entry, dated nine days ago, read like a man writing fast in the dark:

*Delivered. Saw it with my own eyes. The seal is failing — the light comes and goes like breath. They need a keeper, not a courier. There are keepers still. The blood remembers even when the families forget. Going to find the gate-warden's line in Vell. If I can put it in the right hands—*

The entry stopped there. No name. Just *gate-keeper's blood* and a rough sketch: a circle broken by a vertical line, with three small marks above it like birds.

Kess copied the sketch into her notebook twice, then sat for a long time looking at it, because she had seen that mark before. Not in primer school. Not in the archives, though it was probably there. She'd seen it branded into the lintel of a drainage-gate on the city's south side, eight years ago, when she'd been a junior Warden escorting an engineer's survey and had asked, casually, why the old gates carried sigils when all sigils were illegal.

Her captain had told her to stop asking questions about the gates. The gates, he said, were crown property and crown business.

Crown property. Crown business. And a dead courier's journal pointing toward *a keeper of gates*.

* * *

She spent the evening doing what she did instead of sleeping: walking.

Vell at night was two cities stitched together—the lawful city of lamplighters and curfews, and underneath it the other one, the one that paid no attention to statutes because it had never been invited to. The night market lived in the seam between them, down by the river gates where the drainage tunnels exhaled their cold breath into the dark. Kess had raided it twice in her career. Both times they'd found nothing but candles, knotted thread, dried herbs, and vendors with perfectly innocent faces, and both times her captain had told her to write it up as *unlicensed vending* because arresting charm-sellers for selling knots made the Wardens look like bullies, and bullies, eventually, got audited.

She stood at the top of the market stairs now, watching it breathe.

Lanterns strung on wires. Stalls crowded along the canal wall. A fortune-teller laughing too loudly. Somewhere down there, a burned man had pressed his secret into someone's hands before the Wardens took him—or before something else did, because Kess had read the coroner's report twice and the burns still didn't sit right. Fire coming *out*. Men didn't burn from the inside. Unless they were carrying something that did.

Hargrave wanted the map. She was certain of that much; she'd heard him on the steps of the Assembly-house, talking low with the envoy from the palace, using words like *recovery operation* and *containment*. Whatever Vail had carried, the crown considered it an asset. And assets, in Kess's long experience, were things the powerful intended to keep.

Which raised the question she'd been circling for four months, the question that had gotten her dismissed in the first place, spoken aloud at the worst possible moment to the worst possible audience:

If the law protected people, why did the people it protected seem so afraid of it?

She descended the stairs.

The market swallowed her the way markets do—smell and noise and elbows, nobody looking, everybody seeing. She moved slow, bought nothing, touched nothing, and let her eyes work. Charm-sellers, yes, rows of them: luck-knots and fever-strings, love tokens and traveler's blessings, all of it harmless, all of it technically illegal, all of it the kind of crime that only exists so the law can pretend to be busy.

Then she saw the stall near the river-gate end. Smaller than the others. A seller with careful hands and a careful face, working a knot of red thread with a fluency that made the neighboring vendors look clumsy—and there, painted discreetly on the stall's corner post, half worn away by weather, a mark.

A circle, broken by a vertical line. Three marks above it, like birds.

Kess stopped walking.

Around her the market roared on—haggling, laughter, a fiddle somewhere—and she stood very still in the middle of it, feeling the old reflexes wake up one by one: *observe, confirm, report.* Eleven years of training telling her exactly what to do next. Walk back up the stairs. Find Hargrave. Give him the keeper of gates, the bloodline, the whole thread, and maybe—maybe—buy back a scrap of what she'd lost.

And underneath the training, quieter and older, the doubt that had cost her everything: *or don't.*

The charm-seller looked up. Their eyes met across twenty feet of crowded night, and Kess watched recognition flicker there—not of her face, but of her posture, her stillness, the particular way a hunter stops moving. The seller's hands went quiet on the red thread.

*Tomorrow,* Kess told herself. *Watch today. Decide tomorrow.*

She turned and walked back up the stairs into the lawful city, and behind her the market's lanterns swayed, and somewhere beneath the river the old gates breathed their cold breath against the seals her ancestors' oaths were supposed to be keeping.

By morning, she knew, the Wardens would come asking the same questions she'd just finished asking. Hargrave had the journal's existence by now; Pell filed everything. It was only a matter of days before the night market saw torchlight and boots.

Which meant that if she was going to choose—duty or suspicion, badge or truth—she would have to choose fast.

She just hadn't expected the choice to have a face already, and red thread on its fingers, and a mark on its post that the crown had spent three hundred years pretending didn't exist.
