## 5. The Warden's Visit

### Kess

The warrant was a formality, which meant it was a lie.

Kess had written enough of them to know the anatomy. A complaint from an unnamed citizen, corroborated by an anonymous source, citing irregularities in a charm-seller's licensing — it was the bureaucratic equivalent of a locked room with no witnesses. Captain Veyre had signed it without reading past the first line, because Veyre read nothing that might complicate his morning.

*Irregularities in licensing.* In eleven years as a Warden she had never once seen anyone arrested for irregular licensing. You fined them. You posted a notice. You did not dispatch two Wardens and a scribe at dawn to a stall in the Tallowmarket that wasn't even open yet.

Which meant someone above Veyre wanted this shop searched, and wanted it done before its owner could decide to run.

Kess stood across the lane in the shadow of a chandler's awning and watched the shop wake up. It was less a shop than a shed that had ambitions: a crooked door, shutters the color of old tea, a hand-painted sign — *NOOR BRIGHTWATER, CHARMS & KNOTS, LUCK SOLD FAIR* — with the *FAIR* repainted twice over older letters, like someone had kept changing their mind about what they promised.

The door opened. Noor Brightwater came out backwards, hauling a crate of knotwork, arguing silently with a hinge that wanted to stick. They were younger than Kess expected. Thinner. The kind of person who had learned to occupy exactly as much space as necessary and not a finger-width more.

They hung a luck-knot on the sign-hook, stepped back, considered it, moved it half an inch left. Considered again. Moved it back.

*You count things,* Kess thought. *Everything. Because counting is how you stay invisible.*

She crossed the lane.

"Noor Brightwater?"

Noor turned with the particular stillness of small animals deciding whether the hawk has actually seen them. "That's me. If you're here about the awning, I filed the appeal—"

"Wardens." She held up the warrant. "We're going to look through your stock."

The fear arrived on schedule and was, Kess noted professionally, slightly wrong. Guilty people flinched. This one *froze* — a full-body pause, half a heartbeat too long, the stillness of someone recalculating rather than panicking.

Then they smiled, and it was a good smile, warm and rueful, and that was wrong too. Nobody smiled that well at a Warden unless they'd practiced.

"Of course," Noor said. "Come in. Mind the third floorboard, it bites."

### Noor

*Don't touch the counter drawer. Don't look at the back room. Don't think about the map.*

Noor led them inside and immediately thought about the map so loudly they half-expected the Warden to turn around.

It was under the floorboard behind the counter, wrapped in oilcloth beneath a nest of dried feverfew that would make anyone who rummaged sneeze for an hour. Tam had shown them the hiding spot two nights ago with the weary contempt of a woman who'd hidden better things from worse people. *"Wardens search where guilt points,"* she'd said. *"So point somewhere else."*

So Noor pointed. Loudly.

"You'll want to check the register against my license," they said, pulling out the ledger with both hands, making a small ceremony of it. "I know the fees went up in springtide and I'm — honestly, I'm probably behind. I meant to come to the Hall, I just—" They laughed, self-deprecating, terrible. "Markets eat the week, you know?"

The Warden — tall, gray-cloaked, with a face like a closed ledger herself — took the book without comment. Her partner, a round young man with ink-stained fingers, began poking through the crate of charms with the delicacy of a man defusing something.

He shouldn't have bothered. Everything in the crate was exactly what it looked like: knots and beads and braided thread, blessed by nothing but good stitching. That was the whole architecture of Noor's life. A thousand honest objects arranged around a single dishonest secret, like a palisade of sticks around one buried coal.

"Name your supplier of feverfew," the Warden said, eyes on the ledger.

"I grow it. Roof garden."

"Licensed premises for cultivation?"

"It's a roof. With pots." Noor spread their hands. "Am I supposed to license dirt?"

Something flickered at the corner of the Warden's mouth. Not a smile. An annotation.

The young Warden held up a knot. "This one's got a sigil burned into the clasp."

"That's my maker's mark."

"It's not registered."

"Neither is my roof-dirt." *Stop,* Noor told themselves. *You're doing the thing.* But their mouth kept going, galloping ahead of their judgment, filling silence with noise, and every extra word was a brick out of the wall. "Look — if I'd known maker's marks needed registration I'd have picked a boring mark. A dot. A smudge. Something nobody would ever ask about—"

"Brightwater." The Warden said the name slowly, tasting it. "That's a gatekeeper family name. Old district. Southwall."

"My grandmother," Noor said. "Nobody keeps gates anymore. We sell knots."

The Warden looked at them. Really looked, the way you'd look at a lock before deciding whether to pick it or break it.

Then, mildly: "Show me the back room."

### Kess

The back room was a museum of careful poverty. Bundles of herbs sorted by ailment. Thread organized by color and, more interestingly, by *age* — the newer spools sat forward, the faded ones behind, a working inventory rather than decoration. A cot. A cold stove. One chair, one table, one window facing the alley.

And a floor.

Kess had spent six years walking crime scenes, and floors talked to her the way other people heard music. Most of this one was scuffed in the ordinary patterns of a single occupant. But behind the counter — visible only from where she now stood, not from the shop side — three boards sat a shade too clean. Recently lifted, recently replaced. Recently *avoided*, too; there was a wear-path where feet curved around them, subtle as a stutter in a sentence.

Noor stood in the doorway, arms folded, radiating the studied boredom of a person watching a stranger rifle their underwear drawer.

"Spare thread," Noor said. "Old stock. Nothing worth the dust."

"Mm." Kess crouched, pressed her thumb to one clean board. It gave — a hair's width. Hinged or loose. She could have pried it up in two seconds. Her partner was in the front, cataloguing charms. The scribe was outside, bored, writing down nothing.

Two seconds. And then what? A map, most likely. Maybe worse. She'd be obligated to arrest this nervous, funny, exhausted little liar, drag them to the Hall, hand them to whatever machine had drafted her warrant — and she'd never learn why the machine cared. Why the name *Brightwater* had made a senior clerk go quiet yesterday. Why the dead man from the market — burned from the inside out, according to the body-reader, which should have been impossible — had been asking after charm-sellers in the days before he died.

Kess had been a bad Warden lately. She knew precisely how bad, and it was getting worse by the week, and the worst part was that the work felt *more honest* the less of it she did.

She stood. She stepped directly onto the loose board — hard, once, a deliberate knock — and watched Noor's breath catch and release.

"Your floor's uneven," she said.

"Building's older than the city."

"Buildings usually are." She brushed off her hands. "Clean boards, though. You keep a tidy floor."

"I'm a tidy person."

"You're a *terrified* person," Kess said, "who happens to tidy."

For a moment neither of them moved. Then Noor did something unexpected: they dropped the smile. All of it, all at once, like setting down a heavy tray. What was underneath was tired and sharp and surprisingly level.

"And you're a Warden who hasn't called her partner in from the front room," Noor said quietly. "Who's standing on my floor having a conversation instead of searching it. So maybe we're both doing something we haven't quite decided to do yet."

Kess let the silence sit. It was, she reflected, a remarkable thing when a suspect negotiated with you — remarkable mostly because it worked.

"Licensing irregularities," she said. "A fine. Pay it by month's end and we won't need a second visit."

"That's generous."

"That's efficient. Second visits are paperwork." She turned for the door, then paused. "The man who died in your stall last week. Burned."

Noor's face did three things in one second — grief, guilt, and a fast, competent blankness sliding over both. "Tragic. I didn't know him."

"No," Kess agreed. "But he knew you. He chose your stall out of forty." She held up the warrant, tore it cleanly in half, and tucked the halves into her cloak. "Fine's waived. Consider it a clerical error."

"Why?"

"Because whoever sent me doesn't want a fine. They want a map, or a book, or a name — something specific — and I'd like to know what it is before they get it." She met Noor's eyes. "I'm not your friend, Brightwater. I'm worse. I'm curious."

---

### Noor

They watched the Wardens leave, the young one cradling his confiscated list of unregistered sigils like a treasure, the tall one moving like a woman leaving a room she intended to re-enter.

Noor locked the door. Sat on the counter. Put their head between their knees and breathed until the shaking stopped.

Then they lifted the loose board, pulled out the map, and looked at it properly for the first time since the night market. Wax seal intact. Ink dark as coagulated blood. And beneath their fingers, faint as a sleeping pulse — warmth. The paper was warm. It had been warm for three days, and Noor had been telling themselves it was body heat, or imagination, or fear.

It was none of those things.

"She knows," Tam said from the doorway, making Noor jump badly enough to nearly drop it. The old woman leaned on her broom, unimpressed. "The tall one. She stood on your floor like she owned the deed. She found it, girl. She *chose* not to lift it."

"Why would she do that?"

"Because she wants something bigger than an arrest." Tam spat into the corner, an old habit aimed at no one. "Or because somebody upstairs is dirty, and she can smell it. Either way—" She fixed Noor with a look that had ended arguments before they started. "Either way, you've got a Warden who knows and isn't telling, and a piece of paper that gets warmer every day. That's not luck, child. That's a countdown."

Noor looked down at the map. Beneath the wax seal, faintly, unmistakably — the ink had begun to move. A thin line crawling southward along the paper like a vein remembering its route.

South. Toward the mountains. Toward the Emberhold.

Toward home, said a voice in their blood that sounded uncomfortably like their grandmother's.

Tam followed their gaze, saw the moving line, and went white as tallow. The broom handle cracked against the floorboards as her grip failed.

"Where did you get this?" she whispered. "Noor. *Where did you get this?*"
