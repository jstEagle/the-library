## 6. Tam's Ledger

Tam Vetch kept three ledgers under the floorboard of her room at the Drowned Bell, and only one of them was about money.

The first was the money ledger, because a woman who'd spent thirty years running cargo along the Emberhold road learned early that memory was worth nothing and paper was worth everything. Debts owed. Debts forgiven. The going rate for silence in each district of Vell, updated quarterly.

The second ledger held names. Not debts—names. People she'd carried out of the city when the Wardens came knocking. People she'd carried *in*, when the roads were safer than the streets. Some were crossed out. She never wrote why.

The third ledger she hadn't opened in nineteen years, and tonight she sat on her bed with it on her knees and a candle burning low, because a hedge-witch with soot on her fingers had shown her a map that shouldn't exist, and Tam had gone pale in front of a customer for the first time since the reign of old King Aldous, and pale didn't cover half of it.

She opened the third ledger.

The ink inside had gone brown as old blood. The first page read, in her own younger hand: *Cargo manifest, spring run, Emberhold road.* And beneath that, in smaller letters, as if written by someone trying not to be heard:

*What we took up the mountain. What the mountain took back.*

* * *

She'd been twenty-three, and beautiful in the way that made people underestimate her, which she considered a professional asset. The Emberhold road wasn't the smuggler's route it would become—it was a pilgrim's road then, still legal, still lit by waystones that burned without flame. Magic hadn't been outlawed yet. That came later, after the war ended and the crown decided that the thing that ended wars should never be allowed to start one.

Her cargo that spring: iron bands, cold-forged, wrapped in wool so they wouldn't ring against each other. Salt from the eastern flats, blessed by no priest she could name but heavy with something older than blessing. And people. Twelve of them, volunteers all, riding in the wagon bed like sacks of grain, quiet, serene, dressed in ash-grey robes.

The Ashen Order. Even then. Even before they had a name anyone said above a whisper.

She remembered asking one of them—a girl no older than herself, with sunburned cheeks and a smile like she'd already won something—what was up the mountain that needed feeding.

"The seal," the girl said, as if Tam were simple. "It drinks. Everything does. We're just honest about it."

"Honest about what?"

The girl looked at her then, really looked, and her smile faltered for just a moment—the first crack in it, the one Tam would spend decades trying to forget. "You've seen the wards dying," the girl said. "In the villages. You've seen what happens when the fire goes low."

Tam had. Whole villages where the milk soured overnight. Where babies born that winter came out grey-eyed and silent, healthy enough, but with eyes like river stones. Where the old waystones guttered and the dark came back into the world in patches, like rot through cloth.

"They say the Brightwaters will renew it," the girl said. "The gatekeepers. It's their oath. Their blood keeps the door." She smiled again, and this time it reached her eyes, and that was worse somehow. "But blood-oaths are thin, they say. Thinner every generation. So some of us give more than blood."

Tam had delivered them to the outer gate and taken her pay and ridden down the mountain, and she told herself for years afterward that she hadn't known. That a cargo woman carries what's paid for and asks no questions—that was the whole trade, that was the code.

But she'd known. You can't watch twelve people ride up to be poured into a mountain and not know.

* * *

The second entry in the ledger was dated two years later, and her handwriting had changed. Harder. Faster.

*Spring run. Eleven volunteers. One runaway.*

She remembered him too. A farmer's son from the hill country, big hands, soft voice, who'd volunteered because his village's well had gone bitter and the Order told him a willing life given to the seal would sweeten a hundred wells besides. He'd gotten as far as the last waystation before he changed his mind.

He'd knocked on Tam's wagon at midnight and asked her to take him home.

And here was the thing Tam had never written down, not even in the ledger, not even in the privacy of her own skull until now, sitting with the candle guttering and the map hidden upstairs in Noor Brightwater's shop like a coal in a hayrick: she'd said yes. She'd loaded him in among the empty crates and turned the wagon around, and they'd made it four miles before the Order's riders caught them.

She remembered the sound his head made against the road. She remembered that she hadn't screamed, hadn't fought, hadn't done anything but sit very still on the bench while they dragged him off, because she was twenty-five and wanted to live, and living seemed like such a small, shabby thing to want right then.

The riders had let her go. Of course they had. She was the wagon. Nobody punishes the road.

*Entry ends,* she'd written that night, by the waystation's dying light. *Manifest complete. God forgive the manifest.*

She hadn't believed in God since. But she'd kept writing entries like prayers anyway, year after year, run after run, because the alternative was to stop looking at it altogether, and Tam Vetch had one rule she'd never broken: *you look at what you did.* You write it down. You keep the ledger. Guilt you refuse to count is guilt that spends itself on other people.

* * *

The candle was nearly out. Tam turned past the manifests, past the runs, past the page where she'd recorded, in a hand gone suddenly old, the day the crown outlawed magic and closed the Emberhold road forever.

That had been the strangest part. Not the ban itself—she'd expected that, everyone had—but the *reason*. The criers in the square hadn't said *the seal is failing*. They'd said magic was dangerous, magic was chaos, magic had started the Dragon War, and any soul found practicing it would answer to the Wardens. They'd built a wall of law around the mountain and called it protection.

But Tam had been up that road. She knew what a wall looks like when it's hiding something, and she knew the difference between a wall built to keep danger out and a wall built to keep a secret in.

The seal was failing. Had been failing for fifty years. And instead of tending it, the crown had outlawed the only people alive whose blood could tend it—the gatekeeper line, the Brightwaters—and scattered them into cities like Vell to sell luck-knots under Warden surveillance and pretend their hands didn't itch with real magic.

*Smart,* Tam thought, not for the first time. *Cruel and smart. Can't have a dynasty of door-openers if nobody knows there's a door.*

Except somebody always knows. Somebody always keeps a ledger.

* * *

She thought about the girl in the shop. Noor. Hands quick with the charm-work, face arranged into careful blankness, the particular loneliness of a person who is useful to everyone and known by no one. Tam had watched a hundred young people wear that exact face over the years, in her boarding house, in her smuggling days, in the long gray stretch of her retirement. She knew it like she knew weather.

But Noor had unrolled that map with the wax seal broken and the ink crawling like something alive, and asked, *Do you know this mark?*—and Tam had looked at the gatekeeper's sigil, the thorn-and-circle, the mark she'd last seen carved over a door that ate people, and her body had answered before her mind could stop it. She'd gone pale. She'd said too much by saying almost anything at all.

Now Noor was upstairs, probably not sleeping either, holding a map to the Emberhold and a bloodline they clearly didn't understand, and the Wardens had a dead man's associates to question and a thread leading straight to the night market, and Tam Vetch had a choice to make.

She'd been making the same choice for forty years, if she was honest. Every run up the mountain, she'd chosen the road. Every volunteer she'd hauled, she'd chosen the pay. Every time she'd looked away from the girl with the cracked smile or the farmer's son with the soft voice, she'd chosen survival, and survival was a debt that compounded.

There was a page near the back of the third ledger she'd never filled in. Just a heading, written nineteen years ago, the night she'd retired and bought the Drowned Bell and told herself the account was closed:

*Outstanding balance:*

Nothing under it. All these years, nothing under it, because she hadn't known how to price it. What do you charge yourself for twelve lives poured into a mountain? For one boy's head on the road? For forty years of comfortable silence?

She picked up her pen now. Dipped it. Held it over the page a long time.

Then she wrote: *One guide, one road, one chance to set it right. Payment deferred. Interest accruing.*

It wasn't penance. She was too old and too clear-eyed to believe in penance—you couldn't pour the boy back out of the mountain any more than you could unring a bell. But there was a difference between guilt and inheritance, and she'd finally worked out what it was. Guilt sits on you. Inheritance gets *passed on*. And Noor Brightwater was standing in her house holding an inheritance neither of them had asked for, and somebody had to teach them how to carry it without letting it crush them flat.

Somebody who knew the road.

* * *

Morning came gray through the shutters. Tam put the third ledger back under the floorboard—she wasn't ready to burn it; maybe she never would be; some accounts you keep open on principle—and climbed the stairs to the shop with her knees complaining and her mind made up.

Noor was behind the counter, pretending to sort charm-components, doing it badly. Their eyes had the raw look of a night spent arguing with a map.

"I'll take you," Tam said, without preamble.

Noor blinked. "I haven't asked you to—"

"You were working up to it. You've got the face for it. The polite, terrified, please-don't-make-me-say-it-out-loud face." She planted both hands on the counter. "Here are my terms. I know the Emberhold road better than any living soul, because I'm probably the only living soul who ever ran it legally, back when there was such a thing. I know which waystations still stand, which passes the Wardens watch, and which villages along the way will shelter strangers and which will sell them. Without me, you die in the hollow country inside a week. With me, you might die in six. Those are the terms."

"That's not—" Noor stopped. Started again. "Why?"

And there it was. The question Tam had been dreading and rehearsing for, all night, and she found that every rehearsed answer—the practical ones, the cynical ones, the ones that hid the ledger under cleverness—died in her throat.

"Because I owe a debt," she said finally, "and I'm the only one left who knows to whom."

Noor studied her with those careful eyes, the ones that missed nothing, and Tam braced for more questions—the kind she couldn't answer without opening the floorboard and showing the whole ugly account.

But Noor just nodded slowly, the way people nod when they recognize a locked door and decide, for now, not to knock. "When do we leave?"

"Tomorrow night. Dark of the moon. There's a way out of this city your grandmother warded herself, and if my guess about your blood is right, it'll know you." Tam was already moving, pulling crates, counting coin, doing the work because work was easier than feeling. "Get your affairs in order. Tell no one. And Brightwater—"

Noor looked up.

"If anyone asks, I'm taking you to visit a sick aunt in Marrowdale. Practice saying it. You lie like a first-year apprentice, and on the road that gets people buried."

Outside the shuttered window, in the gray morning light, a figure in a Warden's coat paused across the street—lingered a moment too long—then walked on.

Tam watched her go, and felt the old ledger open itself somewhere behind her ribs.

So it begins again, she thought. Cargo on the road. God forgive the manifest.
