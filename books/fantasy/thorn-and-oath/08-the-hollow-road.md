## 8. The Hollow Road

The road out of the drainage-gates didn't have a name on any map Tam owned, and Tam owned maps of places that had been underwater for two hundred years.

"Call it the hollow road," she said, when Noor asked. "Because it's hollow. Because everything that travels it is hollow—pilgrims with empty purses, smugglers with empty promises, soldiers with empty heads. The road doesn't care what you're carrying. It only cares whether you can keep walking."

Noor kept walking.

They had been walking since dawn, and Vell was a smudge behind them now, its walls gray against a gray sky, its wards fading from Noor's skin like the memory of a hand letting go. Their feet hurt in the specific way that meant blisters were coming. Their pack held three changes of clothes, a kettle, forty luck-knots they could no longer legally sell anywhere, and a map that had tried to bite them.

The map rode against their ribs, wrapped in oilcloth, warm as a sleeping animal. It had been warm all morning. Noor was trying very hard not to think about that.

"Smile," Tam said.

"Why?"

"Because we're about to be robbed."

Noor looked up. Ahead, where the road bent around a hillside of blackthorn, a dozen figures had arranged themselves with the studied casualness of people who were absolutely not waiting for anyone. A cookfire. A mule. A banner—a real one, patched and sun-bleached—showing a white stag leaping over a stylized flame. Pilgrims, then, or something wearing pilgrims like a coat.

"The Order of the Stag," Tam murmured. "Road-pious. They walk shrine to shrine and take donations." Her mouth did something that was not quite a smile. "And some of them take other things. Watch the one with the red scarf. He's got pickpocket hands. I'd know. I trained half the pickpockets between here and the coast, back when my knees worked."

"You trained—" Noor started.

"Later. Smile."

The pilgrims resolved into faces as Noor and Tam drew near: weathered, wary, curious. An older woman with a staff topped by a carved stag. Two young men sharing a loaf. A girl asleep against the mule's flank. And at the edge of the fire, sitting slightly apart from all of them, a boy.

He was perhaps twelve, maybe fourteen—it was hard to tell, because he was thin in the way of someone still growing into hunger. His hair was dark and cut short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut by someone who isn't a barber. He wasn't looking at the newcomers. He was looking at the ground beside his crossed legs, where he was drawing in the dirt with a stick.

Noor stopped walking.

Not because of the boy. Because of what he was drawing.

It was a small thing, quick and careless: a loop crossed by two bars, with a dot set in the crook of the loop. Any stranger would have read it as idle scratching, a child's doodle of a fence or a fishhook or nothing at all.

Noor's grandmother would have read it aloud. *Small door, watched, safe to open.*

It was a Brightwater sigil. One of the gate-wards. One of the signs Noor's family had invented, back when they had gates worth warding—and which existed nowhere else on earth, because the family that made them had scattered and hidden and, in Noor's case, run.

The boy looked up. His eyes met Noor's, and Noor saw him see them see the drawing. Something flickered across his face—not guilt, not fear. Recognition, maybe. Or hope. Then he dragged his boot through the dirt and erased it, and looked away, and became just another thin kid by a fire.

"Noor," Tam said quietly. "You've gone the color of whey. Smile."

Noor smiled. It felt like wearing someone else's face.

* * *

The pilgrims' leader was called Mother Sable, though she was neither a mother nor, she admitted cheerfully, particularly sable anymore. She had a laugh like a cart going over cobbles and a way of looking at people that took inventory without apology.

"Two more walkers, then," she said, offering them tea from a dented pot. "Where to?"

"East," Tam said, which was true and useless.

"Everyone's going east these days." Mother Sable poured. "Shrines are east. Hope is east. Trouble's usually east too, but trouble travels well." She studied them over the rim of her cup. "You're not pilgrims."

"We're travelers."

"Mm. I'm not actually a mother, either. We're all lying a little; it keeps the road honest." She waved at the camp. "You can walk with us as far as the ford. There's toll-men past the ford, and they're less polite than I am. Numbers help."

Tam glanced at Noor. Noor, whose entire strategy for staying alive in Vell had been *be unremarkable*, understood that joining twelve strangers was the opposite of unremarkable. But twelve strangers were also witnesses, and witnesses were armor, and the man with the red scarf had already drifted close enough to count the pockets on Noor's coat.

"We'd be glad of company," Noor said.

"Course you would. Everyone is; nobody admits it." Mother Sable laughed her cobblestone laugh. "There's stew at dusk if you contribute. Wood, water, or coin. Coin preferred. Wood accepted. Water mocked."

That evening Noor carried wood, and the man with the red scarf—Dree, he was called—carried off a spoon, two buttons, and, briefly, Tam's knife, until Tam caught his wrist without looking up from her tea and said, "Sweetling, I taught your grandmother this trick, and she was better at it," and Dree went pink to his ears and returned the knife with a bow so deep it was nearly an apology.

The boy with the stick did not come to the fire.

Noor found him at the edge of the light, sitting on a fallen stone, drawing again. This time it wasn't idle. He was working through a sequence, careful and deliberate, each sign flowing into the next the way words flow into sentences:

*Who teaches the gate-speech? Where do the makers go?*

Noor's breath went out of them slowly.

Those weren't doodles. That was grammar. Old grammar—the kind Noor had only ever seen scratched inside the covers of their grandmother's ledger, the kind the family used among themselves because it could be written on any surface and read as nothing by anyone outside the blood. *Gate-speech.* Noor hadn't heard that phrase spoken aloud since they were nine years old, and had never seen it written by a stranger's hand.

The boy watched them read it. He didn't smile. He pointed at the last sign—*makers*—and then, deliberately, at Noor.

"I don't know what you mean," Noor said, in the voice they used on Warden patrols.

The boy's expression didn't change. He picked up his stick and added one more mark beneath the sentence. A single stroke, slanted, with a hook at the end.

*Lie.*

Noor stared at it. The fire crackled behind them. Somewhere a pilgrim was singing a song about a stag and a saint, badly.

"Who taught you?" Noor asked, and hated how much they wanted the answer.

The boy tapped his own throat, then shook his head. Then he wrote, fast now, the stick scraping:

*No one. Found them. In a book that burned. Kept them anyway. You know them. Your hands knew them before your face did.*

"My hands—"

But the boy had gone still, head cocked, listening to something Noor couldn't hear. Then he scrubbed out every sign with his sleeve, stood, and walked back toward the fire as if he'd never left it, leaving Noor alone in the dark with a heart beating far too fast and a map against their ribs that had grown, if anything, warmer.

* * *

Tam found them there, later, when the singing had died and the camp had banked itself down to embers and breathing. She sat down on the fallen stone without asking, which was her way, and handed over half a heel of bread, which was also her way.

"So," she said. "The boy."

"You saw."

"I've got eyes. Old ones, but they work." Tam chewed. "He wrote at you in a dead language and you went pale as a Warden's conscience. Want to tell me why a mute child on the hollow road speaks Brightwater?"

Noor broke the bread instead of answering. The truth sat in their chest like a stone they'd swallowed years ago and never passed: *because my family built the language, and built the seal, and built the lie, and everyone who might explain it to me is dead or gone or five years silent.* What came out instead was smaller.

"It's a family script. Gate-wards. Signs for doors and locks and warnings. Nobody outside the blood ever learned it. It shouldn't exist in a boy's hand."

"And yet."

"And yet."

Tam was quiet for a while. Out past the firelight, the blackthorn hillside made its slow shapes against the stars. "When I ran cargo along the Emberhold road," she said at last, "there were villages that used marks like that. Not many. Little communities, tucked in the folds of the hills. They'd chalk signs on their doors—ward-signs, warning-signs. Most folk thought it was superstition. Old habit." She turned the bread crust over in her fingers. "I always figured the habits had to come from somewhere."

"From us," Noor said. "From the keepers. When the family scattered after the sealing, they must have—the signs would have traveled with them. Taught to children, taught to neighbors, worn down like coins changing hands." They thought of the drainage-gates of Vell, warded by their grandmother, recognizing their blood. "We didn't just build the seal. We seeded the whole country with pieces of it. Pieces nobody knows how to read anymore."

"Except him."

"Except him."

"How does a mute orphan come to read a dead keeper's script?"

"That," Noor said, "is exactly what frightens me."

Tam nodded slowly, and Noor realized she wasn't really looking at the dark. She was looking at the boy, asleep now near the mule, curled small as a comma. Whatever she was thinking, she folded it away behind her face the way she folded everything.

"Get some sleep," she said. "Ford tomorrow. Toll-men after. If the world's kind, that's all today will be."

The world, Noor was learning, was rarely kind. But it was, occasionally, interesting, and they slept anyway, with the map warm against their ribs and the word *lie* scratched into the dirt behind their eyelids.

* * *

The ford was a mile of brown water and bad temper, and crossing it cost them the mule's patience and Dree's boots, which he lost to the current and mourned loudly for an hour. The toll-men beyond it were exactly as advertised: four men and a woman with pikes and a plank table, charging a "road-maintenance fee" for a road that was, visibly, unmaintained.

Mother Sable haggled them down to a reasonable robbery. The pilgrims paid. Noor paid. Tam paid with coin that Noor was fairly sure she'd lifted back off Dree during the ford crossing, and said nothing about it, and Tam said nothing about it, and that, too, seemed to be how things worked on the hollow road.

It was past midday, the road climbing now through gorse and old stone walls, when Ferro—the boy's name, they'd finally gotten it, spelled out in sigils in the dust: *iron without the voice*—fell into step beside Noor.

He didn't look at them. He watched the road. But his stick was moving, tracing signs in the dust at his feet as he walked, casual as breathing, and Noor found themselves reading without meaning to, the way you read a sign over a door.

*They follow,* the signs said. *Two days back. Gray cloaks. Not bandits. Bandits don't measure.*

Noor's stomach dropped. "Ferro. Slow down. What do you mean, they follow? Who follows?"

Ferro glanced up at them, and there was no fear in his face, which somehow made it worse. Just the flat patience of someone who has been hunted before and has made his peace with the arithmetic of it. He crouched, pretending to retie a bootlace, and wrote quickly:

*Gray cloaks ride the ridge line. Stop when we stop. Go when we go. Measured our pace yesterday. Measuring it now. Wardens don't wander the hollow road. Wardens go where they're sent.*

Noor looked back the way they'd come. The road unspooled behind them, empty, innocent, lined with gorse and broken walls. Somewhere on the ridgeline above—there. A flicker of movement, gray against gray, gone before they could be sure.

Kess. It had to be. The ex-Warden who had searched their shop and left unconvinced, who had let them walk out of Vell with a stolen map warming against their ribs. *Intrigued rather than arrest-hungry*, Tam had called it. Noor was beginning to suspect that intrigued was considerably more dangerous than hungry. Hunger could be fed. Intrigue followed you for days.

"They're not closing," Ferro wrote, when Noor told him—told Tam, and Tam told the group in the shapeless way news moved through a walking camp. He crouched again, stick flying. *If they wanted us taken, we'd be taken. They're herding. Keeping us on the road. Watching where we choose to go.*

"Why?" Noor breathed.

Ferro looked up at them, and for the first time since the fire, he smiled. It was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone holding a match in a dry field.

Then he wrote one line in the dust, in his careful, borrowed, impossible script, and stepped on it, and walked on:

*Because they want to see if you lead them home.*

Behind them, far back along the ridge, a gray cloak crested the rise and did not bother to hide.
