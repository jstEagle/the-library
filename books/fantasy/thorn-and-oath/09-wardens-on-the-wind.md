## 9. Wardens on the Wind

The order came down from the Citadel at dawn, and it came with a name.

*Noor Brightwater, charm-seller, night market, Vell. Suspected possessor of restricted cartography. Apprehend alive if practicable.*

Kess read it three times by the light of the garrison lamp, and each time her eye snagged on the same word. *Practicable.* Not *possible*. Not *preferable*. Practicable — which meant the clerk who drafted it had already priced out what a live capture would cost, and had decided Kess could be spared the trouble.

They'd given her two men she didn't want and a warrant she didn't trust, and they expected her back in a week with a hedge-witch in irons and a map in an evidence box.

She folded the order into quarters, then eighths, until it was small enough to disappear into her glove. Old habit. A Warden learned early that paper was the most dangerous thing you could carry.

"Two riders," said Brannic, at the door of the tack room. He was the better of her two — big, slow to anger, honest in the way of men who'd never been lied to by anyone important. "Horses are saddled."

"And Ovett?"

"Complaining."

"Then he's well." She pulled her coat off its hook. The Warden grey had faded at the shoulders where the sun had worked it for eleven years, and there was a burn scar across the left cuff she'd never had mended because mending meant explaining, and explaining meant the story of the fire at Harrow Fen, and she had told that story exactly once and never intended to tell it again.

Outside, Vell was shaking itself awake in the grey half-light, fish-carts and chimney smoke and the first bells of the law-temples. Somewhere under all of it, a charm-seller with clever fingers was packing a bag or burning a map or doing nothing at all, and Kess found she genuinely did not know which possibility she was hoping for.

That was the trouble. That was the whole rotting trouble.

* * *

They rode out through the Southreach gate, past the drainage-works where the city's filth went to become the river's problem, and Kess made herself look at the old ward-stones flanking the road. Every Warden knew them: grey granite, older than the crown, carved with spirals nobody living could read. The doctrine said they were boundary markers from the Dragon War. Kess had always privately thought that was like calling a lock a decoration because you'd forgotten what it kept shut.

As they passed, one of the stones did something.

It was small. A flicker, a shimmer along the spiral grooves, gone before Brannic could have seen it — but Kess saw it, and what she felt in the half-second it lasted was not menace. It was recognition. Like a dog lifting its head as someone with the right scent went by.

She reined in. Looked back. Nothing. Grey stone, dead morning, Ovett grumbling about his saddle-girth.

But the direction the stone had seemed to *face* — south, toward the hollow road — stayed with her like a splinter.

"The Brightwater woman," Brannic said, drawing level. "You know her?"

"I searched her shop."

"That all?"

Kess looked at him. He had the particular blankness of a man asking a question he already suspected the answer to, and she gave him the particular blankness of a woman who had spent eleven years making sure her face told no stories.

"She sells luck-knots," Kess said. "And fever-charms that work, which is either very good craft or very bad law, depending on who's asking."

"And which are you asking?"

Ahead, Ovett snorted something that might have been laughter. Kess nudged her horse forward and let the question die on the road behind them, which was where most questions deserved to die.

But she turned it over anyway, riding, because riding was when she did her honest thinking. The truth was that she had searched Noor Brightwater's shop and found nothing, and had known within ten minutes that she was being lied to, and had known within twenty that she didn't care — and that last part was the part she couldn't put in any report. There had been a moment, standing among the hanging bundles of dried herbs while the charm-seller lied to her with sweat standing out on their brow, when Kess had looked at this terrified person clutching the counter like a drowning man clutches driftwood, and had thought: *I know exactly what you're afraid of. I've been afraid of it for eleven years.*

The lie hadn't offended her. The lie had felt like company.

* * *

They picked up the trail at the crossroads shrine outside Vell, where the pilgrim road bent toward the hollow country. Someone had left an offering at the shrine — a knot of braided cord, red and green thread, tied in a pattern Kess recognized because she'd seen forty of them hanging in a certain stall.

Fresh. The cord still held the smell of the dye.

"Someone bought their luck before traveling," Brannic said.

"Someone bought *that* luck." Kess turned the knot over. In the center, almost hidden in the braid, was a small wrongness — a stitch pulled tight in a way no market charm-seller would bother with unless they were signing their work, or warning someone, or both. "This is a maker's mark. And it's fresh enough that our traveler passed here yesterday evening."

Ovett spat. "So we ride hard and take them tonight."

"We ride at their pace," Kess said, "and we watch."

Ovett's face did the thing it always did when she gave an order he thought was soft — a tightening around the mouth, a glance at Brannic that wasn't quite quick enough to be private. Eleven years ago that glance would have stung. Now it just confirmed what she already knew: that the uniform fit worse every year, and the men wearing it alongside her noticed.

"The order says apprehend," Ovett said.

"The order says *practicable*." She let the word sit. "Which means the Citadel would rather have the map than the map-bearer, and would rather not pay for either. We do this my way. We follow, we observe, and we find out who else is following. Because we're not the only ones on this road."

"How do you know?"

"Because the bounty boards in Southreach had a new notice up before the Citadel's courier reached my door." She swung back into the saddle. "Somebody's paying silver for a burned man's belongings, and the Citadel doesn't post bounties in copper districts. That means private hands. That means whoever killed the stranger in the night market wants what he carried, and they aren't waiting on a warrant."

Brannic exchanged a look with Ovett — a different look, a real one — and Kess pretended not to see it. Let them think she was playing politics, angling for credit, managing the arrest like a career move. It was a useful misunderstanding. It was certainly safer than the truth, which was that she wanted to reach Noor Brightwater before anyone else did, and that she could not have explained why under torture.

Except perhaps under torture. Under torture she might have admitted that when the dying man had been dragged past her in the market, his eyes had found hers — a Warden's eyes, the enemy's eyes — and he had not begged her for help. He had begged her for *witness*. *Tell them,* he'd mouthed, blood on his teeth. *Tell them the fire is going out.* And she had stood there in her grey coat and done nothing, because doing something would have cost her everything, and she had hated herself with a clean and total efficiency ever since.

She wanted the truth first. That was what she told herself. The truth, and then the arrest, in the proper order, like a good Warden.

The mask held. Masks mostly do, right up until they don't.

* * *

They lost the trail at the ferry crossing and found it again at a drover's inn, and by the second day Kess understood that she was not tracking a fugitive so much as following a story. A charm-seller, a retired smuggler old enough to be somebody's scandalous grandmother, and a mute boy who drew symbols in the dirt at waystations — symbols that made Kess's neck prickle, because she'd seen their like once before, in the sealed archives under the Citadel, on documents concerning the Emberhold road.

She copied three of them into her notebook without letting Brannic see.

On the third night they made camp in a fold of the hills above the road, and Kess took the middle watch because she always took the middle watch, and sat with her back against a thorn tree watching the far valley where a small campfire burned — the fugitives' fire, careless and warm, three figures around it like figures in a children's book.

Through the dark she watched the charm-seller teach the boy a knot. Even at this distance she could read the shape of the lesson: hands moving, pausing, correcting. Patience. She watched the old smuggler drink something and laugh at nothing. She watched the firelight make a family out of three people who had known each other, by Kess's best estimate, less than a week.

Something in her chest did a thing she refused to name.

Eleven years in the Wardens taught you that families were cover stories. Everyone you arrested had one — a wife who swore he was home, a mother who swore he was gentle, a sister who forged his alibi with tears in her eyes. You learned to see belonging as camouflage. You learned that the tighter people huddled, the more likely one of them was hiding something.

But nobody had ever taught her what to do with the reverse thought, the one that crept in on nights like this: that maybe the huddle came first, and the hiding was just what got built afterward to keep it safe. That maybe everyone she'd ever dragged out of a warm circle had been guilty of exactly one thing — needing people — and a hundred things besides, and the crown had never once cared about the difference.

The fire below flickered. The boy — Ferro, the innkeeper's wife had called him — looked up suddenly, straight at the dark hillside where Kess sat, as if he could feel her watching. His hand moved, quick, drawing something in the air.

Kess couldn't read it. But she felt it land anyway, like a pebble dropped down a well that took too long to hit bottom.

* * *

The bounty hunters caught up with the fugitives at midmorning on the fourth day, at the ruined tollbridge over the Sere, and Kess knew it before she heard anything, because the crows went up from the bridge stoneworks all at once, a black rag torn out of the sky.

"There," Brannic said, and then, because he was honest: "That's not us."

Six riders coming down on the bridge from the north side, ununiformed, unhurried, armed like men paid by the head. Below them on the near bank, tiny with distance, the three fugitives were just reaching the waterline — and stopping, because the ford was flooded with spring melt and the bridge above was the only crossing, and the bridge now belonged to other people.

Kess brought her horse up short behind a ridge of tumbled wall. Her heart was doing something unprofessional.

"Wardens," Ovett said hopefully, reading the scene the way she'd trained him to. "We announce ourselves, take custody of the fugitives, and send the bounty men on their way."

"Yes," Kess said. "That is what a Warden would do."

She heard it herself as it left her mouth — the conditional, the little door left open — and Brannic heard it too. He looked at her, and for once he didn't bother with blankness.

"Captain," he said quietly. "What are we doing?"

Below, one of the bounty hunters dismounted and started down the bank with a net-pole, the kind used for taking mages alive, because a live hedge-witch sold for triple. Another nocked an arrow. The old smuggler put herself between the boy and the bow, and the charm-seller — Noor — raised both empty hands and did the thing Kess had seen desperate people do in eleven years of arrests: they bargained, gesturing, offering, backing toward the water with nowhere to go.

The archer drew.

Later, Kess would try to reconstruct the decision and fail. There was no moment of choice. There was simply a gap between the mask and the woman, and she went through it like a rider through a gate — spurs in, downhill, out from behind the wall with Brannic pounding after her and Ovett shouting something legal behind them that the wind took away.

The archer heard the hoofbeats and turned, and that turn saved Noor Brightwater's life, because an arrow loosed at a turning body goes wide. Kess was among them before the second arrow cleared the quiver, and she was not thinking like a Warden anymore; she was thinking like a woman with a scar on her cuff and a debt she'd been carrying since a dying man mouthed *tell them* at her across a market crowd.

She took the net-man's pole across the forearm and broke it, took his wrist, put him down. Brannic arrived like weather. Two bounty hunters ran for it immediately — professionals, recognizing a changed situation. The last one, the leader, a lean man with a tax-collector's smile, actually tried to argue.

"This is crown business," he said, showing a writ. "Licensed recovery of stolen property—"

"So is mine." Kess showed him nothing, which was more frightening. "And mine says these people are in Warden custody as of now. Unless you'd like to explain to the Citadel why your license was interfering with an active warrant."

She watched him do the arithmetic. Six blades against two-and-a-half Wardens was fine odds, but six blades against the Citadel's paperwork was ruin, and men like him always chose the smaller grave. He spat, whistled his riders off, and left the broken net-pole in the mud like a bad review.

Silence, then. River noise and crow noise and the fugitives staring up the bank at her.

Noor Brightwater stood shin-deep in the flooded ford with their hands still half-raised, and the look on their face was one Kess knew from mirrors: the arithmetic of a person deciding whether the thing that just saved them was a rescue or a trap.

"You," Noor said. Their voice cracked in the middle. "You're the one who searched my shop."

"I am."

"You lied to your own men about me."

"I did."

"Why?"

And there it was — the question, the real one, arriving ahead of any explanation Kess had prepared, because she had prepared none. She opened her mouth to give the answer a Warden would give. *Custody protocols. Prisoner value. The Citadel prefers live captures.* All true. All beside the point.

What came out instead, quiet enough that only Noor heard it under the river:

"Because a man died in front of me and asked me to tell the truth, and I stood there in my nice grey coat and did nothing. I'm not doing nothing twice."

Noor stared at her. Behind them, the old smuggler had a knife out and wasn't even pretending to hide it, and the mute boy was watching Kess with those too-old eyes, and somewhere behind Kess, Ovett was finally catching up and would very soon have opinions.

The mask was off. She could feel it lying in the mud somewhere back up the slope, and she found, to her considerable surprise, that she could breathe.

"The map," Kess said. "The burned man's map. I know you have it. And I know what's printed on the writ the Citadel gave me, and I know what isn't — and the gap between those two things is where people get killed. So." She held out her open hand, empty, palm up, the first honest gesture she'd made in eleven years. "Before I decide what I am today — Warden, deserter, or something that doesn't have a name yet — tell me what the fire is going out of."

Downstream, where the Sere ran shallow over the stones, the water flickered.

Not with light. With heat — a brief, impossible shimmer, as if the river remembered being something else, and Noor Brightwater went white to the lips and said, in a voice like a person reading their own death warrant:

"You'd better sit down."
