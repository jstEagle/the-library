## 10. Ash Village

The village had no name on any map we carried, which should have been my first warning. Places without names are places the world agreed to forget.

We came down out of the hills at dusk, Tam's mule favoring its left hind leg and Tam favoring nothing at all because she was too proud to limp where anyone could see her doing it. Ferro rode behind her with his arms wrapped around her waist, chin tucked against her spine the way children sleep against their mothers, though I'd have been a fool to say so aloud. Kess ranged ahead of us the whole day, twenty paces, thirty, always just far enough to pretend she wasn't traveling with us.

"There," she said now, without turning around. Her hand came up in the flat-palmed halt signal Wardens used—old habits dying harder than people—and pointed at a break in the bracken below us.

I saw it before I understood it. A shape in the land, geometric, wrong. Straight lines where hills should curve. The skeleton of a street grid showing through a century of grass like bones through thin skin.

"A village," Tam said. She said it the way you name a corpse.

"It'll have walls of some kind," Kess said. "Foundations. Maybe a well that still holds water."

"It'll have other things too." Tam didn't move for a long moment. The wind moved her gray hair across her face and she let it. Then she clicked her tongue at the mule and started down, and none of us asked what other things meant, because none of us wanted to know badly enough to spend the question.

* * *

The village had burned once, thoroughly, and then been left to burn again every season in a slower way—the way of rot and weather and absence. We walked streets with no roofs over them. Doorways opened onto rooms full of nettles shoulder-high. A cradle lay in one doorway, half-swallowed by bramble, its rocking runners still curved as if waiting for a foot.

Nobody spoke. Even the mule went quiet.

"Dragon War," Tam said at last, when we reached what had been the square. A well stood at its center, capped with a slab of stone. "This whole valley held maybe two dozen villages like it. Farming country. Good soil." She nudged a charred fencepost with her boot. It crumbled. "They say the dragon's shadow passed over here in the last year of the war. Just the shadow. That was enough."

"That's not true," Ferro signed.

He did it simply, the way he did everything—hands quick and certain in the last light, no flourish, no apology. *That is not true.*

Kess's hand drifted toward her belt, where a knife lived now instead of a Warden's seal. She'd been three days among us and she still reached for the weapon that proved her rank whenever something unsettled her, and found only steel, and looked briefly lost. "How would you know?" she asked, and then, catching herself: "I mean—"

"He knows," I said.

Because I did know, or I was beginning to. Three days on the hollow road, and I had learned to read Ferro's hands the way you learn a language by drowning in it. And more than that—I had learned that his sigils were *ours*. Brightwater sigils. My grandmother's shorthand, the gate-keeper's script, the marks I'd grown up doodling in the margins of my schoolbooks without knowing they were anything but mine. Somewhere back along his line, someone had taught a family of farmers to write in the language of wardens-of-gates, and I hadn't yet worked out whether that meant Ferro's people had served mine or fled them or been saved by them.

He crouched at the edge of the square where the ash lay thickest—a fine gray drift of it, decades deep, too sterile even for nettles. Nothing grew there. He pushed his sleeve up and drew his finger through it, slow, deliberate, and I felt the hair rise on my arms before he'd finished the first line, because those weren't pictures.

Those were wards. Writing wards in ash, loose, unbound, the way a poet scribbles in the dirt. Not magic—there was no power in it, or if there was, it was the power of meaning only. But my blood recognized the grammar anyway, the way your own name turns your head in a crowd.

* * *

We made camp in the shell of a house that still had half a roof, and while Tam coaxed a fire small and smokeless from damp scrub—she could do things with fire that had nothing to do with magic and everything to do with a life I was slowly realizing had been much longer and stranger than "retired smuggler" covered—Ferro knelt in the square and wrote.

I sat across from him with my knees drawn up, watching the story come out of the ground stroke by stroke.

He began with a dragon. Of course he did. He drew it large, sweeping, wings spanning the width of the old street, tail curling through the nettles. But it wasn't the dragons in the chapbooks they sold in Vell's night market alongside my luck-knots—no rearing wyrm with a knight's lance in its throat. This dragon was curled. Resting. Its wing sheltered something beneath it, and when I leaned closer I saw he'd drawn fields under that wing. Rows of crops. Tiny houses. People.

*A gift,* he signed, pointing from the wing to the fields. Then he tapped his chest, twice: *given.*

"The founding myth says they were conquered," Kess said from the doorway. She'd come back from walking the perimeter. She always came back from walking the perimeter, though she pretended each time that she might not. "The crown's own histories. The Dragon War ended when the last free dragons were broken at Emberhold by the first king's armies and bound beneath the mountain. That's what they taught us in the barracks. That's what's carved on the Triumph Arch in the capital. I've read it. I've saluted under it."

Ferro looked at her for a long moment. Then he went back to the ash.

He drew the mountain next. Emberhold, unmistakable, with the spiral of the gate cut into its flank. And then he drew figures coming down out of the mountain—not soldiers. Figures with their arms raised. And from each figure's hands he drew lines running into the mountain, into the spiral, and the lines were dense, close-packed, hundreds of them, thousands, until the mountain's base was black with them.

Lines going *in*. Nothing coming out.

"What is that?" I asked, though some cold part of me already knew, the same cold part that had known since the map first warmed against my palm, since the wards in my grandmother's drainage-gates had whispered my name.

He answered by drawing people. Ordinary people, stick-thin in the ash-script but unmistakable—a woman holding a child's hand, an old man bent over a staff, a boy carrying water. He drew them walking toward the mountain in lines. He drew the lines going in.

And then he drew what waited outside the villages. He drew riders. Tall riders, and beside each rider a mark I knew from every Warden post in Vell: the crowned tower. The king's men, a century ago, riding from farm to farm.

*Tithing,* he signed. His hands were very steady. That was the worst part. *Every family gave one. Every family. Or the village burned.*

The fire popped in the hearth behind me. Tam didn't turn around, but I saw her shoulders go rigid, saw her hands stop moving in the firewood, and stay stopped.

"Not defeated," Ferro signed, and drew a single line under everything, the line that meant *true*, the line my grandmother had used at the bottom of her ward-diagrams like a signature. *Sacrificed. The seal drinks. It has always drunk.*

* * *

I don't remember standing up. I remember being upright, in the square, with ash dust on my knees, looking at the well-capped stone in the center and understanding suddenly why nothing grew within ten feet of it.

"They fed it," I said. My voice came out strange, flat, like someone else's. "Here. In this square. They brought them here and—" I couldn't finish it. My mind kept sliding off the end of the sentence like wet rope.

"One person," Tam said quietly, behind me. "One person per village, per year. Sometimes two, in the bad years, when the seal guttered faster." She came and stood beside me. She was looking at the well too. "It wasn't called feeding, back then. It was called *keeping the peace*. You'd get a letter with the crowned tower on it, and everyone in the house would know what it meant, and nobody would say the words, because saying the words got you put on the list too, sometimes."

"You know a lot about it," Kess said. Her voice had an edge I hadn't heard from her before. Not suspicion exactly. Something rawer. "For a smuggler."

"I know a lot about a lot of things, girl."

But she wouldn't look at either of us, and I filed that away with all the other things about Tam I was filing away, the ledger of her evasions getting longer by the mile.

"And the Order," I said. "The Ashen Order. The ones who've been following us since the ford." I'd seen them twice now, gray-robed figures at a distance, never closing, never leaving. "This is what they do. This is what they're for. They kept it going. After the war ended, after the tithing was supposed to stop—they kept feeding it. Voluntarily. That's their whole—" Faith. Their whole faith. Walking into the mountain because a hundred years ago the crown decided a dragon's sleep was worth more than a farmer's daughter.

Ferro was drawing again. Faster now, his finger tearing through the ash, and this time the figures going into the mountain wore robes, and this time there were no riders forcing them, and somehow that was worse, and I think he knew it was worse, because after he finished he sat back on his heels and looked at the drawing and then looked at me, and his eyes were wet.

Then he wiped the whole thing out with one sweep of his arm.

"Why?" I asked. "Why erase it?"

He signed slowly, choosing each shape: *Some truths are doors.* A pause. *You walk through when ready.*

"Well, I'm not ready," I said, and my voice cracked in the middle of it, and I hated that, hated the crack, hated the ash, hated the cradle in the doorway and the well and the crowned tower and the map burning its quiet warmth against my ribs where I'd sewn it into my coat. "I'm a charm-seller. I make knots that help headaches. I am not—I was never supposed to be—"

"Noor." Tam's hand landed on my shoulder, heavy as a roof-beam. "Look at the village."

"I'm looking at it."

"No. Look at it." She turned me gently, firmly, the way you turn someone who doesn't want to see. "A century, and nobody rebuilt. Not once. Not one squatter, not one bandit camp, not one fool kid running away from home. People avoid this valley the way a tongue avoids a broken tooth. They don't know why. They just do." Her grip tightened. "You know why. You've known since the gates in Vell sang to you. The wards your family laid aren't in the mountain, child. They're in the *ground*. All of it. Every road we've walked since we left, the land's been checking your blood against a list."

The wind came down the valley then, moving through the dead streets, and I swear—though I would spend years deciding whether I swore truly—that the nettles bowed in rows, in patterns, in lines that ran toward the mountain.

Toward home, said a voice that was not a voice. Toward the keeping.

* * *

Kess took first watch, because Kess always took first watch, because sleeping among us required an act of trust she hadn't yet performed. I lay in the half-roofed house with Ferro breathing slow and even near the fire and Tam's snores rolling in like distant weather, and I did not sleep.

Near midnight, Kess appeared in the doorway. I heard her boots stop. I felt her looking at me.

"The Triumph Arch," she said, into the dark. "When I was sworn in, they march you under it, and you recite the oath, and there's a line—*by the binding, peace; by the sacrifice, order*. I thought sacrifice meant the soldiers who died breaking the dragons." A long silence. "Eleven years I wore the badge, and I was reciting a confession."

"Now you know," I said.

"Yeah." I heard her shift against the doorframe. "Now the question is what knowing costs. Because the envoy will have followed us past the ford by now. And the Ashen Order isn't following anymore, Noor. They stopped at the ridge an hour ago. They're making camp."

My heart went hard and small. "They've caught up."

"No," Kess said, and I heard something in her voice I'd never heard from her before—something that in a lesser voice might have been fear. "Caught up is when they want to talk. They're making camp the way you make camp at a graveside." A pause. "Tomorrow we find out whose grave they think it is."

Outside, far off on the ridge, a single gray-robed figure stood against the stars, unmoving, watching our little fire the way the mountain watched everything.

Waiting, I thought. Everything in this country waits. The village waits. The dragon waits. The dead wait under the ash with their stories written and erased and written again.

Tomorrow, I suspected, the waiting would end for somebody.
