## 17. The Crown's Bargain

The envoy arrived at dusk, which told Kess everything she needed to know about him.

Men who came in daylight wanted things done properly. Men who came at dusk wanted them done quietly. And men who traveled three days from the capital with only two guards and a lacquered case full of promises were the most dangerous kind of all, because they had already been paid for whatever they were about to do.

She watched him from the window of the waystation inn as his carriage rolled into the yard, mud to the axles, the royal crest on its door half-scraped away—deliberately, she'd wager, so it could be repainted with something less memorable before the return trip. He stepped down into the lamplight: a narrow man in dove-gray silk, gloves spotless despite the road, a face like a ledger balanced to the copper.

"Lord Envoy Sarel Vane," announced one of the guards, though no one had asked.

"Master Vane," Kess corrected, when he reached her table. "Envoy is a courtesy title. You're not nobility."

His smile didn't flicker. "And you're not a Warden, Captain Kessrin. Yet here we both are, wearing titles we've outgrown." He sat without invitation and signaled the innkeep for wine. "You burned your credentials at the Marrow checkpoint. Spectacular choice. Very dramatic. The garrison commander wept, I'm told, mostly into his reports."

"I'm told you read reports for a living. So you know what happened at that checkpoint."

"I know a Warden captain let four fugitives walk through a royal inspection because—" he consulted a paper he did not need, unfolding it with theatrical care, "—she had grown *sentimental*." He folded it again. "The Crown prefers a different word. *Resourceful.*"

Kess said nothing. The wine arrived. Vane tasted it, winced faintly, and drank anyway, which was the first honest thing he'd done.

"You've been tracking them," he said. "Don't bother denying it. Your requisitions alone would hang you—a disgraced officer buying trail rations in quantities for five, drawing coin against a dead woman's pension. You've made yourself very easy to find, Captain. I don't think that's an accident."

It wasn't. That was the humiliating part. She could have covered her tracks; she'd taught half the Wardens' corps how to cover theirs. But Noor Brightwater was walking toward the Emberhold with a dying seal at their back and an Ashen Order cult somewhere behind or ahead of them—Kess genuinely didn't know which, and that not-knowing had been chewing her hollow for six days—and she had decided, somewhere on the hollow road between shame and resolve, that if the Crown wanted her found, then being found would buy her time.

Time was the only currency she had left.

"So," she said. "Make your offer."

Vane set down his cup. From the lacquered case he drew a single sheet of heavy paper, sealed but unsigned, and laid it on the table between them like a card turned face-up.

"Full pardon. Reinstatement with seniority. Your name struck from the disgrace rolls—the Marrow checkpoint expunged, the Ashford affair expunged, everything back to your academy years if you like. A captaincy in the capital garrison, which is where careers go to flourish rather than die." He tapped the paper once. "In exchange: the map, the map-bearer, and every person traveling with them, delivered to Crown custody at the Emberhold road's end."

"And what happens to them in Crown custody?"

"They'll be treated according to their usefulness." He said it pleasantly, the way a man describes weather. "The witch has skills the realm requires. The old smuggler knows roads the realm requires. The boy—" a small pause, the first genuine hesitation she'd seen in him, "—the boy is a complication. Complications are handled."

*Handled.* Kess turned her cup slowly on the table. Ferro, who signed his name in sigils older than the kingdom, who had drawn her a picture of a wren on the morning they parted ways because she'd once told him birds were the only honest couriers. *Handled.*

"The dragon," she said. "That's what this is really about. Not the map. Not the witch. The Crown wants the fire."

"The Crown wants *certainty*," Vane corrected. "A living dragon under Crown control ends the succession crisis, ends the border wars, ends the Ashen Order's recruitment problem—people stop feeding themselves to a seal when the thing the seal protects belongs to the throne. One asset, Captain, and every problem in the realm becomes solvable." He leaned forward. "Or the seal fails on its own schedule, the fire gutters out, and everything the ban was built to prevent comes apart anyway—but *unmanaged*. Which future would you prefer to live in?"

"That's not my question. My question is what the Crown intends to *do* with a dragon."

"Use it."

"For what?"

"For whatever the realm requires." His patience was beginning to thin at the edges; she could see it in the way his thumb pressed the signet on his hand. "Captain. You were a good Warden once—I've read the early reports, the ones they buried. You believed the law protected people. Here is your one chance to believe it again, with a salary attached. Take it."

Kess looked at the pardon a long moment. And here was the truth she would never say aloud, not to Vane, not to a tribunal, not to herself at three in the morning: some part of her wanted it. Wanted the uniform back, the name cleared, the ten years of service un-wasted. Wanted to walk into her mother's house in the capital and not have the neighbors draw their shutters.

She reached for the paper.

And stopped, because lying well required touching the thing you claimed to want.

"I need assurances," she said. "Not yours. The Crown's. In writing. What happens to the witch after the dragon is secured. Specifics. If I'm selling people, I want to know the price they fetch."

Vane's smile returned, thinner now. "Negotiating. Good. That's the Warden I remember." He produced a second document, began writing in a small precise hand. "The witch will be remanded to the Royal Collegium for study and service. The smuggler to a work-pardon on the northern roads. The boy—"

"The boy goes free."

"He's a sigil-speaker. Do you understand what that means? The last recorded bloodline that wrote ward-script *natively* died out eighty years ago. He's not a child, Captain, he's a national treasure—"

"He's eleven."

"He's leverage either way. Free, kept, or spent—he will be one of those three things. The only question is whether you get a pardon for choosing which." Vane wrote on. "I can offer: the boy enters Crown fostering. Educated. Fed. *Kept*, but gently. It's more than the world was going to give him."

It was. That was the sick truth of it. Kess thought of Ferro's hands moving through the air, quick and fluent, saying things no voice could say. Thought of the ash village, and the drawings he'd made there—dragons bowing their heads, dragons *choosing*, a history written by no book she'd ever been issued.

"Leave the line blank," she heard herself say. "The boy's disposition. Leave it blank and I'll sign."

Vane looked up sharply. "That's not how—"

"It's exactly how. You want the map more than you want terms. I watched your face when you said *asset*. Men who talk like that have already promised their masters results, and results have deadlines. You'll take a blank line over a delay." She slid the pardon toward herself. "Blank line. Or I walk out of this inn tonight and you spend another month tracking a ghost who knows every trick you were ever going to use."

For a long moment the only sound was the innkeep stacking mugs behind the bar, and wind worrying the shutters.

Vane signed the amendment. Left the line blank. Watched her sign the pardon with a flourish that looked, even to her, like surrender.

"There," he said softly. "Welcome back to the Crown's service, Captain."

"Not yet." She stood, gathering the papers. "Delivery takes time. They're a week ahead of me and moving fast. I'll need supplies, authority to commandeer horses, and—" she extended her hand, palm up, "—your seal-ring."

He blinked. "My what?"

"Your envoy's signet. If I'm delivering fugitives to Crown custody at the Emberhold, I'll need credentials that open doors—literally. The inner vault answers to royal seal-work; you know that as well as I do, or you wouldn't be carrying that ring. I can't present a pardon written by a man whose authority I can't prove." She kept her hand steady. Kept her face bored. "Unless you'd rather come along yourself. Into the mountains. In winter. Chasing a witch who's already survived your Wardens twice."

Every calculation she needed him to make, she watched him make. The mud. The cold. The Ashen Order, whose reputation for treating envoys as offerings had reached even the capital. The comfortable distance between a lord's desk and a mountain pass.

Vane worked the ring off his finger and set it in her palm. Gold, heavy, the royal crest cut deep—and beneath the crest, so fine you'd miss it unless you knew to look, a secondary engraving: a keyway. She closed her fingers around it and felt it warm against her skin, warmer than metal had any right to be, and understood that Tam had told her true: the Crown had keys to the Emberhold, keys forged in the same generation as the seal itself.

Old sins. Old tools. The kingdom had been built on top of this secret like a house built on a grave.

"I'll want it back," Vane said.

"Naturally." She pocketed it. "Send word to the capital that negotiations concluded successfully. Tell them the witch is cooperative. Buy me ten days."

"And in ten days?"

"In ten days," Kess said, buckling her sword on, "you'll have your answer. Whether it's the one you ordered is another matter."

She walked out into the dark. The stable boy brought her horse without being asked; she'd paid him yesterday to be ready at any hour, and the boy had taken the coin with the flat competence of someone raised on roads where readiness was survival. She mounted, checked the ring again—it sat in her inner pocket like a coal that refused to cool—and rode north.

Ten days. She'd bought Noor ten days, and bought herself a key to the one door in the world that mattered, and committed treason so complete that the pardon in her saddlebag was now worth precisely the parchment it was written on.

The funny part, the part that almost made her laugh into the freezing wind, was that it hadn't felt like betrayal at all. It had felt like the first honest work she'd done in years.

Two days later, at a shrine crossroads where pilgrims tied rag-prayers to a dead tree, she found the wren.

Drawn in ash-grey sigil-ink on the trunk, small and quick and unmistakably Ferro's hand: a bird in flight, and beneath it, in the old script Noor's family had invented and the boy somehow knew, a single word.

*Hurry.*

They knew she was coming. They were waiting—or running—and either way the Emberhold lay three days north through country the Ashen Order had begun to salt with their own marks. Kess touched the drawing once, felt the faint residual warmth of fresh ink, and spurred her horse.

Behind her, far to the south, a royal courier was already riding hard with news of a bargain struck.

Ahead, the mountain waited with its door, and in her pocket, the key grew warmer with every mile.
