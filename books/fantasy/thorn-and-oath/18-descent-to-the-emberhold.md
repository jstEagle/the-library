## 18. Descent to the Emberhold

The stolen ring was warm in Noor's pocket the whole way down.

It shouldn't have been. It was a plain band of iron with the crown's sun stamped into it, and iron held no heat that Noor could feel—but it had been against Kess's skin for years before she lifted it off a sleeping envoy's hand, and maybe metal remembered. Maybe everything remembered. The mountain certainly did.

They had come through the outer gate three days ago, and since then the Emberhold had been nothing but stairs. Stairs cut into living rock, spiraling down past walls that sweated a faint amber light, as if the stone itself were honeycombed with old fire. Noor's grandmother's journals had called these passages the Throat. The name fit. Every step felt like being swallowed.

"Tell me again why we're not resting," Kess said behind them. Her voice echoed strangely, arriving at Noor's ears a half-beat after her lips moved, as though the mountain were translating her.

"Because the seal is failing faster than we thought." Noor didn't turn around. "You saw the wall back there."

They had all seen it. An hour ago the passage had opened into a gallery where the amber light guttered like a candle in wind, and where the ward-marks carved into the stone—Brightwater marks, generations of them, each one signed with a small looping *B*—had gone dark in patches. Dead letters. Dead promises. Ferro had run his fingers over one of the darkened sigils and then looked up at Noor with an expression Noor couldn't bear to translate.

"I saw it," Kess said. "I also saw all of us running on fumes. Even the boy."

Ferro, walking beside Noor, drew something on his slate and held it up: a stick figure lying down, with a scribbled blanket.

"He says even you need sleep," Tam said from the rear, and coughed.

Noor stopped. Turned. Tam leaned against the wall with one hand pressed flat to the stone, and in the amber light her face looked like paper left out in rain—the color gone thin, the lines gone soft. She'd been coughing since the memory-ward at the Ashen Order's camp, three days back, the one that had shown Noor their mother's face and made them walk through it. Tam had walked through hers without a word. That should have been the first warning. Nothing about Tam was ever silent unless it cost her.

"We rest when we're through," Noor said. "The vault is close. I can feel it."

"You can feel it," Kess repeated flatly. "The hedge-witch who spent ten years selling fever-charms that were ninety percent knotwork can feel an ancient draconic seal through half a mile of mountain."

"Ninety-five percent knotwork," Noor said. "And yes."

It was true, and they hated how true. The closer they came to the heart of the Emberhold, the more Noor felt it—not the dragon, not yet, but the seal itself, vast and threadbare, like a blanket worn to gauze. And underneath the feeling of it, something else. A pull. Not toward the seal. Through it. As if somewhere below, something enormous had turned its attention toward the sound of their footsteps and was listening.

"Fine," Kess said. "But if any of us collapses, I'm not carrying them. I'm a disgraced ex-Warden, not a mule."

"You carried me for two miles after the bounty hunters," Noor said.

"That was different. You were bleeding on my only good coat. I wanted you off it quickly."

Tam laughed—a short, cracked sound—and pushed off the wall. "Keep walking, children. Argue on the way down. It keeps the fear quiet."

* * *

The fire came an hour later, and it did not burn.

Noor smelled it first—woodsmoke and hot metal and, beneath both, something older, like lightning trapped in resin. Then the passage ahead filled with light, orange-white and total, and Ferro grabbed Noor's sleeve and pointed frantically at his slate: *NO. TURN BACK.* He'd drawn flames eating a house, flames eating people, flames drawn with the frantic pressure of someone who had watched a real fire take everything.

"It's a ward," Noor said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Grandmother wrote about this. The last defense before the inner vault. It doesn't hurt you. It—" They hesitated, because the journal entry had been vague in a way that felt deliberate, the handwriting going cramped and evasive. "It shows you things. Fire shows you what you burned."

"And if I refuse?" Kess asked.

"The mountain decides whether you're refusing or lying." Noor swallowed. "There's no way around. The passage narrows to a crawl beyond it. We go through."

Kess looked at the fire for a long moment. In its light her face lost the Warden's mask entirely—all the practiced boredom, the ironic distance—and what was underneath was just a woman in her forties who had once believed in something enough to ruin herself for it.

"My worst choice," she said quietly. "That's what it will show me, isn't it? Yours too. Everyone's."

"Yes."

"Then let's be quick about our honesty." She stepped forward, rolled her shoulders like a woman wading into cold surf, and walked into the fire.

The flames closed over her and she was gone—not screaming, not falling, simply elsewhere, her outline wavering like a reflection in disturbed water. Then Tam went, jaw set, one hand briefly touching Noor's shoulder in passing, hard enough to mean something. Then Ferro, after drawing one last thing on his slate and pressing it into Noor's hands: a small figure standing alone, and beside it, in the sigil-language Noor's family had invented and Ferro had somehow learned, the mark that meant *I WILL FIND YOU ON THE OTHER SIDE.*

Then Noor stood alone at the edge of the fire, and the fire waited.

They thought of burning the map, that first night in Vell. They had actually held it over the candle, wax seal softening, ink gleaming like it was watching. They thought of every charm sold to someone who needed a miracle and got knotted string. They thought of their mother's last journal entry—*He is not ready. He is not ready. He is not ready*—and the door they had slipped out of at seventeen with a satchel and a lie about the coast.

"No," Noor said aloud, to the fire, to the mountain, to whatever listened. "I already know what I burned. Show it to me anyway. I'll look this time."

They walked in.

* * *

The world became Vell, and it was raining, and Noor was seventeen.

They knew it wasn't real. That was the cruelest part—that the ward let them know, let them stand inside the memory as a guest at their own worst hour, unable to change a single word. The shop smelled the same: beeswax and dried feverfew and the particular dust of a place where magic was hidden so well it had started to rot. Their mother stood at the workbench with her back turned, grinding something in a mortar, and she said, without turning, "The gate won't keep itself, little spark. One day you'll understand that keeping is not the same as caging."

"I know," seventeen-year-old Noor said, and the words came out of present-day Noor's mouth too, because the ward had merged them, because this was the confession the fire demanded. "I know, Mum. I'm sorry. I thought you were asking me to be a lock. I didn't understand you were asking me to be a door."

Their mother turned. Her face was the face from the journals, from the memory-ward at the Ashen camp, from every dream Noor had refused to remember on waking. But this time she didn't say *he is not ready.* This time she smiled, tired and terrible, and said:

"Now you do. Go down. Finish it."

The fire took her away, and the rain, and the shop, and Noor came out the other side on their hands and knees on cold stone, weeping without shame, because the mountain had made them honest and there was no unmaking it.

Kess sat against the far wall, pale as milk, staring at nothing. Ferro was curled in a ball, and when he saw Noor he uncurled just enough to draw on his slate: a stick figure, and next to it a house on fire, and next to that—he hesitated, chalk trembling—the sigil for *survived.*

"Yeah," Noor rasped. "Survived. All of us."

"Speak for yourself," Kess said, but her voice shook, and no one believed her, including her.

Where was Tam?

Noor stood. The passage here was a low vaulted chamber, amber-lit, and beyond it the stair continued down—but Tam wasn't in it. Tam was still standing at the mouth of the fire-ward, except she wasn't standing so much as propped there, one hand braced on the rock, and the fire had passed through her and left something behind. Or taken something. It was hard to tell which.

"Tamsin," Noor said, using her full name for the first time in weeks, and crossed the chamber fast.

Tam's eyes found them. They were clear—clearer than Noor had seen them since the hollow road—and that clarity was worse than confusion would have been. "Ah," she said. "There you are. Don't make that face. I'm fine. I'm just—" She tried to step forward and her leg folded under her like wet rope, and Noor caught her before she hit the ground.

"What did it show you?"

Tam laughed, breathless, ugly. "What didn't it show me? Forty years of it, little spark. Every crate I ran along this road. Every life the Order fed into the seal while I looked the other way because the coin was good and the cause sounded holy." Her hand gripped Noor's wrist with shocking strength. "But that's not the worst. The worst was the night I left my post. There was a keeper then—a Brightwater, your grandmother's cousin, a woman named Odile—and she asked me to stay. Just stay. One more winter. And I had a ship waiting and a debt to outrun, and I told her the seal would hold fine without me watching." Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "She kept it alone for eleven years. Alone, Noor. And I slept warm the whole time."

"That was forty years ago. You came back. You're here now—"

"I'm here now," Tam agreed, "and the mountain knows it, and the mountain isn't impressed." She patted Noor's arm, almost fondly. "Help me sit. My legs will come back or they won't. Either way, you four aren't stopping, and I won't have the boy carrying me. He's carried enough."

"We're not leaving you."

"You absolutely are not leaving me, correct, because I refuse to be left, but you are going down those stairs while I sit here and recover, and Kess is going to guard me, and you are going to stop arguing with me in that tone because I knew your grandmother, child, and I have material." Despite everything, despite the gray in her lips, Tam's grin surfaced, crooked and indestructible. "Go. The seal's dying. I intend to be upright when you come back up, and I intend to be insufferable about it."

Kess crouched beside them. She looked at Tam for a long moment, and then at Noor, and something passed between the two women that Noor wasn't meant to read and read anyway: two people who had both abandoned posts, recognizing the bill come due.

"I'll stay," Kess said. "Someone has to make sure she doesn't do anything noble."

"Perish the thought," Tam said.

Ferro tugged Noor's sleeve and held up his slate. He had drawn the four of them—Noor, himself, Tam seated, Kess standing guard—and above them, a fifth figure: a long, coiling shape with wings folded, drawn not in the crude style of his other pictures but in careful, reverent line, the way pilgrims drew saints.

*It knows we're coming,* the sigil beneath it read.

"Yes," Noor said. "I think it's always known."

* * *

The last stair descended in silence.

Noor went down with Ferro a step behind, their footsteps ringing on stone that grew warmer with every turn, until the warmth was not warmth but heat, gentle and enormous, like standing near a hearth the size of the sky. The dead patches in the ward-walls thickened here—dark sigil after dark sigil, a century of Brightwaters whose promises had gone out—and Noor touched one as they passed, and felt nothing, and understood with a lurch that soon all of them would feel like this. Soon the mountain would be only stone.

Unless.

The stair ended at a door.

Not a grand door—it was smaller than Noor had imagined, barely taller than a man, iron-banded oak gone black with age, set into the rock as casually as a cellar hatch. But across its surface, in silver that still gleamed after five hundred years, ran a single line of script, and Noor's blood recognized it before their mind did, because it was their own family's hand, their own invented alphabet, and it said:

*WE STAYED SO THE WORLD COULD FORGET. REMEMBER US GENTLY.*

Below the words was a keyhole shaped like nothing Noor had ever seen—except that Kess's stolen ring, when Noor drew it from their pocket, fit the shape of the crown-sun stamped on its face exactly, precisely, perfectly, as though the envoy who wore it had inherited it from someone who had inherited it from someone who had stolen it from this door a very long time ago.

"Well," Noor whispered. "That explains a few things about the crown."

Ferro made a sound—his first attempt at speech in all the weeks Noor had known him, a small cracked syllable that might have been *oh*—and gripped Noor's hand.

Noor slid the ring into the keyhole.

The door did not open so much as consent, swinging inward on hinges that had waited centuries, and the heat rolled out over them like a tide, and the light was not amber anymore but gold, deep gold, moving gold, and in the middle of the vast dark vault below, coiled around a pillar of its own extinguishing flame, something the size of a cathedral lifted its head.

Eyes like banked coals found them across the distance.

And the last dragon spoke—not in sound, but directly into the marrow of Noor's bones, in a voice old as the war and tired as the seal:

*"At last. A Brightwater. Tell me, keeper's child—are you here to feed me, to free me, or finally, finally, to answer my question?"*

Noor stood at the top of the last step, hand in a mute boy's hand, a thief's ring in their pocket and their mother's forgiveness in their chest, and realized they had no idea what the question was.

The dragon waited. The fire gutters. Somewhere above, Tam coughed in the dark.

"Ask me," Noor said.
