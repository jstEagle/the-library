## 1. The Valley That Forgot Magic

The trouble with finishing a map of a place you have lived your whole life is that you run out of excuses to keep drawing it.

Freya Vane discovered this on an autumn afternoon so golden and uneventful that she distrusted it on principle. She sat at her drafting table by the south window of the map-house, the last window that still caught the light after midday, and dipped her pen in walnut ink for what she fully intended to be the final stroke. The map of the Warded Valley — *her* valley, the only world she had ever known or, truthfully, ever been permitted to know — lay beneath her hands in a state of near-perfection.

Near-perfection was, in her professional opinion, the most dangerous condition a map could occupy.

She held the pen above the parchment and considered the last blank space: the small triangle of hillside between Miller's Bend and the old orchard wall, where the brambles grew so thick that even goats refused them. Every other cartographer in creation would have written *here be thorns* and moved on with their lives. But Freya had spent three days of the previous spring crawling through those brambles on her stomach, ink-stained and swearing cheerfully, until she confirmed that behind them stood nothing but a mossy boulder shaped rather like a sleeping pig.

She drew the boulder. She drew it accurately. She even gave it a tiny label — *Pig Stone (does not move; verified twice)* — and then sat back and looked at the whole of it.

The valley ran the length of the parchment like a green ribbon caught between two walls of mountains. The Ashenfell range to the east, the Grey Teeth to the west, both rendered in careful hatching that she'd learned from her mother's hand. The river Liss wound down the middle, fattening as it went, past the mill, past the tannery (drawn slightly smaller than it deserved, because Freya maintained that no honest map should flatter a tannery), past the village of Hollowmere with its crooked bell tower and its forty-one chimneys, each one individually counted, because she had counted them herself on a cold Tuesday in March while the chimney-sweeps' children followed her around asking questions she answered honestly, which was more than could be said for most adults.

And all along the borders, drawn in a fine silver-inked line that she mixed herself from powdered mica and patience: the wards.

You couldn't see the wards with your eyes, not exactly. What you saw instead was a shimmer, like heat off a summer road, ringing the entire valley where mountain met meadow. And what you *heard* — if you walked close enough, which Freya had done many times, pressing her ear to the air itself the way other people pressed their ears to beehives — was a hum. Low and constant and faintly comforting, like bees kept in a well-built hive. Caged bees, her mother used to call them. Busy about their business. Keeping the world sweet.

*The world outside isn't wicked, little wren,* her mother would say, braiding Freya's hair by lamplight. *It's just hungry. And the wards keep its teeth out.*

Her mother had been dead six years now, and Freya still caught herself listening for that voice in odd moments — when the kettle sang, when wind moved the eaves, when she found one of Maren Vane's old maps tucked into a drawer like a letter never sent.

She blew gently across the finished map to dry the last ink. Pig Stone gleamed. Forty-one chimneys stood at attention. The silver line of the wards ran unbroken from the northern pass to the southern falls, and looking at it gave Freya the same settled feeling she imagined other people got from prayer.

There. It was done. The definitive map. Every road, every stream, every stubborn boulder. Nineteen years of living and four years of surveying, committed to parchment at last.

Which meant, she realized with a small sinking sensation, that tomorrow she would have absolutely nothing to do.

* * *

She celebrated the completion the way any sensible cartographer celebrates: alone, with bread and honey and the good candle lit early, sitting cross-legged on the floor of the map-house among her chests and tubes and rolls.

The map-house had been her mother's before it was hers, and it still smelled of Maren Vane — cedar oil and iron-gall ink and something faintly herbal that Freya had never identified and had stopped trying to, because some mysteries were better left as housemates. Her mother had been the valley's cartographer too, and her mother's mother before that, back through a lineage of Vane women who had apparently made a family tradition of knowing exactly where everything was.

*Knowing where things are keeps them from surprising you,* Great-Aunt Ysolde had supposedly written in a margin somewhere. Freya agreed with this completely. Surprises were for people who hadn't done their fieldwork.

The map chest sat under the drafting table — a long cedar box with iron fittings, older than Freya, older than her mother, older possibly than the village itself if you judged by the way its hinges groaned like an old man asked to dance. It held the master copies: every finished map of the valley, going back generations. Freya opened it now to make room for her new work, lifting out the older maps one by one and laying them across the floor in chronological order, which she did partly for storage reasons and partly because she loved seeing the valley accrete through time like a pearl forming around a grain of sand.

Her great-grandmother's map showed just the river and a scattering of houses, the mountains drawn as vague worried lumps. Her grandmother's added the orchards and the mill-race. Her mother's —

Freya stopped.

Her mother's maps were always the finest in the chest. Maren Vane had a draftsman's precision and a poet's instinct for where to put a decorative tree. Freya knew every one of them by heart, could name the year of each by the weight of its linework alone.

But this one she did not know.

It lay at the very bottom of the chest, beneath the false bottom, which was a phrase that took Freya a moment to process because the chest did not *have* a false bottom, she had inventoried this chest annually since she was nine years old and could have sworn a solemn and notarized oath on the matter.

Yet here was a shallow drawer set into the base of the box, hidden so cunningly that she'd only found it because the new map's corner had caught on something as she slid it in — something that turned out to be a small brass catch, worn nearly smooth, exactly the color of aged cedar.

She knelt there a long moment, fingers on the catch, telling herself reasonable things. *Old secret compartments often contain old sewing supplies. Or letters. Or someone's emergency cache of candied ginger.* All plausible. All fine.

But her heart had begun doing a slow, heavy thing against her ribs, and the candle flame leaned sideways though there was no draft, and somewhere far away — farther than the mountains, farther than the shimmering silver line — she thought she heard humming.

She opened the compartment.

Inside lay a single book, bound in grey leather gone soft as moleskin, its corners rounded by handling. No title on the cover. A leather tie held it shut, knotted in a way Freya recognized without wanting to — her mother's knot, the peculiar double-loop she'd used on parcels and hair ribbons and the garden gate, the knot she'd taught Freya when she was seven, saying, *A knot should tell the truth about whether it's been touched.*

The knot was intact. Whatever was inside had waited untouched for at least six years.

Freya untied it anyway. Her mother had also taught her that curiosity, handled carefully, was a tool and not a vice — right up alongside the scaling knife and the good compass.

The book was a ledger.

Not a household ledger, though it began like one. The early pages were domestic and faintly funny: sums paid to the cooper, a tally of eggs owed to Widow Marsh ("*she counts dishonestly; add two*"), a note about a rooster named General whose disposition required separate accounting. Freya smiled despite the strange tightness in her chest. Here was her mother alive on the page, exasperated by poultry, exact about everything.

Then, somewhere past the middle of the book, the entries changed.

The handwriting stayed the same — Maren Vane's clear, upright script — but the subject did not. The sums stopped being in coin.

*Tenth turn. Paid: one winter without sickness, valley-wide. Received: continuation.*

*Eleventh turn. Paid: the bell-tower's shadow, taken at dawn. Received: continuation.*

*Twelfth turn. Paid: my memory of the sea. I am told I will not miss what I cannot recall. I find I do not recall missing it.*

Freya read that entry three times. Her mother had never once mentioned the sea. Not a story, not a longing, not a single word in nineteen years of being her mother — and Freya had catalogued her mother's stories the way she catalogued everything, thoroughly, lovingly, completely.

*Paid: my memory of the sea.*

What kind of price was a memory? Who bought such a thing? And why would her mother — practical, unsuperstitious, allergic-to-nonsense Maren Vane, who had once told the village priest to his face that omens were "weather for the timid" — why would *she* pay anything to anyone in a ledger full of words like *turns* and *continuation*?

Freya turned the pages faster now, the candle guttering, her breath loud in the quiet room. The entries marched toward the end of the book like footprints toward a cliff edge. Each one stranger, larger, dearer. A year of the orchard's sweetness. The color blue, for a season, from the whole eastern sky. And beside each payment, always, the same flat receipt:

*Received: continuation.*

Continuation of what? Of the bargain? Of the valley? Of—

She reached the final page.

Only one entry remained. No date beyond a single phrase at its head, and when Freya read that phrase, the warmth drained out of the room so thoroughly she might as well have been reading by moonlight.

*On the thirteenth turn.*

Beneath it, the last line her mother had ever written in this book. The ink was darker than the rest, laid on thick, as if the pen had been pressed hard enough to hurt the page. As if the writer had wanted, needed, the words to be permanent.

*One soul, paid in full.*

Freya sat very still on the floor of the map-house, surrounded by nineteen years of maps, by forty-one counted chimneys and one immovable stone and a silver ward-line running unbroken around the only world she had ever known.

Outside, autumn wind moved through the valley, gentle and ordinary, rustling the late apples in the orchard. Somewhere a dog barked at nothing. Somewhere the evening bell rang its usual seven strokes.

And all along the border of her perfect, finished, thoroughly mapped world, the wards hummed on — low and constant, like caged bees.

Like bees, Freya thought, her thumb resting on the dark final line, that have just noticed something has come to the hive.

* * *

She did not sleep so much as hover near sleep, the ledger open on the blanket beside her like a patient cat.

She had tried, over three hours and two cups of chamomile, to make the entry mean something survivable. *Paid in full* could mean the debt was *finished*. Settled. Closed. Perhaps her mother had sold — something, some soul-shaped metaphor, her mother's *passion*, her *spirit of adventure*, things she'd arguably never possessed anyway — and the transaction was complete, historical, safe in the past tense.

Except the ledger's own grammar argued otherwise. Every previous entry had said *paid*. This one said *paid in full*, which implied installments. Which implied a schedule. Which implied, somewhere out past the silver shimmer and the sleeping mountains, a creditor who kept accounts as meticulously as any Vane.

And there was the arithmetic, which Freya hated, because arithmetic was never wrong.

Thirteen turns. Twelve payments recorded. One due.

She lay staring at the ceiling beams, counting her mother's entries again in the dark, arriving at the same sum each time, and finding that the sum had her own name written under it in invisible ink.

*Nonsense,* she told herself firmly. *You are a cartographer. You deal in verifiable features.*

So she got up, lit the lamp, and did what she did with every problem: she mapped it. On fresh paper she wrote out what she knew. The ledger existed. The payments were real — real enough that her mother had traded away the color blue and never noticed the sky was missing something. The wards hummed. The valley continued. Continuation received.

And on the thirteenth turn of the moon — which, she calculated with a steadiness that surprised her, fell eleven days from tonight — something would come to collect the balance.

Eleven days.

She looked at the map drying on her table. Pig Stone. Forty-one chimneys. Every road and stream and stubborn boulder of home, rendered knowable, kept safe from surprise.

For the first time in her life, Freya Vane looked at her finished map of the valley and saw what it truly was: the portrait of a cage so comfortable she'd never once thought to count its bars.

Far off, beyond the mountains, beyond the shimmering line, the night sky over the wild country hung thick with stars — thousands upon thousands of them, blazing over lands no Vane had ever surveyed.

Tonight, for no reason she could name and every reason she could, Freya found herself counting those too.

She lost track somewhere past a hundred. And in the morning, when she woke stiff-necked at her drafting table with the ledger still open beneath her hand, she would remember that the stars over the valley — over her safe, warded, humming valley — had seemed, just for a heartbeat before sleep took her, to flicker.

All of them.

At once.

Like candles hearing footsteps on the stairs.
