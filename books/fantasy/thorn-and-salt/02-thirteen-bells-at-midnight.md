## 2. Thirteen Bells at Midnight

The twelfth moon-turn fell on a Tuesday, which Freya Vane always thought was an undignified day for anything important to happen.

She had spent the evening doing what she did every night of the year: checking her inks, trimming her pens, and arguing silently with the valley. The argument had been going on for twenty years now, ever since she'd been old enough to hold a compass, and it went like this: *you are the only world I know, and I resent you for it.*

The map she was finishing lay half-dried across her worktable — the definitive one, the one she'd been building since she was nine years old. *A Complete Survey of the Warded Valley of Emberlin*, rendered at a scale generous enough to show every farmhouse, every footpath, every stone wall that some stubborn ancestor had stacked by hand. She'd drawn the millpond three times because she could never get its northern shore to look as melancholy as it actually was. She'd inked the chapel bell-tower with particular care, because if you squinted, the tower looked like a finger pointing reproachfully at the sky, and Freya found that deeply funny.

At the edges of the map, where other cartographers wrote *here be dragons*, she had written something more honest: *here be nothing.* Not because she believed there was nothing beyond the wards — no one in Emberlin believed that, exactly; they simply didn't believe in it loudly enough for it to matter. But because the wards made mapping beyond them impossible. Her instruments spun. Her bearings lied. Once, when she was fourteen, she'd climbed the eastern ridge with her mother's old brass theodolite and tried to sight a line past the border stones, and the needle had whirled like a startled moth until her mother's hand came down gently over hers.

*Some things aren't for us, little wren,* her mother had said.

Her mother had been dead four years now, and Freya still heard that sentence in the exact pitch of her voice, warm and immovable as a hearthstone.

Outside her window, the valley hummed.

That was the thing about Emberlin that visitors — the few traders who passed through twice a year, blinking as though they'd walked through a curtain of warm water — never quite managed to describe. The wards didn't hum out loud. It wasn't sound, precisely. It lived somewhere behind the teeth, a faint sustained note like a caged bee, and after twenty years Freya had stopped hearing it the way you stop hearing your own heartbeat. Tonight, though, working late over her map, she noticed it. Noticed it the way you notice silence.

She sat back. Dipped her pen. Didn't write anything.

"That's odd," she told the candle.

The candle did not answer, but the flame leaned sideways, though there was no draft.

Freya Vane was not a fanciful woman. Fanciful women did not spend six years surveying drainage ditches. But she was a cartographer, and cartographers know that the world gives warnings — a river changing color before it floods, birds leaving a forest before the fire. The world had begun, very quietly, to give warnings.

She stood, went to the window, and looked up.

The stars were all still there. That was the last ordinary thing about the night, so let it be recorded: at half past eleven, the sky over Emberlin Valley was full of stars, cold and bright and indifferent, and the wards were humming their caged-bee song, and Freya Vane was worried about nothing worse than a crooked line on page forty-one.

Then the bells began.

* * *

There was only one bell in Emberlin — the chapel bell, rung by old Master Corvin at dawn, noon, dusk, and emergencies. It was currently ringing at midnight, which was not one of its hours.

And it was ringing wrong.

Freya knew every voice the bell had: the flat clang of emergency, the bright triple-strike of festival, the drowsy single toll of dusk. This was none of those. The bell tolled once, waited, tolled again — and each toll landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well, and between the tolls she could feel something counting. Not the bell counting. Something *listening* to the bell, and keeping count.

One.

Two.

Three.

She was already moving, throwing her coat over her nightdress, grabbing the lantern from its hook — because whatever else was happening, some animal instinct older than sense told her that this was a night to be standing next to a light. In the lane outside, doors were opening. Old Marta from the dairy stood in her doorway in a shawl, arms crossed tight. The Hedley boys ran barefoot toward the chapel square, whooping, assuming a fire or a wedding or any of the good reasons bells rang.

Four. Five. Six.

"Corvin's lost his mind," Marta said. "Or his clock."

"Since when does he ring past twelve?" Freya asked.

"Since never." Marta's voice was steady, but her knuckles were white where they gripped the shawl. "Girl, count with me. I don't like how my own head feels."

Seven. Eight. The hum of the wards — the note behind the teeth — began to change key. It slid upward, thinning, stretching, like a rope bearing weight it was never woven to bear. Freya felt it in her molars. Around the valley, dogs began to howl, not at the bell but at something else, something above.

Nine.

Ten.

People were coming out into the lanes now, dozens of them, faces tilted upward. And Freya understood, in the way you understand things in dreams — instantly, completely, without any path of reasoning — what everyone was looking at, and why nobody was saying it.

The stars were going out.

Not clouding over. Not dimming. Going out — one by one, in no order she could find, as if someone high above were pinching wicks between vast fingers. Each star died with no sound at all, and the darkness left behind was somehow darker than mere absence, a little hole in the world's ceiling.

Eleven.

The ward-hum snapped. Freya heard it go — a sound like the largest string in the world being cut — and felt the silence of it everywhere at once, a great soft pressure against her ears, against her skin, against the inside of her chest. Twenty years of that note, gone. The valley felt suddenly, horribly *open*, the way a house feels open when a door has blown off its hinges in the night.

Twelve.

Master Corvin, gasping in the belfry, stopped pulling the rope. He was a sensible man, seventy years a ringer, and he knew when a bell was ringing itself.

But the thirteenth toll came anyway.

It came from nowhere. From below. From inside. A single deep stroke that no bronze in the valley had struck, and with it every remaining star in the sky went dark at once — not fading, but *snuffed*, all together, like a held breath finally released — and the night over Emberlin became a blackness Freya had no word for. Not cloudy. Not starless-in-the-ordinary-way. It was a blackness with texture, a blackness that seemed to be looking down.

In the lane, the Hedley boys had stopped whooping. Old Marta said, very quietly, in the voice of a woman praying without admitting it: "Ward-keeper. Someone fetch the ward-keeper."

And then, from the direction of Freya's own cottage at the end of the lane, came a sound like a body falling against a door.

* * *

Later — much later, in the years when she would tell this story around fires in countries that weren't supposed to exist — Freya would say that she didn't remember deciding to walk back down her lane alone while the whole valley huddled together in the chapel square. She would say it as though it proved something brave about her. It didn't. It proved only that the cottage was *hers*, and there was a noise at her door, and fear has never once in history outrun ownership.

The lantern shook in her hand. That much she remembered honestly.

Someone was crumpled against her doorstep. A stranger — tall even folded up the way he was, folded like a chair kicked over in a hurry — wearing a traveling cloak that had been fine once and had since lost an argument with several hundred miles of road. His hair was pale, his face younger than his posture, and across his left cheekbone ran a mark that made Freya's stomach drop through the floorboards of the world: a pattern of thin dark lines, branching, delicate, unmistakably like thorns. They moved. Slowly, lazily, the way a vine moves in a week, except this took seconds — creeping a hair's width along his jaw and then stopping, as if remembering itself.

He was whispering.

Freya knelt, because he was clearly dying or nearly, and because kneeling puts you lower than a stranger and therefore, in some ancient arithmetic, safer. Up close she could smell him — cold air and crushed green stems and, underneath, something metallic, like the moment before lightning.

"—came to collect," he was whispering. His eyes opened. They were a strange color, amber shot through with threads of black, and they fixed on her face with an expression she would spend years trying to name and never quite succeed: recognition, grief, and apology, all arriving at once, all too late.

"What came?" Freya asked. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "Who sent you? Are you hurt? The wards just—"

"The wards just *stopped*," he said, and something between a laugh and a cough broke out of him. "Yes. Yes, they did. I ran ahead of it. I ran ahead of it for eleven days." His hand crawled across the step and closed around her wrist — not gripping, just holding, the way a drowning man holds driftwood he doesn't trust. "You're her. You're the daughter. You have her chin. You have her *chin*, oh, that's — that's very unfair, I carried that contract for thirteen years and nobody told me she'd grown up with her mother's chin."

The word *contract* went through Freya like a nail through paper.

"I don't know what you're talking about," she said. "My mother's been dead four years. Who are you? What contract?"

The stranger's eyes were losing focus, sliding toward the black, starless sky and then back to her, and the thorn-marks on his cheek had begun to pulse faintly, dimly red, like embers breathing. Behind them both, far off across the valley, something howled. It was not a dog. Dogs, as established, had been howling all night, and this was different in every particular that mattered: longer, lower, patient. The howl of something that knows exactly where it is going and is in no hurry whatsoever.

"They'll come for the borders first," the stranger murmured. "They always check the borders. Then they'll come for—" his fingers tightened on her wrist, "—for what's owed. Thirteen turns. Twelve paid. One due." He swallowed. "Due tonight. Due at the turn. I'm sorry. I'm sorry, I argued with them, I argued for years, I told them it wasn't right to send me, of all the—"

"*Who are you?*" Freya said, louder now, and hated the crack in it.

He smiled. It was a terrible smile, sweet and exhausted and full of thorns.

"Bramblehoof," he said. "I carried your soul's contract, Freya Vane. Your mother signed it. And I have come—" his eyes rolled back, and the lantern-light guttered though there was no wind, "—to collect the delivery. Or warn you. I forget which. I forget, at the end of running, which side of the errand I'm—"

He collapsed fully onto her doorstep, thorn-marked cheek against cold stone, and went still.

For a long moment Freya knelt there in the starless dark with her dead lantern and her dead mother's name in a stranger's mouth, and felt the vertigo rise up and swallow her whole. The valley — her valley, her complete, surveyed, mapped-to-scale valley, every ditch and drystone accounted for — had just become a single small room in a house whose size she had never once imagined. Somewhere out in that unmapped black, something patient was walking toward the borders. Somewhere in her mother's quiet, careful, hearthstone-warm life there had been a contract, and a signature, and a price.

And the price, the stranger had said, was due tonight.

Freya Vane did the only sensible thing left to a cartographer whose map had just failed her. She put her arms under the thorn-marked stranger, heaved him over the threshold — he weighed nothing, which was somehow worse than if he'd weighed a great deal — dragged him inside, barred the door, and lit every candle she owned.

Then she went to the map chest in the corner, the one her mother had kept locked, the key to which had arrived on her doorstep four years ago with a note reading *when you're ready.*

Freya unlocked it for the first time in her life.

Inside, beneath a false bottom she'd never known existed, lay a ledger bound in gray leather, and the ledger was warm, and the final entry, in her mother's neat surveyor's hand, read:

*One soul. Paid in full — save the last installment. Thirteen turns. Forgive me, little wren.*

Outside, at the edge of the wards, something began to test the borders.
