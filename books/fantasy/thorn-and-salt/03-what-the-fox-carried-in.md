## 3. What the Fox Carried In

Freya had not slept. She doubted anyone in the valley had slept, and she suspected that if the stars ever came back, they would find the whole population standing in their doorways with candles guttering in their fists, blinking upward like a congregation of startled owls.

The stranger, however, had slept like the dead.

He lay on her hearth rug where she'd dragged him — she'd had to drag him; he weighed as much as a sack of wet turnips — and he snored with the deep, unbothered rhythm of a man who had not just watched the sky go out. His coat was still stitched all over with thorns, black brambles that seemed to have grown *into* the wool rather than through it, and every time Freya looked away and looked back, she could have sworn the thorns had shifted position slightly, like a dog rearranging itself in its sleep.

She had spent the night doing what she always did when the world misbehaved: she made tea, she made notes, and she made a list.

*Things that are true,* the list began.

1. The wards have fallen.
2. The stars have gone out.
3. There is a man on my rug covered in thorns who says something has come to collect.

She stared at item three for a long time, then added:

4. I did not invite him in. He arrived already inside. This is either very good manners or very bad ones.

Outside her window, the valley lay under a darkness that was not night. Night had texture — the blue-black of it, the silver edge of moonlight, the way familiar hills showed themselves in silhouette. This was a flat, total dark, like ink poured over the world, and it pressed against the glass of her window as though it were curious about what was inside.

Her mother's ledger sat closed on the table beside the cold lamp. She hadn't opened it again. She didn't need to. *One soul, paid in full.* The words had sunk into her like dye into cloth, and no amount of scrubbing would lift them out.

"Paid in full," she said aloud, to test how it sounded in daylight — except there was no daylight, so it sounded exactly as terrible as before.

On the rug, the stranger stopped snoring.

"You talk to yourself," he said, without opening his eyes. "That's a cartographer's habit, that is. My mother talked to her loom."

Freya's hand went to the poker. "You're awake."

"I've been awake since you wrote 'startled owls' on your list." He opened one eye. It was an unsettling eye, amber-gold, with a pupil that contracted to a slit when the firelight caught it. "Very rude, comparing a man to an owl before he's had breakfast."

"How did you read my list from across the room?"

"Good eyes." He sat up with a groan, joints crackling like green wood in a fire, and the thorns on his coat rustled and resettled. "Also, your handwriting is enormous. You write like someone shouting."

"I write like a *cartographer*," Freya said, with dignity. "Legibility is a virtue."

"It's a shout, is what it is." He rubbed his face, and she noticed for the first time that his hands were wrong — too long in the fingers, the nails dark and slightly curved, more claw than nail. "Now. Before we get into anything difficult, I want you to know that everything I'm about to tell you is true, and that I am deeply sorry about it, and that none of it is my fault except possibly the last part."

"That's a terrible way to begin a conversation."

"It's the only honest way." He stood, swaying slightly, and looked at her with both unsettling eyes now, and there was something in them — old sorrow, maybe, or old guilt — that made her grip on the poker loosen despite herself. "Freya Vane. Daughter of Maren Vane. Mapmaker of the warded valley. Do you know why the wards fell?"

"The wards failed because—" She stopped. Dagny's face rose in her memory, gray and stricken, the ward-keeper who had spent thirty years tending the border stones and who had wept openly in the square when they went dark. "Nobody knows why the wards fell. That's the whole point. They've held for two hundred years."

"They held because they were paid for," the stranger said gently. "And the payments stopped being enough."

Freya's mouth went dry. Her eyes flicked, traitorously, to the ledger on the table.

The stranger followed her gaze. "Ah," he said softly. "You've read it."

"I don't know what you're talking about."

"Your mother hid it in the map chest, third false bottom, beneath the survey of the northern ridge. Wrapped in oilcloth because she was afraid of damp, not because she was afraid of you finding it. She never thought you'd look. You were too busy drawing." He said it without cruelty, but each word landed like a stone dropped into still water. "Am I close?"

Freya set down the poker. Her hands were shaking, and she did not want to be holding a weapon while her hands shook; it felt like an admission. "Who *are* you?"

"My name is Bramblehoof." He bowed — actually bowed, one hand over his heart, the gesture so formal and so absurd in her small kitchen that she almost laughed, hysteria bubbling up like a spring. "I am a spirit of the hedge and the thorn-row, sworn and bound, and for the last thirteen years I have been the courier of the Court Beneath the Mountain."

"The Court Beneath the Mountain," Freya repeated flatly. "The court from the stories. The one Mother used to threaten me with. *Behave, or the folk under the hill will carry you off.*"

"She wasn't threatening you," Bramblehoof said. "She was warning you. There's a difference, and mothers know it even when children don't."

Something cold walked down Freya's spine. "Carried me off."

"Not you." He hesitated, and the thorns on his coat drew tight, creaking, like a man clenching his jaw. "Not your body. Your soul. Thirteen years ago, on the turn of the first moon of the bargain, Maren Vane came to the border stones at midnight and made a contract with the Pale King. Thirteen payments. Twelve of protection for the valley — its wards, its quiet, its freedom from the wild magic that would otherwise have eaten this place alive within a generation. And the thirteenth payment—"

"Don't." The word came out of her like a snapped branch.

"—was you," Bramblehoof finished, because apparently he was the sort of creature who finished sentences people begged him not to finish. "One soul, paid in full. Due on the thirteenth turn of the moon. Which, as of last night, is twelve turns gone and counting."

The kitchen was very quiet. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, in the flat dark, something howled — a long, rising note that did not sound like any wolf Freya had ever heard, and she had heard wolves, because she'd mapped their territory herself.

"That's impossible," she said. "That's — I'm *here*. I've been here my whole life. If my soul belonged to some king under a mountain, wouldn't I—"

"Wouldn't you feel it?" Bramblehoof tilted his head. "Aye. Most do, eventually. A hollowness. A sense of being watched by something patient. Dreams of a door underground." He watched her face carefully as he said it, and Freya realized, with a lurch, that she had dreamed of a door underground her whole life — a low stone archway, cold air breathing out of it, and a voice saying *soon, soon, soon* — and she had never told anyone, because who admits to dreams like that?

"Oh," she said. Very quietly. And then she sat down, because her legs had decided the matter without consulting her.

Bramblehoof did not come closer. She noted that, distantly, the way she noted contour lines. He stayed by the hearth, hands open at his sides, and let her sit in it.

"I carried the contract," he said, after a while. "That's what I mean about sorry, and about fault. The Pale King writes his bargains on vellum made of night, and they must be delivered by a bound spirit, hand to hand, at the moment of signing. I brought it to your mother. I stood at the border stones and I watched her sign, and I watched her cry, and I carried the contract back down under the mountain, and every year since, on the turn of each moon, I've carried the tally-mark back to be renewed." His voice roughened. "Twelve marks, Freya Vane. I carried twelve of them. Last night the wards broke because the King has decided to stop waiting politely. The thirteenth turn comes in twenty-eight days, and he means to collect in person if he must."

"Why are you telling me this?" Freya asked. Her voice sounded strange to her own ears — scraped hollow, like a gully after floodwater. "If you're his courier, why warn me? Why not just — collect?"

For a long moment, Bramblehoof didn't answer. The thorns on his coat shifted, writhed, and then — she saw it clearly this time, no trick of the light — they *tightened*, biting down into the wool and whatever was beneath the wool, and he flinched, and steadied himself, and when he spoke again his voice was strained.

"Because I am bound to deliver you," he said, "and I have begun to hate my binding. Because your mother, at the border stones, thirteen years ago, looked at me — at *me*, a thing of thorns and errands — and said, *someone must remember her kindly, even if it can't be me.* And because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because last night, when the sky went out, I ran. Not toward the mountain. Toward your door. And I don't entirely know why, and that frightens me more than the thorns do."

Freya looked at him — this strange, thorn-stitched creature with his owl insults and his formal bow and his slit-pupil eyes full of old guilt — and she felt something shift in her chest, the way terrain shifts before a landslide. Disbelief was still there, yes. But disbelief, she knew from years of correcting bad maps, was often just the friction of a mind being asked to redraw itself.

She thought of her mother. Steady, practical Maren Vane, who measured twice and cut once, who had never once in her life done anything dramatic — except, apparently, once, at midnight, at the border stones, and it had bought Freya her whole childhood. Every ward-hummed summer afternoon. Every safe, mapped, ordinary day.

*Paid in full.*

"No," Freya said.

Bramblehoof blinked. "No?"

"No, I don't accept it." She stood up. Her legs held, this time. She crossed to the map chest, lifted out the ledger, and set it on the table between them like evidence at a trial. "I don't accept that the answer to this is to walk to a mountain and hand myself over. My mother paid twelve years to keep this valley safe, and I am not going to spend the thirteenth making her payment worthless. If there's a contract, contracts have terms. Terms have loopholes. Loopholes have *margins*—" she tapped the ledger, hard, "—and margins, Master Bramblehoof, are where cartographers live."

The fox-spirit stared at her. Then, slowly, wonderfully, he grinned — and the grin showed rather too many teeth, sharp and white, and for the first time she understood that under the man-shape there was very much a fox.

"Well," he said. "Well, well, well. Maren Vane's daughter, right enough."

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"It means the King expects a frightened girl to come crawling to his gate, ready to trade her soul for her neighbors' safety, because that's the shape of every bargain he's won for a thousand years." Bramblehoof's ears — and she was nearly certain those were *ears* moving under his hair now — pricked forward with unmistakable delight. "He does not expect a mapmaker with a grudge and a ledger. Oh, he is going to *hate* you."

"Good," said Freya, and found, to her surprise, that she meant it.

Then the grin faded, and he grew serious, and the thorns creaked. "Understand what you're choosing. The road to the mountain runs through country that hasn't been mapped since the world was young — country that *won't* hold still to be mapped. The King's Hollow Men are already abroad in it. And you'll need help you cannot buy, because the valley's coin is worthless past the wards." He paused. "There's a salt-witch lives at the western edge. Exiled. Knows the wild roads better than any living soul, and owes the court old debts she'd rather forget. And there's your ward-keeper — the big fellow who cried in the square? He'll want to come. Men like that always do. Shame makes the best traveling companion and the worst dinner guest."

Freya was already pulling paper toward her. Old habit, new purpose. At the top of a fresh sheet she wrote, in her enormous, shouting handwriting:

*Things that are true.*

1. My soul is owed.
2. Debts have terms.
3. Terms have margins.

She looked at the list for a moment, then added a fourth line:

4. We leave at first light. If first light can be persuaded to exist.

"Bramblehoof," she said, without looking up, "how fast can a fox run?"

"Faster than trouble," he said. "But not faster than regret. Why?"

"Because in twenty-eight days I intend to be standing in a king's hall with a counteroffer," Freya Vane said, "and I intend to arrive *early*."

Outside, in the starless dark, something howled again — closer now — and far beneath the valley, beneath the roots of the mountains, something ancient turned over in its sleep and began, patiently, to count.
