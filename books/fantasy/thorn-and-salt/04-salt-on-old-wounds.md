## 4. Salt on Old Wounds

The salt told Sef before the sky did.

She had lived at the valley's edge for eleven years, long enough to know that salt kept its own kind of weather. It sat in her windowsills and her door-cracks and the little clay bowls she set on every shelf like a house full of watching eyes, and when the world was well, it lay quiet as sleeping hens. That evening, all of it — every grain in every bowl from her kitchen to her drying shed — stood straight up on end, quivering, like a dog that has heard something coming up the road that no one else can hear yet.

"Well," Sef said to the salt, "that's not good."

The salt did not answer, which was itself an answer. Salt only spoke in emergencies, and it had never once spoken about anything pleasant.

She went outside. The wards were still humming — she could feel them the way you feel a neighbor's music through a wall, that low caged-bee drone the whole valley took for granted, so constant most folk had forgotten it was there at all. Sef had never forgotten. You didn't forget a sound like that, not if you'd grown up where she'd grown up, where the silence behind the sound was the thing that got you.

Then the twelfth bell of midnight rang out of the valley's heart, and before the thirteenth could follow it, the humming stopped.

Not faded. Stopped. The way a heart stops.

Sef stood very still in her doorway with her hands full of nothing and listened to the shape of the silence, and the silence had a shape, oh yes, it had teeth and a long reach and a smell like wet stone deep underground, and every hair on her arms rose to meet it.

And then the stars went out.

All of them. Not clouded, not dimmed — *out*, snuffed between one blink and the next, so that the sky over the valley became a black lid pressing down, and the mountains lost their edges, and Sef felt eleven years of careful, deliberate exile tilt beneath her feet like a boat hitting open water.

"Twelve payments," she said quietly. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "He always said thirteen."

* * *

Her name wasn't Sef, originally. But that was a story with thorns on both ends, and she had put it down a long time ago along with her court silks and her true name and the version of herself that could walk into the Pale King's hall without flinching. Out here she was Sef the salt-witch, who sold charms against rheumatism and sour milk and unfaithful husbands, who was prickly and plain and gloriously, deliberately unremarkable. She had built the unremarkableness grain by grain, the way she built everything, and she had thought — she had genuinely believed — that it would hold forever.

She spent the rest of that night doing what practical people do while the world ends: work. She packed. Salt in waxed paper twists, iron nails, dried nettle, the good knife, the other knife she kept hidden inside the false bottom of her flour barrel because a woman who hid only one knife deserved whatever happened to her. She fed the goat. She looked at the goat for a long moment, weighing eleven years of companionship against the fact that goats cannot travel fast and complain even faster, and then she turned the goat loose toward old Marta's pasture with a slap on its rump.

"Don't look at me like that. You'll be fat and spoiled by winter."

The goat looked at her exactly the same way. Goats are honest that way.

It was full morning, grey and starless-feeling even though the sun was up, when the knock came at her door. Three knocks, hesitant, the knock of someone who had walked a long way on bad ground.

Sef opened the door on Freya Vane — the cartographer's girl, all elbows and stubbornness, with shadows under her eyes deep enough to plant crops in — and behind Freya, looking like a man being dragged to his own funeral by his own conscience, Dagny the ward-keeper, or former ward-keeper, or whatever you called a man whose wards had just died in his hands.

"You know," Sef said, leaning on the doorframe, "most people bring bread when they come calling."

"Your salt's standing up," Freya said. She wasn't looking at Sef; she was looking past her, into the house, at the bowls on the shelves, every grain at attention. "All of it. Mine too. And my mother's ledger says—"

"I know what your mother's ledger says." It came out harder than Sef intended, and she watched the girl flinch, and hated herself for it, and hardened further to cover it, which is the oldest trick in the coward's book and she played it fluently. "I know more than I've said for a long time. Come in before the whole valley sees you standing at the exiled witch's door and starts asking questions nobody wants answered."

They came in. Dagny ducked under her lintel like a guilty bear and stood there holding his hat, which told her more than any speech. Ward-keepers don't hold their hats. Ward-keepers hold their staves, their keys, their dignity. A hat held in both hands is a man asking to be scolded.

"The wards fell," Dagny said. "I need you to know they fell and it wasn't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I don't know what it was. That's the truth. They were sound. I checked them at sundown, same as every night for thirty years, and they were *sound*, and then at midnight they were gone like they'd never been."

"Something came through them," Freya said. "A fox. Or a man. Both. It collapsed at my door and it knew my name and it said—" Her jaw tightened. "It said my soul was sold. To the Court Beneath the Mountain. By my mother. And that the bill comes due on the thirteenth turn of the moon."

The room was quiet. The salt in the bowls shivered, though there was no wind.

"And you want me," Sef said slowly, "to walk you out of this valley. Past dead wards, into wild country, toward the court of the Pale King himself. With him." She nodded at Dagny. "Whose entire profession just died on his watch."

"That's about the shape of it," Dagny said miserably.

"No," Sef said.

Freya blinked. "But—"

"No. Absolutely not. Do you know what's out there? Do either of you? You've lived your whole lives inside a ward-ring. You think the world is *maps*. Pretty lines and honest distances. Out there the distances lie, girl. Out there rivers run uphill because they're sulking, and forests rearrange themselves out of spite, and half the things you'll meet will offer to help you and mean the exact opposite of help." She jabbed a finger at the shuttered window, beyond which the starless night had left a bruise-colored morning. "And beyond all of that sits *him*, and he does not let go of what he's owed. Ever. I'm not walking back into that man's reach for anyone. I did my time at the edges of his world. Eleven years I have been nobody, and nobody is the safest thing a person can be."

Freya set the ledger on the table.

Sef had seen that ledger before — had held it, in another life, in another name — but she made her face do nothing at all, which cost her more than the walk to the mountain would cost her knees. Freya opened it to the last page and turned it around.

*One soul,* it read. *Paid in full.*

"She bought the valley twelve years of protection with twelve payments," Freya said. "I found the others. Small ones, at first. Years off her own life. Then worse. And the last entry — the last thing my mother ever wrote — is me." Her voice cracked and she dragged it level again by sheer cartographer's stubbornness, the way she'd probably drag a line level across bad terrain. "I'm not asking you to save me, Sef. I'm asking you to point. You know what's out there. I don't. And I am going whether you come or not, and I would rather go knowing which way the rivers sulk."

There it was. There was the thing Sef had been afraid of since the salt stood up: not the danger, not the King, not the Hollow Men she could already smell gathering beyond the fells like storm-crows. This. A young woman standing in her kitchen holding her mother's debts out flat, saying *point*, when Sef knew exactly where the roads went because she had helped build three of them.

Because here was the truth she intended to carry to her grave, or failing that, to the foot of the mountain: she had been there. Not nearby. *There.* In the hall beneath the geode, under caged stars, the night a tired woman from a warded valley knelt on black glass and bargained away pieces of herself one by one. Sef had stood at the King's shoulder in those days, wearing her first name and her second face, and she had watched the bargaining with the professional detachment the court required, and she had done nothing, said nothing, because that was the job and she was very good at the job.

She remembered the woman's hands. That was the detail that had followed her into exile and slept beside her for eleven years: the woman's hands, cracked from farm work, pressed flat on the glass floor, steady as a mapmaker's, saying *not my daughter. Whatever else. Not my daughter.*

And now the daughter was standing in Sef's kitchen asking for directions to hell.

"Fine," Sef said, because her mouth was a traitor and always had been. She was already reaching for the waxed-paper twists of salt. "Fine. But we do this my way. Nobody touches anything glowing. Nobody eats anything offered by anyone prettier than they are. And if I say run, you run, and if I say hide, you hide, and if I tell you to forget my name and walk the other way — which will happen, mark me, it will happen — then by every saint the salt ever kept, you *do it*."

Dagny let out a breath that seemed to empty him entirely. "Thank you," he said.

"Don't thank me. Thanking implies I'm doing it for your benefit." She shoved a pack against his chest hard enough to stagger him. "You get to carry the tent."

* * *

They left at dusk, because Sef refused to leave at dawn — dawn was when watchers watched, and whatever else the fallen wards had unleashed, someone or something had been waiting for them to fall, and patience favored the ones who moved at supper-time.

At the ward-line, Sef stopped them all with a raised hand.

"What is it?" Freya whispered.

"Hush." Sef crouched and poured a thin thread of salt from her palm onto the packed earth, and waited, and the thread lay flat and quiet, and she let out a slow breath. "Nothing following us yet. Remember that feeling. It doesn't last."

She stepped over the place where the border used to be, and it was like stepping out of a warm house into deep winter — except the cold was underneath everything, in the ground, in the light, in the way the trees stood. The wild country spread out before them, hills rolling dark and unfamiliar under a sky with no stars, and Sef felt the old pull of it, the vast hungry gorgeous wrongness of it, and eleven years of careful smallness cracked down the middle.

Beside her, Freya Vane pulled out a notebook and began, unbelievably, to sketch the horizon.

"What are you doing?" Sef demanded.

"Mapping," Freya said. "Nobody's ever mapped this. Somebody should."

And Sef, salt-witch, exile, keeper of one very large secret, looked at the girl whose mother's debt she had witnessed and whose soul she was now responsible for delivering alive, and felt the guilt settle into its usual disguise — gruffness, practicality, complaints about the weight of the tent — and heard herself say:

"Put down that the hill to the northeast is lying about its height. It does that."

Freya wrote it down without question. Dagny adjusted the tent straps. Somewhere far behind them, in the direction of the valley, something began to sing — a thin, sweet, hollow sound, like a child humming in an empty house, and it was moving, and it was keeping pace.

Sef did not turn around. Turning around was how it learned your face.

"Walk faster," she said. "And whatever you do, don't hum along."
