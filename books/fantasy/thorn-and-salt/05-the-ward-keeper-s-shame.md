## 5. The Ward-Keeper's Shame

Dagny Holt had not slept in three days, and the valley knew it.

They said it kindly, mostly. *You should rest, Dagny. You did all you could, Dagny.* The baker left a loaf on his doorstep without a note, which was somehow worse than a note. The miller's wife patted his arm in the lane as though he were an invalid, or worse, a mourner. And the children — the children had stopped asking him to make the ward-lights dance for them at dusk, which was the cruelest thing of all, because it meant they understood something had broken that he could not fix, and they were being polite about it.

Politeness. He'd spent forty years earning the valley's trust and one night losing it to politeness.

He sat at his kitchen table with the ward-keeper's tools spread out before him like a surgeon's instruments after a death: the tuning fork of cold iron, the spool of silvered thread gone dull and gray, the little brass compass that had always pointed toward the nearest standing stone and now spun lazily, drunk, pointing at nothing at all. Every hour he tried the fork against the nearest anchor-stone's resonance and every hour he got back silence — not the hum of caged bees that had sung along the valley's borders since before his grandfather's grandfather, but true silence, the kind that lived in cellars and closed coffins.

Three days of trying. Three days of nothing.

And one memory he could not stop chewing, the way a man chews a gristle he cannot swallow.

* * *

The night the wards fell, Dagny had been asleep, which was already a shame in itself. A ward-keeper was supposed to sleep with one ear open, the way shepherds do, and for thirty years he had. But it was the twelfth turn of the moon, the night before the thirteenth, and there is a peculiar drowsiness that settles over a man when everything has been fine for thirty years. He had banked the fire. He had wound the clock. He had checked the resonance-glass on his windowsill — a small green vial that fogged when the wards strained — and it had been clear as spring water.

Then he woke to a sound like the world clearing its throat.

Not thunder. Thunder comes from above and rolls away. This came from below, from everywhere, from inside his own teeth. He was out of bed and into his boots and running down the lane before his mind caught up with his feet, and what he saw made him stop so hard he nearly went down on his knees in the mud.

The stars were going out.

Not clouds. Not mist. Going out, one by one, west to east, like candles snuffed by a passing hand — and where each star vanished there was a hole in the sky that was not black but *absent*, a place where darkness had been removed rather than laid down. He had studied wards his whole life. He had never once been taught what it means when the stars themselves are part of the architecture. Nobody had, because nobody had imagined the sky could be tampered with.

He reached the first anchor-stone by the millpond at half past midnight. The stone stood nine feet tall, older than the valley's name, carved all over with spirals that his grandmother had taught him to trace before he could write his own letters. Its song was gone. When he pressed his palm flat to it, he felt not deadness but *withdrawal* — the way a hand goes numb when someone else is holding it too tight.

And then, the memory. The one he kept chewing.

Because as he knelt there in the mud with his hand on the dying stone, he heard footsteps behind him. Light ones. Quick ones. Someone crossing the millpond path at midnight on the worst night in the valley's history, and when he turned, he saw only a shape — a shape in a long coat with the hood up, hurrying away toward the elder's row of houses, moving fast and light and, he would swear this on any stone you liked, moving *unafraid*. Everyone else in the valley that night came stumbling out of their doors in nightclothes, white-faced, shouting. Everyone except one person, who walked away from the falling wards as calmly as a woman leaving market.

Dagny had called out. The figure hadn't run. Hadn't looked back. Just raised one hand — a wave? a warning? — and slipped between the houses, and by the time he'd thought to follow, there were more urgent things: the second anchor-stone cracking down its face like an egg, the humming dying, the dark pressing in.

He told no one about the figure. That was the second shame, stacked neatly on top of the first. Because when he described what he'd seen, even to himself, it sounded like an accusation, and accusations require names, and names require courage, and Dagny had discovered at fifty-three years old that he had less courage than he'd always pretended. So instead he did what he always did with things too big for him: he worked. He tuned and tested and re-tuned, and the valley watched him fail politely, and the loaf appeared on his doorstep, and the children stopped asking for dancing lights.

On the third morning, Freya Vane came to his door.

* * *

She stood on his step looking like a woman who had slept even less than he had, which given recent events was saying something. Behind her, at the gate, waited two figures he didn't know — a broad, weathered woman with salt-gray hair braided like rope, and a slight young man in a patched coat who stood with his weight oddly forward, the way foxes stand. Dagny noticed the fox-ness of him before he noticed anything else, because a ward-keeper learns to read the posture of things, and that young man's posture was *wrong* in a way his eyes refused to explain.

"Master Holt," Freya said. "I need your help."

"Freya." He stepped aside to let her in, because that was manners, and manners were the last scaffolding holding some of the valley upright. "Come in, come in. There's tea if the kettle's cooperating."

"It isn't," she said, and then, glancing at the hearth where the fire sat sullen and smokeless, "nothing's cooperating, is it. Not since that night."

"No," Dagny said quietly. "It isn't."

Her mother had been Marta Vane. Marta Vane had brought him honey-cakes on the anniversary of his wife's death for eleven years running, and had argued with him cheerfully about the correct tension for ward-thread as though it were religion, and had died two winters ago with her ledger-books in perfect order and her daughter left alone in a house full of maps. Looking at Freya was like looking at a door he'd failed to knock on often enough.

"Freya," he said again, gentler, "if this is about the wards — I know what people are saying. That I let them lapse. That I got old and careless—"

"I don't care what people are saying." She set a folded paper on his table, and he saw it was a map — of course it was, everything Freya Vane touched turned into a map eventually — marked in her fine, stubborn hand. "I'm leaving the valley. Tonight. Through the gap where the north stone fell. And I need someone who knows ward-craft, because everything past the border is unmapped and warded against us in ways we can't guess."

Dagny stared at her. Then at the map. Then through his window at the gray, starless noon sky, which had begun to feel less like weather and more like a lid.

"Leaving," he repeated. "Out there." He gestured vaguely at the whole of creation, which currently resembled a dropped inkpot. "Child, the wilds haven't been crossed in living memory. The geography doesn't hold still out there. Rivers walk. Hills migrate."

"I know," Freya said. "That's why I'm bringing a cartographer."

Despite everything — the dead stones, the empty sky, the shape in the long coat that haunted the inside of his eyelids — Dagny felt something in his chest that took him a moment to identify. It was laughter. Or the ghost of it, knocking politely.

"And who are these?" he asked, nodding at the window. The broad woman was arguing with the gate latch and winning; the fox-postured youth was examining Dagny's herb garden with intense personal interest, sniffing at the rosemary.

"Sef, who knows the wilds better than anyone alive. And Bramblehoof, who—" Freya paused, and he watched her decide how much truth she could afford. "Who knows things. About my mother. About why the wards fell. About what's coming for me on the thirteenth turn."

Dagny sat down slowly. His knees had opinions these days.

"The thirteenth turn," he said. "Tomorrow night."

"Tomorrow night," Freya agreed. Her voice didn't shake, but he saw the effort it cost, the way you see the effort of someone carrying water across ice. "Whatever was bought with those wards — whatever my mother paid — it's being collected. And I mean to be there when it is. Not waiting for it. *There.*"

* * *

Here is the thing about shame: it is patient, and it is practical.

Dagny had spent three days rehearsing speeches of apology to a valley that wasn't listening. He had composed, in the small hours, magnificent self-flagellations, elegant confessions, dignified resignations from a post that no longer existed because the thing it guarded was gone. And now a young woman stood in his kitchen asking not for apologies but for work — actual, concrete, necessary work — and every rehearsed word evaporated like breath on glass.

What remained was simpler. What remained was: *a ward-keeper keeps.*

"You'll have me," he said. It came out rougher than he intended. He cleared his throat and tried again. "I mean — yes. Of course. If you'll have a half-dead old man whose entire life's work collapsed in one night."

"It didn't collapse," Sef said from the doorway. She had let herself in the way storms do, without invitation and with total confidence. She looked around the kitchen with a professional's eye — at the tools on the table, the tuning fork, the dead compass — and something flickered across her face that Dagny couldn't read and didn't like. "Wards that fall that clean don't collapse. They're *opened*. There's a difference, and your hands know it even if your mouth doesn't."

Dagny went very still. "Opened," he said.

"Mm." Sef picked up the tuning fork, weighed it, put it down exactly where it had been. "Struck from outside. Or unstruck from within. Either way, somebody wanted that border open, and whoever it was knew your stones well enough to pick the night you'd be asleep." She met his eyes, and hers were pale and flat as a winter sea. "Unless you weren't asleep. Unless you opened it yourself. Did you?"

The question landed like a slap, and Dagny understood, distantly, that it was meant to — that this woman tested everyone she met the way you test ice, hard and early, before trusting it with weight.

"No," he said. "I did not. And I've spent three days wondering who did. Because I *saw* someone."

The kitchen went quiet enough to hear the kettle sulking.

Freya turned. "You what?"

"That night. At the millpond stone. While it was failing, someone crossed the path behind me. Hooded coat. Heading for the elder's row." He heard his own voice steadying as he finally, finally said the words aloud, three days late, and hated himself for the lateness even as relief loosened something in his ribs. "Everyone in this valley ran *toward* the trouble that night. Shouting, panicking, good people doing foolish brave things. One person walked *away* from it calm as Sunday. I didn't follow. I didn't call after them properly. I told no one, because—" He stopped. Started again. "Because naming it would have meant believing my neighbors capable of it, and I wasn't ready to believe that. That failure is mine, and I'll carry it. But if there's a chance to carry something useful instead—"

"There is," Freya said simply.

Bramblehoof drifted in through the doorway — drifted, there was no other word for it, though his boots made ordinary sounds on the floorboards — and looked Dagny up and down with amber eyes that had seen considerably more centuries than the face suggested. "A ward-keeper," the fox spirit said thoughtfully. "How quaint. How *useful*. Do you know, in four hundred years I've eaten perhaps six ward-keepers, and every one of them tasted of guilt."

"Bramblehoof," Freya said warningly.

"What? It's a compliment. Guilt is *nutritious*." The fox grinned, showing teeth a shade too many. "Very well, keeper of dead stones. Here is what I know, for free, which from me is extravagance: the Court Beneath the Mountain does not leave doors open out of generosity. Someone paid for that opening. And payments to that court are never simple, and never end where you think they end. Your neighbor with the calm walk may owe more than you imagine. Or less. Or may *be* owed something, which is worse." He hopped lightly onto Dagny's bench, uninvited, and peered at the dead compass. "You'll want this fixed, incidentally. Can't navigate a lying country without honest instruments."

"I know how to fix a compass," Dagny said stiffly.

"Not this one you don't. It's not broken. It's *grieving*." Bramblehoof tapped it once with a claw, and to Dagny's astonishment the needle shuddered, swung, and settled — pointing north-northeast, steady as a heartbeat, toward the mountains. Toward the court. "There. Now it points at what matters. Try not to follow it blindly; that's advice I give everyone and take myself, historically, with poor results."

* * *

They left at dusk, because Sef said the wilds were kinder to travelers in the dark and Dagny chose not to examine what that implied about the wilds' character.

At the border — at the place where the north anchor-stone had cracked like an egg and the humming had died — Dagny stopped and put his hand on the ruined granite one last time. The others walked ahead into the grass beyond, into country his maps had never troubled to describe because no one had ever needed it described. He stayed a moment alone.

"Thirty years," he told the stone. "I kept you singing for thirty years, and I missed the one night it mattered." The stone said nothing, because it was stone, but beneath his palm he felt — or imagined he felt, and decided he could live with *imagined*, since imagination was all the valley had left — the faintest ghost of a hum. Like a sleeper stirring. Like something not dead, only waiting.

He would rebuild them. Whatever it cost, however far he had to walk, he would learn what had opened his stones and he would close it again, and he would stand in the elder's square one day and say the name he hadn't been brave enough to say, and the valley would sing again. That was not forgiveness. Forgiveness might never be on offer. But it was duty, and duty was a road he knew how to walk in the dark.

"Dagny!" Freya's voice, ahead, edged with alarm. "Sef says the ground here — the ground is *moving*—"

He hurried forward, tuning fork in hand, heart hammering in a way that felt, for the first time in three days, almost like purpose. Beyond the fallen stone the hills rolled gently in the starless gloom — and then rolled again, a slow tidal swell, grass whispering as the land itself shifted like a dog settling into a new position.

"Well," said Dagny Holt, gripping his fork like a sword, "in thirty years of ward-keeping, no one ever mentioned the scenery wanders."

And somewhere behind them, faint but unmistakable, the cracked anchor-stone began — very softly — to hum.
