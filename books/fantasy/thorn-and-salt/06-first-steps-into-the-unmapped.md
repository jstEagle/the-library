## 6. First Steps Into the Unmapped

The border stone was smaller than Freya had imagined it would be.

She had drawn it a hundred times — the ward-marker at the valley's western throat, a squat granite finger worn smooth by centuries of rain and the nervous touch of children daring each other to cross. In her maps she always gave it dignity: a bold triangle, *BORDER STONE*, in her neatest capitals. Standing before the real thing at dawn, with the wards dead behind them and the wilds yawning ahead, it looked like nothing so much as an old man's tooth left standing in a field of gorse.

"Well," said Sef, adjusting the salt-iron kettle on her back. "It's not going to get less terrifying if we stare at it."

"I'm not staring because I'm terrified," Freya said. "I'm staring because I'm memorizing it."

"You're staring because you're terrified *and* memorizing it. I can do two things at once; so can you."

Behind them, Dagny made his third circuit of the border stone, running his palms along its base where the ward-grooves ran. He had done this at every marker they'd passed since leaving the village — touching each one like a man visiting a sick relative he couldn't cure. His face had the particular grayness of someone who had spent four days not apologizing and dying of it.

"The grooves are empty," he said, for the third time. "No residue. No scorch. Whatever unmade them didn't fight the wards. It *unwrote* them."

"Cheerful," said Bramblehoof.

The fox spirit sat on the border stone itself, which Freya suspected was deliberate blasphemy, his thorn-scars catching the early light. He looked, as ever, like something a hedge had dreamed after eating a bad berry — russet and black and bristling, with eyes too old for the face around them. A leather satchel hung at his side that had not existed yesterday and which no one commented on, because commenting on Bramblehoof's satchel felt like asking a river why it was wet.

"Last chance to turn back," Dagny said quietly. Not to all of them. To Freya.

Freya looked back down the valley. Smoke rose from the village chimneys, thin and ordinary and heartbreakingly small. Somewhere down there was her map chest, her mother's ledger with its terrible arithmetic, the whole known world folded into nine miles of river and field. She had spent twenty-six years mapping every inch of it, and it had never once occurred to her that the map had edges because the *world* did.

"No," she said. "Something came to collect me. I'd rather meet it walking than waiting."

"Spoken like your mother," Sef muttered, and then went very still, the way she always did when she remembered she wasn't supposed to know things about Freya's mother. She covered it by marching through the gorse, swearing with impressive variety when it stabbed her.

Freya took out her journal, opened to a fresh page, and wrote at the top, in careful letters: *Beyond the Border Stone. Day One.*

Then she stepped over the line.

* * *

For the first hour, the wilds were almost disappointing.

There were hills. There was scrub pine and heather and a stream that babbled in a perfectly respectable direction. Freya walked with her compass out and her pencil moving, and everything behaved exactly as geography ought: the stream fed a tarn, the tarn drained north, the hills rose in orderly succession toward a gray rampart of mountains that even now she could have rendered blindfolded.

"This is it?" Dagny asked. "This is the terrifying unmapped wilds?"

"Don't insult it," Freya said, without looking up from her page. "It's listening."

"It's *what*?"

"New country listens." She said it before she knew she knew it — some instinct inherited from a mother who had mapped more than valleys, apparently. "Old country is settled. It's made up its mind about where things go. New country is still deciding. You want to be polite while it decides whether you're a feature or a mistake."

Sef snorted. "That's superstition."

"That's *cartography*," Freya said, with more confidence than she felt.

Bramblehoof laughed — a sound like dry leaves being strangled — and trotted ahead. "Oh, she'll do. The last soul I carried to the mountain wept for three days first."

"You've carried other souls?" Dagny demanded.

"I've carried *parcels*. The parcels happened to be souls. It's a courier position, not a moral one." He flicked his tail. "You're all very judgmental for people walking into a place that eats roads."

And that was how Freya learned what he meant, because at midday they stopped to eat beside the stream they'd been following, and when they rose and walked on, the stream was gone.

Not dried up. Not diverted. *Gone*, along with the tarn it had fed and the hollow the tarn had sat in, replaced by a stand of birches that had definitely not been there an hour ago, their leaves turning already though it was high summer.

Freya stood very still. Her compass needle swung once, lazily, like a cat deciding whether to bother getting up, and then pointed — firmly, smugly — southwest. It had pointed northeast all morning.

"Everyone stop walking," she said.

"We've stopped," said Sef. "We stopped when the trees changed. What I'd like to know is—"

"Nobody move their feet. Dagny, mark where you're standing. Scratch it into the dirt. Sef, drop salt — a little circle, don't be stingy. Bramblehoof..."

"I know," the fox said, and was suddenly not a fox but the thorn-marked stranger again, tall and wrong-jointed, crouched with his palm flat against the earth. "It's breathing. Feel that? The ground's thinking about us."

Freya knelt beside him and pressed her own hand to the soil. She felt nothing — or rather, she felt everything slightly *out of order*, the way a word looks wrong when you stare at it too long. The earth under her palm was earth. But it was also, faintly, the memory of a streambed, and beneath that, the suggestion of a hill that hadn't arrived yet.

"It's not moving," she breathed. "It's *drafting*. This is rough country — unfinished. The features haven't been inked in yet, so they drift until something fixes them." Her mind was racing now, exhilaration rising in her chest like a swallowed spark. "A river stays put once it's been named. Once it's been *measured*. Nothing here has ever been measured. We're walking through a first draft."

"That," said Sef, "is either the most useful thing anyone's said all day or complete nonsense."

"It's both," said Bramblehoof approvingly. "That's wilds-cartography. Why do you think no one's ever mapped this place? Every survey party that comes in gets redrawn. You can't chart a country that charts you back."

Freya looked down at her journal — at the neat morning's work, the well-behaved stream, the tidy hills. Useless. Already fiction. And instead of despairing, she felt something she hadn't let herself feel since the stars went out: *delight*. Terrible, inappropriate delight. Here was a world with no edges. Here was everything her whole life had trained her for and nothing she had ever imagined.

"Right," she said, standing, brushing off her knees. "New rules. Nobody goes more than ten paces from the salt lines. We fix our camp every night — names, measurements, marks. If we measure it, it stays. That's our anchor."

"And if we can't measure fast enough?" Dagny asked.

"Then we sleep somewhere the country hasn't finished deciding about, and we wake up somewhere else." She grinned, and meant it. "Which, frankly, is faster travel than walking."

* * *

By the third day, the exhilaration had curdled exactly as promised, and Freya hated herself a little for ever having enjoyed it.

The trouble was water. On day two they'd fixed a spring with a salt ring and Freya's best triangulation, and it had held beautifully — right up until the afternoon of day three, when they returned to it and found the spring exactly where they'd left it, but flowing *upward*, a silver rope of water climbing out of its pool and vanishing into the sky like smoke that had gotten ambitious.

"That's not natural," Dagny said, stating the obvious with the flat authority of a man who had recently lost his grip on what natural meant.

"Nothing here is," Freya said. Her voice sounded fine. Steady. Inside, something was doing slow, cold somersaults. "But it's *consistent*. Look — the reversal follows the fault line. Water runs against the grain of the rock here. It's not chaos. It's just rules we haven't read yet."

"And if we misread them?"

"Then we get very thirsty."

They were, in fact, getting very thirsty. Because the second problem — worse than uphill rivers, worse than compasses with opinions — was that the country had started responding to *them*.

It began subtly. Paths appeared when they needed paths, which should have been a mercy except that the paths sometimes led confidently toward places Freya had specifically decided to avoid, as though the land had skimmed her notes and misunderstood the assignment. Then the landmarks began echoing. They passed a lightning-split oak at noon; at dusk, having walked in a straight line the entire afternoon, they passed it again — same tree, same split, but now with a crow's nest that hadn't been there before, and the crow watching them with what Freya could only describe as professional interest.

"It's copying us," she said that night, hunched over her journal by firelight, surrounded by pages that contradicted each other. "Or testing us. We walk a loop, it gives us a loop. We look for water, it makes water — but it makes it *wrong*, uphill, because it doesn't understand why we want it. It's learning from what we do, not what we mean."

"Like a child," Sef said.

"Like a *draftsman*," Freya corrected. "Copying the master's hand without understanding the letters." She turned a page and stopped. "Sef. When you traded that memory at the market—" She caught herself. "Never mind."

Sef's face did something complicated. "Ask it anyway."

"Did the King's agents talk about this place? About how to cross it?"

"They talked about how to *avoid* it. There's a road. The Thornway. It holds still because the court holds it still — paid for, anchored, warded. Everyone going to the mountain takes it." Sef poked the fire hard enough to make it complain. "Everyone sensible."

"But?"

"But the Thornway has tollgates. And everyone who walks it arrives at the mountain *expected*." She met Freya's eyes across the flames. "I didn't take it when I left the court. I walked out here, through the draft-country, and got lost for a season, and nearly died eleven times, and it was worth it, because no one knew where I was. Ever. That's the price of being unfindable, girl. You have to actually be lost."

Freya looked at her map pages — the drifting streams, the duplicated oaks, the compass readings that disagreed with each other like siblings. Unfindable. The word landed strangely. All her life she had wanted the opposite: to be *on* the map, to matter enough to be recorded. Now the safest thing she could be was a smudge, an error, a place the country hadn't finished deciding about.

"We stay off the Thornway," she said finally. "We cross the draft-country. But we do it my way."

"Which is?"

She held up her journal. "We become the map. Every night, we fix what we've crossed — name it, measure it, mark it. Not for navigation." She tapped the page. "For *leverage*. If the country copies what we do, then what we do becomes the country. Every mile we anchor is a mile that stays anchored behind us. We're not just crossing the wilds, we're *finishing* them. Leaving a trail of settled ground straight to the mountain's door."

Silence around the fire. Then Bramblehoof began to laugh again, that strangled-leaves sound, rolling onto his back with all four feet in the air like an enormous, blasphemous dog.

"Oh, that's *vicious*," he said delightedly. "Do you understand what you're proposing? You're going to colonize the wilds with *homework*. The Pale King has spent nine hundred years keeping this place unmapped because unmapped means undefendable-by-anyone-but-him, and you're going to stroll through it inking it in as you go—"

"I'm going to *try*," Freya said. "I've never mapped anything that pushed back."

"Neither has anyone. That's rather the point." The fox rolled upright, and for a moment his grin looked less like mischief and more like hope, which frightened her more than the uphill river had. "Your mother would have loved you."

The words dropped into the fire-warm circle like a stone into still water. Sef went rigid. Dagny looked up sharply. And Freya felt the evening tilt.

"You knew my mother," she said. Not a question. She was tired of questions. "You carried her contract. You've compared me to her twice now. So you knew her."

"I knew her *ledger*," Bramblehoof said, and his grin was gone. "Couriers know ledgers better than faces. Faces lie. Entries don't." He stood, shook himself, thorn-scars glinting. "Sleep. Tomorrow we reach the ridge above the Thornway, and you'll want your strength, because from up there you'll see something that will make the uphill river look like a rounding error."

"What will we see?"

He paused at the edge of the firelight, and the darkness beyond him seemed to lean in and listen.

"A road," he said, "with thirteen milestones. And on the thirteenth milestone, fresh-chalked, someone has written your name — spelled correctly — with an invitation."

* * *

Freya did not sleep much. When she finally did, she dreamed of her mother's hands — ink-stained, sure, turning the pages of a ledger — and woke before dawn with her journal clutched to her chest like a shield, the last starless night pressing down on the tent.

Outside, Sef was already awake, standing at the salt-line, looking east.

"Come see," was all she said.

Freya came. And there, on the eastern horizon, where no light should have been — where the stars had gone out and the moon hung thin and wrong — the dark was *moving*. A slow tide of deeper black, rolling toward them across the draft-country like ink spilled across a wet page, erasing hills as it came.

Behind her, Bramblehoof's voice, quiet and stripped of all its laughter:

"The Hollow Men found our trail. Someone's been reading your map, cartographer. Every place you've anchored — they can follow it now, straight as a ruled line."

Freya's beautiful, vicious, finishing-the-world plan turned to lead in her stomach.

Her map was leading them to her.
