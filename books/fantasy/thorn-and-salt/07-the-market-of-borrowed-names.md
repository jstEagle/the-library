## 7. The Market of Borrowed Names

Sef smelled the market before she saw it — tallow and cardamom and something underneath both, like rain on hot iron. She stopped so abruptly that Freya walked into her back.

"What?" Freya said, peering around her shoulder.

"Nothing," Sef said. "Keep walking."

But of course Freya didn't keep walking, because nobody in this cursed company ever kept walking when Sef told them to. Bramblehoof's ears swiveled forward through his crown of thorns, and Dagny squinted down the slope with the particular squint of a man who had once been paid to notice things and had never quite stopped.

"That's not on any map," Freya said slowly. "And I've *drawn* maps of here."

"No," Sef said. "It wouldn't be."

The market sat in a fold of land like an egg in a spoon — a sprawl of tents and lantern-poles strung with charms that chimed without wind, arranged in no pattern any sane person would choose and yet somehow impossible to get lost in, which was worse. It moved, she knew. It had moved for centuries, drifting from one hollow to another like a hermit crab changing shells, and it appeared only to people who were already lost, which explained why it had found them.

"It's the Market of Borrowed Names," she said at last, because they were going to stand there gawping until dark otherwise. "You don't buy things here. You buy *passage*. And you pay with pieces of yourself."

Dagny crossed himself with warding signs out of habit, then seemed embarrassed about it. "What kind of pieces?"

"Memories. Habits. The way your grandmother's kitchen smelled. Your talent for whistling." Sef shrugged, keeping her voice light, which was how she kept everything. "Small things, if you're smart. The market is fair but it isn't kind."

"And you know this because—"

"I read," Sef said. "I read *extensively*."

Bramblehoof made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely insubordinate. "She knows it because she shopped here," he said. "Forty years ago. I can smell the salt on her bargain-tongue from here."

Sef turned and gave him a look she had once used to blister barnacles off a hull. He wagged his tail, which was the thorn-bound creature's equivalent of blowing her a kiss.

"We need passage past the Hollow Men's picket on the north road," she said instead. "This market drifts. If we enter as customers and exit three valleys east, we skip two days of running and one very probable ambush. That's the whole arithmetic. In, trade, out, don't touch anything, don't tell anyone your real name, and above all—"

"Don't eat anything," Freya said.

Sef blinked. "Yes. How did you know?"

"Mama's stories." Freya's face did the thing it had started doing lately — the small flinch when her mother surfaced uninvited. "Never eat in a place that trades in selves. You might be eating your own."

"Well," Sef said. "Your mother was smarter than she let on." She meant it kindly. It landed strangely anyway, and she watched Freya carry it away into some inner room where the girl was building an entire archive of strange things her mother had said.

*Good*, Sef thought, and hated herself a little. *Stay confused. Confused is safe.*

* * *

Inside, the market was worse than she remembered, or perhaps she was simply older and less armored. Lanterns burned colors that had no names. A stall sold shadows by length — short ones for liars, long ones for lovers. A woman with six fingers on each hand was selling the concept of Tuesday, gently used, and a queue of people stood before her stall looking wistful.

A broker found them within thirty steps, as brokers do. This one wore a coat stitched from letters — actual letters, cut from old books, thousands of them, rustling faintly when he moved like a man dressed in autumn.

"Sef of the Salt Road," he said, bowing.

"Not anymore," Sef said. "Nobody of nowhere. You'll find me in your ledger under 'anonymous, grumpy.'"

"Everyone is somebody here." The broker smiled with too many teeth, all of them borrowed. "Even nobodies are a rare vintage. Now — what does your party wish to purchase?"

They haggled in the shadow of a tent whose walls displayed, moving and silent, the dreams of whoever stood closest. Sef did the talking because the others were hopeless: Freya kept staring at everything with those cartographer's eyes, cataloguing, and Dagny kept flinching whenever a vendor looked at him too long, and Bramblehoof was — well. A fox spirit wearing thorns drew attention in places like this the way a lit torch draws moths, except the moths were all armed.

Passage east for four, skirting the picket: the price came to three truths freely surrendered or two memories of emotional significance, per head.

"Per *head*," Dagny sputtered. "That's eight memories. I barely have eight memories left I'm fond of."

"You have plenty," Sef said. "Most of them are just embarrassing."

"I am not giving a market my memory of my mother's funeral."

"No one's asking for that." She turned to the broker. "We want a better rate. Four travelers, one transaction. I'll cover the difference."

"Sef—" Freya started.

"You're the map," Sef said flatly. "You don't get to pay with your head. Nothing personal." Everything personal. But the ledger entry — *one soul, paid in full* — sat in her chest like a swallowed stone, and she would be boiled in brine before she let this market take one more piece of that girl than the world already had.

The broker's coat rustled. "One seller, multiple debts? Unusual. What will you give?"

Sef took a breath. Here was the trouble with markets like this: you could lie with words all day, but the goods themselves were weighed honestly. Whatever she handed over, she handed over truly, and she could not entirely control what the scales chose to lift.

She reached into herself the way she'd been taught, forty years and a lifetime ago, and offered up the first true thing she touched:

The taste of her sister's honey-bread. The specific gray of the sea off the court's western terrace. The sound of the Pale King saying her name — not her exile-name, her *real* one, the one that had been scrubbed from records and salted behind her — spoken once, in approval, in a warm room full of candlelight.

Three memories. She set them on the scale like coins.

The scale considered. The scale, being a market-scale, took a little extra — a habit, apparently; she felt the whistling go, gone clean as a pulled tooth, and only noticed its absence by noticing the hole where it had been.

"Done," said the broker, and pressed a token into her palm: a small tin ticket stamped with a door. "Present this at the eastern gate at moonrise. The market will have moved. You won't."

"Wonderful," said Dagny.

"It's the least terrible option we've had all week," said Freya, who was watching Sef with an expression Sef didn't like at all — too sharp, too kind. "What did you pay? Really."

"Groceries," Sef said. "Old groceries. Let's find somewhere to sit until moonrise, because if we wander this place bored, we *will* end up owning a haunted hat."

* * *

They sat at a tea stall that served nothing anyone could identify, in cups that were warm on one side and cold on the other, and Sef told herself firmly that the missing memories were no great loss. Honey-bread. Sea-gray. Her name in a warm mouth. Forty years of exile had done its work; those rooms in her had long since been swept and repurposed.

That was what she told herself. Her hands, meanwhile, wrapped around the cold side of the cup, and would not fully stop trembling, and she pretended not to notice that either.

"So," Bramblehoof said, tail curled over his paws with terrible casualness. "The Pale King. Approvingly. In candlelight."

"I have no idea what you're referring to."

"The scale showed me. It shows everyone. Rude of it, really. Very poor manners for a scale."

"Bramblehoof." Sef kept her voice pleasant, which those who knew her understood to be the most alarming register she possessed. "If you finish that sentence, I will sell your name to that letter-coat man for a ha'penny and see how you like answering to 'Anonymous, Grumpy.'"

Freya snorted into her cup. Even Dagny's mouth twitched. And Sef sat there amid their small warmth feeling like a house with one wall removed — still standing, technically, but drafty in ways everyone could feel.

Because here was the truth she'd been carrying like a stone in her boot since the valley: these three were becoming hers. Not hers to protect at arm's length, professionally, the way a ferryman protects passengers. *Hers*. The girl laughed at her jokes now. The disgraced keeper had started saving her the dry seat. The fox — the fox was a complication she refused to examine until absolutely necessary.

And every hour they grew closer was an hour closer to the moment one of them learned where Sef had come from. Not the salt-witch-at-the-valley's-edge version. The other version. The version with the candlelight and the approving voice and the title she'd renounced so hard it had left a scar shaped like a doorway.

She was saved from the spiral — temporarily — by the market itself beginning to sing.

It sang at dusk, apparently. Every charm on every lantern-pole caught the same note, and the tents shivered, and the whole sprawling impossibility began, gently, to pack itself up and slide eastward like a tide deciding to change its mind.

"Gate time," Sef said, standing too fast.

* * *

The eastern gate was a gap between two stalls that hadn't existed an hour ago, hung with the tin-door tokens of previous travelers, hundreds of them, fluttering. Beyond it lay ordinary darkness, hills, honest stars — no, wait. No stars. Still none. The sky remained a scoured black plate, and every time Sef looked up at it she felt the same small vertigo, the world holding its breath.

They passed through single file. Dagny first, then Freya, then Bramblehoof, then Sef, who paused at the threshold because pausing at thresholds was salt-witch discipline, checking the passage behind—

—and that was when she saw him.

Across the market lane, half-shaded under a lantern burning a green that hurt to look at, stood a man studying a ledger. Tall, narrow-shouldered, with ink-stained cuffs and the careful stoop of someone who had spent decades bent over documents. His hair had gone white at the temples since she'd last seen him, but she'd have known the way he held his quill anywhere — pinched high, like a surgeon, like a man afraid of commitment to the page.

Corvess. Third Secretary of the Court Beneath the Mountain. Keeper of Contracts. The man who had drafted the valley's protection agreement in the first place, in triplicate, while her sister-in-service poured wine and Sef herself had stood in the corner being nineteen and certain that whatever the court wanted was right by definition.

He was here. In the flesh. Which meant the King's agents weren't merely picking up the north road trail.

They were shopping.

Sef watched, cold spreading through her middle like groundfog, as Corvess finished his transaction. The broker weighed something invisible, nodded, and Corvess tucked away — a memory, purchased. Someone's memory. Bought wholesale in a market of the lost, the way one buys grain before a war.

He glanced up. For one horrible instant his eyes crossed the lane, crossed the gate, crossed —

Sef stepped through.

The dark hills closed around her. The market slid eastward behind them with its chimes and its singing, taking Corvess and his ledger and his purchased memories off toward whatever errand the Pale King had sent him on, and Sef walked with her companions down into the valley grass and said nothing, absolutely nothing, smiling her gruff practical smile, making a joke about Dagny's haunted-hat potential.

But all that night, as they camped under the starless sky and Freya slept with her mother's ledger under her jacket like a talisman and Bramblehoof dreamed fox-dreams and Dagny kept his faithful, guilty watch — Sef lay awake doing arithmetic she couldn't balance.

Corvess never traveled without purpose. Corvess never purchased anything without a use for it. And there was exactly one item of contract-significance loose in the world that a Third Secretary might cross the wilds to acquire: knowledge of the original sale. Who had witnessed it. Who had stood in that warm candlelit room. Who had let it happen.

Who might, say, be traveling with the soul in question.

Sef stared up at the empty black sky and understood, with the clarity that always arrived too late and always arrived anyway, that her secrets had begun breeding. Each one she kept made the others heavier, and soon she would not be able to carry them, and the choice would be taken out of her hands — by Corvess, or by the market's gossip, or by Bramblehoof's insufferable nose.

Better, then, to choose the moment herself.

Tomorrow, she told the dark. Tomorrow I'll tell her something. Not everything. Something.

The dark, having heard that promise before, did not answer.

At dawn they broke camp, and Freya unfolded her maps across a flat stone with the happy frown of a woman in love with geography, and pointed east, and said, "There's a canyon ahead the map says shouldn't exist. River runs uphill through it. We should reach it by midday."

Sef looked where the girl was pointing, at the thin blue line crawling impossibly toward the mountains, and felt the future close around them like a hand.

Uphill water. Hollow Men behind. A secretary with a bought memory somewhere in the folds between.

"Aye," Sef said. "Let's go see what else this world has decided not to make sense today."
