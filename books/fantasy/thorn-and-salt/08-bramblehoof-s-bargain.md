## 8. Bramblehoof's Bargain

There were three things every fox spirit learned before their first winter of binding: never eat what the dead have salted, never sleep in a circle unless you mean to stay in it, and never — *never* — grow fond of the one you are carrying to the knife.

Bramblehoof had broken the first rule once and regretted it for a century. He had broken the second by accident and woken up married to a river. He was now, on the ninth night out from the fallen valley, in the process of breaking the third with an enthusiasm that would have made his grandmother weep into her tails.

He watched her from the low branch where he sat, curled around himself like a question mark. Freya Vane sat apart from the fire with her knees drawn up and her mother's ledger open across them, tracing lines of ink with a fingertip as though she could feel the handwriting's pulse. She had been at it for two hours. Sef had called her to bed twice. Dagny had offered her his cloak, been refused politely, and set it down near her feet without comment, which Bramblehoof considered the most intelligent thing the ward-keeper had done all journey.

The thorns stirred under his skin.

They always stirred when he thought too long about her. It was how the contract kept its grip — a fine mesh of black bristles grown through his chest, roots hooked behind his ribs, and every time his loyalty leaned one way, the thorns leaned the other, whispering in the old court-tongue: *Yours to carry. Yours to deliver. Not yours to love.*

*I am not loving her,* he told the thorns, with the dignity of a creature licking a wound no one could see. *I am merely appreciating her cartographic aptitude.*

The thorns did not answer. They never answered. That was the worst part. A curse that argued with you could be argued back. This one simply tightened, patient as winter.

* * *

It had seemed a simple errand, at the start.

A hundred years ago — or ninety; time moved strangely beneath the mountain, and stranger still inside a fox who'd spent most of it drunk on fermented rowan berries — Bramblehoof had owed the Pale King a debt. The details were embarrassing. They involved a wager, a duchess's mirror, and a promise he had made while not entirely sober regarding the ownership of his own name. The Court Beneath the Mountain collected debts the way rivers collected rain: slowly, everywhere, and all at once eventually.

The King had not wanted his pelt or his name. He had wanted a courier. Someone small enough to slip between worlds, clever enough to find a girl raised behind wards, and — this was the part Bramblehoof had appreciated least — someone whose heart was famously soft. The court's records said fox spirits could not be trusted. The King had smiled his smile like frost forming on a window and said, *That is precisely why I want one that can.*

"Carry the summons," the King had told him. "Find Freya Vane when the stars go dark. Tell her what she is owed. And bring her home to me before the moon finishes its thirteenth turn."

*Home.* As if a hole in a geode full of stolen starlight could be anyone's home. But foxes were paid in flattery, and Bramblehoof had taken the bargain because refusing the Pale King was not among the available options, and because the thorns went in easy when you agreed, and terribly otherwise.

He had carried the summons for twelve years. He had watched the valley from the ridge above the wardline, unable to cross, pacing out the seasons like a dog chained to a fencepost. He had seen Freya Vane exactly four times in all those years: once flying a paper kite shaped like a fish; once arguing with a miller about the true length of the millstream (she had been right); once sitting alone on her mother's grave talking to the headstone as if maps needed reading aloud; and once, last spring, kneeling in the road to help a child find a lost copper coin, patting the dirt with both hands, laughing when the coin turned up under her own boot.

The thorns had whispered even then: *This one. Remember this one. You will hand her to us.*

And Bramblehoof, being a fool with a soft heart in a family of fools with soft hearts, had begun — quietly, without meaning to — to hope for a loophole.

* * *

"You're staring again."

Sef dropped onto the log beside him uninvited, as was her habit, wrapping her salt-crusted shawl tighter. Up close she smelled like a storm that had lost an argument with the sea. "Foxes stare when they're hungry or guilty. Which is it?"

"I am neither. I am contemplating geography."

"From a tree. At midnight. At the cartographer."

"The best geography is contemplative."

Sef snorted and produced, from somewhere in her shawl, a twist of paper containing smoked fish. She held it up so he could see it, then set it on the log between them — payment, he understood, for information. Salt-witches always paid. It was the only honest thing about them.

"She's decoding the ledger," Sef said quietly, nodding toward Freya. "She thinks she's close. Thirteen entries, twelve crossed off." Her jaw worked. "When she reads the last page clear, she'll know exactly what her mother sold and when it comes due. And she'll look at whoever's nearest and ask why nobody told her." A pause. "I'd rather it weren't me she asks."

"And why would I know anything about that?"

"Because you've got guilt coming off you like steam off a wet dog, and because your left ear twitches when you lie, and it hasn't stopped twitching since the ford." Sef stood, brushing off her skirts. "Whatever oath has you walking her to that mountain, fox — decide soon whose side your feet are actually on. Feet always tell the truth before mouths do."

She walked away. He ate the fish. His ear twitched the whole time.

* * *

The chance came three nights later, and it came the way chances always came for Bramblehoof: badly, at the worst possible moment, wearing the face of something he should have expected.

They had made camp in the hollow of an ancient barrow hill — Dagny swore it was safe, Sef swore it wasn't, and Freya, who had the final word on all matters geographical since the incident with the uphill river, declared it defensible and went to sleep. Bramblehoof took the watch in his man-shape because the thorns rested easier when he wore hands.

Near midnight, the thorns spoke.

Not the usual whispering pressure — words this time, actual words, cold and formal as a summons read aloud in an empty hall:

***Courier. The moon turns in nine nights. Report the quarry's progress.***

Every bound creature of the court owed a report when asked. It was stitched into the same knot as the thorns themselves. And here was the trap, laid neat as a snare under leaves: if he reported truly — *she grows fond of us, we grow fond of her, we have taught her card games and she cheats charmingly* — the court would tighten the leash. They would send the Hollow Men ahead to take her early, roughly, without ceremony. If he reported falsely, the thorns would know, and thorns knew everything a bound tongue touched.

But there was a third road. There was always a third road; foxes were built for third roads.

He could report nothing at all — and buy himself a day, maybe two, before the silence itself became an answer.

Or he could do the thing his grandmother had done, the story every kit was told and no kit believed: break the oath outright. Tear the thorns out by force of will. It killed most who tried. It had killed her. But she had died free, on a warm rock, in the sun, with her name in her own mouth.

Bramblehoof sat with the summons burning behind his sternum until dawn, and did not answer, and did not tear, and hated himself with a thoroughness that would have impressed a tax collector.

* * *

Freya found him at first light, as he'd known she would. She always found him when he was miserable; he suspected she could smell it.

"Bramblehoof." She crouched in front of his branch, ledger tucked under one arm, hair escaping its braid in gray-gold wisps. "You didn't wake anyone for second watch. Dagny slept straight through and he's insufferable about it when he's rested. What's wrong?"

"Nothing. Foxes don't have wrong. We have strategically inconvenient moods."

"Mm." She sat down on the root below him, unhurried, and opened the ledger across her knees. "Then I'll talk, and you can listen, and your mood can be strategic at its own pace."

She turned pages. The morning light caught the old ink.

"I've decoded my mother's cipher," she said. "It was a mapmaker's cipher — coordinates. Every entry in the ledger is a place. First entry is the valley spring. Second is the eastern ridge. She recorded the payments as locations, like she was mapping the debt itself." Her voice stayed level, but her thumb pressed white against the page edge. "Twelve locations. Twelve payments. And the thirteenth — the last one — isn't a place in the valley at all."

"No," Bramblehoof said softly. "It isn't."

"It's a place beneath the mountain." She looked up at him, and her eyes were dry, which was worse than tears. "My soul has coordinates, Bramblehoof. My mother mapped it. Filed it. Like a river source. Like a mountain pass." A breath. "You've known. Haven't you. Since the night you collapsed at my door. You've known what the contract says."

The thorns woke, coiling tight, whispering: *Deny it. Deny her. Yours to carry, not yours to—*

"Yes," said Bramblehoof, and his left ear twitched, and for once he was glad of it. "I've known since before your door. I've known for twelve years, Freya Vane. I was the one sent to carry the summons."

* * *

He told her all of it, because there was no longer any shape of the truth he could hold without it cutting him. The debt. The wager and the mirror and the duchess. The King's frost-smile and the word *courier*, and the thorns going in, and twelve years pacing the ridgeline like a chained dog watching her fly her paper fish-kite.

"I was there when the wards fell," he said. "I ran down the mountain the moment the stars went out. I didn't sabotage them — I need you to believe that, though I know belief is expensive these days — but I came because I was *sent*. Everything I've done since the border, every mile I've walked beside you, began as an order to walk you to that throne room and put you in that chair."

Freya was quiet a long moment. Below them, Dagny was teaching Sef to play knucklebones badly, and Sef was letting him win, which Bramblehoof suspected was its own kind of mercy.

"So the contract binds you to deliver me," she said at last. "What happens if you don't?"

"The thorns take root properly. They've been waiting in me a long time, like seeds in dry ground. Given cause, they bloom." He tried for lightness. "It's very dramatic, I'm told. Lots of screaming. Grandmother rated the experience one star."

"What happens if you *do* deliver me?"

He looked at her. Firelight and morninglight mixing on her face. The coin-finder. The map-reader. The girl who talked to headstones.

"They put you in the chair," he said. "And they keep you. Forever is short in the court — it only lasts until the stars burn out, and they've caged most of those already."

Freya nodded slowly, and then — this was the part that undid him — she reached up and scratched him behind the left ear, the twitching one, the truthful one. No human had ever done that. His leg betrayed him immediately. He forgave her instantly.

"Then here's what I think," she said. "Contracts are just bargains written down by someone who won. And I—" she tapped the ledger, "—am my mother's daughter, and my mother wrote things down too. Before the thirteenth turn, I intend to sit across from your Pale King and renegotiate. Both our contracts. Yours and mine." A small, fierce smile. "So don't bloom yet, Bramblehoof. Stay stubborn. Stubbornness is load-bearing."

* * *

That evening, the thorns spoke again, and this time they did not ask.

***Courier. Silence is answer enough. The Hollow Men ride. Your failure will be harvested with hers.***

Bramblehoof stood at the edge of the campfire's light, tail low, and felt the choice that had been circling him for twelve years finally land on his shoulders with both claws.

If he stayed silent, the Hollow Men would come — perhaps tomorrow, perhaps at the ford, perhaps at the tollhouse — and Freya would be dragged to the mountain in chains, and his cowardice would have purchased nothing but company in the cage.

If he warned them, he broke the seal of the courier's silence, and the thorns would begin to bloom tonight.

His grandmother's story ended the same way every telling: *she died free, on a warm rock, in the sun.* When he was a kit he'd thought it a tragedy. Standing here, watching Sef lose honestly at knucklebones for the first time in her life, watching Dagny laugh — actually laugh, rusty as an unused gate — watching Freya Vane sketch the barrow hill by firelight because she could not bear to leave any place unknown —

He thought: *what a way to go.*

He padded to the center of the camp, sat, and let his man-shape rise around him. Four faces turned. He took a breath, and reached into his own chest, and gripped the first thorn.

"There is something you should all know," he said, as the first root tore loose and the pain sang through him like a struck bell. "We're about to have guests. And I'm about to be very, very rude to them."

The thorns bloomed.
