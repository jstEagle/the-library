## 9. Rivers That Run Uphill

The river announced itself before they saw it — a low, wrong humming in the stones beneath their boots, like a struck bell that refused to finish its note.

Freya felt it through the soles of her feet first, then through her teeth, and finally through the part of her that had spent twenty-two years believing water went where gravity told it. She crested a shelf of grey rock, looked down into the canyon, and stopped so abruptly that Bramblehoof walked into the back of her knees.

"That," she said, "is not possible."

Below them, the river ran uphill.

Not trickled, not seeped — *ran*, full and silver and muscular, climbing the canyon wall in a long sinuous curve as though it had somewhere urgent to be and no patience for the ordinary rules of the world. Where it reached the canyon's rim it spilled over the edge, hung in the air for one impossible heartbeat, and then fell *upward* into the clouds, vanishing somewhere above them like smoke returning to a fire.

"I hate this place," Sef said flatly. She had come up beside Freya, salt-grey braid swinging, arms crossed in the manner of a woman who had already decided the landscape was personally insulting her. "I hated it the first time I crossed it, and I hate it more now."

"You've been here before?" Dagny asked.

"I've been everywhere before, boyo. It's the curse of having lived too long." She squinted down at the ascending water. "The Upside Water. The court uses it as a moat, back when it bothered with moats. Anything that falls in goes up instead of down, and anything going up keeps going up until it reaches—" she waved vaguely at the sky, "—wherever. There are boots in the clouds. And a cow. Don't ask about the cow."

Bramblehoof's ears swiveled forward with what Freya had learned was his equivalent of intense scholarly interest. "Was it a good cow?"

"Bramblehoof."

"I'm asking for the map," he said, with dignity. "She's making a map. Maps require details."

Freya had, in fact, already pulled out her field book. Her hands were steady, which surprised her; three days out from the valley, past the fallen wards, past the market of borrowed names where Sef had traded something precious and wouldn't say what, and her hands had learned to be steady the way a soldier learns to sleep sitting up — not because the danger stops, but because shaking wastes time.

*Upside Water,* she wrote. *Runs south-to-north along canyon floor, reverses at rim. Do not fall in. (Cow.)*

"Good detail," Bramblehoof approved, reading over her shoulder. His breath smelled of pine sap and, faintly, of thorns. "Very authoritative."

"The Hollow Men will have reached the ford behind us by now," Dagny said. He stood a little apart, as he always did, a broad grey man with ward-keeper's knots tattooed up both forearms and the particular stillness of someone who had spent years practicing how to take up less space than he actually did. "They don't tire. They don't eat. They'll follow our trail straight to this rim."

"So we cross the water," Freya said. "It breaks the trail. They can't follow us over an upside-down river."

"They can't swim it either," Sef agreed. "Which is the good news. The bad news is neither can we, and the crossing stones—" she pointed to a line of flat grey boulders that marched upstream along the canyon floor, "—are half-submerged, slick as lies, and exactly wide enough for one person to make a mistake on."

"Well," said Freya, shouldering her pack. "I've made plenty of mistakes. How hard can it be?"

Later, she would think: *that was the moment. That was the exact moment the universe decided to teach me humility.*

* * *

The canyon swallowed sound strangely. Their footsteps came back to them a half-beat late, as if the stone were considering each echo before agreeing to return it. Below, the river climbed past them on their left, close enough to touch, cold enough that the air above it bit at their cheeks. Freya walked the crossing stones with her arms out, her cartographer's balance doing quiet, competent work, and allowed herself — foolishly, briefly — to enjoy it.

"This is extraordinary," she murmured. "Do you know what this means? A river like this shouldn't exist. The watershed alone — where does it *drain?* Is there a second river running downhill somewhere to balance it? Or does the water simply cycle — up into the sky and back down as rain?" She nearly turned around to look at Dagny. "This is the most important hydrological feature in a hundred miles and nobody has ever mapped it because nobody could survive getting here, which means every map of this region is *wrong,* all of them, and I am standing on the correction—"

"Freya," Dagny said.

"—and if the water cycles, then the rain here must fall upward too, or perhaps it falls normally and the river is stealing it, in which case there's a deficit somewhere, there has to be a deficit, you can't just move water against—"

"*Freya.*"

"What?"

He wasn't looking at her. He was looking downstream — which was to say, downhill, which was to say, in the direction they'd come — where the grey morning light had acquired a new texture. A wrongness. The way air looks when something is walking through it that doesn't quite agree to displace properly.

Hollow Men.

There were six of them. Perhaps eight; they kept folding in on themselves at the edges, like figures seen through old glass. They wore the shapes of men the way a scarecrow wears a coat, loosely and without conviction, and where their faces should have been there was only smooth pale blankness, featureless as unmarked vellum. They did not hurry. They did not need to. The canyon floor carried them without stones, without balance, walking directly across the climbing water as though the river had simply agreed to pretend they weren't there.

"Oh," Freya said. "Oh, that's not fair."

"Move," Sef snapped. "*Now.* Move, girl, move your feet, the stones end in fifty paces and I do not intend to find out what happens when the Hollow Men catch someone mid-river—"

They ran. The stones were treacherous with spray; the climbing river threw cold mist upward — sideways — in every direction but the sensible one, and Freya's world contracted to the next flat rock and the next and the next, her field book clutched against her chest like a child, Bramblehoof flowing ahead of her in his fox-shape, low and quick, his thorn-marks glowing a sullen green.

Behind them, the Hollow Men stepped onto the first stone.

It didn't hold them. It held *them* — the stone sank, tilted, and one of the figures pitched sideways into the climbing water, and Freya watched, horrified, as it rose. Up past the rim, up past the cliffs, up into the clouds, arms pinwheeling with terrible slow patience, until the mist took it.

"One down," Sef panted grimly. "Five to go. Keep moving!"

The stones ended.

They ended the way bad things always end — suddenly, and while Freya was looking somewhere else. One moment there was rock under her boot; the next there was only the gap, six feet of rushing upside-down water between her and the far bank, and her momentum — the momentum she had spent on running rather than looking — carried her straight out over it.

Time did something strange. Later she would remember it in pieces: the river beside her, climbing. Dagny's face, turning. Sef's shout arriving too late to mean anything. The vast and ridiculous thought that her mother had never once let her wade in the mill stream without holding her hand, and look at her now, about to ascend into the clouds like the world's most pedantic weather balloon.

Then a hand closed on her ankle.

Dagny had thrown himself flat across the last stone, arm outstretched to its absolute limit, and caught her by one boot as she swung out over the gap. The jolt nearly tore him off the rock. Freya swung like a bell, the river roaring upward inches from her hair, cold spray filling her mouth, and heard Dagny grunt — not a word, just effort, the sound of a man deciding he would rather lose the arm.

"Hold on!" he roared.

"To WHAT?"

"ANYTHING!"

She grabbed his wrist. He hauled. For one endless second nothing happened except the two of them straining against each other and gravity's confused cousin, and then Bramblehoof — gods bless him — sank his teeth into the back of Dagny's collar and threw his whole small weight backward, and the three of them crashed onto the last stone in a tangle of limbs and fur and extremely undignified swearing.

The Hollow Men were four stones away.

"Up," Dagny gasped. "Up the bank, GO—"

They went. Scrambling, sliding, Freya's palms torn raw on wet granite, until they reached the far shore and turned, chests heaving, to face the five pale shapes advancing unhurriedly across the water.

And that was when Dagny did the thing he had sworn, loudly and repeatedly and on at least two separate occasions involving actual oaths, never to do again.

* * *

She saw it happen, and even now she could hardly describe it. Ward-craft was forbidden — not illegal, merely *forbidden*, banned by decree after the Sundering, scrubbed from the guild rolls, its last practitioners scattered or worse. Every ward-keeper left in the world was supposed to be a caretaker only: menders of old wards, never makers of new ones. Dagny had told her this himself, on their second night out, staring into the fire. *"A keeper who builds is a keeper who ends up on a rope,"* he'd said. *"That's the law, and the law exists for reasons I'm not clever enough to explain properly."*

But the Hollow Men were four stones away, and explanations were in short supply.

Dagny knelt and pressed both palms flat against the bank, tattoos down, fingers spread, and spoke — not words, exactly, but the bones of words, sounds stripped of meaning the way a skeleton is stripped of flesh. And the ground answered.

Light moved under the earth. Freya felt it pass beneath her knees like a swallow of strong drink, warmth spreading outward from Dagny's hands in a great expanding ring, and where the ring crossed the river — where it touched the climbing water itself — the river *noticed.*

The Upside Water stopped climbing.

All of it. Along the whole visible length of the canyon, the silver current froze mid-ascent, hung trembling in the air like a held breath, and then, with a sound like the world's largest sheet tearing, it reversed — and came DOWN.

The wave hit the Hollow Men like the fist of an annoyed god. Freya had one glimpse of pale figures tumbling, folding, unraveling — they did not drown so much as *disagree* with the water, coming apart at the seams into ribbons of grey mist — and then the flood hit the banks too, and she grabbed Bramblehoof by the scruff and Sef by the arm and hauled them both behind a boulder as the canyon filled with the roar of a river remembering which way was down.

When it was over — when the water had settled into a churning, furious, entirely conventional river, running the correct direction like a scolded child — Dagny was still kneeling. Still touching the earth. Shaking.

"Dagny." Freya crouched beside him. "Hey. Look at me. You did it. You saved my life."

"Don't." His voice came out scraped raw. "Don't thank me. You don't know what I just did."

"I know exactly what you just did. You broke a law to save my life, and I'd like to say I feel terrible about the trouble it will cause you, but mostly I feel alive, so I'm going to be selfish about it." She took his wrist — gently, the way you'd approach a wounded animal — and turned his hand over. The ward-knots on his forearm were blackened, scorched into his skin as though the tattoos themselves had burned. "What is that?"

"It's the price." He wouldn't meet her eyes. "Forbidden craft doesn't come free. The old wards — the valley wards — they were built by keepers who paid this. Skin-deep burns, every working. My master called it 'the ink arguing back.'" He laughed, a small broken sound. "I told you I'd never build again. I meant it. I want you to understand that I *meant it*, Freya. And then you went off the edge of that stone, and there wasn't a law in the world big enough to matter."

For a moment nobody spoke. The reborn river rushed and foamed below them, gloriously ordinary.

"Well," said Sef, from behind the boulder, dusting herself off with the air of a woman determined to ruin the moment before it ruined her. "That was either very brave or very stupid, and given that it worked, history will call it brave. Which is why history is written by people who weren't there." She looked at Dagny's burned arm, and something flickered across her face — guilt, Freya thought, or its close cousin. "You should bind that. Salt-water, if we had any. We don't. Spit and a rag, then. Don't give me that look, boyo, I've seen worse self-treatment in armies."

"You were in an army?" Bramblehoof asked, ears perking.

"I was in many things." Sef's voice closed like a door. "Fetch the rag."

Freya bound Dagny's arm in silence, working the cloth between his fingers while he stared at the river. When she tied the last knot, he finally spoke.

"I need to tell you something," he said. "About the night the wards fell. I've been carrying it since the valley, and I keep finding reasons not to say it, and I've just realized — watching myself burn my own skin rather than watch you die — that reasons are cheaper than I thought they were." He swallowed. "I saw something that night, Freya. Someone. And it wasn't the King's people."

Freya went still. Beside her, she was distantly aware, Sef had stopped moving entirely.

"The wards didn't fail," Dagny said quietly. "They were opened. From the inside. By somebody who knew the valley better than the wards did — and I think I know who, and I think they're going to be waiting for us at the mountain."

Above them, impossibly high, the missing stars' absence seemed to press down like a hand. Freya looked from Dagny's burned arm to Sef's frozen face to Bramblehoof's unreadable green eyes, and felt the truth of her mother's ledger settle over her like snowfall:

Everyone on this journey knew something about that night. Everyone except her.

"Sef," she said slowly, without turning around. "Why did you stop breathing just now?"
