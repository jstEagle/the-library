## 10. The Salt-Witch Confesses

The rain found them an hour before the sister did.

Sef smelled it first — not the rain, which was ordinary enough, but the salt beneath it. Court-salt, the kind that didn't come from any sea but from the deep places where the Pale King kept his drowned choir. It had a sweetness to it, like honey left too long in a copper pot, and it made her back teeth ache the way it always had, even twenty years after she'd sworn off the stuff.

She said nothing. She just shifted her pack to her left shoulder — the one with the knife under the strap — and began, casually, to count the trees.

They were camped in a hollow of black pines that Freya's map called *the Whisperwood* and Freya herself called "a nightmare with foliage," because every time she tried to chart it the trees rearranged themselves out of spite. Dagny had a fire going small and low, the way ward-keepers do, cupped in stones so it barely breathed. Bramblehoof lay curled at the edge of the light with his thorn-marks glowing faintly blue, which Sef had learned meant he was either sleeping or thinking about running. With foxes, she suspected, there was rarely a difference.

Freya sat cross-legged on her bedroll, redrawing by firelight the stretch of river they'd climbed that morning — uphill, all of it, against every law Sef had ever respected — and muttering to it the way you'd talk to a mule that had thrown you twice.

"Stop glaring at the paper," Sef said. "It can feel it."

"Maps have feelings now?"

"Everything out here has feelings. That's the problem." Sef fed a pinecone to the fire and watched it pop. "In the valley nothing had feelings except the bees in the wards and old Marta's goat. Out here everything is *opinionated.*"

That got a small laugh out of Freya, which Sef counted as a victory, because the girl hadn't laughed much since the ledger. Since *one soul, paid in full.* Since she'd started looking at her mother's memory like a stranger's face.

Sef knew that look. She'd worn it herself once, for about a decade.

The salt-sweetness grew stronger. Closer. Sef turned another pinecone over in her fingers and did the arithmetic she'd been avoiding for three days: the Hollow Men at the ford had been sentries, not hunters. Sentries guarded something. Which meant someone had been driving them along this canyon like sheep toward a gate, and court-trained drivers used sisters when they wanted the sheep caught gentle.

Or caught at all.

"Sef." Freya looked up, pencil still moving. "You've checked the treeline four times."

"I'm admiring the trees."

"You're counting them."

"Admiring *and* counting. A woman can do two things."

Dagny snorted from his side of the fire. The old man had been quiet since the ford — quieter than usual, which for Dagny was saying something, since his usual silence could curdle milk. He kept flexing his left hand where the forbidden ward-craft had burned him saving Freya's life, and he kept looking at Sef with an expression she recognized, because she'd seen it in mirrors during her first year of exile: *I know what you are, and I haven't decided what that means.*

Bramblehoof opened one eye. The eye was amber and ancient and deeply annoyed, which was its resting state.

"She comes," the fox said. "The one who smells of choir-salt. She has been walking behind us since the uphill river, matching our pace, waiting for us to stop."

"You could have mentioned that," Freya said.

"He could have," Sef agreed. "He enjoys theater."

"I enjoy *timing,*" Bramblehoof said, rising and shaking himself until his thorns rattled like dry seedpods. "There is a difference. If I had told you three hours ago, you would have spent three hours being afraid instead of three hours drawing your river. I stand by my choice."

"And if she's here to kill us?"

"Then we will be very well-rested corpses."

The voice came out of the dark before anyone could answer — low, unhurried, and familiar in a way that made Sef's stomach drop through the floor of the world and keep falling.

"*Well-rested corpses,*" it repeated. "The fox is wiser than he looks. Though admittedly, that bar is underground."

She stepped into the firelight the way court women step into anywhere: like the room had been built around her arrival. Tall, gray-robed, silver-haired, with salt-white scars lacing both forearms in patterns Sef knew intimately, because she had the same ones, hidden under her sleeves. Her eyes were the pale gold of the King's own coin.

"Maren," Sef said.

"Little Sef." Maren smiled, and it was the worst kind of smile, the kind that had been practiced on people who then disappeared. "Twenty years, and you still flinch at your own name. Sit, sit. Don't get up on my account. I'm not here for blood."

"You're here for the girl."

"I'm here to *talk* to the girl. Whether blood happens afterward depends entirely on how the talking goes." Maren settled onto a stone across the fire like she owned it, had always owned it, would go on owning it long after everyone present was dust. Her gaze slid to Freya, and softened — or performed softening; with Maren it was impossible to tell, and Sef hated that she still couldn't tell. "You have her hands. Did you know that? Elira Vane. We met once, your mother and I. She struck me as a woman who understood that love and arithmetic are the same discipline."

"Don't," Sef said quietly.

Maren raised an eyebrow. "*Don't?*"

"Don't talk about her like you knew her. You read a file. You were sent to assess a debt. That's not knowing someone."

"No," Maren agreed pleasantly. "Knowing someone is what you did. Isn't it?"

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the pines whispered their rearrangements. Freya had gone very still, pencil forgotten, and Dagny had risen halfway off his log with his burned hand half-curled, ready for ward-craft he wasn't supposed to know.

And Sef sat with twenty years of gruff practicality stacked around her heart like sandbags, and felt every one of them start to slip.

"What does she mean?" Freya asked. Not sharp. Worse than sharp — careful. The way she'd been careful ever since the ledger, testing each new betrayal for load-bearing capacity. "Sef. What does she mean, *knowing?*"

"There was a night," Sef said, "thirteen years ago. The twelfth payment. Your mother came to the edge of the valley to make it, and I—"

"Witnessed it," Maren supplied helpfully.

"—was *there,*" Sef finished, louder, because if she stopped she would never start again. "I was there. And I didn't stop it."

* * *

Here is what Sef had told no one, in twenty years of exile, in twenty years of selling charms at the valley's edge and pretending the salt-scars on her arms were old kitchen burns:

She had not always been a hedge-witch with bad knees and a good line in weather-charms. She had been Sister Sefaera of the Deep Choir, third-ranked of the Pale King's salt-witches, and she had been *good.* Good at binding, good at drowning prayers, good at the particular court arithmetic where everything alive was a number and every number was negotiable.

Elira Vane had come to the mountain on the twelfth turn with a bundle of contracts and a face like winter deciding whether to thaw. Twelve payments already made — twelve pieces of the valley's luck, its health, its hidden years, carved away and rendered up to the Court Beneath. One payment remained. The King's clerks had already chosen it. They were very efficient about choosing.

Sef had been assigned to witness. Standard practice. A sister stood at every signing to make sure no mortal cleverness contaminated the terms.

And Elira Vane, in the antechamber of starless geode, with the contract laid out before her and her daughter's name already inked in the space reserved for the soul, had turned to Sef — to the witness, to nobody, to the lowest-ranking body in the room — and said:

"*You're the one they call the little swimmer. You grew up above ground, didn't you? Under open sky.*"

And Sef, who had not been addressed as a person by a mortal in nine years, had said: "*Once.*"

And Elira Vane had taken her hands — her actual hands, warm, mortal, shaking slightly — and said: "*Then you know what I'm buying. Tell me if I'm wrong to buy it. Tell me, and mean it, and I'll sign. But if you can't say it, sister — if some part of you thinks there's another way — then say THAT.*"

And Sef had stood there with a mortal woman's hands around hers, and the whole machinery of the Court at her back, and the contract between them, and she had thought: *there is no other way. There is never another way. The wards need feeding. The math doesn't care about your feelings.*

So she had said: "Sign. There's no other way."

And Elira Vane had looked at her for a long moment — not angry, which would have been easier, but *sad*, sad the way you're sad about weather — and signed, and thanked her, and walked out of the Court Beneath the Mountain carrying her daughter's soul-price like a woman carrying water from a well she'd poisoned herself.

And Sef had lasted eleven more days down there before the memory of those hands made the salt choke her, and she had run, and been exiled, and spent twenty years telling herself the story ended there.

But it didn't end there. Because there was the other part. The part she'd buried deepest.

After the signing, Elira Vane had paused at the door. Turned back. And said, quietly, so the clerks couldn't hear:

"*You could have lied to me, you know. You could have said 'wait, there's another way' even if you didn't believe it. Mothers will believe almost anything from someone with kind eyes. You chose honesty. Remember that you chose it.*"

And Sef had remembered it. Every day. For twenty years.

* * *

"So no," Sef said now, into the firelight, into Freya's careful, terrible stillness, "I didn't sell you. But I stood in the room. I held your mother's hands while she did. And when she begged me — because she did beg me, girl, don't let anyone tell you different, she begged me with everything she had — I told her there was no other way. Because I believed it. Because believing it was easier than fighting."

The silence went on so long that Sef heard the pines move twice.

"You *begged her to stop it,*" Freya said finally. "That's what Maren said. That's what you just said. She begged you."

"Yes."

"And you said no."

"I said worse than no. I said *there is no other way,* which is the most loyal sentence in the entire Court, and the most cowardly sentence anywhere else." Sef pulled off her left glove, slowly, and held her arm to the firelight so the salt-scars showed — the same patterns as Maren's, faded, half-erased by twenty years of scrubbing and salt-breaking and stubbornness. "I was one of them. Third-ranked. I helped write contracts like yours. I ran before they could make me complicit in anything worse, which is a generous way of saying I ran right after I was complicit in something exactly this bad."

Freya looked at the scars. Then at her mother's ledger, lying closed beside her bedroll. Then at Sef.

"You guided me out here," she said. "You've kept me alive. Why? Was it guilt?"

"Partly."

"What was the rest?"

"The rest," Sef said, and found, to her enormous irritation, that her voice cracked, "was that your mother was the only person in that whole starless hole who treated me like something other than a tool. And when she asked me to lie to her — to give her one thread of hope to hold — I gave her arithmetic instead. So yes, guilt. But also a debt. Not the Court's kind. Mine."

Maren watched all of this with her chin on her hand, wearing the expression of a woman enjoying a play she'd financed.

"Beautiful," she said. "Truly. Twenty years of exile and she still performs better than the choir. Now, girl — Freya, isn't it? — ask her the question she's dancing around. Ask her why she *really* left the Court. Ask her what she saw on her way out."

"Maren—" Sef started.

"Ask her about the thirteenth clause."

The fire seemed to lean away from those words. Even Bramblehoof's thorns dimmed.

Freya's voice was very steady. Steadier than Sef had any right to expect. "What thirteenth clause?"

Sef closed her eyes. Opened them. "When your mother signed, the contract had twelve visible clauses. Payments, terms, protections for the valley. But the Court always writes a thirteenth clause where mortals won't look for it. In the margins. In the binding itself." She exhaled. "Your mother knew. She was smarter than they expected. She read the binding. And what she read made her smile — the only time she smiled that whole night — and she said to me, 'Tell them nothing, little swimmer. Let them think they've won.'"

"And what was in it?" Freya whispered.

"I don't know," Sef said. "That's the truth, and it's the last piece of honesty I have left tonight. Your mother hid it too well. Whatever she wrote into that contract's bones — whatever trap or trick or gift she stitched into the thirteenth clause — only two people in the world could ever read it. The Pale King." She looked at Freya, and the firelight caught the salt-scars on her bare arm. "And the daughter whose name is written in the margin."

For a moment nobody moved. Then Maren rose, smooth as tide, and Sef was on her feet with the knife out of her sleeve before she'd decided to draw it.

"Peace," Maren said, palms open. "I came to deliver a message, and now it's delivered. The King knows the girl carries her mother's clause. He's frightened, little Sef — do you understand? I have served him thirty years and I have never once seen him frightened. He's doubling the guard on the descent. He's waking the Hollow Men." Her pale-gold eyes moved to Freya, and for one unguarded instant there was something underneath the performance — something that might, in a better life, have been pity. "Whatever your mother stitched into that margin, child, he believes it can unmake him. Reach the court before the thirteenth turn. And when you stand in front of him —"

Behind Maren, at the treeline, the pines stopped whispering.

Every one of them, all at once, went silent — and in that silence Sef heard the sound she had hoped never to hear again: the slow, wet dragging of many feet, coming down the canyon from the north.

The Hollow Men. Dozens of them. Waking early.

"—don't sign anything," Maren finished grimly, and reached into her robe, and threw something to Sef: a small vial of choir-salt, stoppered with wax. "You'll need this. And Sef — the clause. When you remember what Elira showed you that night, the thing you've spent twenty years pretending you didn't see —"

The first Hollow Man came around the bend of the canyon, starless-eyed, mouth open.

"—remember that she *smiled.*"

Maren stepped backward into the dark, and the dark took her the way it takes court women, completely, and the Hollow Men came on, and Sef found herself standing shoulder to shoulder with a disgraced ward-keeper, a thorn-bound fox, and the daughter of the only honest woman the Court Beneath the Mountain had ever produced — holding a vial of the magic she'd sworn off, and realizing, with a strange calm, that Elira Vane had shown her the thirteenth clause that night after all.

She just hadn't understood what she was reading.

Not yet.
