## 11. Ledger of the Mother

They made camp in the hollow of a dead giant — some tree so old its trunk had rotted into a room, with a doorway of split bark and a ceiling of woven branches that still, impossibly, held rain out. Sef called it a "grandmother oak" and refused to say more about it, which was how she said most things.

Freya sat with her back against the heartwood and her mother's ledger open across her knees, and for the first time since the stars went out, she let herself actually look at it.

She had carried the book for three days like a stone in her pack. She had touched its cover at night to be sure it was real. She had not, until now, been brave enough to open past the first page twice.

"Are you going to read it or court it?" Bramblehoof asked from his perch on a root. In fox shape he looked like a creature stitched together from hedge trimmings — thorns along his spine, leaves caught in his ruff, one ear permanently folded as if he'd lost an argument with a bramble bush and never quite recovered. His voice, when he chose to speak, came out dry as autumn.

"I'm deciding," Freya said, "how much I want to know."

"That's the trouble with you valley folk. You think knowing is optional." He scratched behind his good ear. "It isn't. Knowing finds you eventually. Better to meet it standing."

Dagny, across the small fire, grunted agreement without looking up from the ward-stones he was re-cutting with a knife worn thin as a promise. Sef said nothing at all, which from Sef was a kind of blessing.

Freya turned the page.

* * *

The ledger was written in her mother's hand — that sharp, slanted script that had filled every margin of every map in their house, the hand that had taught Freya hers. But this wasn't cartography. The early pages were accounts: grain traded, wool sold, a roof mended, ordinary arithmetic of an ordinary life in a valley that had forgotten magic could exist anywhere else.

Then, on a page dated thirteen years back, the entries changed.

*Paid the ferryman's toll. He does not take coin. He takes what is named.*

Freya read it four times. The words didn't rearrange themselves, though she half expected them to; wild country had made her suspicious of anything that stayed put.

"What's a ferryman's toll?" Dagny asked. He'd drifted over despite himself; ward-keepers, she was learning, were constitutionally incapable of ignoring an unexplained ledger entry. It was probably in their blood.

"If I knew, I'd tell you." She traced the line with her finger. Beneath it, smaller, almost squeezed into the margin as if her mother had hoped smallness would make it less true:

*The valley sleeps safe tonight. First of twelve. My hands shook. I did it anyway.*

"Twelve," Freya said aloud.

Sef looked up sharply. Bramblehoof's ears both went flat, even the crooked one.

"Turn the page," the fox said quietly.

She turned the page. And the next. Twelve times over twelve years, the same rhythm: a payment made, unnamed but always *something taken, something named*, and always the same refrain beneath it, growing bolder each year as though her mother's hand grew steadier even as her conscience grew louder.

*Second of twelve.*
*Fifth of twelve. The wards sing sweeter after each payment. I hate that they do.*
*Ninth of twelve. I have stopped telling myself it can't go on. It goes on.*
*Eleventh of twelve. One left. God forgive me, I know what it must be, and I have known for years, and I have kept cooking breakfast anyway.*

The fire cracked. Somewhere outside the grandmother oak, wind moved through trees that had no business being in a forest at all — their leaves chimed faintly, like distant bells, and nobody commented on it because commenting would have meant admitting how strange the world beyond the wards truly was.

"Thirteen payments," Sef said at last. Her voice was rougher than usual, which for Sef meant roughly human. "Twelve made."

"And the thirteenth due," Freya finished. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "On the thirteenth turn."

Nobody argued. Nobody softened it. That was the strange gift of these three — the disgraced keeper, the exiled witch, the thorn-bound fox — none of them lied to her, or at least none of them lied *well*, and she had come to treasure the clumsiness of their honesty.

"The moon turns in six days," Dagny said.

"Five," said Bramblehoof. "I counted the dark hours. There are more of them than there should be."

* * *

Freya walked out of the oak alone, ledger under her arm, because there are conversations a person needs to have with only the sky for witness — except the sky, just now, was a blank black slate where stars ought to be, which made it a poor confessor and a worse comfort.

She found a mossy rise overlooking the river — the normal river, running the correct direction, for once — and sat down and opened the ledger again, and this time she read it the way her mother had taught her to read maps.

"Every map lies a little," Mara Vane used to say, tapping the paper with one ink-stained finger. "But a good map lies *usefully*. Your job is to find what it's hiding, because that's always the most important thing on it."

So Freya hunted the hidden thing.

It was in the margins. Of course it was. Her mother had written her secrets sideways, upside-down, tucked between entries like smuggled letters — and Freya recognized the trick because it was her own trick, inherited before she'd ever known there was anything to inherit. She tilted the book toward the moonless dark, then toward the firelight when that failed, and found that certain lines, held at the right angle to the light, showed indentations: her mother had written a second text beneath the first, pressing hard, then scraped the top layer away.

A palimpsest. A ledger over a letter.

Freya fetched her drafting charcoal from her pack — the soft stub she used for shading coastlines — and rubbed it gently across a margin, and her mother's true voice rose up out of the paper like a figure from a developing print.

*To whoever finds this, and I pray to every star still burning that it is no one, or that it is everyone, or that it is my daughter when she is old enough to forgive me —*

Freya's hands began to shake. She set the book flat on her knees and pressed them steady and kept reading.

*— here is the whole of it, because a debt half-understood is a debt doubled.*

*The valley was dying when I was young. You won't remember; the wards took the memory of sickness along with everything else, that was part of the price. Blight in the wheat. Fevers in the low houses. The border woods creeping a foot closer every season, hungry as anything. I went looking for help the way fools do, following a story my own grandmother told: that beneath the mountain there is a court, and in that court a king pale as bone who buys and sells whatever has a name.*

*I found him. That is the sin I will not dress up in softer cloth. I found him, and he was courteous, and courteous things frighten me more than cruel ones ever have.*

*He named his price for the valley's keeping: thirteen payments across thirteen years, each dearer than the last. The first cost our finest harvest memories — the valley forgot its best years, which is why everyone believes the old days were merely ordinary. The fourth cost the village bell's true voice. The seventh cost the Hendersons' youngest son his shadow; he seems well enough, doesn't he? Look at him on bright days. Look at where his feet end.*

*The twelfth cost me my sister's face from every mind in the valley but mine. You had an aunt, Freya. Her name was Wren. She lives past the eastern ridge now and does not know why she cannot make herself look back.*

*And the thirteenth —*

The charcoal trembled. Freya waited, breathing the way she'd been taught to wait out a storm at her drafting table, and read on.

*— the thirteenth is the keystone. A soul, freely given, freely named. Mine, I offered. He laughed. Not mine, he said; the valley's protection must rest on someone who will live long past the maker. Someone born inside the wards, whose roots run deepest. Someone whose name I would give him freely, which is the only way names may pass.*

*I told him no. I want that written plainly, wherever writing goes when paper burns. I told him no, and I wept, and I begged him to take the valley's luck instead, its future harvests, its unborn children's good fortune, anything.*

*He said — and I write his words exactly, because exactness is all I have left to give you — he said: "Mothers always return to bargain. They simply require time to convince themselves the child would consent, if the child could understand."*

*I swore I would never consent. Here is my shame, then, the whole of it: I wrote your name in his book anyway. Not to give it — do you hear me, heart of mine, do you hear me? — but to hold it. To keep it where I could watch it. A name written in a debtor's ledger is a hostage, yes, but a hostage can be guarded. I spent twelve years learning the ledger's cipher, learning what clauses bind him and what loopholes unbind. If you are reading this, either I failed to finish, or I finished too late.*

*Look to the thirteenth clause. Everything turns on the thirteenth clause. A contract is a map, Frey. It shows where the walls are — and every wall has a door if you draw it honestly enough.*

*A contract is a map.*

Freya read that line until the words blurred, then read it again with her eyes closed, which is a thing you can do when a sentence has become part of you.

Her mother hadn't sold her. Her mother had *gambled* her — badly, desperately, with the stakes stacked against her by something ancient and patient — but she had gambled the way a cartographer gambles: by trusting that the truth, fully drawn, would show a way through.

Grief didn't leave her. Grief simply changed its shape, the way water changes shape but not substance, and settled somewhere deeper and quieter than it had lived before. Thirteen years she had wondered what her mother had done in those long evenings hunched over this book, why she'd flinched at the word "mountain," why she'd taught her daughter every cipher and code and marginal trick the craft possessed, urgently, almost desperately, as if running out of time.

She hadn't been teaching her daughter to draw maps.

She'd been teaching her daughter to survive the one that mattered.

"Oh, Mum," Freya said softly, to the starless sky. "You stubborn, brilliant, terrible woman."

The sky offered no comment. But somewhere downslope, a leaf-bell chimed, once, like an answer, or like applause.

* * *

She was still sitting there when Bramblehoof padded up beside her, quiet as foxes are supposed to be and rarely manage.

"You've read it," he said. Not a question. Thorn-spirits could smell grief, probably. Or truth. Or possibly Freya just had the expression of someone who'd been crying and had given up pretending otherwise.

"Thirteenth clause," she said. "My mother says everything turns on it."

Bramblehoof went very still. Stillness, on him, was loud.

"You knew," Freya said slowly. "That's what you're not saying. You carried the contract, you said. Carried it for thirteen years. So you know what's in it."

"I know what's *written* in it," the fox said carefully. "Knowing what it *means* is different. Contracts beneath the mountain aren't documents, cartographer. They're labyrinths. Every clause is a corridor. Some corridors lead out. Most lead deeper in. Even the King's own clerks get lost in them — that's not an accident, that's design."

"Then I'll need a guide through the labyrinth."

"You have one." He flicked his crooked ear. "Though I warn you, my sense of direction in that place is complicated by the fact that I'm contractually obligated to deliver you to its center."

"So you keep reminding me."

"Someone should. You keep walking toward it *faster*."

Freya looked down at the ledger in her lap — at the sideways secrets and scraped-away truths, at the last line her mother had ever pressed into the palimpsest, the one she'd read a dozen times already and would read a dozen more:

*If you find the door, Frey, don't knock. Draw it open.*

"Bramblehoof," she said. "The thirteenth clause. When the moon completes its turn — what exactly happens to me?"

The fox was silent long enough that the leaf-bells below chimed twice.

"You change hands," he said finally. "Not bodies. *Hands*. Ownership. The valley's protection transfers with you — that's the keystone's cruelty. Your soul becomes the wardstone. Wherever you are kept, the valley stays safe. Which means the Pale King will not kill you, child. He will do something far worse."

"He'll keep me."

"In a cage of gold and courtesy," Bramblehoof said, "for as long as the mountains stand. And he will spend those centuries making certain you never learn what your mother learned — that a contract is a map — because the last thing a king who owns the dark needs is a cartographer drawing doors in his walls."

Wind moved through the impossible trees. Above them, the blank sky pressed down like a lid.

Freya closed the ledger and stood, and found that her legs held, and found that this surprised her less than it once would have.

"Then we'd better reach the mountain before the moon does," she said. "Because I intend to arrive holding every door he doesn't know he has."

Bramblehoof grinned — a fox grin, all teeth and thorn — and for the first time since she'd met him, it reached his eyes.

"There's just one problem with that plan," he said, glancing back toward the camp, where Dagny sat staring into the fire with the particular grimness of a man rehearsing a confession. "Because tomorrow, the ward-keeper intends to tell you what he saw the night your wards fell. And I don't think you're going to like who he saw."
