## 12. Dagny's Night of Falling Stars

Dagny had kept the valley's wards for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had never once been thanked for it.

He'd been scowled at, certainly. He'd been blamed for the frost of '09 — unfairly, since frost was weather and weather was famously indifferent to ward-craft — and he'd been complained about by every farmer whose cattle wandered too near the boundary stones and came back with their tails standing straight up for a week. Old Marta Hollis used to shake her broom at him from her porch as though he personally arranged the hum of the wards just to spoil her afternoon naps.

But thanked? No. Ward-keeping was like sweeping a floor: nobody noticed until you stopped.

So on the night of the twelfth moon-turn, when Dagny climbed the eastern ridge with his satchel of tuning-rods and his thermos of barley tea, he was in a tolerably good mood despite everything. The wards had been singing sweet all autumn. He'd checked them twice that week already, which was once more than strictly necessary, but he was getting old and old men were allowed their rituals.

That was the last ordinary thought he would have for a very long time: *I am allowed my rituals.*

The first wrongness was small. A bird — one of the wren-things that nested in the ward-stones and fed on stray magic the way pigeons fed on crumbs — sat on the third stone of the eastern arc and did not move when Dagny approached. It just looked at him. Wrens did not look at people. Wrens had better things to do.

"Out of my way, featherbrain," he said, and the bird flew off, and Dagny told himself that was that.

The second wrongness was not small at all.

The wards hummed. Everyone knew this; it was the valley's lullaby, its heartbeat, the caged-bee drone that Freya Vane had once tried to transcribe into musical notation and given up because, she said crossly, "it keeps changing key out of spite." The hum was constant. It had been constant for two hundred years, since the founders set the stones.

On the night of the twelfth moon-turn, the hum skipped.

Not faded. Not wavered. *Skipped* — the way a heart skips, the way a record skips, one clean hole in the sound where sound should be — and then resumed, and Dagny felt the hair on his arms rise beneath his coat, because he knew what a skip meant. A skip meant something had passed *through*. Something had touched the wards from inside.

Wards kept things out. That was their whole profession. But they also recorded what crossed them, the way a bell records a hand, and a skip meant a hand had been laid on the bell from within the house.

Dagny set down his tea. He knelt at the third stone and pressed his palm flat against it, the way his master had taught him, the way his master's master had taught her, and he listened with the listening that wasn't hearing — the deep listening, the one that cost him a headache every time and had cost him his marriage, according to Ingrid, who had not appreciated being married to a man who spent half his life with his hand on a rock.

And there, under the hum, he found it.

A thread. Fine as spider silk, dark as a closed door, tied around the stone's heart in a knot he did not recognize. It wasn't court-work — he knew court-work, everyone in the trade did, it had a certain gaudy elegance, all flourish and no humility. This was plainer. Patient. The kind of knot tied by someone who had learned just enough to be dangerous and practiced in secret until they were worse than dangerous.

Someone had untied the wards' memory. Someone had made a door where there was no door, and left it folded shut, waiting.

Dagny sat back on his heels in the cold grass and thought, with terrible clarity: *This is inside work.*

* * *

Here is what Dagny should have done, and he had thirty-one years and one ruined marriage and countless sleepless nights afterward to review the list:

He should have sounded the alarm. He should have rung the ward-bell, woken the council, named the sabotage aloud in front of witnesses. He should have trusted the valley with the truth of itself.

Instead, he did what careful men do. He decided to gather proof first.

"I'll find who tied it," he murmured to the stone, patting it as if it were a horse. "I'll have it undone before anyone's the wiser, and no one will have to panic, and Marta Hollis won't have anything new to shake her broom about."

It was, he would later reflect, a plan with all the structural integrity of a soufflé.

Because the thing about a folded door is that someone has to open it, and the thing about saboteurs is that they do not wait for the ward-keeper to finish his tea.

* * *

Midnight came down over the valley like a lid.

Dagny was still on the ridge, three stones along now, mapping the thread's path with chalk marks on his sleeve — it ran from the eastern arc toward the village, which narrowed matters considerably, which should have comforted him and instead made his stomach fold up like wet paper. The thread didn't wander. It went home. Whoever tied it had walked this ridge, at night, more than once, and walked it knowing the ground.

At the twelfth stroke of midnight, the stars went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out*, all of them, in the same instant, as if the sky had been a lamp and someone had pinched its wick. The darkness that followed was not night-dark. Night had texture — the grey suggestion of hills, the pale smear of the moon. This was a darkness with weight. It lay on the valley like a hand on a mouth.

And the wards screamed.

Dagny had never heard them scream. His master had never heard them scream. There was no instruction in any of his books for screaming wards, because in two hundred years the wards had never had cause. They screamed the way a dog screams when it's been hurt by someone it trusted — high, ragged, betrayed — and Dagny understood, in the marrow-deep way that comes too late to be useful, that the folded door had opened from the inside, and whatever came through had come through wearing the valley's own permission.

He ran. Thirty-one years of duty ran with him, downhill through the bracken, rods clattering in his satchel, an old man's lungs burning, toward the western arc where the scream was thinnest — toward the place where the wards were failing fastest, which meant the place where the saboteur had worked hardest, which meant —

Which meant he saw her.

Or he saw a shape he took for her. The darkness was total; the only light left in the world was the sickly green bleed of the failing wards themselves, and in that light, at the base of the seventh stone, knelt a figure with its hands raised, and the hands were *working*, fingers moving in the quick precise patterns of ward-craft, untying what he had spent his life tying.

"Hey!" Dagny shouted, and his voice cracked like a boy's. "HEY! What are you —"

The figure turned. And here was the thing Dagny could never make himself say plainly, not to the council, not to Sef, not later to Freya with her mother's ledger open between them: he *knew* the silhouette. Knew the set of the shoulders, the tilt of the head, the way the figure rose unhurried, dusting off its skirts as though it had merely dropped something in the market and not murdered the sky.

But the light guttered at that exact moment — the seventh stone gave its last green gasp and died — and the darkness swallowed the face before he could fix it, and when the wards' death-light flared again a breath later, the figure was gone, and the valley was open, and the stars were gone, and somewhere below a child was crying, and Dagny stood alone on a hillside with chalk on his sleeve and the certain knowledge that he had seen a neighbor commit treason and could not say which neighbor.

* * *

Afterward — after the stranger collapsed at Freya Vane's door, after the fox, after the exodus preparations began — the council asked him, naturally, what he had seen.

"A figure," Dagny said. "In the dark."

"Man or woman?"

"The dark," said Dagny, "was considerable."

"You're the ward-keeper," said Elder Corvin Ashleigh, folding his hands atop the council table with the patience of a man humoring a senile uncle. "Thirty-one years. Surely you can tell us something."

And oh, Dagny wanted to. He wanted to stand up and say *the saboteur knows ward-craft, real ward-craft, not hedge-craft, which means someone taught them, which means someone in this room or close to it taught them.* He wanted to say *the thread ran toward the village center, not the outskirts — your saboteur lives among us, eats beside us, sits on this council perhaps.* He wanted to say *and I froze, gentlemen, I stood on that hillside like a post while the sky was stolen, because I was afraid of naming a name I couldn't prove, and my cowardice bought the thief a clean escape.*

What he actually said was: "I'm looking into it."

Corvin nodded gravely, and Dagny saw — or thought he saw, and this was the poison of it, the thing that would keep him awake for the rest of his natural life — a flicker of something behind the elder's eyes. Relief? Amusement? Or nothing at all, nothing but the ordinary tiredness of an old man managing a crisis? Every face in the valley became suspect after that. Marta Hollis shaking her broom: suspicion or habit? The miller's too-cheerful whistle: innocence or guilt? Dagny caught himself cataloguing his neighbors like a customs officer, and hated himself for it, and kept doing it anyway, because dread is a habit that forms faster than conscience can break it.

There was one more thing. One detail he held back even from himself for a long while, the way you hold back a splinter too deep to dig.

When the figure turned in the green light — in that half-breath before the stone died — Dagny had seen its hands. Working hands. And on the left wrist, catching the ward-light, a glint. A bracelet, or a bangle, or a cuff of some pale metal, worn loose the way old folk wore them, for comfort rather than show.

Half the grandmothers in the valley wore such a thing. Half the grandfathers, probably. It proved nothing. It was a needle in a haystack of needles.

But Dagny had lived in the valley sixty-two years, and he had a cartographer's memory for details if not for maps, and there was exactly one pale-metal cuff he'd seen often enough to know its particular dent — a dent near the clasp, shaped like a crescent moon, from some long-ago accident its owner had laughed off with a story about a cellar door.

He could not, in the dark, swear the dent was there.

He could not swear it wasn't.

* * *

Now, on the road east, with the unmapped wilds doing their best to convince him geography was a rumor, Dagny sat his mule and turned the memory over for the thousandth time.

They'd made camp in the lee of a hill that had, according to Freya's increasingly profane mutterings, been a riverbed yesterday and might be a forest tomorrow. Sef was grinding salt into the stewpot with the grim rhythm of a woman punishing the stew for existing. Bramblehoof was curled by the fire in fox-shape, all ears and reproach, pretending not to listen to everything. And Freya sat apart with her mother's ledger on her knees, working at it the way she worked at stubborn coastlines — patiently, furiously, refusing to let the thing keep secrets from her.

She looked up and caught him watching her.

"Dagny." Her voice was careful. Careful the way ice is careful. "You've been quiet since the ford."

"I'm always quiet."

"You're always *grumbling*. Quiet is new. Quiet worries me." She closed the ledger but kept her hand flat on it, keeping it, the way you'd keep a wound. "You saw something that night. On the ridge. Didn't you."

Sef's grinding stopped. Even the fox's ears rotated.

Dagny looked into the fire and found, to his complete lack of surprise, that he was tired of carrying it. Thirty-one years of carrying other people's safety had apparently built muscles for this too, and the weight shifted, and shifted, and finally demanded setting down.

"I saw hands," he said. "Working the seventh stone. Unmaking it. And I saw enough of the figure to know it wasn't a stranger — strangers don't walk our ridges like they own the ground. And I saw a bracelet. Pale metal. Dent near the clasp, like a little moon."

Freya had gone very still. "Whose bracelet?"

"That's the trouble, girl. I don't know whose it *wasn't*." He rubbed his eyes. "But I know whose it might've been. I've known since the hillside, and I've been telling myself every night since that I'm an old fool seeing ghosts in shadows. And maybe I am. But there's a reason the saboteur's work pointed toward the village center. There's a reason whoever it was didn't run when I shouted — just turned around slow, like they had every right to be there." He looked up, met Freya's eyes, and said the name he had not said aloud, not once, not even to the dark:

"Elder Corvin wears a pale-metal cuff with a moon-shaped dent by the clasp. Has worn it as long as I've known him. And Corvin Ashleigh is the one who'll be waiting at the mountain's gate to meet us — because Corvin sent word ahead, before we ever left, that he'd ride out to negotiate with the Court on the valley's behalf."

The fire popped. Somewhere in the dark, the unmapped land creaked like a ship.

"He feared magic more than theft," Dagny said quietly. "Always has. Told the council for years the wards were a debt waiting to be called in. Maybe he decided to call it in himself, on terms he could live with. Or maybe I'm an old fool." He met Freya's gaze. "But either way, girl — we're walking straight toward his door. And I think you should know what's behind it before he opens it smiling."

Freya's hand tightened on the ledger until her knuckles went white.

"Then we'd better hurry," she said. "Before the thirteenth turn does it for us."
