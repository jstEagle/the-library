## 13. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

The tollhouse appeared the way most honest things do in the wilds: all at once, and where nothing should have been.

One moment we were walking a ridge of grey stone that Freya's map insisted did not exist — she had checked three times, growing more offended by the mile — and the next, the ridge simply *ended*, and there stood a little house with a crooked chimney, a lantern burning green in its window, and a sign swinging on a nail that read:

**ALL PASS. NONE FREE. TRUTHS TAKEN. LIES REFUSED.**

"Charming," said Sef.

"It's a tollhouse," Dagny said, in the flat voice of a man reading his own death warrant. "I've heard of these. Old road-magic. Older than wards."

"I know what it is," Sef said. "I'm being sarcastic. It's how I pray."

Freya had stopped dead at the crest of the ridge, her satchel of maps clutched to her chest like a shield. I watched her eyes move over the house the way they moved over everything — measuring, naming, filing away. She was doing it less now than when we'd first set out. That was the strange gift of the wilds: it taught even cartographers to stop counting and start *looking*.

"There's no road leading to it," she said. "And none leaving."

"There will be," I said, "once we've paid."

They all turned to look at me. Foxes get used to that. We are, by nature, the ones who know things slightly before everyone else, and it makes us insufferable company and excellent guides.

"You've been here before," Freya said. Not a question.

"No," I said, which was true, and also not the whole truth, and the lantern in the window flickered as if it could taste the difference. I cleared my throat, which for a fox is an elaborate production involving the whole chest. "But I know what runs them. Come on. It's going to rain."

It was not going to rain. But the sky had been the color of old pewter since the stars went out, and I wanted very much to be indoors when whatever came next arrived.

* * *

The door opened before we knocked.

Inside, the tollhouse was warm and small and smelled of cedar smoke and something sweeter underneath — honey, maybe, or memory. A fire burned in a hearth with no chimney I could find. And sitting cross-legged on the table, because chairs were apparently beneath her dignity, was the keeper.

She looked like a child of perhaps eight years. She had a round face, ink-stained fingers, and eyes that were not a child's eyes at all. They were the grey-green of deep water, and they did not blink often enough, and when they settled on you, you felt *inventoried*.

"Four travelers," she said. Her voice was a child's voice, but it came out slow, like each word had been weighed before use. "Three truths owed. One debt already carried." Her gaze slid to me and stayed there like a hook in fur. "Yours is the biggest, fox. You'll pay last. Sit down. All of you. The stew is free. Only truth costs anything here."

"The stew is free," Sef repeated, pulling out a stool with the wariness of a woman who had been poisoned at least once on principle. "Nothing under this sky is free."

"This sky has no stars," the keeper said placidly. "Under a starless sky, some things come free. It's one of the few mercies left. Hence the name." She ladled stew into four bowls without looking at any of them, and every bowl landed exactly where its owner would have wanted it. Mine had extra of the dark meat. I decided immediately that I loved her and would trust her approximately sixty percent, which for me is a marriage vow.

Dagny ate like a man who expected the meal to be confiscated. Freya ate slowly, watching the child over her spoon.

"What's your name?" Freya asked.

"That's a truth," the keeper said. "You'd be paying me to learn my own name back. Bad trade. Ask something cheaper."

Freya blinked. "You don't know your own name?"

"I know lots of names. I keep them all in jars." She gestured at a shelf crowded with small clay pots, each sealed with wax, each labeled in a spidery hand. Some of the jars hummed. One of them, near the end, rattled continuously, like something furious trapped inside. "Travelers pay me truths. Truths are heavy. Somebody has to carry them. I put them in jars so they don't wander off in the night and get into the stew."

"And what happens," Sef asked quietly, "to the truths?"

"They wait," said the keeper, "until someone needs them. Then I sell them back. At a fair price." She smiled, and it was a sweet smile, and it did not reach anywhere near those water-eyes. "Truths are like salt, witch. Everyone needs a little. Nobody wants to think about where it comes from."

Sef went very still. I filed that away, the way I file everything, in the burrow behind my ribs where I keep the things that frighten me.

* * *

Dagny paid first, because Dagny was the sort of man who volunteered for unpleasant duties the way other men volunteer for pleasant ones, and it was ruining him by inches.

He set down his spoon, folded his big hands on the table, and stared at the fire for a long moment. The keeper waited with the patience of furniture.

"A truth," he said finally. "Here's one. The night the wards fell, I wasn't asleep. I was drunk. I'd found a bottle of plum wine in the ward-house stores, older than me, and I told myself one cup for courage, because I'd been hearing the wards sing wrong for a week and nobody would listen, and I thought — I thought if I heard it wrong, then I didn't have to act. If I was drunk, then whatever happened wasn't mine to stop." His voice cracked down the middle like river ice. "So when they shattered, I was lying in the grass with wine on my chin, watching the stars go out, and my first feeling wasn't horror. It was relief. Because now I didn't have to decide anything ever again."

The silence afterward had weight. The keeper reached out with one small ink-stained hand and touched the air above the table, and something invisible peeled away from Dagny — I saw it, or half-saw it, a grey thread lifting out of his chest — and she coaxed it into an empty jar and sealed it with wax.

Dagny exhaled like a man surfacing from deep water. He looked smaller. He also looked, for the first time since I'd met him, *lighter*.

"That's why you never forgave yourself," Freya said softly. "Not the failing. The relief."

"Aye," Dagny said hoarsely, and studied his stew.

* * *

Sef paid second, and she paid like a merchant closing a bad account — quickly, precisely, and hating every second.

"I knew about the sale," she said. "Before tonight, I mean. Before the confession at the river. Here's what I haven't said: after your mother begged me to stop it, Freya — after I refused her, because refusing the Court openly meant losing everything I'd built — I went home and I slept soundly. That's the truth. Not that I failed her. That I *slept*. I have done terrible things in my life and lost sleep over most of them, and that night, the night I let a mother sell her daughter's soul, I slept like a stone in summer." Her jaw tightened. "Because some part of me thought: well. It's bought the valley another thirteen years. And thirteen years is a kind of mercy, isn't it? Even bought with a child."

Nobody spoke. The keeper drew the thread from Sef's chest — this one black-edged, oily — and jarred it.

Sef stared at her hands. "There," she said roughly. "Now you all know the worst of me. Pass the bread."

Freya passed the bread. And then, in the way that was becoming her particular genius, she said nothing at all, just sat beside the salt-witch shoulder to shoulder, and ate, and let the silence be a room they shared instead of a wall between them.

I felt something twist in my chest that had nothing to do with thorns. Dangerous, that. Very dangerous.

* * *

Freya paid last of the three, and hers was the quietest truth, and somehow the worst.

"I'm glad my mother died before I found the ledger," she said, to the fire, in a voice almost too level. "For weeks I've been telling myself I grieve her and hate the bargain separately. But the truth is there's a part of me — a small, mean, tired part — that is *relieved* I'll never have to sit across from her at breakfast knowing what I cost. That I'll never have to watch her flinch when I laugh, wondering if she's thinking about interest." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious. "I love her. And I am relieved she's dead. Both are true. I want someone else to carry one of them for a while."

The keeper took that thread gently, the way you'd take a bird from a snare, and jarred it, and then — this she did for no one else — she reached across and patted Freya's hand once, twice, like a grandmother who remembered being grandmothers.

"Some truths," the keeper said, "aren't sins. They're just weather. You can't be good or bad about weather. You can only dress for it."

Freya laughed — a wet, broken little laugh — and wiped her face with her sleeve, and something in the room loosened like a knot slipping free.

Which left me.

The keeper turned those water-eyes on me, and the fire leaned toward us, listening.

* * *

"Fox," she said. "Your turn. And before you begin your dancing — and you will dance, you always dance — remember that I take lies and turn the teller into soup stock. The sign is not decorative."

I hopped onto the bench. I arranged my tails. There are nine of them, on good days, and I arranged all nine, because if one must be flayed, one may as well be flayed beautifully.

"I'd like to negotiate—"

"No."

"A counteroffer—"

"*No.*"

I sighed. Somewhere in the burrow behind my ribs, the thing that frightened me shifted its weight.

"Fine," I said. "A truth. Here is a truth, then, and may it choke you on the way into the jar." I looked at the fire, because looking at Freya was impossible. "My thorn-master has a name. Every bound spirit serves a name, and mine has held my leash for two hundred years, and I have never spoken it aloud, because a name spoken is a name *known*, and known things can be found, and found things can be bargained with, and I have spent two centuries making sure no one alive could ever trade with the thing that owns me."

The keeper waited. The fire waited. Even the rattling jar on the shelf seemed to hold its breath.

"But that's not the truth, is it," the keeper said softly. "That's the preamble. Pay the toll, fox."

"The truth," I said, and my voice came out smaller than I wanted, "is that I *chose* him. No one caught me. No one tricked me. Two hundred years ago I was a fox of the high fells, clever and hungry and proud, and I wanted power, real power, the kind that reshapes valleys and unmakes kings, and I sought HIM out. I knelt. I asked. I signed. And when the contract came due and he sent me to carry papers to a dying woman in a warded valley — papers selling her daughter's soul — I carried them gladly, because a task from the master is a gift, and I thanked him for the honor." My claws had sunk into the bench wood. I made myself let go. "I am not the victim of my thorns. I am the *author* of them. And every day I spend with her—" my muzzle betrayed me, turning toward Freya before I wrenched it back "—every day I spend with the girl whose soul I delivered to the mountain, I understand better what I actually sold, that night on the fells. Not my service. My *self*. And I would give anything — anything — to take back the carrying of that paper. But thorn-oaths don't bend. So there. Paid in full. Jar it and be damned."

The silence rang.

Then the keeper reached into me — not cruelly, but thoroughly, the way you'd draw a splinter from a paw — and pulled the thread. It came out long, longer than the others, black shot through with a single bright strand, and it fought her, whipping like a live eel, and she wrestled it into the largest jar on the shelf and sealed it with three rings of wax and sat back, breathing hard, ink-stained fingers trembling.

"Well," she said. "That one's going to rattle."

"Serves it right," I muttered.

"Fox." Her voice changed, and the child-mask slipped, just for a heartbeat, and beneath it was something old and vast and sorry. "The name you wouldn't say. Your thorn-master's name. You kept it back."

"I paid my toll—"

"You paid *a* toll." She leaned forward. "But hear me, fox, and hear me well, because I see further down roads than most: the name is coming out anyway. Soon. Not here. Not by your choice. And when it does, everyone at this table will need to have already decided what you're worth — thorns and all — or the name will break this company like dry bread." Her eyes flicked to Freya, then back to me. "Decide fast. The mountain is close now. Three days, maybe four. And the thing waiting at its gate knows your master's name too — and it *wants* you to say it."

The fire popped. Outside, somewhere on the nonexistent road, something began to walk toward the tollhouse — slow, unhurried footsteps, the tread of a traveler with all the time in the world and no intention of paying for anything.

The keeper rose, and every jar on the shelf began to hum at once.

"Speaking of which," she said pleasantly, "you'll want to leave through the back door. Now. Tollhouse rules: I sell truths, not deaths, and that thing out there doesn't buy — it only takes."

Through the front window, green lantern-light fell across the ridge, and on the ridge stood a figure in a cloak stitched from unlit stars, and it raised one hand — politely, almost kindly — and knocked.
