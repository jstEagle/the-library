## 14. Hollow Men at the Ford

The river found them before they found the river.

Freya knew it first by sound — a low, wrong murmur, like a crowd whispering behind a wall — and then by smell, that cold mineral breath of deep water. But it was Dagny who stopped dead on the trail, one hand rising as if to press against glass, and said, "That's not how rivers are supposed to sound."

"It's running backward," Bramblehoof said, from somewhere near Freya's knee. He had taken fox shape for the morning's travel, his thorn-crown of black brambles flattened against his skull like a bad mood. "Or sideways. Or possibly inside out. The canyon does what it likes."

They had been three days in the reversed country, following the canyon floor where the water climbed instead of fell, pooling in impossible ledges above their heads, hanging in slow silver ropes that swayed without wind. Freya had filled eleven pages of her field book. She had also, she admitted privately, stopped sleeping well. There was something deeply offensive to a cartographer about a river you could not trust to fall.

"The ford is ahead," Sef said. She walked at the front, as she always did now, her salt-gray braid swinging, her staff tapping the stones in a rhythm Freya had come to recognize as *counting*. "Where the water crosses itself. It's the only place in this stretch where the current forgets which way it's going long enough for anything to get across."

"And after the ford?" Freya asked.

"After the ford," Sef said, "the ground starts remembering which way is down, and we make better time." She glanced back, and her eyes — pale as brine — caught Freya's and held them a moment longer than the answer required. "We cross quick and quiet. No fires tonight until we're well past."

"You've crossed here before," Dagny said. It wasn't quite a question.

"I've crossed most places," Sef said, and tapped her staff twice, and that was the end of the conversation, as far as she was concerned.

Freya had learned to read these small evasions the way she read contour lines — not for what they showed, but for what they implied. Sef's silences had shapes. Lately the shapes had been getting larger. And lately, when the salt-witch thought no one watched, she would stand facing back the way they'd come, very still, like a woman listening for footsteps.

* * *

The ford was exactly as strange as promised: a place where the climbing river met itself coming down, the two currents tangling into a broad, shallow sheet of water that lay flat and honest over gray stones. For perhaps fifty paces, the water simply *was water*. Freya felt her shoulders unknot at the sight of it. Something ordinary. Something mappable.

"Boots off or boots on?" Dagny asked, already unlacing.

"Boots on," Sef said sharply. "Move fast. Wet feet dry; dead feet don't."

"Charming," said Bramblehoof, shaking out into his tall, thorn-wreathed man-shape so he could wade. Water beaded off him strangely, sliding around the brambles as though afraid of them. "You know, in the court we had bridges for this sort of thing. Marble ones. Very dramatic. Musicians on the banks."

"How nice for the court," Sef said.

"It was, actually. That was rather the problem with it."

Freya laughed despite herself — a short, startled sound — and Bramblehoof flashed her his sharp-toothed grin, and for one moment the crossing was almost pleasant. The water came to mid-thigh, cold enough to make her gasp, but steady. Honest. She was halfway across, watching Dagny angle upstream of her the way he'd insisted, muttering about undertow even in a river that ran uphill, when Bramblehoof stopped.

He didn't say anything. He just stopped, mid-stride, and his head turned slowly toward the far bank, and every bramble on his body stood up straight as hackles.

"Sef," he said quietly. "How many crossings did you say this river had?"

"One," Sef said. Her staff had gone still. "Why?"

"Because I count four people standing on the far bank," Bramblehoof said, "and none of them were there a moment ago, and none of them are casting shadows."

Freya looked. At first she saw nothing — just the gray shingle and the tangle of willow scrub beyond. Then the light shifted, or her eyes did, and there they were: four figures, upright and still, spaced along the bank with the evenness of fence posts. They were man-shaped but wrong, the way a footprint is wrong when it's pressed into wax — an impression of a person rather than a person. Their surfaces moved faintly, like smoke under cloth. Where faces should have been there was only a smooth hollow, and looking at those hollows made Freya's eyes ache, made her want to look away and made her unable to.

"Hollow Men," Dagny breathed. His hand found the ward-chalk at his belt and gripped it hard enough that Freya heard the stick creak. "I thought — I'd heard stories, but I thought—"

"They're real," Sef said. Her voice had changed. Flat. Cold. A voice from some other room of her life. "And they're not here to talk."

One of the figures raised its arm — not quickly, not slowly, with the terrible smoothness of something that has never once hesitated — and pointed. Not at Sef. Not at Dagny or Bramblehoof.

At Freya.

"*There*," it said, and its voice was the crowd-whisper from the canyon walls, a hundred murmurs braided into one word. "*The thirteenth payment. The debt walks. Take the debt.*"

"Run," Sef said. "Back — no. Forward. Forward and apart, they move worse when you scatter—" 

The Hollow Men stepped into the water.

They didn't walk in. They simply began, all four at once, to be in the river, the way a drawing begins to be on a page, and the water around them went still and gray and dead as pewter. Freya turned and plunged for the bank they'd left, because forward meant wading through four of them, and terror is not a strategist. Behind her she heard Dagny shouting her name, heard Bramblehoof snarl — a sound like tearing canvas — and heard Sef's voice cut through everything:

"FREYA. *Forward.*"

She didn't know why she obeyed. Later she would tell herself it was trust. In the moment it was simpler: Sef's voice had the same authority as the wards had once had, the voice of something that held the world's edges in place, and Freya's legs answered it before her mind did. She wheeled in the chest-deep water and drove toward the far bank, straight at the nearest Hollow Man.

It reached for her.

Its hand passed through the water without disturbing it and closed on her forearm, and the cold that went through her wasn't cold like ice. It was cold like *absence* — like the moment the stars went out, like the silence where the wards' hum used to be. She felt it reaching for something inside her, feeling along her ribs for the door of her, and she heard herself scream, and she heard Dagny roar, and then—

Then the world turned white and sharp as frost.

Sef came past her through the water like a blade coming out of a sheath. Freya had seen the salt-witch work before — little things, practical things: salt lines, preserving charms, a pinch thrown to test for poison. She had thought that was what Sef *was*. A hedge-witch. A grumbler with a staff and a bag of tricks.

What rose out of the ford was not a hedge-witch.

Sef spoke a word that Freya's mind refused to hold — a syllable shaped like the space between stars — and flung both arms wide, and the river *answered*. Every drop of water in the ford leapt upward at once, a roaring silver wall, and hung there. Hung. The Hollow Men froze inside it like insects in amber, their smooth faces pressed against the underside of a sea that had forgotten to fall. And Sef stood beneath it, hair whipping in a wind that touched no one else, and her eyes had gone white as her braid, and when she spoke again her voice came from everywhere, layered, ancient, *courtly*.

"*By the tide that bore me, by the crown I forswore — you will not have her.*"

The words landed on Freya like physical weight. *The crown I forswore.* She stared, mouth full of river-spray, at the small stooped woman who had shared their fire for two weeks, who complained about her knees and hoarded honey, and understood that she had never once seen her.

The Hollow Men struggled in the suspended water. One of them opened the hollow of its face — and sang. It was the worst sound Freya had ever heard, a note of pure unbeing, and the great wall of water trembled, and cracks of gray spread through it like frost on a window.

"Sef!" Dagny shouted. "It's unraveling your working — you can't hold all four—"

"I don't need to hold all four," Sef said, in her own voice again, tight and human and shaking. "I need to hold *this*." She thrust her staff down into the riverbed, and the water-wall collapsed — but not evenly. It collapsed *inward*, onto the Hollow Men, crushing them down, down, into the stones of the ford, folding them into the gravel like hands pressing dough. The gray shapes dimmed, guttered, and went out. The river, released, came down all at once with a crash that soaked everyone to the skin and knocked Bramblehoof — who had been mid-leap toward the fight with both thorn-arms extended — flat on his face in six inches of water.

For a moment there was only the sound of the river relearning its business, and four people breathing hard.

Then Sef pulled her staff from the stones, turned her back on all of them, and walked up onto the far bank without a word.

* * *

They made camp early, in the lee of a leaning stone, because no one suggested going farther and no one dared suggest stopping either. Dagny got a fire going with hands that weren't quite steady. Bramblehoof, wrung out and sulking in equal measure, sat close to the flames and dripped.

Freya found Sef at the edge of the firelight, sitting alone, arms wrapped around her staff, staring at the dark.

"That word you said," Freya said, sitting down beside her. "The first one. I couldn't hold onto it."

"Good," Sef said. "Hold onto it too long and it holds onto you."

"*The crown I forswore.*" Freya kept her voice gentle. "Sef. What were you, in the court?"

Sef was quiet long enough that the fire popped twice. When she finally spoke, she didn't look at Freya. She looked at the darkness beyond, back the way they'd come, the way they were going.

"I told you I was exiled. That part was true. I told you I was a salt-witch, and that's true too — salt is honest work, it keeps things, it preserves. It's the opposite of everything down there." Her thumb moved along the staff, back and forth. "But I didn't tell you that I was *trained* there. Raised there. That the Pale King himself taught me the word I used today. That I wore his colors for thirty years and did things in his name that I will not tell you tonight, because you'd look at me the way you're trying very hard not to look at me right now."

"I'm not—"

"You are," Sef said, not unkindly. "And you should. A little fear is healthy. What you saw today — that's not the deepest I go. That's the *shallow* end, girl. I swore off all of it when I walked out, and I've kept that oath for forty years, and today I broke it in front of witnesses because you were dying in a river." She sighed, long and slow. "So now you know. Your guide is a creature of the court you're walking toward. Sleep on that."

Freya looked at her — at the bowed shoulders, the white braid, the hands that had held back a river. Fear was there, yes, cold in her stomach. But underneath it, something else.

"When it reached for me," Freya said, "inside. It was looking for something. Like it knew exactly what it was sent to collect."

Sef's jaw tightened. "It did."

"And you knew what they were. You knew their voices. You knew *before we ever saw them* that we shouldn't camp past the ford." Freya leaned closer. "Sef. How long have you known they were coming? How long have you known *what* the King sends for me?"

Sef finally turned and looked at her, and in the firelight her pale eyes were tired in a way that had nothing to do with age.

"Longer than I've been honest about," she said. "And there's more I haven't been honest about. About the night your mother made her bargain. About who begged whom. About what I did, and what I failed to do." She looked away again. "Ask me tomorrow. Ask me when we're past the ford-country and I can't use the road as an excuse. I'll tell you everything, Freya Vane. I owe your mother that much, and I owe you more."

From across the fire, Bramblehoof's ears pricked. He said nothing, but his eyes — amber, ancient, knowing — flicked from Sef to Freya and back, and his tail curled slowly around his feet like a closing parenthesis.

Dagny fed the fire another branch and said, quietly, to no one in particular: "Funny thing about secrets. They're like the river here. Run uphill long enough, and eventually they come down all at once."

No one answered him. But nobody slept well, either — and in the small hours, Freya woke to see Sef standing watch, facing not the wilds ahead, but the valley behind, her lips moving around a word Freya's mind refused to hold.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, something whispered back.
