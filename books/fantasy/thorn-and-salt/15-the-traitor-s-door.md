## 15. The Traitor's Door

Dagny had kept watch over the valley for thirty-one years, and in all that time he had learned exactly three things worth teaching: wards are honest, people are not, and never trust a stew Sef makes when she's sulking.

The third lesson had been learned only four days ago, and he was still recovering.

"More salt," Sef said, stirring the pot with the grim efficiency of a woman burying evidence. "It's thin."

"It's a punishment," Dagny said. "We all know it's a punishment. Just say it's a punishment and let us eat with dignity."

Freya laughed from across the fire — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind she'd been rationing like a miser since the night the stars went out. Bramblehoof, curled at the edge of the light in his fox shape, lifted his head and said, "I think it's lovely," which fooled no one, because fox spirits cannot lie about food. It was one of the few laws of their nature, and Sef had once explained, with visible satisfaction, that this was why the Court Beneath the Mountain kept them on such short leashes.

They were camped in the lee of a granite shoulder called, according to Freya's maps, *the Old Man's Elbow*, though no map could explain why. The mountain itself stood two days' march north, its peak lost in cloud that did not behave like cloud. It sat wrong against the sky. Dagny had been staring at it since sundown with the particular dread of a man who has finally finished a puzzle and wishes he hadn't.

Because the pieces fit now. All of them. And they fit around a face he knew.

"Dagny." Freya's voice pulled him back. "You've been quiet even for you. What's wrong?"

He considered lying. He was, by his own admission, rather good at it — a ward-keeper learns to tell frightened families that everything is fine right up until the moment it very much is not. But he had spent eleven chapters of his life — no, he corrected himself wearily, eleven *days* — traveling beside these three, and somewhere along the salt flats and the uphill river and the tollhouse where he'd paid a truth he still hadn't told anyone but the oracle, lying to them had stopped being possible.

So instead he reached into his coat and drew out the thing he'd been carrying for twelve days without telling a soul: a shard of ward-stone, no bigger than a thumbnail, wrapped in linen.

"What's that?" Sef asked, her spoon going still.

"The heart of the valley's border-ward. Or what was left of it after the twelfth bell." He unwrapped it. In the firelight the stone was dull as river gravel, but when he turned it, a hairline seam of silver caught the light — a fracture line, clean and deliberate. "Wards don't break like this. Wards fray. They wear thin at the corners first, like old cloth. This was cut. From the inside."

Bramblehoof sat up fully. "You said the wards failed."

"I said what everyone wanted to hear." Dagny turned the shard in his fingers. "The truth is I found this the morning after, and I've known since then that someone opened the door from within. Someone who knew the ward-song. Someone who had access to the anchor stones." He looked up, and found Freya watching him with those cartographer's eyes that missed nothing and forgave slowly. "There are only four people in the valley who know the full ward-song. Me. My predecessor, dead six years. A girl in the eastern holdings who's nine years old. And Elder Maren Colm."

The silence stretched long enough that the fire popped twice.

"Maren," Freya said at last. Her voice was very level, which Dagny had learned was worse than shouting. "Maren taught me my letters. She paid for my mother's funeral feast out of her own stores. She—" She stopped. Started again. "She held me when I cried after the burial."

"I know," Dagny said gently.

"She's seventy years old."

"That doesn't stop her from being afraid. Fear doesn't check your age at the door." He set the shard down on a flat stone between them, an exhibit in a trial only he had been conducting. "Think it through. Who benefits from the wards falling? Not the Hollow Men. Not the King. They can't cross into the valley while the wards stand — that's the entire point of them. But if the wards fell *from inside*, quietly, on the eve of the thirteenth turn..." He let the thought hang.

Sef got there first, because Sef always got there first when the answer was ugly. "Then whoever cut them wasn't letting magic in. They were making sure the debt came due. Making sure the contract couldn't fail." She set down her spoon. "Someone in the valley *wanted* the collection to happen."

"Why would anyone want that?" Freya asked, and her voice cracked on the last word, and Dagny hated himself a little for what he had to say next.

"Because the alternative was worse. Because maybe they believed the wards would hold another thirteen years if the payment was made. Because maybe they decided one soul was a fair price for ten thousand lives, and they didn't have the stomach to say so out loud, so they did it in the dark with a knife made of song." He met her eyes. "That's not a monster, Freya. That's a neighbor. Monsters are easier."

* * *

Nobody slept well after that, which Dagny supposed was fair, since he hadn't let them sleep well for the past hour. By grey dawn they were walking again, and the mountain had grown until it filled half the sky, its slopes stitched with roads that appeared and vanished depending on whether the mountain felt like being seen.

It was Freya, naturally, who worked out the rest of it, because Freya's mind worked like a net thrown over the world — everything caught, everything sorted.

"The gate," she said, stopping mid-stride so abruptly that Bramblehoof walked into the back of her pack. "Dagny, the gate. The mountain only opens its gate to those who carry a claim. That's how the court works — Sef told us, at the ford. You can't just walk in. You need standing. A contract, a debt, a name written in their ledger." She turned, and her face in the flat morning light was terrible and clear. "If Maren cut the wards, she didn't do it from here. She'd have to be there. At the gate. To present the claim herself — to make sure the collection went through properly. To *witness* it."

"She's meeting us there," Dagny said. It wasn't a question. He'd known it since midnight; hearing it aloud just gave it weight.

"She's waiting for us," Bramblehoof said softly. His fur had gone bristled along the spine, thorns showing through like dark teeth. "Or she's waiting for *you*, little mapmaker. The debtor arrives, the creditor's witness stands at the door, the moon turns, and everything is done neat and lawful and no one's hands are dirty except the dead woman's, and hers are already cold."

Freya's jaw tightened. "My mother isn't here to defend herself."

"No," Bramblehoof agreed. "But her ledger is. And ledgers, unlike mothers, get to speak."

They walked on. Dagny fell to the rear, as he always did, and spent the morning doing arithmetic he didn't want to do. Two days to the gate. One day, if they pushed hard, and they would push hard, because the moon was a sliver now, a paring of itself, and tomorrow night it would complete its thirteenth turn. Which meant that tonight was the last night before everything, and whatever he decided about Maren Colm, he would have to decide it soon, alone, in the dark, the way all his worst decisions had always been made.

* * *

They made camp early, in a hollow of black pine, and Dagny volunteered for first watch mostly to escape the sound of Sef trying to make conversation. She was bad at it in a way that was almost endearing — she treated small talk like a military campaign, advancing on topics and seizing them.

"You're going to do something stupid," she said, appearing at his elbow an hour into the watch, silent as spilled flour.

"Good evening to you too."

"Don't deflect. I'm a court-trained witch, Dagny. I can smell a bad plan the way you can smell rain." She settled onto the rock beside him, uninvited, and stared up at the starless sky where the stars should have been. Even after twelve days, the absence of them made his eyes water. "You're going to go ahead. Tonight or at first light. Alone. Confront her yourself, before the rest of us arrive, because you think it's your shame to carry."

Dagny said nothing, which was confirmation enough.

"Is it true?" Sef asked. Quieter now. "What you told us this morning — that only four people know the ward-song?"

"It's true."

"Then why does it matter so much that it's *you* who goes?" She turned to look at him, and in the dark her eyes had the faint salt-sheen they got when the old training rose in her. "You weren't the one who failed the wards, Dagny. Whoever cut them failed them. You just found the body, so to speak."

"That's exactly it." He rubbed his face with both hands. "Thirty-one years I kept those wards. Thirty-one years of walking the border every dusk, singing the anchors awake, checking every stone twice because my teacher checked them twice and his teacher before him. And the one night it mattered — the one night in three decades when something actually *came* — I was asleep. Warm in my bed. While some old woman I've shared bread with for thirty years knelt in the dark and murdered the thing I was sworn to keep." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "I keep thinking: if I'd been walking the border that night, I'd have seen her. I'd have heard the song change. I could have stopped it. Freya would still have her soul, and her mother's bargain would have bought another thirteen years, and none of us would be marching toward a hole in a mountain."

"And if you'd caught her," Sef said, "she'd have cut the wards another night. Or another. People like that don't stop, Dagny. They just wait for better weather." She paused. "But that's not really why you want to go alone, is it?"

No. It wasn't.

"Because if it's her," Dagny said, "then I have to decide what happens to her. And I don't trust myself to decide it in front of Freya. Because Freya will want justice, and she'll be right to want it, and justice for cutting the wards that sold a child's soul is—" He stopped. Swallowed. "It's a steep price, Sef. And Maren Colm taught me the second verse of the ward-song when I was fourteen and couldn't get the rhythm. She stayed up three nights doing it. She fed me honey-bread and told me I sang like a dying goose but I sang it *true*, and true was what mattered." He laughed, and it came out broken. "How do you weigh that? How do you put honey-bread on one side of the scale and a soul on the other?"

Sef was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had an odd gentleness he'd never heard from her.

"I served the Pale King for twenty years. I told you that at the tollhouse — well, I told the oracle, and you eavesdropped, which is nearly the same as telling you." She picked up a pebble and turned it over in her fingers. "In twenty years I did things I will spend the rest of my life not describing. And the person who finally pulled me out of it wasn't a judge. It wasn't justice. It was a grumpy old exile named Odell who owed me nothing, who had every reason to hand me to the court and didn't, who just said, 'Come home, then, if you mean to be better.' That's it. That was the whole speech." She flicked the pebble into the dark. "Justice would have been cleaner. Justice would have been *deserved*. But I'm sitting here, and Odell's mercy is the reason, and I have never once regretted that he chose wrong by the law and right by me."

"So I should forgive her."

"I didn't say that. I said you shouldn't decide alone, in the dark, before you've even heard her speak." Sef stood, brushing pine needles from her cloak. "Fear makes people do monstrous things, Dagny. I know, because fear is what took me into the King's service in the first place. If Maren cut those wards, she did it terrified — terrified for the valley, maybe, or terrified of something we haven't seen yet. Terrified people deserve to be *heard* before they're weighed." She glanced back at him. "And if you go alone and it goes badly, you won't just lose your life. You'll lose the chance to come back and tell Freya the truth yourself. She's earned that. Don't rob her of it to protect your own guilt."

She left him alone with the watch and the missing stars, which was, he reflected, the cruelest kindness available.

* * *

He almost went anyway.

He had the pack half-packed at moonrise — bread, the ward-shard, his grandfather's knife that he'd never once used on anything but cheese — when Bramblehoof materialized out of the shadows directly in front of him, which was deeply unfair, because fox spirits were supposed to announce themselves.

"Going somewhere?" the fox asked pleasantly.

"Bramblehoof—"

"If you say 'this doesn't concern you,' I will scream, and Sef will wake up, and then *nobody* gets to do anything stupid tonight." The fox sat, tail curling neatly over his feet, thorns glinting. "Sit down, ward-keeper. Let me tell you about oaths."

Dagny sat.

"I am bound by thorns grown into the meat of me to deliver Freya Vane to the Court Beneath the Mountain. Do you understand what that means? It means that every day I walk beside her, wanting to protect her, my own body is a leash pulling the other way. Every day I choose her over my oath, the thorns tighten. I have been choosing her for twelve days." He said it simply, without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "And here is what I have learned about torn loyalties, Dagny, so listen closely: the moment you start sneaking off alone to settle things privately, you have already chosen. You've chosen your own conscience over the people who trusted you with theirs. Maybe you're right. Maybe you're even noble. But noble and alone is just a longer word for *gone*."

"They'll try to stop me."

"Yes. Because they love you. It's very annoying. I experience it constantly." Bramblehoof tilted his head. "Besides — and I say this with great respect — you are a sixty-year-old man whose primary weapon is a cheese knife, planning to confront a traitor at the gates of the most dangerous court in creation, alone, at night. If I were writing this as a story, I would have you eaten by page three."

"There aren't pages in life."

"There are *consequences*, which rhyme." The fox rose and shook himself, thorns rattling like dry seedpods. "Tomorrow we reach the gate together. Tomorrow you look Maren Colm in the face with Freya standing beside you, and whatever justice comes, it comes in daylight, among family. That's not weakness, Dagny. That's the whole point of having any."

Dagny looked at the packed bag. He looked at the fox. He looked, finally, up at the empty sky, where the stars ought to have been and weren't, and thought about the valley behind them, sleeping under wards that no longer existed, trusting him still.

He unpacked the bag.

"Thank you," he said gruffly.

"Don't mention it. Literally. If you mention it I'll deny everything and claim you were sleepwalking toward the privy." Bramblehoof trotted a few steps away, then paused, ears pricked. "Also — Dagny? For what it's worth. When we get to the gate, and you see her, and it's truly her — you may find that the hardest part isn't deciding what she deserves." His voice dropped, and for once there was no humor in it at all. "It's discovering she already knows what she deserves, and has known since the night she did it, and has been carrying it alone ever since. Guilt like that is its own prison. Sometimes the punishment fits so well the jailer starts to pity the prisoner."

He melted into the dark, and Dagny sat alone with the watch and the words, and did not sleep, and did not leave, and counted the hours until dawn.

* * *

They reached the gate at midday on the following day, and it was worse than anything Dagny had imagined, because imagination had at least left room for doubt.

The mountain's gate was a wound of black glass in the living rock, tall as a cathedral door, and before it, on a shelf of bare stone, stood a small figure in a grey traveling cloak, leaning on a shepherd's crook, watching the road.

Watching *them*.

Elder Maren Colm had aged ten years in the month since Dagny had last seen her — no, that wasn't right, he realized as they closed the distance. She hadn't aged. She had been *hollowed*. Her face was the same face that had sung him the ward-song's second verse, but the person behind it had gone somewhere else, and what remained was a woman standing very straight at a door she had opened, waiting to account for it.

Beside him, Freya made a small sound — not quite a word — and Dagny's hand found her shoulder without his permission.

"Freyalass," Maren said, when they were near enough. Her voice was steady. Only her hands, white-knuckled on the crook, betrayed her. "You have your mother's way of walking. Like the ground owes you nothing and you intend to prove it."

"You cut the wards." Freya's voice didn't shake. Dagny marveled at that, and grieved it. "Say it. Say it to my face."

"I did." No flinching. No excuses yet. Just the plain terrible fact, laid out between them like the ward-shard on the flat stone. "Twelve days ago, at the second bell, I sang the anchor-stone open and I cut the valley's protection with my own hands, and I have not slept a whole night since, and I would do it again." Her eyes moved past Freya, past Sef, past the bristling fox, and settled on Dagny. "Hello, Dagny. You always did check the stones twice. I counted on it."

"On *me*?" The word came out of him like a struck bell. "You used my rounds? You waited for my—"

"I waited for the one night in thirty-one years you allowed yourself to rest, because I knew you'd earned it, and because I could not have done it with you watching." Something moved behind her eyes then — not defiance. Something rawer. "I'm sorry for that most of all, if you want the truth of it. Of all the sins on my ledger, using your trust was the one I prayed forgiveness for, and I don't pray."

"Why?" Freya stepped forward, and the mountain's gate hummed behind Maren like a held breath. "Why, Maren? She was my *mother*. I was a *child*. What could possibly—"

"Because I read the ledger too, Freyalass." And now Maren's voice did break, just slightly, like ice underfoot. "Your mother showed it to me, the winter before she died. Thirteen payments. Twelve made. And I sat with that book in my lap and I did the sums, the same as you did, and I thought: *thirteen years*. We have thirteen years to find another way. And then the years went by, one after another, and nobody found another way, and I grew old, and the moon kept counting, and I realized — with the twelfth turn coming, and my bones failing, and no one else in that valley who even *believed* in the debt — that if the payment failed, the wards wouldn't just fall." She looked up at the starless sky. "Everything the King promised in the contract's fine print comes due. Not just the valley. The *valley and everything in it*. Every soul, every field, every memory. Total. Forever. Unless the debt was honored."

"So you fed it a child," Sef said quietly.

"So I fed it the child whose mother had already sold her," Maren answered, and the cruelty of it landed on all of them at once, "because the alternative was feeding it everyone, and I am an old woman who loves ten thousand people and could only save them one way, and I have hated myself every single day since, and I will hate myself at the gate of heaven if I ever reach it." She straightened, and turned to face the black glass door, and Dagny saw that her shadow on the stone had begun, very faintly, to glow. "The moon turns tomorrow night. I am here to witness the collection, and to make sure it is done *gently*, because that is the only mercy left to give. And now you're here, and I don't know whether that ruins everything or saves it, because—"

The gate shuddered.

Not opened — *shuddered*, the way a dog shudders before it barks, and from somewhere deep beneath the mountain came a sound like a thousand bells ringing underwater, and Maren went pale as the King himself.

"That's early," she whispered. "That's *early*. The turn isn't until tomorrow — the gate doesn't answer until the turn — unless—" She turned to Freya, and her ancient face was suddenly, terribly urgent. "Unless something on the other side already knows you've arrived. Child, listen to me. Whatever happens next, whatever comes through that door — remember that the contract names *one* soul. Yours. As long as you're the one it wants, the rest of you can still run."

The black glass began, slowly, to swing inward.

Dagny's hand was still on Freya's shoulder. He tightened it, once, and stepped forward — past her, between her and the opening dark — an old man with a cheese knife, choosing, at last, where to stand.
