## 16. Descent Beneath the Mountain

The mountain did not have a door. It had a mouth.

Freya stood at the edge of it with her map satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, staring up at the sheer black face of stone that rose out of the earth without any of the courtesy of foothills. It simply *was*, the way grief was, the way debts were — abrupt and total and older than anyone who had to deal with it.

"That's not on any map," she said, mostly to herself, because saying it aloud made it slightly less like a hallucination. "I've surveyed every range within four hundred leagues. There is no mountain here."

"There is now," Sef said grimly. She had salt dusted along both forearms, a habit Freya had come to understand meant *trouble ahead*. "The Court doesn't sit in the world, girl. It sits *under* it, and it only bothers to be findable when it wants something."

"It wants me," Freya said.

"Aye." Sef didn't dress it up. She never did. That was one of the things Freya loved about her, buried somewhere under all the things that terrified her. "So let's not keep royalty waiting."

Bramblehoof padded up beside her, his coat bristling with thorns that caught the grey light and held it. In his fox shape he was barely knee-high, but he moved like something much larger wearing a small disguise. He looked at the mountain's mouth for a long moment, and then at Freya.

"Once we're inside," he said, "the thorns will know where I am. He will feel me coming." His voice was rough gravel wrapped in velvet; even now, after weeks of travel, it startled her a little each time. "Whatever happens below, Freya Vane — whatever you see me do — remember that I chose to walk in beside you. The rest is contract."

"I know," Freya said.

"You say that very easily for someone who's about to be surrounded by things that eat certainty for breakfast."

"I'm a cartographer," she said. "Uncertainty is my native country."

Something flickered across his sharp face — she would have called it a smile if foxes smiled — and then he turned and led them into the dark.

* * *

The passage down was not carved so much as *grown*, as though the mountain had swallowed a cathedral and digested it into something stranger. The walls were geode, but starless — great hollows of crystal that should have glittered and instead drank the light of Dagny's ward-lantern and gave nothing back. Freya caught herself running her fingers along the stone as they walked, cataloguing out of habit, and made herself stop. Mapping this place felt dangerously like inviting it to map her back.

Dagny brought up the rear, lantern high. He'd been quiet since the ford — quiet since Sef had burned three Hollow Men into salt statues with magic that made Freya's teeth ache to remember — but his silence now had a working quality to it. He was counting turns in the tunnel. She could tell by his lips moving.

"How far?" she asked him.

"Half a league down, by my reckoning," he said. "Though I don't trust my reckoning anymore. My reckoning let a traitor through the wards, so my reckoning can go hang."

"Dagny—"

"Don't." He said it gently, which was worse. "You're kind, Freya Vane, kinder than this journey deserves, but I'll take absolution from no one until I've put right what I failed to keep whole. Walk. Count. That's all I'm good for just now."

Freya walked. And counted. It was, she reflected, a strange kind of family they'd become — a disgraced keeper, an exile witch, a bound spirit, and a woman who'd learned too late that her mother had paid for her childhood with the only coin that mattered. None of them blood-kin. All of them, somehow, hers.

The air grew warmer. Then sweeter. Then wrong — a smell like honey poured over iron, rich enough to make her stomach turn over with longing for something she couldn't name. Bramblehoof stopped so abruptly she nearly stepped on his tail.

"We're close," he said. "Listen."

Freya listened. At first there was nothing but their own breathing and the drip of water somewhere far below. And then, threading through the silence, came a sound that made the hairs on her arms stand up: singing. Hundreds of voices, wordless, layered, beautiful in the way a blade is beautiful. A choir with no mouths she could see.

"The court," Bramblehoof said. "It sings when it's hungry."

"Wonderful," Sef muttered. "I'd forgotten the acoustics."

"You've been here before." It wasn't a question. Freya had pieced together enough over the leagues. "When you served him."

Sef's jaw worked. "I sang in that choir once. Long ago. Before I understood what the songs were *for*." She looked at Freya, and her eyes in the lantern light were old and tired and honest in the way people are honest when lying has stopped being worth the effort. "Whatever you hear down there telling you how lovely it would be to stay — don't listen with your heart open. That's all I'll say. That's more than I've said to anyone in thirty years."

They descended into the song.

* * *

The Court Beneath the Mountain opened before them like a wound in the world, and Freya forgot, for one full breath, how to be afraid.

The cavern was vast beyond measuring — and she *tried* to measure it, instinctively, sighted along the walls for some reference point and found none, because the walls curved away into darkness the way the sea curves away from a cliff. The floor was polished geode, black shot through with veins of violet and silver, and it reflected everything above it so perfectly that walking across it felt like walking on the night sky of some colder, crueler world.

And hanging above — filling the cavern with cold, shifting, captive light — were stars.

Hundreds of them. Thousands. Each one caged in a lattice of black iron no larger than a birdcage, suspended from chains that ran up into the dark until the chains themselves vanished. They burned and wheeled and strained, and their light fell through the iron bars in stripes, striping the floor, striping the dancers below.

Because there were dancers. Courtiers in robes the color of deep water, moving through slow figures beneath the caged constellations, their shadows fractured into cages of their own. The music came from everywhere and nowhere. And none of them, Freya noticed with a chill that started in her spine and spread outward, cast reflections in the floor. Only the light did. Only the stars.

"Oh," she breathed. "Oh, that's where they went."

"What?" Dagny asked.

"The stars. All of them. Every star that went out on the twelfth turn — he didn't extinguish them. He *collected* them." Her voice shook. She was a cartographer; she knew what it meant to look at a thing that rearranged your understanding of the world, and this was the largest such thing she had ever seen. "That's the Hunter overhead. That's the Weeping Sisters. That—" her throat closed, "that's the constellation my mother named me under. The Cartographer's Chain. He has it hanging over his *table*."

She pointed, and yes — far across the cavern, on a dais of black crystal, a long table was laid as if for a feast, and above it hung thirteen stars in a line, dimmer than the rest, as though they'd been burning longest.

Bramblehoof made a low sound in his chest. "Thirteen," he said quietly. "One for each payment."

"They're not payments." Freya heard her own voice go strange and hard. "They're *receipts*."

A horn sounded somewhere — a single low note that vibrated in her sternum — and the dancers stopped mid-figure, all together, all at once, like puppets whose strings had been plucked still. Every faceless mask in the cavern turned toward the four of them standing small and dusty at the edge of the light.

"Well," said Sef, rolling her shoulders, "no sneaking past that, then."

"No," said a voice from everywhere, warm as banked coals and cold as the space between stars. "There never was."

The Pale King came down from his dais, and Freya made herself watch every step.

* * *

He was not tall. That was the first obscenity of him. Stories had built him into a giant, a colossus, a shadow on the sky, and instead he was merely a man-shaped absence, elegant and spare, in robes that seemed cut from the same starless geode as his kingdom. His face was pleasant. That was the second obscenity. He looked like someone's favorite uncle, like a scholar, like the sort of person who would help you carry a heavy basket and mean it.

His eyes were holes full of caged light.

"Freya Vane." He said her name the way she might say the name of a rare map — with appreciation, with possession, with the particular pleasure of a collector confirming an acquisition. "Daughter of Maren. Cartographer. You've brought me my inheritance."

"She brought herself," Sef said, stepping forward, "which is more than owed, and less than you'll get. We've come to discuss terms, Majesty. Or have you forgotten what terms look like? You wrote enough of them once."

The King's gaze slid to her, and something ancient moved behind it. "Salt-witch. You left without giving notice. The choir remembers."

"Let it hold its grudge in key," Sef said, "and mind the matter at hand."

For a moment Freya thought the whole cavern would crack open with tension — the masked courtiers frozen, the caged stars straining against their lattices, Bramblehoof's thorns creaking audibly as his oath dragged him two steps forward toward the King whether he willed it or not.

But the King only smiled, and gestured, and chairs appeared at his feast table as though they had always been there. Four of them. As though he had been expecting exactly four.

"Then let us discuss terms," he said pleasantly. "Over supper. I have waited thirteen years for this meal; I see no reason to rush it now. Sit, cartographer. Eat. You must be hungry — the descent always takes it out of mortals." He drew back her chair himself, an unhurried courtesy that was somehow the most frightening thing yet, because it meant he considered her already seated at his table in every way that mattered.

"And while we eat," the Pale King continued, folding himself onto his throne-chair at the head of the table, beneath the thirteen dimmed stars that had been bought and paid for, one soul per year of valley peace, "you may look around my court all you like. Admire my collection. I understand you're a woman who appreciates knowing where things are."

His smile widened, gentle as a knife going in.

"But do notice, as you look, that every star in my keeping hangs *within reach of my hand*. And that the thirteenth payment comes due when the moon completes its turn — tomorrow night."

Freya sat, because sitting was better than falling. Across the table, Dagny's hand had gone white-knuckled around his lantern; Sef had gone very still in the way she did before violence; and Bramblehoof stood rigid at the table's edge, thorns drawn taut, his eyes fixed on Freya with an expression she was beginning to be able to read.

*Help her*, it said. *Even if it breaks me.*

The first course arrived on plates of polished bone, carried by Hollow Men who did not breathe. And beneath the caged Cartographer's Chain — beneath the stars her mother had named her under — Freya picked up her fork, met the Pale King's patient, hungry gaze, and began to plan.

She had mapped her way out of worse places than this.

She just hadn't mapped them *yet*.
