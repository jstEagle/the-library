## 17. Audience with the Pale King

The throne room of the Court Beneath the Mountain was not what Freya had expected, and she resented that. She had spent three days descending through geode corridors, and she had expected — she had *planned* for — a monster's hall: dripping stone, bones of petitioners, a king seated on something with teeth.

Instead there were chandeliers.

Hundreds of them, hanging in tiers into the dark above, each one a caged star burning behind crystal lattice. Their light fell soft as lamplight through honey, pooling on floors of polished black stone so deep and still that walking on them felt like walking on the surface of a held breath. The hall smelled faintly of cold iron and, absurdly, of baking bread.

"Bread," Sef muttered beside her. "He's baking bread. That's a siege tactic, that is."

"It's working," Bramblehoof admitted. His thorn-marks had darkened since they'd entered the mountain, black vines crawling up his neck like ink spilled upward. He kept flexing his paws as if shaking off water. "I haven't eaten since the ford."

"You ate half my rations at the ford."

"I ate *some* of your rations. There's a difference, and it's called dignity."

Dagny said nothing. He hadn't said much since the gate, where he'd stopped dead at the threshold and stared at the ward-sigils carved into the arch — sigils that matched, Freya knew, the ones burned into his own forearms — and then walked through as if crossing a river he couldn't swim. Now he stood at her left shoulder with his hands folded like a man at a funeral, which, she supposed, in a way he was.

The Pale King waited at the far end of the hall.

He was not tall. That was the first shock. He sat on a throne of white salt-crystal — salt, Freya thought, and caught Sef flinch — and he looked like nothing so much as a tired scholar who had been interrupted at his reading. Grey robes. Grey hands. A face like weathered limestone, pleasant and blank, with eyes the color of starless sky, which was to say: eyes the color of nothing at all, because there were no stars left anywhere but here, caged and burning overhead.

"Freya Vane," he said, and his voice was warm. That was the second shock. It was the voice of someone who had known you your whole life and had only just remembered to say hello. "You've brought me quite a party. A fox in debt, a witch in exile, a keeper in disgrace." His gaze moved down the line of them gently, like a hand smoothing a wrinkled page. "And a cartographer. Come all this way to see me. I'm honored."

"We're not here for your honor," Freya said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "We're here to settle an account."

"Yes." He rose from the throne, and even that movement was unhurried, kind, the motion of a host standing to greet guests. "One soul, paid in full. Your mother's handwriting, I believe. She had lovely penmanship. You get yours from her, I think — I've seen your maps." He smiled, and it was a good smile, and Freya hated how much she wanted it to be genuine. "Come. Walk with me. Contracts are dreary things; let us not conduct business standing in a doorway like salesmen."

*Don't go with him,* Dagny's posture said. *Don't,* said Sef's.

Freya went with him.

* * *

They walked along the edge of the hall, beneath the caged stars, and the King talked the way a fire talks — low, steady, warming everything it touched without seeming to try.

"Do you know why I keep them lit?" he asked, gesturing up at the chandeliers. "The stars. Everyone assumes cruelty. Kings are supposed to be cruel; it saves everyone time. But no. I keep them because the dark is honest, and honesty is exhausting. Out there—" he nodded toward the distant ceiling, toward the world, "—your people live under a sky full of lights that promise things. Guidance. Wishes. Destiny. All lies, of course. Stars don't know you exist. But here, under my roof, when the lights burn, they burn for someone. They burn for whoever walks beneath them." He glanced sideways at her. "You've spent your whole life under wards that hummed like caged bees. Did you ever feel the stars were burning for you?"

"No," Freya said honestly.

"No," he agreed softly. "The valley protects its own by forgetting them. That's what a ward is, child. A wall made of being overlooked. Your mother bought safety and paid for it with significance. Every soul in that valley lives small and safe and *unremarked*, and you — you grew up drawing maps of a place that refused, in its heart, to be seen by anything. Even yourself." He stopped walking. "I wonder if you know how lonely that is. I do. I have had several thousand years to become expert in it."

"That's very sad," Freya said, "and also you stole the sky."

Something flickered across the limestone face — amusement, maybe, or appreciation. "Stole. Yes. Well. One does accumulate things." He resumed walking, and she fell into step beside him, hating herself for how natural it felt. "Let me tell you what I can offer, and then you may tell me your counteroffer, because I know you have one. You crossed the unmapped wilds with three liars and a fox. People like you always have one."

"They're not liars."

"All four of them are liars," the King said pleasantly. "Two of them are lying to you right now. But we'll come to that."

* * *

He led her to a gallery off the main hall — a long room whose walls were hung not with paintings but with doors. Hundreds of doors, each one different: cottage doors, palace doors, a ship's hatch, a garden gate, one door made entirely of woven reeds that dripped actual water onto the floor.

"A door for every life," the King said. "Every life you might have lived, if the valley had never warded itself shut. Open any of them."

Freya looked at the nearest door. It was plain pine, the kind every farmhouse in the valley had, except that through its window she could see a kitchen she almost recognized — her mother's kitchen, but brighter, warmer, and her mother was at the table, older than Freya remembered her, laughing at something out of sight.

"Don't," she whispered.

"Why not? It's real. Or near enough. The Court keeps possibilities the way other courts keep wine." The King's voice dropped, gentle as snowfall. "Here is what I offer, Freya Vane, and I offer it honestly, because a bargain forced is a bargain weakened, and I would rather you *choose*. Surrender willingly — walk through any door you like — and I will give you everything the valley never did."

He began to count on grey fingers.

"Belonging. A court where your name is spoken with respect, where your work is studied, where you will never again be the odd girl who draws pictures of a world that doesn't want looking at. Power. My cartographers hold more influence than my generals — knowledge of roads wins wars before swords are drawn, and you, you could map realms no living being has seen. Comfort." He gestured, and the bread-smell strengthened, and somewhere a hearth crackled. "A home that is *warm*, child. You've been cold your whole life and told it was safety. I am offering you the choice to stop being cold."

Freya's eyes stung. She blinked hard and stared at the pine door, at the laughing shape of her mother.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then at moonset on the thirteenth turn, the contract activates, and I take what I am owed. It will not hurt. I give you my word on that — pain in a collection is waste. You'll simply... arrive here, whole and unharmed, and everything you were will belong to me instead of you." He tilted his head. "You would still be able to draw. I'm not a monster. You simply wouldn't mind."

"Wouldn't mind what?"

"Anything." He smiled again, that good smile. "That's what a soul is, Freya. Not a ghost. Not a light. It's the part of you that *minds*. The part that looks at a wrong thing and refuses to leave it alone. Sell it, and you'll be comfortable forever, and you will never once care about anything again." He spread his hands. "Comfort, power, belonging. All of it true. All of it yours. And all of it, I promise you, worth exactly nothing, which is why I can afford to offer it so generously."

For a moment — a long, shameful moment — Freya wanted it anyway.

Not the power. Not even the comfort. The belonging. She thought of the valley's polite distance, the way neighbors spoke to her mother's orphaned daughter with careful kindness, kindness with walls around it. She thought of twenty years of being the girl at the edge of every gathering, useful with her maps and never quite included, and here was a king — a *thief*, a *monster*, she reminded herself, a thief and a monster — saying *I see you, I have always seen you, come inside.*

Her mother had sold her soul to buy a valley full of people who would never quite open their door to her.

And the Pale King's door was open. Had apparently always been open. Was open in *hundreds*.

"You're crying," the King observed, not unkindly.

"I'm mapping," Freya said. "It looks similar from the outside."

He laughed — a real laugh, startled out of him, and for one strange instant he looked less like a king and more like a lonely old scholar who had finally found a student who argued back. Then the limestone composure settled again.

"So," he said. "Your counteroffer."

* * *

But before she could speak, the gallery doors banged open and Bramblehoof trotted in, thorn-vines crawling visibly now past his collar, his fur bristling, with Sef and Dagny close behind — Sef moving like a woman walking into a house fire to fetch a cat, Dagny moving like a man who had decided that if this was where he died, at least he'd die between Freya and the door.

"She's not deciding anything alone," Sef announced. "Court rules. Any bargain struck must be witnessed by the petitioner's party or it's void. Article nine. You wrote it yourself, you pedantic fossil."

"I did write it myself," the King said, delighted. "Sef of the Salt Order. Still quoting statute after all these years. How is your sister Marisol? Still pretending she didn't testify against you?"

Sef went white. Dagny's hand found her shoulder.

"And Dagny Stonehand," the King continued, turning, and his voice softened into something almost tender, which was somehow worse. "Last of the ward-keepers. Tell me — have you worked out yet *who* loosened the valley's wards? Because I'll tell you for free: it wasn't me. I don't sabotage contracts. Sabotage voids them. Ask your fox." His empty eyes slid to Bramblehoof. "Ask him what his thorn-master paid him to carry, and to whom, and on whose instruction the valley's border-stones were chalked with failure-runes while the whole valley slept. Ask him who gave the order."

Bramblehoof made a sound like a dog swallowing a bee.

"He can't answer," Freya said quietly. "Thorn-oath."

"Mm. Convenient, thorns." The King turned back to her, and now the warmth in his face was gone, and what remained was vast and patient and terribly old, a winter landscape wearing a scholar's robe. "Well then, cartographer. The moon turns within the hour. Speak your counteroffer, or choose a door. Either way — choose *now*."

Freya looked at her companions. At Sef, jaw set, guilt worn like armor. At Dagny, whose shame had calcified into something that looked, from certain angles, exactly like courage. At Bramblehoof, trembling, thorns blooming, loyal against his own binding.

Her mother had sold her soul alone, in secret, to save people who never knew the price.

Freya Vane was not going to make her mother's mistake. Whatever she offered next, she would offer it *out loud*, with witnesses, with family — chosen, quarrelsome, lying, beloved family — standing at her back.

She stepped forward, unrolled the master atlas from her pack, and laid it open on the salt floor between herself and the Pale King.

"Your Majesty," she said, "let's talk about what a map is actually *worth*."

Above them, through a shaft in the mountain's crown, the first sliver of the thirteenth moon came into view — and every caged star in the court began, softly, to sing.
