## 18. The Thirteenth Turn

### Bramblehoof

The thorns woke before the moon finished its turn.

Bramblehoof felt them first as a prickle along his spine, the way a dog feels a storm in its teeth — then as a tightening, a slow reeling-in, as though some great fisherman below the mountain had felt his line go taut and begun to draw it home. He had carried this leash for two hundred years. He knew every knot of it by heart.

They stood at the mouth of the final gallery, that long throat of black crystal leading down into the court proper, and Freya Vane was rolling up her maps with the brisk efficiency of a woman preparing to argue with a king.

"You're doing the thing again," she said without looking at him.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you look at me like I'm already lost."

"I am looking at you like a fox looks at a hen who has decided to march into the coop of her own accord," Bramblehoof said. "It is a look of professional respect and profound despair."

Freya smiled — a thin, brave, infuriating smile — and shouldered her satchel. "Then keep respecting me. And stay close."

*Stay close.* Oh, the cruelty of small kindnesses. The thorns tightened another notch.

He had told himself, all these miles, that he was merely her guide, her reluctant escort, a creature bound by oath to deliver her to the Pale King and nothing more. He had recited the terms of his binding like a prayer against feeling: *carry the vessel to the court beneath the mountain, see the thirteenth payment made, and be released.* Two hundred years of thorn and salt and servitude, ended by one girl's soul.

But somewhere between the uphill river and the tollhouse of small mercies, between Dagny's terrible singing and Sef's grudging laughter and Freya falling asleep against his flank on cold nights because he was the warmest thing in the party — somewhere in all of that, the arithmetic had changed. He no longer counted the steps to his freedom. He counted the ones she took ahead of him.

And now here they were, at the bottom of the world, and the moon above was sliding into its thirteenth turning like a coin dropping into a slot, and the thorns were singing.

"Fox." Sef's voice, low, from the rear of the column. She had gone still the way only an old soldier goes still. "Do you feel that?"

"The thorns? Yes. They've been chatty since midnight."

"Not the thorns." Her face, lit by the pale glow of the geode walls, was grim as a headstone. "The contract. It's waking up. I remember this feeling from — " She stopped. Swallowed. "From before. When a debt comes due, the whole court leans toward it. Every guard, every servant, every stone. They'll come for her now. Not at the audience. Not when it's polite. Now."

As if her words had been a summons, the gallery behind them filled with light — not starlight, there was no such thing down here anymore, but the cold white gleam of Hollow Men kindling out of the dark, one after another, like lamps being lit in a house where nobody lived.

Dagny swore in three dialects. "How many?"

"All of them," said Sef. "I'd guess."

Freya stepped forward, and Bramblehoof's heart — that shriveled, thorn-wrapped thing — did something it had not done in two centuries. It broke a little, out of sheer admiration, because the fool girl planted herself in front of four Hollow Men with nothing but a satchel of maps and said, "We have an appointment with the King. You can announce us or you can move."

The nearest Hollow Man raised its hand, and the air went sharp as broken glass.

Bramblehoof felt the choice arrive the way winter arrives — not suddenly, but all at once, everywhere, undeniable. The thorns around his heart drew tight for the last time and whispered their old bargain: *deliver her, and be free.*

He thought about the tollhouse. He had paid that oracle the name of his thorn-master, thinking it would lighten him. It had only shown him how much else he was carrying.

"No," Bramblehoof said aloud, to the thorns, to the King, to two hundred years of himself. "No, I don't think I will."

He turned his back on the Hollow Men, put his body over Freya's like a roof over a candle, and let the thorns do their worst.

The pain was — well. There are pains, and then there are the ones you make room for. This one made room. Every thorn that had ever bound him drove inward at once, blooming through muscle and marrow, and he heard himself make a sound he did not know a fox could make, and he stayed exactly where he was.

"Bramblehoof!" Freya's hands were on his shoulders, trying to pull him around. "What are you — you're breaking your oath, they'll — "

"They'll unmake me," he agreed cheerfully, through his teeth. "Terrible plan. No alternatives. Do shut up and let me die with dignity."

"You absolute *fox*—"

"That is the *nicest* thing anyone has ever called me—"

The thorns screamed. Not him — the thorns themselves, an oath breaking is a loud thing, and this one tore loose root and branch, and something vast and furious far beneath them roared like a mountain discovering it could feel pain. The Hollow Men staggered as if the floor had tilted. The binding that had held Bramblehoof together came apart — and did not kill him. It simply *stopped*, and what remained underneath, blinking in the starless dark, was smaller than he remembered being, and lighter, and shaking.

"Oh," he said faintly. "Oh, that's what my legs actually look like."

Freya was crying and laughing at once, holding his face in both hands. "You stupid, wonderful creature."

"Family," he said, testing the word like a mapmaker tests new ground. "I'm told that's what we are. I intend to be insufferable about it."

Behind them, Sef rolled up her sleeves.

### Sef

Sef had sworn, thirty years ago, face-down in exile dirt with her sisters' curses still smoking off her skin, that she would never again use what the Court Beneath the Mountain had taught her. Court-craft was not magic the way ward-keeping was magic, honest as carpentry. Court-craft was magic the way a knife was dinner: it depended entirely on where you pointed it.

She looked at the Hollow Men closing down the gallery, and she looked at Bramblehoof bleeding light from a hundred torn wounds, and she looked at Freya Vane standing her ground like her mother had stood, once, begging someone — anyone — to stop a bargain.

Well, Sef thought. Point the knife.

"Hollow Men," she called, and her voice came out in the old cadence, the court cadence, the one that made stone sit up straight. "By salt and severance, hear the terms of your making."

Every Hollow Man stopped. Of course they stopped. She had helped write their making-terms; she'd been junior sister on the drafting circle, back when she thought elegance was the same as goodness. They were bound by protocol the way Bramblehoof had been bound by thorns, and protocol had a door in it, and she knew where the hinge was.

"You are constructs of refusal," she said, walking toward them, hands open, heart hammering like a traitor. "You refuse warmth, you refuse names, you refuse the sky. Very good. Very tidy. But a thing made of refusal cannot refuse its own refusals — so when I say *be refused*, you must refuse to exist. It's in your charter. Clause nine. I checked the spelling myself."

Dagny, behind her, whispered, "Is that true?"

"It's true that they can't check," Sef murmured. "Pray they never learn to read."

She snapped her fingers — salt and ceremony both — and spoke the word of severance, the ugly one, the one that tasted like her sisterhood's kitchen smoke and her own worst decade.

The nearest Hollow Man considered the matter with the leisurely attention of a bureaucrat, then politely came apart into a drift of grey dust, as though it had been waiting years for permission to stop pretending.

The others hesitated. That was their flaw, always had been: they were made of hesitation, hollowed out and given legs.

"Clause nine," Sef repeated, and snapped again.

Two more came apart. The rest, wiser than their makers, withdrew up the gallery in perfect silent formation, which was the closest thing to applause the Court Beneath the Mountain permitted.

When she turned around, the three of them were staring at her — Freya with wide eyes, Dagny with something horribly like awe, and Bramblehoof, still leaking light, wearing the smuggest expression a fox has ever worn.

"Court-trained," Dagny said. "You said you'd *left*."

"I did leave." Sef flexed her fingers; they ached, the way old debts ache. "Leaving and never looking back are different skills. I only ever managed the first one."

"Sef." Freya's voice was careful, gentle, the voice she used near wounds. "That was — you saved us."

"Don't." Sef held up a hand. "Don't thank me yet. What I just used, they felt it. All of them, all the way down. She felt it too — the Queen's left hand, if she still lives. Someone down there knew that working like a sister of the court tonight, of all nights." She exhaled. "There will be a price on my head by morning. There may be one on all of ours. So let's not stand here admiring the view. Let's go meet a king while I still frighten people enough to get us in the door."

Bramblehoof fell into step beside her, limping grandly. "For what it's worth," he said, "I always suspected you were terrifying."

"Thank you, fox."

"And now confirmed. It's very comforting. Like finding out the grumpy neighbor has a sword collection."

"Out of my way, fox."

But she was smiling, and she didn't stop smiling until the doors of the court opened, and then there was no room in her face for anything but what came next.

### Freya

The throne room of the Court Beneath the Mountain was full of her mother's stars.

That was Freya's first thought, and it was obscene, and it was true: hanging from the vaulted dark in cages of black iron, twelve constellations' worth of stolen stars burned in their prisons, casting a light like a memory of night. The valley's sky had gone blind the night the wards fell, and here was where the blindness lived — hoarded, displayed, beautiful in the way that cruelty is sometimes beautiful if you refuse to look too closely.

Freya looked closely. She was a cartographer. Looking closely was the whole of her religion.

The Pale King sat upon a throne of uncut geode, and he rose when she entered, which she had not expected, and smiled, which she liked even less.

"Freya Vane," he said, and her name in his mouth sounded like it belonged to someone else, someone more important and less loved. "Thirteenth payment. Right on time. Your mother was punctual too, at the end."

"My mother," Freya said, walking forward because stopping would have meant running, "was a woman who kept her accounts. I've read the ledger. Twelve payments. Twelve turns of protection bought for a valley that never knew what it cost." Her voice held. She was almost proud of it. "I'm here to settle the thirteenth."

"Are you?" The Pale King descended one step, then another. Up close he was less a man than a shape a man might leave in snow. "Or are you here because your fox betrayed his oath, your witch betrayed her silence, and your keeper betrayed his office — because everyone you've collected on this road has chosen you over their bindings? Do you know how rare that is, little cartographer? To be *chosen*?" Another step. His voice went soft, and softness was the sharpest thing he owned. "I could give you that permanently. Belonging, without ledgers. A court that wants you. Power to redraw any border you like — and oh, the realms you could redraw. Or comfort. A quiet valley, warded forever, no more debts, no more dead mothers. Whatever the wound is, child, I stock the salve. All I ask is the small matter of your soul, freely surrendered."

And there it was — the exact shape of every lonely night of her life, offered back to her gift-wrapped. A valley that had forgotten magic. A childhood of tracing borders because she could never cross them. A mother who died with a secret in a map chest.

Freya felt the pull of it, honestly, fully — and that was how she knew it was a lie. Real belonging didn't need to be purchased. Real belonging had just bled itself half-unmade in a corridor to buy her sixty seconds. Real belonging was currently standing behind her, a disgraced keeper, an exiled witch, and a freed fox, none of whom owed her anything, all of whom had come anyway.

Above the throne, the caged stars began to pulse — slowly, rhythmically, like a heartbeat counting down. The moon completing its turn. The contract waking all the way through.

"The turn is nearly done," the Pale King said. "Choose."

Freya reached into her satchel.

"I have a counteroffer," she said.

Behind her, Bramblehoof muttered, "Oh, I *do* love her."

The King's empty eyes narrowed. "A counteroffer. To *me*. On the night your debt comes due."

"You want souls. Souls are currency, fine — but currency needs somewhere to *spend*, and here's what no one under this mountain understands: you're running out of world. Everything unmapped stays unmapped because unmapped things can't be taxed, conscripted, conquered, or traded. Your court is rich in stars and poor in leverage, and every year the wilds shrink and your reach with them." She pulled free the topmost roll of vellum and held it up between them, and her hand did not shake, because she had spent her whole life learning to hold a line steady. "So. My soul — or my life's work. Every unmapped realm, rendered knowable. Including yours. Think carefully, Your Majesty, about what it means for your enemies to finally find you on a map."

The heartbeat of the caged stars stuttered.

Somewhere above, the moon completed its turn — and for the first time in thirteen turns, the Court Beneath the Mountain did not collect.

The Pale King looked at the roll of vellum in a cartographer's steady hand, and Freya Vane saw, plain as a coastline, the moment the most ancient power under the earth realized he was outbargained.
