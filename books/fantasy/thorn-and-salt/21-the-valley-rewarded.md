## 21. The Valley, Rewarded

The wards were still broken when they crested the eastern ridge, and somehow that was the first thing that made it feel like home again.

Dagny had spent forty years of mornings walking this ridge with a warding-rod in his hand and a knot of quiet pride in his chest — the particular pride of a man whose job was invisible when done well. Now he came back up the same path with thorn-scars on his forearms, salt crusted in his beard, a fox spirit trotting at his heel like an opinionated dog, and no pride at all. Just the knot. The pride had been the first casualty of the night of falling stars; everything else had followed.

"There it is," Sef said, and her voice did something complicated around the word *it*.

The valley lay below them in the grey light before dawn: the river silvering through its meadows, the rooftops of Hollowmere huddled together like sheep in cold weather, smoke rising from a dozen chimneys because people still woke up, still made breakfast, still lived even under an empty sky. The fields looked wrong without starlight to season them, but they looked like *his* fields. The ones he'd failed.

Bramblehoof sat down at the crest of the hill and scratched an ear with a hind leg, which for a creature bound in living thorns should have been impossible and was instead merely undignified. "Smaller than I imagined," the fox said. "Places that sell their souls usually build bigger."

"It didn't sell anything," Freya said. Her voice had an edge on it these days, honed on the whetstone of the ledger, the court, the king's smiling face. "It *paid*. There's a difference."

"Is there," said Bramblehoof, in the tone of a fox who had delivered contracts for three hundred years and knew exactly how much difference there was.

Dagny let them argue. It was easier than looking down at the valley and thinking about what waited in it. Not the Court's remnants — those had scattered when their king bargained away his conquest to a cartographer with ink-stained fingers, and Dagny would treasure the memory of that moment until his dying day, the Pale King's expression as he realized he'd been outmaneuvered by someone who thought in contour lines. No, what waited in the valley was worse. What waited in the valley was Elder Corwin Hale, third chair of the valley council, keeper of the granary ledgers, the man who had taught Dagny his letters.

The man who had opened the wards from the inside.

* * *

They came into Hollowmere at midmorning, and the valley came out to meet them like water finding a crack.

Word had traveled ahead of them — of course it had; news moved faster than any of them walked, especially news with stars attached. The stars had come back two nights ago, all at once, blazing into the sky like a struck flint, and every soul in the valley had stood in their doorways and wept or laughed or both, and then everyone had asked each other the same question: *who?*

Now they knew. Or they thought they did. A crowd gathered at the well square as the five of them came up the lane — farmers and millers and the cooper's family and old Marta who claimed to remember the last time the wards flickered, sixty years ago, though she claimed to remember a great many things. They stared at Freya like she was a returning saint. They stared at Sef like she might curse their cows. They stared at Bramblehoof and several children immediately decided he was the finest thing they had ever seen, which the fox bore with the weary grace of a celebrity.

And they stared at Dagny, the disgraced ward-keeper, and nobody quite met his eye.

He'd expected that. He'd rehearsed it, in a way, during the long walk home — rehearsed the shame of it, the way neighbors become careful around you, the way your name gets spoken in past tense. He'd built a little shelter out of the expectation and prepared to live in it.

What he hadn't prepared for was Elder Hale pushing through the crowd with his cane, white-bearded and upright, face arranged into solemn joy, arms opening wide.

"Dagny! Boy!" And the old man gripped his shoulders and looked into his eyes with such naked relief that Dagny felt something in his chest turn over. "Thank the stars — thank *someone* — you've come home. We feared the worst. We all feared the worst." His voice cracked just so. His hands trembled just enough.

It was masterful. That was the thing that struck Dagny first, standing there in the smell of the crowd and the woodsmoke — not horror, not rage, but a kind of terrible professional admiration. He had watched this man preside over council meetings for thirty years. He had seen him soothe feuds over fence lines and water rights. He had thought, all that time, that the skill was kindness wearing patience's clothing.

It wasn't. The skill was just skill.

"You're welcome, Elder," Dagny heard himself say. His voice sounded very far away. "We've brought back the stars."

"You have. You have, and the valley owes you—" Hale turned to the crowd, arms spreading, the born orator finding his congregation— "owes *them*, every one—"

"The wards didn't fail on their own."

Dagny said it quietly, but the square was quiet enough that quiet carried. The words dropped into the crowd like a stone into a well, and he heard the splash go all the way down.

Hale's hands stopped trembling. That was the tell. Grief trembles; guilt goes very, very still.

"I'm sorry?" the elder said.

"The wards didn't fail." Dagny stepped back, out of the man's grip, and found — oddly — that Freya had drifted to stand at his left shoulder, and Sef at his right, and that Bramblehoof had stopped scratching his ear. "I've spent four months telling myself I misread the resonance, that I missed a decay curve, that I fell asleep at my post one too many times. I've carried it like a stone in my boot the whole way to the mountain and most of the way back." He reached into his coat and drew out the thing he'd carried since chapter — since the tollhouse, since the oracle-child had taken his truth as toll and given him this in exchange: a splinter of ward-stone, blackened at one end, cut clean at the other.

Not burned through. Cut. From the inside.

"This is from the anchor-stone beneath the council house. Wards don't cut themselves, Elder. Someone opened them. Deliberately. From within the valley."

The silence stretched so long that Dagny could hear the well-rope creak in the wind. Somewhere a chicken complained about the interruption to commerce.

Then old Marta, sixty years of memory in her face, pointed one knotted finger across the square — not at Hale, but past him, at the council house steps — and said, "Well. Somebody ask him why, then."

* * *

They didn't drag him anywhere. That mattered to Dagny later, when he thought about the day. Nobody dragged Corwin Hale anywhere. The crowd parted around him the way crowds do around a sick man, and he walked to his own study in the council house on his own legs, leaning on his own cane, and the five of them went in after him and shut the door, and that was the whole of the arrest.

The study smelled of paper and lamp oil and dried lavender, the way it always had. Dagny had learned to read sitting in this room, cross-legged on the floor while Hale read aloud from the valley's founding charter. There was the same lopsided bookshelf. The same cracked globe of the world with half its continents rubbed away by children's fingers — by *Freya's* fingers, probably, and her mother's before her.

Hale sat behind his desk because a man will cling to the furniture of his authority even as it burns under him. His hands were folded. His face had finally stopped performing.

"You cut the anchor-stone with a mason's chisel and a prayer to no one," Dagny said. "You did it on the twelfth moon-turn, between the second bell and the third, while I was doing rounds on the north wall. I know because the resonance signature is in the fracture. I've been reading that stone's song since I was nineteen years old, Corwin. I know its voice better than my own mother's."

"Yes," Hale said simply.

"Why?"

And here is where Dagny had expected — had braced for — the villain's speech. The cackle. The grand design. Men who do great harms want their harms to be great; it's the only consolation they get.

Instead the old man looked down at his folded hands and said, "Because my wife died of the flux in the spring, and I am eighty-one years old, and I could not bear for the valley to die with me owing a debt I couldn't see."

The room stayed still.

"The protection was bought," Hale went on, and now the words came faster, thirty years of them dammed up. "Everyone knew the wards were *given* — that's the story, isn't it? The founders were blessed, the valley was chosen, magic cannot follow us here. A gift. But I kept the granary ledgers for forty years, boy, and I learned that nothing stays paid forever. Sooner or later every account comes due. And I thought — what if it comes due when there's no one strong enough to pay it? What if it comes due on some child? On some generation that never agreed to the bargain?" His eyes were wet and furious. "Better a wound we choose than a debt we don't see coming. Better the dark, honest, than safe on borrowed coin."

"So you broke the wards," Sef said, "to find out what the protection cost."

"I broke them to *see*," Hale said. "I told myself I'd mend them after. If the price was bearable." He laughed, a small horrible sound. "If."

"And the price was a soul," Freya said.

She'd been silent at the window the whole time, and when she spoke, everyone turned. She was holding her mother's ledger — she seemed to hold it constantly now; Dagny suspected she slept with it — and she laid it on the desk between them with a gentleness that made Dagny's throat hurt.

"Thirteen payments. Twelve made. One due." She turned the final page toward him. "My mother wrote it all down. She sold my soul before I could walk, to keep this valley fed and warded and safe, and she hated herself every year of my childhood, and she never once told me, and she died carrying it alone." Freya's voice didn't shake. That was somehow worse than shaking. "So you're right, Elder. Debts come due on people who never agreed to them. I'm the proof. I walked into a king's court and nearly stayed there because of a bargain made over my cradle."

"I didn't know," Hale whispered. "Stars forgive me, I didn't know it was you. I only meant to open the door and look."

"The Pale King doesn't leave doors open out of courtesy," Bramblehoof said from the hearth, where he'd curled up as though the conversation bored him. "A door opened is a door owed."

Silence again. Outside, faintly, the sounds of the valley going about its afternoon — someone chopping wood, the mill turning, life being stubborn.

"So," Hale said at last. His voice was the voice of an old man who has set down something very heavy and discovered his arms don't remember how to rest. "What happens now? The council will want justice. The valley will want someone to hate. I've given them a fine candidate." He almost smiled. "An old fool who mistook fear for wisdom."

Dagny looked at him for a long time. He thought about the walk home — the whole long, starving, terrified walk home — and how often he'd imagined this moment, and how in every imagining he'd been holding something: a rope, a verdict, a fist. In every imagining, vengeance had fit his hand perfectly.

In the actual room, his hands were empty, and what he felt wasn't forgiveness yet. Forgiveness was a crop you planted and tended. But the ground for it was here. He could feel that too.

"What happens now," Dagny said, "is the wards get rebuilt. Properly. With the valley watching every knot and knowing every price — all of it written down, posted in the square, no more gifts and no more secrets. And you're going to help. Every anchor-stone, every sigil-line. You'll learn the craft you sabotaged, at your age, with your hands, and when we're done, the wards will hum stronger than they ever did under me, because they'll be held up by everyone instead of one tired man and a lie."

Hale stared at him. "That's punishment?"

"That's Tuesday," said Sef, and something in Dagny's chest loosened for the first time in months.

Freya leaned forward. "And one more thing. You keep the granary ledgers. You'll keep this one too." She pushed her mother's ledger across the desk. "Every payment, every entry, in the open. My mother hid the truth to protect us. Look how that ended. We do it differently now."

The old man put his hands on the ledger like a drowning man taking hold of a rope, and if he wept a little, well — the light was low, and the lavender was strong, and no one in that room was cruel enough to say.

* * *

They started the next morning, because grief keeps poor hours but work keeps excellent ones.

The anchor-stone beneath the council house had to be recut, and that meant the whole square got to watch, which Dagny privately considered half the medicine. Sef chalked the new sigil-circles herself, refusing help, tongue between her teeth, muttering in a dialect that made two elderly women clutch their shawls and one teenage boy start covertly copying the shapes onto his slate. When she caught him, she didn't scold. She handed him the chalk and showed him the next line.

"Healing circles," she said gruffly. "Ward-work's just healing that argues back."

Bramblehoof turned out to be unexpectedly useful — fox spirits, it transpired, can smell resonance the way dogs smell rain, and he'd trot the perimeter of each new sigil and announce, with devastating editorial commentary, exactly which lines ran true and which "wobbled like a drunkard's promise." The children of Hollowmere adopted him wholesale. By the third day he had a following of six who brought him scraps and demanded stories of the mountain, all of which he improved upon shamelessly.

Freya mapped everything. Of course she did. New anchor-points, new lines, a fresh survey of the valley as it actually was rather than as legend remembered it — and Dagny found her one evening at the north wall, rod in hand, drawing the ward-lines into her atlas with the fierce tenderness other people reserved for love letters.

"It'll be the first fully documented ward-network in the wild lands," she said, catching him looking. "Anyone could rebuild it. Anyone could *study* it. No more secrets."

"Your mother would be proud," Dagny said, and then winced, because he'd promised himself not to invoke dead mothers casually around this woman anymore.

But Freya just smiled, small and real. "She'd complain about my lettering. Then she'd be proud."

On the seventh day, they lit the wards.

It was full dark — true dark, but a friendly dark now, the stars thick overhead like a spilled purse of silver — and the whole valley had gathered in the well square. Dagny stood at the anchor-stone with the warding-rod that had been his father's, feeling foolish and terrified and exactly where he belonged. Hale stood beside him, chisel-callused palms flat on the new-cut stone, murmuring the closing words he'd spent seven days learning, stumbling twice, corrected gently by a nine-year-old in the front row.

"Together," Dagny said.

They poured the power in — his craft, Sef's salt, Freya's mapped certainty, the fox's low singing note that no one else could hear, and underneath all of it the valley itself, hundreds of small willing hands' worth of attention, the whole town leaning its weight against the dark.

The wards rose like dawn.

Dagny felt them settle into place — that familiar hum, the caged bees, the sound of home — but different now. Deeper. Layered. Not one tired man's secret labor but a chord sung by everyone, and when it locked, the crowd made a sound he'd never heard a crowd make: not a cheer, exactly. More like an exhale held for four hundred years.

Old Marta patted the anchor-stone and told it it had behaved disgracefully and she hoped it was ashamed.

* * *

Later — much later, when the square had emptied and the lanterns had gone down to embers — Dagny found Hale still sitting by the stone, wrapped in a blanket, looking up at the stars like a man reading a letter that took decades to arrive.

"They're brighter than I remembered," the old man said.

"They are."

"Dagny." Hale didn't look at him. "Why? You'd every right to see me pilloried. Half the valley wanted it."

Dagny sat down on the cold stone beside him, joints protesting, and considered the question honestly, because the man had earned honesty if he'd earned nothing else.

"Because I know what fear does to a careful mind," he said finally. "I spent four months blaming myself for your crime, and part of me — a part I'm not proud of — preferred it. Guilt is simple. You carry it, that's all. Forgiveness is work. Trust is work." He nodded at the humming stone beneath them. "But look what the work built. I'd rather live in a valley that forgives than one that's merely safe. Safe valleys rot from the inside. Ask the Pale King — oh, wait, you can't. Freya out-bargained him into a very small box."

Hale laughed — really laughed, wet and crackling — and then grew serious. "There's something else. Since we're being honest." He fumbled in his coat and produced a letter, sealed, dusty, addressed in a hand Dagny recognized with a jolt. *To the finder of my ledger.* Freya's mother's writing.

"She left it with me," Hale said. "Years ago. 'For whoever learns the truth first,' she said. I never opened it. Couldn't. Cowardice compounds interest, too." He held it out. "It belongs to her daughter."

Dagny took the letter and turned it over in his hands, feeling the weight of it, the years inside it. Somewhere across the square a light was still burning in the mapmaker's cottage — Freya, up late as always, beginning whatever came next.

"Well," Dagny said, standing, joints protesting again, heart lighter than it had been since the night the stars went out. "Best deliver it, then. In this valley, we've decided secrets don't keep."

He crossed the square under the bright and crowded stars, and knocked on the lit door.
