## 22. Maps of What Comes Next

The first page of the new atlas was the hardest one Freya had ever drawn, and it was blank.

She had been staring at it for three days.

It sat on the long table in her mother's old workroom — the same chestnut table where she'd once learned to grind oak gall ink and stretch vellum until it stopped arguing with her — and it was perfect. Freshly sized, taut as a drumskin, white as a held breath. Every other map in her life had begun from something: a valley's edge, a ward-line, a known thing waiting to be measured. This page began from nothing at all, and nothing, Freya was discovering, was a very loud kind of silence.

"You're scowling at it like it owes you money," Sef said from the doorway.

"It might." Freya didn't look up. "Blank pages are shifty."

Sef crossed the room with her usual gait — half walk, half swagger, all of it a performance she'd never quite dropped even now that nobody in the valley flinched when she passed. She'd taken to wearing her salt-grey braid coiled at her neck like a crown, which Dagny said made her look like a duchess and Bramblehoof said made her look like a woman who'd lost a fight with a rope. Both assessments delighted her.

Sef leaned over Freya's shoulder and studied the emptiness. "In my experience," she said, "the best way to start a page is badly."

"That's terrible craft advice."

"It's excellent life advice. Craft just hasn't caught up yet."

Freya laughed — a real laugh, unguarded, the kind that still surprised her when it arrived. There had been a season, not so long ago, when she'd thought grief would live in her chest permanently, taking rent, rearranging the furniture. It did still live there. But it had stopped being a landlord and become, somehow, a lodger — quiet, occasionally weepy, mostly harmless. Her mother's ledger sat closed on the shelf above her desk now, its last entry read and reread and finally understood: *one soul, paid in full* — paid by a woman who loved a valley more than she loved her own daughter's future, and who had spent thirteen years trying to find another way before the moon ran out. Freya had stopped calling it betrayal. Most days.

*Most days* was an honest measurement. Cartographers dealt in honest measurements or they dealt in nothing.

"Where will you start?" Sef asked. "Geographically speaking."

Freya looked out the workroom window. The evening was coming on gold and slow, and over the eastern ridge — where the wards had stood for two hundred years humming their caged-bee hum — the sky was doing something it had never done in her lifetime.

Magic was coming back into the valley. Not all at once; the elder council had voted, after much shouting and one memorable fainting spell from Elder Maren, to lower the rebuilt wards a hand's width at a time, like letting light into a cellar. And what came through first, everyone agreed, was color. The moss along the millstream had gone emerald overnight. The bees were making honey that tasted faintly of thunderstorms. Old Tam's crooked apple tree had straightened itself while he slept, and he'd taken it personally for a week.

And above it all, the stars. All of them. Returned from the Pale King's chandeliers and blazing back into their old houses, so thick on clear nights that the valley children had invented eleven new constellations in a month, most of them rude.

Freya dipped her pen. Then, deliberately, she set it down again.

"No," she said. "Not yet. I want to start this atlas properly, and properly means company."

* * *

Company arrived the next morning in its usual order: loudly, then reluctantly, then sideways.

Dagny came first, because Dagny always came first — it was his nature, forged in twenty years of walking ward-lines alone at dawn. He knocked on the workroom door with the formal triple rap he used for official business, which was absurd, since his official business these days consisted chiefly of delivering bread.

"Ward report," he announced, setting a loaf on the corner of her table. "Eastern line holding at reduced strength. Western line humming nicely — Sef's salt-lattice is better than anything the guild ever taught me, which I have told her twice and she has made me regret both times. Northern line—"

"The northern line has a fox living under it," Freya said.

"The northern line," Dagny corrected with dignity, "has a *consultant* living under it."

As if summoned — and Freya had long suspected he could be summoned by any mention of his name within a mile — Bramblehoof flowed in through the open window. In his fox shape he moved like poured ink with opinions, thorn-scars gone silver along his flank now instead of black, the binding that had once dragged him toward the mountain broken clean. He hopped onto the table, considered the blank page with one amber eye, and sneezed on it.

"Bramblehoof," Freya said warningly.

"What?" said the fox, shaking into his other shape — a lean, sharp-faced man with red hair going grey at the temples and the same unrepentant amber eyes. "I was marking territory. It's traditional. Your people do it with flags; mine do it with noses. Mine is more honest."

"You're not marking my atlas."

"Your *atlas*," he repeated, settling into the chair he'd claimed months ago and never surrendered, and there was something in his voice Freya had learned to listen for — a softness under the sass, the sound of a creature who had carried a contract for a hundred years and still couldn't quite believe he'd been allowed to put it down. "An atlas. A whole book of worlds. And you're starting it with an empty page, which is either profound or procrastination, and I've watched you long enough to know it's the second."

"It's neither." Freya pulled the page back from him protectively. "It's a dedication problem. Every atlas opens with what it's *for*. My mother's opened with 'For the Valley.' The Great Guild Atlas opens with 'For the Crown.'" She turned the pen in her fingers. "Mine doesn't fit either of those anymore."

Dagny lowered himself into his chair — the third chair, the one nobody had assigned him but everybody saved anyway — and was quiet in the way he got when he was working up to something. Freya waited. She'd gotten good at waiting on Dagny. Underneath the shame he'd carried for years about the night the wards fell — shame that had finally found its target in Elder Corvin, who had sabotaged the lines out of a fear so old and cold it had calcified into principle — there had always been a man who noticed things. He noticed them slowly, but he noticed everything.

"For the road," Dagny said at last. "That's what you'd write. If you asked me. Forgive me if I'm overstepping the craft."

Freya stared at him. "Say it again."

"For the road?"

"*For the road.*" She was already reaching for the pen. Because he was right — of course he was right, he was always right in that plodding, apologetic, load-bearing way of his. Her old maps had been for places. Places sat still and let themselves be measured. But everything worth mapping now — the reversed river canyon where Dagny had pulled her back from the water with forbidden ward-craft singing off his hands; the Market of Borrowed Names where Sef had traded a memory of her sister's laugh for their passage and gotten the laugh back later, secondhand, from a stranger who'd bought it and found it too heavy to keep; the tollhouse where a childlike oracle had taken truths from each of them like coins and given back, in exchange, the strange lightness of being known — none of it sat still. All of it had happened *in motion*, between here and there, on roads that only existed because four unlikely people had decided to walk them together.

She wrote it small and clean in the upper margin, in the hand her mother had taught her:

*For the road.*

Then, after a moment, she added beneath it: *and for the ones who walk it.*

Bramblehoof leaned over and read it upside down. His ears — he kept the fox's ears even in man-shape, a vanity he defended fiercely — went slightly pink at the tips.

"Hmph," he said. "Adequate."

From him, it was a eulogy of praise.

* * *

They worked through the morning, all four of them, though only Freya touched the vellum. That had become the arrangement, unspoken and then spoken and then fiercely defended whenever the guild's envoys came sniffing from the capital with their offers of patronage. Dagny dictated distances and landmarks from his decades of patrol notes. Sef handled anything involving salt — coastlines, marshes, the strange tide-marks left where magic pooled and evaporated — because salt remembered water in ways no instrument could. And Bramblehoof provided, in his words, "intelligence," and in everyone else's words, "gossip," about the wild country beyond the fallen border, which he had crossed more times than he admitted and enjoyed more than he should have.

By midday the blank page was no longer blank. It was becoming a map of the valley itself — but not the valley as Freya had ever drawn it before. Her old maps showed borders. Wards. The neat blue line beyond which the world ended, because for her it truly had.

This map showed doors.

"There," Sef said, tapping the western edge, where the salt-flats began. "Mark the crossing place. Where we walked out. Put a little symbol."

"What symbol?"

"The one you used for safe fords, but add a star above it. Our star." She meant the constellation the four of them had jokingly named on the road home — the Lopsided Kettle, four bright stars in a formation no astronomer would defend. The valley children had adopted it instantly and refused to be talked out of it.

Freya drew it. A ford-mark crowned with a star. And then, because the page was hers and the rules were hers now, she drew another at the tollhouse of Small Mercies, and another at the market grounds, and another at the mouth of the canyon, until the margins of the valley map bloomed with little starred symbols like a sky brought down to earth.

"What are they?" Dagny asked.

"Waypoints," Freya said. "But not the kind that mark distance." She sat back and looked at what they'd made. "The old atlases tell you where a place is. These tell you where a *moment* is. Where something happened that changed the route. Any cartographer following this map won't just know the terrain — they'll know where trust was built. Where secrets came out. Where somebody chose somebody else over safety." She glanced up. "I'm going to call them mercy-points."

Silence around the table. The good kind.

"That's the worst name I've ever heard," Bramblehoof said warmly, "and I've heard Sef sing."

"I was *chanting*," Sef said, "and it was a war ritual, and you volunteered to hold the drum—"

"—because the alternative was listening to it—"

Freya let them argue, pen moving steadily, and outside the window the afternoon sun came slantwise through the glass and lit the ink to a soft brown shine, and somewhere below in the village square the bell rang the hour with a sound that was just a sound now, ordinary and welcome, no longer counting down anything.

* * *

Near dusk, a knock came at the door — not Dagny's formal triple rap, but the shy double-tap of someone young.

Mira from the miller's house stood on the step, twelve years old and ferociously earnest, clutching a roll of paper to her chest the way Freya had once clutched her first compass. Behind her hovered two other children from the lane, pretending not to be there and failing completely.

"Mistress Vane? We — that is, I — we made a map." She thrust the paper forward. "Of the valley. Well. Of our bit of the valley. The good hiding spots and the bee-tree and where the magic came through strongest after the wards opened, the moss place, and — and a mercy-point. At the ford. Where you all came home." Her voice wobbled on the last words and steadied itself through sheer stubbornness. "We heard that's what they're called."

Freya took the paper with both hands.

It was, objectively, a disaster. The scale wandered. North migrated twice. The bee-tree was drawn larger than the mill. And every landmark on it — every single one — was marked not by distance or bearing but by what had happened there: *best spot to hear Bramblehoof complain about rain. Place where the honey tastes of storms. Where Mira's dog dug up a ward-stone and howled at it for an hour.*

It was the truest map Freya had ever seen.

"It's wonderful," she managed.

"It's wrong," Mira said miserably. "The proportions are all—"

"The proportions are *fine*." Freya knelt so they were eye to eye. "Listen to me. Maps are wrong on purpose. Every map chooses what to keep and what to leave out, and the choosing is the whole art. You chose what mattered. Do you know how long it took me to learn that?" She held up the paper. "May I keep this? I want to put it in the atlas. On the first pages. Before any of mine."

Mira's whole face went incandescent.

After the children had thundered away down the lane, arguing joyfully about whether the bee-tree needed a second drawing, Freya stood in the doorway a long moment. Sef appeared beside her with two cups of the bitter tea she pretended wasn't an acquired taste, and handed one over.

"You're going to start a school," Sef said. "I can see it happening behind your eyes. Don't fight it."

"I'm not going to start a school."

"You're going to start a school."

"I'm going to *consider*—" Freya sighed. "Fine. Possibly a school. An informal one. A *workshop*."

"With apprentices."

"With *students*, plural, briefly, on trial—"

Sef grinned into her tea, satisfied, and Freya realized, with the small vertigo that still ambushed her on good days, that this was what belonging felt like from the inside: people knowing your next move before you admitted it to yourself, and loving you for it, and being right.

* * *

Night came down soft and crowded with stars.

Later — after supper, after Dagny had gone home to his ward-rounds with the loaf's end wrapped in cloth, after Sef had headed for her cottage at the flats trailing complaints about the damp and meaning none of them, after Bramblehoof had curled up on the workroom's deep windowsill in fox-shape because, he claimed, the atlas smelled interesting and someone had to guard it — Freya sat alone with the nearly finished first page.

The valley spread across the vellum in warm browns and blues, its borders no longer walls but seams, stitched with starred waypoints. Beyond the edges she had left the vellum bare on purpose — the wild country would come in later chapters, mapped properly, walked properly, with company. She had already started planning the routes. East, toward the sea Sef missed more than she said. South, past the tollhouse, to see what the oracle-child made of a visit rather than a payment. North, someday, to the foothills where Bramblehoof's old pack still ran, if any of them still lived, if he could bear to look — a journey he hadn't agreed to yet but had stopped changing the subject from, which among fox spirits was practically a signed contract.

And beneath the mountain — well. Beneath the mountain, the geode halls stood empty now, starless chandeliers swaying in the dark, and Freya had left that region of the atlas pointedly unmapped. Some places deserved to stay unknown a while longer. Let them grow their own stories first. Let some other cartographer, a hundred years on, arrive curious instead of afraid.

On the windowsill, one amber eye opened. "You're still here," Bramblehoof observed. "The page is finished. Even I can see the page is finished, and I think in smells."

"It's finished." Freya capped the ink. "It's just... the first one. Twenty-one chapters of road to draw after it. Whole lifetimes, maybe."

"Mm." The fox stretched, thorn-silver scars catching starlight. "Terrible burden. However will you cope, with all those people to share it with." He resettled, tail over nose, but his voice drifted out soft from underneath: "Your mother would have liked it. The dedication. She was a 'for the road' woman herself. She just never got to write it down."

Freya's throat tightened pleasantly, painfully. "You never met her."

"I carried her contract for thirteen turns. You get to know a person." One ear flicked. "Goodnight, cartographer."

"Goodnight, consultant."

Outside, the Lopsided Kettle hung lopsidedly over the eastern ridge, exactly where four travelers had once named it under impossible skies, and inside the valley the wards hummed low and open-armed, less like cage-bars now than like a held breath about to become a song.

Freya turned to the next page. It gleamed up at her, blank and enormous and no longer frightening — not an ending, she understood now, and never had been. Just the part of the map where the ink runs out and the road keeps going.

She dipped her pen, and began.
