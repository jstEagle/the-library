## 1. The Taster's Portion

The poison came in a cup of spiced honey-wine, and Freya Underhill drank it standing up, as she always did.

Standing was important. If she sat, her body might decide to be lazy about it, letting the toxin pool and linger where a faster death could find purchase. Standing kept the blood moving, kept the liver working at its grim arithmetic, kept everything flowing toward the exits. She had learned this in her first year of service, at fifteen, from an old taster named Maren Cobb who had died at forty-one of accumulated damage rather than any single dose, her veins gone thin as cobweb, her gums bleeding at every meal. *Stand up when you take it, girl,* Maren had told her, wheezing. *Die on your feet if you have to die at all. And for the gods' sake, count.*

Freya counted.

The wine was good—Thornfell blackberry honey, warmed, cut with a thread of cinnamon that was meant to disguise exactly what it disguised. She let it sit on her tongue for three heartbeats before swallowing, because some poisons announced themselves in the mouth: the almond ghost of bitter cyanide, the metallic slickness of certain mineral salts, the numbing creep of hemlock extract that made the tongue feel like a stranger's. This one tasted only of honey and spice and the faint sourness of a vintage kept too long in oak.

So it wasn't in the wine itself, then. It would be in the aftertaste. It usually was.

She set down the goblet with a soft click against the silver tray and inclined her head to the steward—a narrow man called Pellam who never met her eyes, because meeting the eyes of the woman whose job was to die first was apparently bad luck, or bad manners, or simply too much like looking into a mirror he didn't want to own. "Her Grace's portion is sound," Freya said. Her voice came out steady. Good. The tremor would come later; it always came later, in the third hour, when the body began its accounting.

Pellam took the tray and vanished through the service door toward the queen's private solar, where Queen Maren would drink her evening wine believing—because she chose to believe, because belief was cheaper than the alternative—that the woman in the grey dress had already paid the price of its safety.

Freya stood by the wall and waited for the poison to introduce itself.

* * *

She had catalogued two hundred and eleven distinct poisons in her nine years at court. Not officially—the crown did not keep such records, preferring its tasters illiterate and interchangeable—but in the small waterproofed notebook sewn into the lining of her left sleeve, written in a shorthand of her own devising, compressed to symbols no one else could read. *Bitterleaf, 4 drops in wine: numbness of lips within ten minutes, sweating by twenty, vomiting by forty, sleep by second hour, waking headache like a nail driven through the eye.* *Greywort steeped in broth: harmless to taste, kills in three days, so the trick is not detection but memory—remember every face at table, remember who served what, remember which stewpot sat uncovered near the window where a man with a grudge might pass.* *Queen's grief, powdered, in anything sweet: no symptoms at all for six hours, and then the heart forgets its work mid-beat, like a dancer who stops mid-turn and simply stands there, confused, while the music goes on without her.*

She'd tasted Queen's grief once. Only once. She remembered the confusion most of all—not pain, not fear, just the strange embarrassment of her own heart stalling, as though she'd been caught out in public wearing nothing.

Tonight's poison, when it finally spoke, spoke softly.

The first sign was warmth. A bloom of heat behind the breastbone, spreading outward like water poured on cloth, and Freya marked the time against the candle-clock on the pantry shelf: seven minutes past the swallow. Then the warmth turned to prickling, a thousand tiny needles under the skin of her forearms, and she thought—*nightshade family, or something cousin to it. Old recipe. Someone's grandmother's recipe.* That was the thing about poisoners in the lowlands: they rarely invented. They inherited. A grandmother's grudge passed down like a lace collar, a mother's recipe card annotated in fading ink, *a little more, dear, the last batch didn't take.* Freya had tasted poisons older than the queen's reign, older than the dynasty, recipes that had crossed the Thornfell passes in the packs of brides and refugees and one memorable spice merchant who had tried to poison an entire diplomatic delegation and succeeded only in killing himself, because he'd sampled his own work out of professional pride.

By the quarter-hour her fingertips were going numb in slow succession, pinky first, then ring finger, then middle, a wave of sleep rolling up each hand. By half past, the sweat started—not fever-sweat but cold sweat, the body dumping moisture the way a ship dumps ballast—and her stomach gave its first low warning growl, a dog turning over in its sleep.

She walked herself through the corridor to the tasters' anteroom, a narrow chamber off the kitchens furnished with a cot, a basin, a bucket, and a copy of the Litany of the Veiled Lady that someone had thought comforting. She did not sit yet. She stood at the basin and breathed through the rising nausea and made her notes while her hand still obeyed her.

*Nightshade-cousin. Onset 7 min. Heat, pins, digital numbness ascending L→R. Cold sweat. Nausea moderate. Antidote: none known. Course: 6–8 hrs. Survivable at this dose. Barely.*

Then she sat down on the cot, folded her hands in her lap, and let the night do its work.

* * *

Nobody thanked her. This was not bitterness speaking—or not only bitterness. It was simple observation, and Freya prided herself on observation above nearly everything else. In nine years, no one had thanked her. The queen did not know her name; she knew her function, the way one knows the function of a lock or a moat. The courtiers stepped around her in corridors as if misfortune were contagious. Once, early on, a young lordling had watched her drink from the queen's cup and said, with a laugh, "I say, they ought to pay you double," and his companions had laughed too, and Freya had smiled her taster's smile—the flat, pleasant, empty one—and thought: *they pay me in staying alive, my lord, and even that they begrudge.*

But here was the thing she had learned, the thing that kept her walking back through those kitchen doors morning after morning: somebody had to know what was in the food. Somebody had to stand between the crown and the hundred small deaths waiting in every cellar and spice jar and wedding feast. And the crown would never hire anyone clever enough to wonder why. Clever tasters got curious. Curious tasters asked questions. Questions, in Thornfell Keep, were more dangerous than arsenic.

So the crown hired orphans and border-folk and women with no family name worth poisoning, and it taught them nothing, and it expected them to die neatly if the need arose, and Freya had looked at that arrangement at fifteen years old and decided, with the cold clarity that would define her whole life, that she would learn everything the crown declined to teach her. Herb-lore from the herb-wife who sold to the kitchens. Anatomy from the butcher's block. Poison-craft from the poisoners themselves, because a city this size always had poisoners, and poisoners loved an audience almost as much as they loved their secrets. She had spent coin she couldn't spare on a dead apothecary's notebook. She had spent nights she couldn't reclaim memorizing the veins of a leaf, the weight of a lethal dose of foxglove seed, the smell of arsenic heated in a spoon—garlic, always garlic, the dead apothecary wrote, *arsenic betrays itself as garlic, so serve it with garlic, you fool.*

Weary pride. That was the trade's whole wage. Nobody thanked her, nobody saw her, and every morning the queen woke because a grey-dressed woman she'd never once addressed had swallowed death on her feet and counted the minutes until it passed through.

Freya found that she could live on weary pride. It was thin fare, but she'd eaten thinner.

* * *

The worst hours were the third and fourth, when the nausea peaked and the cramps came in long rolling waves and the only thing to do was breathe—four counts in, six counts out, the rhythm she'd learned from a mountain midwife who swore it worked for labor and would therefore work for anything. She vomited twice into the bucket, cataloguing the contents with detached professionalism (wine, bile, a fleck of the afternoon's pigeon pie undigested, which meant the toxin slowed gastric emptying, which confirmed nightshade-kin), rinsed her mouth with well-water and a pinch of salt, and lay back down.

Somewhere beyond the anteroom wall, the castle went on being a castle. Cooks shouting. The clang of the great hearth doors. A fiddler tuning up in the lower hall, badly, the same four notes over and over as though repetition might eventually become music. The ordinary machinery of a place that believed itself safe because a woman in grey had made it safe, invisibly, at the price of a night spent curled around a bucket.

At the sixth hour the needles faded. At the seventh, feeling crept back into her fingers like blood returning to a frostbitten limb, painful in the particular way of recovery, which was a pain Freya had learned to love. By midnight she could walk a straight line along the anteroom floor, heel to toe, which was her test, her own private standard, before she ever declared herself fit to leave.

She washed her face in the basin. Grey dress, grey eyes, brown hair pinned back without ornament—invisibility was a uniform, and she wore hers well. In the spotted mirror above the sink, the woman who looked back at her was twenty-four years old and had the stillness of someone much older. There was a birthmark on her left forearm, high near the inner elbow: a coiled, branching mark, dark as dried ink, that her grandmother had once told her was shaped like a thorn wrapped around a flame. Freya had never seen the flame. She saw only the thorn. Tonight, in the mirror, with her sleeve pushed back to check her pulse, it seemed darker than usual—or perhaps that was the light.

She pulled her sleeve down and went to collect her cloak.

* * *

There were four ways out of Thornfell Keep. Three were gates, and gates meant names logged, lanterns, guards who noticed things. The fourth was a crack in the world that the builders had never intended: an old drainage passage from the keep's founding era, sealed officially since before Freya's birth, unofficially maintained by the smugglers who moved untaxed wool and unlicensed brandy and occasionally people through the mountain's ribs. The passage emerged a mile downslope, behind a curtain of ivy, into the ruined chapel of Saint Vessa—which was no saint anyone prayed to anymore, and no chapel anyone tended, and therefore perfect.

Freya had found it in her first winter, following a cat. The cat had known what it was doing. Cats always did.

The passage was low and smelled of stone-water and old smoke, and she moved through it without a lantern, counting her steps because the dark was safer navigated by number than by sight. Two hundred and six steps to the junction where the ceiling rose enough to stand upright. Four hundred and twelve to the root-choked bend where she had to turn sideways. Six hundred and thirty to the ivy curtain, where the mountain air came through cold and green and smelling of pine and coming snow.

She stepped out into the night and allowed herself, as she allowed herself every night, one long breath with no purpose but breathing.

The Thornfell mountains stood black against a sky scoured clean by wind, stars scattered across it like spilled salt. Below her, the lowlands glowed with the dirty amber of town and torchlight, the crown's country, lit and walled and afraid. Above and behind her, the true mountains climbed into cloud, the high valleys where the old paths ran and the old shrines stood and the folk who lived there baked bread with rye grown in soil that remembered older kings than any on the throne now.

Freya belonged to neither country. That was the arrangement. The crown owned her days; the mountains owned everything else. She slipped between them the way water slips between stones, and had done for nine years, and had never once been caught, because the first rule of survival—before herb-lore, before antidotes, before any of it—was *be no one*. Be the grey dress. Be the empty chair. Be the woman the guards forget by supper.

She took the smuggler's path down through the pines, a track no wider than a deer trail, worn glossy by generations of feet that had agreed, without ever meeting, to pretend it didn't exist. Her boots knew every stone of it. Her shoulders knew where the low branches reached. Once, years ago, a revenue man had waited along this stretch for three nights running and gone home with nothing but a cold, because the traffic on this path simply stopped whenever his kind came sniffing—the mountain telling its own, the way mountains do.

Halfway down, the cramps returned, a final insult from the nightshade-kin, and Freya paused against a boulder until they passed, breath fogging in the dark, and used the still moment to do what she always did on this path: take inventory. Body: recovering, pulse sixty-two, hands steady. Coin: eleven coppers, one silver, hidden in boot and bodice respectively. Notebook: present. Knife: present. Tomorrow's obligations: the market before dawn—wormwood for the tincture, flour, salt, the wax she melted into her boots each autumn—and then the noon tasting, and then—

And then whatever the night offered. Because the other thing the mountains gave her, the thing she never said aloud even to herself, was purpose. Every secret route, every whispered name, every smuggler's toll and herb-wife's grudge she collected was a thread, and she was, thread by thread, weaving a map of the Thornfells that existed nowhere else—not in the crown's archives, not in the abbeys, not in any living memory. Why she wove it, she couldn't have said. Perhaps because a map was the one inheritance a nameless woman could build. Perhaps because knowing the roads felt, in some way she didn't examine, like owning them.

She came down out of the pines as the sky went from black to iron-grey, and the hamlet of Undermere resolved around her: a dozen roofs, a millpond going white at the edges with the season's first skin of ice, woodsmoke standing straight up in the windless cold. Her cottage sat at the hamlet's ragged edge, leaning toward the trees like a listener. She had rented it from a widow who'd gone to live with her daughter in the valley, and the widow had left behind a garden gone gloriously wild with things Freya had spent three years encouraging—feverfew and foxglove and valerian, yarrow and comfrey, and along the north wall, in the shade, the bitter little herbs that had no business growing anywhere but in a taster's garden.

Inside, she banked the fire, ate bread and honey because her stomach demanded blandness and she had learned to obey it, and sat for a while in the grey light with her notebook open, transcribing the night's shorthand into fuller notes while her memory was fresh. *Nightshade-kin, dose approx. 4 drops per cup. Source unknown. Kitchen staff unchanged? Ask Wick's contact about the new under-cook.* She paused over that last line. There was a boy at the market, an orphan called Wick who ran messages and sold kindling and had eyes like a magpie's—for bright things, for dropped things, for things people wished unseen. He'd mentioned a new under-cook hired straight from the lowlands, no references, recommended by a name no one recognized. Probably nothing. It was usually nothing.

Probably.

Freya closed the book, and her arm ached as she reached for the latch-bar, and she glanced down at the birthmark without meaning to. In the firelight it did look darker. Coiled. Almost warm.

*Fanciful*, she told herself, and meant it. Nine years of poison gave a woman a rich imagination for her own body's treacheries; that was all this was, the tail end of the toxin reading itself in ink.

She barred the door, slept four hours by choice and habit, and rose with the market bells.

* * *

Undermere's market gathered where the mountain road met the valley cart-track, at a crossroads old enough that the crossroads marker was less a post than a suggestion—a leaning stone hung with ribbons gone grey, ringed by mud and hoofprints and the trampled ghosts of a hundred years of bargaining. Freya bought her wormwood from the herb-wife Brannoc, who bit each copper coin and complained about the weather with the same religious devotion. She bought flour and salt and, after a visible internal struggle, a strip of salt pork. She was haggling over wax—haggling being less a negotiation than a social obligation performed in the region's native tongue of theatrical grievance—when she noticed the woman.

She was young, younger than Freya, hollow-cheeked in the way of a mother feeding children before herself, and she carried a loaf wrapped in cloth the way one carries something precious and shameful at once. She did not shop. She did not speak to anyone. She drifted to the edge of the market, to the far side of the leaning stone, where the ground fell away toward a fold of hillside—and there, half-swallowed by gorse and moss, stood a shrine Freya had passed a thousand times and never once registered: a waist-high cairn of fieldstones, ancient, lichened, crowned with a flat slab worn smooth as a kneading board. A forgotten god's seat, or a boundary marker, or nothing at all. The sort of thing the eye agreed not to see.

The woman knelt, laid the loaf on the slab, pressed her brow briefly to the stone—her lips moved, shaping words Freya was too far away and too wise to try to catch—and then she rose and hurried away across the mud, shoulders bent, leaving the bread behind like a debt paid to a creditor she feared more than hunger.

Leaving bread she plainly could not afford to lose.

Freya stood with her wax parcel in her hands and told herself it was custom, that mountain folk still fed the old markers at sowing and solstice, that the birds would take it by dusk, and she began to turn back to the wax-seller's grievance—

—and stopped.

Because the bread was already gone. Not scattered, not pecked, not dragged off by crows making their usual racket. Gone, cleanly, completely, between one heartbeat and the next, off a bare stone in open view, without a wingbeat or a shadow or a sound.

The wind moved the gorse. The leaning stone leaned. The market roared on around Freya with its grievances and its copper-biting, and nobody else had seen anything, and the hollow-cheeked woman was a shrinking shape on the valley road, and the flat top of the old cairn gleamed faintly damp in the morning light, as if something had just finished eating.

Freya Underhill, who trusted her eyes above scripture, above the crown, above her own aching stomach, stood very still in the mud of the crossroads and began, methodically, to list every animal that could have taken that loaf and every reason why none of them could have.

It was a short list. It was, she realized, with the first cold needle of a feeling she hadn't let herself feel in years—an empty column.

She bought her wax, and she went home the long way, and she did not look back at the shrine. But all that day, and through the noon tasting, and into the evening while she wrote up her notes in careful shorthand, one thought kept returning to her like a tongue returning to a broken tooth:

In nine years of tasting everything the crown could put in front of her, Freya had never once encountered something she couldn't explain.

Until now.
