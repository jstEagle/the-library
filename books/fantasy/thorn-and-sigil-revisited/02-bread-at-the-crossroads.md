## 2. Bread at the Crossroads

The poison from yesterday was still working its way out of Freya's blood when she came down out of the high pass, and she felt it the way she always did—a fine tremor in her fingertips, a sourness behind her teeth, a weariness so deep it had become a kind of weather. Three days now since the taster's portion. Two more before her body finished arguing with it.

She catalogued the symptoms as she walked, because that was how she kept herself alive. Fingertips: trembling but responsive. Vision: clear at distance, a faint halo around the late sun. Appetite: present, which mattered most of all. The day she stopped being hungry after a dose was the day she'd know something had gone wrong beyond mending. She'd seen it happen to old Tam Wickerdown, back when she was an apprentice to no one and learning the trade from a man who drank himself brave every evening. Tam stopped being hungry. Then he stopped tasting anything at all—said the world had gone to porridge—and within a season he was dead of something the physicians couldn't name and Freya, even at fourteen, could.

*Hemlock and silverleaf, steeped too long.* She'd tasted it in the queen's honeyed wine three nights ago, identified it before the second swallow, swallowed anyway, and then sat through four hours of court while her lips went numb and her heart stuttered like a bird against glass, smiling at nothing, invisible as furniture. That was the trade. Not the swallowing—that was only the coin. The trade was being *unseen*. A taster who drew eyes died young. A taster who became part of the table, part of the wall, part of the air the lords breathed—she lived to be paid another day.

Freya Underhill had been part of the air for eleven years, and she was very good at her work, and nobody thanked her for it, and she had made her peace with that or something shaped enough like peace to pass for it in poor light.

The smuggler's pass spat her out above the valley of Millbrook just as the light went long and gold, and below her the village lay in its bowl like a coin dropped in a purse. She meant only to cut through on her way north, to the croft road, to home. She did not mean to stop.

But hunger has opinions, and hers were loud. She'd eaten nothing since dawn but a heel of black bread and a handful of dried bilberries, and the smell of woodsmoke and baking drifting up from Millbrook made her stomach clench like a fist. Besides, there was a woman at the crossroads.

Freya saw her from two hundred yards and read her the way she read everything: quickly, precisely, without mercy. A village woman, youngish, dressed in clothes gone thin at elbows and knees, patched with cloth that hadn't matched in years. She stood at the little shrine where the crossroads met—the old one, the forgotten one, the moss-eaten waystone with its worn face that might once have been some god or other and was now just a stone children dared each other to touch. Nobody left offerings at that shrine anymore. Nobody had in decades. The new temple in Thornfell town had seen to that, with its incense schedule and its donation box and its priest who smelled of candle wax and disapproval.

The woman knelt in the trampled grass with a bundle in her arms.

Freya slowed her pace without quite deciding to. Curiosity, in her experience, was cheaper than most vices and occasionally paid better.

Up close, the woman was worse off than she'd looked. Younger than Freya had guessed—maybe five-and-twenty—but worn the way river stones are worn, all soft edges over hard bone. Her cheeks had the hollowed look Freya knew intimately, the look of someone who had been feeding her own portion to smaller mouths. There was flour on her hands. Real flour, white and precious, not the grey barley-dust the mountain folk stretched through their loaves.

She didn't notice Freya at first. She was arranging something on the flat top of the waystone with a care that seemed almost liturgical—a square of linen, folded back at the corners, smoothed flat with both palms. And then, from a cloth wrapped inside her shawl, she lifted out the bread.

It was a good loaf. That was the first wrong thing, though Freya didn't understand yet that it was wrong. It was white bread, properly risen, crust glazed dark and shining, the kind of loaf that belonged on a lord's table in Thornfell keep, the kind Freya herself had tasted a hundred times and never once been allowed to eat. In Millbrook. In this year of failed rains and burned granaries and the queen's taxes collected with cheerful punctuality by men with cudgels. White bread here was not food. It was a small miracle, or a small madness.

"You'll want to be careful with that," Freya said, because the woman was setting it down on open stone beside a road traveled by smugglers and soldiers and worse, and because saying something useful was easier than admitting what she actually wanted, which was to ask where in the name of every dead saint a starving woman had gotten such a thing.

The woman flinched, then looked up. Her eyes were red-rimmed and dry, which meant she had cried past the point of crying anymore.

"It's not for eating," the woman said. "Not by us."

"No?"

"It's for the Lady Under the Hill." The woman said it simply, without embarrassment, the way you'd say *it's raining* or *the well's run low*, and something cold moved along Freya's spine that had nothing to do with the mountain wind. "My mam's grandmother used to leave offerings here, when the shrine still had a name. When the rains failed and the fever took my little boy last month and the miller's taking our cottage for debt—" Her voice snagged. She smoothed it flat again, the way she'd smoothed the linen. "I've nowhere else to ask."

"Ask who?" Freya kept her voice gentle. Pity was cheap too, and she had some to spare. "That stone hasn't heard a prayer in a hundred years. Whatever god sat under it is long past hearing."

"The old ones hear," the woman said. "They're waking. Everyone says." She said it the way people said things they needed to be true. "The Widow Crane's well turned sweet again, sweet as spring water, after she poured cream on the roots of her rowan. And the Hendersons' boy—fever three days burning, given up, the physician said given up—and then one morning he sat up asking for porridge. They'd hung a knot-charm on his bedpost. Old knots, the kind nobody ties anymore." She pressed her chapped hands together. "There's no price asked. Not yet. Just... remember them. Leave a little. Say the names."

"And if they don't hear?" Freya asked. "If it's just bread going stale on a rock?"

The woman looked at her then, really looked, and Freya saw herself as the stranger must see her: travel-stained, hooded, armed with a knife and a walking staff, a woman of no village and no obvious allegiance. But the stranger's eyes held no fear. Fear was a luxury; Freya could read that much. When you have nothing, strangers stop being frightening and start being irrelevant.

"Then I'm out one loaf," the woman said. "And we were out a hundred things already. It's cheap, hoping. Hope's the only thing left in this house that doesn't cost."

She laid the loaf on the stone. She arranged it precisely in the center of the linen square, turned it so the scored cross on its crust faced upward, and bowed her head over it. Her lips moved. Freya caught fragments—*Lady of the Hill, keeper of thresholds, hearth-mother, bread-giver*—names so old they sounded less like words than like erosion, like water wearing away stone. Names that had never appeared in any temple service Freya had ever tasted her way through.

Then the woman rose, gathered her empty cloth, and walked away down the western road toward the village, not looking back. That, too, was old practice. You don't watch the offering. Watching is doubt, and doubt spoils the gift.

Freya should have kept walking.

She told herself later that she stayed for practical reasons—to make sure the loaf didn't draw scavengers onto the road while the woman was in sight of it, to note whether any of Corvane's riders passed, to eat whatever remained once it was clearly abandoned. All true, in the way that a dozen plausible reasons can be true around a single unreasonable one. The unreasonable one was this: Freya Underhill did not believe in the old gods. Had never believed in them. Belief required a suspension of vigilance she could not afford, and besides, her whole life was built on the proposition that the world ran on chemistry and cruelty and nothing else. Hemlock was hemlock. Wells went bad because of dead animals in the springs, and fevers broke because they broke, and prayers were the sound desperate people made when they'd run out of useful actions.

She leaned against a birch tree thirty paces off, downwind, half in shadow, and watched the shrine.

The light went from gold to copper to ash. The first moths came out. Somewhere down the valley a dog barked twice and gave up. Freya counted her pulse out of habit—steady, steady, the poison's tremor fading toward sleep—and told herself she would give it until full dark and no longer.

The bread sat on the stone, catching the last light, smelling of yeast and warmth. It was still faintly warm, she realized. Fresh from the oven. The woman had baked it that afternoon—with what flour, on what borrowed hearth, at what cost to a household already bleeding out its substance—she had baked a lord's loaf in a pauper's oven and carried it two miles to kneel at a nameless stone.

*Hope's the only thing left that doesn't cost.*

"That's a lie," Freya murmured to the birches. "Hope costs everything. It's just the only debt you can pay in installments."

Full dark came. A moon rose, three days off full, laying pale silver across the crossroads. Freya pushed off from the tree, meaning finally to go, meaning to take the loaf—no, she wouldn't take the loaf, she decided, surprising herself—or rather, she'd break off a portion, fair payment for guarding it—

The bread vanished.

Not stolen. Not crumbled. Not carried off by fox or crow or the light fingers of a passing beggar, any of which would have been explicable, cataloguable, filed away and forgotten by morning. It *vanished*.

One heartbeat the loaf lay whole on the linen square, silvered by moonlight, its scored cross facing upward. The next heartbeat it was simply not there. No motion. No sound. No shadow departing into the grass. The linen square remained, corners still folded neatly back, undisturbed, as though the bread had never touched it—as though the bread had been *taken through* the stone beneath it, or unmade where it sat, or accepted.

Freya did not move for a long time. Long enough for the moon to shift a hand's width. Long enough for the cold to climb through her boots and settle in her knees.

Her mind, trained for eleven years to detect adulteration in everything, went through its motions. Trick of light: no, she had been looking directly at it, and the moonlight had not changed. Animal: no animal alive lifts a warm loaf off bare stone without sound or disturbance; crows would scatter crumbs, foxes would drag the cloth. A confederate: absurd—who would stage such a thing at a forgotten crossroads for an audience of one hidden woman? A conjurer's wire, a trap, a test? For her? Nobody knew she took this road tonight. She had decided it an hour before she walked it.

The explanations ran out, one by one, like candles guttering in a draft, and what remained when they were gone was the plain fact, sitting in the middle of the crossroads the way the bread had sat: something had taken the offering. Something that answered.

Freya walked forward slowly, knife loose in her hand—not that a knife would help; she understood that with a clarity that frightened her more than the vanishing itself—and knelt at the waystone. Up close, with the moss rubbed away by the woman's careful hands, she could see the carving beneath. She had passed this stone a hundred times and never looked. Now she looked.

A figure. Small, worn nearly to abstraction, but discernible: a woman seated, holding something out in both hands. Bread. A bread-giver. And below her, in lettering older than any alphabet taught in the queen's schools—deep-cut, deliberate, made to last—ran a line of symbols, and the central symbol, larger than the rest, repeated at the start and end of the line like a signature:

A flame, curled like a question mark. A flame wrapped around a hill.

Freya knew that mark. She had known it her whole life without knowing she knew it, the way you know your own face in the dark. It was the shape of the birthmark on her left shoulder blade—the one the midwife had frowned at, the one her grandmother had traced with one finger when Freya was small and said, in the voice she used for important things, *"That's yours to carry, child, and no one else's."*

The wind moved through the birches. On the linen square, where the bread had been, the fabric was still warm.

Freya stood up very slowly. She told herself it was an animal. Some night creature, shy and silent and utterly unknown to her lifetime of mountain lore. She told herself the woman had accomplices, sleight-of-hand, a reason. She told herself she was tired, poisoned, seeing what grief and hunger wanted her to see.

She told herself these things all the way up the croft road, and they held about as well as wet paper holds water, tearing a little more with each step, and beneath them, through them, one thought showed through like bone:

*Everyone says the old ones are waking.*

And she had knelt at the stone. She had touched the carved flame. Her blood-mark, quiet all day, had begun—very faintly, very far down, like an ember breathing under ash—to burn.

At the croft, Freya banked her fire, checked her door-bar twice, and sat awake until dawn with her back against the wall and her knife across her knees, listening to the mountains breathe. In the morning she would tell herself again it was an animal. In the morning she would pack her satchel, count her antidotes, and walk down to Thornfell keep to taste the queen's breakfast, because the crown expected her and invisibility was life.

But she did not sleep, and before the sun cleared the ridge she had made a decision she did not let herself examine: on her way down, she would bake a loaf. White flour—she had a little hoarded against emergencies. She would carry it up past the crossroads, to the mossy stone, and she would set it down, and she would say the names the woman had said.

Just to see, she told herself. Just to rule it out. A taster tested everything before she trusted it.

That was all.

It was, she would understand much later, the first lie she ever told herself that she couldn't swallow clean.
