## 3. The Queen's Long Table

The summons came at dawn, and that alone told Freya something had changed.

In eleven years of service to the crown, she had never once been summoned. She was summoned *for*, the way one summoned a physician or a priest—a tool called when needed, dismissed when not. The taster did not receive invitations; the taster received assignments, delivered by a steward with a list of dishes and a schedule of meals. But this morning a page in royal livery stood outside her door in the Taster's Quarter, breath fogging in the cold, holding a folded card of heavy cream stock sealed with the queen's own sigil.

*Her Grace requires your presence at the long table. Tonight.*

Not the tasting room behind the kitchens, where Freya sat on her plain stool and worked through each course before it traveled the forty paces to the dais. Not the antechamber where visiting dignitaries' food was tested in private. The *long table*. The great hall itself, where the court dined in full view of one another, where the queen held her high feasts and her quiet ones alike, where every face was a performance and every glance a transaction.

Freya read the card three times, then set it on her table beside the mortar and pestle she'd been cleaning, and allowed herself the luxury of thinking the thought she never permitted during working hours: *I don't like this.*

She had rules for disliking things. Rule one: name the dislike precisely. She named it—she did not know why she had been summoned, and not knowing was the most dangerous state a person in her trade could occupy. Rule two: determine what you can control. She could control what she wore, what she ate beforehand, how much antidote she carried in the false seam of her left boot, and how carefully she watched. Rule three: assume the worst until proven otherwise, and dress accordingly.

So she dressed accordingly. Her court gown was grey wool, deliberately unremarkable, cut to make her look narrower than she was, with sleeves long enough to hide the faded scars on her forearms where the poisons of her apprenticeship had written their early lessons. She braided her hair back tight and plain. She took a half-dose of broadleaf tonic—not enough to blunt her palate, which would be malpractice, but enough to steady her stomach against whatever the evening demanded. And she checked the false seam twice, because paranoia, in her experience, was simply foresight wearing an ugly coat.

The Thornfell mountains had taught her that. You learned the passes by walking them in bad weather. You learned which mushrooms lied and which berries kept their promises. You learned that the mountain did not hate you—it simply did not know you existed, and would kill you anyway. Court was the same, except at court, the mountain sometimes noticed you. And being noticed, Freya had long ago concluded, was the single most dangerous thing that could happen to a person whose entire survival depended on being overlooked.

She arrived at the great hall as the light was failing, and the hall swallowed her whole.

It was built to intimidate, and it succeeded. Vaulted ceiling lost in shadow above banks of candles; banners of the crown hanging in ranks down the length of the walls, crimson and gold gone dark as dried blood in the low light; the long table itself running nearly sixty feet from the servants' door to the dais, polished oak reflecting the candleflames like a river of fire. Forty seats were filled tonight—ministers, marshals, foreign envoys, the great landed families of the lowlands in their winter silks. Conversation hummed beneath the music of a string quartet positioned too far away to be properly heard, which Freya suspected was intentional. Music at court was not for listening. It was for filling silence so that no one had to admit they were afraid of it.

She was seated at the far end, below the salt, among the minor functionaries—the keeper of the privy seal's second cousin, a tax assessor, two ambassadors' attaches who spoke only to each other. Perfect. Invisible. She arranged her napkin and let her eyes do what they always did: catalog.

The queen sat at the center of the dais, and Freya studied her with the particular attention she reserved for the person whose life her body was pledged to protect. Queen Maren was forty-one years old and looked older tonight—or perhaps Freya was only now allowing herself to see what she had been declining to see for a year. The queen's hands, resting on either side of her plate, were thin. The skin over her collarbones seemed to have been stretched over the bone by someone else's fingers. She smiled at the Marshal of the Eastern March, and the smile reached her mouth and stopped there, like a traveler who loses heart halfway up a pass.

*Sick,* Freya thought. *Sicker than the court knows. Sicker, maybe, than the queen has admitted to herself.* She had tasted the queen's private meals for years and found nothing wrong with them—no poison, no toxin, nothing a taster could catch. Whatever was eating Queen Maren from inside did its work too quietly for Freya's craft. There were illnesses like that. Her grandmother had called them *the slow guests*: sicknesses that came into the house politely and refused to leave.

Freya filed the observation away and turned her attention to the table itself, because the table was hers. Every dish that emerged from the kitchens passed the tasting station first, where she had already cleared the soup—a venison broth, clean—and the bread, and the butter. The roasts would come next, then the sweetmeats. She had eaten her portions an hour ago in the stone-walled tasting room, sitting alone with her ledger, recording times, textures, aftertastes. Everything had been clean. Everything was always, almost always, clean. The poisons she caught were rare now; the last attempt on the queen's life had been four years past, a slow arsenic in the honey wine that Freya had identified by the faint almond ghost of it and the way the wax seal on the cask had been re-poured by a left-handed man. They had hanged the cellarman. Freya had gone home and vomited from the arsenic dose and slept for two days, and no one had thanked her, and she had not expected anyone to.

That was the trade. That was the whole of it. You swallowed the wolf so the kingdom could sleep, and the kingdom never learned your name.

The first courses moved through the hall in their choreography of silver and steam. Freya ate mechanically, cataloging, watching the queen eat what she had cleared and feeling the small, worn satisfaction of another night survived. Weary pride, her grandmother would have called it. Pride like a stone you carried in your shoe: painful, familiar, and somehow proof that you were still walking.

Then the seventh course arrived, and everything changed.

It came up from the kitchens under a covered salver, borne by a footman Freya didn't recognize—which was the first wrongness, because she knew every servant in the palace kitchens by face and gait, and this man was tall and narrow and moved like someone doing an impression of servitude rather than practicing it. The second wrongness was the dish itself when he lifted the cover: a small game hen, glazed dark with honey and something else, served on a bed of bittercress, garnished with a sprig of a herb Freya could not immediately name. The third wrongness was that it had not passed her station. She had cleared six courses tonight. This was the seventh, and it had come to the table without touching her lips.

She felt the chill move up her spine like water finding a channel.

Around her, the other diners received the same course—she saw the salvers moving down the tables—but Freya's attention had narrowed to a point. She looked toward the dais. The queen had been served the same dish. So had the Lord Chancellor, the Marshal, the visiting envoy from Callowmere. A full court course, untested. That did not happen. In eleven years, that had *never* happened, and there were only two explanations: catastrophic failure in the kitchen chain of command, or intention.

Freya signaled the under-steward, a nervous boy named Tam who owed his position to her quiet word two winters ago. He leaned down.

"The seventh course," she murmured. "Who cleared it?"

Tam's face did something complicated. "Begging your pardon, mistress, but—" he dropped his voice further, "—it come with orders. From upstairs. Said it weren't to go through tasting. Said it were already seen to."

"Seen to by whom?"

"I don't know, mistress." And by the sweat on his upper lip, Freya believed him.

She sat back. The hall continued its glittering noise around her. The hen sat on her plate, fragrant, the glaze catching the candlelight in a way that reminded her unpleasantly of lacquer. She had three options, and she ran through them with the speed of long practice. One: refuse the dish publicly, which would cause a scene, draw every eye in the hall, and mark her permanently. Two: send it back on some pretext, which would achieve the same result more slowly. Three—

Three was what a taster was for.

She picked up her knife and fork. If the dish was meant for the queen, then every minute it sat uneaten on the dais was a minute of danger, and if it was meant for *her*—and some cold, quiet part of her mind had already begun whispering that possibility—then refusing it would tell whoever had sent it exactly what she suspected. Either way, the mathematics came out the same. She cut a small portion of the breast meat, took a sliver of the glaze, a leaf of the bittercress, and the unnamed herb, and put it all in her mouth.

For ten seconds, nothing. Game bird, well-hung, cooked competently. Honey, good honey, mountain honey with the resin note of pine feedstock. Bittercress, correct bitterness. The unknown herb was thyme-adjacent, oily, with a peppery finish that sat strangely on the tongue—

And then the burn began.

It started low, in the pit of her stomach, a warmth like she had swallowed a coal from a hearth. Not the heat of poison—Freya knew the heat of poison intimately, had a mental library of every species of it, the creeping colds and the prickling fires and the numbnesses that started in the fingertips. This was none of those. This heat *rose*. It climbed her sternum like a hand pulling itself upward rung by rung, spread across her chest, and gathered—there was no other word for it—gathered at her left shoulder blade, just below her neck, where she had been born with a birthmark the size of a spread hand.

The mark had never done anything in her life. It was brownish-red, sigil-shaped—her mother had joked it looked like a coiled serpent swallowing its own tail, though Freya had privately always thought it resembled nothing so much as a stylized flame—and it was as inert as any other quirk of her skin. Now it burned like a brand pressed fresh from the forge.

Freya did not move. This was the discipline of her life distilled into a single moment: she did not flinch, did not gasp, did not let her knife so much as click against her plate. She finished chewing. She swallowed. Inside, the heat crested, peaked, and began—slowly, grudgingly—to ebb, leaving behind a strange ache, deep and muscular, as though the mark had been clenched for years and had finally released.

She breathed. Once. Twice. Around her, the court ate the same dish and felt nothing. On the dais, the queen ate her portion and showed no sign of discomfort. Whatever this was, it was not poison. Poison did not choose its victim by blood.

*It chose me.*

The thought arrived with terrible clarity, and with it, the awareness that she was still sitting in a hall full of people, any of whom might be watching. Freya lifted her wine cup, drank to wet her throat, and let her gaze travel the table with the idle, bored expression she had perfected over a decade of invisibility. Cataloging. Searching.

She found him on her second sweep.

Lord Corvane, the queen's spymaster, was seated halfway up the high table, and he was not looking at the queen, or at the Chancellor, or at the Callowmere envoy whose alliance everyone pretended mattered. He was looking down the length of the hall—at her. He held a goblet he was not drinking from. His expression was pleasant, attentive, the mild look of a man following a distant conversation. But his eyes were fixed on Freya with a focus that had weight to it, physical, almost tactile, like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades directly over the burning mark.

He did not look away when she met his gaze. That was the worst of it. Courteous men looked away. Guilty men looked away. Corvane simply held her eyes for a count of five—she counted, because counting was what she did instead of panicking—and then inclined his head slightly, a small courteous nod, as if acknowledging an acquaintance across a market square. Then he returned to his dinner as though nothing had occurred, and began speaking warmly to the Chancellor about grain tariffs.

Freya's mouth had gone dry despite the wine.

She knew Corvane the way everyone at court knew Corvane: the spymaster, the queen's quiet right hand, a man of perhaps fifty with iron-grey hair worn unfashionably long and a reputation for two things—unmatched loyalty to the crown, and an unsettling habit of knowing things about people before the people themselves knew them. He had risen under the old king and survived the transition to Queen Maren, which told Freya he was either indispensable or very careful, and she suspected both. She had spoken with him perhaps four times in eleven years. Each conversation had been brief, courteous, and had left her feeling like a specimen briefly removed from its case and replaced before anyone noticed.

Now the case was open, and the collector was looking.

*Think,* she ordered herself. *What does he know? What did that dish carry?*

The herb. She turned her mind to the unnamed herb, running through her grandmother's teachings, the old mountain lore that predated the court pharmacopoeia by a thousand years. An oily, thyme-scented leaf with a peppery finish that burned the blood rather than the belly. There was a plant the high shepherds used to call *drakenwurt*—dragonwort—in the oldest songs, said to grow only where dragonfire had scorched the earth centuries ago. Nobody harvested it anymore. Nobody remembered what it was for. Except, apparently, somebody in the queen's own kitchens, or above them, had remembered enough to glaze seven courses with it and serve it to a hall full of people who would feel nothing at all.

Nothing at all—except, perhaps, one person. One person born with a certain mark.

The heat was fading now, retreating back into her shoulder blade like a tide going out, but it left her shaken in a way she hadn't been since her first year of apprenticeship, when she'd taken hemlock by accident and spent three days learning the precise geography of her own mortality. This was worse than hemlock. Hemlock wanted to kill her. Whatever this was had *recognized* her.

The meal crawled onward. Freya ate nothing more of the seventh course—she had quietly scraped the remainder beneath the bittercress, a taster's trick—and declined the sweetmeats with a headache she did not have. The quartet played. The Chancellor told a story about a bear and a bishop that everyone laughed at on cue. And twice more during the remaining courses, Freya felt it again: that flat, patient pressure of Corvane's attention settling between her shoulder blades, deliberate as a palm placed on a door to see if it was locked.

When the queen rose to withdraw, the hall rose with her, and the feast dissolved into its aftermath of bowing and cloak-fetching. Freya moved with the crowd toward the doors, unhurried, one grey figure among dozens, and she was nearly clear—nearly into the corridor, nearly into the anonymity she had spent a decade building around herself like a wall—when a voice spoke at her elbow.

"Mistress Underhill."

Corvane. He had crossed the hall without her seeing him move, which should not have been possible in that crowd, in that light. Up close he was unremarkable in the way of dangerous things: medium height, neat dress, a face designed to be forgotten. Only his eyes contradicted the rest of him—pale grey, quick, and lit with an interest he made no effort to disguise.

"My lord." She bowed the exact depth required for a crown servant to a privy councilor. Neither more nor less.

"You are the queen's taster." Not a question. "Eleven years in the post, is it? Remarkable tenure. Most tasters don't last three."

"The mountains teach patience, my lord."

"Do they." He smiled, and it was a warm smile, genuinely warm, and that frightened her more than anything else that had happened tonight. "I've often thought your profession must be a lonely one. Swallowing death nightly so that others may dine in peace, and never a word of thanks. I want you to know that your service is noted, Mistress Underhill. Noted and valued. More than you know."

Every instinct she owned was screaming at her, and she kept her face smooth as pond ice. "Your Grace is kind."

"Merely observant." He stepped back, releasing her, and his pale eyes flickered—just once, briefly, involuntarily—to her left shoulder. To the place beneath the grey wool where the mark lay quiet and aching. Then he bowed, precisely, and melted into the departing crowd, and was gone.

Freya stood in the emptying corridor a long moment, listening to her own heartbeat.

*More than you know.*

She walked home through the cold, taking the long way, checking the shadows out of habit and finding, for once, that the habit offered no comfort. The palace behind her blazed with light; ahead, the Taster's Quarter huddled dark and modest against the cliff. Somewhere above the city, the Thornfell peaks stood black against the stars, and somewhere beneath them—she did not know why this thought came, only that it arrived with the certainty of a symptom—something old had smelled her blood tonight and stirred in its sleep.

Back in her rooms, she barred the door, lit one candle, and pulled off the grey gown. In the small looking-glass, by guttering light, she examined the mark for the hundredth time in her life and the first time with fear. It looked the same. Coiled, sigil-shaped, a flame swallowing its tail. But when she pressed her fingertip to it, the ache answered—deep, resonant, like a bell struck hours ago still humming in the metal.

She wrote in her ledger, because writing was how she thought and how she survived. *Seventh course. Untested. Herb unidentified—likely drakenwurt. No toxic effect on court. Mark responded. Corvane observed. Corvane knows.*

She stared at the last two words a long time. Then, because a taster's first duty is to herself, she added a final line:

*Assume the worst. Prepare accordingly.*

Outside, far above the sleeping city, snow was beginning to fall on the high passes of the Thornfells—falling, though no one marked it, on a croft long abandoned, where above a cold hearth a carved sigil waited under the dust for someone to come home and read it.
