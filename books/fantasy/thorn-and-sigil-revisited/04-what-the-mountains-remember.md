## 4. What the Mountains Remember

Freya told herself she was going up the mountain for herbs.

It was a good lie. She had been telling good lies her whole life—to the queen's stewards about the state of her stores, to the court physicians about the precise hour her symptoms faded, to herself, most often and most skillfully of all, whenever the truth grew too heavy to carry in plain language. So she packed her gathering basket with shears and linen squares and a little trowel, and she wrote *drakenwurt survey, high meadows* in her ledger in her neat, upright hand, and at first light on the third day after the feast she went out through the postern gate with a hooded cloak and a face like a woman thinking about moss.

The guards did not look at her. Nobody ever looked at the taster. That was the whole architecture of her survival: a person so thoroughly unremarkable that even Corvane's web of informants had never thought to spin a thread around her—until now, perhaps. She felt watched anyway. She felt watched the way one feels a draft from a door left open somewhere in a large house.

She took the smuggler's pass instead of the pilgrim road. It added half a day, but it was hers—the way through the Thornfells that existed on no chart, taught to her by a dead grandmother who had learned it from a dead grandfather who had learned it from no one living would admit. The path began behind the shrine of Saint Aldhelm-the-Forgotten, which was fitting, because everything in these mountains was forgotten by somebody, and the mountains kept what the lowlands discarded.

By noon she was above the snowline, and the world had gone quiet in that particular high-altitude way that made a person's own breathing sound like company.

*You are being foolish,* she told herself, climbing. *The mark burned. Marks burn. Spices burn skin; metals do stranger things still; you have seen court ladies weep over rings that turned their fingers green. A birthmark warming under an unfamiliar herb is not a summons.*

But she climbed.

She climbed because there was a croft two days' walk into the high country where her grandmother Maren—no, not Maren, that was the queen's name, and the coincidence had always sat oddly in Freya's chest like a swallowed stone—where her grandmother *Sorrel* Underhill had lived alone for forty years and died alone eleven years ago, and no one had gone back. Not Freya, who had been nineteen and hungry and newly sworn to the crown's table. Not anyone. The mountain folk said the place was unlucky. The mountain folk said a lot of things, and lately, Freya thought grimly, the mountain folk were saying them and then watching bread vanish off shrine-stones.

She had not been able to stop thinking about that bread.

Three days now, and the image returned every time she closed her eyes: the loaf sitting on the frozen altar, gray with frost, whole and honest; the blink; the absence. No tracks. No crows. Crows would have taken the bread but they'd have left prints, and crows did not take bread whole and silent while a starving woman stood ten feet away with her eyes open. Freya had interrogated the memory the way she interrogated every dish set before her—color, smell, texture, context, the angle of the light—and the memory had passed every test of strangeness and failed every test of sense.

And last night, unable to sleep, she had done something worse than remember. She had opened the old family ledger. Not her working ledger—the other one, the one bound in cracked green leather with a brass clasp gone brown, the one Sorrel had pressed into her hands at fourteen with the words *"when you're ready,"* and which Freya had never once been ready for.

Her grandmother's handwriting filled its pages, small and slanted and certain. Recipes, yes—but also other things. Notes on plants that did not appear in any herbal Freya had ever seen in the palace library. Remedies keyed not to symptoms but to names, as if a fever could be addressed personally. And on the inside cover, drawn once, precisely, with a ruler's care:

A sigil. A flame within a circle, the flame's three tongues curled inward like fingers closing around something held.

Freya knew that shape. She had known it all her life without knowing she knew it, the way you know your own heartbeat. It was the shape of the birthmark on her left forearm—a birthmark everyone had always called a scald, a stain, a sign her mother had gripped too hard when she came into the world. Three inches long if it was anything, pale as old scar tissue, in the exact form of a flame curled around a secret.

She had compared them last night by candlelight, ledger beside bare arm, and the match had been so perfect it felt less like discovery than like confirmation of something she had always refused to confirm.

So: drakenwurt survey, high meadows. A good lie. The best kind, which was the kind that was almost true, since drakenwurt did grow up here, and she did intend to gather some, and if along the way she happened to climb past the tree line to a croft that had no business standing after eleven winters—

Well. Tasters learned early that the truth traveled best in disguise.

* * *

The croft stood where it had always stood, in the cupped palm of a valley too narrow to appear on maps, sheltered from the worst winds by a shoulder of granite. Snow lay on its slate roof in a smooth unbroken sheet, and the chimney rose out of the white like a bone out of flesh. The turf walls had held. Underhill hands had built those walls, Freya reminded herself—hands that knew stone and weather the way priests knew psalms—and Underhill hands, apparently, built better than they explained.

There should have been a door. There wasn't; Sorrel had taken it down before she died, or someone had, and the doorway gaped black and patient, hung with a curtain of icicles that glittered like teeth.

Freya stood in the snow for a long moment, basket on her hip, breath smoking, and felt the strange vertigo of returning to a place that had been waiting for her.

"Grandmother," she said aloud, and her voice fell into the silence and did not echo, as if the valley drank sound. "I'm sorry I didn't come sooner."

Nothing answered. But the cold seemed to shift its weight, the way a sleeping animal shifts, acknowledging without waking.

She broke the icicles away carefully—they fell and shattered like glassware at a feast—and stepped inside.

Dust everywhere, thick as fur, silvered by the gray light coming through gaps in the turf roof. A table with one leg mended with antler. Two chairs. A bed with a blanket still folded, though mice had eaten much of it into lace. On the mantel shelf, jars: Sorrel's jars, rows of them, labeled in that same small slanted hand. Freya read the labels and her professional heart stuttered. *Wolfsbane, for the low fever that walks at dusk. Ash-root, ground fine, for the child who hears voices. Marrow-mint — only with the sigil uncovered.* 

Only with the sigil uncovered.

Her arm prickled under its layers, absurdly, as if the birthmark had heard itself mentioned.

She set down the basket and worked with the discipline of her trade, because work was how she kept fear at arm's length. She catalogued the jars, noting which contents had survived, which had rotted to dust, which had somehow—this troubled her—not rotted at all. Three jars near the hearth end of the shelf looked exactly as they must have looked the day Sorrel sealed them, wax uncracked, contents vivid. Eleven years. Nothing organic kept like that. Nothing natural.

*Like the bread,* whispered a voice in her head that sounded unpleasantly reasonable. *Whole and honest and then gone.*

"Not the same thing," she said firmly, to the empty room, and hated how loud her own voice was.

The hearth itself was the largest thing in the room, a great stone throat wide enough to roast a lamb whole, and it had been the center of Freya's childhood summers more than the bed or the table had been. Here Sorrel had brewed and baked and talked, endlessly talked, though never plainly—always sideways, always in riddles wrapped in recipes. *Mind the fire, child. Fire remembers what it's fed. Mind what you feed it.*

Freya knelt in the ash—actual ash, decades of cold fires sifted into a gray velvet floor—and looked up at the lintel stone above the hearth opening.

And stopped breathing.

Someone had carved into that stone. Carved deep, deep enough to have survived smoke and frost and time, deep enough that the soot of a hundred years of cooking fires had only served to outline it, so that now, with the fire long dead and the light falling just so through the broken roof, the carving stood out in shadowed relief like a bruise rising on pale skin.

A flame within a circle. Three tongues curling inward, closing around something held.

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought—or perhaps a dedication, or perhaps a warning—ran a line of letters in the old mountain script that the priests called heathen runes and the smugglers called trail-sign and Sorrel had called simply *the true writing*, teaching it to Freya one summer with sticks in the dirt, never explaining why a girl needed to read a script no one else alive could parse.

Freya's lips moved. The reading came back slowly, like walking a road after many years, and each word arrived colder than the last.

*UNDERHILL KEEPS THE DOOR.*

She sat back on her heels in the ash. Her arm was burning now—actually burning, not prickling, a slow heat spreading across the birthmark as if someone had laid a warm coin against her skin—and she pushed back her sleeve with hands that were steadier than they had any right to be, because panic was a poison too and she had trained her whole life against poisons.

The mark had darkened. In the gray light it no longer looked like scar tissue. It looked like the carving above the hearth, rendered in flesh: the flame, the circle, the three curled tongues. As if the stone had been copied from her arm, or her arm from the stone, and the question of which came first was suddenly, horribly, not academic.

"Keeps the door," she said. "Keeps *what* door?"

The room declined to answer. Rooms are like that. But the silence had a texture now, a listening quality, and Freya—who had spent years learning to taste the difference between a clean dish and a dosed one, between emptiness and absence—tasted the silence and found it full.

She got up. She searched the croft the way she did everything, methodically, turning the search into procedure so it could not turn into terror. Under the loose flagstone by the bed: a strongbox, empty but for a dried sprig of something purple that she did not recognize and that hummed faintly against her fingertips, or perhaps that was her blood humming, or perhaps she was finally going mad, which would honestly be a relief. In the chest beneath the window: winter clothes, a woman's, moth-eaten, smelling of cedar and cold. In the rafters: nothing but soot and spiderwebs.

And tucked behind the mantel shelf, wedged where only someone with a taster's eye for concealed compartments—someone raised by the woman who built it—would think to look: a bundle of oilcloth tied with waxed cord.

Letters. Dozens of them, bundled by year, in Sorrel's hand, addressed to no one—or rather, addressed in the salutation line, over and over, in varying degrees of urgency and fading ink, to the same absent recipient:

*To my heir, whoever you are, whenever you come.*

Freya sat down on her grandmother's chair, dust rising around her like a slow gray ghost, and opened the earliest letter with fingers that trembled only slightly, and only at the very tips, which for Freya constituted open weeping.

*Child,* the letter began. *If you are reading this, then either the door is failing or you are, and I am past helping either way. First: the mark on your arm is not a stain. Do not let anyone call it one, least of all yourself. Second: you will have noticed by now that certain things answer you that do not answer others. This is not imagination. Third, and most important: our blood does not make the seal. Our blood feeds it. There is a difference, and the difference is everything, and the crown—if it is still the crown in your day—must never learn it.*

Freya read it twice. Then a third time. Outside, the light was dying, blue shadows pooling in the valley like water finding its level, and she did not light a fire because lighting a fire in this hearth suddenly felt like knocking on a door, and she did not yet know what she would do if it opened.

*They will tell you the sigil-bearer is a sacrifice,* Sorrel had written, in a later letter, the ink hurried, the letters pressed deep enough to tear the page. *That is the story the lowlanders made when they forgot the truth. We were never meant to pour ourselves out. We are keepers. The flame closes as well as opens, child. Remember that when they come smiling.*

*When they come smiling.*

Freya thought of Lord Corvane at the feast, his eyes resting on her with the mild attention of a man admiring a painting he intended to acquire. She thought of the seventh course, the unidentified herb, her mark leaping to heat like a hound hearing its name. She thought of the queen's thin, hungry face, and of bread vanishing at a roadside shrine, and of wells sweetened and fevers broken in villages that had prayed themselves hoarse, and of the price that miracles, in her experience of the world, always eventually demanded.

Somewhere below her, far under miles of granite and older things than granite, something stirred in its sleep and dreamed of doors.

Freya folded the letters with great care, tied them, and put them inside her cloak against her body, where their paper warmth could be mistaken for her own. Then she gathered her basket, and the purple-sprigged jar, and three of the impossible preserved ones, and stood a last moment before the hearth with its carved commandment.

*Underhill keeps the door.*

"I never asked for this," she told the stone quietly. It was not quite a protest. It was not quite acceptance. It was the honest ledger-entry of a woman whose whole art was precision: *inheritance received; weight unknown; assume the worst.*

The hearth, the room, the listening dark—none of them answered. But as she turned to go, the birthmark on her forearm pulsed once, gently, unmistakably, like a second heartbeat answering the first. Like something on the far side of a very old wall, turning toward her in the dark.

Two days later, back at court, a page in palace livery caught her sleeve in a corridor and delivered, with bored efficiency, a message that stopped her breath: Lord Corvane requested the pleasure of the royal taster's company. He wished, the note said, to discuss her family history.

He had used the word *family* as though he knew it belonged to her. He had used it as though he knew it was a door.
