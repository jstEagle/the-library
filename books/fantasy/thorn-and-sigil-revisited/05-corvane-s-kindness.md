## 5. Corvane's Kindness

The census rolls smelled of mouse and mildew and, faintly, of the tallow candles that had burned above them for a hundred years before anyone thought to move them somewhere drier. Lord Aldous Corvane found the smell comforting. Archives were honest in a way people never were. Paper could not lie; it could only be ignored, and he had built a career on being the man who did not ignore it.

He sat alone in the undercroft of the Raven's Rest, the spymaster's residence—a building that had once been a monastery and still kept the habits of one, all stone and silence and long corridors where footsteps went to die. Three lamps burned at his elbow. Before him lay an open ledger bound in cracked calfskin, its pages soft as cloth with age, and on those pages, in a clerk's careful hand gone brown as old tea, was a name.

*Underhill, Maren-of-that-line. Sigil-marked. Hearth-keeper, Thornfell.*

And beneath it, entered eleven years later in a different ink: *Underhill, last of line. Female child. Mark confirmed at naming. Entered into the Taster's Registry by order of the Crown.*

Corvane read the entry again. Then he closed the ledger, laid both hands flat upon its cover, and allowed himself—for precisely the length of one slow breath—the luxury of satisfaction.

There she was. There *she* was.

Freya Underhill. The queen's taster. The woman who swallowed poison every evening so that Her Majesty could dine without fear, who walked through the court like a shadow given chores, whom half the nobility could not pick out of a lineup of servants and the other half dismissed as a necessary unpleasantness, like a latrine or a headsman. Eleven years in the palace and no allies, no lovers, no debts worth the name, no faction. Invisible.

Or she had been.

Corvane rose, crossed to the shelf behind him, and drew down a slim folio marked with the sigil of the Office of Whispers—his office, though the seal on the door said something more boring, because the best rooms were always labeled with lies. Inside the folio were his own notes, compiled over three weeks of quiet inquiry, each fact sourced twice.

The dish had been quail in honeyed rowan sauce, served at the autumn equinox feast. The cook's ledger showed the rowan berries had come from a new supplier in the Thornfell foothills—berries from trees that grew, if the surveyor's map was accurate, within a league of the old Underhill croft. When the taster had taken her portion, she had flinched. A small thing. A twitch at the collarbone, quickly mastered. Corvane had watched her hands afterward: steady, professional, unbothered. But he had also watched her sleep that night—or rather, he had read the report of the chambermaid he'd placed in the servants' wing two years prior, a girl named Sela who earned her keep by noticing—and Sela wrote that the taster had not slept. That she had sat up past the third bell with her shift pulled from one shoulder, looking down at her own skin by candlelight.

Looking at her mark.

Corvane poured himself a measure of plum brandy from the decanter on his desk and did not drink it. He rarely drank. He liked his vices the way he liked his knives: displayed, admired, and never actually used.

The old texts were maddeningly incomplete—the archives of the Dragon Wars having been purged, twice, by monarchs who preferred their history without teeth—but what remained agreed on the essentials. Before the wars, there had been a bloodline sworn to the keeping of the deep prison beneath Thornfell. Not priests. Not kings. *Keepers.* Their blood carried something the old books called the hearth-fire, and their bodies bore the sign of it: a mark shaped like nothing so much as a flame folded inside a shield, or a shield made of flame, depending on which monk had been doing the describing. The mark responded to dragon-workings. It burned near the old seals. And it could, if the texts were to be believed—and Corvane believed them, because the texts were paper and paper did not lie—do considerably more than burn.

One text, a fragment of a fragment, spoke of the *opening*. Another, older, spoke only of the *keeping*. Corvane had spent three weeks and a regrettable amount of gold establishing which was true, and had gotten nowhere, and had decided it did not matter yet. First things first. You did not carve the roast before you caught the pig.

Freya Underhill was the pig. She simply didn't know it yet.

He finished the brandy without tasting it and went up to bed, where he slept the dreamless sleep of a man whose conscience had been filed away decades ago, in a drawer, in an archive very much like this one.

* * *

Three days later, he arranged his first kindness.

It was a small thing, engineered to look like an accident of bureaucracy. The taster's quarterly stipend had been delayed—not stopped, merely delayed, a clerical error involving a misdated warrant that would take, oh, six or seven days to untangle through normal channels. Freya Underhill, Corvane knew from Sela's reports and from the pawnbroker's records his office quietly purchased every month, lived close to her coin. She sent money down-mountain. To whom, the records did not say; the remittance house in Thornfell village was discreet even by mountain standards. But the pattern was clear enough: a woman supporting someone, or something, in the hills.

So when the stipend stalled, and the taster came to the paymaster's office on the fourth day with her receipt-book and her patient, tired face, Corvane happened to be there.

He was very good at happening to be places.

"Lady Underhill," he said—it pleased him to give her the courtesy title her bloodline technically carried, and it pleased him more to watch her startle at hearing it. "Trouble with the paymaster?"

"My lord." She bowed the precise degree required for a servant addressing a great officer of state, no more. "A delay. It will sort itself out."

"Delays have a way of becoming habits." He turned to the paymaster's clerk, a nervous man whose gambling debts Corvane held like leashes. "Warrant for the taster's stipend. I believe it was misfiled. See to it this afternoon."

The warrant appeared within the hour. Corvane made sure to be passing through the courtyard when Freya collected it, and made sure to say, in a voice pitched for her ears alone: "The mountains send us their best and forget them fastest. I try not to be that kind of host."

She looked at him then—really looked, for the first time—with eyes the grey-green of wet slate, and he saw the calculation in them, quick and cold and thorough. Good. Clever women were harder to groom but far more useful once groomed. Stupid trust broke; intelligent suspicion, patiently converted, became loyalty of iron.

"Thank you, my lord," she said, and there was not one grain of warmth in it, and he loved that about her.

"You're welcome," he said warmly, for both of them.

* * *

The second kindness took longer, because it required a death.

Not hers, obviously. Not yet, in any case—one did not kill the goose, and besides, the texts were clear that the blood had to be given freely at the moment of working, or at least appear to be. A dead bearer's blood was ash. This was inconvenient, and it meant that everything Corvane did from here forward had to wear the face of friendship.

The death belonged to a porter named Hask, an aging drunk in the kitchens who had been selling scraps of palace gossip to a Thornfell wool merchant for coppers, entirely unaware that the wool merchant sold to Corvane, who resold upward at a considerable markup. Hask was, in short, a leak. In any other season Corvane would have let him dribble harmlessly along. But this season Hask had begun asking questions about the taster—where she went on her days down-mountain, whether she received letters, whether she ever met with strangers at the crossroads shrine.

Someone else was watching Freya Underhill. Someone using drunks and merchants instead of trained instruments, which meant someone local, someone impatient, someone who had noticed what Corvane had noticed and lacked the patience to do it properly.

That would not do. Rivals were untidy.

Hask fell from the granary stairs on a Tuesday. Drunk men fell from stairs; it was practically a civic institution. The kitchen mourned him with genuine affection and free ale, and within the week, Corvane arranged for the taster to overhear—through Sela, naturally—that the spymaster had known about Hask's little trade for months and had protected him from worse consequences out of, quote, *a certain respect for hungry men*.

Let her chew on that. Let her think: *he is hard, but he is fair. He sees people like me—the invisible ones—and he does not crush them for sport.*

The truth, that Hask had died because his questions might have drawn another player to the board before Corvane had finished setting it, was nobody's business but his own.

* * *

Winter came down off the mountains early that year, and with it came the third kindness, and the first conversation that mattered.

He found her in the herb garden behind the infirmary, on a morning so cold the rosemary had gone silver with frost. She was cutting stems with a paring knife, her breath smoking, her movements economical as a housewife counting coins. Up close, Corvane noted what the court's gossip never bothered to note: she was handsome, in a weathered way, with lines around her eyes that spoke of squinting into mountain sun, and hands that were a working apothecary's hands, scarred and sure.

"Taster Underhill." He inclined his head. "You know your plants."

"I know my poisons, my lord. The herbs are collateral knowledge." She didn't stop cutting. Bold, for a servant. Or perhaps just tired; exhaustion made bold people of the careful ones, eventually.

"A valuable trade." He drifted along the bed beside her, hands clasped behind his back, a man admiring winter roses. "My mother grew rosemary. She swore it kept the melancholy out of a house." A lie; his mother had grown nothing but grievances. But the detail was warm, domestic, humanizing, and he delivered it with the exact wistfulness of a man remembering better days. "Tell me—do you miss the mountains?"

The knife paused. Only for a heartbeat. But Corvane had spent thirty years reading heartbeats.

"They are my home, my lord."

"And yet you've been here eleven years." He plucked a frost-killed rose, turned it in his fingers, let it fall. "Eleven years swallowing poison for a table that doesn't know your name. Forgive me—I make it my business to know names. Freya. It's a mountain name, isn't it? Old stock."

"Old enough, my lord."

"The Underhills." He said it lightly, idly, a man remarking on weather. "Hearth-keepers, the old rolls call them. There was quite a family once. Croft above the Thornfell pass. Gone now, of course." He watched her profile with the patience of a fisherman watching a float. Nothing moved in her face. Excellent training. But her breathing had shallowed by a fraction, and the knife had resumed cutting at a slightly different angle, and these were the tells that faces learned to hide and bodies never did.

"Gone, my lord?" she said. Perfectly level. Perfectly curious, in the way of a servant making conversation.

"Dead out, mostly. The rolls list a last child, born up-mountain, brought into the Taster's Registry some years back." He smiled at the rosemary, not at her. "I confess I find such survivals moving. A whole line reduced to one woman, standing between a queen and her poison, thanked by no one. There's a wrongness in that. If you ever find yourself in need of a friend at court, Taster Underhill—" he finally turned to her, and put every ounce of sincere warmth he possessed into his face, and it was a great deal, because he had practiced— "my door is less frightening than its reputation."

She met his eyes. Grey-green, wet-slate eyes, and behind them the calculation again, turning over and over like a millwheel. Weighing him. Weighing the offer. Weighing, he suspected, exactly how much it might cost to refuse.

"That is kind of you, my lord," she said.

"It is practical," he corrected gently. "Kindness is just strategy that has learned good manners."

She almost smiled. Almost. "I'll remember that, my lord."

He left her to her frost and her rosemary, and walked back across the frozen garden with the light, unhurried tread of a man whose day had gone well.

* * *

That night, in the undercroft, he opened a fresh folio and wrote at its head, in his small precise hand: *Underhill, F. — Cultivation.*

Below it, he set down what he had learned today. That she feared him—he had seen it, buried deep, a hare's stillness—and that fear, properly fed, ripened into reliance. That she had lied to him about the Underhills, or at least withheld, which amounted to the same thing and was far more interesting. A servant with nothing to hide would have said *never heard of them, my lord* with the flat boredom of truth. Freya Underhill had asked *gone?* in a voice carefully sanded smooth, and only a person with something buried asks questions like that.

She knew what she was. Or she knew enough to be afraid of knowing.

Better and better.

He dipped his pen again. *Next mercies:* the winter cloak allowance (delayed, then restored, with his compliments implied but never spoken). A word in the right ear to keep the master-at-arms' son from harassing her boy—if she took an assistant, and she would, solitary women in hostile houses always eventually took someone small to protect; it was a weakness he intended to encourage. Perhaps, in spring, a gift of mountain honey from a supplier she'd recognize as home-country. Small things. Threads. A web is not woven in a day, nor in silk, but in patience.

At the bottom of the page he wrote the folio's true purpose, the one line that would never appear in any report:

*The blood must be willing. Make her willing. And if willingness fails—make her grateful enough that she gives it before she understands what she has given.*

He blotted the page, closed the folio, and sat for a while in the lamplight, listening to the monastery silence of the Raven's Rest. Somewhere below the city, beneath the mountain, whatever slept down there had slept for three hundred years, and the crown had forgotten why, and the smallfolk were already beginning to pray to things that answered.

He had read the reports too—the sweetened wells, the broken fevers, the bread vanishing from shrines. His colleagues called it superstition. Corvane called it inventory. The gods were waking, and waking things could be owned, and the key to the lock was a tired woman with a birthmark who cut rosemary in the frost and thought herself invisible.

Soon, Taster Underhill, he thought, blowing out the lamps one by one. Soon you and I will be great friends.

In the dark, on his desk, the open ledger of the old census rolls stirred in a draft from the window, pages turning themselves back—to the entry, to the name, to the mark confirmed at naming—as though the paper itself were reminding him, patiently, hungrily, of everything written there.
