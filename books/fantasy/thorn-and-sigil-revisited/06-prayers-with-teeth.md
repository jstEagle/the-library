## 6. Prayers with Teeth

The first miracle came to Millbrook, and it came the way most dangerous things come to small places: quietly, on a Tuesday, to a woman nobody much listened to.

Freya heard about it three days later, in the back room of Tam Vetch's alehouse, where the smugglers drank and the taster counted her poisons. She had come down from the pass with aching joints and a mouth still numb from the morning's work—a nice bit of spotted hemlock in the queen's pheasant pie, nothing dramatic, just enough to keep her hands steady for the rest of the day—and she wanted nothing more than bread, beer, and a chair that didn't move.

Instead she got Hesta Cobb, the miller's widow, standing on a bench with a loaf held over her head like a trophy.

"Sweet as spring water," she was saying. "I'm telling you all, I dropped it in bitter as gall—well water gone foul since the rockslide, three years now, we've been hauling from the far spring like beggars—and I knelt at the old shrine because there was nowhere else to kneel, and I said, *please*, I said, *whoever's listening*—" Her voice cracked. "And my boy drank first thing this morning and laughed. Laughed! First time since the fever took his sister he's laughed."

The room murmured. Freya sat very still with her tankard halfway to her lips.

"Shrine?" said Tam Vetch, wiping a mug that didn't need wiping. "There's no shrine at Millbrook."

"There is if you know where to look." Hesta set the loaf down and broke pieces off it, handing them around like communion. "Back of the mill race, under the hawthorn. Old stone, older than the keep, older than anything. My gran used to leave cream out for it when she thought no one saw. Everyone's grans did, once. We just stopped."

"And you started again," Freya said, before she could stop herself.

Hesta turned to her with the shining face of a woman who has been given something back. "Aye. Because there was nowhere else to go, taster-girl. You'd know about that."

Everyone knew what Freya Underhill did for her living. It bought her a certain respect of the wary kind, the way people respect a snake they've decided not to step on. She drank her beer and said nothing more, but she walked home by way of the mill race, because her feet had always been smarter than her pride.

The shrine under the hawthorn was exactly what she expected: a squat stone older than any kingdom, its carvings worn to suggestion, its hollow cupped like two hands pressed together. What she didn't expect was the smell. Every shrine she'd ever passed had smelled of rain and rot and pigeon droppings. This one smelled faintly, sweetly, of honey.

She crouched and looked into the cupped stone. Inside, in the dark, something glistened. Not water. Not cream. A thin film, amber-colored, that moved when she breathed on it—moved *toward* her breath, the way a plant turns toward light.

Freya Underhill had spent eleven years learning every poison that grew in the Thornfells and half the ones that didn't. She knew things that mimicked, things that lured, things that glowed sweetly in the dark while they waited. She stood up slowly, wiped her fingers on her skirt though she hadn't touched anything, and told herself, in the firm voice she used for apprentices and fools:

It's sap. It's beeswax. It's a trick of the light.

Her feet carried her home faster than usual.

* * *

By the end of the month, the miracles had teeth enough to be counted.

That was how Freya thought of it, anyway, because counting was what she did. She kept her ledger of symptoms for the crown—onset, duration, antidote administered—and in a second book, one she kept wrapped in oilcloth behind a loose stone, she began keeping a different ledger. She called it her ledger of answered prayers, and she hated the name even as she wrote it, because names made things real and she wanted very badly for this not to be real.

Millbrook: well sweetened. Price: unknown.

Dunmarrow: fever broken in six children, the same night their mothers walked to the standing stone on the ridge. Price: the eldest Rooker girl stopped speaking. Not struck dumb—she simply said, calmly, that she'd given her words away, and smiled too much afterward, and would not say to whom.

Harlow Ford: a calf born alive where the last nine had died; a harvest brought in ahead of the frost. Price: old Aldous Weaver, who'd prayed loudest, woke one morning unable to remember his wife's face. He remembered everyone else's. He wept about it without knowing why.

Freya rode the smuggler roads and mapped it all, because mapping was the other thing she did. The pattern was there before she was willing to see it: a rough ring of villages around the high country, tightening inward, each miracle closer than the last to Thornfell Keep itself. And beneath the keep—if her grandmother's croft, if the sigil carved above that cold hearth, if the shape burned into her own skin meant what she feared it meant—something old and sealed and patient had begun, at long last, to answer.

The prices were what kept her awake. Not the miracles. Miracles were easy; she'd seen mountebanks work greater wonders with lamp oil and sleight of hand. But a mountebank took your coin. Whatever lived under the mountain took *specific* things. A girl's words. An old man's memory of love. In Wetherlow, a woman prayed for her husband's broken leg to knit, and it knit, and the woman lost the ability to taste sweetness forever—ate honey and wept, ate honey and wept.

"That's not a god," Freya said to Wick, before she'd ever met Wick, saying it instead to Tam Vetch over cards. "Gods don't haggle. That's a merchant. That's a moneylender with better manners."

"Or it's a god who's learned manners from watching us," Tam said, and won the hand.

She should have let it lie. Eleven years of survival had taught her the first rule of the mountains: what feeds itself will eventually feed on you, so stay small, stay quiet, stay unremarkable. The gods—or the god, or whatever it was—was answering the desperate, and Freya Underhill was many things but never desperate. She had poison immunity and a warm croft and a full pantry and no one whose loss could be leveraged against her.

Then, in the second week of the frost month, the prayers reached Harrowgate, which was four miles from her door.

* * *

She went because of the child. That was the story she told herself, and it was mostly true.

Maren Cobb—no relation to the Millbrook Cobbs, though everyone swore otherwise—had a daughter burning with lungfever, the bad kind, the kind Freya herself had survived at seven only because her grandmother had known things about moss and steam that no lowland physician did. Maren had no grandmother like that. Maren had a shrine: a fallen menhir in the blackthorn brake above the village, half-swallowed by the hill, its carved spiral still sharp where the weather couldn't reach.

Freya arrived at dusk and found half of Harrowgate gathered there, holding candles against the wind, and the sight of it made her stomach turn over—not the candles, but the *orderliness*. They'd formed lines. Someone had organized the prayer the way you organize a queue at a tithe-house. Faith, in the space of a month, had learned bureaucracy.

Maren knelt at the head of the line with her daughter's name written on a scrap of linen pinned to her breast. "Please," she was saying, to the stone, to the hill, to whatever listened. "Please. She's all I have. Take anything. Take anything of mine, I'll pay it gladly, only let her live."

Freya pushed through the crowd. "Don't," she said. "Maren. Don't promise open-ended. Never promise open-ended."

They stared at her—the whole candlelit crowd, faces turning like sunflowers—and she understood, with a sick lurch, that she had just done the one thing her whole life had been built to avoid. She had stepped forward. She had made herself visible.

"It's the taster," someone whispered, and the whisper went around with a strange reverence, as though her trade were itself a kind of holiness. Poison-touched. Death's own cupbearer. Of course she'd know how such bargains worked.

"Mistress Underhill." Maren looked up at her with wet, furious hope. "You know things. Tell me what to do. Tell me what it takes."

What it takes is everything you won't miss until it's gone, Freya thought. What it takes is a piece of yourself, cut away while you sleep, and you'll wake lighter and call it mercy.

"Ask narrowly," she said aloud. "Name the price yourself. Say: *her fever breaks tonight, and in payment take the color from my winter cloak, or the song from my spinning wheel, or some small thing I'll not mourn.* Bind it small. Never let it choose for you."

Someone laughed nervously. Someone else shushed them. And then, because the crowd needed a sign and the night obliged, the wind died—all at once, between one breath and the next, the way a door closes—and the air above the menhir went sweet with honey.

Every candle flame leaned toward the stone. Not flickered. *Leaned*, all together, like a congregation bowing.

Freya felt it then, for the first time, through her skin rather than her reason: attention. Something vast and buried turning its regard upon the little knot of warmth and want on the hillside, the way a man at a feast turns his eye down a table of dishes. And beneath the sweetness, unmistakable to a nose trained on eleven years of poisons, the undertone she had been smelling for weeks and refusing to name.

Not honey. Honey was only the coat it wore.

Beneath the honey: carrion. Old, patient, sugared-over carrion, the smell of a trap baited long ago and checked faithfully ever since.

"Go home," Freya said, and her voice came out strange, flat, carrying. "All of you. Go home and bar your doors and pray to nothing tonight. Pray to your own two hands. They're the only powers up here that don't charge interest."

Nobody moved. Maren gripped the linen scrap with her daughter's name and said, gently, pityingly, "You're frightened, mistress. That's all. You've seen what poison does, so you see it everywhere. But this is grace. Can't you feel it? It feels like being *heard*."

And that was the worst of it—that was the thing Freya carried home through the dark with the honey-smell caught in her hair—that Maren was right. It did feel like being heard. Freya had spent her whole life unheard: the invisible girl, the tool, the cupbearer, thanked by no one, noticed by no one, her whole existence a service rendered silently at other people's tables. If something in the deep of the world had opened one ancient ear to the smallfolk of the Thornfells, she understood better than anyone alive why they came running, why they knelt in lines with candles, why they'd pay any price and call it grace.

Being heard was the rarest miracle of all. That was precisely what made the thing below so dangerous. It wasn't offering bread. It was offering *witness*—and charging for it in the coin of the self.

* * *

Three nights later, Maren's daughter's fever broke.

Freya knew because the news ran up the valley faster than frost, and because she went to check, telling herself it was professional habit. The girl slept pink and breathing in her mother's arms, and Harrowgate wept and sang, and Maren Cobb stood in her doorway looking ten years younger and one piece missing.

"You were right to warn me," Maren said, when Freya came. There was no triumph in it. "I asked narrowly, like you said. I named my price." She turned her left hand over. Her little finger was there. Her *ring finger* was there. But when she tried to close her fist, the fourth finger simply would not bend, and would not bend ever again, and when she spoke of her late husband—the girl's father, dead two winters—her face stayed smooth as a shut door. "I can't remember his voice," Maren said. "I remember that I loved him. I remember that I loved him the way you remember a sum you've done. But his voice—" She looked at her straightened finger. "It chose small, like you said. It chose smaller than I'd have chosen. I keep thinking I should mind more."

"Do you mind?" Freya asked.

"I mind that I don't mind." Maren smiled, and the smile was the one Freya had heard described in Dunmarrow, the too-much smile, the smile of the Rooker girl who'd given her words away. "That's the price under the price, isn't it? It doesn't just take. It makes you glad it took."

Freya walked home under a sky full of hard stars, and at her own door she unwrapped the oilcloth ledger and wrote by candlelight, her hand steady the way it only went steady after poison:

*Harrowgate: fever broken. Price: grief for the dead husband, excised. Subject reports gratitude. Subject reports joy.*

*Pattern holds. Ring tightening. Next villages inside the circle: Undermill. Then the high road. Then the keep.*

She sat back. The candle guttered. And because she was alone, and because no one would ever read it, she added one more line in a smaller hand:

*It answers everyone who asks. Sooner or later it will answer me.*

Outside, far off toward the heart of the mountains, the wind moved through the passes with a sound almost like a page turning. And in her ledger, in the space below her final line, Freya found—when she looked again, though she would swear until her dying day she had written nothing there—one word, in ink darker than her own, in a hand like her grandmother's:

*Sooner.*
