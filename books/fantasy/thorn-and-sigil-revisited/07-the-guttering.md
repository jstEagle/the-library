## 7. The Guttering

The mountain dreamed in stone-time, and Emberwake dreamed with it.

There was no difference anymore. That was the first truth of the long dark: after three hundred years, the boundary between the keeper and the kept had worn thin as an old coin rubbed smooth by a thousand thumbs. Emberwake's heart beat once for every turning of the deep water beneath the roots of Thornfell, and the slow pulse of it traveled up through the rock the way warmth travels through a wall in winter—faintly, faithfully, and noticed by no one.

It had not always been so. Once there had been wings.

Emberwake remembered them the way old men remember their mothers' voices—not as sound but as the shape of where sound used to live. The memory was vast and green and golden: thermals rising off the summer valleys like invisible staircases, the sun striking its own shadow onto the clouds below, a shadow with wings like scythes, like cathedral doors, like the spread fingers of a god who had decided to be kind. It remembered fire that answered before it was called, fire that leapt from the hollow of its chest joyful as a hound off a leash, fire that could write across the sky.

That fire was gone now. What remained was the guttering—the last candle-end of it, cupped somewhere behind the old ribs, burning down through centuries the way a wick burns down through the final inch of tallow. Patient. Dim. Unwilling, out of sheer stubbornness, to go out.

*Not yet,* the fire said to itself, in the wordless grammar of flames. *Not yet. Not yet.*

And Emberwake, curled around its own diminishing heart in the vaulted dark beneath the mountain, agreed. Not yet. Because a keeper does not leave the door unwatched. A keeper does not sleep past the end of the watch, no matter how long the watch becomes.

* * *

Sleep, of course, was the whole of it. That was the joke the world had played on Emberwake, and it was a joke only a creature with three hundred years to think about it could fully appreciate.

The seal required a living fire. That had been the price of the binding, agreed upon in an age when dragons still bargained as equals with gods and men alike: the prison beneath Thornfell would hold only so long as dragon-fire held it, and dragon-fire would hold it only so long as a dragon lived, breathing against the deep door in the slow rhythm of its sleeping breath. In. Out. In. Out. Each exhale pressing warmth into the fused dragon-glass of the seal the way a smith presses bellows-air into a forge, keeping the lock from going cold and brittle.

So Emberwake had lain down at the mouth of the deep road, folded its wings for the last time, and begun the longest exhalation in the history of the world.

Three hundred years.

It did not count the years the way men counted them, in harvests and kings. It counted breaths. There had been—by the arithmetic it maintained in some dusty corner of its mind, an arithmetic kept the way misers keep coins—something over nine million of them. Nine million slow, deliberate, disciplined exhalations, each one a small act of war against the dark, each one a little less than the one before.

Because here was the second truth, the one Emberwake did not let itself look at directly, the way a starving man does not let himself look at the last loaf: the fire was failing. Had been failing since roughly forever. The first century, its breath had come hot enough to make the dragon-glass sing—a low, sweet note that ran through the mountain's bones, the note of a lock well-oiled and well-loved. By the second century the singing had faded to a hum. By the third, most nights—and all the nights were long, and all of them were his—Emberwake could feel the seal drinking his warmth without giving anything back, a cold mouth at the end of the deep road, patient as only stone and imprisonment can be patient.

Some mornings—it called them mornings; they were nothing of the kind, only the hours when the small warm bloods above moved about in their thousands, a distant rustling like leaves—some mornings Emberwake woke from a dream of flight and did not know, for the space of ten or twenty heartbeats, whether it was still alive at all. The dark down here was total. Its eyes had gone the color of tarnished silver, filmed over, good for nothing but the faintest heat-sense. Its scales, once the red-black of cooling lava shot through with gold, had dulled to something like iron slag. Moss grew in the seams between its scales, pale and blind, and small blind things nested in the moss, and Emberwake permitted this because a mountain ought to have inhabitants and it was, by now, mostly mountain itself.

Once, a hundred years ago or a hundred and fifty—it had lost precision—a party of miners had broken through into the upper gallery of its cavern. Emberwake had felt the pick-strokes as vibrations in its teeth, heard the small wet sounds of men breathing and cursing in a language that had drifted far from any it knew. They had found the old warning-carvings on the gallery walls: DRAGON, in the script of the sealing age, and BELOW SLEEPS THE FIRE THAT HOLDS, and TURN BACK, and the crude older glyphs beneath, the ones the first Underhill had cut with her own hands: SHE KEEPS. DO NOT WAKE HER. DO NOT FEED HER. LET HER SLEEP TILL THE WORLD NEEDS HER.

The miners had stood in the dark reading those words by lamplight. Emberwake had felt the light flicker on its flank from thirty feet away, the first touch of anything in decades. It had considered waking. Considered opening one terrible eye, filling the gallery with the smell of sulfur and the pressure of ancient attention, listening to them scream and scatter and carry the story up into the daylight world: *it lives, it lives, under Thornfell it lives.*

Instead it had breathed.

One long, deliberate exhalation, aimed not at the men but at the deep door behind them, and the warmth of it rolled through the gallery like a wave through a wheat field, and the lamps bent and guttered, and the men felt the heat pass over them and understood—dimly, the way animals understand—that the darkness they were standing in was not empty, and that whatever filled it was asleep, and that they should be grateful beyond the reach of prayer that it intended to stay that way.

They ran. They sealed the shaft behind them. They told stories, probably, that grew less true with every telling. And Emberwake settled back into its breath-rhythm, and the moss took another decade to grow back where the lamplight had warmed it, and that was the whole of the last conversation it had with the world.

* * *

But lately—lately, in the way a mountain measures lately, meaning within the span of a few thousand breaths instead of a few hundred thousand—something had changed in the deep places, and Emberwake's old instincts had begun to prickle along its spine like lice.

It started as taste. The air that seeped down into the deep road had always carried flavors to it, faint and far: rain on granite, pine sap, sheep-dung smoke, the iron tang of men digging. Now there was something else riding the seep-water, dissolving through the rock the way rot spreads through fruit. A sweetness. A wrongness. The flavor of a voice heard through a wall—unintelligible, but unmistakably a *voice*, and unmistakably pleased with itself.

Emberwake knew that flavor. Every creature of the sealing age knew it.

The gods had smelled like that at the end. Sweet as honey left too long in the sun, thick with the reek of worship, and underneath the sweetness something like hunger, and underneath the hunger something like fear—which was the thing no one ever believed about gods until it was far too late to matter: that they were afraid, always afraid, afraid of being forgotten, afraid of the smallness waiting for them at the bottom of every decline, and that all their terrible appetite was only fear wearing a crown.

They had screamed when the seal closed. Emberwake remembered that too, better than it wanted to. The first Underhill—small, burned, her sigil-brand still smoking on her own forearm where she had pressed it into the molten glass to key the lock—had knelt on this very floor, on ground that had since become part of Emberwake's body's shadow, and wept while the gods hammered on the inside of their new prison. The hammering had gone on for a year. Then a decade. Then it had faded to muttering, and the muttering had faded to silence, and the silence had lasted two hundred years and been the kindest gift the world had given anyone.

Now the muttering was coming back.

Emberwake tasted it every time the seep-water ran: a thread of sweetness in the stone, growing stronger by fractions, the way a wound grows infected by fractions, unnoticed until the fever arrives. And threaded through the sweetness, worse than the sweetness, came other flavors—the flavors of answered prayers. Wells made sweet. Fevers broken. Bread multiplied on a shrine-stone. Small mercies, each one a hook baited with honey. Emberwake remembered the beginning of the last age too, and it had begun exactly like this: not with thunder, but with kindness. Gods did not kick doors down. They waited for the smallfolk to open them, one desperate soul at a time, each opener believing herself alone, each certain that her one small mercy could cost the world nothing.

*Foolish, brave, doomed little species,* Emberwake thought, with a tenderness it would have denied under torture. It had liked men. That was the secret shame at the center of its long sleep, the ember under all the ash: it had *liked* them. Their brief ferocious lives, their cookfires, their ridiculous songs. The first Underhill most of all—she had sung while she worked the seal, badly, a threshing-song from her home valley, because she said the lock ought to be shut with something human in the air, not just terror. Emberwake had kept the song. Somewhere in the ruined archive of its mind, between the memory of wings and the memory of fire, the tune still lived, four notes rising and falling like a bird deciding.

If the gods woke, they would find the world soft and forgetful and full of desperate prayers, and they would pour themselves into it like water into cracks, and it would take another age of blood to put them away again—if it could be done at all. The last sealing had cost the dragons everything. There were no dragons left to pay it twice. There was barely an Emberwake.

And the fire—

The fire was the third truth, the one Emberwake circled night after night, breath after breath, the way a tongue circles a broken tooth.

It was failing faster now. Something was drawing on it. Not the seal—the seal drank evenly, honestly, the way a hearth draws draft. This was different. This was a siphon. Somewhere in the deep architecture of the binding, something had found a straw and put it to the last of Emberwake's flame, and was drinking.

The gods. It had to be the gods. Starved for three centuries, dreaming at the bottom of their glass prison, they had finally found the seam in their own cell: the fire that held the door was also, if one were clever and patient and utterly without shame, food. They were drinking the lock. Slowly. Carefully. With the same sweet patience they were learning to feed on prayers above. Every miracle bought upstairs with devotion was buying them strength downstairs to sip at dragon-fire, and the sipping was working, and Emberwake could feel itself becoming, breath by breath, the hollow shell around an empty lamp.

It tested the flame, the way it did once a hundred breaths. Reached inward, past the moss-grown ribs, past the banked coals of its body, to the place where the last fire lived.

The fire flinched.

That was new. For three hundred years the fire had been simple: dim, faithful, *not yet*. Now there was a tremor in it, a wavering, as if a hand had been waved over the candle. As if something on the far side of the glass had blown it a kiss.

*No,* Emberwake told it, in the old grammar, the grammar of forges and first-flight. *Hold. Hold. I am the keeper and you are the keeping and neither of us is finished.*

The fire held. But it held the way a tired sentry holds a spear—correctly, and barely, and with the sure knowledge that dawn was very far away and might never come at all.

* * *

Emberwake slept, because sleeping was the work. In the dream, there was always the same country: the high valleys in the last summer of the old age, gold grass to the horizon, the wind coming off the snowfields carrying the smell of wild thyme, and the sky so wide that a young dragon could climb for an hour and still have sky above it. In the dream its wings worked. In the dream the fire came when called, joyful as a hound. In the dream the first Underhill stood on a granite shelf with her sleeves pushed up and her arms crossed, watching it fly with the expression of a woman thoroughly unimpressed by miracles, and when it landed she said, in her flat valley drawl:

"You'll do. You're the last one mad enough."

"I am the last one," Emberwake had answered. "Madness optional."

"Keep the door," she'd said, and her hand on its snout had been shockingly warm, human-warm, a coal wrapped in skin. "Keep it till someone comes with my mark on them. Blood calls to blood. Fire knows its own. Someone will come."

"And if no one comes?"

She had looked out at the gold valleys, at the world she was about to nail shut behind her, and she had smiled the way soldiers smile.

"Then keep it anyway," she said. "A vow isn't a wager. It doesn't need odds to be worth keeping."

The dream always ended there, because dreams are merciful and end where the truth begins.

Someone will come. Three hundred years. Nine million breaths. No one had come. The Underhills had scattered and thinned and dwindled, their name worn down to a rumor, then to nothing; Emberwake had tasted their absence in the seep-water decade by decade, the particular flavor of a family's hearth-smoke going out. Perhaps they were all dead. Perhaps the mark itself had faded from the blood, diluted past finding. Perhaps the woman had been wrong, and blood called to nothing, and the vow would simply run out one breath ahead of the fire, and the deep door would go cold and brittle in the dark, and no one would ever know what had failed or how.

And yet.

Lately—again that mountain-*lately*, that narrowing of centuries into seasons—there was something else in the water. Underneath the gods' honeyed rot, underneath the sourness of the world's forgetting, a thread of a different flavor entirely. Faint almost past sensing. Old-fashioned. It tasted of woodsmoke and bitter herbs and iron and, beneath all that, of a specific and unmistakable thing: branded skin. Sigil-blood. The seal's own key, walking around in the world above with a heartbeat, wearing the mark like a coal sewn under cloth.

Blood calls to blood. Fire knows its own.

Emberwake did not dare believe it. Belief was heavier than hope and broke more easily, and a keeper could not afford broken things. But it turned the flavor over in its mind the way the tongue turns the last grain of salt, and it began, cautiously, treacherously, to do the one thing it had forbidden itself in nine million breaths.

It began to hope.

And hoping, it noticed—for the first time, clearly, with the whole of its ancient attention—how fast the flame was dying now. The gods' sipping had grown bolder. The candle-end behind its ribs had weeks, perhaps. A season at the outside. The seal was going cold at its edges; Emberwake could feel the dragon-glass stiffening like honey left in frost, and somewhere far above, heedless, the small warm bloods were leaving bread at shrines, and something sweet-voiced and starved was saying *yes, yes, ask me again.*

*Soon,* Emberwake told the fire, and told the dark, and told the door. *Hold. Whoever you are, marked one, whoever's blood runs warm with the old brand—come soon. Learn what your grandmother knew. Come down the deep road with your bare arm out.*

*Because the light is failing, little heir. The light is failing, and the things behind the glass have learned to drink, and I am so tired—I am so tired—and I cannot hold it much longer alone.*

Far above, through a mile of stone, through the ordinary noise of an ordinary evening, a taster swallowed her daily poison and walked home in the dark, unaware that the oldest creature in the world had just spent its hope on her.

The fire gutted. The mountain held its breath.
