## 8. A Taster's Network

The map lived in Freya's head, which was the only place in the Thornfells it had ever been safe.

She had been building it since she was nine years old, the year her grandmother died and the crown's coin first bought her silence along with her service. Every pass, every goat track, every smuggler's stair cut into the rock by men who had never been caught and never would be—it all lived behind her eyes, drawn in the same careful hand she used for her poison ledgers. She knew which trails flooded in spring melt. She knew which waystations kept honest beds and which ones sold their guests' boots while they slept. She knew how long it took a mule train to move from Hollowmere to the Salt Stair, and how much faster a man could move if he left the mule and his dignity both at the bottom of the climb.

For twenty years that knowledge had been worth exactly what the court paid her: enough to live on, not enough to matter.

Now, sitting cross-legged on the floor of her cottage with a sheet of butcher's paper before her and a stub of charcoal in her fingers, she found the knowledge rearranging itself into something else. Something with a shape.

*Three wells sweetened at Hollowmere,* she wrote. *One fever broken at Ashcombe—whole household, seven souls. Bread multiplied at the Crossroads shrine—the widow Maren swears by it, and the widow Maren does not swear by anything but the weather.*

She drew each village as a small circle, because drawing them as they truly were—smoke-stained roofs, muddy lanes, the particular lean of a chimney that meant poverty rather than neglect—would have taken longer than the night allowed. Then she sat back and looked at what she'd made, and felt the hairline crack in her rational world widen by another degree.

Because the circles were not scattered. They were arranged.

* * *

It had taken her six days to gather the intelligence, and she had gathered it the way she gathered everything: invisibly, patiently, one small transaction at a time.

The taster's trade had given her a network no spymaster could buy, though none of its members knew they belonged to one. There was Doss, who ran salt and iron past the border wardens and expected Freya to taste any wine he brought her before he drank it, because even smugglers feared a rival's poison more than they loved their own profit. There was the ferryman at Greywater, whose wife brewed a tonic Freya tested for safety every season. There were the herb-wives of three villages who traded with her in whispers—foxglove for valerian, hemlock for honey—and who saw everything that moved through their valleys because seeing was how they stayed alive.

And there was Wick.

She hadn't meant to notice the boy. He was one of a dozen orphans who haunted the market town below Thornfell keep, all elbows and hunger, selling kindling and running errands for coppers. But he had appeared at her door three times now with herbs she needed, and the third time he had said, without being asked, "The foxglove's from the north slope, mistress, so it's stronger—you'll want less of it," and Freya had felt something shift in her chest that she did not have a ledger column for.

He also talked. Endlessly, artlessly, the way children do when no one has ever taught them that talk has consequences. And what he talked about, these days, was miracles.

"Old Tam's cow came back," he'd reported, hunkered on her doorstep eating the bread she'd pretended not to notice him staring at. "Gone a fortnight, and then just standing in his field at dawn like it had never left. And Tam says—" here Wick dropped his voice into a fair imitation of a shepherd's rasp—"Tam says he prayed to whatever was listening, and something listened back."

"What did it cost him?" Freya had asked, because that was the question that mattered, the question the village talk always skated past.

Wick had shrugged. "Tam says nothing. But Tam's daughter stopped speaking after. Not sad-quiet. Just—" he waved a crust vaguely— "like words cost too much now."

Freya had written that down too, later, in cipher: *Miracle returned. Price: the girl's voice.* And beneath it, the question she could not stop circling: *What kind of god takes payment in silence?*

* * *

By the fourth day of mapping, the pattern had begun to itch at her.

She laid it out with a taster's precision. The sweetened wells formed an arc through the western valleys. The healed fevers clustered east of them. The returned livestock, the bread that did not diminish, the child at Millbrook who had woken from a fever-sleep speaking a language nobody knew and then, mercifully, forgotten it—all of these fell within a rough ring perhaps thirty miles across.

And at the center of the ring, when she finally forced herself to draw it, sat Thornfell keep itself.

No—not the keep. The keep was new stone on old bones; the crown had raised it two centuries ago atop foundations nobody claimed. Beneath it, according to the oldest maps, the ones locked in the queen's own archive, there had been something else. A shrine. Sealed by royal decree in the reign of the first Queen Maren, sealed so thoroughly that the decree itself had been burned and the sealing recorded only as "the Deep Door closed, by the grace of the Dragon's Peace."

Dragon's Peace. Her birthmark pulsed faintly under her sleeve when she read those words, as if the sigil recognized something her mind refused to.

Freya set down the charcoal and pressed her palm flat against the paper, over the center of the ring, over the place where the keep stood on its buried bones.

"It's under them," she said aloud, to the empty room. "It's been under them the whole time."

* * *

Doss came to her on the sixth night, as she'd asked him to, entering through the back way with the practiced ease of a man who had been entering places uninvited for thirty years. He was gray-bearded and bowlegged and he smelled of mule and pipe smoke, and he was the closest thing to a friend her trade permitted—which is to say, he had never once betrayed her, and she had never once given him reason to.

"You look like a woman mapping a funeral," he said, lowering himself onto her second chair without ceremony.

"I might be." She turned the butcher's paper toward him. "Tell me what you hear on the roads. Not the usual. The strange."

Doss studied the map for a long moment, chewing the inside of his cheek. Then he tapped one thick finger on Hollowmere. "Well water went sweet there. Sweet as a bride's smile, they say. Man could sell that water in the lowlands for silver." His finger moved. "Ashcombe, the fever house. Whole family up and well in a morning. And at the Salt Stair—" he hesitated, and Freya marked the hesitation, because Doss did not hesitate.

"At the Salt Stair what?"

"There's a shrine. Old one, half-swallowed by the scree. Nobody tended it in living memory. Now there's candles. Dozens of them. And ribbons tied to the stones, and little offerings—bread, buttons, a wedding ring last week." He shook his head slowly. "And here's the thing, Freya. The folk who leave the offerings don't pray to the Four. Won't say the names. They pray to 'the listener beneath.' That's what they call it. The listener beneath."

Beneath. The word settled into the center of her map like a stone dropped into a well.

"And the price?" she asked quietly. "There's always a price, Doss. What are they paying?"

He looked uncomfortable for the first time. "That's the other thing. Folk come back from those shrines lighter somehow. Happier. And then—" he rubbed his jaw— "then they start going quiet. Not all at once. Bit by bit. Old Nan at the Stair used to sing while she worked. Doesn't anymore. Says she can't remember the words to anything. Her grandson gave up his dreams—says he sleeps like the dead and wakes like the dead too. They don't mind, Freya. That's what turns my stomach. They don't mind at all. They say it's a fair trade. They say it's *mercy*."

Mercy with teeth. Prayers with teeth. She thought of the widow at the Crossroads, the bread vanishing between one blink and the next, and told herself again, one last time, that it had been an animal. The lie fit worse every day.

"Doss." She met his eyes. "If I asked you to carry word along your routes—not letters, just questions—who answers prayers where you travel? What do they give? What do they take? Could you bring me that?"

He was silent a moment. "You're tasting something bigger than wine," he said.

"I'm trying to find out what I'm tasting."

"That's the whole trade in a sentence, isn't it." He grinned, gap-toothed and rueful, and stood. "I'll ask my questions. But Freya—if this goes somewhere bad, you send me down the mountain first. Promise me. I'm too old and too mean to die surprised."

"I promise," she said, and both of them knew it was the kind of promise made to be broken by circumstance rather than intent.

* * *

Alone again, she worked until the candle guttered.

The pattern resolved the way a poison resolves in the blood—one symptom, then another, then the terrible clarity of diagnosis. The answered prayers were not random acts of divine generosity. They radiated. Faintest at the edges of the ring, strongest near the center, strongest of all in the reports that came from the market town in the keep's very shadow. Whatever listened beneath Thornfell was waking slowly, testing its reach the way a sleeper tests a stiff limb, gathering strength from every candle lit and every ribbon tied and every voice surrendered.

And the shape of its prison—because she was increasingly certain it was a prison, that the Dragon's Peace had been a locking-away and not a blessing—matched the ring on her map almost perfectly. Thirty miles. The old texts spoke of the gods bound "beneath the mountain's heart," and the mountain's heart, by every account she could dredge up, lay directly under the keep's foundations, behind the Deep Door that had been closed and cursed and deliberately forgotten.

Her birthmark burned. Not painfully—not yet—but steadily, like a coal remembered rather than felt, and she pushed her sleeve up and looked at it in the candlelight: the coiled, winged shape at the inside of her wrist, the mark every healer had called a birth-stain and every lover had called beautiful and her grandmother alone had covered with a bandage and never explained.

*Underhill,* the seal had said, in her grandmother's croft, carved deep into the hearthstone. *Keepers of the flame.*

Keepers. Not servants. Not sacrifices. Keepers.

Freya rolled the map, tied it with string, and hid it in the false bottom of her poison chest, beneath the vials that could kill a man in a breath and the antidotes that could save him. Then she sat by the dead fire for a long time, hands wrapped around a cup of tea gone cold, and let herself think the thought she had been refusing for six days.

*I am invisible. I have spent my whole life learning to be invisible. Nobody watches the taster. Nobody wonders where the taster walks, or who she speaks to, or what she carries in her ledgers.*

*But I see everything. I have always seen everything. Every road, every whisper, every secret that moves through these mountains passes within arm's reach of a woman nobody bothers to look at.*

For twenty years that had been her cage. Tonight, for the first time, it felt like a key.

Corvane watched her—she knew that now, had known it since the dish at court that made her wrist burn like a brand. The spymaster's attention was a weight she could feel across a room. But Corvane watched the court, the queen, the levers of power. He did not watch the smugglers' paths or the herb-wives' kitchens or the shrines the crown had forgotten it ever sealed. Those roads were hers. That darkness was hers.

Whatever woke beneath the keep, whoever meant to use it—she would know first. She would know before the queen, before Corvane, before the gods themselves finished stretching their ancient limbs. And knowing first, in her experience, was the difference between the poisoned and the taster.

She banked the fire, checked the salt line at her door out of habit, and lay down without undressing, as she always did.

Sleep came slow. When it came, she dreamed of bread vanishing from a stone, and of a voice beneath the mountain saying, gently, with infinite patience: *I heard you too, little keeper.*

She woke before dawn with her wrist throbbing and made herself a list of what she would need: another pair of hands. Someone quick, quiet, hungry enough to be loyal, and young enough to go where she could not without raising every eyebrow in the market town.

She knew exactly the boy.
