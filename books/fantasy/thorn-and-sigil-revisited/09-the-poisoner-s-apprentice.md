## 9. The Poisoner's Apprentice

The boy was stealing from her.

Not from her, precisely—Freya had learned long ago that precision mattered more than pride—but from the drying racks behind her croft. Someone had been taking foxglove leaves, a few at a time, over the course of a fortnight. Not enough to be missed by anyone who didn't count, and Freya counted everything the way other people counted their sins: compulsively, in the dark, with a certain grim comfort.

She noticed on a Tuesday, because Tuesdays were foxglove days, and there were nine leaves where eleven should have been.

Nine leaves of foxglove would not kill a child. Nine leaves, brewed carelessly, could stop a heart or steady one, depending on the hand that held the mortar. Whoever was taking them either didn't know what they'd stolen, or knew exactly—and neither answer sat well with her.

So she did what she always did with problems too small for swords and too large for sleep. She made tea, sat by her window with the shutter cracked to a finger's width, and waited.

* * *

He came an hour past dusk, when the last grey light had given up on the mountains and the wind had settled into its evening habit of moaning through the pass like a widow at a graveside. He moved well for a child—not silently, no child was ever truly silent, but with the particular economy of someone who had learned that noise invited boots.

He went straight for the racks, as she'd known he would. His hands were quick, his fingers stained green-black at the tips from handling dried leaf, and he took only two this time. Two leaves. Enough to slow a fevered pulse, if you knew the dose. Enough to kill a grown man, if you were foolish about it.

Freya opened the shutter.

"Foxglove's a liar," she said.

The boy froze with the gracelessness of a startled hare—one heartbeat of pure animal panic, all elbows and held breath—and then did something she had not expected. He didn't run. He turned, slowly, and looked at her with eyes too old for the rest of him, and said:

"I measured."

"You measured." She let the word sit between them, unimpressed. "With what?"

"My hands." He held them up as if presenting evidence at trial. They were small hands, knuckle-bruised, the nails bitten to the quick. "I watched how much you use when you make the fever draughts for Widow Marsh's grandsons. Two leaves, torn small, steeped in a cup and a half of water off the boil."

Freya set down her cup. This was, she reflected, the trouble with living among people who thought you were nothing but the queen's taster—the moment one of them actually looked, they saw more than you wanted.

"And how many times have you watched me?"

"Lots." He shifted his weight. In the dimness his face resolved into angles: sharp chin, hollow cheeks, hair the color of wet ash falling into his eyes. Ten years old, perhaps eleven. Underfed in the way that meant not starving but never full, the body rationing itself out of hard-won wisdom. "You grind it finer when it's for little ones. You say a charm under your breath but I don't think it does anything. You taste the water first before you put anything in it."

"Why would I do that?"

"So nobody's poisoned the water." He said it as though it were obvious, which, to be fair, it was. "That's what tasters do. My mam told me. Before."

*Before* hung in the room like smoke. Freya knew that word's whole family—before the fever, before the mine closed, before the reiving, before. Thornfell villages were built on *befores*, the way lowland towns were built on stone.

"What's your name?"

"Wick."

"That a name or a job?"

"Both." A ghost of defiance crossed his face. "My da worked the pitch-works down at Hollowmere. Said I screamed so loud the day I was born they called me after the candle-end he was burning. Then the works shut and everyone left and—" He stopped himself, jaw tightening around whatever came next, and Freya recognized the motion because she performed it daily. The swallow. The sealing of the mouth. "It doesn't matter."

"It matters," she said, "that you've been dosing someone with stolen foxglove. Who's sick?"

The boy's shoulders dropped half an inch, the confession arriving without words. "Nan Serra. She raised me after my mam. She's got the wasting sickness—her heart stumbles, beats funny, then goes quiet a while. Herbwoman up at Crossroads says foxglove can steady a stumbling heart if you get the dose right. But she wants three coppers a draught and Nan's got nothing, so I—" He gestured vaguely with the two leaves, this small criminal holding his contraband like a bouquet. "I've been practicing. On myself. First."

Freya went very still.

"On yourself."

"A little each time. To learn what it feels like." He lifted his chin, daring her to mock him. "How else do you learn?"

And there it was. Freya looked at this ragged, stubborn, self-poisoning scrap of a boy and felt something crack open in her chest—a door she had nailed shut years ago, back when she'd decided that caring for people was a luxury her trade couldn't afford. Because she knew exactly what he was. She had been him. A child teaching itself poison because no one else would teach it anything, swallowing danger in careful measures and calling it knowledge.

"How much have you taken?" Her voice came out flatter than she intended. "In total. Over how many days?"

"Three weeks. Small sips. I keep a tally." He produced, from somewhere in his rags, a strip of birch bark marked with neat charcoal strokes, grouped in fives. Thirty-one marks.

Thirty-one doses of foxglove, self-administered by a child who didn't know that foxglove accumulated in the blood, that yesterday's medicine became tomorrow's poison, that the difference between a remedy and a death lay not in the leaf but in the ledger. Freya had seen strong men die of less, thinking themselves careful.

"Give me your wrist," she said.

* * *

His pulse told her everything his bravado wouldn't. Slow. Too slow, and uneven in a way that made her jaw tighten—his heart already stumbling, already learning the leaf's lesson too well. She sat him by her fire, wrapped him in a blanket that smelled of yarrow and smoke, and fed him broth while she worked.

"What are you doing?" Wick asked, watching her measure out honey, ginger, and a pinch of dried nettle.

"Counteracting your arithmetic. Foxglove drains the body's salts as it slows the heart. You've been pouring out your own strength for three weeks and counting it as progress." She stirred the mixture and held it out. "Drink. All of it. It tastes foul."

He drank. He made a face of profound betrayal. "Tastes like pond."

"Pond is accurate. Pond is safe." She took the birch-bark tally from him and fed it to the fire, watching the fives curl and blacken. "Your lessons are over. Your own body is not a classroom."

"You don't know what it's like." The words came out raw, faster than he meant them, the way truth escapes children. "Watching her get worse and knowing there's a thing growing on the hillside that would fix her, and the woman who knows how to use it wants coin we'll never have. What am I supposed to do? Nothing?"

"No," Freya said quietly. "You're supposed to come to me."

He stared at her.

"I know every plant on this mountain that heals and every one that kills, and I know the difference to the width of a hair. I taste poison for the queen of the lowlands; do you imagine a village herbwoman frightens me?" She kept her voice level, matter-of-fact, offering the enormity as though it were nothing. "Come to me, and I will teach you properly. Which plants, which doses, which signs. And I will prepare draughts for your nan myself, for free, for as long as she needs them."

"Why?" It came out suspicious, the way reasonable questions sound from children who've been cheated before. "What do you want?"

Freya considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty even if she couldn't give him all of it.

"I want someone to count with," she said at last. "This work is lonely. You count well. That's worth more than you know."

* * *

He came back the next morning, and the morning after that, and by the end of the week he had claimed the stool by her hearth as though he'd always owned it. She taught him the way she wished someone had taught her: systematically, ruthlessly, with the understanding written plainly between them that mistakes here cost lives.

They began with the alphabet of the trade. Nightshade and hemlock, monkshood and thorn-apple—the killers first, because fear was a fine teacher and respect, once earned, lasted longer than affection. She made him draw each plant until his fingers ached, leaf and stem and root, and then made him draw them again blind, from memory, describing the differences aloud.

"Hemlock's stem is smooth and purple-spotted. Wild carrot looks near identical, but hairy. One makes soup, one makes corpses."

"Hairy good, spotted dead," he recited.

"Say it right. Hairy means food. Spotted means the grave."

"Hairy means food. Spotted means the grave." He grinned suddenly, gap-toothed, and Freya felt the grin land somewhere beneath her ribs and take root against her will.

They gathered in the afternoons, working the slopes above her croft while autumn gilded the bracken. She taught him to read a hillside the way she read a court: where the shade pooled, where the water ran, what the presence of one plant whispered about its neighbors. Yarrow loved the path edges. Foxglove haunted the clearings where trees had fallen, standing sentry over decay. And in the high scree, where nothing sensible grew, the pale spikes of aconite stood like funeral candles—*wolfsbane*, the mountain folk called it, *queen of poisons*, and she made him look at it a long time without touching.

"Remember it," she said. "One day someone will bring you a tea and tell you it's chamomile, and you'll need to know the difference before it crosses your lips."

"Has that happened to you?"

"Yes."

He waited for the story. She didn't give it. Some ledgers stayed closed.

But she gave him other things. She taught him to taste water before trusting it, to weigh powder in his palm and know the amount by pressure alone, to brew willow bark long and valerian short. She taught him that the same mouthful could be medicine, poison, or miracle depending on the measure, and that the person holding the measure held a power no sword could match—which was why, she told him, tasters walked through courts unnoticed and unthanked, and why that invisibility was worth more than any title.

"You're invisible," Wick said one evening, chewing a stem of sweetgrass she'd approved for the purpose. "Nobody sees you at all, do they? At court or here."

"Not if I'm lucky."

"That sounds terrible."

Freya paused, mortar halfway through its grinding. Through the window, the first stars were pricking out over Thornfell's black spine, and somewhere far below, in villages she was only beginning to understand she loved, wells were being sweetened and fevers broken by powers that asked prices. Somewhere farther still, Lord Corvane's grey eyes were almost certainly open, watching, patient as frost.

"It keeps you alive," she said.

"Yeah." Wick spat out the grass stem with great ceremony. "But my nan says a life nobody sees is just a long way of being alone."

Out of the mouths of candle-ends. Freya ground the pestle down harder than the herbs required and said nothing, because the boy was wrong, obviously—he was eleven and sentimental and had never swallowed belladonna on a stranger's behalf—but he was also, in the way of children, unbearably close to something true, and she wasn't ready to look at it directly.

* * *

On the ninth day, she prepared the first proper draught for Nan Serra and carried it down to Crossroads herself, Wick walking beside her with his chest puffed like a robin's. The old woman's cottage smelled of woodsmoke and cat, and Nan herself was a tiny bundle of a person propped by the fire, her breath fluttering irregular as a moth against glass.

Wick explained the dosing with the gravity of a physician, using Freya's own phrases back at her—*half a cup at dawn, never more, and if her fingers tingle you stop at once and fetch us*. Nan Serra listened, and then looked past the boy at Freya, and her ancient eyes were shrewd and kind.

"He's been stealing from you, then."

"Nan—" Wick went scarlet.

"For a fortnight. Two leaves at a time." The old woman's smile was dry as August. "You think I don't notice what goes missing from my own stores? I let him think he was clever. A boy needs to believe he can save somebody, or he grows up hard." She took Freya's hand in both of hers, papery and warm. "Thank you, taster. Folk say you're cold. Folk say a lot of things."

"They do," Freya agreed, and found, to her mild alarm, that she was smiling.

Walking home in the blue dusk, Wick chattering beside her about hemlock spots and the correct brewing of willow bark, Freya caught herself cataloguing the day the way she catalogued symptoms—and realized what she was actually doing was memorizing it. Laying it down carefully, like something breakable, in the part of herself she'd long ago walled off and labeled *do not open*.

Because here was the truth she'd been circling for nine days: she had spent her whole adult life making herself safe to lose. Invisible, unattached, unclaimed. The crown used her body and the mountains ignored her and that arrangement had kept her breathing through winters that killed better people. And now there was a boy on her hearth who counted in fives, and an old woman drinking her draughts at dawn, and every single one of them was a door a enemy could kick in, a name Corvane could find, a soft place where a knife would go deep.

She should send him away. Every survival instinct she possessed, honed by twenty years of tasting death in small cups, said the same thing: *attachments are liabilities, liabilities are fatal, walk away now while it costs only a little.*

Instead, that night, she cleared a shelf by the window and set out a second cup, a second bowl, a second spoon. She told herself it was practical. An assistant needed equipment. It had nothing to do with the way the croft had started to feel less like a hiding place and more like a home whenever small feet came through the door.

She was lying to herself, and she was getting noticeably better at it, and she filed that fact away with a taster's precision to be examined some other, safer day.

* * *

It was Wick who brought the news, ten days later, running up the track with his ash-grey hair wild and his face flushed with more than exertion.

"There's talk in the village," he panted, catching himself on her gate. "At the well. Old Tam's cow—dying of the bloat, sure to go, everyone said so—and Tam prayed. At the shrine, the crossroads one, the broken one nobody uses. Prayed out loud like a fool. And the next morning the cow was up and eating like nothing happened."

Freya went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the mountain air.

"Prayed," she repeated carefully. "To whom?"

"That's the thing." Wick lowered his voice, glancing back down the track as though the words might be following him. "There isn't anybody to pray to anymore. The shrines are empty. Everyone knows the gods are gone—or asleep, my nan says, sleeping under the mountain since forever." His eyes were wide, bright with the particular terror of a child whose world has begun rearranging itself. "But Tam swears something answered. And Marta's boy, the one with the bad lung—he slept a whole night without coughing after she left bread at the same shrine. And there's more, Freya. There's stories coming down from every village on the north slope."

He stopped, breathless, searching her face.

"Something's waking up," Wick said. "Isn't it? Something under the mountain. And it's answering people."

Freya looked past him toward the peaks, where the evening shadows were pooling thick and blue in the high passes, and thought of bread vanishing from a forgotten altar, of a dish at court that had made her birthmark burn like a coal, of Lord Corvane's patient, measuring gaze. The pieces were moving on a board she'd only just admitted existed.

"Wick," she said quietly, "how would you feel about learning to count faster?"
