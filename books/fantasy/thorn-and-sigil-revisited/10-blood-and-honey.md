## 10. Blood and Honey

Lord Corvane had learned long ago that the truth of a person could be read in the way they ate.

The courtiers of Queen Maren's hall chewed with ambition, swallowed with calculation, pushed food around their plates as though it might testify against them. The queen herself, in these last months, ate like a woman settling accounts—each bite deliberate, each swallow a small defiance of the physicians' murmurs. But the taster, Freya Underhill, ate like no one Corvane had ever seen. She ate the way a soldier crosses a bridge that may be mined: quickly, completely, and with her whole attention given to the ground beneath her feet.

He watched her now from the shadowed end of the long table, his hands folded around a goblet of spiced wine he had no intention of drinking, and he marveled—as he marveled nightly—that the most dangerous creature in Thornfell keep was this thin woman in grey who smelled faintly of herbs and whose name appeared on no list of importances.

Three weeks since he had confirmed it. Three weeks since he had sat alone in his tower room with the old census rolls spread before him, candlelight guttering over ink faded to the color of dried blood, and traced the Underhill line down through exile and erasure and quiet death until it stopped—with one name left living. *Freya Underhill, daughter of Marren, daughter of Sable, of the croft at Grey Hollow.* A line the crown had struck from its records three hundred years ago and yet had never quite managed to kill. He remembered sitting back in his chair, feeling something rare move through him—not surprise, for he had suspected since the night he first saw her birthmark flare at the queen's table, but a kind of grim satisfaction, the sensation of a lock yielding to a key cut precisely for it.

*The sigil-bearer,* he had thought. *Serving me my wine.*

Now she stood at her station beside the queen's chair, tasting each course before it touched the royal plate, and Corvane let his gaze rest on her with the patience of a man who has already won and is merely arranging the manner of the winning.

Tonight would settle it.

The dish arrived on schedule—the honey-glazed quail that the kitchens had spent two days preparing under his instruction, though only he knew the full measure of that instruction. His man in the kitchens, a round-faced sous-chef named Pell who owed Corvane more than his life and knew it, had done the work exactly as specified. The poison was not poison, not truly. It was an extract of emberthorn root, the same mountain herb the smallfolk brewed into teas for fevered children, refined through three distillations into something far more potent. In a healthy body it did nothing worse than quicken the pulse and warm the skin. But the old texts were specific, and Corvane had paid a great deal of gold to have them smuggled out of the sealed archives beneath the Sept of Records: *emberthorn, in concentration, will wake the blood of the seal-keepers. The mark will burn where the blood runs true.*

If Freya Underhill's birthmark flared when she tasted it, then everything was confirmed—not merely her lineage, which paper could prove, but the thing that mattered infinitely more: that the sigil lived in her blood, active, responsive, harvestable.

And if it did not flare—well. Then he had misread the situation, and he would spend another year watching, because Corvane did not make mistakes twice.

"Lord Corvane." The queen's voice pulled him from his reverie. Maren sat at the table's head, and even now, even knowing what he knew of her condition, the sight of her gave him pause. Death had been circling her for a year, patient as a vulture, and she had grown thinner and harder in equal measure, her beauty gone sharp as frost on iron. "You are miles away."

"Only reviewing the day's correspondence, Your Majesty."

"You review everything," she said. There was no accusation in it; there never was. That was the tragedy of Maren, Corvane thought—she had hired a wolf to guard her sheep and then convinced herself the wolf was a hound. "Tell me instead about the mountains. My marshals say the villages grow restless. Wells sweetening, fevers breaking. They call it the old gods stirring, and my marshals call it sedition, and I suspect both are wrong."

Corvane chose his words with care. "I believe something is moving in the high valleys, Majesty. Whether it is god or rumor, I mean to know within the fortnight."

"And your taster?" The queen's eyes, still fierce, flicked toward the grey-clad woman standing so quietly at her shoulder. "She has served me nine years without a single failure. Sometimes I wonder if she is bored."

"I imagine," Corvane said smoothly, "that boredom is not a condition Miss Underhill suffers from."

Across the table, Freya lifted the silver fork, took her portion of the quail—a precise, practiced bite from the thigh, where any dressing would pool thickest—and Corvane watched.

He watched the way her jaw paused mid-chew, a hesitation so slight that no one else in the hall could possibly have caught it. He watched her throat move as she swallowed, watched her set the fork down with the same unhurried grace she brought to every dish, watched her turn to the queen and give the small nod that cleared the plate.

But he also watched her left hand, hanging at her side below the tablecloth's edge, curl slowly inward until the knuckles went white.

And he watched—oh, this was the moment he had waited three weeks for, the moment he would remember with pleasure for years—as a flush crept up from beneath the collar of her grey dress. Not the flush of poison. The flush of heat, localized, spreading in a pattern he knew from the archive drawings: up the side of the neck, along the jaw, toward the temple. Toward the birthmark shaped like a coiled serpent devouring a star, hidden all these years beneath her hair.

She felt it. She felt it burning, and she was fighting not to show it, and she was losing.

"Miss Underhill." Corvane raised his voice just enough to carry. "You've gone pale. Are you unwell?"

Every eye at the near end of the table turned to her. Corvane saw her spine straighten, saw the discipline of nine years assert itself over whatever fire was licking at her skin. When she turned to face him, her expression was composed, courteous, faintly puzzled—the perfect mask.

"A moment of dizziness, my lord. The kitchens run warm tonight."

"The kitchens do indeed." He smiled, warm as a hearthfire, and lifted his goblet to her. "Perhaps you should sit. We cannot have our finest servant collapsing. Her Majesty depends on you more than she knows."

Something moved behind her eyes—something quick and cold and assessing—and Corvane had the pleasure of watching her realize, in real time, that she could not tell how much he knew. That was always his favorite part. The moment when the clever ones understood that the game had changed while they weren't looking.

"I am quite well, my lord," she said. "Thank you for your concern."

"Of course you are." He drank, though the wine was long past its best. "Eat, then. Finish the quail. It would be a shame to waste Pell's work—he's very proud of the glaze."

A heartbeat of silence. Then Freya picked up her fork again and took a second bite, larger than the first, holding his gaze across the candles as she did it. Defiance, or discipline, or perhaps simply the calculation of a woman who understood that refusing would look worse than complying. Either way, she swallowed, and the flush deepened almost imperceptibly at her collar, and Corvane felt the last doubt leave him like air leaving a bellows.

*It responds to her. The mark burns when the blood wakes. She is the seal, and she doesn't yet know what that makes her worth.*

The feast wound on. Corvane made pleasant conversation with the Master of Coin about grain tariffs, laughed in the right places at the fool's juggling, and all the while tracked Freya Underhill the way a falcon tracks a hare it has no intention of flying at yet. By the time the sweet course arrived—honey cakes, naturally; he did enjoy a theme—she had recovered her full composure. Only someone who had studied her as closely as he had would notice that she kept her hair loose over her left temple tonight, when usually she pinned it back. Covering the mark. Clever girl.

When the tables were cleared and the court dispersed, Corvane intercepted her in the corridor outside the great hall, where torchlight made the stone walls pulse with shifting shadows.

"Miss Underhill. A word, if you please."

"My lord."

"I wanted to tell you," he said, pitching his voice low and kind, "how much Her Majesty values you. Nine years, and not one failure. Do you know how rare that is? Men have built careers on less. Men *have* built careers on less." He reached into his coat and produced a small cloth-wrapped bundle, pressing it into her hands. "Honey lozenges. For the throat. Tasters ruin their voices by thirty, or so I'm told. Consider it a token of appreciation—from someone who notices such things."

Freya looked down at the bundle, then up at him. Her face gave away nothing, but her eyes—her eyes were doing rapid, silent arithmetic, weighing the gift against the evening, against the quail, against the warmth she could not quite explain away.

"That's generous of you, my lord."

"Not at all. Generosity costs me nothing." He stepped closer, close enough that she would catch the scent of ink and cold iron that clung to him, close enough to speak the next words softly enough that they belonged entirely to the two of them. "You should know, Miss Underhill, that I consider you one of the crown's most valuable assets. And I take very good care of what's valuable." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat on silk. "Whatever troubles find you—and in this world, troubles find everyone—you may come to me. Before anyone else. Before anyone at all."

For a moment, something flickered across her face. Fear, perhaps. Or the beginning of understanding. Or maybe just the exhaustion of a woman who had spent her whole life being invisible and had just heard, unmistakably, the sound of someone seeing her.

"I'll remember that, my lord," she said.

"I know you will." He inclined his head, the picture of courtesy. "Good evening, Miss Underhill. Sleep well. You've earned it."

He watched her walk away down the corridor, straight-backed and unhurried, the honey lozenges held in her hand like something she hadn't decided whether to keep. At the corner she paused—just briefly—and glanced back at him, and Corvane smiled and raised two fingers in a small salute.

Then she was gone, and he turned back toward the great hall, humming under his breath.

In his tower study, he drew out the archive drawing once more and laid it beside his notes. The sigil of the Underhills: a serpent coiled around a falling star, the mark of the bloodline sworn to the dragon-fire that had caged the gods beneath Thornfell. Three hundred years the crown had believed that power lost, that bloodline dead, that war finished. And all the while it had been standing at the queen's elbow, tasting her soup, thanking the cooks.

Queen Maren wanted the gods as weapons before she died. Corvane intended to deliver them—but not to her. A dying woman's ambition was a candle in wind; his own would be a bonfire. The regency that would follow her death would need a foundation no army could shake, and there was no foundation like a god on a leash.

All of it hung on one thin woman in grey.

He dipped his pen and wrote a single line in cipher in his private ledger:

*Confirmed. The mark answers. Proceed to secure the source — gently, if possible.*

He blotted the page, and as he did, he found himself thinking of her face in the corridor—that flicker of fear, quickly mastered. She was clever. She was disciplined. She would run, eventually, when she understood the shape of the trap; the clever ones always ran. Let her. He knew every road out of Thornfell keep, every gate, every name on every gatekeeper's roster. And when she ran, she would run straight into the arms of the only man in the kingdom who had ever offered her kindness.

He snuffed the candle and stood a while in the dark, listening to the mountain wind moan against the shutters, and permitted himself the rare luxury of anticipation.

Soon, Miss Underhill. Soon you and I will have a much longer conversation.

* * *

Two floors below, in the narrow room she kept above the taster's quarters, Freya set the honey lozenges on her table and stared at them for a long time.

Emberthorn. She had recognized it in the quail within three heartbeats—recognized the taste the way she recognized every poison in her repertoire, that was her trade, that was her pride—but emberthorn was not a poison. It was a fever remedy. Refined, concentrated, yes, but harmless to a healthy body, and she had tasted enough of it in village teas to know exactly what harmless felt like.

Except it hadn't felt harmless. It had felt like swallowing a coal. It had felt like the mark beneath her hair waking up and pressing hot fingers against the inside of her skull, whispering in a language older than the crown, older than the keep, older than the names of things.

Someone had put it there on purpose. Someone who knew what it would do.

She unwrapped one lozenge, held it to the candlelight, and thought of Lord Corvane's smile—warm as a hearth, and about as safe to lean into. She thought of the census rolls she'd found in her grandmother's croft, the sigil carved over the hearth matching the mark on her skin. She thought of bread vanishing at a roadside shrine, of wells running sweet, of prayers answered with teeth.

Then she wrapped the lozenge back in its cloth, tucked it into the false bottom of her herb chest alongside the vials no taster should own, and blew out her candle.

Let him watch. Let him smile. She had survived nine years at a poisoned table by learning one lesson better than anyone alive: the deadliest dish is the one served with kindness, and the only way to eat it is slowly, carefully, and with your eyes open.

Tomorrow she would visit the smuggler roads. Tomorrow she would start asking questions of people who owed her silence.

Tonight, she slept with her back to the wall and her hand curled around a vial of antidote, and dreamed of fire.
