## 11. The Shrine Below

The door was where the map said it would be, which was the first thing that frightened her.

Freya had spent eleven days cross-referencing the pattern of answered prayers against the smuggler routes she knew better than her own heartbeat, and every line she drew converged on a single point beneath Thornfell Keep: the old sealed shrine, the one the court called a cellar and the mountain folk called the Deep Door. She had expected to find rubble, or nothing at all. She had not expected to find a door that fit its frame so perfectly, so deliberately, in a corridor that the keep's own stewards swore did not exist.

She stood before it now with a shuttered lantern in one hand and Wick's iron pry-bar in the other, wearing the plain gray dress of a kitchen servant and the expression of a woman who belonged exactly where she was. That expression had kept her alive through twenty years of tasting poison for the crown. It would have to serve her here.

The corridor itself was wrong. Freya knew old stone; she had grown up in her grandmother's croft, where the walls sweated in summer and wept in winter and every crack told you the weather three days before it came. This stone did none of those things. It was dry as bone, smooth as river glass, and warm—warm in the way a sleeping animal is warm, faintly, patiently, as though the mountain itself were breathing around her. The lantern light slid across it without catching, as if the walls were drinking the glow rather than reflecting it.

And there were no guards. That was the thing no one would ever believe: the most closely held secret in Thornfell Keep, the sealed shrine beneath the queen's own hall, guarded by nothing but a lock older than the kingdom. As if whoever built this place had known that locks and soldiers were beside the point. As if the true defense was simply that no one would ever think to come looking.

Freya thought of the village woman at the crossroads, leaving bread on a forgotten altar and watching it vanish into thin air. She thought of sweetened wells and broken fevers, each miracle followed by a price—a lamb dead in its pen, a child's fever broken only to return twice as fierce, a widow's grief lifted and replaced by something colder. She thought of Lord Corvane's eyes tracking her across the queen's long table, patient and hungry, like a man watching a purse he intended to cut from a stranger's belt.

*Every road leads down,* her grandmother used to say, when Freya asked why the croft's back path wound so far below the ridge before climbing again. *You can't get anywhere worth going without going under something.*

She set the pry-bar against the seam of the door and leaned her weight into it.

The door did not resist. It swung inward with a sigh, slow and heavy, exhaling air that had been shut away for centuries—air that smelled of hot metal and honey and something underneath both, something organic and vast, like the inside of a closed mouth. The warmth rolled over her, and with it came a sensation she had no name for: a pressure behind her eyes, a fullness in her ears, as if she had stepped into water too deep to stand in.

Her birthmark began to burn.

Not the mild flare she'd felt at the queen's table, when Corvane's poisoned dish had set her skin alight beneath her sleeve. This was deeper, older, a heat that started in the marrow of her arm and worked outward, tracing the sigil's lines beneath her skin like a finger following a map. Freya gritted her teeth and stepped through the doorway, pulling it shut behind her—not all the way, just enough to leave herself a thread of escape—and raised the lantern.

What she saw made her forget, for a long moment, how to breathe.

The chamber was round, or nearly so, perhaps forty paces across, and its ceiling arched up into darkness beyond the lantern's reach. The floor was the same glass-smooth stone as the corridor, but here it was veined with lines of dull gold that ran in patterns too regular for nature and too organic for craft—rivers of metal frozen mid-flow, converging, all of them, toward the far wall.

The wall.

It filled the entire curve of the chamber, floor to unseen ceiling, and it was not stone. It was glass—or something like glass—black shot through with veins of deep red and molten orange, as if someone had taken fire itself and hammered it flat while it still burned. Fused dragon-glass, the old stories called it, though Freya had always assumed the stories meant metaphor. There was no metaphor here. The wall radiated heat the way a hearth does, steady and immense, and when she pressed her palm near it—not touching, never touching—the hair on her arms rose and curled.

And within the glass, things moved.

At first she took them for reflections, distortions of her own lantern flame playing across an uneven surface. Then one of them turned and looked at her. It had too many joints in its neck and eyes like drowned stars, and it regarded her with an attention so ancient and so patient that Freya felt her knees soften. She did not look away—some instinct older than reason told her that looking away would be a mistake—but she did step back, once, twice, until her heel found the chamber's center, where the golden veins converged.

There, inlaid in the floor, was the seal.

It was a circle perhaps three paces wide, wrought in that same gold, and its design stopped her heart. A thorned branch, coiled. A flame cupped in a leaf. And at the center, rendered in lines fine as hair and unmistakable as a signature: the sigil. Her sigil. The mark on her arm, the birthmark shaped like a branching flame inside a ring of thorns, reproduced here at the heart of the prison wall's anchor-point, worn by centuries but still sharp enough to draw blood from the eye that traced it.

Beneath it, in letters whose alphabet she half-recognized from her grandmother's oldest letters—the letters she'd assumed were decorative—ran an inscription. Freya knelt, wincing as the movement dragged the burning mark across her sleeve, and read it aloud because she could not help herself, because some part of her needed to hear it in a human voice:

*"UNDERHILL KEEPS THE DOOR."*

Four words. Fourteen letters. They sat in the gold like a knife left standing in a table.

She read them again. And again. And each time, the words rearranged themselves in her understanding, settling into a shape she had been avoiding her whole life without knowing it.

Underhill keeps the door. Not *kept*. Keeps. Present tense, a duty described as ongoing, as if the seal itself considered the family bound to it still—as if somewhere in the mountain's long memory, the Underhills had never stopped existing, never stopped mattering, never been permitted to be merely dead.

Freya sat back on her heels in the center of the seal and laughed, once, a short ugly sound that the chamber swallowed whole.

All those years swallowing poison for the crown. All those years cataloguing symptoms in her own flesh, learning every hidden road and whispered secret of the Thornfells, priding herself on knowing everything that moved beneath the surface of the world. And beneath her feet, beneath the very keep whose tables she served, had been *this*—and her own name written on the lock of it.

She thought of her grandmother, who had never spoken of the family's history beyond a few cryptic sayings about doors and going-under. Who had taught her herb-lore with a strange urgency, as if the knowledge were a relay baton being pressed into her hands by someone running out of breath. Who had died with her letters bundled and labeled in a hand too steady for a dying woman, as if she'd known they'd be needed.

*You can't get anywhere worth going without going under something.*

Grandmother had known. Grandmother had spent a lifetime keeping a secret so large it had a wall of fused dragon-glass and a prison of starving gods attached to it, and she had passed it down not as words but as instinct, as habit, as a child raised to know every smuggler's pass and every forgotten shrine in the mountains. Not because the Underhills were hiding from something.

Because they were guarding it.

Freya pressed her hand flat against the inscription, and the gold was warm as skin.

The pressure behind her eyes intensified. In the glass wall, the shapes had gathered—drawn closer to the surface, drawn closer to *her*, dozens of them now, pressing against the inside of their prison like faces against a window. Some were beautiful in the way storms are beautiful. Some were beautiful in the way open wounds are beautiful. One of them, larger than the rest, positioned directly opposite the seal, watched her with an expression she recognized with a lurch of horror.

Recognition. It looked at her the way a man looks at a face he has seen in a crowd a hundred years ago and never forgotten.

"You know what I am," she whispered. The words fell into the chamber and did not echo; the room ate sound the way it ate light. "You know whose blood is on this seal."

The great shape at the center of the wall did not answer in words. It did something worse. It *leaned*, the way a starving man leans toward the smell of bread, and everywhere its mass pressed against the glass, the red veins brightened, flaring like coals breathed upon. For one vertiginous instant Freya felt the chamber tilt, felt the golden rivers in the floor begin to move beneath her knees—

—and then the flare guttered. The veins dimmed. The shapes receded, slowly, reluctantly, like a tide being pulled back by a moon that had grown tired. The pressure behind her eyes eased to a dull ache. The burn in her arm settled into a low, persistent throb, the sigil's heat banking itself like a fire raked over.

Leaving her kneeling alone in the dark, on her family's name, with the taste of something in her mouth she could not identify.

No—that wasn't true. She could identify it. Twenty years of tasting had trained her palate to catch anything, and she caught this now, faint and unmistakable, rising off the warm gold beneath her fingers.

Honey. Underneath the hot-metal smell of the chamber, underneath the vast organic breath of the prison, the seal tasted of honey. Sweetness laid down deliberately, the way a poisoner lays sweetness over bitterness to make the dose go down easy.

Freya rose on unsteady legs. Her mind, that meticulous survivalist mind that had kept her alive through a hundred poisoned dishes, began doing what it always did: cataloguing. The seal was intact but strained—she had seen the strain, felt it lean toward her. The gods inside were awake and aware and *hungry*, and they had been fed recently; the crossroads miracles, the priced prayers, all of it funneling down here like rain finding a drain. Someone or something was feeding them devotion, and devotion was clearly a currency the prison understood.

And the seal bore her name. Which meant that whatever happened next—whatever Corvane wanted, whatever the queen dreamed of, whatever the starving things behind the glass intended—would happen *through* her, or not at all.

She had carried secrets before. Poisoners' secrets, court secrets, the names of smugglers who trusted her, the symptoms of toxins that could end a royal line. She had carried them lightly, professionally, the way a courier carries a sealed letter—aware of the weight, respectful of it, but never owned by it.

This was different. This secret had a pulse. This secret had looked at her through a wall of black glass and *known her*. This secret was made of her own blood, her own name, her own inheritance, and it was straining at its seams while a spymaster sharpened his knives upstairs and a god learned to smile through the mouths of desperate smallfolk.

Freya picked up the lantern. Its light seemed thinner now, thinner than when she'd entered, and she wondered briefly how much of it the chamber had drunk.

"I don't know yet what I'm going to do," she said aloud, to the wall, to the shapes, to the name in the floor. Her voice sounded small and human and stubborn, and she was glad of it. "But I'm the last Underhill, and I keep the door. Whatever that means—it means *me*. So you'll wait. You'll wait until I understand what I'm holding."

The great shape at the center of the wall regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, it inclined its many-jointed head—an inclination that might have been mockery, might have been courtesy, might have been patience. The red veins dimmed further, all along the wall, in a wave that traveled outward from the seal like a bow passing through a crowd.

Acknowledgment. Or agreement. Or simply a predator conserving its strength.

Freya backed away, one careful step at a time, until her shoulder found the corridor door. She slipped through it and pulled it shut, and stood a long while in the dry warm dark, listening to her own heartbeat slow.

Then she climbed. Up through corridors that did not exist, past storerooms and wine cellars and the ordinary busy noise of a keep that had no idea what slept beneath its floors, out through the kitchens where the evening bread was coming golden from the ovens, out into the grey afternoon where Wick was waiting for her at the market well with two apples and a question he was too disciplined to ask.

She gave him one apple. She ate the other walking, mechanically, tasting nothing.

That night, in her narrow room above the taster's quarters, Freya sat by candlelight with her grandmother's letters spread across the blanket, reading them now with new eyes, hunting for the word *door*, the word *seal*, the word *Underhill*—hunting for any instruction, any warning, any scrap of what she was supposed to do with a secret that had a pulse.

Near the bottom of the oldest bundle, in handwriting gone brown with age, she found a single line that stopped her cold:

*"If you are reading this, the door has begun to open. Do not go to the crown. The crown will want to use what's behind it. Come to the croft, little spark. Everything grandmother couldn't say, the house will say for me."*

Freya folded the letter closed with hands that almost didn't shake.

Tomorrow, she thought. Tomorrow she would ride for the croft. Tomorrow she would learn what her grandmother had died knowing.

Tonight, she blew out the candle and lay in the dark with her sleeve pushed back, watching the sigil glow faintly in the blackness—watching it pulse, slow and steady, in time with something far below her, something vast and patient that had waited centuries and could evidently wait a little longer.

In time with her own heartbeat.

Or she realized, as sleep finally took her—almost, but not quite, in time.
