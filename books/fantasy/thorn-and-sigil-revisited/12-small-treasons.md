## 12. Small Treasons

The lie was small at first. That was the thing about lies—Freya had learned this from the other side of the table, from watching courtiers sweeten their words like cooks masking bitterness—the first one is always small enough to swallow whole.

It happened on a Tuesday, over a pheasant pie.

She stood at her station in the tasting chamber below the queen's kitchens, the morning light falling gray through the arrow-slit window, and she lifted the silver spoon to her lips and tasted—clearly, unmistakably—a thread of henbane in the spiced custard beneath the crust. Not enough to kill. Enough to sicken, to slow, to make a body miserable for a week and cast suspicion on every cook in the palace. The kind of poison that was less an assassination than a message.

Her tongue catalogued it the way it always did: bitter edge behind the sweetness, a faint numbing at the root of the teeth, the ghost of it already prickling along her gums. Henbane, steeped too long, strained carelessly. Someone had wanted it found.

And Freya—who had spent eleven years swallowing death so that Queen Maren could eat her supper in peace—looked down at the spoon, and thought of Lord Corvane's eyes on her across the great hall, patient as a spider's, and of the sigil burning under her sleeve when she'd tasted the saffron dish three weeks past, and of the sealed shrine beneath Thornfell keep with her family's name carved into its door.

*If I report this,* she thought, *Corvane will have another thread to pull. Another test. Another reason to keep me close.*

She swallowed the taste. She always swallowed; that was the trade. Then she set down the spoon, wiped her mouth on the linen cloth, and said, in the flat, bored voice of a woman who had tasted ten thousand dishes and been surprised by none of them:

"The custard's oversweet. The crust has taken the smoke wrong. It'll sit heavy on the queen's stomach, but there's nothing in it."

The under-steward scribbled his note. The pie went up to the long table. And Freya Underhill committed her first treason against the crown of Thornfell with henbane still numbing her teeth.

* * *

She told herself it wasn't treason. That was the second thing about lies: they came with their own alibis, pre-written, waiting.

*You didn't clear the poison,* she reasoned, walking the long corridor toward her rooms with her hands folded and her face arranged into its usual nothing. *You simply declined to investigate it. There's a difference. A taster's duty is to the queen's plate, not to the crown's quarrels. If someone in the kitchens wants to send messages via henbane, that's the steward's affair, the guard's affair—*

But she knew who would investigate. She knew whose men would come asking gentle questions of scullery maids, whose ledgers would grow one line longer, whose quiet web would draw one degree tighter around a taster with a glowing birthmark and a bloodline written in census rolls. Corvane did not waste poisons. Every dish that came to her laced or unlaced was a question, and every answer she gave him was a brick in whatever wall he was building around her.

So she had stopped answering honestly. That was all. That was the whole of it.

She was still telling herself this when she reached her door and found Wick sitting on the step outside it, knees drawn up, gnawing the last of yesterday's bread with the concentration of a boy who had learned never to eat quickly and never to leave food unattended.

"You're late," he said.

"I'm exactly on time."

"You're late," he repeated, with the serene confidence of the young, "and Cook Aldric asked where you'd got to, and I said you were checking the pepper stores because the new shipment smelled off." He paused. "Did it? Smell off?"

Freya looked down at him. Wick was eleven, maybe twelve—he didn't know himself, which was its own small tragedy—and he had the kind of face that made lying look like a civic duty: sharp-chinned, quick-eyed, built for surviving places where truth got people hurt. She had taken him on three months ago because he was starving and clever and because, though she hadn't admitted it even to herself then, she had wanted someone in the world who was hers to protect rather than hers to use.

Now he was covering for her without being asked. Without knowing what he was covering.

"The pepper's fine," she said quietly, and unlocked the door. "Come in. We have work."

* * *

The work, that afternoon, was foxglove and feverfew, and Freya taught it the way her grandmother must once have taught it—hands moving, voice low, the knowledge passing from skin to skin like warmth.

"Tell me what you see."

Wick bent over the two cuttings laid on the table. "They look near enough the same. Same leaf-shape. Same stem."

"Look again. Slower."

He looked. Freya watched him do it—the way his eyes moved now, not darting but methodical, corner to corner, learning patience the way she'd learned it: as a survival skill. "The veins. This one's veins go pale at the edges. The other stays green all the way."

"Feverfew keeps its color to the tip. Foxglove fades first. If you're ever brewing for a fever and you take the wrong one, you won't cure the fever—you'll stop the heart." She turned the cutting over. "Everything in this room can be food or poison or medicine, and most things are all three. The difference isn't in the plant. It's in the dose, the preparation, and the hand."

"Like the pie," Wick said.

Freya went very still.

"I heard the kitchen talk," he said, not looking up from the leaves. "Cook Aldric swears the custard was clean when it left his ovens. He's half-mad about it. Says somebody's trying to get him flogged." He glanced up then, quick and unreadable. "You tasted it, didn't you. Before you said it was clean."

The fire popped in the little hearth. Outside, rain had begun against the shutters, soft and steady, the mountain weather rolling in off the peaks the way it always did by autumn, closing the high passes one by one.

"Wick," Freya said carefully, "what you hear in the kitchens, you forget. That's the first rule of working for me. What you see here, you keep. But the rest—"

"I know." He set down the cutting. "I'm not saying anything to anyone. I'm saying it to *you*." His chin came up, that stubborn set she was beginning to know too well. "You lied to the steward. I watched your hands while you did it. You fold your thumb under when you lie. You've done it twice since the solstice."

Freya stared at him.

For eleven years she had been invisible—not merely unnoticed but *unnoticeable*, a fixture, a piece of the palace's furniture, the woman with the flat face and the iron stomach whom no one looked at twice. She had built her whole survival on being unreadable. And an orphan boy with flour still under his fingernails had read her in an afternoon.

"You fold your thumb under," she said at last, "when you're frightened. Did you know that?"

"No," said Wick, and folded it.

Something in her chest cracked—painfully, almost laughably—like ice going out of a river. She sat down across from him at the table, among the herbs and the knives and the little clay pots of numbered powders, and she heard herself say:

"There's a man at court. Lord Corvane. He's been watching me, and I don't know yet what he wants, but I know it isn't good, and I know my name is part of it. My family's name." She turned her wrist over, pushed back the sleeve, and let him see the sigil—faintly luminous even in daylight now, a coiled knot of lines like thornwork, like a dragon seen through smoke. "This is why. I don't understand all of it yet. But until I do, some of what I tell the court will be untrue, and if anyone ever asks you whether I've been strange lately, you'll say no, and you'll fold your thumb under, and they'll believe you, because you're better at this than I am."

Wick looked at the mark for a long moment. He didn't flinch. She had known grown men—grown *tasters*—who flinched.

"It's warm," he said. "I can feel it from here. Like standing near an oven."

"It's been warm since midsummer." She pulled the sleeve down. "Do you understand what I'm asking of you? This isn't forgetting a smell in the pepper stores. If Corvane decides I'm lying, people who help me lie will hang beside me. You could walk out that door right now and ask for a place in the scullery, and I'd write you a reference myself, and I'd never think less of—"

"I'm staying." He said it the way he did everything now: without drama, without appeal, a boy stating facts about weather. "Nobody's ever taught me anything before. Nobody's ever had a door I was allowed to sit outside." A pause, and then, more quietly, with the terrible precision of children: "You're the first person who chose me instead of just keeping me. I remember the difference."

Freya looked away, because her eyes were doing something she refused to permit them, and because the rain against the shutters sounded suddenly like the sea, and because there was no honest answer to that except the one she couldn't afford to give.

"Eat something," she said gruffly, pushing the bread board toward him. "Then we'll do nightshade. If you're going to perjure yourself for me, you'll do it well-fed and properly trained."

* * *

The lies grew easier, which frightened her more than any single one of them.

That was the treachery of it—not the moment of speaking falsehood, which still cost her a heartbeat's nausea every time, but the way the cost shrank. By week's end she was declining to report a trace of arsenic in the queen's honeyed wine (a trace, only a trace, likely contamination from a badly cleaned vessel—but *likely* was doing heavy labor in that sentence). By the next week she was reporting flavors that weren't there, small invented notes of bitterness that sent the steward's men chasing down harmless corridors while her own nights went to other business: the croft, the letters, the carved seal, the map she kept rolled inside her mattress with its growing constellation of marked villages.

Because the miracles hadn't stopped. They had spread.

A well at Hollowmere that had run brackish for a generation now ran sweet—but the woman who'd prayed at it had lost her singing voice. A fever that should have emptied half of Thornmill village broke overnight—but the priest who'd prayed hardest now woke screaming from dreams he wouldn't describe. Every answered prayer left a small wound somewhere else, and Freya, with her smuggler's network and her taster's habit of noticing what nobody noticed, was beginning to see the shape of the pattern: all the wounds pointed downhill. All the sweetness flowed from below. From beneath Thornfell keep itself, where the old road ended at a door her grandmother's letters called *the deep door*, and where her family name had been carved into stone by hands that had known what they were sealing and why.

Every day she bought herself with a lie. Every evening she spent the purchase.

"You're different," the under-steward remarked one morning, friendly, incurious, arranging the day's dishes. "Lighter. Thought court life would grind you down by now."

"I'm sleeping better," Freya said, and folded her thumb under, and hated how easily it came.

* * *

Corvane nearly caught her on the ninth day.

She should have known better than to relax—that was the lesson, and it was delivered with the spymaster's characteristic elegance. She was leaving the tasting chamber at dusk, mind full of the map and the next village to visit, when his voice came from the shadow of the colonnade, mild as milk.

"Mistress Underhill. A word."

My lord. Her spine performed its customary obeisance while her mind ran cold and fast. "Lord Corvane."

He fell into step beside her, unhurried, gloved hands clasped behind his back, a man strolling through his own garden. "Strange reports from the kitchens lately. Cook Aldric insists his custard left his ovens clean. One of the scullery boys swears he saw a stranger near the spice stores. And yet your report said nothing beyond oversweetness." He glanced sideways at her, smiling faintly. "You've such a reliable palate, Mistress Underhill. It would trouble me to learn it was failing."

"The custard was oversweet," Freya said. "Smoke in the crust. Nothing else. If Aldric says otherwise, perhaps he's protecting whoever let the sugar burn."

"A tidy answer."

"I'm a tidy woman, my lord."

He laughed—softly, genuinely, which was somehow worse than suspicion. They walked three steps in silence. Rain dripped from the colonnade eaves, counting time.

"And the scullery boy," Corvane said idly. "The one who claims a stranger was asking after the queen's table. Dark-haired lad, works the flour room. Yours, I believe? Your apprentice?"

The world narrowed to the width of a knife's edge. *He knows about Wick. Of course he knows about Wick. He knows everything that happens in this palace; that is the entire function of him.*

"He's mine," Freya said. "I took him on in spring. He sweeps my floor and grinds my samples and steals my bread. If he saw a stranger, he saw a stranger—I don't monitor the boy's eyes, my lord, only his hands."

"Of course." Corvane stopped walking. When he turned to her, his smile remained, but his eyes had gone to the place they always went eventually: her covered wrist, briefly, like a man checking a lock. "You know, Mistress Underhill, I've been reading old records. Census rolls, tax ledgers, parish books. Fascinating things, old records. They remember what families prefer to forget." He said it lightly, a man making conversation about weather. "The Underhills, for instance. Mountain folk, once. Crofters. Gone from the rolls these thirty years and more." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Gone entirely, one might say. Which makes you rather remarkable, doesn't it? The last leaf on the tree."

The rain counted. Somewhere across the courtyard, a cook shouted about oysters.

"My grandmother came down from the mountains," Freya said. "Married a cooper. Died when I was nine. I don't hold with trees, my lord. They fall on people."

"They do," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "Usually when someone's been cutting at the roots." He bowed, brief and correct. "Good evening, Mistress Underhill. Do sleep well. You look tired lately. I'd hate for anything to happen to your health."

He walked away into the dusk, and Freya stood in the colonnade with her heart slamming against her ribs and understood, with total clarity, that the game had changed. He wasn't probing anymore. He was *telling* her he knew. The courtesy of it was the threat: *I could take you today. I'm choosing not to. Ask yourself why.*

* * *

Wick was waiting in her rooms, and he knew before she spoke—she could see it in the way he'd positioned himself between the door and the window, the way his hands were very still.

"He talked to you," Wick said.

"How did you—"

"Your thumb's folded under and you haven't said a word yet." He stood up. "What did he want?"

"To remind me he exists." Freya crossed to the shuttered window and put her eye to the slat. The courtyard below was empty, but empty meant nothing; Corvane's web didn't show its threads. "He mentioned you. The scullery boy story—it was his. He planted it to see if I'd flinch."

"Did you flinch?"

"I'm flinching now."

"Good," said Wick, with grim satisfaction. "Means you're still human. Sit down. You look gray." And then, before she could object, he was moving around the room with the brisk competence she'd taught him—banking the fire, setting water to heat, laying out the willow bark she chewed for her headaches—and Freya sat, absurdly, obediently, in her own chair in her own rooms while a twelve-year-old tended her, and thought: *this is what belonging feels like. It feels like being cared for badly, by someone you taught, and letting it happen.*

"We need to move faster," she said. "The shrine. Whatever's down there—my grandmother's letters say the seal is weakening, that the prayers are leaking through the cracks, and Corvane knows what I am and is choosing patience, and I don't think his patience is long. I need to go down. Soon. Before the roads close for winter, before he decides his harvest is ripe."

"Then we go soon." Wick handed her the willow bark and a cup of hot water, matter-of-fact. "I'll cover the tasting days. I know your reports. I've copied them out for practice—you said to practice the handwriting, remember?" He almost smiled. "Oversweet. Smoke in the crust. Nothing else. I can write you a week of those in my sleep."

"That's exactly what worries me," Freya said. "You sound like me."

"Someone has to, if you're going to be underground talking to gods." He said it lightly. He didn't know how close it landed.

She drank the bitter water and looked at the boy she'd chosen and the city she'd betrayed and the mountains darkening beyond the shutters, and felt the two halves of her life grinding against each other like millstones—with her, inevitably, in between.

One thing remained true above all the lies, and she held onto it the way a drowning woman holds a rope: whatever Corvane wanted from her blood, whatever the god beneath the mountain wanted from her prayers, the queen's plate was still the queen's plate. Smallfolk ate at that table too—servants, guards, kitchen girls, Wick himself on feast days. Every poison she failed to catch might land in any mouth.

So she would lie to the crown, but she would not lie to the dead. Each evening, alone, she would taste-test the next day's dishes anyway—properly, secretly, with Wick watching the corridor—and if death waited in the gravy, she would swallow it herself and say nothing, and let her body be the wall it had always been.

The treason, she decided, would be precise. That was the taster's creed, after all: *know your dose.*

* * *

On the twelfth night, the dreams started.

Not nightmares—she'd had plenty of those, ordinary human ones, falls and fires and Corvane's smiling face. These were different. These were *downward*. In them she stood at the top of a stair that went into the mountain's roots, and far below something vast exhaled, and the breath rose past her like warm wind smelling of ash and honey, and a voice that was not a voice said—not to her, not quite, but through her, the way a current passes through a wire—

*—so hungry, so long, and now they pray, they pray, they pray—*

She woke with the sigil blazing through her sleeve, bright enough to throw shadows on the ceiling, and Wick asleep in the chair by the fire where he'd insisted on keeping watch, and outside the window the rain had stopped and the sky over Thornfell peak glowed faintly, faintly red, like a banked fire deciding whether to wake.

Freya lay still and listened to her own heartbeat, and beneath it, through the stone, through the miles of rock, something answering it.

Almost in time.

Not quite.

Three days later, a child went missing from Hollowmere—the sweet-water well, the praying woman, the price come due—and the first thing Freya heard, from a smuggler's mouth over cheap wine in a cellar that smelled of tar, was that the child had walked to the well at midnight, alone, barefoot in the frost, smiling, and stepped *down*.
