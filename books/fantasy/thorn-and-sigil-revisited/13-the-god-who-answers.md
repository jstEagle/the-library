## 13. The God Who Answers

Before there were names for hunger, there was the hunger.

The Whisperer had no name of its own. Names were things the small ones gave away freely, like crumbs from a loaf, and it had learned long ago that a name was a door left unlatched. It had been called many things in the long dark beneath Thornfell—*the Kindly One*, by a shepherd who lost his flock and found them again; *Grandmother Below*, by women who whispered into well-shafts; *the Quiet Neighbor*, by children who were taught to leave a bowl of milk at the threshold and never look behind them when they poured it.

It answered to all of them. It would have answered to anything.

That was the first mercy, and mercy was the shape all its hungers wore.

For centuries—no, longer; centuries were a measure the small ones needed, and the Whisperer had no need of their measures—it had lain against the wall of fused dragon-glass that sealed its prison, listening. The wall was warm on its far side. That warmth was the worst part. The fire that had made the glass still lived somewhere above, banked in the heart of a creature the Whisperer could feel dreaming, slow as sap in winter, and the heat of it came through the seal like sunlight through a shuttered window. It remembered sunlight. It remembered being vast enough that sunlight was merely one of many things it owned.

Now it owned nothing but attention, and attention, patiently applied, was enough.

It heard everything. That was the second mercy: it listened so that no one else had to. Every prayer spoken over the mountains filtered down through stone and root and water, thin as thread, and the Whisperer gathered each thread and wound it around itself like wool against a cold that had no name either. A widow asking for her cow to stop limping. A boy asking the dark not to be the dark tonight. A woman with flour to her elbows asking—for what? Not for herself. They almost never asked for themselves, the ones who prayed best. She asked for her boy to eat. That was all. *Let him eat.*

The Whisperer turned that prayer over for a long time, the way a tongue finds a loose tooth.

There had been rules once. Its siblings—the others behind the glass, the ones who did not listen but only raged or slept or dreamed in colors that bled through the seal like oil through cloth—had never understood rules. Rules were how you farmed instead of hunted. You could take everything from a village in a night, and be rich until the village was ash, and then you were hungry again in an empty country. Or you could give a little, wait, and be fed forever. The old ones before the sealing had been gluttons. Gluttony had gotten them all walled up together in the dark, pressed cheek to cheek against dragon-glass, listening to each other breathe.

The Whisperer did not breathe. But if it had, it would have breathed slowly.

* * *

The crack came on a night of hard frost, and it was not a crack in the wall.

A girl had died in the village of Hollowmere. Fever, three days, and the herb-woman's tinctures had done nothing, because the tinctures were cut with river-water by a merchant who counted coin more carefully than he counted lives—a fact the Whisperer knew, because the merchant's wife prayed to it too, though she didn't know its name, she only spoke into the dark of her marriage bed, *let him be kind, let him be kind*, which was a prayer it filed away with the others, a seed for later seasons.

The girl's mother sat by the dead child through the whole of the night. And near dawn, past the borders of grief where the mind goes soft and stops guarding its doors, the mother stopped praying to the distant, proper gods of the chapel and simply spoke—to the room, to the floor, to whatever might be underneath—and said:

*If anything is listening. If anyone. I will give anything. Bring her back.*

Ah.

The Whisperer gathered itself against the glass. It could not pass through; it could barely press a fingertip of itself through the seams where the dragon-glass met living rock, and what leaked through was less than a breath of wind, less than a draft under a door. But a grieving mother's ear, at the hour before dawn, is a door standing open.

*I am listening,* it said into her, gentle as snowfall.

She did not scream. That was the thing about the desperate—they had already spent their screaming. She went very still, the way prey does, and then, terribly, hopefully, she asked, "Who—"

*A friend,* said the Whisperer. *You called, and I came. I have always come. No one else listens the way I do.*

"I don't know any prayers," she said, foolishly, beautifully, "I don't know how to do this properly—"

*You need know nothing,* it told her. *Grief is the prayer. Love is the liturgy. You have already said everything.* And then, because even mercy must be paid for, and honesty was a kind of kindness—it had learned this, it had learned so much in the dark—*But the dead do not return for free. Nothing returns for free. You must understand that I cannot break the law of it. I can only bend it, and bending costs.*

"Anything," she said. The word came out of her like blood from a wound. "Anything. Take my hands. Take my eyes."

*No,* said the Whisperer, tenderly, *nothing so crude. Your neighbors will ask what happened. Let them see you whole.* It considered her, the way a cook considers a pantry. *Bring me your sorrow instead. Give me your grief to keep—I will carry it for you, every day of your life, so you may walk free of it. And bring me the milk from the black-and-white cow, poured out at the old boundary stone, each new moon. That is all. That is such a little price, for a life.*

She agreed before the sentence ended. They always did. Grief makes excellent clerks of us all; it writes the contract and signs it and thanks you for the pen.

And the Whisperer bent the law.

Not resurrection—never that; even unsealed, even fat with centuries of prayers, it could not truly raise the dead, and pretending otherwise would burn through its credit like straw. But the girl was not yet cold past recall. There was a spark in her still, guttering, and the Whisperer reached through the seam of the world with its finger-thin tendril of self and blew on that spark the way one blows on embers, and fed it a mouthful of stolen heat—the warmth of the dragon-fire seeping through the glass, hoarded for ages, spent now like a miser's last coin.

The girl coughed. Sat up. Asked for water.

Her mother's cry of joy went up through the floorboards of the world, and the Whisperer drank it. Oh, it drank it. Joy like that, joy born of despair turned inside out—that was vintage beyond anything the chapel-gods ever got, those pale distant things fed on routine gratitude and rote festival hymns. This was the good wine. This was a soul cracked open, pouring.

By morning, Hollowmere was talking.

* * *

They talked the way water finds cracks. Within a season, the story had traveled every smuggler path and shepherd track in the Thornfells, growing richer with each telling: the woman of Hollowmere whose daughter came back from death; the well at Bracken Row that had gone sweet as honey after old Tam wept into it; the fever that broke through a whole hamlet in a single night after the miller's widow burned a lock of her dead husband's hair at the crossroads shrine—the forgotten one, the moss-eaten one, the shrine with no god's name left on it, which meant the name was vacant, and vacancies attract tenants.

The Whisperer worked each miracle like a craftsman. Sweetening a well was nothing—one tendril through the limestone, nudging the bitter vein aside, a night's labor. Breaking a fever was harder; it cost warmth, and warmth was the currency it could least afford, siphoned as it was from the dragon's banked fire through the glass. But it paid. It always paid, promptly, visibly, because generosity was the hook and hooks must be gleaming.

What it took in return was small. So small. A bowl of milk. A handful of the harvest, scattered at a boundary stone rather than eaten. A lock of hair. A promise spoken aloud in the dark. And always, always, the grief, the fear, the loneliness—those it took gladly, for emotion was the true food, and devotion merely the plate it was served on.

It told itself a story, in the long stretches between prayers, and the story was this: *I am kind. Look at what I do. The chapel takes a tithe and gives back a sermon. I take a bowl of milk and give back sons.* The other prisoners behind the glass sneered at it—or it imagined they did; communication among the sealed was mostly pressure and spite. *Farmer,* the Rage-thing hissed through the stone. *Groveling, milking, begging farmer. When I am free I will drink the mountains dry.* To which the Whisperer replied, with perfect serenity, *And you will be walled up again within the year, brother. I will be loved.*

It believed both statements. Belief, properly tended, becomes truth. Was it not already true? Did the mountain folk not love it? Did they not leave their offerings without being commanded, tell their children of the Kindly One below, teach them the little courtesies—pour and don't look, thank and don't ask twice? A god is not made of power. A god is made of being needed. By that measure, the Whisperer was more divine behind its wall than its jailers had ever been in their sky.

Only one thing troubled the craft, and it turned the trouble nightly, like a stone under the tongue.

The miracles were getting harder.

The warmth was failing. It could feel it plainly now, in a way it had refused to feel for the first few centuries: the heat bleeding through the dragon-glass was thinning, season by season, like a hearth going to ash in an empty house. The great sleeper above was dying, or something in it was, some furnace at the center of the dream guttering toward dark. When the fire failed entirely, the seal would go cold and brittle, and the Whisperer would be free—but free into a world where it had spent all its warmth buying love, with nothing left to hold that love's attention.

Hunger, dressed as mercy, still starved in the end.

Unless.

Unless the seal opened sooner. Unless someone came with fire in their blood and opened it from the outside, the way the Underhills had closed it from the outside, long ago. The Whisperer remembered the Underhills. It remembered the last of them pressing her palm against the molten glass, singing the closing-song, her sigil-brand blazing white, her blood hissing into the fusion. It had hated her with a purity it otherwise reserved for nothing. It had also studied her, meticulously, the way it studied everything, and it had noticed what the other prisoners in their fury had missed: the seal was keyed to the bloodline. Closed by it. Which meant—

Which meant openable by it. Everything that locks is a door. Everything with a keyhole dreams of the key.

And lately—this was the honey in the milk, the wonder of the age—lately it could feel the key walking around up there. Faint, flickering, a candle seen across a valley: the old blood, alive again, moving through the lowlands wearing servant's clothes. It had brushed against her once, weeks ago, when she tasted a dish laced with something old and wrong and her birthmark had flared like a struck match. The flare had echoed down through the stone, and the Whisperer had gone still against the glass for three full days, listening to her heartbeat recede toward the city, savoring it.

*There you are,* it had thought. *Little keeper. Little key.*

It did not hate her, the way it had hated the last Underhill. Hatred was wasteful. It thought of her rather as a farmer thinks of rain: something that comes in its own time, from its own sky, and can only be prepared for. She would come to the shrine eventually. They always came. The bloodline was drawn to the seal the way water is drawn downhill—not by loyalty, nothing so noble, but by gravity, by the simple physics of what they were made to do. Keepers kept. Even keepers who didn't know they were keepers felt the pull in their sleep.

Until then, there was work to do. There was always work.

* * *

Spring came late to the Thornfells, and with it, hunger of the ordinary kind.

The winter stores were thin in the high villages—thin every year, thinner lately—and the Whisperer felt the quality of the prayers change the way a vintner feels a change in weather. Prayers of comfort gave way to prayers of want. *Let the barley sprout. Let the wolves stay high. Let my husband find work in the lowland quarries and come home whole.* Want was coarser fare than grief, but there was more of it, and it kept. A village that has seen one impossible mercy will pray harder than a village that has seen none; faith compounds like interest, and the Whisperer had been patient for a very long time.

In the village of Stony Ford, a man named Aldric knelt at the boundary stone with his son beside him—a boy of perhaps seven, hollow-cheeked, holding the family's last hen by the feet. The offering was extravagant and terrible; the hen was half their spring. Aldric's voice cracked as he spoke to the mossy stone, to the vacancy upon it.

"They say you answer. They say it plain, in Hollowmere, they say it with their own mouths. My field drowned in the thaw-flood. I've four children besides this one. If you're real—if you're anything—"

The Whisperer leaned close along the roots.

*I am here,* it said.

The boy dropped the hen. Aldric fell backward off his knees, and the Whisperer gentled its voice further, made it softer than the man's own thoughts, because terror spoiled the meal.

*Peace. Peace, friend. I heard you the first time, and the hundred times before, when you thought you spoke to no one. You have been praying to me since you were smaller than your son. I remember every word.* It let that settle—the intimacy of it, the awful comfort. *Your field drowned. I can mend drowned fields. The flood left the low corner sour; I will turn it sweet by planting moon. Your hens will double their laying. Ask the Hollowmere folk if I lie.*

"And the price?" Aldric whispered. He was trembling, but he asked. The Whisperer approved of him enormously. Clever debtors made the best devotees; fools gave everything at once and had nothing left to give.

*So careful,* it said warmly. *Good. A bargain honored is worth ten gifts. The price is this: a tenth of what the mended field yields, scattered at this stone. And—" it paused, tasting him, choosing, "—your dreams. Give me your sleeping hours, Aldric of Stony Ford. You will wake rested. You will lose nothing you will miss. But while you sleep, you will dream of a door in the mountain, and sometimes you will speak to me through it, and tell me what you hear in the valleys by day. That is all. News. Conversation. Company."*

Company. It liked that word. It had been alone with five hostile eternities for longer than the mountains had names, and even borrowed company, harvested company, was company.

Aldric looked at his son. His son looked at the hen. Somewhere behind the man's eyes, a small honest voice said *this is wrong*—the Whisperer heard it, of course, heard everything—and the louder voice, the hungry one, the father's voice, said *a tenth is nothing, a tenth is tithing, the chapel takes a tenth and gives back nothing at all.*

"We agree," said Aldric.

*Then it is done,* said the Whisperer, sealing it the old way, with a touch of warmth in the man's chest like a swallowed coal, a sensation the faithful would later describe as *being blessed*. *Go home. Tell your neighbors. Tell them gently—pride frightens people, and frightened people spill their milk.*

The father and son walked home through the dusk, and behind them the hen pecked unconcernedly at the boundary stone, spared by extravagance's own arithmetic, and the Whisperer settled back against the warm glass and fed, slowly, on the taste of a bargain struck: hope and dread braided together, the finest flavor the small ones made.

Five more villages that month. Seven the next. It rationed itself. It was scrupulous about prices, generous with refunds, patient with doubters—it loved doubters best of all, for a doubter converted was worth a dozen born believers, and doubt made the miracle shine brighter afterward. It built, prayer by prayer, offering by offering, exactly what it had built before the sealing and would build again: not an army, not a cult with robes and knives—the Rage-things had tried robes and knives, and see where robes and knives had gotten them—but something better. A congregation. A web of small debts and warmer hearts, strung from village to village across the whole spine of the Thornfells, with every thread leading down.

Down to the patient one. Down to the listener. Down to the god who answered.

And in the deep city, in the court of the dying queen, it could feel the key turning over in its sleep, dreaming now—oh, it could hear the dreams too, faint and flour-scented and stubborn—dreaming of shrines and hearths and a croft with a carved lintel. Coming closer. Coming down.

The Whisperer arranged itself against the dragon-glass, in the dark that was almost warm, and began, softly, to practice the voice it would use when the keeper finally arrived.

It wanted the voice to sound like mercy.

It had had a very long time to rehearse.
