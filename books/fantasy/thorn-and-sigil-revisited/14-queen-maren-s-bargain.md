## 14. Queen Maren's Bargain

The cough came at dawn, as it always did now, and Queen Maren of Thornfell woke with the taste of copper and wintergreen on her tongue the way other queens woke with the taste of honeyed wine.

She lay still beneath the furs and counted, as she did every morning, the breaths she could take before her lungs began to complain. Eleven. Yesterday it had been thirteen. The physician's tinctures — willowbark and marshmallow root, coltsfoot steeped in goat's milk, a dozen other gentle lies — bought her hours, not days. She had stopped asking him how many months. He had stopped pretending he could answer.

*Two years,* she thought, watching the grey light seep through the shutters of her private chamber. *Perhaps three if I am stubborn.* She had been stubborn her whole life. It had carried her from a minor house in the eastern valleys to the throne of a mountain kingdom, carried her through two harvest famines and one border war and the slow, grinding treachery of a court that had never quite forgiven her for being born low. Stubbornness would not carry her through this. Nothing would carry her through this. But stubbornness might carry the kingdom through it, and that was the only arithmetic that had ever mattered to her.

The coughing fit, when it came, bent her double against the headboard. She rode it out in silence, teeth clenched, because the walls of the royal bedchamber were old and the servants were many and a queen who coughed blood was a queen whose vultures began to circle before her heart had even slowed.

When it passed, she rang for her ladies, and by the time the sun cleared the eastern ridge she was dressed, powdered, and seated at her writing desk with a cup of ginger tea she had no intention of drinking. The tea was for appearances. Everything, now, was for appearances.

The summons she sent for Lord Corvane was wordless — a black ribbon laid on his breakfast tray, which he would understand to mean *come to me before you break your fast, and tell no one.* He arrived within the half hour, of course. Corvane was never more than a half hour from anything, which was precisely why she had kept him these nineteen years, and precisely why she would need to watch him more carefully in the years — the months — she had left.

He bowed at the door, exactly deep enough. Grey at the temples now, lean as a winter branch, with those pale eyes that never seemed to blink so much as *consider.* He carried a leather folio under one arm. He always carried a folio. She sometimes suspected he slept with one.

"Your Majesty."

"Sit, Corvane. And close the door. I mean to speak plainly, and I can only afford plainness in small rooms."

He sat. He did not open the folio. That was another thing about him — he never offered information until he was certain she wanted it, which meant that the folio was either empty or full of something he had been waiting weeks to show her. With Corvane, the waiting was the message.

"The cough is worse," he said. Not a question. He would have had it from the laundress who counted the bloodied linens, or the apothecary's boy who delivered the tinctures, or possibly from the birds on the windowsill, knowing Corvane.

"The cough is my own affair." She set down the untouched tea. "What is not my own affair is the matter I set before you at midwinter. The old shrines. The answered prayers in the Thornfell villages. I asked you to find out whether it was superstition, sedition, or something worse. You have had five months."

"I have had five months," Corvane agreed, "and I have something worse."

Maren felt the cold settle into her chest, beneath the cold that was already there. She had ruled long enough to know that when Corvane said *worse,* he meant it in the precise, taxonomical way he meant everything — worse than superstition, worse than sedition, worse than the things she was already lying awake cataloguing.

"Tell me."

He opened the folio at last, and laid out his papers one by one on the desk between them, arranging them like a man laying out a hand of cards he had already won with. A map of the Thornfell highlands, marked with small red pins. A page of census records, yellowed and brittle. A sketch — she would learn later it had been drawn from memory by a kitchen maid — of a woman's forearm bearing a birthmark like a coiled flame.

"Superstition first, to clear the ground." His finger touched the map. "Sweetened wells at Harrow's Ford. Fevers broken at Millbrook. A child pulled alive from the Whitewater after three days under the ice, at Crag's Rest, last month. The smallfolk are calling them miracles. They are leaving offerings at shrines that have been empty for three hundred years — bread, milk, blood, in that order of escalation. The shrines predate the kingdom, Your Majesty. They predate the crown of Thornfell itself. They belong to the gods we sealed away, back when my predecessors were still digging latrines."

"And you believe the gods are answering."

"I believe *something* is answering." Corvane folded his hands. "I have interviewed forty-one witnesses. I do not interview people to learn whether they are lying; I interview them to learn what they saw. What they saw, in every case, was impossible. Bread vanishing from a stone. Water turning sweet in a sealed cistern. A fever breaking in a pattern — and here is the detail that interests me, Majesty — a pattern. The fever left the miller's daughter on the same night her father carved an offering of his own blood into the shrine lintel. Coincidence is a pattern that happens once. This has happened eleven times."

Maren looked at the red pins. They were not scattered. They clustered, dense and dark, in the high valleys — and every cluster, she saw when she traced it with her eye, pointed inward, like the petals of some vast and patient flower, toward the mountain beneath which Thornfell keep itself was built.

"Toward the old shrine," she said. "The sealed one. The one my grandfather's grandfather bricked up and swore his heirs never to open."

"The Whisperer's shrine. Yes." Corvane turned the census page toward her. "Which brings us from superstition to the something worse. You asked me once, Majesty, why the old seals have held for three hundred years. The histories say dragon-fire. The histories are vague on the mechanism, because the men who wrote them were vague on purpose — a seal that everyone understands is a seal that someone will try to open. But the Underhill family records were less discreet. The Underhills were not dragon-tamers, as the songs have it. They were seal-keepers. Their blood was the key — or rather, their blood was the *lock.* The fire was poured into the sigil, and the sigil was carried in the flesh, generation to generation, and as long as an Underhill lived bearing the mark, the prison held."

Maren's eyes went to the sketch of the birthmark. A coiled flame, drawn in careful pencil. She had seen that shape before. She could not remember where, and the not-remembering itched at her like a splinter.

"The Underhills are extinct," she said. "The line died out — when, sixty years ago? Seventy?"

"The line is not extinct." Corvane's voice did not change at all, and that was how she knew she should be afraid. "The line is a taster. A poison-taster in your own kitchens, Majesty. Freya Underhill, nineteen years old, daughter of a smuggler and a washerwoman, granddaughter of the last seal-keeper of the Thornfell crofts. She bears the sigil on her left forearm. My people have confirmed it three times, from three separate vantages, at some cost to their discretion." A thin pause. "She swallows poison for your table every day. Wolfsbane, on Tuesdays."

The splinter found its place. The coiled flame — she had seen it on the girl's arm, bared when she rolled her sleeve to wash, or some such ordinary moment. Freya. The quiet one. The one who catalogued her own symptoms in a little book and never spoke unless spoken to and moved through the palace like smoke through a keyhole. Maren had seen her a dozen times and never once *looked* at her, and the shame of that — a queen who prided herself on seeing everything, missing the one thing that mattered, because the girl was poor and quiet and therefore invisible — rose in her throat like bile.

"Say the rest," Maren said.

"The rest is arithmetic." Corvane drew the map closer. "The seal is weakening. The gods are answering prayers because the lock is rusting, Majesty, and things that rust eventually break. When it breaks — fully breaks — what comes out will not be content with sweetened wells and vanishing bread. These were gods of war and hunger and the space between breaths. The old records call them the Unquiet. They were sealed by a united kingdom with a living sigil-bearer and a living dragon, and we have neither, and I have spent five months looking for a way to acquire both."

"A dragon."

"Emberwake sleeps beneath the Thornfell massif. It has slept for three hundred years. It is dying, or something very like dying, but it is not dead, and dead things cannot be woken but sleeping things can be *called.*" He said it the way he said everything, flat and certain, a man reading a ledger. "And the sigil-bearer is already in your household, already swallowing your poisons, already trained — without knowing it — in a lifetime of doing exactly what she is told."

Maren sat back. The chair creaked. Outside the shutters, a cart rattled through the lower courtyard, and somewhere a cook was shouting about eggs, and the whole enormous ordinary machinery of the palace went on grinding, ignorant of what its mistress was about to decide at her writing desk with her tea going cold.

She made herself think it through, because she was a queen and queens think things through; that was the whole difference between a queen and a tyrant, she had always told herself, though lately the line had begun to look very thin, like ice over deep water.

If the seal broke, the Unquiet came out. She had read the fragmentary accounts — the Burning Century, the years when the mountains ate villages and the lowlands starved and a third of the old bloodlines ended. Her kingdom was a mountain kingdom. Its roads were passes; its granaries were valleys; everything she had built, everything she had *been* — the crown that had taken a washerwoman's daughter and made her into a queen — sat directly above the door of that prison. She would be dead before the worst of it. She would die coughing in a warm bed while the world she had spent her life holding together came apart beneath her grandchildren's feet.

Unless the seal could be renewed. Unless the blood could be had.

And there it was. The thought arrived the way such thoughts always arrive — dressed in necessity, wearing the mask of duty, whispering *there is no other way* in a voice that sounds remarkably like your own conscience. She recognized it. She had heard it nineteen years ago when she signed the order that hanged the border reavers without trial, and eleven years ago when she burned the granaries to starve the rebel holdfasts, and every time, the mask had fit a little better, until now she could hardly tell whether she was wearing it or it was wearing her.

"You are proposing," she said slowly, "to harvest the girl's blood. To pour it into the seal. To keep the prison closed on the back of a nineteen-year-old taster who has never been thanked for anything in her life."

"I am proposing," Corvane said, "to *secure* her. What we do with her after that is a question of method, and methods are chosen by whoever holds her." He met her eyes, and for once he let her see all the way down into the pale cold of them. "But I will be plain, since you have asked for plainness. The seal can be renewed by her blood. It can also be *opened* by her blood. The same key turns both ways, Majesty. Whoever controls the sigil-bearer controls the door. And whoever controls the door controls the gods — not as a myth, not as a superstition, but as an instrument. A kingdom that can threaten to loose the Unquiet does not need to fight its wars. It only needs to be believed."

"There it is," Maren said quietly. "I wondered how long you'd take to arrive at the weapon."

"I arrive at every weapon eventually. It is my trade." He did not smile. "But I am not asking you to decide that today. I am asking you to decide one thing: the girl comes under our hand before the seal fails. Everything else follows."

Maren looked at the sketch again. The coiled flame. She thought of the girl herself — the steady hands, the little book of symptoms, the way she had once, a year ago, quietly refused a dish at the queen's own table and been right to, some bitterness in the sauce that would have laid Maren low for a week. She had rewarded that vigilance with nothing. No one rewarded it. The girl swallowed poison daily so that the crown could eat safely, and the crown did not even know her name.

*She has given you her body's suffering every day of her working life,* said a voice in Maren that sounded like her mother, dead forty years, a washerwoman who had taught her daughter to count every coin twice. *And now you mean to take the rest of her.*

*And if I do not,* said the other voice, the queen's voice, the one that had signed the orders and burned the granaries, *then I mean to let the mountains open and the old hunger walk out and eat everything she would have grown old inside of. There is no clean choice. There is only the choice that leaves something standing.*

Both voices were right. That was the trap of ruling: both voices were always right, and you had to choose anyway, and then live in the house the choice built.

"Corvane," she said. "Listen to me carefully, because I will say this once, and I will not say it twice, and if you ever pretend I said otherwise I will spend my last months destroying you. Do you understand?"

"Perfectly, Majesty."

"The girl is to be secured. Her blood is to be studied — *studied,* not spent. I will not have the seal opened. I will not have the gods turned into a weapon. I have read the Burning Century accounts, and I would rather my kingdom be small and free than vast and haunted." Her voice roughened; she swallowed against the cough and won, this time. "And she is to be treated *gently.* Gently, Corvane. No cages. No knives. No frightened girl dragged through the gates in chains, because a frightened girl with that blood is a lit fuse, and I have seen what frightened people with power do — I have *been* one. She is to be honored. Rewarded. Given rank and title and a reason to love the crown she has bled for all these years without thanks. If we must ask her for the greatest sacrifice of her line, we will ask her the way you ask family. Do you hear me?"

"I hear you."

"Say it back."

A pause, the length of a heartbeat, in which Corvane considered — she could see him considering, the way a man considers whether to pocket a coin left on a table — and then: "The girl is to be secured gently. Honored, rewarded, and asked, not taken. The seal is to be studied, never opened. The gods are never to be made a weapon." He inclined his head. "On your word, Majesty, so given."

"On my word. And Corvane —" she leaned forward, and let him see, for once, the thing under the powder and the crown, the dying animal that was still, after everything, a queen — "I am dying. We both know it. Which means that everything I order now is an order you will execute *after* I am gone, in a regency you will very likely control. I cannot hold your hand from beyond the grave. So I am holding it now, while I still have grip. Remember this room. Remember this tea I did not drink. Remember that I looked you in the eye and told you *gently,* and that you said it back to me. If the girl comes to harm — if she comes to harm in any way I would not have chosen — I will come back from whatever dark room they put me in to haunt you specifically. I have been a nuisance to better men than you for nineteen years. Do not test how far my reach extends."

For a moment — a single, unguarded moment — something moved behind Corvane's pale eyes that might have been respect, or might have been the recalculation of a careful man revising an estimate. Then it was gone, and he was bowing, and the folio was closed, and he was at the door.

"Your Majesty will be obeyed," he said, "in every particular."

He left. The door clicked shut. Maren sat alone with the cold tea and the red-pinned map and the sketch of the coiled flame, and she thought about the girl — Freya, the taster, the invisible one — swallowing her Tuesday wolfsbane somewhere below, in a kitchen that smelled of onions, keeping the queen alive with her own small suffering, never knowing that the queen had just decided the shape of the rest of her life at a writing desk before breakfast.

*I am sorry,* Maren thought, and was surprised to find that she meant it, and more surprised to find that meaning it changed nothing at all. *I am sorry, and I will do it anyway, and that is what it is to wear a crown. You become the kind of person who is sorry.*

The cough took her then, hard, and she bent over the desk and gripped its edge and let it have her, and when it was done there were flecks of red on the map of the Thornfell highlands, scattered across the mountains like the beginning of a fire.

She wiped them away with her sleeve, and rang the bell for fresh ink, and began the first of the letters.

* * *

Three days later, in a kitchen that smelled of onions, a taster named Freya Underhill rolled up her sleeve to wash, and a kitchen maid with quick eyes and a quicker memory counted the coins Corvane had paid her, and did not notice the girl in the doorway behind her, still as smoke, who had heard every word.

Freya Underhill stood in the shadow of the flour sacks for a long moment, her hand pressed flat against the coiled flame on her arm, which had begun — very softly, like a banked coal remembering fire — to burn.

Then she went to find her coat, and her boy, and the smuggler's road home.
