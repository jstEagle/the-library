## 15. Run, Taster

The coat was where she'd left it, folded in the bottom of her chest beneath a false lining of moth-eaten wool: a smuggler's coat, oiled canvas gone soft with years of rain and road-dust, cut for someone broader than Freya so that it hung on her like a shadow wearing a woman's shape. She had not touched it in four years. It still smelled of pine tar and the cold stone of the passes, and when she pulled it against her chest her body remembered before her mind did — the weight of it across her shoulders, the way the hem caught at her calves on steep scree, the particular silence a person learned to keep in high places where sound carried like a thrown knife.

She laid it out on the bed. Then she packed.

Not quickly — that was the discipline of it. Panic made people pack wrong. Panic made them take things they loved instead of things they needed, and love was heavier than iron over a mountain pass. Freya had watched men flee before; she had once hidden in a root cellar for two days while Corvane's riders turned over a village looking for a debt-runner, and she had listened to what flight sounded like from underneath: boots, shouting, a cart too heavily loaded to turn, and afterward a long quiet full of weeping. The man had run with everything he owned. That was why they'd caught him.

So she took almost nothing. Her herb-kit went first — the little tin boxes nested in oilcloth, each one labeled in her own cramped hand, *wolfsbane, bitterleaf, queen's mercy, ash-root*, the poisons and their antidotes sleeping side by side like old enemies forced to share a bed. Then the shard of dragon-glass wrapped in three layers of linen, warm as a sleeping bird against her palm even through the cloth. A flint and steel. A knife with a good spine. Bread, hard cheese, dried apples, a twist of salt. The letters from her grandmother's croft, because some weights you carried whether or not they were sensible. And at the very bottom, wrapped in a scrap of silk that had once held a court lady's perfume, a small clay vial of her own blood, drawn that morning with a lancet while the kitchen maid's back was turned.

It was the blood that frightened her most. Not carrying it — carrying it meant she controlled it, kept it out of other hands. What frightened her was how much of it there had been lately, how often she'd needed to see it, dark and ordinary in the lamplight, just to remind herself it was still only blood. That it did not make her something else. That the sigil coiled on her arm was a birthmark and nothing more, no matter what Corvane believed, no matter what the burned thing beneath Thornfell keep whispered into the dreams of desperate people.

*Run,* said a voice in her head that sounded like her grandmother. *And while you run, think.*

She blew out her candle and sat down in the dark to do exactly that, because running blind was just falling with extra steps.

Corvane knew about the sigil. That was certain now; the kitchen maid's confession had been plain enough — he'd paid for word of any mark or burning on the taster's skin, any oddness in her habits, any visitor. He had tested her with the poisoned dish at the autumn feast and watched her arm flame through her sleeve, and he had smiled at her afterward with the particular gentleness he reserved for things he intended to own. He would not come for her tonight. Men like Corvane did not snatch; they harvested, slowly, arranging conditions so that the taking looked inevitable, even welcome. Which meant she had perhaps two days. Three if the queen's coughing kept him busy at her bedside.

Which meant Wick.

Her hands went still over the packed bag.

She could leave him. That was the sane arithmetic: one adult moving light and fast through country she knew better than anyone alive, versus an adult and a half-starved eleven-year-old boy who talked too much when he was nervous and froze when he was frightened. Every survival instinct she possessed said *leave him*, said he was safer as an anonymous orphan in a castle full of ten thousand people than he would ever be on a mountain road beside a fugitive.

Every survival instinct she possessed also lied to her regularly, and she had learned to catch it at it.

Because Corvane knew about the sigil, and Corvane was thorough, and the boy who ground her herbs and fetched her water and slept in the alcove off her workroom was not anonymous to a man who had been reading her life for months the way she read a dish. If Corvane came for her, he came for everyone who belonged to her. And Wick *did* belong to her. She had chosen that, in the winter, when she'd fed him and taught him foxglove from comfrey and let him call her by her name instead of *taster*. She had built him into her life the way you set a keel into a hull, and you could not unbuild a keel without sinking the ship.

"Damn," Freya said quietly to the dark room, and went to get her boy.

* * *

He was awake. Of course he was awake. He was sitting cross-legged on his pallet in the alcove with his blanket around his shoulders like a small furious king, and he had clearly heard her open the outer door, because the lamp was lit and there were two bundles already on the floor beside him — his own, packed with the lopsided competence of a child who has fled before.

"I heard the maid," he said, before she could speak. "Marta. She's been crying all evening in the scullery. Crying and counting money." His chin came up, and there was something in his face that hurt her to look at, because it wasn't fear. It was readiness. "You're leaving. Take me or I follow you. I know the drain under the tannery."

"The drain under the—" Freya closed her eyes briefly. "How do you know the drain under the tannery?"

"Because I lived in it for six weeks before you found me, and nobody ever looks down." He stood up, blanket sliding off, and she saw that he'd dressed properly, boots laced, knife at his belt where she'd taught him to keep it. "I'm not asking. I'm telling you how it's going to be."

For a moment — one long, suspended moment — Freya considered lying to him. Telling him to stay, promising to send for him, watching his face do the thing children's faces did when adults handed them pretty lies instead of truth. And then she thought of the root cellar, and the weeping after, and every child in every village whose parents had told them *wait here* and never come back, and she heard herself say:

"All right. But you do exactly as I say, when I say it, without arguing and without questions. On the mountain, my word is law. Not because I'm cruel. Because the mountain is."

Wick nodded so fast his hair flopped. Then, more quietly, with the sudden gravity children put on like borrowed cloaks: "Where are we going?"

"Home," Freya said, and was surprised to find she meant it. "My home. The high valleys." She shouldered her pack and snuffed his lamp between finger and thumb. "Now hush, and walk like smoke. We're going down the servants' stair, out the gatehouse wicket, and we're going to be two nobodies walking to the night-market for a jug of wine, and we're going to be so boring that God himself looks elsewhere."

The castle at midnight was a different animal than the castle by day — softer, slower, all its sharp ceremonial bones wrapped in fat darkness. They went down the back stairs single file, Wick's hand hooked in her belt as she'd taught him, past the great kitchens where banked fires breathed like sleeping beasts, past the buttery where a pair of grooms argued in whispers over dice. At the gatehouse wicket the porter was asleep in his chair with a jug tipped against his thigh, exactly as he was every night, because Freya had tasted his wine for three years and knew precisely which vintage put him under and precisely when. She had planned this without knowing she was planning it, the way she seemed to plan everything lately — all those years of invisibility turning out to have been reconnaissance.

The wicket door opened on greased hinges with a whisper. Cold air poured in, smelling of frost and horse and river. And then they were outside, and the castle was behind them, and Freya Underhill was walking away from the only roof she'd had for four years with a stolen future in a silk wrap and a boy's warm hand in hers, and she felt —

— lighter. That was the shameful part. That was the part she'd examine later, guiltily, by firelight in whatever hollow took them in: that beneath the fear ran a current of something dangerously close to joy. Four years she had swallowed poison for that place. Four years of being furniture, of being thanked the way one thanks a lock or a well-rope, useful and invisible. And here she was on the far side of its walls with the whole black-and-silver night open in front of her, and her legs wanted to run.

She didn't run. Running drew eyes. But she walked fast, and Wick matched her, and the city of Thornfell slid past them lane by lane like a story being told backward.

* * *

They left the city by the fish-gate, where the tide-smell covered all other smells and the watch was thin because no smuggler worth the name used a gate with a customs officer within sight of it. From there the true road began: not a road at all, but a sequence of gaps and gullies and forgotten drovers' paths that Freya's grandmother had called *the veins*, because they threaded through the mountains the way blood threaded through flesh, mostly hidden, all connected, keeping everything alive.

Freya had learned the veins at nine years old, running errands for her grandmother's trade, and she had never entirely stopped learning them. Smugglers' roads changed with the seasons — a rockfall opened one year closed another, a bribed shepherd moved his fold and took his path's safety with him — but the bones stayed constant, and Freya carried the bones in her head the way other people carried prayers.

They walked the first hour along the leat above the tanneries, flat and easy, letting Wick find his rhythm. Then the land began to lift, and she took them up through the bracken-cutting where the path was really a deer-trail really a wish, switchbacking through gorse that raked at their coats, until the city lights fell away below and the mountains rose around them like patient gray animals.

"Tell me where we're going," Wick panted, somewhere in the third hour. "Really."

"The Hallowmere valley, first stop. There's a widow there, Sorrel, who owes my family older debts than she can count. We sleep in her barn, eat her bread, and are gone before her rooster finishes insulting the dawn." Freya picked her way over a shelf of wet rock, testing each hold before trusting it. "Then up the Long Vein to the high passes. Then north."

"North is where the villages are. The ones that pray."

Freya didn't answer immediately. Below them the lowlands lay in their basin of mist, and somewhere in that mist was a castle where a dying queen waited for a cure and a spymaster waited for a bloodline, and both of them thought Freya Underhill was still inside their walls, still theirs, still furniture.

"Yes," she said at last. "North is where the praying is. And north is where the answers live — mine and theirs." She glanced back at him, a pale smudge in the starlight. "There are people up there who will shelter us because of what I am, and there are people up there who will hunt us because of what I am, and I need you to understand that I'm not leading us to safety. I'm leading us to the fight, because the fight is coming regardless, and I'd rather choose our ground."

Wick was silent for a few steps. Then, in the matter-of-fact tone that always undid her a little: "Good. I was worried you were going to do something stupid, like hide."

Despite everything — despite the burn in her legs and the coal-warmth of the shard against her back and the memory of Marta's coins — Freya laughed. It came out ragged, surprised out of her, and echoed off the cutting wall and came back sounding like someone else's laughter, someone freer.

"Sleep-walk if you must," she said, "but don't fall asleep standing. Next ridge there's a shepherd's bothy, dry, no fire. We rest till the moon sets."

* * *

They made the bothy as the moon went down behind the western peaks, a low stone beehive half-swallowed by the hillside, smelling of old peat and older sheep. Freya checked it out of habit — corners, rafters, the cold hearth — and then let herself slide down the wall onto the straw, pulling Wick in against her side. He was asleep almost before he stopped moving, boneless with exhaustion, one hand still curled around his knife.

She sat awake a while longer, because someone had to stand watch even sitting down.

Through the bothy's low doorway the world arranged itself in bands of silver and black: the near slope, the treeline, the vast shoulders of the range going up and up into stars. Somewhere under all that stone, she knew, the last dragon dreamed its failing dreams, and the god it had been set to guard pressed its hunger against the seal like a tongue against a tooth. Somewhere south of here a queen coughed blood into silk, and a man with gentle hands sharpened instruments for harvesting her. And somewhere ahead, in the high valleys, people who had never met her were lighting candles at shrines that hadn't held candles in three hundred years, praying to anything that would listen.

*They'll know your name,* she thought. *Sorrel's neighbors, the hearthfolk, all of them. Sigil-bearer. You spent four years learning to be no one, and now you're going to be the most somebody in these mountains.*

And the strangest thing, the thing she turned over and over like a stone found in a streambed, was that this did not feel like loss. Losing the court was losing nothing — she had already lost herself there years ago, one swallow at a time. No: what tightened her throat was smaller and worse. It was Marta's face, tear-streaked and coin-clutching. It was the head cook who'd slipped her extra honey-cakes every solstice and never asked why her arm sometimes glowed. It was the whole soft web of small kindnesses that had made the castle bearable, which she was now severing with every step north, and which some of those kind people might pay for.

Flight, it turned out, was both doors at once — one opening, one closing, and no way to stand in the threshold forever.

Freya shifted the sleeping boy gently against her shoulder, drew the coat over them both, and let her eyes close. The shard against her spine gave one slow pulse of warmth, like a heart settling into sleep, or waking.

Two ridges east, unseen in the pre-dawn dark, a rider on the low road turned his horse toward the mountains, and did not look back at the city either.
