## 16. The Hearthfolk

The village of Hollowmere did not exist on any map Freya had ever tasted.

She had known that before she arrived, because she had helped make it so. Three years ago, a customs officer in Lowbridge had been paid to strike the name from his rolls, and a surveyor had been paid to draw the road bending east of it, and a tithe-collector had simply never been sent. The crown's blindness was not an accident. It was a purchase. And now that same blindness folded around her like a second cloak as she and Wick came down out of the pines at dusk, following a path that smelled of woodsmoke and wet barley and the particular green rot of a millpond.

Wick stopped at the tree line, his breath ragged from three days of hard walking, and stared.

"There's nothing there," he said. Then he squinted. "No—there is. There's *definitely* something there."

"That's how it works." Freya shifted her pack higher on her shoulder. The shard from the shrine rode against her spine inside it, wrapped in wool, and even through the layers she could feel it now—a faint warmth, like a stone left too near a fire, like something patient waiting to be noticed. She tried not to notice it. Noticing seemed to encourage it. "You can't find Hollowmere unless someone from Hollowmere wants you found. Old smuggler trick. Older than smuggling, probably."

"Then who wanted us found?"

Freya didn't answer, because the answer was standing up out of the barley.

She was an old woman, broad as a door, with a scythe over one shoulder and no fear anywhere on her face. She looked at Freya the way Freya imagined a person might look at weather they'd been praying for—and then, just for a heartbeat, looked lower, at Freya's left wrist where the birthmark coiled beneath the sleeve, as if she could see straight through wool and skin to the sigil underneath.

"You'll be the taster," the old woman said. Not a question. "And this will be the boy. Tam said you'd come by the north path, and Tam's never wrong about paths."

"Tam?"

"The miller." The old woman swung the scythe onto her back and started down through the barley without checking whether they followed, which was its own kind of answer. "Name's Maren—no relation to the other one, thank all the quiet gods. Come on, then. There's stew, and there's questions, and you'll get them in whichever order your stomach prefers."

Wick fell into step beside Freya, and under his breath, in the careful whisper he'd learned in her kitchen at court—the whisper of a boy who understood that words could be poisoned too—he said, "She called you by name before you gave it."

"I know."

"And she knew about—" He touched his own wrist, mirroring hers.

"I know, Wick."

Above the trees, the first stars were coming out, indifferent and cold. Somewhere behind them, days south now, Corvane would have discovered the empty rooms, the servant with the sympathetic eyes already in irons or worse, the long machinery of the crown grinding into motion. Freya had spent her whole life learning which roads were watched. She was beginning to understand that she had fled along exactly the road that everyone in these mountains was watching—for her.

*Sigil-bearer*, Tam the miller called her that night, standing up in the smoky common room of the only inn Hollowmere had, raising a cup of small beer. *The Underhill comes home.* And forty faces turned toward her, and every one of them held something Freya had spent thirty years making sure no face ever held when it looked at her: hope.

She had escaped a cage made of suspicion only to walk into one made of faith. She wasn't yet sure which weighed more.

* * *

They gave her the room over the mill, because Tam said the mill was the oldest building in the valley and the walls remembered things. Freya suspected the walls mostly remembered damp. But the bed was clean, the window latched, and Wick slept in the loft below like a coal dropped in water—all his noise going out at once, gone deep, safe. She lay listening to him breathe and to the wheel turning in the dark below, and she took out the shard and set it on the sill, because hiding from it had begun to feel like lying.

In the moonlight it was beautiful. That was the worst of it. Dragon-glass, the grandmother's letters had called it—or rather, the letters she'd memorized in the croft had called it that once, in a hand gone shaky with age: *the glass remembers what we asked of it*. It was black shot through with veins of deep red, and when she tilted it, the red moved, slow as blood through honey. It had been warm since the burned village. It had been warm since her rage. She still did not know what she had done beside those graves, only that the shard had answered something in her, and that it had been answering her ever since, faintly, the way a dog answers footsteps on a road—hoping, always hoping, that this time it was the one it waited for.

"Emberwake," she said softly, testing the name the dragon-shard had pressed into her dreams. Beneath Thornfell, something vast and dying stirred in its sleep, dreaming of the first Underhill. Dreaming of her bloodline. Dreaming, maybe, of her.

The shard pulsed once, gently, like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

She wrapped it away again and did not sleep much.

* * *

The miracles began on the second morning.

Not real ones. That was the thing Freya kept telling herself, standing in the mill yard while a queue formed in front of her as naturally as water finding a channel—*not real ones*, because what the villagers wanted wasn't sorcery at all. What they wanted was attention. They wanted their suffering carried to someone whose suffering the world had finally decided to look at.

An old man named Brackish came first, hat in hands, asking her to bless his well, which had gone bitter since midsummer. Freya went and looked at it, because refusing outright felt like cruelty, and found what she'd expected: a dead ewe upstream, bloated and foul, poisoning the springhead. She and Wick dragged it out, buried it, and scrubbed the well chain with vinegar, and by evening the water ran sweet. The whole village drank to her. Brackish wept into his cup and told everyone who'd listen that the sigil-bearer had cleansed the well with a word.

"It was a sheep," Freya said to Maren, flatly, over the noise.

"Aye," Maren said comfortably. "It usually is. Doesn't stop folk needing it to be more."

A mother brought a child with fever blisters and asked her to lay hands. Freya laid hands—on the willow bark tea she brewed, on the cool cloth, on the little hot forehead at intervals through the night—and by morning the fever broke, as fevers sometimes do when someone stays up all night tending. The mother kissed Freya's wrists, both of them, right over the mark, and Freya had to stand very still and let her do it, because pulling away would have hurt worse than being worshipped.

By the fourth day the queue included people from two valleys over. A man asked her to bless his seed grain. A girl asked her to curse the boys who'd broken her sister's heart. A widow asked—not timidly, but with the terrible directness of grief—whether the sigil-bearer could speak to the dead, because her husband had gone down the mine at Kettledeep and never come up, and there had been no body, and she needed to know if he was at peace.

That one Freya could not turn into a sheep.

"I can't," she said, and the words came out rougher than she meant them to, scraped raw on the way. "I'm sorry. I'm not—I don't do what they say I do. I taste food. I know herbs. I know roads. I'm not a priestess and I'm not a miracle and the mark on my arm is just a mark."

The widow looked at her for a long moment. Then she nodded slowly, and something in her face settled rather than broke, and she said, "You stayed up all night with Nella's boy. You dragged a dead sheep out of a well with your own hands. You're doing more than most gods manage." And she pressed a twist of dried lavender into Freya's palm and walked away, leaving Freya holding the little bundle like it was heavier than iron.

Maren appeared at her elbow with two cups of cider, as she seemed to whenever Freya was drowning. "Word of advice, taster."

"I suspect I need several."

"They'll ask you for miracles every day you're here. You can't give 'em, and they'll keep asking anyway, because asking is half the cure." Maren drank. "But here's the trap, see. One day someone will ask you for something you *can* do—something ordinary, something you'd do anyway—and if you refuse it because you're sick of being asked for wonders, that's the day you break their hearts. Don't refuse the ordinary things. Ordinary things are all any of us actually want done."

Freya looked out across the yard—at Brackish hauling sweet water, at Nella's boy chasing chickens with the reckless vigor of the recently fevered, at Wick up on the mill roof helping re-thatch, laughing at something, actually laughing, for the first time since they'd run. Her chest did something complicated and painful.

"When Corvane finds this place," she said quietly, "it'll be because of me. Because they shouted my name from the rooftops and lit beacons with their gratitude. Every day I stay, I'm painting a target on every hearth in this valley."

"Mm." Maren considered this with the unhurried air of a woman who had outlived three husbands and two famines. "And when you leave tomorrow—which you should, mind, and we both know it—what happens to the well? To the fevered? To the widow?" She handed Freya the second cup. "Belonging isn't a burden because it asks too much, girl. It's a burden because it doesn't care whether you feel ready. Drink your cider."

* * *

On the sixth night, they asked her about the seal.

It happened after supper, in Tam's kitchen, with the good candles lit—the tall ones, saved for matters that mattered. Maren was there, and Tam, and the hedgewitch Sorrel, who was ninety if she was a day and had the eyes of a kestrel, and a lean grey man called Aldous who spoke rarely and was, Freya had gradually worked out, the one who kept Hollowmere off the maps. Wick had fallen asleep sitting up, the way boys do, and no one had moved him, and his head was heavy and warm against Freya's side.

"The shrine under Thornfell keep," Sorrel said, without preamble. "Your family's shrine. Is it true you've seen it."

Freya's cup stopped halfway to her mouth. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Because Aldous's grandfather helped pour the last course of the door-stone," Sorrel said, "and my grandmother sang the closing songs, and neither of us has forgotten, whatever the lowlands have." Her kestrel eyes fixed on Freya's wrist. "We are the hearthfolk, taster. The Underhills kept the flame; we kept the *hearth*—the villages, the roads, the memory, so that when an Underhill was needed again, there would be somewhere for them to stand. Three hundred years we've swept the doorstep. We'd begun to think no one was coming."

"The seal is failing," Aldous said. His voice was dry as old paper. "The god beneath it has begun answering prayers. Small ones. Cheap ones. That's how they start—it feeds the desperate until the desperate are strong enough to feed it. Your flight from court, the queen's sickness, the soldiers moving in the passes—all of it stirs the pot. And you've been below. You've stood in front of the glass. Tell us what you saw."

So Freya told them. The fused wall of dragon-glass, vein upon vein of it, older than masonry. The names cut into the seal—Underhill after Underhill after Underhill, a ledger written in a family's bones. The way the dark behind the glass had seemed to lean toward her, attentive, *hungry*, like a guest smelling dinner. She told them about the Whisperer's voice in her head, silk over gravel: *you carry the key, daughter of the key-makers.*

When she finished, the candle flames had guttered, though there was no draft, and no one remarked on it, and everyone had moved a little closer together without appearing to decide to.

"What does the crown want with her blood?" Tam asked the table generally.

"To reopen it," Aldous said. "To own what comes out. A dying queen, a hungry spymaster, and a war they mean to win with stolen gods." He turned to Freya. "And what do *you* want?"

It was the question she'd been avoiding since the croft. She looked down at Wick's sleeping head, at the shard's faint warmth through her pack where it leaned against the chair leg, at the candle flames steadying themselves as though listening.

"My grandmother's letters said the sigil closes as well as opens," Freya said. "Seal-keepers, not sacrifices. If that's true—if the mark isn't just a key but a *hand* that can hold the door shut—then what I want is to close it. Properly. Before Corvane gets here, before the god gets stronger, before anyone else's village burns." Her voice caught on the last part; the burned village was still there behind her eyelids, ash and small shoes. "But the letters also said it would cost. Grandmother never wrote what."

Sorrel and Aldous exchanged a look so long and so old that Freya felt suddenly like a child at a grown-ups' table.

"No," Sorrel agreed softly. "She wouldn't have. Underhills always learned it the hard way instead." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took a candle, and nodded toward the door. "Come and see the hearthstone, then, sigil-bearer. All of you. It's time you knew what you inherited besides the mark."

* * *

The hearthstone of Hollowmere sat in the oldest house in the village, a low turf-roofed place at the end of the lane that everyone called the First Hearth and nobody lived in. Inside, the fire had been kept burning continuously—so Maren claimed, and Aldous confirmed with the flat certainty of a man reading from a ledger—for three hundred years. Generation after generation of hearthfolk had fed it, a chain of hands passing flame like a relay baton through plague and famine and war.

And set into the stones of the great chimney, above the fire, worn nearly smooth by heat and smoke and centuries of reaching hands, was a carving.

Freya knew it before her eyes finished seeing it. She knew it in her wrist, in her pulse, in the shard in her pack going abruptly, urgently warm.

The sigil. Her birthmark. Her grandmother's hearth-mark. A coiled shape like a dragon seen from above, wings folded, tail curled round a small enclosed space—an egg, or a door, or a prison; from this angle she couldn't tell which, and suspected the carver hadn't either. Around it, ring upon fading ring, were names. Hundreds of names. Underhills, yes—but others too. Vances. Merrowes. A name carved in a script so old it predated the kingdom itself.

"Not sacrifices," Aldous said quietly behind her. "Keepers. Every one. And every hearth in every hidden village in these mountains holds a copy of that stone, and every keeper of every hearth swore the same oath your grandmother's grandmother swore." He paused. "Say the words, Sorrel. Let her hear them properly."

Sorrel's voice, cracked and thin, filled the little room:

*"While the hearth burns, we remember. While we remember, the door holds. And if the door must be held by one alone, then let that one hold it knowing she was never alone—only last."*

The fire popped. Wick, awake now without anyone noticing when, had found Freya's hand and was gripping it hard enough to hurt, and she gripped back, and neither of them said anything, because there was nothing in either of their vocabularies for this.

*Last*, Freya thought. The word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Only last.* All those generations keeping fires lit for a duty that might never come due, and the duty had come due to her, the one who'd never been told, the one who'd spent her life swallowing poison for a crown that meant to bleed her dry. Belonging, Maren had said, doesn't care whether you feel ready.

She reached up and laid her marked wrist against the carved sigil, and the stone was warm—not fire-warm, not stone-warm, but warm the way the shard was warm, warm the way a living thing is warm—and deep beneath the mountain, miles down in the dark, something immense exhaled in its sleep.

Far off, down the valley, a dog began to bark. Then another. Then the watch-bell in the mill tower, ringing fast and arrhythmic, the way bells ring when the ringer has run to it rather than walked.

Tam was first out the door. From the rise above the barley fields, they all saw it together: a light in the southern pass. Not a beacon-light. Not a traveler's lantern.

Torches. Many torches, moving in formation, disciplined as a closed fist, coming down the smuggler's road that only someone who knew the mountains as well as Freya did could have found.

"They shouldn't be able to follow that road," Freya whispered. Her mouth had gone dry as ash. "Nobody follows that road. It doesn't exist on any map."

Beside her, Aldous said, very quietly, "Maps burn, taster. Loyalties don't. Someone sold the road—and I'd wager everything in Hollowmere they sold it with a description of you attached."

Behind them, in the First Hearth, the three-hundred-year-old flame bent sideways, all at once, as if something enormous had drawn breath.
