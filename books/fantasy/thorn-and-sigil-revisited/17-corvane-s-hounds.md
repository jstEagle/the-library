## 17. Corvane's Hounds

The map was older than the war it was about to start, and Lord Corvane liked it that way.

He had spread it across the long table in his tent at dawn, weighting the corners with candlesticks and a dagger and a dried orange no one had ever been permitted to eat. Thornfell's mountains crawled across the vellum in brown ink, ridges like knuckles, valleys like veins. Someone—some dead cartographer with steady hands—had drawn tiny pines along the high passes, and Corvane had spent three nights studying them until he could close his eyes and see the whole range as if he'd been born on it.

He had not, of course, been born on it. He had been born in a lowland market town with mud streets and a father who sold rope, which was why he understood rope: how it held, where it frayed, what weight snapped it. Mountains were only another kind of knot. You did not storm a knot. You found its end and pulled.

"Ser Aldric." He did not look up from the map. "Report."

Aldric came to the table smelling of horse sweat and last night's wine, which Corvane noted and forgave, because Aldric was useful in the way a hammer is useful—you didn't ask a hammer to be subtle. The man was broad, gray at the temples, with a face like a door that had been kicked too many times. Behind him hovered Lieutenant Vess, who was everything Aldric was not: narrow, quiet, patient as a fisherman. Corvane collected people the way other men collected knives. A blade for hacking, a blade for carving.

"Harrowmere first," Aldric said. "The tinker confirmed it. She came through ten days ago with the boy. Bought bread. Paid in court silver, which nobody in Harrowmere has seen since the queen's tax collectors passed through in spring."

"Court silver," Corvane repeated softly. It pleased him, that detail. Freya Underhill had spent her whole life being invisible, invisible so thoroughly that she had forgotten invisibility was a discipline and not a nature. Even now, running, she moved like someone who believed she could not be seen—and so she left coins behind her like breadcrumbs, bright little confessions scattered down every road.

"She'll have gone up the shepherd trails," Vess said. Her voice was dry, unhurried. She unrolled a second map over the first—a hand-drawn thing, ink still sharp, marked with small crosses. "The smuggler roads. My people have been mapping them for a year, my lord. Every goat track the excise men never bothered with. There are four ways out of Harrowmere valley that don't touch the king's highway, and all four converge—" her finger moved, "—here. Millbrook. A mill, a shrine, forty households. Water, grain, and an old priest who stopped reporting to the diocese years ago."

Corvane studied the convergence point the way he'd once studied a locked strongbox: not for what it held, but for where it would break.

"You're certain she's there?"

"No," Vess said. "I'm certain it's where I would go."

That was the difference between them, and it was why he kept them both. Aldric chased certainty and found smoke. Vess chased likelihood and usually found flesh. And when neither of them found anything, Corvane went looking himself, because there were questions in this business that subordinates could not be trusted to answer honestly—the question most of all: *is this person telling me what is true, or telling me what they believe I want?*

"And if she isn't there?"

Vess's eyes flicked up, just briefly. She understood him well enough by now to know the question wasn't really a question.

"Then Millbrook will tell us something anyway," she said.

"Yes," Corvane said. "It will."

* * *

They rode up through the valley in the gray hour before true morning, thirty riders in dun cloaks with no colors and no crest, because a fire needed no banner to spread. Corvane rode at the front on a plain gelding—he had long ago learned that command was more convincing when it arrived without ornament—and he watched Millbrook assemble itself below them out of mist and chimney smoke, a handful of rooftops cupped in the valley's palm like water in a hand.

He felt nothing in particular about it. That was the thing his enemies never understood, when they imagined him pacing some tower in the capital, wringing his hands over the cost of necessary cruelty. There was no wringing of hands. There was arithmetic.

Queen Maren was dying. That was the fact beneath every other fact, the load-bearing wall of the entire age. A dying queen meant a contested succession, and a contested succession meant that whoever controlled the old gods' prison controlled the only weapon in the world that could not be counterfeited or bought or argued with. Corvane had served the crown for twenty-six years, through two wars and one plague, and he had learned the single lesson that mattered: crowns did not survive on loyalty. Crowns survived on leverage. Everything else was embroidery.

And leverage was sleeping under Thornfell keep, sealed behind dragon-glass, waiting for one drop of Underhill blood.

Which made Freya Underhill the most valuable woman in the kingdom, and also the most dangerous loose thread in it. Not dangerous because of what she wanted—by every report she wanted nothing but her poisons and her privacy—but dangerous because of what she *was*. A sigil-bearer loose among the mountain folk, with smuggler roads under her feet and a boy at her side and no idea of her own worth. Unclaimed assets invited claimants. If Corvane did not harvest her, someone else would find her eventually—the rebels, the god itself, some rival spymaster he hadn't yet had the pleasure of ruining. The mathematics admitted only one safe outcome.

She had to come to him. Willingly, if possible. Brokenly, if necessary.

And a person ran toward shelter. So you burned the shelters, one by one, until there was only one door left open, and you stood in it smiling.

"The priest," Corvane said quietly, to Vess. "When we take the village, bring him to me alive and sober. I want names. Who fed her, who saw her, who spoke to her. And I want to know whether anyone here has been praying lately."

Vess's mouth curved very slightly. "To the new god, my lord?"

"To any god," Corvane said. "Desperation is a door. I like to know which doors are standing open."

* * *

Millbrook woke to hoofbeats and did not resist, which Corvane noted with professional approval. Resistance would have wasted an hour and gotten useful villagers killed before they'd told him anything. Instead they stood in their doorways in nightshirts and shawls, forty-odd faces pale in the early light, watching the riders fan out through their lanes with the particular stillness of prey animals deciding whether to bolt.

Aldric took the mill and the granary. Two sergeants took the shrine on its knoll above the village—that squat, moss-eating little building Corvane had already marked on Vess's map, one of the forgotten ones, older than the diocese, older maybe than the kingdom. And Corvane sat his horse in the square, hands folded on the saddlebow, and waited for the village to come to him.

It always did. That was the secret no one taught in the officers' schools: you never dragged information out of peasants. You arranged circumstances in which giving you the information felt like the safest available act, and then you let them hand it to you, trembling and grateful, and hated themselves after. Fear opened mouths faster than pain ever had, and it left fewer marks to investigate later.

The priest came within the quarter hour. Father Anselm, according to the miller's wife, who had volunteered the name before anyone asked her—already currying favor, already learning the shape of the new weather. He was old, straight-backed, with a scholar's ink stains still ghosting his fingers, and he looked at Corvane the way few men dared: levelly, with something like recognition.

"Lord Corvane," the priest said. "I know your name. The queen's shadow."

"The queen's servant," Corvane corrected mildly. "Shadows are only servants who work late."

"What does the crown want in Millbrook? We pay our taxes late, but we pay them."

"A fugitive. A woman, dark-haired, thirty or so, traveling with a boy of perhaps twelve. She would have paid with silver. She may have asked odd questions—or answered none." Corvane dismounted slowly, letting his boots find the mud, bringing himself down to the priest's own ground. Small mercies, small equalizations. He had learned long ago that men talked more freely to a lord at eye level than to one looming above them; the loom was for later, if later was needed. "You will understand, Father, that she is not a criminal in any ordinary sense. She has stolen something that belongs to the crown. Something precious."

"She stole bread," the priest said. "And a sack of oats, and a jar of honey, and I sold her the lot myself and charged her fairly, and I'd do it again." His chin lifted a fraction. "She was hungry, and polite, and the boy hadn't eaten in days. Whatever she stole from your queen, Lord Corvane, it wasn't from my larder."

"There it is," Corvane said warmly. "Honesty. Do you know how rare that is, Father Anselm? Most men lie to me before they've finished greeting me. You've confessed outright. That takes either courage or a clear conscience, and I find I can't yet tell which." He turned, surveying the square, the shuttered windows, the faces retreating behind them. "So. She stayed here. How long?"

"One night."

"Where did she sleep?"

"I don't know. She wouldn't say. She left before dawn."

"But someone walked her out. Someone showed her which trail." Corvane let the silence stretch, pleasant, unhurried, a host allowing his guest time to appreciate the room. "Someone always does. Villages are kind that way, especially villages with shrines. Especially villages whose wells have recently turned sweet, or whose fevers have recently broken. Miracles make people generous, Father. Generosity makes them talkative. Talkative makes them useful."

Something flickered in the old priest's eyes—not guilt exactly, but the neighborhood of it. Corvane catalogued it and pressed, gently.

"Wells," he said. "Fevers. Tell me about the prayers in Millbrook."

* * *

By noon he had the shape of it, and the shape was worse than he had hoped and better than he had feared.

The girl had come and gone in a night, yes—but the shrine above the village had been busy for a month before she ever arrived. Bread left on the altar vanishing by morning. A fevered child given water from the old spring and walking by evening. A widow's cow, barren three years, freshened in the dark of the moon. And always, always the same whisper attached to these small resurrections, passed mouth to mouth in the way that mountain folk passed everything worth keeping: *ask small, thank quick, and never ask twice.*

The Whisperer. Corvane had read the fragments—the pre-dynastic carvings, the mad marginalia of monks who'd lived too near the deep places. An old god of the space between voices, the pause before an answer, the thing that heard you in the gap when no one else would. Sealed away with its siblings behind dragon-glass by the first Underhills and the fire that had once owned the sky.

And now it was leaking through the seal like water through a cracked dam, finding the desperate, feeding on them, growing. Every prayer was a chisel-tap against the prison wall. Every miracle widened the crack.

Which reframed everything. Corvane had come up this mountain believing he hunted a runaway asset. He now understood he was in a race with three competitors: the rebels who wanted the gods freed, the god who wanted itself freed, and the dying queen who wanted a weapon before death took her ledger out of her hands. Freya Underhill was not merely valuable. She was the key, and keys did not get to choose which doors they opened.

"Father Anselm," he said, back in the square, the sun now fully up and hot on the slate roofs, "has she returned? Or sent word? Think carefully. A woman like that leaves debts everywhere she goes. Debts are strings, and strings lead somewhere."

"She owes us nothing," the priest said. "And we owe her our discretion."

"Discretion." Corvane tasted the word. Then he sighed, and it was almost genuine. "Father. I am going to explain something to you, and I want you to listen, because you are the only man in this valley with the learning to understand it, and I dislike wasting explanations on stone walls." He began to walk, slow, hands clasped behind him, and the square rearranged itself around his motion the way iron filings follow a magnet. "There is a war coming to these mountains. Not a border skirmish—a real war, the kind that eats villages whole and asks for seconds. On one side, the crown. On the other side, things older than crowns, waking up hungry, and the folk foolish enough to feed them. When that war arrives, every household in this valley will be asked which side it stands on. And I am offering Millbrook the chance to answer early, correctly, and be remembered for it."

"And if we decline to answer?"

"Then you haven't declined," Corvane said. "You've simply chosen to be scenery. And scenery, Father, burns the same whichever side wins."

The priest's jaw worked. Behind him, Corvane could feel the village listening from every window, every crack in every door, forty households drinking in the arithmetic of survival. Good. Let them hear it plainly. Half of mercy was clarity.

"Now," Corvane said, pleasantly, "the trail. Which path did she take when she left, and who drew her the map?"

Silence. Real silence, the collective held breath of a village deciding. And Corvane watched it happen—the flickering glances, the miller suddenly fascinated by his own hands, the blacksmith's wife stepping back into her doorway's shadow—and he understood, with a small cold click, that they had already decided.

They were protecting her. All of them. Not from fear of him—from loyalty to her. The taster who swallowed poison so their queen could eat safely; the mountain-born daughter who knew their passes and bought their bread and paid fair. They had chosen her in the space of one night, these people who had known him for half a day, and they would rather watch their village burn than sell her.

Well. That was informative too.

* * *

He gave them until dusk. He made it look like patience—men watering horses, soldiers eating from the village's own stores, Vess conducting polite interviews at the shrine while Aldric drank the miller's ale and laughed too loudly. Corvane even took supper in the square, at a trestle the miller's wife laid for him with shaking hands, and ate the stew, and complimented it, and tipped her silver. He was aware of the performance from inside it the way a puppeteer is aware of his own wrist: every gesture calibrated, every smile filed under *later*.

At full dark, he stood at the edge of the square and addressed the village one final time. Torches. His voice pitched to carry without shouting.

"The woman is called Freya Underhill. Some of you know the name already—I saw it in your faces this afternoon. She carries something the crown requires, and I will recover it, with her cooperation or without it. What happens next depends entirely on you." He paused. Let the firelight move. "One name. One direction. That is all I require, and then my hounds ride on, and Millbrook keeps its roofs."

Nothing. A child started crying somewhere and was hushed. The torches spat.

Corvane nodded slowly, as though they'd answered a different question—one he'd been hoping they'd fail.

"So be it."

He had brought the pitch-oil up from the lowlands in six casks, disguised as salt pork, because he had known before he ever crossed the valley line that it might come to this. Not cruelty. Contingency. A fire in a mountain valley at night was visible for thirty miles; every shepherd, every smuggler, every hidden watcher on every ridge would see Millbrook burning and understand the terms of the new age with a clarity no proclamation could achieve. And word would travel—mountain word traveled fast—to a certain dark-haired woman hiding in some hollow with a hungry boy, and she would do the arithmetic herself: *they burned forty homes because of me.* Guilt was a leash longer than any chain. Grief was a road sign pointing straight at you.

Burn the shelters, and she comes to the last open door. His door. Smiling.

"Take the priest to the wagons," he said quietly to Vess. "Alive. He knows more than he gave me, and grief loosens scholars wonderfully." Then, louder, to Aldric: "Light it at the granary and the mill. Wind's from the west; it'll carry. Keep the eastern lane clear."

Aldric frowned. "Clear, my lord?"

"Some will run for it. Let them." Corvane watched the first torch dip into the first cask. "A village with survivors tells the story louder than a village of ash. And I am not in the business of waste, Ser Aldric. Only of message."

The mill caught first, dry grain catching like a held breath finally released, and then the granary, and then the flames found the thatch and went up the lanes like rumor. Corvane stood in the square with his hands folded and watched Millbrook burn with the same attention he gave to maps and ledgers—cataloguing, measuring, confirming the wind, noting which houses resisted longest, which families fled east through the gap he'd left them, how long the church roof held (it held; he'd ordered the shrine spared; desecrated gods made poor informants). Somewhere a woman screamed a name into the smoke. Somewhere a man beat at a flaming door with bare hands and was pulled away by neighbors who loved him enough to stop him.

Corvane felt nothing in particular. Arithmetic had no smell, whatever the poets claimed about the reek of burning thatch. This was the price of the only outcome that kept the crown standing and the gods caged and the succession from drowning in blood, and prices were what they were; you paid them and you did not weep at the till. The queen would understand. The queen, in her rotting body and her gilded terror, understood better than anyone alive that kingdoms were kept by terrible men doing terrible sums. That was why she kept him.

"Message delivered, my lord?" Vess asked at his shoulder, as the eastern lane emptied of its stream of refugees.

"Delivered." He turned from the fire at last, and the heat fell away from his face like a curtain drawn. "Now we follow the survivors. Frightened people scatter to the ones who sheltered them before. Within a week, someone in that crowd will lead us to the Hearthfolk's camps—and she won't be able to stay away from the ash, Vess. People like her never can. She'll come back for the graves."

He mounted, and the gelding picked its way east through the refugees, past children with singed blankets and old men carrying nothing but each other, and none of them looked at him, and that too was a kind of answer.

Behind them, Millbrook burned against the mountains like a sigil drawn in fire—a message written large enough for a taster to read from any ridge in Thornfell:

*I know where you are. I know who loves you. Come to me, or I will keep reading.*

Far off to the north, deep under the roots of the highest peak, Corvane fancied—for just one moment, a trick of heat and distance—that the mountain itself had shifted. As though something vast had turned over in its sleep.

He put the thought away with the rest of the night's inventory, and rode down into the dark.
