## 18. Ash on the Wind

The smoke reached her before the smell did.

Freya had been walking since before dawn, following the smuggler's track along the spine of Cragmoor Ridge, and from that height she had watched the plume rise out of Hollowmere like a grey finger pointing at an empty sky. By the time she came down through the birches to the village's edge, the wind had shifted and carried it all to her at once—char and wet ash and something underneath, something sweeter and far worse, that she recognized the way a taster recognizes poison: not by name but by what it did to her body. Her stomach clenched. Her throat closed. Her eyes watered, and she let them.

She knew this smell. She had smelled it once as a child, when a hay barn went up outside Thornfell with two stable boys inside. The body remembers what the mind refuses to name.

*They burned it,* she thought, and the thought was very small and very cold, like a coin dropped down a well. *Corvane burned it because of me.*

Hollowmere had been the third village to shelter her. The first had turned her away at the headman's insistence, frightened faces in doorways, and Freya had not blamed them; fear was a sensible thing to hoard when soldiers rode the passes. The second had hidden her for a single night and given Wick a honey cake and pretended not to know what she was. But Hollowmere—the Maren family, old Tam and his wife Bess, their daughter Sela with the crooked smile and the baby on her hip—they had taken her in without being asked twice. Tam had stood in his doorway with his arms crossed and said, "You're the one who tastes poison so the queen can eat her supper. My girl, you've done more for folk like us than any crown ever has. You'll sleep under my roof."

That roof was gone now. All the roofs were gone.

Freya stood at the edge of the village green and made herself look. It was a discipline, looking. She had spent eleven years making herself look at dishes that might kill her, examining every glaze and garnish for the small wrongness that meant death, and she brought that same terrible attention to bear now. The forge still smoked. The well house had collapsed into its own foundations. The great oak by the shrine—she could see where the fire had climbed it and died, leaving it a black skeleton against the pale morning, half its branches sheared off like a bird shot mid-flight.

And there were bodies.

Not many. Corvane's hounds had been efficient rather than thorough; they had come for information, not slaughter, and when the villagers gave them nothing—or gave them nothing fast enough—the hounds had made an example. She counted five from where she stood. An old man near the well, curled as if asleep in the cold. Two figures close together by the ruined smithy, one clearly shielding the other even in death. A woman face-down in the trampled mud of the green, her apron still tied, the fabric scorched but not consumed. And near the shrine itself, small, so small—

Freya's legs took her forward before her mind agreed to go. She walked past the old man, past the pair at the smithy, past the woman in the apron, and she knelt beside the smallest shape and she already knew, she had known since the ridge, since the smoke, since the sweet-under-ash smell, and knowing did nothing at all to soften it.

It was Pip. Sela's boy. Eighteen months old, or near enough—he had been walking for only a season, proud of it, tottering after the chickens with his fists out for balance. He had grabbed Freya's thumb the first evening and refused to give it back, and Bess had laughed and said, "He knows a safe hand when he holds one."

His hand was still curled now, around nothing.

Freya did not make a sound. That was the horror of it, later—that she didn't weep, didn't cry out, didn't tear her hair. She knelt in the ash with a dead child's hand in hers and felt something in her chest go very quiet and very hard, the way water goes quiet and hard when winter takes it all the way down. Somewhere behind her eyes a voice that sounded like her grandmother said, *Grief is a dish too, child. Some you swallow. Some you serve.*

She laid Pip gently back down. She stood. She looked at the blackened oak and the smoking forge and the five shapes on the green, and she heard herself say, aloud, in a voice she barely recognized—flat, level, the voice she used to report a poisoned dish to the queen's steward:

"I will bury them properly. Every one."

There was no one to hear her say it. That didn't matter. Some vows are made to the dead, and some are made to whatever is left standing when the dead are gone.

* * *

The digging took the rest of the day.

She found spades in the collapsed tool shed—two of them, one handle charred—and she chose the gravesite herself, on the low slope above the village where the soil was dark and soft and the birches grew thin enough to let the light through. Mountain custom, her grandmother's custom: the dead above the living, facing the sunrise, so they'd be the first to see each morning what the living were still fighting for. She had never dug a grave before. She learned quickly. The body taught her what the body always teaches: how much a spade weighs by the hundredth stroke, how blisters open, how sweat stings a cut you didn't know you had.

She buried the old man first. Then the pair from the smithy—she could not tell which was father and which was son, and she decided it did not matter, and wrapped them together in the same blanket, because they had died holding on to each other and she would not be the one to pry them apart. Then the woman in the apron, whose face she turned gently upward and whose name she did not know, and whom she therefore named aloud, over the grave, "Sister of Hollowmere," because the dead deserve at least a word.

Pip last. She wrapped him in her own cloak, the good wool one, dyed mountain-blue, and she carried him up the slope herself without letting the cloth touch the ground, and she put him in the ground with his fists curled the way he liked them, and she filled it in slowly, and she said, "You held a safe hand, little one. I'm sorry it wasn't safer."

Then she sat down among the five mounds of fresh-turned earth and looked at what remained of Hollowmere, and the quiet hardness in her chest began, very slowly, to crack—not into weeping, though the weeping came later, in the night—but into something else. Something with heat in it.

*Rage.* She examined it the way she examined everything, with a taster's care, turning it over, testing its edges. This was not the hot rage of the moment, the rage that makes people do stupid things and die. This was slow. This was patient. This was the rage of someone who had spent her whole life swallowing poison so that other people could eat safely, who had told herself that was enough, that invisibility was a virtue, that the best revenge on a cruel world was simply to survive it usefully—and who now understood, sitting among graves she had dug with her own blistered hands, that survival was not the same as service, and usefulness was not the same as justice.

Corvane had burned this village to flush her out. Not because she had stolen anything. Not because she had killed anyone. Because she existed, and her existence was valuable, and men like Lord Corvane did not dig for treasure with shovels. They set fires and waited to see what ran from the smoke.

Well. She had run. And now she was here, and the smoke was settling, and she was not finished.

* * *

It was full dark when she went back down to the village, because there was one thing left to do and she wanted no witnesses, not even the birches.

The shrine. Hollowmere's shrine was an old one, older than the queen's grandmother, a waist-high cairn of field stones beneath the fire-scarred oak, and villagers had been leaving bread and milk and cut flowers on it for generations without quite remembering why. Freya had seen it her first night here, had noted it with her taster's eye—a forgotten thing, a harmless thing—and had thought nothing more of it.

Now she stood before it in the moonlight and drew the shard from the inner pocket of her coat.

She had taken it from the sealed shrine below Thornfell keep, weeks ago, in the hour when she had first understood what her blood was and what it was bound to. A splinter of dragon-glass, fused and black and faintly iridescent, no bigger than her littlest finger. She had carried it since without quite admitting why—because it hummed sometimes, very faintly, against her skin, and because the humming had grown louder these past days, and because a part of her, the part that catalogued symptoms for a living, had begun to suspect that the shard was not humming at random.

It was humming *now*. She could feel it through the cloth, a fine tremor, like a trapped moth.

"Grandmother," Freya said quietly, to the night air, to the cairn, to whoever listened, "you told me the Underhills kept things. You never told me the things could keep *us*." She worked the shard free of its wrapping. In the moonlight it drank the silver and gave back nothing, a sliver of absolute dark, and against her bare palm it was warm—warm as a living thing, warm as a bird's breastbone.

She had meant only to leave it at the shrine. That had been the whole of the plan, if a tangle of grief and exhaustion could be called a plan: return the stolen piece to a holy place, ask forgiveness of gods she didn't believe in, walk away lighter. She knelt and set the shard atop the cairn, between two fire-blackened stones, and she began to draw her hand back—

And the shard flared.

Not with light. With *heat*—a sudden, shocking warmth that sank through her palm and up her wrist and into the old scar-tissue of her birthmark, and the sigil on her forearm woke like a struck bell. She gasped. She should have pulled away. She didn't. Because in the instant of contact she *felt* something—felt it the way she felt poison on her tongue, instantly, totally, below thought:

A vastness. A depth. A fire banked so long it had nearly forgotten it was fire, coiled in the dark under the roots of the mountains, breathing once a century, dreaming of a world that had stopped needing it. And threaded through that vastness, thin and bright and unmistakable, a thread of *attention*—turning, slowly, like a great eye opening at the bottom of a well—turning toward *her*.

Toward the sigil. Toward the blood. Toward the last Underhill kneeling in the ashes of a burned village with murder in her heart.

"You feel that too," Freya whispered. Her voice shook. "Don't you. All those years under the stone, and you feel it when I—" She stopped. Swallowed. The taster's discipline: name the symptom, then name the cause. "When I'm angry. When I mean something. When I *burn*."

The shard pulsed against her palm, once, twice, slow as a heartbeat, and the sigil on her arm answered it, heat for heat, and for one vertiginous moment Freya felt the shape of a connection running down through the mountain's bones to something enormous and dying and *waiting*, and she understood—with the same cold clarity that had told her, years ago, which dish would kill her—that the dragon-glass was not a relic. It was a *doorbell*. And she had just rung it.

She snatched her hand back. The flare died. The shard sat on the cairn, dark again, ordinary again, except that the air around it shimmered faintly, the way air shimmers above a forge.

Freya knelt there a long time, cradling her burning arm, watching the shard watch her back.

She thought about Tam, who had given her his roof. About Bess, who had salted the soup exactly right. About Sela, wherever she had fled with the other survivors—three families' worth of hollow-eyed folk scattered up the passes, alive because they'd been at market in Millbrook when the soldiers came. About Pip's small curled fist.

And she thought about Corvane, in his clean tower, with his census rolls and his patient smiles and his hunters with their torches, waiting for her to run out of villages willing to burn.

The rage in her chest settled at last—not gone, never gone, but *shaped* now, poured into a mold, the way molten metal is poured into a form to become a blade. Grief transmuted. Her grandmother would have known the word for it. Freya only knew the feeling: she was done running blind. She was done being the invisible thing that survived. If the mountains remembered—if the old powers were stirring, if even a dying dragon's glass could wake at her touch—then she would stop fleeing the war and start choosing its ground.

She picked the shard up. It came willingly, warm and quiet, like a creature deciding to be carried.

"They'll come looking for me here," she said to the graves on the hillside, to the dead who deserved the truth. "Let them. I'll be gone by morning—but I won't be gone *far*. There's a road only smugglers and tasters know, down under the Wyrm's Tooth, and it runs straight to the deep places. To the shrine below Thornfell. To everything Corvane wants and doesn't understand."

She stood. She brushed the grave-earth from her knees with the same care she used to brush flour from her hands.

"And I'm going to learn what Grandmother knew before he does. Whatever it costs."

Far beneath her feet, deeper than any grave, deeper than the roots of the fire-scarred oak, something vast turned over in its long sleep—and for the first time in three hundred years, it dreamed of a name.

*Underhill.*

Freya felt it pass through her like a spark through dry grass, and she smiled, and there was nothing kind in it at all.

She shouldered her pack, took one last look at the five mounds on the hillside, and walked north into the dark—toward the Wyrm's Tooth, toward the deep roads, toward the sleeping fire that had just begun, very softly, to call her home.
