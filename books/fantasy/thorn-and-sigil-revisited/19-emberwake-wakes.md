## 19. Emberwake Wakes

The dreaming had lasted three hundred years, and in all that time the fire had never gone out.

It had guttered. It had sunk so low that Emberwake sometimes—*sometimes*, in the long slow centuries of half-sleep—wondered if it had already died and simply not noticed, the way a mountain does not notice it has become a hill. The flame lived behind its heart now, a coal no larger than an ember of ordinary wood, and Emberwake curled around it as it had curled around the seal for a thousand years before that: keeper, warden, furnace, grave.

It dreamed in stone-time. A decade was a yawn. A century was a turning-over. It dreamed of the first Underhill's hands, small and clever and scarred with burns, pressing molten glass into the seams of the world. It dreamed of the oath spoken over its own egg, before it had eyes to see who spoke it: *You will hold the door. You will not open the door. You will burn until there is nothing left to burn.* It dreamed of snow falling into the caldera for ten thousand years without once melting, because even the snow knew better than to touch what slept below.

And then, one night—or what would have been night, had there been sky instead of a mile of granite overhead—the shard sang.

Emberwake did not wake. Waking was a country it could no longer afford to visit. But something in the deep sediment of its sleep *shifted*, the way silt shifts when a great fish turns far beneath a lake, and the coal behind its heart flared from grey to orange for the first time in living memory, which is to say, the first time in three hundred years, since the last of the Underhills had climbed down into the dark and never come back up.

*Underhill,* it thought, in the language that predated mountains. *A hand on the glass.*

Not a hand. Not yet. A shard—a splinter of the prison-wall itself, torn loose by grief or rage or some third thing Emberwake could not name—and carried up into the thin bright world where dragons were stories and gods were rumors. The shard still remembered being part of the door. The shard still remembered the shape of the blood that had made it. And it was calling, calling, calling, the way a lost child calls for a mother it cannot name, and every note of it fell like a spark into the dry tinder of Emberwake's ancient sleep.

The coal behind its heart grew warm.

Warm became hot. Hot became a wordless ache, an itch beneath scales that had not moved since the reign of the grandmother-queens, and Emberwake stirred—not the body, not yet, the body was stone now, fused to the caldera floor, rooted through with quartz veins grown across its wings like frost across a window—but the *self*, the long attention coiled at the center of everything, turned its face toward the sound.

*Who carries my door?*

No answer came. Only the singing, fainter than a heartbeat heard through a wall, and threaded through it—this was the strange thing, the thing that made the old attention narrow to a point—threaded through it was blood. Underhill blood. Thin, diluted, shaken through generations like ink through water, but *recognizable*. Emberwake had known four hundred Underhills by the taste of their blood in the air. It had known their oaths, their deaths, their stubborn generational refusal to let the fire go out. This blood was weaker than any it had known.

But it was *the same blood*. The last drop of a wine whose vineyard had burned.

*One left,* Emberwake thought, and the thought was so heavy with grief that a tremor passed through the caldera, and somewhere far above, shepherds spoke for a season afterward of the night the mountain sighed.

---

Time moved strangely after that. Stone-time loosened; the decades began to pass like years. Emberwake drifted at the edge of waking the way a drowning man drifts at the edge of light, and in that borderland it began, for the first time in centuries, to *listen* to the world above.

It heard the smallfolk prayers first. They came down through the rock like rain through thatch—thin, scattered, mostly falling harmlessly onto stone. But some of them did not fall. Some of them were *caught*. Emberwake felt them being caught, felt the old hunger below the glass-wall flex and drink, and its keeper's fury rose for the first time in three hundred years, hot enough that the quartz veins in its wings flushed pink.

*They are feeding again,* it thought. *The Whisperer has found mouths.*

It strained against its own stone. It was the keeper; it had always been the keeper; keeping was the whole of its purpose and most of its misery. But a keeper needs hands, and hands need blood, and the blood it could feel singing through the shard was growing stronger day by day—closer, more often near, carrying with it scents Emberwake had catalogued in another age: woodsmoke, bitter herbs, the mineral tang of poison swallowed daily and survived. A taster. The blood-scent belonged to a taster. Of course it did. The Underhills had always been the ones who swallowed what others could not.

*Come down,* Emberwake thought toward the surface, knowing the thought could not carry, knowing prayer was a door that opened only one way. *Come down and do your family's work.*

But the shard sang louder. And the singing changed.

---

It happened in the deep winter, when the mountain's bones were coldest and the coal behind Emberwake's heart had settled back into its long grey smolder. The shard called—and this time the call had a *shape*. Not the diffuse ache of blood-in-the-distance but a specific, singular presence, walking the high passes with a second, smaller warmth beside it, a child-shape, quick and bright as a struck match. And the shard was in the larger one's keeping. Held against skin. Warmed by Underhill blood directly, constantly, the way a coal is warmed by a cupped palm.

Emberwake's attention snapped to it like a hook finding flesh.

*There.*

It poured itself along the thread. It had no eyes up there, no ears, only the shard's crude senses borrowed and magnified through a thousand feet of living rock—and what it sensed made no sense at all, at first. The blood-warmth was walking *away* from the shrine. Away from the deep door. Away from duty, as every instinct of a thousand years understood duty. And yet the shard sang happier in her keeping than it ever had in the dark, and the blood—

The blood was grieving.

That was the thing Emberwake could not look away from. Grief soaked through the blood-scent like rain through cloth: grief for a burned village, grief for graves dug in frozen ground, grief worn the way the old Underhills had worn theirs—silently, usefully, converted hour by hour into forward motion. Emberwake had watched the first Underhill grieve exactly this way, three thousand years ago, kneeling in the ash of the first war with the molten glass cooling in her hands, refusing to weep because weeping took time and the door would not seal itself.

*Little keeper,* Emberwake thought, and the tenderness in it startled the old creature, tenderness being a thing it had believed burned out of it ages past along with everything else. *Little last keeper. You do not even know what you carry.*

It tried to reach her. It pushed against the stone of itself and the stone did not care; it sent its attention up the shard-thread and the thread frayed at the distance; it gathered the dregs of its failing fire and breathed, once, into the roots of the mountain, and far above a hot spring that had run cold for a century burst steaming from a hillside, and villagers walked a day to see the miracle, and prayed beside it, and the Whisperer drank their prayers, and Emberwake lay in the dark hating itself for feeding the enemy even accidentally, even once.

*Patience,* it told itself, in the voice of the first Underhill, the voice it had kept alive in memory longer than memory should be able to hold anything. *You were patient for the sealing. Be patient for the waking.*

But patience, it was discovering, is a different discipline for a dying creature than for a living one. Every year of waiting cost it fire it could not replace. The coal behind its heart was smaller than it had been a century ago. Smaller than it had been a decade ago. There would not be many more turnings-over. If the last Underhill did not come down soon—if she spent herself on the wars of men, on smugglers' roads and poisoned courts and the small desperate kindnesses that bled a person drop by drop—then the door would stand unkept, and the Whisperer would find another way out, and everything the Underhills had paid across forty generations would have bought nothing at all.

*Wake me,* Emberwake begged the shard, silently, in the oldest grammar. *Sing louder. Burn her hand if you must. Wake me while there is still fire left to keep the door with.*

The shard sang. Whether it sang louder, Emberwake could not tell. Hope and fear, in a creature that old, sound identical.

---

Then came the night the mountain broke.

Emberwake felt it building for days beforehand—a pressure in the deep places, the Whisperer thrashing against its wall as the war above swelled its congregation, thousands upon thousands of new prayers rising like smoke from the lowlands, prayers for victory, for vengeance, for the deaths of enemies, and the god drinking and drinking and swelling against the dragon-glass until the whole prison groaned like a ship in a storm. Emberwake braced itself against the caldera. It put the last of its strength into the seams of the rock, shoring, holding, doing the work of a keeper with no hands and almost no fire.

And then the shard called with a voice it had never used before.

Not singing. *Screaming.* Joy and terror braided together, and through the thread poured an image so vivid that Emberwake's sleeping body convulsed, cracking a shelf of stone the size of a village: the last Underhill, standing on a frozen ridge with the wind tearing at her, holding the shard up to the grey dawn light—and the shard answering her. Glowing. Reaching. Calling down through three thousand feet of granite with everything it had, *here, here, HERE,* and the call went through Emberwake like a spear of sunlight through a sealed tomb.

She had heard it. She was listening. She was afraid, and she was not running.

*Yes,* Emberwake thought, and the coal behind its heart flared white, and for one impossible moment the fire was what it had been in the age of winged wars, a sun caged in bone. *Yes. Come. I have kept your door. I have kept it alone. I am so tired, little keeper, and I have been alone for so much longer than I have been tired.*

It reached for her with everything remaining. And here is the truth of very old creatures, the truth no ballad sings: they do not see clearly. Three hundred years of sleep had worn the present thin as paper, and when Emberwake finally touched the edges of Freya Underhill's mind—gently, so gently, the touch of a mountain deciding not to fall—it did not see a taster, a smuggler, a woman who swallowed poison daily so strangers could eat safely. It saw *Maren Underhill*. It saw the first. The founder. The burned-handed, iron-willed, grief-carved woman who had stood before the open door at the end of the world and said *no further*.

The blood was thinner. The hands were softer. None of it mattered. Blood remembers what minds forget, and the blood said *Underhill*, and Emberwake had waited three hundred years to hear that word spoken in flesh.

*Maren,* it called, pouring the name up the thread like honey poured slow. *Maren, I know your step on the stairs of the world. Maren, the door holds. The door has always held. Come and see what your hands built, and bring your fire, for mine is nearly done, and the thing below us has learned to count the days.*

Far above, on a frozen ridge, a woman stopped walking. Emberwake felt her stop—felt the shard flare against her breastbone like a second heart, felt her breath catch in the pattern that meant a body has just understood it is being spoken to. The small bright warmth beside her—the match-child, the boy—clutched at her sleeve and said something too faint to read.

She looked down. At the ground. At the mountain itself. And though no human ear could have caught it, and no human throat could have made it, Emberwake felt the shape of what she said next travel down through stone and root and sleeping fire, arriving at last in the dark like a coin dropped into a well:

*"I'm coming."*

Two words. Swallowed, like everything else she had ever swallowed, and survived.

Emberwake settled back into its own heat, and for the first time in three hundred years, the settling was not surrender. The coal behind its heart pulsed in a rhythm it had almost forgotten—the rhythm of purpose. Above it, the Whisperer threw itself against the glass and screamed in the voices of the desperate. Around it, the mountain groaned and shed ice from its shoulders. Ahead of it, somewhere in the shallow bright world, the last of the Underhills was gathering her grief and her shard and her smuggler's roads and beginning, step by step, the long walk down.

*Hurry, little keeper,* Emberwake thought, as the dream closed over it once more, warmer now, shallower, a harbor rather than a grave. *Hurry, for the god below has learned patience too—and I am not certain, anymore, which of us the door was built to keep in.*

Deep beneath the Thornfell mountains, in a caldera no map had named for a thousand years, something vast turned over in its sleep—and did not go back under.

Above, on the ridge, Freya Underhill stood in the wind a moment longer, her hand pressed flat against the shard, listening to a voice older than kingdoms say her name like a homecoming.

Then she turned south, toward the deep door, and began to walk.
