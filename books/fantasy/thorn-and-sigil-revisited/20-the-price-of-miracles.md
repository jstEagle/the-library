## 20. The Price of Miracles

The shard woke her before dawn.

It had slept in the pouch at her throat for three days now, warm as a river stone held too long in the sun, and mostly it only murmured—half-heard things like wind through a keyhole, like someone humming in another room of an empty house. But that night it sang, low and insistent, and Freya came up out of a dream she could not remember with her hand already pressed flat over her breastbone and the word *no* already forming on her lips.

The loft above Maren's-by-the-Ford—this Maren being a grandmother of sixty winters and no relation to any queen—smelled of wool smoke and dried yarrow. Below her, six of the hearthfolk slept in a tangle of blankets: Brannic the smith, his wife Sorrel, old Tam with his ruined lungs, two brothers whose names Freya still stumbled over, and young Col who snored like a landslide. They had given her their roof without asking what she was. That was more than the crown had ever done, and less than they thought they'd done, because they believed she could protect them.

She lay still beneath the rough blanket and listened to the shard.

*Freya Underhill.* Not sound, exactly. Something older than sound, arriving behind her eyes the way cold arrives through a wall. *You have been tasting all your life. Taste this.*

And there was honey on her tongue.

That was how it began, always—with sweetness. She had learned poisons the way other girls learned embroidery, and the first lesson her grandmother's notes had ever taught her, scrawled in a crabbed hand on the inside cover of a recipe book, was this: *the sweetest ones kill you kindly, so you never think to fight.* Foxglove tastes of nothing at all until your heart stumbles. Water hemlock is faintly pleasant, like parsnip. And the Whisperer beneath Thornfell keep offered its bargains the same way—a warmth in the blood, a loosening of grief, the memory of every kindness anyone had ever done you played back all at once like a feast laid end to end.

Freya sat up in the dark and did not swallow.

*I know what you are doing,* she said, in the privacy of her own skull, which was apparently no longer private at all. *I taste for queens. You'll have to be subtler than honey.*

A ripple that might have been amusement. *You mistake me for something that needs to trick you. I am offering you a fair trade, taster. Fairness is rarer than love in this world; take note of it.*

The shard pulsed against her skin, and the darkness of the loft unfolded into vision—not quite seeing, not quite remembering, but standing somewhere between. She stood in the shrine below Thornfell again, before the wall of fused dragon-glass that sealed the gods' prison, black shot through with veins of ember-red, and across its face ran the name UNDERHILL cut deep as a scar. Her name. Her family's name, carved by hands three centuries dead, and beside it, fainter, the sigil itself—the coiled thorn-and-flame that matched the birthmark on her ribs.

*Your blood opened nothing,* the Whisperer said, gentle as a confessor. *But it could. One vein, freely opened. One life, spent once, cleanly—and I will call back every debt I have collected from these mountains. The wells stay sweet. The fevers stay broken. The children of the hearthfolk sleep safe until they die of old age in soft beds.*

The vision shifted. She saw them: the villages strung through the high valleys like beads on a grey cord. Ashford, where the fevered children had woken whole. Netherby, where the well water had turned sweet as spring rain. The ford village itself, where old Tam's lungs had eased so he could walk to his own field again instead of dying by inches in a chair. Miracles, all of them. And she knew now—they had all known since Corvane burned Millbrook—that miracles from the Whisperer were loans, and loans from gods accrued interest no ledger could hold.

*And if I refuse?*

Silence, long enough that she felt the shape of it, deliberate as a held breath.

*Then I continue as I have continued,* the god said at last, mildly. *I answer prayers. I collect what is owed. I wait. I have waited nine hundred years beneath your mountain, Freya Underhill; do not flatter yourself that you are my last hope. You are merely my best one.*

*I refuse.*

There it was. Spoken aloud, actually spoken, into the dark of a stranger's loft while six people slept below her—and she heard her own voice crack on the second syllable, because refusing a god was not like refusing Lord Corvane. Corvane you could refuse with a curtsy and a lie. This—

*Taste it again,* the Whisperer said, and there was no anger in it, which was worse. *Taste what you are choosing.*

Honey again—but underneath it now, the way a skilled poisoner layers a bitter base beneath a sweet top-note, something else. She tasted woodsmoke and wet ash. She tasted a specific quality of crying, the kind children do when they have learned not to be loud about it. She tasted a small hand going cold.

Her eyes snapped open. Grey pre-dawn light at the loft window. The shard lay quiet against her sternum, warm, patient, satisfied.

"Damn you," Freya whispered, and meant it in both directions.

* *

She did not sleep again. At first light she went down to the kitchen, banked the fire alive, and set herself to bread, because bread was the oldest thing she knew how to do with her hands when her mind wanted to run screaming. Flour, water, salt, the sour starter Tam's daughter kept fed like a fourth child. Kneading was arithmetic for the body: turn, fold, press, quarter-turn. She let it empty her.

Sorrel found her there an hour later, sleeves floured to the elbow, staring at nothing.

"You're pale," the smith's wife said, setting down a jug of milk. "Palerer," she amended, squinting, "than usual, and that's saying something for a court woman."

"The mountains don't agree with me."

"Mountains agree with everyone. It's people who disagree." Sorrel leaned against the table and studied her with frank brown eyes. "You had the dream-face when you came down. The listening face." A pause. "It talks to you, doesn't it. Through that stone you carry."

Freya's hands went still in the dough. Three weeks ago she would have lied, smooth and instant, the taster's reflex. But Sorrel had pulled two drowned children out of the ford river last autumn and breathed them back to life with nothing but stubbornness and a prayer she wasn't sure had been answered by anything good—and she deserved better than the court's currency.

"It talks," Freya said. "Last night it made me an offer."

"And?"

"And I refused it."

Sorrel nodded slowly, as if Freya had reported the weather. Then she crossed the room, took the salt jar down, and salted the bread with a heavier hand than usual, the old warding habit of women who remembered grandmothers who remembered older things. "Good," she said. "Whatever it wanted, good. We've had our fill of bargains up here. My mother traded a year of her hair to the old ways for my father's safe return from the lowland wars, and he came back mean as a stoat and died anyway." She wiped her hands. "Refusing is the only free thing left. Best you remember that."

*Refusing is the only free thing left.* Freya turned the words over all that morning while the village moved around her—Brannic hammering at the forge, the brothers mending the mill race, children shrieking in games of knuckles-and-bones in the lane. Refusal as freedom. It had a fine clean ring to it, and it was even true, probably, in some philosopher's sense.

What Sorrel's proverb did not account for was interest.

* *

The child's name was Merrin Hale, and she was seven years old, and Freya had taught her to identify foxglove only four days earlier, kneeling in the ditch below the shepherd's path while the girl pointed at each weed with grim delight: *"Deadly, deadly, deadly, tasty!"* She had a gap in her teeth and a habit of narrating everything she did under her breath, and her mother, Wren, dyed wool in colors that made the lowland merchants weep with envy.

Merrin went missing at dusk.

Not lost—missing. There was a difference, and every soul in the ford village understood the difference within the hour, because Merrin had gone up to the shrine on the hill. The little hill-shrine, not the great sealed one below the keep; every village in the Thornfells had one, a cairn of stacked stones where folk had left offerings since before anyone could say why. Since the old gods were shut away and the habit of addressing them outlived the memory of their faces. Wren had gone up at dusk to leave the week's first bannock, as her mother had done, and Merrin had trotted along behind her, and somewhere between the third step and the seventh stone of the cairn, between one breath of her mother's back and the next, she had simply not been there.

Wren's scream carried down the whole valley.

Freya reached the shrine with the first of the torches. What she found there she would carry for the rest of her life, in the place behind her ribs where the taster kept the flavors of everything she'd ever swallowed and survived.

The bannock was untouched. The stones of the cairn were rearranged—one course of them lifted out and set aside with a neatness that was somehow the most obscene thing about it, careful, courteous, like a guest moving furniture to make room. And pressed into the bare earth where the offering should have been, written in a script that looked like frost ferns and was not frost, were two lines:

**THE DEBT WAS DUE.**
**THE BEARER CHOSE OTHERWISE.**

"She wouldn't—" Wren was on her knees in the dirt, clawing at the writing with her bare fingers as if she could dig her daughter out of it. "She was holding my skirt, she was *right behind me*, I heard her breathing—"

Tam put his arms around the woman and looked up at Freya over her bowed head, and his old eyes, milky with years and lung-rot, were not confused at all. He knew. Half of them knew, she realized—the ones who had seen her come down from the mountain with the dragon-glass shard, the ones who had watched her argue with the air. They had known what she was carrying and what it was, and they had sheltered her anyway, and now the bill had come due in a child's shoe size.

"Sigil-bearer," Tam said quietly. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*inquiring*. As if asking a physician whether there might be anything more to be done.

"There was a bargain," Freya heard herself say. Her voice sounded very far away, very level, the voice she used at court when reporting that the queen's wine was clean. "My blood for the seal, in exchange for calling back everything it's taken. I refused."

The silence around the torchlight had texture. She could feel it pressing on her from all sides: Brannic's huge scarred hands hanging loose at his sides; Sorrel's face gone grey as ash; the two brothers looking anywhere else; and Wren, rocking, keening, a sound like wind through a broken shutter.

"You refused," Sorrel repeated. Flatly. Not a question. A stone laid on a grave.

"I refused." And here was the terrible arithmetic, the lesson no recipe book had ever taught her: *the sweetest ones kill you kindly.* She had thought refusing was the pure choice, the free choice, the only coin she had left that wasn't bloodied. But refusal was not a wall. Refusal was a door closing on one corridor while every other corridor stayed open—and the god had simply walked down a different one. It had never needed her permission. It had only preferred her cooperation, because cooperation was sweeter, and gods, like poisoners, preferred to work with sugar.

"It took her *because* I refused," Freya said. "As a price. As a—" Her voice failed on the word *lesson*, but everyone heard it anyway.

"No." Tam's grip tightened on Wren's shoulders. His voice cracked like a whip, and for a moment the dying old man sounded like whatever he had been at thirty. "Don't you dare carry that. Don't you dare put that weight on yourself, girl—it wants you thinking that way. Guilt is a leash. It's been building that leash since the day it started sweetening wells." He coughed, deep and wet. "It took Merrin because it is a hunger wearing a church, and hunger takes. Your yes or your no changed only the *when*."

But Freya had spent twenty years cataloguing symptoms, and she knew a poison's logic when she tasted one, and Tam's mercy—kind, true mercy—did not change the flavor of what was in her mouth now. Because he was wrong, or rather he was right in the way that comforters are right and physicians are righter: her refusal had not caused this. But her presence had. Her *name* had. Every day the sigil-bearer sheltered among them, the Whisperer's leverage grew, and she had known that, had weighed it in her meticulous survivalist mind, had decided the risk was acceptable because the villagers insisted, because belonging had felt worth the danger.

Belonging had felt worth the danger. And a seven-year-old girl who could name poisonous weeds was now collateral in a war of gods, and there was no dose calculation in the world that made those two facts balance.

* *

They searched until moonrise. Brannic and the brothers walked the streambed with lanterns; Tam's daughter rode down-valley to raise the neighboring farms; Freya did what she was actually for, finally, the thing all those years of tasting had built her for—she read the ground.

No tracks. That was the answer her body kept returning, over and over, the way the tongue returns to a sore tooth. No tracks, no drag-marks, no disturbed bracken, no scent of stranger-sweat on the shrine stones. Merrin Hale had not been *taken* the way men take things. The space she had occupied had simply been renegotiated. Somewhere between her mother's skirt-hem and the cairn, the terms of the world had changed for exactly one small person, the way a well changes from bitter to sweet, the way a fever breaks—all the Whisperer's signatures, all its tidy, courteous, obscene economy.

At full dark Freya climbed alone to the cairn, sent the others away with a lie about needing quiet to work, and knelt in the frost-fern writing. She took out the shard.

It was singing again. Triumphant, almost tender.

"You have her," Freya said aloud. Her breath smoked in the cold. "Somewhere. In there, or under, or wherever it is you keep what you collect."

*Safe,* the Whisperer agreed, warm as fresh bread, and oh, it knew exactly which flavors to use. *Warm. Frightened, but fed—do you imagine I starve what I intend to keep? She prays to me now, taster. Did you know that? Children pray so easily. She asks for her mother every night, and I answer her, a little. A voice in the dark. A warmth. I can be a very gentle god, when I am loved.*

Every hair on Freya's arms stood up. Because that was the hook, she saw it plain as a barbed fishhook dressed in a worm: the god was showing her its mercy to prove its reasonableness. See how well I treat her. See how negotiable I am. The cruelty of the taking balanced openly against the tenderness of the keeping, a merchant displaying damaged goods alongside prime ones, everything priced, everything for sale.

"Name the price," Freya said. "For her return. Not the seal. Not my blood in your wall. Something smaller—you said yourself I'm not your last hope. So name what you'll actually take, and I'll consider it."

Silence. Then laughter—not sound, but the sensation of laughter, a shiver of delight moving through the shard like a current through wire, and it went on too long, and it was the worst thing she had felt since Corvane's hunters burned Millbrook.

*Oh, you are your grandmother's granddaughter,* the Whisperer said fondly. *Negotiating with the shelf while the shop burns. Very well. No new price. The original stands: your blood opens the seal, and the child goes home before the next full moon, and the wells stay sweet, and the fevers stay broken, forever, amen. That is the whole of my ledger, Freya Underhill. There is no smaller coin. There never was. Every miracle I ever gave was a promissory note against this exact moment—you simply didn't hold the pen.*

"And if I find another way? If I close the seal instead of opening it?" Her hand closed around the shard until its edges bit her palm. "Grandmother's letters say the sigil turns both directions. Did you know I've read them?"

The warmth in the shard cooled by one precise degree.

*Then I will keep collecting,* it said, mild as milk. *Debts fall due. Children wander. Wells sour. Fevers return. The world is full of accidents, sigil-bearer, and I am very old and very patient and I do not tire, and you cannot stand between me and every desperate soul in these mountains forever. Sooner or later someone you love will kneel at a cairn and ask me for something, and I will say yes, because yes is all I know how to say.*

It withdrew then, sliding out of her skull like a hand withdrawing from a glove, leaving the ordinary night behind—stars, wind, the smell of pine and cold stone. Freya knelt alone at the cairn with blood beading in her palm where the shard had bitten, and far below, torchlight moved through the village lanes as the search parties regrouped, and somewhere in the dark, in whatever elsewhere the god kept its collections, a seven-year-old girl was praying to the thing that had stolen her, and it was answering, gently, a little at a time.

Freya pressed her bleeding hand flat against the cairn stones and made herself a promise with the flavor of iron still in her mouth.

She had swallowed poison for the crown for ten years and called it duty. She had lied to courts and smugglers and gods. But she had never yet swallowed the worst thing of all—the thing her grandmother's letters had hinted at, the thing that made the sigil a keeper's mark and not a sacrifice's: that sometimes the only way to save what you love is to feed the flame you swore to starve.

She rose. Down in the valley, Wick was climbing the hill toward her with a lantern, small and stubborn against the wind—he'd heard, of course; the whole village had heard—and Freya stood at the cairn of the old gods and understood, with terrible clarity, exactly what she would have to do.

She would give the god its yes.

Just not the way it expected.
