## 21. Wick's Choice

Wick had learned to count in heartbeats before he'd learned to count in numbers.

It was the first thing Freya had taught him, back when he was still a gutter-thief with a stolen apple in his sleeve and no name anyone used twice. *A taster counts her pulse,* she'd said, pressing two of his grimy fingers against the soft hollow of his own throat. *The body tells you the truth before the mouth can lie about it.* He'd thought it was a strange thing for a woman who tasted poison for queens to care about. Now, crouched in the black throat of the mountain with the Whisperer's voice coiling through the dark like smoke poured backward, he understood it completely.

His heart was beating too fast. Ninety, ninety-five, a hundred. The body telling the truth.

He was going to do it anyway.

* * *

He had left the hearth-village of Hollowmere an hour past midnight, when the watch-fire burned low and Freya slept at last — truly slept, for the first time in three days, her face slack and young-looking without its armor of watchfulness. She'd spent those days refusing the god. Everyone in the village knew it by now. The Whisperer had made its offer in dreams and in whispers from wells, from chimney-flues, from the mouths of children too young to know what they were saying: *Give me the sigil-bearer's blood, and I will give you back your dead, your harvests, your safety. Refuse, and I will take my price elsewhere.*

And then it had taken Tam Arrowsmith's daughter.

Maren — seven years old, gap-toothed, forever underfoot in the bake-house where Wick had been learning to knead dough badly — had simply not woken one morning. Not dead. Worse than dead. Her eyes open and moving, her chest rising and falling, but *no one home*, as if someone had scooped out everything that made Maren Maren and left only the shape of her breathing. Her mother had screamed until her voice broke. The village healer had wept over her and found nothing wrong with her body at all.

Freya had gone gray as ash when she saw the girl. "This is what refusal costs," she'd said quietly, so quietly Wick wasn't sure she'd meant him to hear. "I always knew refusal would cost."

That night, Wick lay on his pallet in the loft above the bake-house and listened to his own heartbeat and did arithmetic with it.

*One girl, seven years old, taken because Freya said no.*

*One boy, twelve years old, worth nothing to anyone except one woman who had chosen to protect him.*

*One god, hungry, willing to trade.*

The sums were simple. It was the simplest sum he'd ever done. He was nobody. He was an orphan off the streets of Lowmarket whose most valuable possession was a knife with a broken tip and a jar of dried yarrow Freya had given him for his cough. If the god wanted devotion, wanted blood, wanted *someone* — then let it have someone whose loss would break nothing. Freya was the sigil-bearer. Freya was the thread a hundred lives were wound around now, the woman the mountains called to, the woman Emberwake's shard sang to in the night. Wick was just the boy who swept the floor and remembered which herbs went in the fever-bags.

Except — and here was the part of the sum that wouldn't stay solved — except that she'd chosen him. Out of everyone, out of all the world, she'd looked at a thieving street-rat and said *stay*. And he knew, with the terrible clarity of a boy who had never been chosen before, exactly what it would do to her if he vanished into the dark the way Maren had.

Love, he was learning, was not a clean thing. It had edges like a broken pot. You could hold it carefully your whole life and still bleed.

He went anyway.

* * *

The shrine the Whisperer favored was not the great sealed prison beneath Thornfell keep — that door was Freya's burden, sealed with dragon-glass and Underhill blood. This was smaller, older, meaner: a wayshrine half-swallowed by the mountain's shoulder, three days' hard walk from Hollowmere, where travelers had once left offerings to a god whose name had been worn off the stone by weather and neglect. The villagers called it the Quiet Place now, and avoided it, and told their children stories about it, which was exactly the kind of attention a starving god fed on.

Wick climbed the last stretch in the hour before dawn, when the sky was the color of dirty pewter and the frost made the scree crackle under his boots. He'd brought offerings. That was the part that shamed him later, how earnestly he'd prepared: a heel of the bread he'd baked himself, dense and slightly burnt on one side; a twist of salt; the yarrow, because it was the only thing he owned that anyone had ever given him, and giving it away felt like the kind of sacrifice gods were supposed to want.

"Hello," he said to the dark mouth of the shrine. His voice came out small and bounced back at him off wet stone. "I've come to make a trade."

The silence stretched. Long enough that he almost turned around, almost let cowardice win, almost climbed back down the mountain to the smell of baking bread and the sound of Freya's voice correcting his knife-work.

Then the dark answered.

*Little one.* The voice didn't come from the shrine. It came from inside his own skull, warm as broth, gentle as a hand on his hair. *Little thief. Little counter-of-heartbeats. What trade could you possibly have that I would want?*

"I—" His rehearsed speech crumbled. He'd practiced it against the hillside for three days, words tumbling over each other, and now every one of them fled. So he said the true thing instead, the naked thing. "You want Freya Underhill. Take me instead. She's — she matters. She's the sigil-bearer, she's the only one who can seal you away or open you, whatever it is she's going to do. And I'm just — I'm nobody. But I'm offering. Nobody offers, do they? People pray at you because they're desperate, they pray because they *want* things. I'm giving. Doesn't giving count more?"

The silence that followed had texture to it. Weight. Wick felt it settle over his shoulders like a cloak cut from cold velvet.

*Oh,* said the Whisperer, and there was something new in its voice now, something that made the fine hairs on his arms lift and his heartbeat stumble. *Oh, little one. Do you know what you have just done?*

"I've offered myself."

*No.* The voice curled closer, delighted, and delight from a thing like this was worse than anger. Anger he could have run from. *You have prayed. Don't you see? You climbed my mountain in the dark, afraid, alone, carrying gifts you could not afford, and you gave them up gladly for love of another. That is not a bargain, child. That is* worship. *Pure and uncut. Do you know how long it has been since anyone worshipped me honestly?*

The temperature dropped. Or Wick's blood did; he couldn't tell anymore. Frost feathered across the flat stone of the offering-table where his burnt bread sat untouched.

"You're supposed to take the deal," he whispered. "Take me. Let her go. Let Maren go."

*Maren.* The name rolled around the dark like a marble in a goblet. *Ah. The little vessel. You think I took her as punishment. No, no. She is not a hostage, little one. She is a* cup. *When her mother prays for her waking — and oh, how that woman prays, hours upon hours, such rich, sweet despair — I drink. Every prayer is a sip. Devotion is the only food there has ever been, child. Blood is nothing. Blood is a door. Prayer is the meal.*

Wick's hands had gone numb. He told himself it was the cold.

"So if Freya gives you her blood—"

*I drink deep, yes. A feast. A coronation. And then, when the door stands open and my brothers and sisters pour through — why, imagine the prayers then. Whole kingdoms on their knees. War-prayers are the richest of all, thick with terror and hope.* The voice sighed, luxurious, terrible. *Your Freya understands this. I think she has always understood. It is why she refuses me so beautifully. Her refusals are delicious too, you know. Righteous defiance is a vintage.*

The world tilted. Wick grabbed the edge of the offering-table to keep his feet, and the stone was so cold it burned.

"You're not going to take the deal," he said. "There was never a deal."

*There is always a deal,* the Whisperer said, almost kindly. *But you misunderstand what is being bought and sold. You came to sell yourself. Very well. I accept. Come inside, little counter-of-heartbeats. Come and be devoted.*

The darkness in the shrine's mouth moved. Not wind. Not shadow. It *moved*, the way a throat moves when it swallows, and Wick — gutter-quick, alley-trained, alive precisely because he had always known when to run — turned and ran.

* * *

He ran down the mountain the way water runs, taking falls that should have broken bones, sliding on frost-slick stone, catching himself on roots and outcrops until his palms were ribbons. Behind him — above him, inside him — the voice laughed, and it was not angry, and that was the worst thing. It laughed the way a cat watches a mouse reach the wall.

*Run, then,* it said, conversational, unhurried. *Run home to her. Every step you take running back to the woman you love, you love her harder. Every breath you spend terrified for her feeds me. Did you think devotion meant kneeling? Oh, child. Devotion is fear for the beloved. Devotion is sleepless nights. Devotion is a boy bleeding down a mountainside because he cannot bear for her to be hurt. You have been feeding me since you left the village. You are the richest morsel to come up my mountain in a century.*

Wick stopped. He couldn't help it. His legs quit beneath him and he sat down hard on the frozen path, blood dripping off his fingertips onto the stones, and he stared back up at the small dark wound of the shrine against the graying sky.

"That's not fair," he said. His voice cracked in the middle. Twelve years old, and the crack ran right through the middle of him. "That's not — you can't just *eat* caring about people. That's not how it works. That's cheating."

*It is exactly how it works,* the god said, and for one moment the warmth fell away and Wick heard what was underneath: hunger, vast and old and bottomless, a hunger that had eaten every name off its own shrine. *It is how it has always worked. Every prayer ever prayed was fear wearing the mask of hope. Your kind built us out of your terror of the dark, and now you are surprised that we are hungry. Go home, little one. Love her loudly. Grieve beautifully. I will be listening.*

And then it was gone, and the mountain was just a mountain, and the dawn came up thin and gray over the Thornfells like it did every morning without caring what had happened beneath it.

Wick knelt in the frost and was sick, and then he cried, briefly and furiously, wiping his face on sleeves already stiff with blood, and then he did the thing that Freya had trained into him without either of them knowing it: he counted his heartbeat until it slowed. Sixty. Fifty-five. Steady.

The body tells the truth. The truth was: he had failed. The truth was: there was no trade to be made, no heroic arithmetic, no way to buy Maren back or buy Freya free, because the god didn't deal in trades. It dealt in meals. And the truth was the worst truth of all, the one that sat in his chest like swallowed ice:

Caring about someone was a door, and the god walked through doors.

* * *

He reached Hollowmere at dusk, filthy, limping, hands wrapped in strips torn from his shirt. Freya was waiting at the village gate, and the look on her face when she saw him coming down the road was a thing he would carry for the rest of his life — first the flash of pure fear, then the fury, then, underneath both, the terrible tenderness she tried so hard to keep locked away.

"You left," she said.

"I went to the Quiet Place." There was no point lying. She read lies off people the way he read weather off the sky. "I tried to trade myself for you. For Maren."

For a moment she said nothing at all. Then she took his ruined hands and turned them over, examining the scrapes with a taster's eye, cataloguing damage, and her jaw was tight enough to crack stone. "And?"

"And it doesn't work like that." His voice shook. He hated that it shook. "It doesn't *want* deals, Freya. It wants — it eats the praying. It eats the loving. Maren isn't a hostage, she's a cup, her mum's prayers pour into her and the god drinks them, and it said—" He stopped. Made himself say it. "It said I fed it just by running back to you. Being scared for you. That *counts*. That's food."

Freya went very still. Wick watched her take it in, watched the knowledge land behind her eyes and rearrange the whole shape of the war she'd been fighting. When she spoke, her voice was low and flat and more frightening than any shout.

"It fed on your love for me."

"Yeah."

"Then it has been feeding on mine for years." She closed his fingers gently around the cloth-wrapped scrapes, holding his hands shut like she was sealing letters. "Every night I couldn't sleep wondering if Corvane's men had found you. Every time I taught you herb-lore and worried I was painting a target on you. Wick — I've been laying a table for this thing since the day I took you in."

They stood in the gate-shadow while the village behind them lit its fires one by one, and somewhere a woman was praying over a breathing, empty-eyed girl, pouring devotion into a cup that a god drank from, and Wick understood at last why Freya carried herself like a woman walking on a frozen lake. It wasn't the poison. It had never been the poison. It was this — knowing that everything you loved could be turned into a leash on you, and choosing to love anyway.

"We can't fight it by wanting things," he said slowly. "Wanting is how it gets in."

"No," Freya said. "We can't." Something moved behind her eyes then, some piece clicking into place, some plan assembling itself out of grief and granite. She looked east, toward the lowlands, toward Thornfell, and her expression was the one she wore when she'd identified a poison and was calculating the dose that would kill it.

"Wick," she said, "how would you feel about a journey? There's a man I need to understand — Corvane — and I think the answer to this god might be buried in whatever the crown knows about the seal. And I think," she added, with a grimness that was almost a smile, "that it's time we stopped hiding in these hills and started hunting."

Behind them, far off toward the river country, a horn sounded — once, twice, the rebel signal for *prisoner taken* — and a rider came pounding up the valley road with news that would change everything.

But that was an hour yet. For now there was only the gate, and the fires, and Freya's hand brief and warm on top of his head like a blessing, and Wick leaning into it, learning the shape of love's limits — and of what lay beyond them.
