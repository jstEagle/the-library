## 22. Corvane's Confession

They had taken his boots.

That was the detail that irritated Lord Aldric Corvane most, in the first hour of his captivity, and the fact that it irritated him told him something useful about himself. Not the ropes—ropes were a professional courtesy, a language he spoke fluently. Not the blindfold, though that had been lifted an hour ago. Not even the cell, which was less a cell than a shepherd's bothy with a new lock and a floor that smelled of lanolin and old smoke. It was the boots. Someone had decided he should not have his boots, and that someone had thought about it, had weighed the small humiliation against the practical risk of laces and hidden blades, and had chosen to make him sit barefoot on a mountain floor like a penitent.

He respected the thinking. He intended to find out who had done it and, when circumstances permitted, reward them. Or bury them. With Corvane the two impulses lived comfortably in the same house.

The bothy's single window was shuttered, but light came through the gaps in the planking in thin gold blades, and by those blades he could track the afternoon dying. Somewhere beyond the wall, men argued in the lowland dialect and then in the mountain speech, which meant the rebels had lowland defectors among them, which meant discipline was already fraying at the seams of ideology. Good. Frayed things could be picked apart. Corvane had built a career on the principle that every knot loosens if you know where the fraying started.

His hands were bound behind him with hemp, not wire. Sloppy, or merciful, or both. His ribs ached where they'd worked him over after the ford—not out of cruelty, he judged, but out of fear, which was worse. Fearful men hit harder and stopped sooner, and they remembered afterward that they had stopped, and shame curdled into hatred, and hatred was a currency he knew how to spend.

Footsteps outside. Two sets, one heavy and deliberate, one lighter, favoring the left side. The lock turned—the new lock, bought from a Thornfell smith, because the rebels had not trusted the old one—and the door swung open on the smell of peat smoke and wet wool.

The heavy one came in first: a broad woman with a burn-scarred jaw and a sword she carried like a tool rather than a weapon. Maera Duncastle. Corvane had a file on her three fingers thick. Former quarry foreman. Lost a brother to a crown levy. Organized the Ashford toll riots without losing a single person to the noose—a feat of logistics Corvane privately admired the way a chess master admires an opponent's gambit. She was the kind of rebel who frightened chancellors, because she did not want glory. She wanted functioning granaries and open roads, and she would burn a garrison to get them.

The lighter footsteps belonged to a man Corvane did not have a file on, which was itself information. Young, lean, mountain-born, favoring his left leg as though the ford had given him a souvenir. He stood by the door with his arms folded, doing a poor job of looking indifferent and a good job of looking angry.

"Lord Corvane." Maera pulled the room's only stool closer and sat, elbows on knees, close enough that he could smell woodsmoke in her hair. "You're wondering why you're still breathing."

"I assumed," Corvane said, "that killing me would be wasteful, and your people are not wasteful. I've read your supply ledgers."

Something flickered across her face—annoyance, or the effort of not being flattered. "You've read our ledgers."

"I've read everything I could get. It's my trade." He shifted against the wall, making the movement slow and unthreatening, letting them see the ropes and remember them. "You captured me at the ford with a dispatch case containing nothing but grain requisitions and road surveys. If I were carrying anything worth hanging for, you'd have found it. So either I'm a fool who travels light, or I'm worth more than paper. You've concluded the second. I'm curious what you intend to do about it."

"You talk like a man selling something," the young one said from the door.

"I am," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "But not yet. First I'd like some water, and then I'd like to tell you a story. The story is free. What you do afterward is your own affair."

Maera studied him the way she must once have studied a quarry face before setting the charge—looking for the seam, the flaw, the place where the whole mass would come down. Corvane let her look. He had spent thirty years learning how to be looked at. There was the version of him that was a cold instrument of the crown; there was the version that was a self-made man who'd clawed up from a customs clerk's desk through sheer diligence; there was the version that was tired, that was loyal to the realm rather than the throne, that understood the mountains' grievances better than the queen's own council did. All of these were true. None of them were the whole truth. He offered her whichever layer her eyes asked for, and her eyes, after a long moment, asked for water.

She nodded to the young man, who fetched a clay jug and held it to Corvane's mouth himself, deliberately, so that drinking became another indignity. Corvane drank without hurry and thanked him by name—or rather, by the name he guessed. "Thank you, Tam."

The boy stiffened. He wasn't in any file. Which meant he was new, or local, or Freya Underhill's creature. Given the way the rebels' camp had reorganized around rumor since the ford—given the word *sigil-bearer* that moved through their fires like sparks through dry grass—Corvane would have wagered heavily on the third.

"Now," he said, settling back, "the story. I'll ask you to hear all of it before you decide what I am. It concerns the queen, the gods, and a woman whose blood is worth more than every mine in the Thornfells."

"The taster," Maera said flatly.

"The taster," Corvane agreed. "Freya Underhill. Yes. You've been sheltering her, feeding her, calling her by a title she didn't ask for. And you believe she's yours. Your miracle-worker, your sigil-bearer, your answer to three hundred years of crown theft." He let a pause sit there, delicate as a scale finding its balance. "I'm here to tell you what she actually is. And then you can decide whether you've been protecting a treasure or guarding a fuse."

* * *

He began, as he always began, with the part that was true and verifiable, because a lie stands longest on a foundation of stone.

"The old gods are real. You know this now; half your villages have seen wells sweetened and fevers broken, and the other half has seen what happens when the price comes due. The Whisperer beneath Thornfell keep is awake, and it is hungry, and it feeds on devotion the way a leech feeds on blood—patiently, invisibly, and always leaving the host weaker than it found them. Three hundred years ago, the Underhills helped seal those gods beneath dragon-glass, and the seal was made with their blood, and only their blood can open it. This much your grandmother's generation knew, and mine, and we buried it under census rolls and land grants because knowing it served no one."

"And the crown?" Maera said. "What does the crown want with it?"

"The crown wants what dying crowns always want." Corvane closed his eyes briefly, and when he opened them, he gave her something rare: the sight of Lord Corvane choosing his words with visible care, as though the sentence were a bridge over a gorge and he meant to cross it carrying weight. "Queen Maren is dying. You may have heard. The cough that began last winter has gone into her lungs, and the physicians give her until midsummer, perhaps. Her heir is nine years old and beloved by exactly no faction strong enough to hold the realm together when she goes. The eastern lords smell blood. The lowland guilds are arming their factors. There will be a war of succession whether anyone wants one or not—it is already beginning in the margins, in the smuggling and the tariff raids and the burned tollhouses. Your rebellion is one fire in a burning house."

"So she wants a weapon."

"She wants a *deterrent*," Corvane corrected, and here his voice changed—flattened, became almost gentle, the tone of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "Understand me, Mistress Duncastle. I have served four monarchs. I have read every secret dispatch this kingdom has produced for twenty years. I am not a cruel man, whatever your people say around their fires. I am a man who has looked at the future with clear eyes, and the future is a succession war that will kill more smallfolk than every plague of the last century combined. Unless something exists that makes war impossible. Unless one side holds a power so absolute that no lord dares raise a banner."

"The gods," the young one said quietly.

"The gods," Corvane confirmed. "Bound, controlled, aimed. The old texts are specific—the seal doesn't merely imprison, it *channels*. A bearer of the Underhill blood, properly prepared, standing at the seal with the proper rites, does not simply open the prison. They become the valve. The hand on the spigot. Whoever holds the sigil-bearer holds the leash on powers that flattened mountains when they walked free." He smiled, and it was the smile he used in council chambers, the one that made hardened ministers look away. "Imagine it. No succession war. No eastern lords carving up the river valleys. No mercenary companies selling the same village twice. One power above all powers, held by the crown, and every border in the world quiet for a hundred years. That is not madness, Mistress Duncastle. That is the only rational endgame available to a dying queen who loves her son."

"And you?" Maera's voice was gravel. "What do *you* get?"

"Order," Corvane said simply. "I am fifty-three years old. I have spent my life keeping this realm from tearing itself apart by inches, and I have watched the inches add up anyway. I would see it stop. If the price is one woman's blood in a ritual she'll survive—" he spread his bound hands, an odd, eloquent gesture, "—then I confess the arithmetic does not keep me awake at night."

* * *

It was a lie, of course, in the way that mattered. The arithmetic kept him awake constantly. But not for the reasons they'd assume.

Because here was the thing Corvane had never said aloud, the thing that sat in his chest like a swallowed coal: he had met Freya Underhill eleven times before the ford, and every time, he had liked her.

Not desired—desire was a lever he knew how to pull and distrusted precisely because it pulled back. Liked. She was the only person at court whose mind worked the way his did: cataloguing, weighing, surviving. He had watched her taste poison at the queen's table with the same disciplined calm he brought to reading a traitor's letters, and he had thought, *there, that one understands the world correctly.* When he sent her the honeyed wine after her grandmother's death—a small mercy, genuinely felt—he had meant it. When he tested her with the poisoned dish, he had hated the necessity of it the way a surgeon hates the knife.

And none of it changed the plan. That was the horror of himself that Corvane examined now, barefoot in a shepherd's bothy, while two rebels decided his fate. He could like a person entirely and spend them completely. The liking cost nothing. It purchased everything. Somewhere along the decades, his conscience had learned to live in a separate room from his work, and the two never spoke, and the arrangement had been so comfortable for so long that he had mistaken it for peace.

He wondered, idly, whether the Whisperer felt the same way about its worshippers.

"So," Maera said, dragging him back. "You're telling us this why? To warn us off her? To make us hand her over before you come take her?"

"I'm telling you because you will learn it eventually, and I would rather you learn it from me, accurately, than from panic and rumor." He leaned forward as far as the ropes allowed. "Here is what happens next, and you may verify every piece of it. The queen is dead by now or nearly—I left court before the end, because the regency council convenes within days, and I will be named regent protector whether I'm in a dungeon or a throne room. When I am, I march on the shrine with two thousand men and a cage of blood-iron forged to hold a sigil-bearer alive and intact. Alive, Mistress Duncastle. Intact. Because dead blood opens nothing, and damaged blood opens it wrong, and a seal opened wrong releases the gods *unbound*—no valve, no leash, no hand on the spigot. Just the flood."

He let that image settle. He watched it land on the young one first—Tam, going pale in the doorway—and then on Maera, whose jaw tightened but whose eyes stayed hard, calculating, the quarry-foreman's eyes running load calculations on his words.

"You think I'm lying."

"I think you're very good," Maera said.

"I am very good. That's not the same as lying." He tilted his head toward the shuttered window. "You have a spy in my baggage train—you took my dispatch case, so you have my cipher keys, and if you haven't broken them yet you will within the week. Break them. Read the correspondence with the royal artificers about the cage. Read the requisition for blood-iron, forty pounds of it, drawn from the foundry at Greywater. Read the letter where I instruct the cage-smiths that the interior must be padded, because bruising the vessel degrades the rite. *Padded*, Mistress Duncastle. Does that sound like a man planning murder? It sounds like a man planning a harvest, and harvests require the crop be kept sound."

Silence. Outside, a bird called twice and went quiet, embarrassed by the company.

"Why tell us about the regency?" the young one asked. "You're handing us your battle plan."

"Because your battle plan is bad," Corvane said, with genuine regret. "You mean to fortify the shrine entrance and fight two thousand regulars in a pass you can hold. You might even win—the pass favors you, Emberwake favors you, assuming the dragon still answers your sigil-bearer. But winning costs you the flower of your fighting men, and while you bleed in the pass, the eastern lords cross the undefended lowlands, and the war you hoped to prevent happens anyway, larger and uglier. Whereas—" and here he paused, and gave them the gift he had actually come to give, the one wrapped inside all the others, "—whereas if the regency falls to someone else, someone less patient, less precise, less *restrained* than I am—Chancellor Vess, say, who proposed simply cutting the sigil out of her and grinding it into the rite—then Freya Underhill dies in a cellar and your mountains burn, and no one writes songs about it because no one is left to sing."

He sat back. His shoulders ached. He was suddenly, profoundly tired, in the way he permitted himself to feel only when alone, and this was nearly alone.

"So there it is. My confession, as your people call it. The crown means to use her blood as a leash. I mean to be the hand that holds it, because I am the only candidate for the position who will not break what I hold. Kill me, and you roll dice with Vess. Free me, and I owe the mountains a debt I pay in grain tariffs and garrison withdrawals. Keep me, and I am a hostage you cannot afford to feed." He smiled again, smaller this time, almost human. "I've laid out the board honestly. It's the only courtesy I have left. Choose."

* * *

Maera stood, and for a long moment she said nothing, and Corvane understood that the silence was not indecision but architecture—she was building her answer carefully, the way he built his sentences, and he waited with something like professional respect.

"You talk about her like a resource," she finally said. "A vessel. A crop. You sat there and told us you *like* her, and in the same breath you described padding her cage." She crouched to his level, and her scarred jaw was inches from his, and her voice dropped to something almost private. "I've known men like you my whole life. Quarry owners. Tax men. You don't hate the people you crush. That's what makes you worse than the ones who do. You'll spend her and sleep fine, and call it saving the world."

"Yes," Corvane said. "Probably."

The honesty of it seemed to strike her harder than any argument would have. She straightened, looked at the young one—Tam—and jerked her chin toward the door.

"We're not deciding anything tonight," she said. "Get him water. Get him his boots back, too—if we hang him, he hangs with dignity, and if we don't, I want him walking." At the door she paused, hand on the frame, and did not turn around. "One question, Corvane. Answer it true, because I'll know. The girl. The taster. When you watched her all those years at court—did it ever once occur to you to just *leave her alone*?"

Corvane considered the question with the seriousness it deserved. He thought of Freya in the queen's shadow, tasting death in small measured sips so that others might eat safely, thanked by no one, invisible by design. He thought of the honeyed wine. He thought of the padded cage, drawn in his own hand, in his own cipher.

"No," he said. "It occurred to me to protect her. It's not the same thing, but I confess I've never understood the difference."

Maera left. The door shut. The lock turned.

In the dark, alone with the smell of lanolin and cold ash, Lord Corvane began, methodically, to work his thumbs against the hemp—not to escape, not yet, but to keep his hands supple, the way a musician keeps his fingers. Outside, somewhere down the mountain, he could hear the rebels' fires, and beneath them, faint and rising, the sound of singing—one of the mountain hymns, the ones about the sigil-bearer who would deliver them.

They sang her name like a prayer. And Corvane lay back against the wall, barefoot and patient, and thought: *they have no idea what prayers cost here.*

By morning, he knew, they would move him deeper into the mountains—to the rebel stronghold, to where Freya was. And that suited him perfectly. Every plan he had ever made had begun exactly like this: bound, underestimated, and already inside the walls.

Let them carry him to her. He had a confession still to finish, and this time, he intended to deliver it in person.
