## 23. The Rebel Table

The croft they'd given her had been a shepherd's once, and it still smelled of lanolin and old smoke, as if the walls themselves remembered the flocks that had huddled here through a hundred winters. Freya stood in its hearth-room at dawn with flour dusted to her elbows and understood, with the slow certainty of a taster reading poison in a spoonful of broth, that she was afraid.

Not of Corvane. Not of the god beneath the shrine, whose whispers she had learned to recognize the way one recognizes a draught under a door—a coldness in the room, a wrongness in the quiet. She was afraid of a table.

Specifically, the long plank table that stood outside the croft's door, dragged up from the burned village of Harrowmere by six men who had not spoken to her while they carried it, and set beneath the rowan tree where the mountain folk held their councils. Tonight the factions would sit at it. Tonight they would decide whether the sigil-bearer was a weapon to be spent or a woman to be trusted—and they would do it over food she had made.

She had cooked for queens. She had cooked for poisoners, for priests, for a spymaster whose smile she could still feel on her skin like frost forming. She had tasted death in a hundred disguises and swallowed it with steady hands. But she had never once cooked for anyone because she *wanted* them fed.

The distinction should have been a small thing. It was not.

* * *

Wick found her elbow-deep in the dough trough, attacking the barley flour as though it owed her money.

"You're kneading it wrong," he said, hauling himself onto the stool by the window the way he did everything—loudly, sideways, as if the world were a wall he intended to climb whether or not anyone offered a handhold. He was eleven, sharp-boned, missing two teeth, and had appointed himself her shadow sometime around the flight from court without ever asking permission. "Ma used to say you fold it toward you and push away, like you're angry at it but forgiving it."

"I'm not angry at it."

"You're angry at something." He peered into the trough. "You're strangling the bread."

Freya looked down at her own hands. The dough had gone tough and sullen beneath them, worked past softness into something dense and defensive. She laughed—short, surprised out of her—and let go.

"Fine," she said. "Show me."

He slid off the stool, rolled his sleeves with enormous ceremony, and took over the far end of the trough. His hands were small and quick and certain. Watching him work was like watching a wren build a nest: no wasted motion, no doubt. His mother had died in the fever winter; his father in the mines before that. Nobody had taught him to cook so much as survival had beaten it into him, and yet there was no bitterness in the way he folded the dough toward himself and pushed it away. Anger at the bread, but forgiving it.

"Where did you learn the honey cakes?" he asked, not looking up. "The ones you made last night. For the Harrowmere children."

"From my grandmother." The answer came easily now, though three months ago it would have stuck in her throat like a fishbone. "There was a recipe book. Burnt at the edges. She wrote in the margins—substitutions, mostly. *If no honey, use boiled rowan berries and lie about it.*" She smiled despite herself. "That was very much her philosophy."

"Was she a good cook?"

"She was a good liar who happened to be a good cook. Which is most of the same trade." Freya wiped her hands on her apron and went to the fire, where the mutton simmered with wild garlic and the last of the dried thyme. The broth smelled honest—no masking agents, no antidotes lurking beneath, nothing but fat and herb and salt. It still amazed her sometimes, cooking a thing she did not need to test. Her whole life had been a conversation with danger conducted through food, every dish a negotiation, every meal a small war she fought invisibly so that other people could eat in peace.

Tonight there would be no tasting cup. No silver needle. Just food, and people, and whatever happened between them across the wood grain.

"They'll fight," Wick said, with the flat pragmatism of a child who had watched adults ruin things his whole life. "The Hollowmen and the Ford clan. They'll fight even with your bread in their mouths."

"Probably."

"Then why bother?"

Freya considered the question honestly, which was more than she'd managed to consider anything honestly for the first twenty-six years of her life. She lifted the pot lid. Steam rose, fragrant and ordinary.

"Because I've spent my whole career proving food can carry poison," she said finally. "I'd like to spend one evening proving it can carry something else."

* * *

They came at dusk, when the rowan berries had gone dark against the sky and the first stars pricked through the smoke-haze above Thornfell.

The Hollowmen came first—six of them, lean and hard-eyed, led by a woman named Serra Vane who had been a gamekeeper before the crown's tax collectors turned her into something else. She wore her grief the way she wore her knife: close, plain, always within reach. Behind her walked her people, and Freya catalogued them the way she catalogued everything, automatically—the limp on the tall man, the burn scarring on the youngest girl's forearm, the way none of them sat until Serra did. Discipline born of loss.

Then the Ford clan, eleven strong, loud where the Hollowmen were silent, led by Tam Ford, a blacksmith with forearms like bridge cables and a voice that had clearly been designed for shouting across a river. They filled the bench seats, joked with each other, and looked at Freya exactly as long as politeness required—which is to say, they looked at her the way farmers look at weather that might turn.

Last came the shrine-folk, the ones who had begun leaving offerings at the forgotten altars even before the gods woke, led by an old woman called Mother Aldith who walked with a hazel staff and regarded Freya with eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. Aldith believed in the sigil-bearer. That was precisely what frightened Freya about her.

Twenty-odd people. Two factions with blood between them—Ford men had been beaten by Hollowmen enforcers back when the Hollowmen still served the crown's writ in the mountains, and the scars had not faded from either side. A third faction that worshipped the ground she stood on and terrified her worse than either. And a table between them all, laid with the only argument she knew how to make.

The mutton went down in steaming bowls. Dark bread, torn rather than cut, passed hand to hand. Honey cakes from her grandmother's burnt-edged book. Beer brewed by the Fords, tart with bog-myrtle. Freya did not sit at first. She moved along the table filling bowls, and she watched, and she listened with the trained ear of a woman who had spent years reading a royal household through its silences.

She heard the Hollowmen go quiet when the Fords laughed too loudly. She heard a Ford woman mutter *tax-men* under her breath as Serra passed, and heard Serra pretend not to. She saw Mother Aldith watching all of it, patient as stone, waiting for the cracks to show.

And then Wick dropped a full bowl of broth into Tam Ford's lap.

It was entirely an accident—the boy tripped over the shepherd dog that had adopted the croft and was now underfoot everywhere—and the scalding liquid spread across the blacksmith's thighs and the bench in a spreading stain, and the whole table went silent, and Wick stood frozen with the empty bowl in both hands, white-faced, every orphaned instinct telling him what came next. Freya had seen it a hundred times in the servants' hall below the queen's keep: the flinch, the bracing, the blow or the word that landed harder than any blow.

Tam Ford looked down at his soaked trousers. Then at the boy. Then—heaven help her—he started to laugh.

"Well," he boomed, "that's the finest welcome I've had since my wedding night. Sit down, lad, before you drown me proper." And he hauled Wick onto the bench beside him, and pulled off his soaked outer coat, and kept eating with his shirt sleeves rolled up as if nothing in the world were amiss.

Something loosened in the table. Freya felt it happen the way she felt a poison take hold—first in the shoulders, then the hands, then the voices. A Hollowman asked the Ford brewer what he put in the beer. The Ford brewer told him, lying cheerfully about the bog-myrtle proportions, and the Hollowman called him a cheat, and they argued about it with their mouths full, and by the time Freya finally sat down at the table's end with her own bowl, Serra Vane had eaten half her bread and Mother Aldith was laughing at something, low and rusty, like a door long unopened.

Nobody thanked her aloud. Mountain folk didn't, she was learning; thanks was a coin they distrusted. But Tam Ford pushed the last honey cake down the table toward her without a word, and Serra refilled her bowl from the pot herself, and when the Hollowman and the Ford brewer's argument wound down into companionable grumbling, Serra said, to the table but looking at Freya:

"The taster cooks better than the queen's own kitchens, I'd wager."

"She'd know," someone said. "She ate enough of it."

The laugh that went around the table was rough and real, and Freya found, to her astonishment, that it didn't sting. It was the first joke anyone had made about her trade that wasn't a blade wrapped in humor. It was just humor. She picked up the honey cake and bit into it and thought: *this is what it tastes like.* Belonging. Warm and faintly sweet, with rowan berries standing in for the honey, and a lie told kindly about it.

* * *

Later, when the bowls were scraped and the beer had gone down to dregs and the talk turned—as it inevitably turned—to roads and soldiers and the regency's army gathering in the valleys, the table became a war council again, and Freya became useful instead of merely present.

"The ford at Stonebridge is watched," Serra was saying, tapping the scarred plank with one finger as if the map lay invisible upon it. "Corvane's riders cross twice a day. We can't move grain, we can't move wounded, we can't move *anything* south of the river without—"

"The smuggler road doesn't use Stonebridge," Freya said.

The table turned to her.

"There's a drover's path that crosses at the willow shallows, half a mile upstream. It floods in spring but it's knee-deep now. There's a hollow on the north bank—old charcoal burner's camp, still has a chimney stone—big enough to hold thirty people and a cart if the cart's broken down and hidden under bracken. You cross at dusk, rest in the hollow, cross the high pasture at dawn when the mist comes off the river and nobody sees past their own boots." She said it evenly, the way she'd report a dish's ingredients. "I've moved contraband that way for nine years. Silk, brandy, letters worth killing for. Corvane's riders have never once caught me on it."

Silence, of a different texture than before. Serra Vane studied her with those keeper's eyes, and Freya watched the recalculation happening behind them—not *the sigil-bearer*, the myth, the miracle the shrine-folk wanted—but *the smuggler*. The professional. Something a soldier could actually use.

"You've known this road the whole time," Serra said, "and you're giving it to us now."

"I've known it the whole time," Freya said, "and until tonight I wasn't sure it was ours."

The honesty landed harder than flattery would have. She saw it take hold around the table—saw Tam Ford nod slowly, saw the young Hollowman with the burned arm sit up straighter. Mother Aldith alone said nothing, but her grip shifted on the hazel staff, and Freya caught the old woman watching her with an expression that was not quite approval and not quite warning, but some older cousin of both.

"The willow shallows," Serra said at last, committing it to memory. "Dusk crossing, dawn pasture. Good." She paused. "You'll guide the first convoy yourself?"

It was not quite a request. It was also not quite an order, and Freya recognized the difference, and understood that this too was trust being built plank by plank, the way Wick built his loaves. Folded toward her. Pushed away. Angry at the world, but forgiving it.

"Yes," she said. "I'll guide it myself."

* * *

The factions left in ones and twos as the fire burned down, the Fords singing something bawdy about a miller's wife, the Hollowmen silent and swift into the treeline, pausing only to nod—actually *nod*, two of them—at Freya as they passed. Mother Aldith lingered last. She stood at the edge of the firelight, staff planted, and looked at Freya for a long moment.

"You fed their bodies," the old woman said. "Now feed their hope, sigil-bearer. It starves faster."

"It's not mine to feed," Freya said. "I'm a cook. I'm a taster. I'm not—"

"You are what the Underhills always were," Aldith interrupted, gently and implacably, "which is more than you know yet." And she touched the head of her staff to the doorstep, an old gesture Freya almost recognized, and went down the path into the dark.

Wick appeared at her shoulder, yawning, carrying the last stack of bowls. "Aldith's strange," he announced.

"Aldith knows things."

"So do you. You knew that drover's path like you'd walked it yesterday." He squinted up at her. "Have you? Yesterday?"

"No. Years ago. Many times." She took the bowls from him. "Go sleep. Dawn convoy means early work."

He went, but stopped at the croft door. "The bread was good tonight," he said, with the gravity of a state pronouncement. "Even the part Tam wore."

Then he was gone inside, and Freya stood alone in the cooling yard with the rowan tree black against the stars, and let herself feel it—all of it, the laughter and the nods and the honey cake passed down the table, the terrifying weight of having chosen these people and been, impossibly, chosen back.

She began stacking the bowls in the basket to carry in, and that was when she saw the packet.

It lay on the table where Mother Aldith had been sitting, tucked half beneath the woodpile of clean plates: a bundle of papers tied with waxed thread gone brittle with age, wrapped in oilcloth that had protected it through decades at least. Someone had left it deliberately. Freya untied the thread with fingers that had gone suddenly cold, and opened the oilcloth, and the moonlight fell across a sheaf of letters in a cramped, slanted hand she knew at once—had known since childhood, since the burnt-edged recipe book with its margins full of kind lies.

Her grandmother's writing.

The topmost letter was dated forty-one years ago, and began: *To whoever keeps this after me—if you bear the mark, read all of it before you touch the glass, because I have learned what happens to those who don't.*

Freya stood in the empty yard with the letters trembling slightly in her hands, listening to Wick breathe behind the croft wall and the river running somewhere in the dark toward Stonebridge and the war, and she did not go inside. She sat down on the cold bench, drew the fire's last light close, and broke the seal.
