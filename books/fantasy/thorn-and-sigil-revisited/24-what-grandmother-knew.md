## 24. What Grandmother Knew

The croft smelled of dust and rosemary and forty years of absence.

Freya had swept it clean twice since coming back to the mountain—once with a broom, once with her hands—but the smell of her grandmother lived in the stones themselves, in the soot of the hearth where the sigil was carved, in the cedar of the little chest that sat beneath the window like a sleeping animal. She had opened that chest a dozen times since learning what the mark on her shoulder blade meant. She had found dried herbs gone to powder, a christening gown folded with military precision, a wooden rattle worn smooth by generations of Underhill gums.

She had not found the letters until now, because she had not thought to look *under* the false bottom, because she had never been a woman who assumed the people she loved were hiding things from her.

That, she reflected, kneeling on the cold floor with the loose board lifted and a bundle of oilcloth in her lap, had been her first mistake as an Underhill. Assuming honesty was simply how the world worked.

"Wick," she called. "Bring the lamp closer."

He came at a run, the way he did everything—fourteen years old and all elbows, hair that refused any arrangement but chaos, a smudge of ashwort stain on his fingers from the morning's lessons. He set the lamp on the floor beside her and crouched, peering at the bundle with the naked hunger he brought to everything new. "Is it treasure?"

"It's paper."

"Paper's treasure." He said it with the conviction of a boy who had owned nothing until three months ago, who still sometimes woke in the night and checked that his blanket was real. Freya's chest did its usual complicated thing when he said things like that—the thing that felt too much like love to be comfortable—and she turned back to the bundle before he could see her face do something soft.

The oilcloth crackled like old leaves. Inside were letters. Dozens of them, tied in three bundles with string gone brittle, the paper foxed and soft at the edges. The topmost letter bore no address, only a single word in a hand Freya knew better than her own mother's, better than her own: *Maren.*

Her grandmother's name for her granddaughter. Not the queen. Just Maren.

"She wrote to me," Freya whispered. "All those years, and she never sent them."

"Maybe she couldn't." Wick settled cross-legged beside her, uninvited and entirely welcome. "Maybe they weren't letters meant to be read yet."

"Everything in this family is meant to be read later," Freya said. "Later, later, later. Later is where we keep all our truths." She broke the string.

* * *

The first letters were ordinary, and that ordinariness nearly undid her. Her grandmother writing to a granddaughter sent down the mountain at seven years old, sold to the crown's kitchens as taster-stock because the family needed the coin and the crown needed someone expendable with good blood. *I hope you are eating well, though I know you are the one who makes sure of it for others.* *Your mother sends her stitches; she is making you a coat for winter, though I told her the lowlands are warm.* *Do not forget the names of the herbs. A plant forgotten is a friend lost.*

Freya read them aloud, some of them, because Wick liked hearing about the croft, the goats named after queens, the way frost came down the valley like a slow white tide. She read until her voice went rough, and then she kept reading, because stopping would mean thinking, and thinking would mean feeling, and there was a third bundle waiting whose earliest letter was dated the winter her grandmother died.

No. Not quite. She checked again. Dated two months *before* her grandmother died. Which meant the old woman had known something was coming. Had sat at this hearth, in this room, with the sigil carved over the fire behind her, and written letters to a granddaughter she knew might come looking.

*My Maren. If you are reading this, then the mark has woken, or someone has made you afraid enough to come home and dig. Either way, forgive an old woman her cowardice. I should have told you at seven. I should have told your mother at seventeen. But telling is a door, and doors let in the wind.*

Freya set the letter down. Her hands were steady—taster's hands, trained against tremor—but something behind her sternum was not.

"Freya?" Wick's voice, small.

"Keep the lamp lit," she said. "This may take the night."

*You will have learned by now what Corvane's people believe, if he has found you. That the Underhill blood opens the deep prison below Thornfell. That our blood is the key. They are half right, which is worse than being wrong, because half a truth is the shape of a knife.*

"The Underhills were never sacrifices," the letter went on. "We were keepers. There is a difference wider than the whole mountain between them. A sacrifice is poured out and spent. A keeper holds the lock shut—and the sigil is not a key to the door, child. It is the hand upon the door. It can open. It can also close. It can also *make fast*, which is neither opening nor closing but a third thing, the thing my grandmother's grandmother died doing, and why our line is thin now as winter milk."

Wick had gone very still beside her. In the lamplight his eyes were enormous. "Close the prison," he breathed. "It can close it?"

"So she says." Freya heard her own voice and disliked it: flat, guarded, the voice she used at court when a dish was suspect and she wanted no one to know she suspected. Hope was a flavor she had trained herself not to taste first. Hope was how poisoners got you.

But her grandmother had never lied to her. Not once in thirty-one letters of goat news and herb lore. And this last bundle—

She read on.

*Here is what the songs get wrong, and what Corvane will never understand, because men like him think power is a thing you take rather than a thing you tend. The seal was made of three parts. Dragon-fire, to burn the gods small. Stone, to hold the shape. And blood, to remember—to be the living memory of the binding, generation after generation, so the prison would never be forgotten into weakness. We are not fuel, Maren. We are memory. The sigil doesn't open the door any more than your name opens your mouth. It lets the door *know* who stands before it.*

*A bearer can open it. A bearer can close it. And a bearer can do the third thing, the sealing-fast, which binds the door to a life—not to a bloodline, but to one life, one heart, one death. My grandmother did it. It is why the prison held through the plague years when every other ward in the world failed. She gave her whole life to it, not just her blood. She aged a year for every season after. She chose it. No one made her. That is the part Corvane cannot comprehend, and the part you must never forget.*

Freya stopped reading aloud. Some sentences she read three times, silently, her lips moving.

*The price, then, since you deserve it plain. To seal fast costs everything the bearer has that is hers alone. Not her life entire—I will not lie to frighten you—but everything singular in it. The mark burns out of the skin and into the door. The bearer lives, mostly. But she lives as a woman with no inheritance left, no gift, no strangeness. Ordinary. The fire passes out of her forever, and no Underhill born after carries it either, because it does not pass down a line once it has been spent whole. Seal the door fast, and you are the last. There are no more bearers after the last. Whatever comes for our blood afterward finds nothing to take.*

"That's—" Wick started.

"Don't," Freya said quietly. "Let me finish the letter."

*You will want to know if it is worth it. I cannot tell you. I only know my grandmother did not regret it, and she was not a woman who suffered fools or regrets gladly. I know the gods below are waking—I have felt the mountain dreaming louder these ten years, and the bread vanishing at the shrines, and the wells going sweet, and sweet is never free. I know that if the Whisperer gets its door opened and no one seals it fast, the war that buried the world once will bury it again, and this time there is no dragon left to burn anything small.*

*I know also that you are a taster, and a taster understands what I am about to say better than any priest: everything offered has a price, and the only sin is paying it blind. So pay with your eyes open, my Maren. Open, closed, or sealed fast—choose it yourself. Never let a crown choose for you. Never let a god beg you into it. And never, never believe that belonging to something means owing it your blood. Belonging is bread shared at a table. Everything else is appetite wearing a friendly face.*

*Come home when you can. The goats miss you. I miss you. Forgive me the cowardice, and burn these letters after, or don't—an Underhill keeps her own counsel, and now, at last, so do you.*

*All my love, longer than the mountain is old,*
*Grandmother Elspet*

* * *

The lamp had burned down to a thumb's width of oil. Outside, dawn was doing its gray work on the eastern peaks, and somewhere below the croft a goat was complaining about it in the voice of all goats, which is the voice of ancient grievance.

Freya sat with the last letter in her lap and did not move for a long time.

"Seal it fast," Wick said finally, testing the words. "Everything singular. What does that—what's singular? Like... the mark?"

"The mark. The fire. Whatever it is in me that isn't ordinary." Freya looked at her hands, turned them over, palms up. Taster's hands. Poison-scarred, herb-stained, steady. "And maybe other things. Maybe the knowing. Maybe..." She didn't finish. She had been thinking, since the burned village, since the shard of dragon-glass had sung under her grief like a struck bell, that whatever lived in her blood was growing, not fading. That the sigil was less a birthmark now than a banked coal.

"And if you do it, there's no more bearers. Ever. Your kids, their kids—" Wick caught himself, went red, plowed on with the courage of the young. "Nobody after. If the door ever needs opening again, ever, there's no one."

"No one," Freya agreed. "A door sealed fast against everything. Including need."

"Is that bad?"

"I don't know." And she didn't, and the not-knowing sat in her like a swallowed stone. "That's the terrible arithmetic of it, Wick. Close it fast, and we're safe forever, and frozen forever. Leave it merely closed, and someday another bearer might be needed—and there won't be one. Open it, and the gods walk free, diminished maybe, but free, and free gods are just weather, just luck, just mercy or cruelty with no leash." She laughed, short and without humor. "Every road costs. Grandmother was right. The only sin is paying blind."

"You're not blind," Wick said. Fiercely, the way he defended things. "You've seen more than anyone. You taste everything first. That's the whole—that's your whole *life*, tasting first so nobody else has to."

Freya looked at him. Fourteen years old, orphaned twice over—once by fever, once by Corvane's fire—and sitting in a dead woman's kitchen at dawn defending her right to make an impossible choice. Something in her chest cracked along a seam she hadn't known was there.

"Come here," she said, and hugged him, brief and hard, the way she did everything she couldn't say. He endured it with the graceless pleasure of boys, and then pulled back and pretended to study the letters.

"There's more bundles," he said. "The middle one. When'd it stop?"

Freya untied the second bundle and scanned the dates. The letters ran from her grandmother's fiftieth year to her sixty-eighth. The final letter in the middle bundle was dated eighteen years ago—the year Freya's mother died, the year Freya, eleven years old and already sworn to the queen's table, had not come home for the funeral because the court would not release her.

She almost didn't open it. But her grandmother had asked forgiveness for cowardice, and cowardice, Freya decided, was not a family trait she intended to inherit along with everything else.

*My Maren. Your mother is gone. I know you could not come. I know what they take from you, year by year, and I am sorry, sorry, sorry. Here is what I can give instead: the truth, early, so that when it finds you, it finds you prepared.*

*Corvane came to me today. Yes, that Corvane, young then, hungry then, smiling the way cats smile. He had census rolls in his saddlebag and my family name underlined in them, and he asked me questions about the shrine below the keep as if he were asking about weather. I gave him a foolish old woman's answers, all goats and rheumatism. But he will come back, or his kind will, and they will keep coming as long as the blood lasts. So listen: the rolls only prove descent. They cannot prove the mark wakes. Only the mark knows when it wakes, and it wakes for three reasons—when the prison weakens, when dragon-fire draws near, and when the bearer's blood is spilled near the door. Guard all three. Spill nothing near the deep places. Trust no kindness from the lowlands that arrives without a bill attached.*

*And one more thing, the most important, the thing I have saved for last because it is the hardest: the shard. There is a piece of the prison-glass in this house. There always has been, keeper to keeper, so the door would know us and we would know it. It is in the hearth. Behind the third stone, the one that sits crooked no matter how often I straighten it. If the day comes when you must choose, take the shard with you. It is heavy beyond its size and it remembers everything. It will not lie to you. Nothing else in this world can promise that.*

Freya was on her feet before she finished reading, crossing the room to the hearth, to the sigil carved black into the stone above it, to the row of hearthstones she had cleaned and scrubbed and never once counted properly.

Third from the left. Crooked. She had noticed it the first day and straightened it, and it had sat crooked again by evening, and she had blamed the settling of an old house.

Her fingernails found the mortar line. The stone came away easier than any stone in any wall she had ever moved, as if it had been waiting eighteen years for exactly this grip.

Behind it, wrapped in leather gone stiff as bark, lay a shard of glass the length of her forearm, black as a starless sky, and warm.

Not sun-warm. Not hearth-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, warm from the inside, and as her fingers closed around it—as her blood touched it for the first time in the presence of her grandmother's letters, her grandmother's truth, her grandmother's long patience—the shard shivered.

And far below, deeper than the cellar, deeper than the roots of the mountain, something vast and dying turned over in its sleep and dreamed, for the first time in three hundred years, of a woman's hands.

* * *

They walked out onto the croft's high ground as the sun cleared the eastern ridge, because Freya could not stay in that room a moment longer with the knowledge humming in her bones. Wick carried the letters bundled against his chest like an infant. Freya carried the shard wrapped in her scarf, and even through the wool she could feel it—alive, attentive, *remembering*.

Below them the valley lay in morning gold: smoke from the hearthfolk's chimneys, the river running bright toward the ford where men had died, the green scars of Corvane's burning already softening at the edges the way wounds do when the living refuse to stop tending them. Somewhere south, past all that gold, Thornfell keep sat on its crag like a black tooth, and in it a regent who believed he was owed her blood, and a dead queen's ambition wearing his face.

"He'll march soon," Wick said. "For the shrine. Everyone says so. The rebels are digging in at the ford and the pass, but everyone says he'll come with ten times what they've got, and the cage." He glanced at her sidelong. "The iron cage they built. With the drains."

"For the blood," Freya said. "Yes. I know."

She looked down at the shard in her arms, and it seemed to look back—or not the shard itself, but what slept behind it, what her grandmother's grandmother had bound herself to, what waited patient as stone beneath the keep: a door that knew her name, a fire that remembered her family, a choice with three roads and every one of them paid in full.

Open. Close. Or seal fast, and be the last.

"We go down tomorrow," she said. "Ahead of him. Ahead of everyone." Wick's head came up sharply, questions crowding his face, and she shook her head—not *no*, but *not yet*. "Tonight we bake. Every loaf in the croft. If I'm walking into the deep dark to argue with a god and a spymaster over the price of my own blood, I'm not doing it hungry, and neither is anyone who ever fed me."

Wick grinned—the first grin either of them had managed since the letters began—and turned toward the kitchen.

Freya stayed a moment longer on the high ground, the shard warm against her heart, the morning wind combing through grass that her grandmother had planted and her grandmother's grandmother before her.

*Choose it yourself,* Elspet had written. *Never let a crown choose for you.*

Far to the south, faint as a lie, she could almost hear the sound of hammers: an army building a cage, a regent sharpening his patience, a door below the world beginning, very slowly, to dream of opening.

She turned and went down to bake bread.
