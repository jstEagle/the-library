## 25. The Queen's Last Feast

Queen Maren of Thornfell had not been able to keep down a full meal in eleven months.

She knew the number precisely because she counted everything now—spoons of broth, hours of sleep, drops of the tincture that dulled the burning in her belly without ever quite putting it out. Counting was what remained of power when power itself began to slip through a body like water through cupped hands. She counted, and she ruled, and she dressed each morning in the full weight of the crown because a queen who looked frail was a queen who was already dead; she simply hadn't stopped moving yet.

The feast was her own idea. That much she would insist upon to the grave, and it seemed increasingly likely the grave was listening.

"Solstice," she said to the mirror while her ladies pinned the sable mantle at her shoulders. "Everything happens before solstice."

The woman in the glass was still recognizably Queen Maren—still tall where she could manage it, still gray-eyed and sharp-boned, still wearing the silver thorn circlet that had sat on four generations of her house's heads. But the skin over her collarbones had gone translucent as parchment, and no amount of rouge could convince anyone that the color in her cheeks came from health. Her physicians called it a wasting of the humors. Her herbalist called it nothing at all, because Maren had dismissed him for saying the word *cancer* within her hearing.

She did not fear the word. She feared the thing it named, which was already seated inside her like an unwelcome guest who had learned the layout of the palace better than she had.

"Your Majesty." The chamberlain appeared at the door, bowing to exactly the correct depth. "The tables are laid. Lord Corvane asks whether you wish him presented before or after the first course."

"After," Maren said. "Let him watch me eat. He thinks I don't notice how he counts my bites."

The chamberlain's face performed innocence so smoothly that she almost admired it. Almost. Eleven years on the throne had taught her that everyone in this palace was performing something—the chamberlain his loyalty, the ladies their grief at her illness, the cooks their ignorance, and Corvane most of all, performing devotion with the meticulous care of a man polishing a blade he intended to use.

"Then the court awaits," she said, and rose, and let the crown settle its cold familiar weight against her temples.

* * *

The Long Hall of Thornfell Keep had been built for a kingdom richer than the one it now served, and every feast held there was a kind of argument with history: look, we are still great, see the candlelight on the plate, see the boar glazed in honey and bitter oranges, see the queen in her sable laughing at some ambassador's joke. Maren had given that argument forty times since her diagnosis. Tonight she gave it for the forty-first, and she did it well, moving down the line of dishes with her tasting cup held high, pausing to praise the spicing, to touch the shoulder of the young lord whose father had died at the border last spring, to say the word *solstice* often enough that it began to sound like a promise rather than a deadline.

Freya Underhill tasted from the same dishes, three paces behind and one step to the left, as she had for nine years.

Maren watched her without appearing to. That, too, was a skill the crown had taught her: the sideways eye, the attention that never settled long enough to be caught. The taster moved through the feast like a piece of furniture that had learned to walk—a plain woman, dark-haired, unremarkable, swallowing poison twice a day so that the crown could eat safely. Maren had never once thanked her. Gratitude, she had learned early, created debt, and debt between a queen and a servant curdled into presumption.

But lately Maren had begun watching her differently. Lately, when Corvane spoke of the taster in his soft, unhurried voice—*the bloodline is confirmed, Your Majesty, the old rolls leave no doubt, she is the last Underhill*—Maren found herself looking at the woman and seeing not furniture but fire. A hearth-fire, banked and small, but fire all the same. The fire that had sealed the gods beneath the mountain. The fire that might, if the whispering priests were to be believed even one part in ten, burn a sickness out of a dying queen the way a hot knife burns rot from a wound.

Her stomach clenched around its emptiness. She lifted her cup and smiled at the ambassador from the Salt Marches and thought: *You are eating off plates made safe by the last drop of dragon-blooded line in the world, and none of you know it, and soon I will know it entirely.*

The boar course passed. The honeyed pears. Between them, Corvane rose from his place at the lower end of the high table and made his circuit of the hall, exchanging words here, laughter there, a hand on a shoulder, a coin pressed discreetly into a serving-man's palm. Maren tracked him the way a hawk tracks a field mouse that has learned to move only under cover. He was good. He was very good. In twenty years he had never once been caught at anything, which was itself the most damning evidence of his guilt that existed.

He returned to her side as the last course was cleared, and leaned close enough that his words reached her alone beneath the noise of the hall.

"You are pale, Majesty."

"I am always pale. It is fashionable."

"It is dangerous." His voice stayed warm, conversational, a man remarking on weather. "The hall is full of eyes. Half of them belong to men doing sums about what happens when the throne empties."

"And the other half?"

"The other half are mine."

Maren laughed—it cost her more than she showed—and turned her head so that the hall saw a queen sharing a private jest with her spymaster. "Walk with me, Lord Corvane. I find I want air, and you find you want whatever I'm about to give you. We shall both be satisfied."

* * *

They walked the gallery above the great hall, where the old kings hung in paint and gilt, and where the windows looked north across the sleeping city toward the black mass of the Thornfell mountains. Maren stopped at the third window, the one her father had favored, and looked out at the peaks going violet in the dusk.

"Do you know what I dreamed last night?" she said.

"No, Majesty."

"A door." She kept her voice level, almost idle. "A door under a mountain, and behind it a hunger the size of a country. And in front of it, a woman with a mark on her arm, holding it shut with nothing but her own blood. I woke with my hands bleeding from gripping the bars." She turned to face him. "I have not told the physicians that dream. I have told no one but you. Consider what that means about how frightened I am."

Corvane's face did the thing it always did—softened into concern without ever fully committing to it, like a man trying on gloves. "Dreams are the mind's tax collectors, Majesty. They come to collect on what we've hidden. You have been carrying the knowledge of the sigil alone for too long. Let me carry some of it."

"No." She said it flatly, and watched the flicker cross his eyes—there and gone, quick as a fish turning. There it was. There was the ambition, naked for half a heartbeat before the velvet came down over it again. Twenty years, and he still couldn't quite hide it from her. Perhaps he didn't try as hard anymore. Perhaps he had begun to count her bites too.

"Majesty—"

"I am dying, Corvane." She said the word plainly, at last, in the gallery where only painted kings could hear it. "You may stop arranging your face. I have known since midwinter, I have perhaps until spring planting, and every hour I spend pretending otherwise is an hour stolen from the one task that matters. So we will not speak of it gently, because gentle is for people with time."

For once—for the first time in their long acquaintance—he did not immediately answer. The silence between them filled up with the sound of the feast below, the murmur of two hundred voices dining on safety bought with another woman's blood.

"The Underhill woman," Maren said. "Freya. Bring her to me. Before solstice."

"She is in the mountains, Majesty. Among the rebels, if our reports hold. The roads—"

"I did not ask for a report on the roads. I asked for a woman." Maren stepped closer, and was gratified to see him hold his ground only through visible effort; there were advantages, occasionally, to being a dying thing. People flinched from death even when it wore a crown. "You will deliver her to me whole, unharmed, and willing—or appearing willing, I am not particular about the difference—by the night of the solstice. Her blood opens the seal. My physicians believe—" she paused, allowing herself one flicker of the fury she kept banked at all times, "—my physicians *hope*, which is the currency they trade in, that what opened the prison can open other things. A body is only a prison of a smaller kind."

Corvane studied her for a long moment. When he spoke again, his voice had changed register—lower, more careful, the voice he used for truths he usually withheld.

"Majesty. What the blood does, no one knows. The records are ash and rumor. If it can open a seal, it can unmake a body. It can wake things that make your dream-hunger look like a lapdog. I have spent twenty years keeping this kingdom alive by knowing what not to touch. This is the largest *not* I have ever seen."

"And if I die untouched?" Maren smiled, and knew the smile was terrible, and spent it anyway. "Then your princess-ward takes the throne at thirteen years old with you as regent, the gods wake whenever they please, the rebels hold the mountains, and the Salt Marches renegotiate every treaty we have. I have read the same ledgers you have, Corvane. I taught you half of what's in them. Do not mistake a dying woman for a blind one."

Something shifted in his posture then—something small, a settling of weight from one foot to the other, a man coming to rest on a decision. Maren had spent a lifetime reading bodies the way other people read letters, and she read this one clearly: *she will not last past winter; the girl is malleable; the regency is nearly in reach.*

"There is another matter," Corvane said mildly. "The succession. Princess Elira is young, and the council grows restless. Perhaps—if the solstice brings success—we should discuss arrangements that would spare the princess the burden of—"

"Get out."

The words cracked across the gallery like a whip. Above them, a painted king with a broken nose looked down at them both with indifferent gilded eyes.

Maren's hands were shaking, and she clasped them at her waist to hide it, and hated him for making her hide anything. "You forget yourself, my lord. You forget *whose* regent you would be. Touch one hair of my daughter's future and I will spend my last living breath destroying you—I am dying, Corvane, which means I have nothing left to lose and therefore nothing you can threaten me with, which makes me the most dangerous creature in this kingdom. Remember it. Now get out of my sight before I remember that I once had you flogged for less."

He bowed—deeply, correctly, the picture of chastened service—and withdrew down the gallery without hurry, because hurrying would have admitted fault, and Corvane never admitted anything.

Maren stood alone among the dead kings and watched him go, and felt the sickness turn over in her gut like a thing settling deeper into its den.

*He means to replace her,* she thought. Not tonight, perhaps not this month, but the arithmetic was done and the ledger balanced. A regent did not need a queen dead; he needed her merely irrelevant, and irrelevance could be arranged with committees and councils and the slow erosion of being *consulted*. He would bring her the Underhill woman, because bringing her served him too. And then, somewhere between the opening of the seal and the spending of the blood, the question of who controlled the fire would be answered—and Corvane had never once lost a contest over control.

"Then I will spend faster than you steal," she murmured to the empty gallery. "I have been a miser of myself long enough."

Below, the feast was ending. She could hear the servers stacking plate, the ambassadors taking their leave, the ordinary machinery of a kingdom chewing through its evening. Somewhere north, in the mountains, a taster with a birthmark shaped like a flame was hiding from the most patient man in Thornfell.

Maren pressed one hand flat against her stomach, against the guest that never left, and made herself a promise in the language she trusted best—not prayer, not hope, but command.

"Solstice," she said aloud, to the painted kings, to the mountains beyond the glass, to whatever hungry thing slept beneath them and listened. "Before solstice. All of it."

Far away, under the mountain, something heard her.

It had been listening for centuries, and it was not particular about whose prayers it answered, or what it charged.

And in the shadow of the gallery stairs, Lord Corvane paused, drew out a small notebook, and wrote three lines in his precise, unhurried hand:

*The queen will not see spring.*
*The girl must be taken before the old woman spends her.*
*A regent's authority is best established early.*

He closed the book, smiled faintly at the dark, and went down to finish the evening's work.
