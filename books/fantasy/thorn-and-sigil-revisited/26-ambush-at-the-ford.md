## 26. Ambush at the Ford

The river spoke first.

Freya had learned to read water the way other people read letters—every riffle a sentence, every slack pool a held breath—and the Greywater at Miller's Ford was saying something wrong. It came to her as she crouched in the alder scrub above the crossing, Wick's shoulder warm against hers: a hollowness beneath the usual chatter of the current, the sound of a river running over stones that had been recently moved.

"Someone's been rearranging the shallows," she murmured.

Wick squinted down at the water, silver-grey in the hour before dawn. "To make it look shallow where it's deep?"

"To make it look deep where it's shallow." She touched his arm. "Look at the foam lines. They break wrong on the near side. There's a bar of gravel someone piled up under the surface—shallow enough to wade, if you didn't know better. And if you did know better—" she nodded toward the far bank, where the willows grew too thick and too conveniently, "—you'd funnel everyone into the deep channel beside it."

Below them, the rebel column was making ready to cross.

There were perhaps sixty of them—Hearthfolk from the high valleys, lowland deserters, two one-eyed brothers who'd once run brandy for her grandmother, old Marta with her herb-basket and her terrible singing. Dagan Ashfell led them, a quarryman turned captain with a voice like falling slate, and he was standing hip-deep in the reeds now, gesturing his people forward into the grey water. Behind them, three miles back along the river road, lay the burned husk of Hollowmere and its unburied dead. Ahead, across the Greywater, lay the forest roads that would carry them north to Emberwake's mountain and whatever safety a dragon's shadow could offer.

And somewhere between here and there, Freya knew with the cold certainty of long experience, Corvane's soldiers were waiting.

She had tasted that certainty all night, lying awake in the bracken like a fox with a snare-scent in its nose. She had spent eleven years reading poison in food and treachery in faces; she knew what an ambush looked like before it sprang. The ford was too quiet. No herons fishing the shallows. No cattle coming down to drink from the village that should have been stirring on the far bank. Even the midges were absent, as though the whole river valley were holding its breath.

"Dagan," she breathed, and slid down through the alders.

* * *

He didn't want to hear it.

"We've no time to go round," he said, not unkindly, which was worse than shouting. His people were already wading in, skirts and trouser-hems knotted up, children passed hand to hand above the water. "The queen's riders are behind us. You said so yourself."

"The riders are behind us because they *want* us here." Freya kept her voice low, urgent, pitched only for him. "Dagan. I have carried poison in my mouth for the crown for eleven years. I know what a trap tastes like, and this ford tastes of iron and oil. Look at the willows on the far bank. Look at how still they are. There are men in them."

"There are men everywhere we could go. That's what a war is." He turned to wave the next family forward. "We cross now, quick and quiet, and we're in the trees before their scouts can—"

The horn sounded from the ridge.

It was a hunting horn, a court horn, sweet and clear and utterly wrong in the wild morning, and it broke over the valley the way ice breaks over a pond—one crack, then everything giving way at once. Arrows came off the eastern slope in a dark hissing arc. The willows on the far bank stood up and became soldiers. And out of the reeds downstream, where the water lay flat and innocent, rose the poles of pikes and the round steel heads of Corvane's river-guard, forty men at least, wading in from their hiding places with the unhurried confidence of people who had set a table and were merely waiting for guests.

"Screens!" Dagan roared, and the word went down the column, and the rebels did what desperate folk do—they made walls of their bodies around the children and the old ones and began to back toward the bank they'd just abandoned.

Too late. Too slow. Freya saw it all in the space of a heartbeat, laid out with the terrible clarity of a dish she'd already tasted: the arrows falling among the rearmost families, old Marta going down with her herb-basket spilling feverfew across the shallows, the river-guard closing the downstream gap in disciplined silence while the horsemen on the ridge began to pick their way down, patient as butchers.

Corvane hadn't come to slaughter them. That was the worst of it. He'd come to *harvest*—to drive them into the deep channel, take as many alive as possible, and let the rest serve as an example. She could see it in the geometry of his dispositions, the same careful plating, the same presentation-over-substance logic she'd watched him bring to a hundred court dinners. Everything arranged. Nothing wasted.

"Wick." Her voice came out flat and calm, the taster's voice, the voice she used when the poison was already in her mouth and panic would only speed it to her heart. "The alder stand, upstream. There's a path behind it—the drovers' cut. Do you remember me showing you?"

His eyes were huge, fixed on the arrows, on Marta's basket bobbing away downstream. "The—the goat track, through the—"

"The drovers' cut. It comes out above the ford on the far side, behind the willows, behind *their* line. It's wide enough for a cart; it's wide enough for sixty frightened people if they go in single file and don't stop." She gripped his collar. "I need you to get to Dagan and tell him. Not shout it—*tell* him, close, in his ear, and tell him it's from me and that I swear it on my grandmother's grave. Can you do that?"

"He'll never listen to—"

"He'll listen because you're small and fast and because he hasn't got anything left to lose. Go."

Wick went. She watched him streak along the bank like a wren escaping a hawk, ducking pike-poles, sliding under a fallen alder, and then he was at Dagan's shoulder, mouth to ear, hands flying. For three endless heartbeats nothing happened. The river-guard closed another yard. A woman screamed somewhere in the middle of the column, high and hopeless.

Then Dagan Ashfell turned his head and looked directly up at the alder scrub where Freya crouched, and even across fifty yards of chaos she felt the weight of that look, and he bellowed—not the word, just her name, *Underhill*, torn out of him like a root—and began turning his whole broken column upstream against the current, against the arrows, against every instinct of a man whose road ran straight ahead.

They came up the bank in a ragged flood. Freya met them at the mouth of the drovers' cut, standing on a flat stone with the dawn breaking red behind her, and she pointed the way with her whole arm, screaming herself hoarse: *"Single file! Don't stop, don't look back, follow the white blaze on the birches—someone paint-blazed this path when your grandmothers were girls and it goes all the way to the high forest!"*

They poured past her. Children with wet hems and white faces. The one-eyed brothers carrying a wounded man between them like a sack of meal. A girl of six clutching a hen that had somehow survived the night's flight, its feathers smoking loose. Old Marta, bloodied at the temple, limping but alive, gripping Freya's wrist for half a stride and saying, "Knew you'd be the death of me or the saving, girl, never guessed both in one morning."

Freya counted them past the way she'd once counted courses, counted drops, counted heartbeats between symptom and collapse. Fifty-one. Fifty-two. The horsemen were on the flats now, and she could hear the bellowed orders—*take the leaders, take the tall one, the woman with the basket*—and she understood with a lurch that they weren't chasing the column at all.

They were looking for *her*. Someone had described her. Someone at Hollowmere, someone who'd seen her bury the dead, someone who'd carried the description to Corvane with the price of it already agreed: *a woman, dark-haired, moves like she owns the ground, the sigil-bearer.*

Fifty-eight. Fifty-nine. Dagan last, bleeding from a graze along his jaw, shoving her ahead of him into the cut. "Go, go, they're—"

The first rider came into the mouth of the path behind them.

* * *

What happened next, Freya would remember afterward in fragments, the way she remembered the worst tastings—in flashes of sensation without sequence.

The drovers' cut narrowed where it climbed, squeezed between a rock face and a drop to the river, and the horses couldn't follow more than two abreast. She heard the riders cursing, dismounting, boots on stone. She heard Dagan turn to hold the narrows with a quarryman's hammer and no thought at all for his own life. And she found herself standing on the high ledge above the path with Wick pulling at her sleeve and the whole fleeing column stopped below her, jammed and panicking, and the soldiers coming up the rock like beetles, and something in her chest simply—opened.

Not courage. She'd never trusted that word; courage was for people who had something to fall back on. This was older and colder. This was eleven years of swallowing death on schedule and being thanked with a nod, eleven years of eating ruin so that queens could dine in peace, eleven years of being furniture, utensil, *instrument*—and she looked down at the men climbing toward her with fire in their eyes and she thought: *no. Not this table. Not today.*

"Down!" she shouted, to her own people, and then she stepped to the lip of the ledge, over the heads of the climbers, and she did the thing she had sworn since the shrine, since the shard, since the ash of Hollowmere, that she would not do until she had no other choice.

She let the mark burn.

It started at her wrist and climbed her forearm like sunrise, the Underhill sigil waking under her skin, and the dragon-glass shard at her throat answered it—a soundless pressure, a note below hearing, the feeling of a vast door leaning against its hinges. Light came off her arm. Not torchlight, not lightning: a deep amber glow, the color of coals under ash, the color of the fire that slept beneath Thornfell and had slept there for three hundred years, and it poured down the rock face and lit the narrows like the inside of a forge.

The soldiers stopped climbing. Men do not climb toward that. One of them made a sound she'd only ever heard from animals in a burning byre, dropped his pike, and went backward down the path on his heels.

"*Sigil-bearer!*" someone screamed from below—not her people; one of Corvane's, a young officer with his sword half-drawn and his face gone the color of curdled milk. "*It's her—it's the Underhill woman—Gods preserve us, it's true, it's all true—*"

"Go," Freya said to her own people, quietly now, and the quiet carried farther than the shouting had. Sixty rebels scrambled up the drovers' cut past the frozen soldiers, past the glowing woman on the ledge, and not one of them looked at her the way they'd looked at her a week ago—as the taster, the cook, the quiet one who knew the roads. They looked at her the way people look at weather. At something that has decided, for reasons of its own, to spare them.

Dagan was the last up. He stared at her arm—at the sigil blazing there, at the way the light bent around her fingers like heat off summer stone—and for a moment the quarryman's pragmatism warred openly with the fear on his face, and pragmatism won, the way it always does in mountains.

"Well," he said hoarsely. "That explains the bread."

Then he took her elbow, gently, as if she might shatter or detonate, and steered her up into the trees after his people.

* * *

They walked until noon, single file along the blazed path, through birch stands and old burn-cleared slopes where the rowan berries hung like strings of coral. The soldiers did not pursue. Freya kept expecting the horns to start again, the arrows to come whispering through the canopy, but the forest behind them stayed empty, and gradually she understood why: Corvane's men had come to take a taster, and they had instead seen a legend put on flesh in front of them, and legends do not get dragged back to camp in cages. Legends get reported. Carefully. In writing. To a regent who would read the report four times and then begin, very quietly, to plan differently.

Word traveled faster than they did. By midafternoon, when the column halted at a spring to drink, there were already people waiting—scouts from the high valleys, a crofter woman with two children, a graybeard who had walked half a day on a bad knee. They didn't come for Dagan. They stood at a respectful distance among the trees and looked at Freya, and when she caught them at it they didn't look away, the way mountain folk don't, and one of them—an old woman with a face like a walnut shell—pressed her fists together and bowed, actually *bowed*, the way you bow to a shrine.

"Stop that," Freya said, more sharply than she meant.

"You lit the ford," the old woman said simply. "My grandmother told me the sigil would light again before the end. She told me I'd know it by the color. Coal-under-ash, she said. Coal-under-ash, exactly so." And she bowed again, and would not be stopped, and Freya stood there with spring water dripping off her fingers and felt the last door of her old life swing shut behind her.

Invisibility surrendered. That was the truth of it, and she turned the phrase over in her mind as they walked on, testing its weight the way she tested a suspect dish. For eleven years her survival had been built on being unseen—the taster who swallowed and was forgotten, the shadow on the edge of the queen's long table, the woman nobody thanked and therefore nobody watched. She had given that up on a rock ledge above the Greywater, freely, with her arm on fire. Every eye in the Thornfells would find her now. Corvane's. The god's. The crown's. And the eyes of these sixty-odd broken, grateful, believing people, who had already begun, she realized with a chill, to look at her the way the starving woman at the crossroads shrine had looked at the vanished bread.

"They'll want miracles," she said to Wick, quietly, as the light went long and gold through the trees.

He walked beside her with his chin up, trying to match her stride, trying very hard to seem like someone who had not spent the morning certain he would die. "You saved sixty people. That's a miracle, isn't it?"

"It's a map and a loud voice." But she put her hand briefly on his head, because he needed it and, she admitted privately, so did she. "Miracles are what the thing under the shrine sells. Remember that. Whatever they ask of me, whatever they *pray* to me for—I'm not that. I bake bread. I taste poison. I know the roads."

"Aye," Wick said solemnly. Then, after a dozen strides: "Freya? What happens when Corvane tells the regency? About the—the coal-under-ash?"

She had been avoiding the thought all day, the way she avoided thinking about symptoms until she'd catalogued them properly. Now she let it arrive, whole.

Corvane would not send forty men next time. He would not send four hundred. He would come with an army, and iron cages, and priests to declare her an abomination, and he would come soon—because a dying queen wanted a sigil-bearer delivered before solstice, and because a man like Corvane could not abide a weapon walking free in the world that he did not himself hold.

But there was another player at the table, and Freya had not forgotten it, though the thought sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. The shard at her throat had been singing since the ford—softly, steadily, a thread of sound below hearing, growing warmer with every mile they climbed northward. It had sung the same way in her dreams for three nights now. And in the dreams there was always the same thing at the end of the song: a darkness under the mountain shifting its weight, like a sleeper turning over, like something enormous beginning—after three hundred years—to wake.

They crested the ridge at dusk, and the whole column stopped.

Below them the valley opened toward the high peaks, and there, black against a sky the color of cooling embers, stood Thornfell itself—and as Freya watched, a tremor ran through the mountain's shoulder, a long slow shudder that sent a ribbon of snow-slides whispering down slopes that had been silent for centuries. The rebels murmured. Somewhere behind her the old walnut-faced woman whispered a prayer, and this time Freya didn't tell her to stop, because the shard at her throat was ringing now like a struck bell, and the mountain was answering it, note for note.

"That's not an earthquake," Wick said.

"No," Freya said. Her arm, under her sleeve, glowed faintly in the gathering dark—coal-under-ash, patient, awake. "I think it's an introduction."

Far below and far within, something ancient exhaled, and the first thread of smoke rose from a peak that had been cold for three hundred years.
