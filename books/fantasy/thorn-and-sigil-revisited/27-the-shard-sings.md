## 27. The Shard Sings

For three hundred years, Emberwake had dreamed in the dark.

Not slept—dreamed. There was a difference, and the difference was the whole of its suffering. Sleep would have been a mercy, a black and dreamless drowning. But dragons did not sleep as lesser creatures slept. Dragons kept their fires banked but never out, and so Emberwake had lain beneath the roots of the Thornfell mountains with its eyes sealed shut and its mind half-lit, turning over and over the same slow memories the way a prisoner turns a stone in his hand: the first flight, the last flight, the taste of storm-rain on the high crags, the weight of the first Underhill's palm pressed flat against the plates above its heart.

*Keep it,* that first Underhill had said, in a language that no longer existed anywhere except inside Emberwake's skull. *Keep the fire, keeper. Keep it until we come back for it.*

They had not come back.

Emberwake had counted the years the way old creatures count everything—by hunger. Three hundred years of hunger, deepening by slow degrees from an ache to an emptiness to a vast and ringing silence, the way a bell goes silent not because it stops being a bell but because there is no one left to strike it. Its fire had guttered. It had felt the flame draw inward, year by year, century by century, pulling back from its throat, from its chest, from the great furnace of its belly, retreating toward some last stubborn coal at the center of itself the way a dying hearth retreats to one orange eye among the ash.

It had made its peace with that. Or something like peace—the closest thing a creature of fire could make, which was a kind of grim arithmetic. The seal beneath the mountain held. The gods stayed behind their wall of fused glass. The smallfolk above forgot dragons entirely, then forgot they had forgotten, and told their children that the shapes in the high passes were only shadows thrown by clouds. That was fine. That was good, even. A keeper whose charge was safe could be permitted to fail. Could be permitted to gutter. Could be permitted, at the end, to simply stop.

That had been the plan. That had been the peace.

And then, two hundred days ago—or perhaps two hundred years; time moved strangely in the deep places, pooling and stagnating like water in a cave—a shard of dragon-glass had begun to sing.

* * *

It had not sung at first. It had only warmed. Emberwake had felt it the way a man feels weather change in an old wound: a faint wrongness in the bedrock far above, a thread of heat where there should have been none, threading down through stone and root and the long cold veins of the mountain. The shard had been torn from the shrine wall—Emberwake knew the seal's every grain the way a mother knows her child's face, and it had felt the theft as a wound, a missing tooth, a note struck violently out of the prison-song—and carried up into the world of rain and small lives.

Then the singing started.

It was not sound, exactly. Sound required air, and there was precious little of that down here; what air there was sat stale and mineral in the dark like water too long in a cistern. No—the song came through the stone itself, through the bones of the mountain, through the same deep channels along which Emberwake's own fire had once flowed when the mountain was young and the keepers were many. It was a thin thread of a thing, that song. Barely a whisper. A frequency at the very edge of perception, the way a dog hears a whistle that empties the room of human hearing.

But Emberwake heard it. Oh, how it heard it.

Because the song was *familiar*. Not the melody—it had no melody, or none yet—but the material of it. Dragon-glass remembered. Dragon-glass was made of fire compressed past all patience into something like stone, and what was compressed into stone could sing again if you struck it rightly, and somewhere above, in the world of rain and small lives, someone had struck it. Someone had bled on it. Someone had grieved over it with such force of feeling that the grief had soaked into the glass the way water soaks into chalk, and the old fire locked inside had woken and remembered what it was for.

Fire, Emberwake thought, in the wordless way it thought, which was less language than pressure, less pressure than tide. *Fire remembers being given.*

The first Underhill had given it. Had knelt in this very cavern, in the age before the age before memory, and pressed her marked palm against Emberwake's heart-plates, and the sigil had burned through scale into flesh into the deep muscle where the fire lived, and for one moment—one shining, unbearable moment—keeper and bearer had been a single creature with a single pulse. Emberwake had never forgotten it. It had spent three hundred years trying to forget it, because remembering was a hunger with no bottom, and now the shard above was singing that memory back to it, note by note, grain by grain, like a voice calling down a well.

Come back, said the song. Come back. Come *back*.

And Emberwake, who had made its peace, who had done its arithmetic, who had planned to gutter out like a courteous candle—Emberwake began, against every instinct of its ancient exhaustion, to climb.

* * *

It climbed the way glaciers move, which is to say: imperceptibly, and then all at once.

For a hundred days it did nothing but listen. The song grew stronger. It learned things from the song, the way a blind creature learns a room by echo. It learned that the shard was moving—carried, always carried, in a small warm hand that trembled sometimes and steadied other times. It learned that the hand belonged to a woman, because the song carried blood in it, and blood has a sex and a season and a history the way wine does. It learned that the blood was *Underhill* blood—not descended-from, not diluted-through-a-dozen-marriages, but the true strain, the keeper-strain, the sigil burning live and unbroken in a birthmark shaped exactly like the mark carved over a croft hearth in a valley Emberwake had flown over when the valley was new.

A bearer. After three hundred years. After the last of them had died in some petty border skirmish and been buried under a nameless stone, and Emberwake had felt the line go out the way you feel a candle go out in a far room—not seen, only *sensed*, a diminishment in the texture of the world.

And now this. Now a woman with the sigil alive on her skin, carrying a shard of the prison-wall, walking the mountain roads, grieving, raging, refusing bargains offered by hungry gods, burying dead villagers burned by a crown that wanted her blood for a weapon—

The song told Emberwake all of it. Not in words. In pressure. In temperature. In the particular flavor of grief that soaked through stone, which is different from the flavor of rage, which is different from the flavor of resolve. Emberwake tasted them all, day after day, and slowly, over the turning of a season, it understood what it was tasting:

It was tasting itself. Its own history, replaying in a smaller body. A keeper, alone, used by powers she never asked to serve, carrying a burden she never asked to carry, choosing anyway, choosing *again*, the way the first Underhill had chosen at the sealing and the second had chosen at the re-sealing and every Underhill after had chosen in their quiet unthanked ways until the line ran out.

The song was not calling Emberwake home.

The song was Emberwake, called home.

*So,* it thought, in the tide-way it thought. *So. Then I am not finished.*

It opened its eyes.

* * *

There is no describing what it is to open eyes that have been closed for three hundred years, and Emberwake would not have tried, even if it had possessed a tongue suited to description rather than to flame. The lids peeled with a sound like a ship breaking on rocks—scale grinding against scale, three centuries of mineral deposit cracking away in sheets—and light came in, and the light was nothing. Mere darkness, to any other eye. The faintest phosphorescence of wet stone, the grey ghost-light of deep places. But to eyes that had drunk only darkness for three hundred years, it was a flood, a sunrise, a wound.

Emberwake lay still and let the pain of seeing wash through it and recede, the way it had once let the pain of the first flight wash through it, long ago, when its wings were new and the sky was an enemy to be negotiated with. And then, when the pain had settled into something bearable, it looked at itself.

Its reflection was not available—there was no water still enough, no mirror in the deep—but a dragon knows its own body the way a king knows his kingdom, boundary by boundary, and Emberwake took the census and did not like the report. Scale gone dull, gone grey, gone loose in patches, hanging like bark off a dead tree. Wings folded tight against its spine, the membranes dry and brittle as autumn leaves, and when it flexed them—carefully, so carefully—they crackled, and one hairline split ran along the trailing edge of the left wing like a fault-line waiting for an earthquake. Its claws had grown long and curled inward from disuse, and had begun, horribly, to grow *into* the stone of its own resting-place, so that it was not lying on the mountain but grown through it, rooted, part of it, a fossil that had not yet stopped breathing.

And the fire. It reached inward, down the old channels, to take the census of the fire, and found what it had feared and half-expected: a single coal. One ember, deep in the furnace-room of its heart, no larger than a fist, glowing a sullen red-brown like a dying forge. Around it, ash. Around the ash, cold. Around the cold, three hundred years.

Once, its fire had been a lake. Once, it had breathed storms. Once, whole armies had laid down arms at the mere rumor of its shadow crossing a valley. Now it cupped its entire inheritance in the space of a clenched fist, and understood, with the terrible clarity of the very old, that it had exactly enough fire for one act.

One breath. Perhaps two. One rising. One flight, short and desperate. One kneeling.

It would have to choose what the fire was *for*.

But not yet. First—the climbing. First the song, growing louder. First the woman, coming closer, though she did not know she was coming closer, though she thought she was running *from* something, a spymaster with hounds and a cage of blood-iron, and did not know she was running *toward* the oldest thing in the mountains, the thing her whole line had served, the thing that had been dreaming her name.

*Freya,* the song whispered, in the pressure-language, using the name it had learned from the shard, from the blood soaking into the glass. *Freya Underhill. Freya, daughter of no one, taster of poisons, keeper of the keeper's mark. Come down. Come down. I have been waiting longer than your people have had names.*

* * *

The rising nearly killed it.

Breaking free of the stone that had grown through its claws was the first agony—each curl of horn tearing free of living rock with a sound like trees uprooting, and Emberwake roared then, for the first time in three hundred years, a sound that had no air to carry it and so traveled instead through the mountain itself, through the bones of Thornfell, and every villager in every valley above felt it in their teeth and their floorboards and their soup-pots and did not know what it was, only that the mountain had spoken, and fell silent, and listened.

Then the wings. This was worse. Three hundred years of brittleness, and the membranes tore in four places simply from unfolding, and warm fluid—wept fire-blood, thin and gold and precious beyond all reckoning now—ran down the joints and hissed where it struck cold stone. Emberwake wept without weeping, in the way of creatures whose grief is physical. Every tear was fuel it could not spare.

But the wings opened. However badly. However scarred. They *opened*, wide as the fall of night across a valley, and the shape of them filled the cavern end to end, and the cavern—which had been dug by the keepers of old precisely to hold a waking dragon, and had waited empty so long that even the stone had forgotten its purpose—remembered.

Emberwake stood.

The standing took an hour. Its legs had forgotten load-bearing; its balance had forgotten anything but lying down. Twice it collapsed, and the second collapse cracked a rib, and the rib's grinding protest echoed in its own skull like a struck bell. It lay in the wreckage of its own dignity and breathed—breathed out the stale air of three centuries, breathed in the thin cold clean air of the deep galleries—and then rose a third time, and the third time held.

And then it climbed the last gallery, the great stair-cut tunnel the first keepers had carved, wide enough for a dragon's shoulders, sloped enough for a dragon's grip, and it went up through the dark toward the thin place where stone gave way to sky.

The pass above was called the Kestrel's Throat. It was a notch in the spine of Thornfell, a wind-gap between two peaks, choked with scree and old snow and the gnarled pines that clung to altitude the way the poor cling to dignity. On the far side of it, in a high meadow, a woman stood with a boy and a shard of dragon-glass wrapped in cloth against her heart, and around the meadow's rim, soldiers in the livery of a regent were closing like a hand around a fly.

Emberwake did not know about the soldiers. It knew only the song, and the song was directly below, straight down through forty fathoms of rock, singing now not a whisper but a chord.

It put its head against the roof of the pass and pushed.

* * *

What happened to the Kestrel's Throat happened in nine seconds, and the people who survived it would spend the rest of their lives failing to describe those nine seconds, and their children would inherit the failure, and the story would grow in the telling until it became the kind of story that gets told in lowland taverns with a wink—*a dragon, sure, and my uncle saw it*—because the truth was too large for belief.

The mountain did not explode. Mountains do not explode; that is the business of volcanoes, and Thornfell was old and cold and proud of it. What the mountain did was *open*. The peak above the pass shrugged, the way a great beast shrugs, the way a shoulder comes out from under a burden. The scree-fields let go all at once, ten thousand tons of broken stone finding, after millennia of hesitation, a reason to move. The pines went down like grass. The snowfields sloughed and slid and turned to white thunder. And through the middle of it all, through a gap that widened from a crack to a wound to a valley of its own making, came the head of a dragon.

Grey. Grey as the stone it had slept in, grey as ash, grey as the underside of storm-cloud, so that for the first full second of its emergence it was invisible—just another mass of moving rock among the moving rock—and then it opened its jaws and exhaled, one long plume of breath that was almost smoke and almost steam and almost nothing at all, the ghost of a fire, the memory of a fire, and against that pale exhalation its outline showed, and the outline was the size of a church, and the outline had *eyes*.

Amber eyes, banked low, like coals under ash. Eyes that had watched the first Underhill kneel. Eyes that had watched the last one die. Eyes that had watched three hundred years of nothing, and were now, at the very end of all that watching, looking at the sun for the first time since the sun had last set on a world that believed in dragons.

The sun was setting now, too. Of course it was. Emberwake had timed nothing—it had no cunning left for timing—but some deep animal wisdom in it had climbed through the long night of the tunnels and broken the surface at dusk, and dusk was right. Dusk was merciful. A dragon rising into noon would have blinded the world; a dragon rising at dusk merely *stopped hearts*.

In the meadow below, the soldiers stopped theirs first. Later, the survivors would disagree about everything—how big, how grey, whether it roared (it did not roar; roaring was for creatures with fire to waste)—but on one point every account agreed: the silence. When the mountain opened and the head came through, wreathed in stone-dust and evening light, the battlefield made no sound at all. No screaming. No orders. Several men dropped their weapons, quietly, the way you set down something fragile when you realize you are in a temple. One young lieutenant sat down in the grass and began, very softly, to laugh, and could not stop, and his comrades did not tell him to stop, because they understood that laughter was simply what some bodies did when they met the impossible and lived.

And in the center of the meadow, in the trampled grass between the two armies that had come to hunt her, a woman stood very still with a bundle of cloth pressed to her chest, and did not run.

Emberwake saw her.

Three hundred years of dark, and then dusk, and then her: a small figure, human-sized, ordinary, mud on her boots and ash in her hair and a birthmark on her wrist that the last light of the sun caught and lit like a brand. The shard sang in her arms. Emberwake felt the song crest, felt it change, felt it stop being an echo and become a meeting—the way two voices singing the same note apart suddenly stand close enough to touch.

It knew her. Not her face—it had never seen her face—but everything else. Her grief, which it had tasted through forty fathoms of stone. Her rage. Her refusals. The child taken and not returned. The village burned. The bread she baked for funerals. The poisons she swallowed daily so that strangers could eat safely, a trade of swallowed harm that Emberwake recognized with a shock that went through its old body like lightning through a dead tree, because that was *keeper-work*, that was the oldest work there was, taking the poison into your own body so the world outside you might live.

She was not the first Underhill returned. She was better than that. She was the first Underhill *continued*.

Emberwake came down the mountain.

* * *

It came down the way avalanches wish they could come down: deliberately. Each step a choice. The broken wing dragged; the torn membranes streamed; the grey bulk of it poured down the shattered slope like a river deciding, after three hundred years, to remember its bed. Stone leapt aside from it. Pines that had survived the first breaking did not survive the descent. And the soldiers—Corvane's soldiers, blood-iron cage abandoned on its wagon, horses screaming and bolting, officers shouting orders that dissolved into noise—broke and scattered before it like chaff, because there is a fear older than discipline, older than loyalty, older than language, and it woke in them all at once, the fear that was written into the marrow of every human child born in the three centuries since the dragons went away: *the sky can have teeth.*

But the woman did not run. The boy beside her did not run—he stood his ground with a cook's knife in his fist, absurd and magnificent, thirteen years old and planting himself between a dragon and his person as though size were a matter of conviction. Emberwake saw him too, and catalogued him, the way it had once catalogued the small quick warm creatures who had tended the keepers' fires: *this one belongs to her. Guard this one.*

And then the ground was level, and the meadow was under its shadow, and Emberwake stood above the last bearer of the sigil in the last light of the day, close enough that its breath stirred her hair, and it did the thing it had rehearsed in the dark for three hundred years without knowing it was rehearsing.

It knelt.

The knees of a dragon hitting a mountain meadow sound like the doors of the world closing. The impact rolled outward through the earth, and the soldiers fleeing at the meadow's edge were knocked from their feet by it, and the birds went up from the forests in a black revolving cloud, and in the villages of Thornfell, miles away, pots rattled on hooks and grandmothers pulled children close and said the old words, the forbidden words, the words that had not been said aloud in living memory: *the keeper returns, the keeper returns.*

Emberwake lowered its head. All the way down. Down past its own dignity, down until its brow—the broad grey shelf of bone above its amber eyes—rested against the torn turf a hand's breadth from the woman's feet. The posture of a knight before a king. The posture of a dog before its master. The posture, oldest of all, of a keeper before the one who holds the key to its heart.

And into the silence, into the space between its own nostrils and the small trembling warmth of her, it spoke—not in the pressure-language, not in the song, but in the only way left to it, with the last of its failing fire, pushing a thread of heat up its ruined throat so that its voice, when it came, was a whisper of embers, a voice like a hearth an hour after the flames have gone:

*"Bearer."*

The word landed on the meadow like a stone into still water. The woman flinched—of course she flinched; the voice was the size of its maker—and then, Emberwake felt it through the ground, through the soles of her boots, through the shard against her heart which rang like a struck bell at the sound of its own history: she *stepped forward*. One step. Toward the head. Toward the teeth. Toward the three-hundred-year hunger wearing a mountain's body.

Her hand came up. Small. Scarred. Taster's scars, needle-scars, work-scars, the hands of someone who had fed herself to the world daily and been thanked by no one. The hand hovered over the grey brow-plate, over the place where, three hundred years and more ago, another hand had rested, and the sigil on her wrist had begun to burn—not with pain, Emberwake could see that, could smell the difference in her sweat between pain and recognition—with *light*, a thin gold tracery glowing through her skin like a lamp behind parchment, answering the dragon the way a tuning fork answers its twin.

"Emberwake," she said. Quietly. Testing the name in her mouth, the name the shard had taught her in dreams she had probably told herself were only dreams. "You're the last one."

*I am the last one,* Emberwake agreed, in the ember-whisper, and the agreement cost it more than the climbing had, because admitting lastness is the one confession no keeper ever wants to make. *I am the last, and my fire is one coal, and I have one act left in me, and I have come down the mountain to ask you what it should be.*

She could not have heard all of that. She heard enough. Her fingers touched the brow-plate, feather-light, and the contact closed a circuit three centuries open, and the shard in her arms sang one clear final note, and the dragon-glass in the ruined shrine far below them both rang in sympathy, and the gods behind the wall of fused glass—all of them, even the Whisperer, even the hungry ones—went suddenly, utterly silent, listening.

Above the meadow, the last light left the peaks. Somewhere in the scattering dark, a spymaster who had built a cage of blood-iron for a woman he thought was only a woman stood in the ruins of his certainty and began, with the terrible patience that was his whole nature, to think.

And Emberwake, kneeling, felt the small hand steady against its brow, and heard the small voice say—half question, half vow, in the tone of a woman swallowing poison and daring the world to be worth it:

"Then let's not waste it."
