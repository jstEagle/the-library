## 28. Riders and Ruin

The first thing Freya learned about war was that it smelled like a kitchen gone wrong.

Not the romance of it—the banners and the horns and the songs the rebels already sang around their fires—but the actual stink: scorched axle-grease, burnt grain, charred leather, and beneath all of it the sweetish reek of men who had died badly and were not yet buried. She had spent her whole life cataloguing smells, tasting danger in the air before it ever touched her tongue, and so she knew, before anyone told her, what the smoke rising off the King's Road meant.

"Grain convoy," said Brannoc, shading his eyes against the morning glare off the snowfields. He was an old smuggler with a face like a dried riverbed and the calm of a man who had been hunted by better soldiers than Corvane's and lived anyway. "Forty wagons, maybe fifty. Flour, salt pork, arrow-iron. Enough to feed his forward camp for a month."

"And enough to feed three villages through winter," Freya said.

Brannoc glanced at her sidelong. "That's one way of counting it."

"It's the only way I count anything anymore."

Emberwake shifted behind them on the shelf of stone, and the mountain shifted with it—a groan in the rock like a ship settling at anchor, a slide of scree that would have killed any creature smaller than itself. Three hundred years of dreaming had left the dragon thin as a sword-blade under its scales, fire banked low in a body built for conflagration, but even starving it was the largest living thing Freya had ever stood beside. When it breathed, the wind changed direction. When it looked at her—and it was always looking at her now, with those furnace-door eyes—she felt catalogued, weighed, found somehow familiar.

*You smell like her,* Emberwake had said, on the second night, meaning some Underhill grandmother dead four centuries. *But you taste like yourself.*

She still didn't know whether that was a compliment.

"The wagons will have outriders," she said, forcing her mind down from the heights where dragons lived and back onto the road below, where forty men with crossbows guarded the bread of an army. "Six ahead, six behind, pairs on the flanks if Corvane's learned anything since the ford."

"He's learned," Brannoc said grimly. "He learns faster than any man I've robbed. That's why we don't fight his outriders. We let her do it." He nodded upward—not at the sky, but at the ridge above the road, where the morning haze lay thick as wool. "One pass. Low. No flame on the wagons—we want the grain, not ash. Just the fear."

Freya looked up at Emberwake.

The dragon's head swung around, slow as a drawbridge lowering. Up close its scales were not red but the color of coals at dusk, black shot through with veins of dying orange, and here and there—along the neck, across the great wing-joints—new marks shone faintly gold. The sigil she had burned into its heart was working outward through it like ink through water, remaking the old fire into something else. Something that answered to her name when she called it quietly.

*I can be afraid for them,* Emberwake rumbled. Its voice was less a sound than a change in the pressure of the world, felt in the teeth and the breastbone. *Fear is easy. It is the oldest thing I know. But you must tell me where the line falls, taster. Which men are the disease, and which are only carrying it.*

Freya's hands curled inside her gloves. That was the question, wasn't it. It had always been the question. Every dish she'd ever tasted, every poison she'd ever swallowed, had been a wager about which deaths were necessary and which were merely convenient—and she had never once been comfortable with her own answer.

"The ones holding weapons are the line," she said. "The drivers and cooks walk away. If they run, let them run."

*And the grain?*

"The grain is ours. Winter doesn't care whose banner feeds you."

Emberwake made a sound then that she had learned to read as laughter—deep, dry, rusted, like a door opening in a house long shut. *Spoken like a woman who has starved politely for other people.* It lowered its shoulder to the stone, an invitation older than kingdoms. *Climb, sigil-bearer. Let's go ruin someone's supply lines.*

* * *

Riding a dragon was nothing like riding a horse, and everything like being swallowed.

She sat in the hollow between two neck-scales that fit her spine as if they'd been cast from a mold made of her, gripping a strap of cured hide that Emberwake had shed and offered with something like embarrassment. There was no saddle, no bit, no reins. There was only agreement, renewed moment by moment, which Freya thought was more honest than any tack ever stitched.

They went off the ridge into the haze, and the world folded away.

No scream. That was the thing the ballads got wrong, or hadn't lived long enough to get right. Emberwake fell rather than flew, wings half-furled, dropping out of the cloud like a stone with intent, and the first the wagon-guard knew of them was the shadow passing over the road—a shadow with wingspan enough to darken forty wagons at once—and then the wind of it, hot and sulfurous, flattening the grass on both verges, and then the voice.

Not words. A sound. The sound a mountain makes when it decides to move, channeled through a throat built before men built walls. The lead horses went mad instantly; the rear guard simply dismounted and ran, throwing down their crossbows without loosing a single bolt, because some part of every human soul still remembered dragons the way the body remembers falling. Two of the flank riders froze in their saddles, faces upturned, and did not run at all. Freya marked where they stood. She would find their names later, from the village rolls. She was learning to keep such accounts.

"Hold!" she shouted, and Emberwake heard her through its own bones, and landed.

Landing was worse than flying. The impact traveled up through the dragon's shoulders into hers, and the road bucked like a deck in weather, and then the great head came around over the convoy, close enough that its breath rippled the flour-dust off the nearest canvas cover, and Emberwake said—in the common tongue, in a voice like a landslide learning to speak, because Freya had asked it to use words whenever men could hear:

*This road belongs to Thornfell. The bread belongs to Thornfell. Go home to your queen and tell her the mountains have woken.*

Then it simply stood there, vast and patient, while forty men discovered that terror has a speed limit, and the wagoners—one of them hardly older than Wick—climbed down and walked east with their hands raised and their faces gray, and nobody burned, and nobody died, except the two frozen riders who made the mistake of drawing steel at a legend. Emberwake took their weapons and their horses' bridles and left the men themselves sitting stunned in the road-grass, alive, stripped of everything but the story they would tell for the rest of their lives.

By noon the rebels had fifty wagons of grain rolling up the smuggler roads toward three villages, and Brannoc was grinning like a boy, and Freya was sitting alone on a wheel-hub, shaking.

It was not fear. She examined the trembling with the detached professionalism of her trade and identified it, with some surprise, as fury—at how easy it had been. One shadow, one voice, and armed men became sheep. Corvane would learn this within the day, and he would learn the lesson she had just learned: that a dragon was not a weapon. Weapons could be aimed. A dragon was a verdict, and verdicts did not distinguish finely among the condemned.

"We won," Brannoc said, offering her a flask.

"Today," she said, and drank.

* * *

The trouble with miracles was that people started expecting them.

She saw it first in Harrowgate, the village two valleys over that had sheltered her after the burning of Millbrook. They had fed her, hidden her, mourned their own dead with her—and now they came to her door with their sick on litters and their disputes in their mouths, because she rode a dragon's shadow and therefore, surely, she could do anything.

"Widow Cass says the well's turned bitter again," the miller's wife told her, wringing her apron. "Since the fighting started. And Tam's boy, the wheezing one—he sleeps better when the dragon flies over, everyone says so. If you'd just have it circle the village once a day—"

"I'm not a priestess."

"No," the woman agreed pleasantly, in the tone of someone agreeing with a modesty that was clearly mistaken. "But you're *hers*, aren't you? The fire-bearer. It listens to you."

*Hers.* Freya closed her eyes. Somewhere in the last month she had stopped being a woman and become a relic, and relics did not get to be tired, or wrong, or small.

She tasted the well water herself, because that at least was her trade. Bitterness at the back of the throat, a mineral tang that didn't belong—she knew this taste. She'd met it once before, in the shrine below Thornfell keep, in the air around the fused dragon-glass wall. It was the flavor of the Whisperer's attention. The god was drinking again, somewhere close, feeding on the war's fresh crop of desperate prayers—and where it drank, things curdled.

"There's a price coming to this valley," she told the miller's wife. "I can't stop it by circling your roofs. Keep your children indoors at dusk. Don't pray to anything you can't see. And if a voice answers you kindly—" she gripped the woman's wrists, hard enough to make her gasp, hard enough to make her listen—"*do not thank it.* Gratitude is how it gets in."

The woman nodded the way people nod at prophecy they intend to interpret however suits them. Freya let her go and stood in the doorway watching the evening come down on the smoke of the lowlands, and felt the momentum of events like a current under a boat, strong enough now that rowing was a formality. She had wanted to protect these people. She had never imagined that protecting them would mean riding at the head of something that grew whether she steered it or not.

* * *

The news from the lowlands arrived with a peddler and a deserter, separately, three days apart, and agreed in every particular.

War fever had the river towns in its teeth. The dragon's raids—six convoys taken, a granary at Redford cracked open like an egg, the garrison at Stony Ford abandoned without a fight—had done what three years of rebel petitions never could: made the mountains real to people who had always thought of them as scenery. In taverns from Greywater to the capital, men drank to the Fire-Bearer and argued about whether she was a lost princess or a witch or the old gods' own daughter. Children played dragon-and-taster in the lanes. The broadsheets had woodcuts of Emberwake that made it look like a cathedral with teeth.

And the queen—

The deserter had stood guard outside the council chamber at Thornfell keep, close enough to hear. He repeated it in the flat voice of a man unburdening himself of something heavy: the proclamation, read in the queen's failing voice to a silent hall. *Whereas certain persons in rebellion against the crown have consorted with creatures of ancient malice, and whereas the peace of the realm is threatened by forces both mortal and otherwise, the crown does hereby declare the Thornfell territories in open revolt, and does command all loyal subjects to give neither food nor shelter nor faith to the traitors of the high country, upon pain of—*

"Pain of what?" Freya asked.

"Fire," the man said. "Both kinds."

So Maren had named it at last. Not banditry, not unrest—*rebellion*, the word that turned neighbors into categories and made burning a policy instead of a crime. Freya sat with the deserter's account all one night in the rebel camp at Hollowmere, listening to the fires crackle and the sentries walk their rounds, and understood with terrible clarity what was happening: the war had stopped needing her.

Oh, they still needed the dragon. They needed the grain, the roads, the terror that moved garrisons without battle. But the story had grown legs. In the lowland taverns she was already whatever they needed her to be; in the queen's proclamations she was already the enemy entire; in the villages she was already the miracle-worker whose shadow healed wheezing boys. Everyone had a version of her, and none of the versions asked her permission, and every day the versions fought each other in her name, and every day the real Freya Underhill—who had once been satisfied to swallow poison unnoticed and slip home through a smuggler's pass—grew harder to find in the noise.

"They'll march before solstice," Brannoc said, crouching beside her fire. "Corvane's regency or the queen's crown, doesn't matter—they need a victory over us before the harvest fails completely, or the lowlands starve either way. An army needs a story, girl. You've given them one."

"I gave them bread," Freya said. "And a road home for the drivers. That's all I've given anyone."

"Aye." Brannoc spat into the flames. "And the Widow Cass's well went bitter, and Tam's boy sleeps when the shadow passes, and the queen has declared holy war on a mountain. Nobody's listening to what you give. They're listening to what they need."

Emberwake was awake on the far side of the mere—she could feel it now, a warmth behind her sternum where the sigil's echo lived, a banked companionship that was the only belonging she trusted anymore. She rose and walked around the black water to where the dragon lay with its chin on the frozen shore, eyes half-lidded, watching the stars turn.

*You're troubled,* it observed.

"The war's running downhill without me. Toward the villages. Toward the shrine."

*Wars do that. They are like rivers—all the gathering, none of the choosing.* The great eye rolled toward her, coal-deep. *But rivers have sources, taster. Yours has two. A spymaster with a cage, and a god with a church. Cut the headwaters or drown downstream.*

"You make it sound simple."

*Nothing is simple. Some things are merely clear.* Emberwake exhaled, and the steam drifted up and erased the stars, one constellation at a time. *The god is quiet tonight. Too quiet. It fed well on the queen's proclamation—I felt it drink from here, a long slow pull, like a leech at the world's throat. When a thing that hungry goes silent—*

"—it's not resting," Freya finished. "It's building."

Below them, down in the valleys where the war fever burned brightest, the night was full of new prayers. She couldn't hear them. But she could feel them arriving somewhere, being gathered somewhere, sorted and stacked like cordwood against the coming winter—and she knew, with the certainty of a woman who had spent her life detecting poison by taste alone, that the Whisperer had stopped asking for devotion.

It had started organizing it.

Far off to the south, past three ridgelines, a light appeared on the horizon that was not dawn—it was the wrong color for dawn, too green, too steady—and it burned for exactly as long as a sermon might take, and then it went out.

Freya stood very still in the cold.

"Emberwake," she said quietly. "How many people can hear a god at once?"
