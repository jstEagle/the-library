## 30. Freya's Bargain

The shrine beneath Thornfell keep had a mouth, and Freya had walked into it once before with a lamp and a lie in her throat. This time she came without the lamp.

She did not need it. The dragon-glass shard she carried had begun to glow three days ago, softly at first, like a coal remembering fire, and now it threw a pale rose light across the descending stair that was almost warm to look at and utterly cold to touch. It lit the walls of fused black glass where the old gods had been hammered shut behind dragon-fire, lit the Underhill name carved deep in the seal-stone, lit her own hands as they gripped the railing her grandmother's grandmother's grandmother must have gripped in exactly this way, on exactly these steps, going down to do exactly this work.

*Seal-keepers,* the letters had said. *Not sacrifices. Never sacrifices.* Grandmother's hand had pressed so hard on that last word that the ink had nearly torn the page.

Freya went down anyway.

Behind her, two valleys east, Wick waited in the rebel camp with a knife he barely knew how to hold and instructions he knew by heart: if she was not back by moonrise, burn the letters, take the shard's twin—the plain river stone she'd swapped for it—and ride for Marrow's Ford and tell no one anything, ever. He had argued. He had cried, briefly, furiously, the way boys cried when they were trying not to. Then he had said, "You taught me tasters don't go in blind," and she had told him the truth: "No. Tasters go in first. That's the whole trade."

The stair ended. The prison opened before her like a wound in the world.

She remembered it from her first descent: the wall of glass, vast as a cathedral window, dark veins crawling through it like frost on a pond. But the frost had spread. Where before the veins had threaded the glass like hairline cracks in a cup, now they webbed it whole, and through them something moved—not shapes, not faces, but *attention*, dozens of eyes' worth of attention packed into pressure and hunger, all of it turning toward her the moment her boot touched the floor.

The temperature dropped. Her breath came out white. The shard against her sternum pulsed once, twice, like a second heart deciding whether to be afraid.

"You came back." The voice did not come from the wall so much as from everywhere the wall touched—up through the floor, out of the stone, humming in her teeth. It had learned voices since her last visit. It had been practicing. There was a woman's warmth in it now, a mother's cadence, the voice of someone who would sit up all night with a fevered child. "Little sigil-bearer. Little keeper. We felt you coming for three days. Your blood sings ahead of you like a kettle."

"I brought bread." She set the loaf on the flat stone before the seal. Rye, dark, dense, baked that morning over the rebel fires. "Unsalted. The way they leave it at the crossroads shrines."

"We know what bread is."

"Do you?" Freya kept her voice level, the voice she used at the queen's table when the dish in front of her might kill her and the only weapon she owned was composure. "Because the last time we spoke, you took a child instead of accepting a refusal. I've been wondering whether you understand bread at all. Bread is given freely or it isn't bread. It's theft with flour in it."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on the eardrums. Somewhere in the webbed glass, a vein brightened and dimmed, brightened and dimmed, slow as breathing.

"You are angry about the girl," the Whisperer said at last.

"Her name is Maren. Maren Ostler. Seven years old. She has a gap where her front tooth should be and she's afraid of geese." Freya had gone to see the family herself, after. Had sat with the mother who could not stop wringing a washcloth that had long since gone dry. "And you took her because I said no, and you wanted to teach me that refusal has a price. Lesson received. Now here's mine."

She stepped closer to the glass. Close enough that her breath fogged it. Close enough to see, deep in the black, the faint suggestion of movement—vast, slow, patient, like whales under ice.

"Release her. Whole, unharmed, unmarked in mind or body, back at her mother's door by tomorrow's dusk. And stop feeding on prayers—all of them. Every well you sweetened, every fever you broke, every desperate soul in these mountains who knelt down and gave you a piece of themselves because they didn't know there was a piece to give. You let them go. All of them. You close your mouth and you open your hands."

"And in return?" The hunger in the voice did not bother hiding itself anymore. It never really had; hunger was simply what the thing was, the way venom was what an adder was. "What does the little keeper offer for two such enormous mercies?"

"My blood." Freya pulled off her left glove, finger by finger, and held her bare hand up to the glass. In the shard's rosy light, the scar where her birthmark had been showed faintly—a paler ghost of the sigil, thorn-and-circle, still faintly warm. "Underhill blood. Seal-bearer blood. The one key in the world that fits this lock. I'll open the seal myself. Not Corvane with his cages and his knives, not some stolen drop in an iron bowl. Me, willingly, at this stone, with my own hand. The gods walk free. All of them. Isn't that what three hundred years in the dark buys?"

The pressure in the chamber changed. She felt it against her skin like the moment before a storm breaks, like standing under a dam. Every vein in the glass flared at once, and for one vertiginous instant she saw *through* the wall—not images, but appetites, hundreds of them, layered and ancient and ravenous, and beneath them all one appetite older and larger than the rest, the one that had done the whispering, the one that had spent centuries learning the exact frequency of human desperation.

"You would do this," the Whisperer said, and for the first time there was something almost wondering in it. "The crown sends hunters and iron. The rebels send arrows. And you come alone with bread and a demand."

"Tasters negotiate for a living," Freya said. "Everyone else at the table wants something. My job is finding out what, and what it will cost them to get it, and whether they'll still want it after they know the price." She let that hang. "So. Do we have terms?"

"Terms." The god tasted the word. "Yes. Terms. The child returned. The feeding ended. The seal opened by your hand, your blood, your willingness." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "And when it is opened, little keeper, what then? Do you imagine we will simply... disperse? Wander off into the world to be worshipped politely at proper intervals?"

"That's my problem, not yours."

"It is precisely the sort of problem," the Whisperer murmured, "that becomes everyone's."

"The terms," Freya said, "or I walk back up those stairs and Corvane gets here first, and you can negotiate with a man who keeps his blood-iron cage oiled and his knives sharper than his promises. He doesn't want you free, god. He wants you *leashed*. He wants to pour me into you like water into a winepress and pour you out again onto battlefields until there's nothing left of either of us but a tool. Is that the future you've been dreaming about under your mountain? Three hundred years of silence, and the first hand offered to you belongs to a man who sees you as ammunition?"

The silence stretched so long she counted her own heartbeats. Forty. Sixty. The shard against her chest beat with them, slightly out of time, like a nervous second drummer.

Then, slowly, like frost retreating from a window, the nearest veins dimmed.

"Terms," the Whisperer said. "Accepted."

Something in the air shifted—she felt it pass over her like a current, cold and enormous, a weight lifting off the mountain above her, off valleys she couldn't see. Far away, she somehow knew, a seven-year-old girl was opening her eyes in a dark place that smelled of violets and rot, and finding a door where no door had been.

"Swear it," Freya said. "Not to me. To the seal. To the fire that made it. Old things keep old oaths better than new ones, and you are nothing if not old."

A sound moved through the chamber that might have been laughter, if laughter could be carved from basalt. "You speak like an Underhill."

"I am one."

"To the fire, then. To the seal. To the last dragon sleeping its long sleep under the stone." The voice dropped into a register that made the floor hum, formal, archaic, terrible: *what is promised is promised; what is sworn is sunk; let the oath bind the bower and the bone.* The words settled over the chamber like snowfall. Freya felt them settle over her too, and had to school her face very carefully, because half of what she felt was relief—

—and the other half was the cold, clean steadiness of a woman who has already decided to break her word.

* * *

She climbed the stairs slowly, because her knees insisted, and sat for a while on the third landing with her back against the wall, letting the shaking come and then letting it go, the way she'd trained her body to do between courses at the queen's table. Poison swallowed. Symptoms catalogued. Antidote prepared. That was the trade. That had always been the trade.

Except this time the poison was her own promise, and the antidote was a plan she'd built out of smuggler roads and grandmother's letters and a dying dragon's fire, and the symptoms wouldn't show for days.

She took out the letters. Not grandmother's—the older ones, the ones written in a hand so faded it was more impression than ink, the ones she'd read eleven times and understood fully only on the eleventh. *The seal is not a wall,* the first Underhill had written. *It is a recipe. Fire, blood, and will, folded together. What was sealed can be sealed differently. The gods need not be caged. They need only be made small enough to live among us without devouring us—as rain is small enough to drink.*

Small enough to drink. She'd thought about that phrase for six days straight.

The Whisperer believed it had bargained for freedom. Let it believe. Let it count the hours until the seal cracked and dreamed whatever gods dreamed about open sky. When the moment came—when her blood was on the stone and the fire was in her hand and the god was leaning forward, hungry, certain—it would learn the oldest lesson in her trade:

Never trust the person whose job is to swallow poison on your behalf. She'd been swallowing the court's poisons for nine years. She could swallow her own just as easily. And she could serve a dish that looked like surrender and tasted like a trap, and smile while it went down.

That was the part that frightened her, if she was honest—and she made herself be honest, sitting in the dark on the stairs, because dishonesty got tasters killed faster than arsenic. The part that frightened her wasn't the god. It was how easy the lying had become. How natural. Small treasons, she'd called them once, and they had grown into large ones, and somewhere along the way the woman who poisoned herself daily out of weary, unthanked duty had become a woman who lied to queens and gods with a steady hand and a clear eye.

Was that corruption? Or was it just what strength looked like when it finally arrived?

She didn't know. She suspected she'd have the rest of her life to find out, assuming she had a rest of her life, which the plan assigned rather narrow odds.

She put the letters away and climbed.

* * *

Moonrise found her walking into camp, and moonrise plus ten heartbeats found Wick colliding with her at a dead run, arms around her ribs hard enough to bruise, saying her name twice and then running out of words entirely.

"I'm fine," she said, which was a lie, and noticed herself telling it, and decided to allow it, just this once, the way you allowed a child one sweet before supper.

"Maren Ostler," he said into her shoulder. "Freya. Freya, she *came back*. A shepherd found her on the road at dusk, walking home, not a mark on her, doesn't even remember being gone. Her mum sent word down the valley an hour ago. Everyone's saying—" He pulled back and looked up at her, and his face did something complicated. "Everyone's saying it was you. That you went down and barged with it and it *kept its word*."

"It kept its word," Freya agreed.

Wick studied her. Thirteen years old, orphaned twice over, apprenticed to poison and herb-lore, and sharp-eyed as a wren—he missed almost nothing, and what he missed tonight was only because she'd gotten better at hiding it. "And you're going to keep yours?"

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Around them the camp murmured—rebels banking fires, sentries calling the watch, someone laughing low near the horse lines. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who believed, as of an hour ago, that the god under the mountain could be dealt with honestly.

"Wick," she said, "go wake Brannoc and the others. Tell them the seal opens in four days, at the dark of the moon. Tell them everything goes as planned." She smiled, and it even reached her eyes, mostly. "And tell them to make sure Emberwake is ready to fly."

He hesitated—one heartbeat, two—then nodded and ran.

Freya stood alone among the fires and flexed her bare left hand, watching the ghost-sigil catch the firelight. Four days. One dark moon. One god counting hours toward a freedom she had already measured for a smaller coffin, and one dragon sleeping under the stone who would wake to find the world had finally, finally need of it.

Swallow the poison. Smile at the table. Wait for the symptoms.

She banked the nearest fire and went to sharpen her knife.
