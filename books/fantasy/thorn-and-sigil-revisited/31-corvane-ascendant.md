## 31. Corvane Ascendant

The queen died at the hour of the wolf, when even her physicians had stopped pretending to watch.

Corvane stood in the shadowed alcove beside the great bedchamber's door and watched them do it—the ritual of it, the choreography of grief that had been rehearsed in whispers for a year. The chamberlain knelt first. Then the ladies, folding down like cut stems. The royal physician pressed two fingers to Queen Maren's throat, held them there a decent interval, and withdrew them with the expression of a man closing a ledger he had long since balanced.

Corvane did not kneel. He had done his kneeling years ago, in this same room, when Maren had still been able to lift her head from the pillows and say his name without coughing between the syllables. *You will keep the realm safe, Aldric. Whatever it costs. Whatever it costs you.* She had gripped his wrist then with a strength that surprised them both, and he had understood that she was not giving him an instruction but transferring a weight—setting down a burden she could no longer carry onto shoulders she trusted to be broad enough and cold enough.

He had been flattered once by that trust. He was past flattery now. Trust was simply a tool that happened to be shaped like a hand.

Outside the shutters, Thornfell keep lay quiet under a sky the color of wet slate. Somewhere below, in the city, the bells would begin soon. He counted the seconds out of habit, and when the first bell struck—a low bronze moan rolling up from the harbor temples—he permitted himself one slow breath, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the way he taught his interrogators to breathe before they began their work.

Grief, he reflected, was like any other instrument. In clumsy hands it made noise. In his, it would make music.

He stepped from the alcove into the light of the deathbed candles, and the room turned to him as if on hinges.

"My lords." His voice carried without effort; it always had. "Her Majesty is gone. The realm does not stop for grief, and neither can we. Summon the council."

* * *

They gathered within the hour, because fear gathers men faster than loyalty ever did. Corvane took his place not at the foot of the council table where the spymaster traditionally sat, half in shadow, half in deniability, but at the queen's own chair. He did not sit in it. He stood behind it with both hands resting on its back, and let the symbolism do what a hundred speeches could not.

Lord Chancellor Vessin arrived pink-faced and breathless, clutching a scroll of succession law as though parchment were armor. "My lord Corvane," he began, "the princess-heiress is a child of six, and the law requires—"

"The law requires a regent," Corvane said pleasantly. "Yes. I have read the law, Chancellor. I helped write three of its clauses."

Vessin blinked. Around the table, the other lords performed the small arithmetic of survival: who had Corvane's ear, who owed him money, whose secrets sat in which of his famous black ledgers. Corvane watched each man arrive at his answer. It was almost tender, watching them realize they had already decided.

"There will be those who whisper," said old Lord Halloway carefully, "that the spymaster's power should not grow larger than the crown it serves."

"Then let us be grateful," Corvane replied, "that I am not asking for the crown. I am asking for the burden of it. There is a difference, my lords, and history will draw it clearly." He let his gaze travel the table, unhurried, warm even. "Queen Maren spent her last year fighting a war inside her own body while the mountains slipped toward open rebellion and something old woke beneath our feet. She asked me to hold the realm together until the princess is old enough to hold it herself. I intend to do exactly that. No more. And—" here he smiled, thin and genuine, "—considerably less than some of you fear."

It was a good speech. He had written better ones for worse men. But speeches were only the visible tenth of the work; the rest had been done over months, in the small economies of favor and fear. Halloway's son's debts, purchased quietly through a factor in the capital. Vessin's brother-in-law, whose smuggling operation survived only because Corvane's customs men looked elsewhere. The Marshal of the East, whose ambition Corvane had fed for two years like a gardener feeding a hedge he intended to shape.

By nightfall the council had voted. By midnight the proclamation was drafted: *In the name of the Princess Royal, the Lord Protector Aldric Corvane holds the regency of the realm.* By dawn, the printers' shops smelled of ink, and the word *regent* was being spoken in every tavern in the capital with the particular hush reserved for words that might get a man hanged if he pronounced them wrongly.

Corvane read the final proof copy standing at his study window, the city gray and smoking below him, the mountains a jagged black line at the world's edge. He touched the seal of office where it hung at his breast—a heavy thing of iron and gilt, deliberately plain, deliberately martial—and felt nothing so vulgar as triumph.

Triumph was for men who had wanted things. He had *needed* this, and need was cleaner.

The realm was sick. He had known it longer than most, seen it in the intelligence reports the way a physician sees fever under skin: the granaries running thin, the coin debased, the border lords building private armies, and above all the mountains—those sullen, superstitious heights where prayers were being answered now, actually answered, and answered *with prices*. He had read the reports from his listeners in the Thornfell villages with a scholar's fascination and a soldier's alarm. Wells sweetened. Fevers broken. A child returned from a ravine no living climber could have reached. And in exchange—always in exchange—something small and irreplaceable. A memory. A year of luck. A voice, in one case, taken clean from a shepherd boy's throat.

The gods were waking, and the smallfolk were lining up to feed them.

A realm could survive famine. It could survive rebellion. What it could not survive was a people who had found something to worship more reliable than their crown. Faith was the deepest aquifer, and whoever tapped it owned everything above it.

Which was why, somewhere beneath Thornfell keep, behind a wall of fused dragon-glass older than the kingdom, there waited a prison that must never be opened by accident—or by anyone but him.

* * *

The cage arrived on the ninth day after the queen's death, crated in fourteen pieces and accompanied by a master smith who would not meet anyone's eyes.

Corvane had commissioned it eight months ago, back when Freya Underhill had still been merely a curiosity—a taster with a birthmark that burned at the wrong moments, a bloodline traced through census rolls and parish records back four generations to a croft above the smoke-line. The smith had worked in a sealed yard under the old armory, sworn to silence on pain Corvane had described in detail and meant entirely. The design had come from fragments: a thirteenth-century writ authorizing "an iron vessel lined in blood-salt for the conveyance of the marked one," and a single surviving page of Underhill family papers describing how the sigil-blood responded to proximity with its own kind of metal. Iron, yes—but iron quenched in a solution of the bearer's own tears and grave-dirt from the seal itself. The old kings had understood what the sigil was. They had simply lacked the imagination to use it.

The assembled cage stood in the armory's inner hall, and Corvane walked around it slowly, the way he had once walked around a captured enemy courier, reading the whole story from the object alone.

It was beautiful, in the way instruments are beautiful. Man-high, narrow, its bars wrought into interlocking thorn-patterns that left no gap wider than a finger. The interior was padded with wool—not mercy, but preservation; bruised blood told poorly. At its base, set into the floor plate, a shallow channel ran in a spiral, ending in a stoppered drain. He crouched and studied the channel. The smith had followed the specifications exactly.

"It works?" he asked.

The master smith's throat moved. "As to the—the draining, m'lord, we couldn't—we didn't have—"

"No. Of course not." Corvane rose, brushing dust from his knee. "We'll test the channels with water. The rest we test when it matters." He laid one palm flat against the bars. The iron was cold in a way the hall's chill did not explain, a deep, mineral cold, like touching the face of a mountain. Blood remembers, the old papers claimed. Iron quenched in grief holds grief. He found the poetry of it faintly distasteful and entirely useful.

"When does it move?" asked Captain Dreth, who commanded the escort and had the blessed simplicity of a man who thought in straight lines.

"At the head of the column," Corvane said. "Visible. On an open wagon, draped in the regent's colors."

Dreth frowned. "M'lord? I'd have thought—concealment—"

"Concealment is for thieves, Captain. We are not stealing anything. We are *retrieving* a subject of the crown—a woman, incidentally, not a relic—and we want the mountains to know it." He turned, and let Dreth see the whole of his face, calm and reasonable and utterly without exit. "Every village that has whispered to a well, every grandmother who has left bread at a shrine—they will see that wagon pass and understand what the realm thinks of their new gods. Fear travels faster than cavalry. Let it ride ahead of us."

What he did not say—what no one in that hall would ever hear him say—was that the cage served a second purpose, subtler than transport. The sigil responded to its own metal; the reports from his listeners confirmed it. Within twenty miles of the blood-quenched iron, Freya Underhill's birthmark would begin to burn, whether she willed it or not. The cage was not merely a vessel. It was a compass, a lure, and a leash all at once. If she fled deeper into the hills, her own body would betray her position, flaring like a signal fire with every mile the wagon gained. She could run. She could not hide from what she was.

Corvane had learned that lesson about himself long ago, and he had never once tried to run from it. He almost admired her, in advance, for the running she was about to do. Almost.

* * *

The army mustered on the flood-meadows south of the capital: four thousand foot, nine hundred horse, two companies of arquebusiers, a siege train of six pieces hauled by oxen, and the supply columns that turned the whole into a river of mud and canvas stretching nearly a mile. Corvane reviewed them from horseback in a drizzle that could not decide whether to be rain or fog, and found them adequate. Not inspiring—adequate. Inspiring armies won battles; adequate ones won wars.

The rebel strength in the mountains was harder to count. His listeners reported scattered companies, mountain folk armed with hunting bows and heirloom steel, led by names that meant nothing yet—Harrow, the miller's daughter from Coldbrook; a defected sergeant named Pike; others rising like weeds after rain. And threading through every report, growing louder each week, the dragon.

Three hundred years the last dragon had slept beneath Thornfell, a rumor, a fossil, a story grandmothers used to frighten children off the high passes. Now shepherds spoke of tremors in the deep valleys. Now a shattered pass blocked the Salt Road where no avalanche had fallen. Now—eleven days ago—a drover had come down from the high country white-faced, swearing on every saint that he had seen it rise against the dawn sky, vast and red as a wound, and kneel before a woman on a ridge.

Before *her*. Before the taster with the burning mark.

Corvane had read that report four times. Each time, the same sentence snagged in him like a fishhook: *it knelt.* Dragons did not kneel. Dragons were force of nature given scale and hunger; the histories agreed they had been bound to the Underhill line as hounds are bound to hunters, and the histories also agreed that the binding died with the last Underhill. Unless it hadn't. Unless the blood had endured, hidden in a poison-taster's veins all these years, waiting.

Waiting for him, if he was honest about it. Everything waited for him now. That was what the regency meant—not a title but a convergence, the moment when every loose thread in the realm drew tight into his hand.

He rode the length of the columns that evening, because armies needed to see their lord and lords needed to see their armies, and both lessons were the same lesson. The soldiers cheered in the tired way soldiers do, and he acknowledged them with the raised fist that his propagandists had already begun putting into woodcuts. At the rear of the baggage train he drew rein beside the open wagon and looked for a long moment at the cage beneath its drape of regent's blue.

"You understand your purpose," he said quietly, to iron, to the grief soaked into it. "Better than she does, I think. She believes this is about her. It has never been about her. It was about her before she was born."

The rain thickened. Somewhere ahead, the mountains stood up out of the dark like a closed fist, and beyond them, beneath them, the shrine waited with its wall of dragon-glass and its sleeping, hungry tenants. He had spent thirty years learning what men feared and what they wanted and how little distance lay between the two. He knew precisely what he wanted. That was the difference between him and everyone else in this gathering storm—the queen had wanted to live, the rebels wanted their hills, the smallfolk wanted miracles, the girl wanted, presumably, to belong to someone. Wants were chains. He wore none. He had melted his down years ago and hammered them into this: purpose, plan, patience.

The regency was proclaimed from every temple steps and market cross by morning. By noon the vanguard crossed the river. By dusk the road climbed, and Thornfell's first foothills rose around them, dark with pine, silent in a way that made the veterans glance upward and touch whatever charms they pretended not to carry.

Corvane rode at the head with the cage creaking behind him on its wagon, and that night, in camp, his senior listener came to his tent with rain in her hair and urgency in her voice she did not bother to disguise.

"M'lord Regent. Word from the high valley. The rebels have moved—Harrow's company, three hundred or more, marching east along the ridgeline. Toward the shrine."

"Toward it," Corvane repeated. "Not away from it."

"They mean to defend it, m'lord. And there's more." She hesitated, and he watched her decide to say it. "The girl was seen. Two days ago, at the ford at Marlow's Crossing, riding at the head of them. Openly. They're calling her the sigil-bearer now. They're calling her—" the listener's mouth twisted around the word— "*the Taster Queen.*"

For a moment Corvane said nothing. Rain drummed on the canvas overhead. In the dark beyond the tent walls, four thousand men breathed and muttered and dreamed, and beyond them the mountains kept their counsel, and beneath the mountains something ancient turned over in its sleep.

Then he smiled, and it reached his eyes, which was rare, and therefore frightening to everyone who saw it.

"The Taster Queen," he said, tasting the words the way she had been trained to taste wine—for what was hidden underneath. "Good. Let her wear a crown in her own mind. Crowns are heavy, and mine—" he touched the iron seal at his breast— "mine is already fitted."

He turned to the map spread across his field table, the candle flame shivering over the inked lines of the high passes, and set one finger on the shrine's marked entrance, the deep door in the mountain's root.

"She's going to open it for me," he said softly. "She simply doesn't know it yet. Break camp at first light. We march through the night."
