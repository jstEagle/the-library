## 32. The Long Road Down

They left before dawn, the way Freya had left most places in her life: quietly, with a pack already weighed and a lie ready for anyone who asked.

Only this time she wasn't lying.

"The army moves at midday," Tam Verrick had said the night before, spreading his rough map across the rebel table while the fire guttered low. "Corvane wants the solstice. He'll march from the ford with three thousand men and that cage of his." His jaw tightened around the word *cage*. Blood-iron, they called it, forged in the lowland foundries, lined with runes old enough that no living scribe could read them. Built for one person. "You don't have to be here when he comes, Freya."

"I know," she'd said. "That's rather the point."

Now the pass above Hearthmere was silvered with frost, and Wick walked beside her with his hood up and his breath steaming, carrying more of their supplies than she'd allowed him to and pretending not to notice when she shifted weight out of his pack anyway.

"You could still stay," he said, for the fourth time since the village. "Verrick said—"

"Verrick said a great many things last night, most of them after his third cup." Freya picked her way over a shelf of ice-glazed stone. Below them the valley held its breath, smoke rising thin and straight from a hundred hearths, all of them lit for people who would still be alive tomorrow because an army was three days away instead of three hours. "He said he'd hold the deep door with two hundred men against three thousand. He said the mountain folk have held worse odds. He even said it like he believed it."

"You didn't believe him?"

"I believe he believes it." She glanced sideways at him. In the gray light, Wick looked older than his twelve years — thinner through the face than he'd been in autumn, but steadier in the eyes, and there was a new thing in him lately, a settled quality, like a fire banked down to coals that would last the night. It frightened her a little, how much he had come to look like someone who understood what was coming. Children shouldn't learn that particular patience. But then, she hadn't learned it either; she'd been born into it, swaddled in poison and silence. "Belief holds walls, Wick. I need something else for what's below. I need a taster's hands, and yours are the only ones I trust down there besides my own."

He absorbed that the way he absorbed everything now — silently, turning it over, testing it for poison before he swallowed. Then he nodded once and said nothing else for a mile, which was its own kind of agreement.

The road down through Thornfell was not a road at all, properly speaking. It was a succession of smuggler paths stitched together by three generations of Underhills and refined by Freya alone: a goat trail behind the waterfall at Cold Sorrow, the hollow under the roots of the Split Oak where you crawled flat on your belly with your pack pushed ahead of you, the long shale chute the locals called the Devil's Staircase because it shed climbers the way a dog sheds water. She knew every stone of it the way other people knew the lines of their own palms. She had carried death along these paths in vials no bigger than her thumb, had memorized the smell of each bend so that she could travel it blind, and she had never once, in all those years, thought of it as anything but a tool.

Walking it now, with Wick's footsteps crunching behind hers and no poison in her pack except the ordinary kinds, she felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted.

"It's beautiful," Wick said at the top of the Devil's Staircase, looking back the way they'd come. The mountains fell away in folds of blue and white, range behind range, and far below the lowlands lay hazy and golden under morning light, the queen's country, Corvane's country, the country that had used her name without ever learning it. "I never saw anything like this from the court. From the court it was just — mountains. Gray stuff between us and everywhere else."

"From the court," Freya said, "everything is either useful or in the way. That includes mountains. It includes people." She started down the first pitch, boots finding holds by memory. "The mountain folk never had the luxury of that kind of arithmetic. When your whole world is a valley, everything in it is kin or weather."

"Which are we?"

She paused. The question sat in the cold air between them, simple and enormous.

"Kin," she said. "Obviously."

Wick smiled — quickly, trying to hide it, the way he still did with anything that mattered — and came down after her.

* * *

They stopped at noon at a place Freya had not intended to stop: the burned croft at Ashen Hollow.

It looked smaller than grief remembered it. Two seasons of rain had softened the charcoal of the beams, and green shoots were pushing up through the ash — fireweed, the mountain folk called it, because it came first after burning, because it insisted. Someone had set a marker stone at the edge of the ruin. On it, cut shallow but clean, ran the sigil: the coiled serpent about a flame, the mark that lived on Freya's skin beneath her shoulder blade and had lived on her grandmother's croft and on the seal beneath Thornfell keep and now, if the dreams were true, in the failing heart of the last dragon in the world.

"Wick," she said. "Wait by the path."

He waited. She walked to the marker alone.

Fourteen graves. She had dug eleven of them herself, that terrible week after Corvane's hounds came through, and the villagers had finished the rest. She knew every name. Maren Osgood, who had sung while she spun. The twins, Brann and Sorrel, who had been seven. Old Halda, who had taught her which mushrooms forgave a mistake and which did not. She crouched and pulled a few fireweed stalks away from the stone, gently, though they had done nothing wrong. Nothing here had done anything wrong. That had been the whole horror of it — that methodical, unashamed horror, men following orders with clean hands and cleaner consciences, burning a village the way a farmer burns a field, to flush out one woman.

"I'm going down to fix it," she told the graves quietly. Her breath fogged and drifted apart. "Not the burning. That can't be fixed. But the reason for it. Whatever grandmother left me, whatever this mark means — I'm taking it down into the dark and I'm ending it. The gods get to be small again. Corvane gets nothing. And none of you get asked to bleed for it ever again."

The wind moved through the fireweed. It sounded, if a person were foolish enough to listen, almost like assent.

When she stood and turned, Wick was not waiting by the path. He was standing ten feet behind her, hat in his hands, head bowed in the awkward fierce way he prayed now — not the court's way, palms pressed and eyes shut, but the mountain way, eyes open, watching the horizon as if God might be a storm coming over the ridge.

"They'd want you to eat something," he said. "Before the hard part. Halda especially. She always fed people before hard parts."

Freya laughed despite herself — a short, cracked sound that surprised them both. "Yes," she said. "She absolutely would. Come on, then. Bread's in the side pocket. Don't give me that look, I baked it myself."

"You bake?"

"I'm a poisoner, Wick. Do you imagine I got good at the opposite trade by accident?" She broke the loaf and handed him the larger half, out of habit, out of something older than habit. "Every taster learns to cook. Half of keeping people safe is knowing exactly what good food tastes like, so you know when it's been tampered with. The other half—" she chewed, swallowed, watched him do the same, some ritual only the two of them observed, checking each other's food and lives with a glance, "—the other half is making sure somebody gets to enjoy it."

They ate sitting on the marker stone, which felt right, and afterward Freya added her own small offering: a pinch of the queen's salt she'd stolen from the palace stores years ago, scattered over fourteen graves. Salt for keeping. Salt for truth. Let what was buried here keep its truth until she came back up.

If she came back up.

* * *

The second night they sheltered at the Waymeet, the smugglers' rest carved into the rock above the Greywater gorge, and found it occupied.

Bracken, the old toll-keeper of the hidden roads, was roasting a hare over a fire that smoked too much, and he rose when he saw them with the slow careful motion of a man whose knees had opinions. Behind him, filling the cave's mouth with shadow, stood six of Verrick's rebels — the ones who had drawn the short straws, Freya suspected, for escort duty nobody wanted and everybody volunteered for anyway.

"We're not going all the way," Bracken said, before she could speak. "So don't argue. We walk you to the Deep Door, we stand watch outside, and when you come back up—" he said *when*, deliberately, meeting her eyes, "—we carry you home if your legs won't do it. Those are the terms. Verrick wrote them himself." He produced a scrap of paper, folded twice. On it, in the rebel captain's blunt hand: *Bring her back. Bring the boy back. This is not a request.*

"He can't order me," Freya said. "Nobody orders me anymore. That was sort of the entire point."

"No," Bracken agreed. "But he can ask. And so can I." He looked at her for a long moment across the fire, this man who had known her grandmother, who remembered when Underhills were just another croft family with too many secrets and good bread. "Your gran walked this same road, girl. Thirty years ago. Same direction. Same look on her face." He turned the hare, spat fat into the flames. "She came back up different. Lighter, somehow. Like she'd finally put down something heavy. I've wondered my whole life what she left down there."

Freya's hand went unconsciously to her shoulder, to the mark beneath her clothes. "I think I know," she said. "And I think I know how she managed it. Grandmother's letters were very clear on one point, Bracken. The seal doesn't want blood poured out. It wants a choice made. A bearer choosing freely what the sigil is *for*." She looked into the fire. "Corvane thinks it's a weapon. The Whisperer thinks it's a key. My grandmother knew it was neither. It's a promise. You make it to the dark, and you keep it, and then the dark keeps its own."

"And what does the promise cost?" one of the young rebels asked. He couldn't have been more than nineteen, with a scabbed-over burn along his forearm — Ashen Hollow's harvest, she realized, one of the survivors.

Freya considered lying to him. It would have been easy; she'd spent a lifetime feeding comfortable lies to powerful people, and this boy was neither. That was precisely why she owed him better.

"Everything you were planning to be afterward," she said. "Whatever future you'd imagined for yourself — the promise costs that. You go down one person and come up another, and the second one has to live in the world the first one made." She met his eyes. "It's worth it. But it isn't cheap, and I won't tell you it is."

The boy nodded slowly. Around the fire, nobody argued. They were mountain folk; they understood prices.

Later, when Wick had gone to sleep curled against the warm stone, Bracken sat beside her at the cave mouth and watched the stars wheel over Thornfell's teeth.

"He calls you mum," the old man said quietly. "When he's half-asleep. Thinks nobody hears."

Freya's throat tightened. "He lost his mother to the fever two winters back."

"Aye. And gained something else. Funny how the world does that, when it's feeling generous." Bracken knocked his pipe out against the rock. "You take care of each other down there. That's the real magic, girl, if you want my opinion. Not dragons. Not gods. Just — people deciding, over and over, day after day, that somebody else matters more than their own skin. Everything else is decoration."

He shuffled off to his bedroll. Freya stayed at the cave mouth a long time, listening to Wick breathe, and let the truth of it settle over her like snowfall: that she was afraid, properly afraid, in the marrow-deep way she hadn't been even fleeing Corvane's hunters. Fear for herself she had always carried lightly; her life had been forfeit from the moment she first swallowed for the crown. But fear for Wick — fear that the god below would find some seam in her plan, some price unpaid, some clever cruelty she hadn't foreseen — that fear had teeth, and it gnawed.

*I chose him,* she reminded the dark. *Out of everyone in the world, I chose him, and he chose me back. Whatever happens below, that part is already saved. Already true. Nothing down there can take it back.*

It helped. A little.

* * *

On the third day they passed the standing stones at Widow's Rest, where a dozen villagers from the high valleys had gathered — not to fight, not to follow, but simply to see her pass. Word had run ahead of them along the mountain paths, the way word always did, faster than any rider: *the sigil-bearer goes down to the Deep Door.* An old woman pressed a bundle into Freya's hands — dried herbs, honeycomb, a child's knitted cap far too small for Wick. "For the road," she said. "And for after. Whichever after comes." A shepherd simply touched her sleeve and walked away without a word. Two girls had woven sprigs of rowan — protection, warding, old as the stones themselves — into the straps of Freya's pack while she slept, and when she found them she did not remove them.

By afternoon they could see the smoke of Corvane's army: a brown-gray smudge on the southern horizon, vast and patient, moving with the unhurried certainty of a thing that believed itself inevitable. Three thousand men. Blood-iron. A cage built for one.

Wick stared at it a long time. "They're not hurrying," he said. "Why aren't they hurrying?"

"Because he thinks I have nowhere to go but down, and that down is a trap he's already sprung." Freya shouldered her pack. "Let him think it. The best poisons work slowly, Wick, and the best lies work best when the liar seems cornered. Come on. The Door's close now."

They reached it at dusk.

The Deep Door of Thornfell had no door, not anymore — only the wound in the mountain's flank where the entrance had been fused shut with dragon-glass three hundred years ago, a wall of black-green glassy stone taller than five men, veined with faint light that pulsed, very slowly, like something breathing in its sleep. The rebels took up their positions in the rocks above without being told. Bracken squeezed her shoulder once, hard, and said nothing, because everything had already been said.

Freya stood before the glass with Wick at her side and her grandmother's letters against her heart and the shard of the same glass — taken from the shrine, singing softly in her pocket, singing louder now, singing to what lay beyond — warming against her hip.

"It knows we're here," Wick whispered. The light in the veins had begun, very slightly, to quicken.

"Yes," Freya said. "It's been waiting for an Underhill a long time." She drew a breath, tasting the air the way she tasted everything — bitter stone, cold iron somewhere deep below, and underneath it, faint as a rumor, the scent of honey. The Whisperer's perfume. Its invitation. *Come down, little taster. We had an arrangement.*

"One last thing before we open it," she said, and turned to face Wick fully in the dying light.

He looked up at her, and she saw that he already knew — children always know — and had been waiting for her to say it aloud.

"You are not my apprentice," Freya said. "You haven't been for a long time. You're my son. Not by blood — by choice, which is stronger, because blood is given and choice is kept. Whatever happens on the other side of this wall, you remember that. If the god takes me, you climb back up those rocks, you find Verrick, you live a long loud life and you bake bread badly and you tell them all stories about me until I'm insufferable. That's an order from your mother. Are we clear?"

Wick's eyes were wet and furious and shining. He threw his arms around her and held on with the desperate strength of the small, and when he spoke his voice was muffled against her coat and perfectly steady.

"Clear, Mum. Now let's go poison a god."

Beyond the dragon-glass, deep beneath the mountain, something ancient laughed — soft and sweet and full of hunger — and the veins of light in the wall began, one by one, to blaze.

Freya pressed the shard to the seal, and the Deep Door began to open.
