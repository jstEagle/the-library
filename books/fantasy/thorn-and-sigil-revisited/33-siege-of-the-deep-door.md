## 33. Siege of the Deep Door

The army came up the valley at dawn, and the mountain answered with fire.

Corvane watched it from the ridge above the ford, gloved hands folded behind his back, and permitted himself a single moment of satisfaction. Below him, two thousand soldiers in the queen's—no, in *his*—black and silver wound along the river road like a serpent of iron. Behind them rolled the supply wagons, the siege ladders, the blood-iron cage on its groaning cart, its bars etched with wards that had cost him three hedge-mages and most of a season to commission. The cage was empty. It would not be empty by nightfall.

"Signal from the vanguard, my lord," said Captain Vess, her breath steaming. "Rebels holding the shrine road. Pikes and hunting bows. Maybe six hundred."

"Six hundred." Corvane tasted the number and found it thin. "And the dragon?"

"No sign, my lord. Scouts say it roosts somewhere on the high crags, but it hasn't flown since the pass came down."

*It will come,* Corvane thought. *It is bound to her, and she is bound to the door, and the door is what I have come to open.* Everything in this war was a chain, and he had spent his whole life learning where each link could be gripped. "Then we take the road, hold the entrance, and wait for the mountain to hand us what we want. Form the lines. And Captain—if a small woman with dark hair comes out of that crowd, or goes toward it, you send for me before anyone so much as breathes on her. She is not to be harmed. She is not to be *touched*. Am I understood?"

Vess's eyes flicked to the cage and back. "Understood, my lord."

Below, the horns began.

* * *

Freya heard the horns from inside the rebel camp and felt them in her teeth.

She had heard armies before, from a distance, the way one hears weather. This was different. This was an army coming for *her*, for the thing under her skin, for the name carved over her grandmother's hearth—and every horn-note landed like a hand on her shoulder.

"They'll hit the pike line first," said Brannoc, the rebels' one-eyed commander, crouched over a map weighted with stones. "Here, at the narrows. Corvane's patient. He'll probe all morning, spend his levies cheap, and keep his household troops fresh for afternoon." He looked up at her, and his eye was very tired. "You don't have to be here when it starts, sigil-bearer."

"I do, actually." She kept her voice level. "The door answers the Underhill mark. If something goes wrong down there—if the seal fights me—you'll want me close, not hiding in a hole up-valley."

"And if Corvane takes the field?"

"Then I'll be underground while your people are dying above it, and I'll hate every minute, and I'll still be doing the only useful thing I know how to do." She met his gaze. "I've spent my whole life being swallowed so other people can eat safe, Brannoc. One more time won't kill me."

Something moved behind his weathered face—not quite a smile. "Your grandmother would've liked you."

"You knew her?"

"I carried her flour once, when I was a boy." He turned back to the map. "Go on, then. Wick's waiting at the treeline. The Whisperer's been quiet since midnight, and quiet from that thing frightens me worse than horns."

Wick was waiting where he'd promised, small and sharp-eyed in a borrowed mail shirt three sizes too big, and Freya's chest did the painful thing it always did now when she saw him—the thing that was half fear and half fierce, unreasonable love.

"You're supposed to stay with the healers," she said.

"I'm supposed to stay with *you*." He fell into step beside her as they slipped into the trees, away from the camp, toward the goat track that wound down behind the falls to the shrine's back approach. "You taught me the herbs. You taught me which mushrooms lie. You think I'm going to stand around boiling bandages while you walk into a god's house alone?"

"I'm not walking in alone. You're coming with me as far as the antechamber, and then you're staying there, where it's stone and defensible, and if I'm not back by moonrise you run. Up-valley. To Emberwake."

"He'd eat me."

"He'd *listen* to you. There's a difference, and it matters." She caught his shoulder, made him look at her. Above them, muffled by rock and distance, the first screams of the morning reached the treeline, thin as birdsong. "Wick. Listen to me. Whatever happens today—I chose you. Out of everyone. Remember that, whatever else happens."

His chin came up, stubborn, terrified, thirteen years old and trying to be brave about it. "Come back by moonrise, then. So I don't have to remember anything sad."

They went down together, against the noise, against the whole grinding weight of the world above—and the mountain closed around them like a cupped hand.

* * *

By midmorning the narrows ran red.

Vess had told herself she was hardened to this. Ten years in Corvane's service; the border wars; the village she would not let herself think about, the one that smelled even now of wet ash whenever it rained. But there is a difference between burning a village at dusk from a distance and watching farm boys with hunting bows die by inches in the mud of a river ford, and the difference was that here she could see their faces.

"Hold the line!" she shouted, riding the length of the shield wall as arrows hissed out of the alder thickets. "Push! Push them off the crossing!"

The rebels fought like men defending their own doorsteps, because they were. Every fallow field burned for this war, every well the crown had taxed dry, every neighbor taken by press-gangs—it was all in their arms, and arms like that are heavy. They gave ground by feet, not yards. They made the queen's veterans pay for every stone of the ford.

And through it all, Corvane watched from the ridge and fed the fire exactly as much wood as it needed.

"Third company forward," he said, mild as a man ordering wine. "Not the fourth. Let them bleed the archers' quivers down before we commit the crossbows." He stood with his spyglass raised, and what he watched was not the battle. It was the treeline beyond it. The slopes. The high crags where the morning clouds tore and knitted.

*Where are you, dragon?*

Because that was the flaw in the day's architecture, the hairline crack running through all his careful masonry. He had planned for pikes and bows, for mud and numbers. He had not planned for three hundred years of sleeping fire, and every hour it failed to appear made the itch between his shoulders worse.

"My lord." A courier, mud to the thighs, saluting with a shaking hand. "Word from the rear guard. Riders coming up the south road—rebel horse, maybe two hundred, hitting our wagons. They've fired the powder train."

For the first time that day, Corvane's jaw tightened. "The cage?"

"Untouched, my lord. Wardens held."

"Of course they did." He lowered the spyglass. Below, the third company was faltering at the narrows, the rebel line bending but not breaking, and somewhere in that crush of screaming men was exactly the chaos a certain taster would need to slip past his lines and down into the dark. He had accounted for her cunning. He had accounted for her desperation. What he had perhaps not fully weighed—he admitted it now, in the cold professional corner of his mind that never stopped working—was how much of both she'd had time to accumulate.

"She's already moving," he said quietly.

Vess reined up beside him, bleeding from a graze along her jaw she hadn't mentioned. "My lord?"

"The shrine. She went down before the horns finished, or I'm a fool. Which means the door may already be opening, which means—" He turned, and his voice dropped into the register he saved for orders that would end careers. "Captain, take your household troop and clear me a path to the entrance. Not the ford. The goat track behind the falls—the locals use it, someone in this rabble knows it, *find someone who does*. I want the deep door in my hands within the hour."

"And the dragon, my lord?"

Corvane looked up at the tearing clouds, and smiled the smile that had opened more doors than any key.

"If it wanted to stop us, it has had all morning. Let it sleep. We are only here to collect what it was left guarding."

* * *

The battle, seen from below, was a heartbeat.

That was what struck Freya, descending the last stair with Wick's torchlight trembling on the walls—that all that screaming above came down through the mountain as nothing but a slow, vast pulse, like blood in the ears of a sleeping giant. Dust sifted from the ceiling in time with it. The dragon-glass walls drank the torchlight and gave back a faint red gleam, as though the stone itself remembered fire.

The antechamber opened around them, vast and cold, and there it was: the seal. A wall of fused glass forty feet high, veined with channels that had once, she understood now, run with living flame. Her family's work. Her family's prison. And across its face, in letters of darker glass, the old script she had learned from her grandmother's letters:

*UNDERHILL KEEPS THE DOOR.*

"It's humming," Wick whispered. "Freya. It's *humming*."

It was. A note below hearing, felt in the bones of her feet, in the scar on her forearm where the shard had burned her, in the birthmark at her throat—which was warm now, warmer than it had ever been without her blood on it. She pressed her palm flat against the glass.

The vision took her like a wave.

*A hall of dragons, wings folded like cathedral rafters. Her grandmother's hands, younger than any memory, pressing a blade of black glass into a heart the size of a wagon. A voice like a landslide saying* keeper, not killer *and* we choose you *and then—fire, everywhere, going out all at once, like a city of candles snuffed by one long exhale—*

She came back to herself on her knees, Wick's hands under her elbows, shouting her name.

"I'm fine. I'm fine." She got up. Her legs shook. Above, the pulse of the battle had changed—faster now, closer, boots on stone somewhere far up the stair. "They found the track. Wick—"

"I know. I heard." His face was white but set, and he did the thing she had taught him without knowing she was teaching it: he breathed once, slowly, and put himself between her and the stair. "How long do you need?"

"To cut the channel open? With the shard?" She drew it from her breast—a sliver of dragon-glass no longer than a knife, singing now, hot as a coal. "If nothing interrupts me—ten minutes. If something does—"

Boots. Many boots, echoing down. Torchlight bloomed at the top of the stair, and a voice she knew better than her own heartbeat called down into the dark, courteous as a dinner invitation:

"Mistress Underhill. You've made me climb a *mountain*."

Corvane stepped into the antechamber with twenty swords at his back, spattered to the knees with the ford's red mud, and his eyes went past her—to the seal, to the script, to the shard in her hand—and Freya watched twenty years of patience arrive all at once at the only door it had ever wanted.

"There's no need for the cage," he said gently. "There never was. All I have ever needed is your cooperation, and here you are, cooperating already. Step away from the wall, Freya. Let me finish what your family started."

Behind him, up the long stair, the mountain shuddered—and somewhere far above, through leagues of stone, something ancient opened one eye, and the dust began to fall like snow.
