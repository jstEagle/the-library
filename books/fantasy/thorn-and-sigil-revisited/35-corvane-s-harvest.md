## 35. Corvane's Harvest

The road down to the shrine was a throat of black stone, and Lord Aldric Corvane descended it the way a man descends into his own ambition—steadily, deliberately, with the certain knowledge that whatever waited at the bottom had been waiting for him.

He had dressed for it. Not armor, not quite. The blood-iron of his breastplate was polished to a mirror finish, and beneath the open front of his riding coat he wore the grey silk of mourning for Queen Maren, three days dead and barely cold in her crypt at Thornfell Keep above. The regency seal sat against his ribs on its chain, warm from his skin, as though it already knew what he intended to make of it. He had signed the first orders of his regency by lantern light before dawn: the mountain roads closed, the passes sealed, the rebel leaders named traitors in absentia. Then he had ridden down into the earth with two hundred soldiers and a cage.

The cage rode in a wagon behind him. It was a beautiful thing, in the way that instruments of cruelty are always beautiful to their makers—wrought iron and blood-iron braided together, every bar etched with sigils copied from the census rolls of a hundred years ago, sigils meant to hold a bearer of the Underhill mark. He had commissioned it in secret eight months past, when he had first confirmed what the girl was. He had told himself then that it was insurance. Now he understood it had always been a throne room in miniature: a small enclosed space where one person's blood could be taken under controlled conditions, without waste, without mess, without the interference of gods or rebels or sentiment.

Corvane did not think of himself as a cruel man. This was important to him, though he would never have admitted how important. He thought of himself as a necessary man. The realm was sick—the queen had died raving about dragons, the lowlands were starving, the mountains were in open rebellion, and something ancient and hungry had been answering prayers in the dark like a moneylender answering knocks at midnight. Someone had to take hold of all of it. Someone had to be strong enough. He had spent thirty years becoming that someone, and if the price was a taster's blood and a god's freedom, then the price was cheap, because he intended to pay it and keep the change.

He had killed the god's avatar an hour ago. He was still thinking about it.

* * *

It had happened below, in the antechamber before the shrine proper, where the fused dragon-glass wall glowed faintly green in the dark like a window into some drowned world. When Corvane's vanguard had fought through the rebel line at the Deep Door—and it had been a slaughter; he had spent men freely, spending them the way he spent everything, as currency convertible to purpose—he had expected to find Freya Underhill at the wall, blade at her wrist, blood running into the channels cut into the ancient floor. Instead he had found the Whisperer.

It wore a body now. That was the only word for it. The god had gathered itself a shape out of the desperate devotion of a year of answered prayers—a tall figure, half-shadow and half-substance, its face a smooth oval with no features but two points of pale light where eyes should have been, its hands too long, its voice coming from everywhere in the chamber at once, courteous and cold. It had been standing over Freya, who lay crumpled against the base of the glass wall, her wrists bound in cords of shadow, her blood already drawn in thin lines down her forearms. The god had opened the seal. The wall had split along the seam the Underhills had made six centuries ago, and the first of the freed powers had been pouring through when Corvane came down the stair with forty men and a crossbow volley already loosed.

The avatar had turned to him with something almost like interest. *You brought me an army,* it had said, or something like saying, its voice a pressure more than a sound. *How generous.*

Corvane had watched it long enough to understand one thing: it lied beautifully. Everything it said sounded like mercy. It had promised the villagers safety and taken a child. It had promised Freya release and seized her instead. It was, he realized, exactly like him—it made bargains it never meant to keep, because it understood that belief was a resource and trust was the tax collected on it. And that understanding had settled in him with a strange calm, because he knew one other thing about creatures like himself.

They believed nothing could touch them.

"Loose," he had said, and forty crossbows spoke, and the volley went through the avatar's gathered body like stones through smoke—and the smoke screamed. Not the body. Something deeper. The bolts were tipped with blood-iron, forged from ore mined above the shrine itself, quenched in water mixed with ash from the burned village of Harrow's Ford, and whatever the god had made itself out of, it could not abide the stuff. The avatar had come apart in slow motion, its long hands dissolving, its smooth face cracking like river ice, the pale lights of its eyes guttering. It had reached for Corvane once—one last gesture, elegant even in ruin, offering him partnership, offering him the child back, offering him anything—and he had stepped inside the reach of those dissolving hands and driven his sword, blood-iron hilt to tip, up through the center of the thing's chest, and held it there while the god poured itself out around the blade like water around a dam.

The last thing it had managed, in a voice gone thin and ragged, was: *We are the same.*

"No," Corvane had said quietly. "I keep my bargains to myself."

Then the god was gone—not destroyed, he suspected, gods of that depth did not destroy easily, but unmade in this place, this shape, this moment of confidence. Its power had gone somewhere. He intended to find out where, and collect it.

The child the god had taken stood in the corner of the chamber, alive, blinking, wrapped in a blanket one of his sergeants had fetched from a dead rebel. Corvane had looked at the boy for a long moment—at the small stunned face, at the miracle of him being alive—and felt nothing whatsoever, except a mild satisfaction that the ledger balanced. A life returned. A god spent. An asset secured. He had ordered the boy carried up and out with the wounded, and had thought no more about him.

Freya was another matter.

* * *

She was conscious by the time they moved her. Of course she was. She was a poisoner's daughter of the old school; she had catalogued her own symptoms since childhood the way merchants catalogue coin. She knelt where they put her, at the base of the great glass wall, her bound wrists resting in her lap, and she watched him with an expression he had studied across a hundred court tables and never once seen directed at him until now.

Pity.

"You're bleeding," she said. Her voice was rough, scraped raw by the god's cords, but steady. "Your left hand. You should let someone look at it."

"It's nothing." It was nothing. A gash from the fighting at the door, already clotted. But she had seen it, and named it, and offered care, and that small transaction had lodged in him like a splinter. She was doing to him what he had done to her for a year—small mercies, precisely measured, each one a hook. He knew the technique. He had invented half of it. And here it was, turned on him by a woman kneeling in chains, and he could not decide whether to be furious or admiring, so he settled, as he always settled, for control.

"Do you know what you cost me?" he asked her. "A year. A year of careful work. I fed you kindnesses you never noticed. I moved your smuggler contacts where I needed them. I let you run, twice, because a hunted animal leads you to its den. And now—" he gestured at the split wall behind her, at the green light leaking through the crack like light through a door left ajar, "—now the seal is open, the queen is dead, the realm is mine to hold together, and the only thing missing is the ingredient."

"My blood," she said.

"Your blood." He crouched, unhurried, bringing his face level with hers, and let her see the cage-wagon's key hanging from his belt. "Six centuries ago your family learned how to bind fire into flesh. The sigil isn't a birthmark, Freya. It's a vessel. Every drop of your blood carries the pattern, and the pattern can hold a god—or command one. The queen wanted you harvested gently, in a tower, by physicians, over months." He smiled, and it was his real smile, the one no one at court had ever been shown, the one that lived underneath all the others. "The queen is dead. We will do it my way. Quickly. Thoroughly. Here, at the source, where the power is thickest."

"And then?" she asked. "When I'm empty?"

"Then you'll be a footnote," he said, "and I will be the man who ended the war before it began. The gods come through that wall—I've heard them moving behind the glass all morning, like wolves behind a door—and I will bind them, one by one, into instruments. Armies that pray to me. Weather that obeys me. A realm that finally, finally holds still." He stood. "You should feel honored. Your family kept the lock. I intend to keep the key."

Something moved behind her eyes. He had spent a year learning to read that face, and he read it now, and what he saw frightened him more than the god had—not fear, not despair, but patience. Her patience. The terrible professional patience of a woman who had swallowed poison every day of her adult life and survived it by knowing, always, exactly how much time she had.

"You're wrong about one thing," she said softly. "My grandmother's letters. You had them copied. Did you actually read them?"

"I read everything."

"Then you know the sigil closes as well as opens." Her voice stayed level, almost gentle. "And you know what happens to the hand that holds the key when the door swings both ways."

He did know. He had dismissed it. Letters written by a croft-dwelling old woman sixty years dead, superstition dressed as scholarship, the kind of thing peasants told each other to make their bloodlines feel important. He had filed it under noise. But the way she said it now—not arguing, just placing the fact on the table between them like a dish she was tasting for him—made the hair rise on his arms, and he hated her a little for that, and hated himself more for noticing.

It didn't matter. Nothing mattered except sequence. First the blood. Then the binding. Then the realm. He signaled his men, and they lifted her, and the procession moved toward the wagon and the cage, and Corvane walked at its head feeling taller than he had ever felt, feeling, for the first time in thirty years of careful service, that history had finally caught up to him and was waiting for him to catch up to it.

That was when the mountain shook.

* * *

It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes had a character he knew—rolling, indifferent, geological. This was directional. This came from the east, from the shattered pass where the dragon had risen three days ago, and it came with a sound like a bell the size of a cathedral being struck underground, and every soldier in the column stopped moving at once, and every torch flame leaned eastward as though the air itself were being inhaled by something vast.

Corvane stood very still and listened, because listening was the first discipline they had ever taught him and the last one he trusted.

Under the bell-sound, under the groan of shifting stone, he heard wings. Not near. Not yet. But coming, with the unhurried rhythm of a creature that had flown for six hundred years before men built walls and would fly six hundred after the walls were dust, a creature that did not need to hurry because nothing in the world could outrun it and nothing in the world could stop it and it knew both facts the way it knew the taste of wind.

Emberwake. Coming for the shard. Coming for the girl. Coming, Corvane understood with a clarity that felt almost like grace, for *him*—because the dragon had knelt to Freya Underhill in front of ten thousand witnesses, and the last living keeper of the seal was in a cage on his wagon, and the fire that had slept beneath the mountain since before his grandfather's grandfather had woken, and it remembered whose blood had fed it.

His officers begged him to withdraw to the keep. Two hundred men against a dragon was not a battle; it was a catering order. Corvane refused. Withdrawal, at this moment, with the seal cracked and the god's power loose in the deep places and the regency seal warm against his chest, would mean surrendering the only window in six centuries through which a man could reach out and take hold of the divine. He had not climbed thirty years to flinch at the threshold.

Besides. He had the girl. And the girl, he was beginning to suspect, was worth more than the dragon.

He ordered the column formed into a defensive ring around the wagon, shields locked, the blood-iron pikes angled outward, the crossbows reloaded with the ash-quenched bolts that had unmade a god. He sent riders east to buy him time with their lives. He took Freya's cage-key from his belt and held it in his injured left hand, and pressed the wound closed around the iron, and told himself the sting was nothing, a nothing, a detail.

Freya watched him do it from inside the cage bars, and said, very quietly, so that only he could hear:

"There it is."

"There what is?"

"The look," she said. "On your face. The one my grandmother wrote about. The one the last Underhill saw on the king's man, six hundred years ago, right before the fire took him." She leaned her forehead briefly against the iron bar, tired, almost tender. "You think you're harvesting me, Lord Corvane. But you're standing in the oldest trap in the Thornfells, and you've just put the bait in your own fist."

Above the eastern ridge, against the bruised pre-dawn sky, a shape rose—vast, winged, burning along its seams like a coal remembering it was ever in a fire—and began to descend toward the shrine road, unhurried, inevitable, its shadow sliding up the mountainside toward the ring of shields and the wagon and the man holding the key.

Corvane looked up at it, and did not step back, and turned the key over once in his bleeding hand.

"Come, then," he said to the darkening sky. "Everything burns. Let's see which of us it burns first."
