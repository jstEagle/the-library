## 36. Emberwake's Fire

The world had grown small.

That was the first thing Emberwake understood as it dragged itself out of the mountain's throat and into the thin cold air of the pre-dawn — not that the world had changed, but that it had shrunk around the dragon the way a shell shrinks around a body too long asleep in it. For three hundred years its universe had been a chamber of fused glass and warm stone, the slow drip of mineral water, the vast slow conversation of its own failing heart. It had measured time in heartbeats, then in decades of heartbeats, then in the long grey intervals between dreams when it could not be certain any time passed at all.

Now the sky was above it, enormous and indifferent, and the wind moved through the seams of its wings like fingers through rotted cloth, and Emberwake thought: *so this is what I spent three centuries keeping.*

It was not disappointed. It was past disappointment. It was mostly past everything.

* * *

The waking had come in pieces, over years, though the dragon had no way to know how many. First there had been the taste — iron and honey on air that should have tasted of nothing but stone, the flavor of old bargains seeping up through the mountain's bones. Then the sound: prayers, thin as insect wings, beating against the seal from the other side. The Whisperer had never stopped whispering; Emberwake had known that even in its deepest sleep, the way a dog knows rain is coming through the ache in an old wound. But lately the whispers had found throats to speak through. Lately they had found *believers*.

And then, months ago — or days, the dragon's reckoning was unreliable now — a new thing. A small hot thing, singing.

A shard of the seal-glass, torn loose and carried into the daylight by hands that did not understand what they carried. Emberwake had felt it the way a mother feels a child cry in another room: not hearing, exactly, but knowing. The shard sang with the Underhill fire, the binding-fire, the fire that had been poured into the prison wall a thousand years ago by a woman whose face the dragon still remembered better than its own.

The dragon had tried to rise then. It had failed. Its legs would not answer; its heart stuttered like a guttering lamp. So it had done what keepers do. It had waited, and it had listened, and it had burned what little remained of itself down to a single steady coal, hoarding the heat the way a miser hoards coin, so that when the moment came — if the moment came — there would be *enough*.

There was almost enough.

* * *

The shrine road below was a wound of torchlight and screaming.

Emberwake saw it from above as it descended, and the sight assembled itself in the dragon's mind out of fragments of older memories: men in ranks, shields overlapping like scales, the glint of blood-iron — oh, the *blood-iron*, forged for caging things like it, things like her — and among them, small and swift as a spark blown along a hearthstone, a figure running against the whole tide of the battle. Toward the deep door. Away from safety.

The dragon knew that walk. It had watched that walk descend these stairs a thousand years ago, in a body that was young and terrible and full of fire. The Underhill gait: shoulders squared against the weight of a decision already made, feet finding the sure path because hesitation killed more keepers than enemies ever had.

*Little one,* Emberwake thought, and the thought came out as sound for the first time in three centuries, a sound that rolled down the mountainside and flattened the screams beneath it. *You are late. And you are early. Both at once. That is how it always goes.*

The army broke. Of course it broke. Men are made to fight men, and a dragon descending out of a bruised sky is not a man, is not anything their grandmothers' stories prepared them for, because the stories all ended with the dragons gone. Emberwake did not breathe flame at them. It had counted its remaining fire the way Freya Underhill counted drops of poison — precisely, lovingly, with a professional's respect for the dose that kills. There was fire enough for one act. Perhaps two, if the second was small.

It chose where to fall the way water chooses: the path of least resistance toward the thing it must reach. The courtyard before the deep door cracked under its weight. Blood-iron cages — Corvane's beautiful cage, built for a woman and large enough, the spymaster had hoped, for a dragon's heart — shrieked and folded like wet paper. Somewhere below, a man with a key in his bleeding hand looked up, and did not step back, and Emberwake marked him. *Later,* the dragon promised itself. Or perhaps: *never.* The accounting would have to wait. Everything waited on the small figure running toward the door.

* * *

She reached the threshold just as the dragon's head came down through the shattered roof, and Emberwake saw her clearly for the first time.

Not the first Underhill. He had expected — some stubborn ancestral part of him had insisted — the tall woman with the storm-grey eyes and the burn-scarred forearms, the one who had stood at this door with a bowl of her own blood and said *hold fast* to a wall of glass. But the woman below was smaller, younger, worn thin as winter honeycomb, with a birthmark at her collarbone that flared like a live coal as the dragon's shadow crossed her. The sigil. The sigil answering its keeper's fire across a thousand years of silence.

*Granddaughter,* Emberwake thought. *Or near enough. Near enough for the fire to know you.*

She did not run from it. That was the thing the dragon would remember longest, in whatever remembering was left to it. She stood in the falling dust with her arm raised against the grit and looked up into the oldest eyes in the world, and she did not run.

"You're dying," she said. Not a question. Tasters know death's smell the way priests know incense.

*I am,* Emberwake answered, and its voice was less a voice now than a great warmth moving through the chamber, rattling the fused glass of the prison wall behind her. *I have been dying since before your grandmother's grandmother learned to walk. A keeper outlives its purpose by exactly as long as the purpose holds. Yours has begun to fail. So mine ends tonight, one way or the other.*

Behind her, up the broken stair, the battle noise swelled and fell like surf. She glanced back once — the dragon saw her weigh it, saw her count the seconds the way she counted everything — and turned back.

"The god," she said. "The Whisperer. It has my bargain. It has a child. I opened the seal as agreed and it reneged, and now—" Her voice caught, steadied. Taster's discipline. "Now it wants more than I ever meant to give."

*It always does,* Emberwake said. *Hunger dressed as mercy is still hunger. I told the first Underhill the same thing, at this same door, and she laughed at me and said she knew, she'd fed enough lords to recognize the appetite.* The dragon's breath came slower now. Each word cost heat it could not spare, and it spent them anyway, because words were the cheapest thing it had left. *Listen to me, granddaughter-of-Underhill. My time is short, so I will say the true thing plainly, which is a luxury neither of us has often enjoyed.*

It lowered its head until its brow nearly touched the floor beside her, until she stood within the cavern of its breath, close enough to see the fire banked behind its eyes like coals seen through a grate.

*The gods cannot be unmade. They can only be given a shape small enough to live among us without devouring us. Your blood alone can open the seal — that is the trap you have already sprung. But your blood alone cannot make them small. For that, the seal must be remade, not broken. Bound into something that can hold them willingly. Something that burns.*

Freya went very still. "The wall," she said. "Dragon-glass."

*The wall is dead glass. Dead glass only imprisons, and imprisonment breeds hunger, and hunger breeds Whisperers.* The dragon's laugh was a low rumble that shook grit from the ceiling. *No, little taster. Living fire. A living heart. The first seal was poured into me and my kin, and we held it gladly, because holding it was our purpose and our pride. When the last of us sleeps, the seal must pass to a new heart — or the prison opens forever.*

"And whose heart," Freya said quietly, "is left?"

Emberwake held her gaze. In the dark behind its eyes, the last coal of three hundred years of keeping pulsed once, twice, patient as a held note.

*Mine,* it said. *What remains of it. Enough to forge one final binding — if a bearer of the sigil will write it there. If she will take my fire and burn the sigil into the living heart of the last dragon, and let the gods pour out diminished, mortal-ish, harmless as old men by a winter fire. Free. Small. No one's weapons. No one's prisoners.*

"And the price," Freya said. There it was — the taster's instinct, reaching for the poison hidden in the sweet dish. "Everything has a price. You taught my family that, didn't you? What does it cost?"

*Me,* said Emberwake simply. *All of me. The forging spends the last of my fire, and what is spent does not return. I have kept this flame for a thousand years, granddaughter. I would rather spend it well than watch it gutter in the dark while the Whisperer wears the world like a stolen coat.*

*But hear the rest, because you deserve the rest:* the dragon's voice gentled, became almost intimate, a warmth against her skin. *The sigil written in my heart passes out of your blood. You will be only a woman afterward. No crown will want you. No god will fear you. The mark becomes a scar. You will belong to no one — not the throne, not the mountain, not the fire. That is the gift inside the cost, and I give it knowingly, because I have watched what the sigil does to those who carry it, and no one should carry it who was never asked.*

Freya's hand rose to her collarbone, to the burning mark, and pressed there as if holding shut a door.

"I was never asked," she said. "Not once. Not by the court, not by the queen, not by the god." She looked up, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely clear. "So ask me now. Properly. With all of it on the table."

* * *

They spoke for a long time, there in the ruined shrine, while above them the world tore at itself.

Emberwake told her about the first Underhill — how she had come down the stairs with poisoned wine for the enemy lords and bread for the starving, and how the dragon had smelled both on her hands and known at once what kind of creature it was binding itself to. It told her about the forging, the long terrible night when seven dragons gave their fire and six of them did not survive the giving, and how the seventh had lain down beneath the mountain afterward because someone had to stay awake at the door. It told her about the dreams — the long grey centuries of listening to the Whisperer scratch at the glass, counting the scratches, learning patience the way stones learn smoothness.

Freya told it about the poison. About swallowing death daily so that a table of strangers could eat safely, and how no one had ever thanked her, and how she had made a strange weary pride of that anyway. She told it about Wick — *my boy, I chose him, he's mine to protect* — and about the child the god had taken, and about the bargain she had made intending to break it.

"You planned betrayal," Emberwake observed.

"I'm a taster," Freya said. "Swallowing the poison is the job. Sometimes the poison is a promise."

The dragon rumbled something that in a smaller creature might have been laughter.

Then, from up the stair, came a sound that ended the conversation: boots, many, and the ring of blood-iron, and a voice like a well-dressed knife calling orders. Corvane had regrouped. Corvane was coming down. Behind him, deeper in, from the direction of the opened seal, the air had begun to move wrong — a pressure, a pull, the sensation of being *listened to* by something vast and delighted. The gods were pouring through the broken wall like light through a crack, and the brightest of them had found the woman who opened the door.

*It comes for you now,* Emberwake said, rising with a groan of ancient joints, folding its ruined body between her and the stair. *Both of them come. The man who wants your blood for his war, and the god who wants it for its freedom. You have perhaps the length of a heartbeat to choose, granddaughter. So choose as a keeper chooses: not what saves you, but what saves the world after you are done saving yourself.*

Freya looked at the dragon's heart — visible now through the failing seams of its chest, a single ember pulsing in a hollow of blackened scale, small as a candle and older than kingdoms.

"If I do this," she said, "you die."

*I died three hundred years ago,* Emberwake said. *I have merely been polite about mentioning it.*

She set her jaw the way her grandmother's letters said the first Underhill had set hers, and stepped forward, and put her hand flat against the dragon's burning breast.

"Then give me the fire," Freya Underhill said, "and I'll write us both free."

Above them the roof split wide, and Lord Corvane descended into the shrine with the key in one hand and a drawn blade in the other, and behind him, pouring up out of the deep dark, the Whisperer came wearing a smile it had borrowed from the desperate — and the last coal of the oldest fire in the world began, at last, to blaze.
