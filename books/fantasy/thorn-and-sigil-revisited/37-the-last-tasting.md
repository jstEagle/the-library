## 37. The Last Tasting

The shrine was full of light that had no source.

Freya stood at the center of it, in the chamber beneath the Deep Door where her family's name had been carved into fused dragon-glass for a thousand years, and the light came from everywhere and nowhere—from the cracked seal bleeding dawn across the floor, from the gods pouring through in their diminished bodies, from the blood on her own hands that was not hers to give.

Emberwake lay dying at the edge of the chamber, folded into itself like a mountain learning to kneel. The dragon's scales had gone grey as old ash along the belly, and each breath was a long, laboring thing, a bellows with a cracked seam. Its eye—the size of a cart wheel, gold shot through with dying embers—found her across the distance.

*Little keeper,* it said, and its voice was not sound so much as heat against the inside of her skull. *You are not the first Underhill.*

"No," Freya said. Her voice sounded very small in all that light. "I'm the last."

*Then you know what must be done.*

She did. She had known since the shard of dragon-glass in her pocket had begun to sing when she buried the dead at Harrow's Ford—had known in the way she knew a poison from its smell alone, before the first symptom ever showed. Some knowledge lived below thought, down where a taster kept her oldest instincts: this is death, do not swallow; this is the antidote, drink fast.

Except this time there was no antidote. There was only the choice of what to pour the poison into.

Behind her, boots on stone. Measured, unhurried, the walk of a man who had never once needed to run.

"Freya." Corvane's voice carried beautifully in the shrine, the way it did in throne rooms and interrogation chambers, pitched to fill space without seeming to try. "Step away from the beast."

She turned. He stood at the mouth of the stair with the blood-iron cage behind him and six of his household guard fanned out around him, swords drawn but lowered—they had seen what happened to the men who'd raised them against the god's avatar. Corvane himself looked immaculate. That was the horror of him. The queen was two days dead, the regency freshly pinned to his shoulder like a medal, half his army brawling with rebels above ground, an old god dissolving into motes of light not thirty feet away—and Lord Corvane's collar sat straight, his boots unmarked, his face composed into something almost gentle.

"You're bleeding," he observed. His eyes went to her hands, and there it was—the hunger, banked but bright, a hearth-fire he thought he hid well. "And the seal is open. Do you understand what you've done?"

"I bargained," Freya said. "It's what I'm for."

"What you're *for*"—he took a step forward, and the guards moved with him like shadows—"is the crown's. You have always been the crown's, Freya. Your grandmother tasted for Queen Elsbeth. Your mother's mother before that. A thousand years of Underhills swallowing poison so the throne could eat in peace, and not one of them ever imagined they were anything more than a kitchen servant with a strong stomach." Another step. The light of the broken seal played across his face and made him look younger, kinder, a man remembering a fondness he genuinely believed he felt. "But you are more. You are the last drop of dragon-fire left in the world, and you were going to give it away. To a god." He clicked his tongue, softly, a tutor disappointed in a promising student. "I confess I didn't expect the bargain. It shows sentiment. But the outcome is the same. The blood is yours until I take it, and I am here to take it."

"The god is already leaving."

"The god is already *leaving*," Corvane agreed pleasantly, "because it spent its strength breaking a prison your family built. Which means the only power left in this room worth having is standing in front of me with blood on her hands." He gestured, and two guards began moving wide, flanking. "You've read the old rolls. You know how it works. The sigil responds to fire and to fear and to blood freely given or forcibly taken—it hardly distinguishes. I built a cage of blood-iron to hold you while we harvested, gently, over months. But I find I no longer have months. So we will do it here, quickly, and the flame will pass to the crown, and the wars of the next age will be won before they are fought. Your name will be spoken with honor, Freya. I promise you that. I keep my promises."

"Do you," Freya said. "To the queen?"

Something flickered behind his eyes—the first true thing she'd ever seen in his face, and it looked almost like grief, if grief could be filed and categorized. "The queen wanted to live. Everyone wants to live. I have simply never confused wanting with deserving." He spread his hands. "Come willingly. Spare us both the cage."

Freya looked at him for a long moment. Then she did the thing she was best at in all the world, the thing no one had ever thanked her for, the thing that had kept her alive through nine years of the queen's table:

She smiled, and swallowed, and lied.

"All right," she said. "Gently."

Corvane's face softened with triumph. He believed her. Of course he believed her—he had spent his whole career buying people, and people who sell themselves always think everyone has a price. He waved his guards back and crossed the chamber himself, unhurried, hands open, coming to collect her like a debt of grain.

Which was why he was close enough when Emberwake opened one eye fully and exhaled.

Not flame—not yet. Just breath. Heat rolled across the chamber like the opening of an oven, and Corvane staggered, cloak snapping, and in that heartbeat of imbalance Freya moved—not toward him, not away, but to the dragon, scrambling up the slope of its foreleg the way she'd climbed a hundred times in her dreams since the shard had started singing, hand finding the warm groove between two scales the way her palm had found it in visions she'd told no one about.

*Little keeper.* Emberwake's voice was fainter now. The light in its eye guttered. *My fire will not last the hour. Take it.*

"And you?" Freya's throat closed. She had buried strangers at Harrow's Ford without weeping. This was different. This creature had knelt to her in a shattered mountain pass, had mistaken her for a woman ten centuries dead, had been alone under the mountain longer than any nation had existed—and it had woken only to die.

*I have kept my post three thousand years past its purpose,* Emberwake said. *A keeper who cannot keep should be permitted to set down the burden. But a burden must be passed, little keeper, not dropped. That is the whole of the old law. That is what your grandmothers knew.*

"I'm not giving my blood to the seal," Freya said. "I promised the Whisperer my blood, and the Whisperer got nothing, and Corvane wants my blood, and Corvane gets nothing. I'm done being poured." She pressed her palm flat against the great scaled neck, over the place where, beneath muscle older than kingdoms, a heart the size of a threshing floor beat slow and failing. "Show me another way."

For a long moment the dragon said nothing. The gods' departure-light dimmed overhead as the last of the old ones squeezed through the broken seal—she could feel them going, feel the pressure in the air easing the way a fever breaks, feel the Whisperer's attention withdrawing up the stair like water down a drain, cheated and furious and too weak now to do anything about it. Somewhere above, armies were still killing each other for reasons that would not matter by morning.

Then Emberwake turned its head, slowly, with the grinding patience of glaciers, and brought its eye level with hers.

*There is the old way,* the dragon said. *And there is the new way. The old way pours the sigil into stone and starves the world of miracles for a thousand years. My mothers made that seal with their dying breaths, and your grandmothers fed it with theirs, and it worked, and it was cruel, and it broke anyway, because cruelty always breaks.* The great eye narrowed. *The new way requires trust. From both of us.*

"I've trusted exactly one person in my life," Freya said. "An orphan boy who tried to trade himself to a god for me. I'm apparently bad at choosing safe things to love." Her hand tightened against the scale. "Tell me."

*Burn it into me,* Emberwake said. *Not into stone. Not into your veins. Into a living heart. A seal that breathes can bend. A seal that sleeps can wake. The gods will come out small, Freya Underhill—small enough to be prayed to and small enough to be refused. No longer weapons. Never again weapons. And the sigil will pass out of your blood entirely, into mine, and die with me, and no crown will ever harvest it.*

"And you'll burn out faster."

*I am already burning out faster.* The dragon's laugh was a rumble she felt through her knees. *This is not sacrifice, little keeper. This is bookkeeping.*

Below them, Corvane had recovered. Freya heard him before she saw him rise—heard the particular controlled breathing of a man reassembling his composure piece by piece, and then his voice, no longer gentle: "Seize her. Now. Cut her down if you must—the blood keeps."

The guards hesitated. They had watched an avatar of an old god unravel into smoke that morning. They looked at the mountain of dying scales, at the girl clinging to it, at their lord with his straight collar, and Freya saw the exact arithmetic cross their faces that she had spent her life watching cross the faces of servants and smugglers and starving villagers: *what does obedience cost me today?*

One of them threw down his sword. Then two. Then the rest backed away, and Corvane stood alone in the shrine's dying light, and for the first time since she'd known him, Lord Corvane, spymaster, regent, architect of wars, looked *small.*

"So," he said quietly. "The taster poisons the table after all."

"It's the only skill I have," Freya said. "You should have let me retire."

He ran for her. Of course he did. She had known he would—had counted on it, in the cold ledger-keeper part of her mind that catalogued symptoms and exits and the weight of every person in a room. He was quick, Corvane, quicker than a man his age had any right to be, and he reached the base of Emberwake's foreleg and got one hand on the scale-groove and began to climb, teeth bared, eyes fixed on her with the pure unclouded want that had been the truest thing about him all along.

*Now,* Emberwake said. *Little keeper. NOW.*

Freya pulled the shard of dragon-glass from her pocket—the fragment she'd pried from the seal at Harrow's Ford, the piece that sang, the piece that had woken a sleeping mountain—and drove it into the groove between the scales, into the soft living seam above the dragon's heart, and opened her own palm with its edge, and let her blood run down the glass.

The sigil woke.

She felt it leave her. There was no other word for it—a drawing-out, a draining, the birthmark on her shoulderblade igniting like a coal blown to flame, and every memory she had of heat and hunger and inheritance pouring out of her and down through the shard and into the vast dark body beneath her hands. It did not hurt. That surprised her most. Nine years she had expected her ending to hurt, and instead it felt like setting down a sack of stones she'd carried so long she'd forgotten it wasn't part of her spine. Like the first breath after swallowing poison and knowing, this once, that it wouldn't kill her.

Emberwake's heart caught fire.

Light exploded outward from the dragon's chest—not red, not gold, but white-shot-with-every-color, the color of the forge and the kiln and the first sunrise, and the fused dragon-glass of the ancient seal answered it, glowing in sympathy across the whole chamber, the carved name UNDERHILL flaring once, bright as a signature on a finished letter, and then going dark forever.

Corvane screamed.

He was halfway up the foreleg when the fire took him. Later—if anyone asked later, and someone always would—Freya would say she never saw whether he reached for the flame or simply couldn't stop climbing. What she remembered was his hand closing on empty air an arm's length from her boot, and his face turning up to hers, and the hunger in it finally, finally satisfied by the thing it had wanted, which was to touch power, and power touching him back. Fire poured up his arm like water finding a channel. He did not burn the way men burn. He burned the way a candle burns when it has finally reached the bottom of itself—quickly, thoroughly, and with a small sound like a sigh. When the light faded there was ash in the shape of a man drifting down to the shrine floor, and a straight-collared ghost of it settling last, almost neatly, over the blood-iron cage he'd built for her.

Freya found she could not summon pity. She searched for it honestly, the way she'd search a dish for arsenic, and found nothing. Perhaps it would come later. Perhaps it wouldn't. She had spent her whole life tasting truths she wasn't allowed to spit out; she was not going to pretend to a feeling she didn't have.

*It is done,* Emberwake said.

The voice was different. Smaller. The great eye had gone from gold to a soft ember-orange, and the fire beneath the dragon's scales had settled into a slow, even glow, like a hearth banked for the night. The sigil shone faintly through the scales over its heart—a branching mark, thorn-and-flame, the shape Freya had worn on her skin since birth. She touched her own shoulder. Smooth skin. Warm, ordinary, unmarked skin.

The inheritance was gone. She was, at last, nobody's bloodline. Nobody's vessel. Nobody's harvest.

Just Freya.

She slid down the dragon's flank and sat hard on the shrine floor, and the shaking started then, the delayed tremor of every poison she'd ever swallowed arriving all at once and finding nothing to do. Emberwake lowered its head until its brow rested on the stone beside her, near enough to touch, near enough that its slow breath stirred her hair.

*What happens now, little keeper?*

"Now," Freya said, and her voice cracked, and she laughed at herself for it, wet-eyed and filthy and lighter than she had been since she was a child, "now I go upstairs, and I find Wick, and I check whether he's eaten anything stupid, and then—" She stopped. Above them, muffled by a hundred feet of rock, the sounds of battle were faltering. Not stopping—battles didn't stop—but changing pitch, the roar of men giving way to shouting, to confusion, to the particular stunned silence of soldiers watching the sky change.

Because the sky was changing. She felt it through the stone, through her bones: the pressure of the sealed world lifting, the old gods walking abroad small and mortal and strange, and somewhere far above, dawn coming through the broken mountain pass onto an army that had just heard a dragon speak.

Emberwake's ear-slits twitched. *They will need to see me,* the dragon said. *A living seal must be seen to believe in. That was always the flaw in the old law—we were hidden, and hidden things become stories, and stories become weapons.* It gathered itself, groaning, scales grinding, and for the first time in three days the movement did not look like dying. It looked like rising. *Help an old keeper up, Freya Underhill. We have a war to disappoint.*

She laughed again—really laughed—and put her shoulder against the enormous jaw, pushing with all the strength her slight body had, which was nothing, which was beside the point. The dragon rose because it chose to rise, and she rose inside its shadow, and together they turned toward the stair and the light pouring down it.

Halfway up, she paused. Below, in the dark of the ruined shrine, the ash that had been Lord Corvane lay scattered over the blood-iron cage, and among the bars something glinted—small, round, unmistakable even at this distance to a woman who had spent nine years reading tables at a glance.

An egg. Pale as riverstone, veined with faint fire, sitting in the wreckage of the cage as if it had been waiting there all along—as if the seal, in its last act, had set aside one seed of everything it had been.

Warmth pulsed off it, faint and steady, like bread fresh from the oven.

Freya stood on the stair a long moment, looking down at it, the light of the new dawn climbing the walls behind her.

"Well," she said to the dragon. "That's going to complicate the funeral."
