## 38. Ashes and Bread

The ovens of Thornfell Green had not been lit in a year, but they remembered the shape of fire.

Freya stood before them in the grey hour before dawn, sleeves rolled, flour to her elbows, and watched the coals settle into that deep orange patience she had loved since childhood—the only kind of fire left in her life that asked nothing of her. The great communal oven behind the shrine hall was built for feasts, for weddings, for the harvest loaves that once fed three hundred people. Now it would feed mourners. It did not seem to care. Ovens were honest that way. They burned what you gave them and gave back bread, and bread was the oldest answer grief had ever gotten from the living.

She had been baking since midnight. Seven batches already cooling on trestle boards under linen cloth: dark barley loaves dense as river stones, white wheat braided into the knot pattern the mountain women used for burials, small round rolls for the children because the children's tables always needed something small enough for small hands. There would be many children at the funeral tables today. Corvane's march down the Vale of Sorrel had taken forty-one souls, eleven of them under ten years, and the mountain folk kept count the way they kept seed grain—carefully, in separate jars, against the winter of remembering.

Her hands moved without her. That was the mercy of bread; it required exactly enough attention to keep the mind from wandering where it wanted to go—down the long dark corridor of the shrine, to the smell of scorched feathers and molten glass, to Emberwake's eye closing like a door. Her fingers kneaded instead. Folded, turned, pressed. Grief could live in the shoulders if you let it, so she put it in the dough and beat it flat.

"You've salted that one twice."

Wick stood in the doorway with his collar inside out and sleep still printed on one cheek, thirteen years old and suddenly taller than he had any right to be, holding two pails of water like an offering. He'd learned to read her work from across a room. She supposed she had taught him more than herb-lore after all.

"Then it will be the loaf for the soldiers," Freya said. "Let them taste what salt tastes like when there's too much of it."

"That's cruel."

"That's bread with an opinion." She took the pails anyway, because his arms were thin even now, even after a year of eating properly. "There's barley water on the shelf. Drink it before you argue with me further."

He drank. He always did as he was told about food, which was how she knew he'd been hungry once, truly hungry, in the way that never entirely leaves a body. She remembered that hunger from her own girlhood—the specific patience of it—and it was the reason she had taken him on when half the court thought tasters should be nameless, interchangeable, disposable. Nobody who had ever been truly hungry wasted food or trusted it blindly. Both lessons made excellent poison-tasters. Only one of them made decent human beings.

Outside, the valley was waking into its second spring since the war. Since the *end* of the war—though no one said war anymore, they said *the troubles*, the way people say a dead man's name only in past tense. The terraces below the village were green again where the fires had passed, green in that aggressive, apologetic way of new growth covering old wounds. Somewhere downslope a smith was hammering, and the sound carried up clean in the cold air. Ordinary sounds. She catalogued them out of habit, the way she once catalogued symptoms: numbness of tongue, prickling at the throat, warmth spreading beneath the sternum. This was what safety felt like, apparently. She was still learning its symptoms too.

* * *

They came down from the high villages at midmorning, and the lowlanders came up from the ford, and for a while the funeral field looked like a market day, which was its own particular heartbreak.

The talks had been going badly for a month. Everyone said so. Queen Maren's regency council—what remained of it, stripped of Corvane and his ambitions and his ash—had sent envoys with silk sashes and carefully worded letters about *amnesty* and *reconciliation*, words that sat in the mouth like unripe fruit. The mountain folk sent weavers' guildsmen and one very old woman named Maren-of-the-Fords, who shared a name with the late queen purely by accident and enjoyed saying so. They met in the shrine hall because it was the largest roofed space standing, and because the dragon-glass wall at its far end—fused smooth and dark as a frozen lake—seemed to make men speak more honestly. Or more quietly. Hard to tell the difference sometimes.

Freya did not attend the talks. She baked for them. It had become a joke of sorts, gentle as such jokes could be: send the sigil-bearer to negotiate and she'll stare at you until you confess your grandmother's sins; send her to the kitchen and everyone eats well and no one dies. She preferred the kitchen. She had spent her whole life being looked *at*—first as furniture, then as a weapon, then as a miracle—and bread asked only to be looked *for*.

But the talks drifted outdoors toward the funeral tables, as talks do when there is food, and by afternoon the negotiation had dissolved into something rawer and more useful: two hundred people eating beside each other in silence.

That silence was the whole treaty, Freya thought, watching from the oven's shadow. A lowland wool merchant breaking bread with the widow of a man whose orchard his king's soldiers had burned. Neither speaking. Both chewing. The merchant's boy stealing a heel of barley loaf and the widow pushing the butter toward him without looking. You cannot draft that. You can only bake it and hope.

Maren-of-the-Fords found her there, as old women reliably find whoever is working.

"They're asking for you inside," the old woman said. "Not the envoys. Our own folk. They want your word that the seal will hold."

"It held," Freya said. "You all saw it hold."

"We saw gods walk out of it small as beggars and start doing chores." The old woman's mouth twitched. "The Whisperer himself hauled stones for the bridge crew last week and complained about the weather. But small isn't gone, girl. Small is just patient. We want the keeper's word."

And there it was again—that word, *keeper*. Grandmother's letters had used it too, in the cramped hand of a woman who'd learned too late that her family's burden was guardianship rather than sacrifice: *We are not the offering, Freya. We are the lock.* Freya carried those letters in a oilcloth packet against her ribs like a second heartbeat, and some days they weighed more than the sigil ever had.

"The mark is scarred over," Freya said, turning her forearm so the old woman could see it. Once it had been a living thing, a whorl of silver-fire lines that burned when the old gods stirred, that flared like a brand when Corvane's poisoned dishes touched her lips, that sang to a dying dragon across half a mountain. Now it was pale ridges of ordinary scar tissue, raised and white, a map of a country that no longer existed. "Whatever I was, I'm not anymore. The seal is in Emberwake's heart now, or in whatever egg came after. Not in my blood. No crown can harvest me. No god needs to bargain with me. I'm just—" she gestured at the flour, the ovens, the mourning bread, "—this."

Maren-of-the-Fords looked at the scar a long time. Then she reached out and touched it with one finger, dry as bark, surprisingly gentle.

"Scar means it healed," she said. "That's better than never-wounded. That's better than untouched." She turned back toward the noise of the crowd. "Come say the words anyway. Keepers don't get to retire, they just get quieter jobs."

* * *

The funeral itself was mountain-style, which meant it was also a meal, which meant Freya cooked through most of it, which was exactly as she wanted it.

They buried the forty-one in the orchard above the village where the apple trees had come back blooming crooked, each grave marked not with stone but with a sapling—a plum for the elders, rowan for the warriors, and for the eleven children, eleven young apples that would bear fruit in five years, when the grief would have softened into something a family could carry to market alongside everything else. Wick had dug most of the holes himself, refusing help, and now stood beside them with dirt permanently under his nails and his jaw set in that way he had when he was determined not to cry in public.

Freya spoke last, because the old woman insisted, and because keepers apparently didn't get to retire.

"I was a taster for nine years," she said, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds do for someone they've decided matters. "Every day I swallowed poison so the queen could eat safely. Nobody thanked me. I don't tell you this for sympathy—I tell you because it taught me something I want to give you today. It taught me that harm is often invisible, and that survival is a craft, and that the ones who protect us are usually the ones nobody sees." She looked down the rows of faces: mountain folk in their good wool, lowlanders in their city colors, the diminished god of the crossroads standing shyly at the edge in a beggar's coat with a bundle of wildflowers he'd clearly picked himself and wasn't sure whether offering them was allowed. "So see each other. That's the whole ceremony. See each other, and eat together, and remember that the opposite of war isn't peace. It's bread. Peace is just the promise. Bread is the practice."

Then she handed out the loaves, and that was the rest of the eulogy.

The Whisperer approached her near dusk, when the torches were being lit and the first songs—hesitant, off-key, mountain-forked melodies—were starting up. Up close, diminished, the old god looked like nothing so much as a tired farmer: broad hands, weathered face, eyes the brown of turned earth. His voice still had that quality underneath, though—the quality of a thing that had listened for centuries and could not entirely stop.

"You fed them well," he said.

"I fed them honestly. Different thing."

"Is it?" He smiled, and it was a smile with no teeth in it anymore, no price, just weariness. "I answered prayers for a thousand years and called it love. You bake bread and call it work. Perhaps we were both lying about the smaller things to survive the larger ones." He set the wildflowers down at her feet—an offering, then, after all. "The child I took. The one from the ford. I have been sitting with her mother every evening. Telling stories. It does not fix anything. But it is what I have."

Freya studied him for a long moment. A year ago this voice had offered her bargains in the dark and taken a child when she refused. Now it apologized in the only currency it had left: presence, unpaid.

"She likes the story about the fox and the moon," Freya heard herself say. "Her mother told me. Tell that one tomorrow."

The god blinked. Something moved across his ruined, mortal face that might have been surprise, might have been gratitude, might simply have been the expression of a creature learning, at last, to receive instructions rather than worship.

"I will," he said.

He left the flowers. Freya picked them up and put them in water, because that was what you did with offerings now—they weren't sacrifices, they were just gifts, and gifts belonged in vessels, on tables, in hands.

* * *

Full dark now, and the fires burning low, and Freya finally alone in the kitchen with the last of the washing-up and Wick asleep in the chair by the hearth, his head tipped back, an unfinished honey-cake still in his hand. She covered him with a blanket and let him keep the cake.

Through the window, the dragon-glass wall of the shrine hall caught the firelight and threw it back in slow ripples, dark and patient as deep water. Somewhere beneath the mountain—or perhaps not beneath anything anymore, perhaps curled in the warm dark of the rebuilt undercroft where they'd moved it—there was an egg the size of a wine cask, mottled grey and gold, warm as a sleeping child.

It had begun to crack three days ago.

She'd felt it before she'd seen it—one fingertip against the shell, and a pulse answering, faint but unmistakable, like a second heartbeat tucked inside her own. The old texts said a new seal needed a new keeper. Grandmother's letters said the Underhills were finished, their line ended in scar tissue and flour, their work done. Both could be true. Both could be wrong.

Freya dried her hands, banked the coals, and stood a moment in the quiet kitchen listening to the enormous small sounds of a world trying again: Wick breathing, coals ticking, the distant murmur of songs, the faint, unmistakable, patient *tap... tap... tap* of something ancient and new knocking at the inside of its own beginning.

Tomorrow there would be more talks, more funerals, more bread. Tomorrow the envoys would ask again for the keeper's word, and she would give it, quieter jobs and all.

Tonight, she just listened to the egg knock.

It sounded, she thought, almost exactly like hunger.
