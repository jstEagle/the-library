## 39. A New Kind of Prayer

The first prayer Freya heard answered after the war was a small one.

She had been kneading dough in the kitchen of the Hearthstone inn when old Maret from the mill came shuffling through the door, hat in her hands, muttering to herself the way she always did. Freya had learned to half-listen to Maret's mutterings years ago—they were mostly complaints about her knees and her late husband's laziness—but this time the words snagged something in the air.

*"Just let the wheel turn true till harvest. That's all I'm asking. Just till harvest."*

It wasn't addressed to anyone. It barely had the shape of a prayer at all. But the light through the kitchen window bent—Freya saw it bend, a slant of gold going briefly green-gold like sunlight through deep water—and when Maret left, whistling, the millwheel turned that evening without a sound for the first time in forty years. No creak. No groan. Just the clean whisper of wood and water doing what it was made to do.

Freya stood in the doorway with flour on her hands and watched the wheel turn, and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted.

No price. She waited three days, four, five, watching Maret for the cost—the wasting, the bad luck, the debt called in. Nothing came. The god who had taken Tam's child at the ford, who had fed on desperation the way Corvane had fed on fear, would have named its fee by now. Would have come smiling into someone's sleep with terms and bargains.

Instead, on the sixth day, Maret baked bread and left half a loaf on the shrine-stone by the crossroads. Not as payment. As thanks. The loaf sat there untouched for two days until birds found it, and the world went on.

*So this is what you look like,* Freya thought, to whatever remained of the Whisperer beneath the mountain, *when no one is starving.*

*This is what we were,* came the answer—not a voice so much as a warmth behind her ribs, faint as banked coals, *before you taught us hunger.*

She flinched, then laughed at herself, alone in the doorway. A year ago she would have catalogued every symptom of that contact: the temperature of her blood, the pressure behind her eyes, the taste of ozone or honey or ash. Now it was just a presence, distant and diffuse, like hearing music from a house two fields away. The gods had poured out of the prison diminished, scattered thin across the Thornfell valleys, and what remained of them was small enough to be honest.

Small enough, perhaps, to be trusted.

* * *

By midsummer, the shrines were being rebuilt.

Not grandly—nothing in the mountains was ever grand—but with the particular stubbornness of people who had decided a thing mattered. The forgotten wayside stones got their moss scraped off and fresh carvings cut into them, crude figures of hearth and river and crow. The shrine beneath Thornfell Keep itself, the sealed place where Freya had once read her family name in dragon-glass, had been walled shut again at her own insistence, but the surface shrines multiplied like spring flowers after fire.

And the prayers changed shape.

During the war, during the Whisperer's ascendancy, the prayers had been teeth: *give me victory, give me revenge, give me his blood.* Freya had watched villages hollow themselves out offering devotion like coin to a moneylender. She still dreamed about it some nights—the child they'd taken, small hands reaching up out of the dark water—and woke with Wick's name in her mouth before she remembered he was safe, asleep down the hall, growing like a weed.

But now the prayers she overheard were different.

*Let the rain hold off till the hay is in.*

*Let my mother's hip mend enough for stairs.*

*Let him write to me. He doesn't have to come home yet. Just a letter.*

Small prayers. Honest ones. Prayers asked without bargaining because the askers knew, somehow, in the way folk know things in their bones, that the price had been lifted. The gods of the Thornfells had become what Freya suspected they'd been at the very beginning, before crowns and seals and dragon-fire made monsters of everyone: neighbors. Strange ones, powerful ones, but neighbors all the same—the kind you left bread out for not out of fear but out of friendliness.

She tested them herself, once, carefully, the way she tested everything. Standing at the crossroads shrine at dusk, she whispered a prayer for Wick—that the boy would stop growing out of his boots so fast, that she could afford the cobbling. It was such a mundane wish it embarrassed her even as she spoke it.

Three days later she found a pair of good boots on her doorstep, only slightly too large, with no note. Wick swore he hadn't done it. Old Hollis the cobbler swore he hadn't either, and looked genuinely puzzled, and then shrugged and said, "Well. Someone did. Wear 'em in health."

Freya wore them in health. And she thought, often, about how strange it was to receive a gift from powers she had helped unchain, and to feel gratitude instead of dread.

* * *

It was Tam's mother, Wren, who finally put the question to her directly.

Wren had lost her son to the Whisperer's bargain at the ford—had spent the whole war believing her boy traded himself for nothing—and when the diminished gods came back gentle, she was among the first to build a new shrine, right outside the cottage where Tam had been born. She tended it daily. She brought other mothers with grieving hearts to sit beside it. And by the first anniversary of the siege, half the valley spoke of her as if she held some office.

So it was Wren who came to the Hearthstone kitchen on a grey morning, sat down heavily on Freya's workbench stool, and said: "They want you to be our priestess."

Freya set down the knife she'd been using to slice onions and looked at her.

"The valley," Wren said. "All of it. Millbrook, Hollowvale, the ford settlements, even some of the lowland folk who've started walking up. They want someone to speak for the shrines. To teach the old words, the proper offerings. To—" she gestured vaguely, "—be the one. You burned your name into the last dragon's heart. You opened the seal and closed it better than it was. If anyone knows how to talk to what's under the mountain, it's you."

"Wren—"

"They've already talked about building you a house up by the high shrine. Stone. With a south-facing window."

Freya wiped her hands on her apron slowly, buying time. Through the kitchen window she could see the village below: children running past the smithy, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys, the ordinary machinery of an ordinary day that a year ago had seemed impossible. Belonging. That was the word for it, the one she kept circling in her own head like a moth around a lamp. For thirty years she had belonged to no one—to the crown that used her tongue and her stomach, to the poison trade that owned her days, to the sigil that owned her blood. And now here was a whole valley saying *we choose you*, and the feeling of it was so warm and so terrifying that she could hardly breathe around it.

Which was exactly why she had to be careful.

"Wren," she said gently, "do you remember what happened the last time the mountains had a priestess?"

Wren's face went still.

"I do," Freya said. "Her name was Underhill too, generations back, and the crown turned her line into lock-keepers and then into keys, and then into blood to be harvested. The moment someone stands between the folk and the gods—a *designated* someone, with a title and a stone house—the crown starts asking questions. What does she know. What does she owe us. Who does she answer to." She shook her head. "I spent my whole life invisible, Wren. I'm not going to make myself visible just to hand the next Corvane a target."

"There's no more Corvane."

"There's always another Corvane." She said it without heat, just the flat certainty of long experience. "Power doesn't die; it changes clothes. Queen Sarel is young and decent, and decent queens get replaced."

Wren was quiet a long moment. Then, stubbornly, because she was Wren: "Then don't take the title. Take the work. Someone has to teach the children which offerings mean what. Someone has to tell the lowlanders that the gods aren't weapons anymore. Someone has to—" she faltered, and her voice cracked on it, "—someone has to help me say the words at Tam's stone. I don't know the words, Freya. My mother didn't teach me because her mother thought the old ways were dead. And now the gods are *here*, sitting at our tables practically, and none of us know how to greet them properly. We're making it up as we go and it frightens me."

And there it was. Not ambition, not politics—just a mother standing at her dead son's shrine wanting to say something true.

Freya sat down across from her, forgetting the onions entirely.

"Here's what I can do," she said. "I won't be your priestess. I won't live in a stone house above the village or wear special colors or stand between you and anything. But I'll teach. Anyone who wants to learn—herb-craft, taster's lore, how to listen for the gods and how to tell an honest presence from a hungry one, how to bake the crossroads bread and why, what the old words meant before they became magic. All of it. Everything I know." She smiled, tired and real. "Knowledge spread thin keeps longer than knowledge hoarded. My grandmother taught me that, in her way. A priestess is one throat the crown can cut. A hundred teachers are a hundred throats, and every one of them passes it on."

Wren stared at her. Then she laughed—a wet, surprised laugh—and wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand.

"A school," she said. "You're talking about a school."

"I suppose I am."

"Whoever heard of a school for priests?"

"For *listeners*," Freya corrected. "For healers and tasters and grandmothers and anyone with ears. We'll call it whatever makes the tax collectors ignore it. School of practical herb-lore. Society for the preservation of regional baking traditions." She was grinning now despite herself. "Trust me. Invisibility is a craft, Wren. I have decades of practice."

Wren reached across the table and gripped her forearm, hard, the mountain way of sealing a thing.

"It's settled then. We'll start with the children. Mine first." Her voice caught again on *mine*, on the singular that had become plural, and Freya covered her hand with her own and let the silence hold what neither of them could say.

That evening, Freya walked up to the crossroads shrine with a fresh loaf, still warm, and broke it open on the stone. The smell of it rose into the dusk. Somewhere behind her ribs, faint as banked coals, something stirred and paid attention.

"Not a priestess," she said aloud, to the valley, to whatever listened. "A teacher. That's the offer. Take it or leave it."

The warmth behind her ribs pulsed once—amused, she thought, or pleased—and the wind coming down off the peaks smelled, very faintly, of woodsmoke and honey.

Accepted, then.

* * *

The school opened in autumn in the old tithe barn below the Hearthstone, with fourteen students the first week and twenty-two the second. Word traveled the way word always traveled in the Thornfells—down smuggler paths and shepherd trails, faster than any official road.

Freya taught everything. She taught the children to identify foxglove by its leaf so they'd never need to gamble on the flower. She taught taster's discipline: how to hold a foreign substance on the tongue, how to read the body's early warnings, how to stay calm while your own blood told you terrible news. She taught the old prayers, but stripped of their bargaining—she made each student rewrite one, removing every *give me* and replacing it with something offered instead, and the results ranged from beautiful to hilarious and she kept the best ones pinned to the beam overhead.

Wick taught the younger ones. He had grown half a head taller over the year and had developed, to Freya's mingled pride and exasperation, a talent for making lessons feel like adventures. He could keep eight children riveted for an hour on the subject of mushroom identification, which Freya considered proof of genuine sorcery. Some evenings she watched him chalk diagrams on the barn wall—leaf shapes, root structures, the sigil-shapes of the old shrines drawn carefully from memory—and felt the ache in her chest that was not grief and not quite joy but lived somewhere between, in the country of things earned.

He was hers, in the way she had chosen and been chosen back. That was the miracle she never prayed about, because some things you don't ask for. You just guard them.

One evening in late autumn, after the students had gone home, Freya stayed behind to bank the fire and found Wick sitting on the chopping block outside, looking up at the mountain.

"You feel it too?" she asked, sitting beside him.

"Yeah." He frowned at the peak where Thornfell Keep's ruins made a broken tooth against the stars. Below them, sealed and silent, lay the shrine, the fused glass, the empty prison. "It's been humming for three days. Like before a storm."

Freya had felt it too—a tremor in the deep places, familiar now, the way you learn the moods of a house you've lived in long enough. She had assumed it was the gods settling, the mountain adjusting to its diminished tenants, the ordinary geology of a world finding its balance.

But this was different. This had a direction. A focus.

"Come on," she said quietly. "Bring the lantern. Let's go see what the mountain wants."

They climbed the shepherd path under a sky full of hard bright stars, Wick carrying the lantern, Freya carrying the old iron key she'd never had the heart to throw away. At the high shrine-stone overlooking the keep, they stopped, and Freya pressed her palm flat against the cold granite.

The hum rose through her bones like a struck bell.

And beneath it, unmistakable, unmistakably *alive*, came a second sound—a small, rhythmic tapping, patient as a heartbeat, coming from far below the sealed door.

From inside the shrine. From the warm dark where they had burned the seal and buried the dragon's ashes.

Something was knocking to get out.

Freya and Wick looked at each other in the lantern-light, and for the first time since the war ended, Freya felt the old taster's alertness sharpen every sense at once—not dread, exactly. Something closer to wonder, wearing dread's coat.

"Well," she said. "I suppose we'd better go down and answer it."
