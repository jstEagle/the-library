## 40. Thornfell Green

The bell over the schoolhouse door had been cast from a melted-down poison vial.

Freya had insisted on that. The smith—old Marta, who had lost a son at the ford and said almost nothing about it except in the way she hammered iron—had looked at her strangely when she'd asked, then nodded once, as if she understood something Freya hadn't said aloud. *You take the thing that was meant to kill and you make it sing instead.* That was the whole of the trade now. That was the whole of her life, and it fit her better than any crown ever could have.

She stood at the window of the schoolhouse on the first true morning of spring and watched the frost burn off Thornfell Green. The village had grown in a year. Where there had been eleven crofts there were now nineteen; where the burned houses of Harrow's Bend had stood, new timber rose pale and raw, and children ran between the scaffolds with the particular shrieking joy of children who had been given back their futures and intended to spend every coin of it. Beyond them, the terraced gardens climbed the lower slopes—wolleysbane fenced carefully apart from feverfew, hemlock behind a locked gate with a sign Wick had painted himself: **KNOW IT BEFORE YOU FEAR IT**.

Below the window, in the yard, twelve students were grinding dried root to powder under Wick's supervision. He stood among them with his sleeves rolled and his voice carrying in the crisp air, and he did not stand like an orphan anymore. He stood like a man who belonged exactly where he was standing.

"*Slower,*" he was saying to a red-haired girl named Bess, who was attacking her mortar like it owed her money. "Poison's patient. You have to be more patient than the poison or it wins. That's the first law."

"And the second?" Bess demanded, not slowing down at all.

"The second is that most things people call poison are only medicine in the wrong hands, at the wrong hour, in the wrong amount." He glanced up—at the window, at her—and grinned. "Ask the mistress. She's swallowed enough wrong doses to write a book."

"Ask the mistress," the students chorused, delighted, because they always did this, because they had learned within their first week that the Taster of Thornfell could be teased if you did it gently, and that being able to tease her meant you were safe with her, and that safety was the entire point of everything.

Freya opened the window. "The second law," she called down, "is that you taste for *yourself* before you ever taste for another. A taster who won't test her own work is a murderer with good manners." She let the laughter rise and settle. "And the third law is breakfast. Come inside before the porridge goes cold. Poison keeps. Porridge doesn't."

They came clattering up the steps, twelve pairs of boots, twelve mortar-stained hands, and she counted them the way she counted everything now—not out of fear, but out of the deep habit of a woman who had spent her whole life making sure everyone at the table survived the meal.

Twelve. All present. All fed.

It still astonished her, some mornings, that this was what her carefulness had become.

* * *

The great room smelled of porridge and honey and the rosemary bundle hanging over the hearth, and Freya moved through it pouring, ladling, correcting grips on spoons, and listening. Listening was half of teaching, she had found. Children told you what they feared by what they asked twice.

"Mistress Underhill." It was Tam, the miller's boy, small and serious, holding out his bowl. "Is it true you tasted for the Queen of all the lowlands? That you ate poison every day for years?"

"It's true."

"Why would anyone do that?"

Freya crouched so they were eye to eye. Around them the room went quiet in the way rooms did when she answered questions like this one honestly, which she always tried to do.

"Because someone I served would have died otherwise," she said. "And because no one else would do it. And because—" she turned her left forearm over, showing the scar where the sigil had been, a pale branching mark now, faded almost to nothing, "—I thought for a long time that being useful was the same thing as being loved. It isn't. But the work itself was good work. Keeping people alive usually is. You just have to be careful the work doesn't eat the worker."

Tam considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Did it eat you?"

"A long way down," Freya said. "But I climbed out. And now I teach climbing."

He nodded, satisfied, and returned to his porridge, and the room resumed its noise, and Freya stood in the middle of it with her heart doing that slow unfamiliar swelling it still did sometimes—the feeling of a door opening in a wall she had believed was solid all the way through.

Wick appeared at her elbow with two cups of willowbark tea, pressed one into her hand, and touched his cup to hers without a word. They had a whole language of gestures now, the two of them. This one meant: *you're doing it again, standing in the middle of things feeling surprised that you're allowed.*

"I'm allowed," she murmured.

"You're allowed," he agreed. "Say it faster next time."

* * *

Midmorning brought the lowlanders.

They came every few weeks now—a cart over the pass, a nervous clerk or a gray-cloaked healer or, once, an actual lordling who kept looking at the mountains like he expected them to bite. The regency council that had replaced Corvane's ambition sent letters sealed in green wax, polite and slightly frightened, asking after grain yields and border stones and whether the mountains truly meant to keep their own law. The mountain folk sent back answers written in Wick's neat hand, courteous and slightly frightening, confirming that yes, they did.

Today it was a healer named Serah, young, competent, with ink stains on her fingers and the desperate look of someone who had traveled a long way to ask a favor.

"They're dying in the river valleys," Serah said, in the schoolhouse office, surrounded by Freya's labeled jars and her grandmother's letters framed on the wall. "A fever. It started near the old shrine road. We've used everything we know."

Freya poured tea and thought. Fever near the shrine road. In the year since the gods had come out diminished—mortal-ish, the scholars argued about the right word, though the villagers had settled on *the Quiet Ones*, because they no longer thundered, they only murmured—the land had been healing in strange uneven ways. Old wells running sweet. Blighted orchards fruiting. And occasionally, where some old wound in the world met some new tenderness, things growing wrong.

"Show me the symptoms," Freya said. "All of them. Especially the ones you think aren't important."

Serah talked. Freya listened, asked questions, made notes, and by the time the sun stood high she had a working theory involving contaminated water, a flowering weed the lowlanders didn't recognize, and a treatment that was three parts feverfew and one part humility. She packed a satchel herself—old habits—and then stopped, hand on the strap.

"Come with me," she said to Wick, who was waiting in the doorway with his own bag already shouldered. Of course he'd packed. Of course he had.

"Both of us?" Serah blinked. "The school—"

"Will keep for three days," Freya said. "Bess can run the beginners. She's ready, even if she grinds root like she's avenging something." She smiled to take the sting out, and got a wet-eyed nod from the lowland healer, and felt the familiar double weight of leaving: guilt and rightness, always together, always balanced.

Before she left she did what she did every time she left now. She walked down through the terraces to the warm south-facing hollow behind the schoolhouse, where the ground was soft and black and planted over with thorn roses, and she stood a while among the graves of Harrow's Bend. Forty-one markers. She knew each name. She had buried each name. Corvane's ashes had gone into unmarked ground far away, unmourned, which was its own kind of justice and not a satisfying one, and Freya had learned to live with unsatisfying justice the way she'd learned to live with everything: carefully, and without pretending it didn't hurt.

"I'm going down to the valleys," she told the roses. "Back in three days. Keep growing."

The wind moved through the thorns, and if it sounded a little like an answer, well. The Quiet Ones were everywhere now, small and murmuring, answering honest prayers without price. Freya had stopped pretending she couldn't hear them. She just didn't pray often. She preferred to talk to gardens.

* * *

The valley fever broke in four days instead of the predicted ten, and Serah wept on her, and the lowland village—which a year ago would have barred its doors against a mountain witch—fed them roast fowl and asked shy, hungry questions about the school. *Could they send a daughter? A son? How much did it cost?*

"Nothing," Freya said, and watched hope move across faces like weather. "Nothing but the willingness to learn the laws. There are only three."

On the road home, with the pass rising gold-green around them and Wick whittling a whistle from alder wood as he walked, she let herself feel it: the future, not as a cliff edge or a cage, but as a road. Her road. Chosen. Guarded, because she would never stop guarding anything—but hers.

"Wick," she said. "When we get back, I want to add a fourth law."

"There are only ever three laws. It's traditional."

"The fourth is that we send healers down as well as up. Lowlanders here, mountain folk there. Both directions, forever. If Corvane taught me anything—" she spat the name like a seed, "—it's what happens when people only ever look one way across a border."

Wick carved a moment longer. Then: "Bess will want to go first. She's been drawing maps of the lowlands for a month and thinks I don't notice."

"I notice everything. It's my defining trait."

"It's very annoying," he said fondly, and threw the finished whistle to her, and she caught it, and blew one clear note that startled ravens off the pines, and the sound went ringing down the mountain ahead of them like a herald.

* * *

They came up over the last ridge at dusk, and Freya knew before she saw it that something was different, because the whole village was standing in the green.

Not panicking—standing. Gathered in a wide ring around the hollow behind the schoolhouse, around the thorn roses and the graves, around—

Around the egg.

They had carried it up from the shrine in the first thaw, Marta and six others, wrapped in wool and prayer and an enormous amount of argument about whether it was wise. Emberwake had been dead a year; the great bones lay far below in the shattered dark of the Deep Door, and the mountain folk had wanted the egg where a keeper's last fire had once warmed a seal. Freya had built it a cradle of heated stone and river sand behind the schoolhouse, checked it daily, recorded its temperature in a ledger with the same meticulousness she'd once catalogued poisons. Warm. Steady. Patient. An egg could wait a century; dragons were like that.

It was not waiting anymore.

The shell glowed.

Not reflected firelight—*glowed*, from within, a deep amber pulse like a heartbeat slowed to the tempo of mountains, and with every pulse the hairline cracks across its surface spread a little further, and from the cracks came a sound Freya felt in her teeth and her scars and the place where the sigil used to be: a thin, reedy, unmistakable note of hunger.

The crowd parted for her the way it always did now—not servile, not fearful, just making room, the way you make room for the person whose trade it is to walk toward the dangerous thing first.

"Mistress," Marta said quietly. "Started an hour past sundown. We didn't touch it. We didn't know—"

"You did right." Freya knelt beside the cradle, close enough to feel the heat on her face, and laid her scarred forearm against the warm stone rim. "Everyone back a dozen paces. Wick—"

"Already here." He was at her shoulder, calm, his bag still slung, his eyes huge and shining. "What do we do?"

"What we always do." She heard her own voice come out steady and marveled at it, somewhere beneath the roaring of her blood. "We prepare for every outcome, we stay calm, and we feed whatever hatches before it decides we're food." She glanced back at the ring of faces—Marta, Bess, Tam, all of them, the whole improbable village of Thornfell Green lit amber by the egg's glow. "Someone fetch the goat carcass from Marta's cold store. The big one. And honey—dragons are said to love honey, according to grandmother's letters, and grandmother was never wrong about food."

People scattered, laughing shakily, and then it was just her and Wick and the egg, pulsing, cracking, singing its thin fierce note into the spring dark.

"Do you think it knows?" Wick whispered. "About—him. About what his—parent?—did. What you did."

"I think," Freya said slowly, "that it knows warmth when it feels it. Everything else it'll learn." She put her palm flat against the shell, and the shell pressed back, and through her arm ran a tremor that was not quite heat and not quite sound and not quite language, but was unmistakably, anciently, *greeting*.

Her eyes stung. She let them.

"One year ago I belonged to a crown," she said softly, to the egg, to Wick, to the graves and the roses and the green. "Then to a bloodline. Then to a war. I'm done belonging to things bigger than me." The crack nearest her palm widened with a sound like ice on a lake in thaw. "So here's the bargain, little one, and I learned bargaining from gods and queens, so mind it well: you belong to yourself. And I'll belong to this school, and these people, and this mountain. And between us we'll hold the borders—all of them, both directions—"

The shell burst.

Amber light flooded the hollow, and the crowd gasped as one creature, and out of the ruin of the egg lifted a head no bigger than a cat's, slick and dark and blinking, with eyes like coals catching their first breath of air. It looked at the ring of strangers. It looked at the roses on the graves. It looked, last and longest, at Freya—at her face, at her scarred arm, at whatever it was dragons saw when they looked at the last of the Underhills—and it made a sound halfway between a chirp and a challenge, and opened a mouth full of brand-new fire.

"Hungry," Freya translated, already reaching for the honey. "Of course it is. First law of everything alive."

Behind her, Wick laughed—wet, bright, unguarded—and the village surged gently closer, and above the schoolhouse the first stars of the year came out over Thornfell Green, and the small dragon drank honey from a taster's scarred hand while the mountain, patient as an egg, held them all.

Somewhere far below, in the dark where a keeper slept at last, Freya liked to think something heard the note of that first cry and was, finally, at peace.

She had work tomorrow. Lessons, letters, a fourth law to write over the door.

But tonight she sat in the warm grass with honey on her fingers and her family gathered round, and for the first time in her whole careful life, Freya Underhill tasted nothing at all—

—and found it sweet.
