## 1. The Ledger of Impossible Things

The candle had burned down to the width of a thumbnail when Astrid Vasquez finished reconciling the third ledger of the night, and the silence of the counting room settled over him like a coat he'd worn so long he'd forgotten it wasn't his own skin.

He dipped his pen, blotted it against the rim of the well, and turned to the next entry in the Gilded Thorn's autumn inventory.

*One stolen year. Vintage: the summer of a shepherd boy's twelfth birthday. Condition: sun-bleached at the edges; the taste of honey still detectable. Provenance discreet.*

His handwriting did not waver. That was the first thing they taught you in the scriptorium beneath the Thorn—not penmanship, not arithmetic, but steadiness. A clerk's hand that trembled invited questions, and questions, in the Gilded Thorn, were a currency spent faster than gold. Astrid had been writing for the guild for eleven years, and his hand had not trembled once. He sometimes wondered if that was an accomplishment or a symptom.

Around him, the counting room breathed with the quiet industry of the other night-clerks. Old Merrick two desks down, cataloguing bottled griefs by weight—griefs, Astrid knew from experience, were weighed rather than measured, because sorrow changed density depending on how long it had been carried. Pellia at the far table, entering names into the Registry of Unspoken Words: names that had died on someone's tongue before they could be given to a child, names that had been swallowed at deathbeds, one name that had allegedly never been spoken at all and which Pellia handled with tongs, literally, silver tongs, because paper touched by that name tended to forget it had ever been written on.

Astrid did not look at them. Looking invited conversation, and conversation invited *noticing*, and being noticed was the one inventory item Astrid Vasquez had spent a decade ensuring would never appear in any ledger.

It was not difficult, being invisible among clerks. Clerks were the most self-absorbed creatures in any city; they saw each other the way furniture sees furniture. Astrid had simply made himself good at his work—unremarkably, reliably, boringly good—and boredom was the best cloak ever stitched. When Veyra's auditors came through twice a year, their eyes slid off him like rain off waxed canvas. *The quiet one. The one who balances columns. Is there something wrong with him?* No. Nothing wrong. Nothing remarkable. A man shaped like a footnote.

He was thirty-one years old and he could not remember the last time anyone had said his name aloud.

The thought arrived, as it always did around midnight, uninvited and unwelcome, and Astrid did what he always did with it: he folded it small and filed it under *irrelevant*, in the vast internal ledger he kept behind his ribs, where all the entries he refused to read were stored in perfect order. Loneliness, filed. Fear, filed. The dream he'd had last week—the burning city, the tower of white stone singing as it fell—filed, though that one kept trying to climb back out of its drawer.

*Steady hands,* he reminded himself, and wrote on.

*One grief, decanted. Source withheld per contract. Note: heavier than standard; may contain guilt. Do not shelve near joy.*

The Gilded Thorn occupied three converted granaries behind the Silk Exchange, and to the city of Vhalen's customs officers it appeared, on paper, to deal in rare dyes and antiquarian books. This was true in the way that a knife's reflection is true. The dye trade was real; the books were real; and threaded through them both, hidden in crates marked *mordant* and *fixed alum*, moved the guild's actual commerce: the impossible things. Stolen years sealed in amber vials. Bottled griefs with wax stamped seals. Courage extracted from dying soldiers and sold to cowards with deep pockets. First kisses, purchased from widows clearing out a dead husband's effects. One entire childhood, currently shelved in Vault Nine, belonging to no one, because the man who'd sold it had wanted to stop flinching at raised voices and had neglected to consider what he'd be afterward.

Astrid had appraised that childhood himself. It had smelled like rain on hot stone and someone else's cooking. He had entered it into the ledger in his steady hand and gone home—he had a room above a chandler's, narrow as a coffin standing up—and lain awake until dawn doing nothing at all, which was as close as he came to weeping.

That was the trade, and he understood the trade. Everyone who sold to the Thorn sold because carrying the thing had become worse than losing it. The guild did not steal your joys; it bought your sorrows, and paid fairly, and this made it somehow more monstrous than simple theft could ever be. Theft left you angry. Sale left you *lighter*, and lightness, Astrid had learned from the ledgers, was its own kind of wound. He had watched sellers walk out of the front office looking like amputees who hadn't yet noticed which limb was gone.

And he had stayed. Eleven years. Because the Thorn paid in coin that spent anywhere, and because the counting room was warm in winter, and because a man who is no one has nowhere better to be.

The candle guttered. Around him the other clerks worked on, bent and gray, and Astrid realized with a small cold start that he was looking at his own future: Merrick, sixty and alone, who spoke to the griefs more tenderly than to people. Pellia, who had once told him—Pellia! initiating conversation!—that she'd sold her mother's voice to the Thorn twenty years ago and sometimes came down to the vault just to hold the bottle. *You can't hear anything through the glass,* she'd said. *But you can feel it. Like a bird waiting.* Then she'd looked horrified at her own confession and never spoken to him again.

He had thought about that bottle for weeks.

Astrid set down his pen and flexed his fingers, and told himself the ache in his chest was the candle smoke.

* * *

At half past midnight, the runners brought the new acquisitions down from the receiving floor, and the counting room's rhythm changed the way a river changes around a submerged rock. New items meant new entries, and new entries meant appraisal, and appraisal meant opening things that were sometimes better left shut.

Astrid took the manifest without being asked, because taking work was another way of staying invisible—clerks resented the eager ones, but they forgot them even faster than the lazy ones.

The first crate held years. Seven vials, amber-sealed, each labeled in the receiver's blunt script. *Boy, age 9, festival day. Woman, age 40, last day of health. Man, age 55, ordinary Tuesday (seller noted "nothing special about it, that's the point").* Astrid entered each one, verified the seals, noted the vintage years. Routine. The work of it soothed him the way prayer was supposed to soothe other men, each column a rosary of other people's losses.

The second crate was stranger. It held, according to the manifest, *one echo, still wet*—an echo of a bell that had rung in a village that no longer existed, salvaged before the village finished forgetting itself. And beneath the straw padding, wrapped in oilcloth:

*One silence, gently used.*

Astrid stopped.

He had catalogued strange silences before. The Thorn dealt in them more often than civilians would credit—a widow's silence where her husband's voice had been, sold by sons who couldn't bear the shape of it at the dinner table; the silence of a house after the children left, purchased by a speculator who collected architectural melancholies. Those silences came in bottles like everything else. You could hold them to the light and see nothing, which was the point, but the glass always fogged slightly from the inside, as if the absence were breathing.

This one had no bottle.

It lay in the crate as an actual absence, a hand-shaped hole in the world about the size of a loaf of bread, and the straw around it had bent away from it as though afraid. When Astrid leaned close, the lamplight didn't quite reach the center of it. His ears felt full, the way they do before thunder.

*Gently used,* the manifest said, in a receiver's hand he didn't recognize. *Seller unknown. Seller's agent found the item already packaged. Agent declined to enter the vault room. Agent resigned.*

Below that, in different ink, older ink, ink that made Astrid's eyes slide off it and have to be dragged back by force:

*Note: This is not the absence of sound. This is a listening that has stopped. Handle accordingly.*

Astrid sat very still.

The counting room went on around him—Merrick's pen scratching, Pellia's tongs clicking, somewhere below a vault door booming shut—but all of it seemed to happen at a distance now, behind glass, the way sounds reached you from underwater. Because he knew this silence. Not this specific silence, perhaps, but the *species* of it, the way you know a face across ten years and a crowd. It was the same quality of stopped-listening that had lived in his prayers for a decade.

Every night since he was nineteen, Astrid had knelt at a shrine the size of a shoebox—a scrap of white stone, a stub of black candle, a name he'd found scratched into the wall of the orphanage loft and copied onto paper because the wall itself was being plastered over. *Vesperian.* No doctrine. No priesthood. No church anywhere in Vhalen or the nine kingdoms beyond it. Just the name, and the oath he'd sworn at nineteen with the particular ferocity of a boy who has nothing else to give: *I will keep your accounts. I will remember what is forgotten. Where you are silent, I will speak.*

And every night for ten years, the god had said nothing back. No sign, no warmth, no presence. Astrid had prayed into a well so deep the stone never even got damp. Other mercenary-brothers bound to living gods received visions, strength, small miracles; Astrid received nothing, and had built his whole faith on the stubborn architecture of one-sided devotion, and had told himself for a decade that this was holiness rather than abandonment.

Now here, in a crate, in his counting room, lay a piece of that same silence. Gently used. As though someone had taken it out and worn it.

As though it had been *removed* from somewhere.

His hand, steady for eleven years, hovered over the entry line and—trembled. Once. He pressed it flat against the desk until the shaking stopped.

*Item: one silence, gently used,* he wrote. *Provenance unknown. Appraiser's note: resembles nothing in catalogue.*

It was a lie. It resembled one thing in catalogue. It resembled the inside of his own chest, the place where his prayers went and did not echo, and Astrid sat with that knowledge while the candle burned lower and the other clerks' pens scratched like insects in the walls.

He should log it for the vault. Standard procedure. Item goes to Vault Six, the acoustically dead one, and a senior appraiser examines it at dawn, and by tomorrow noon some collector will have bought it and Astrid will never think of it again except at night, the way he thought of Pellia's bottle, the way he thought of the burning city in his dreams.

Instead, he found himself leaning close again, close enough that the cold of the thing touched his face like the memory of a hand.

And the silence *noticed* him.

There was no other word for it. The stopped listening turned, the way a head turns. Ten years of praying into emptiness, and now the emptiness had turned around to look at him from inside a shipping crate, and Astrid felt something move through the shelves of his internal ledger—all those carefully filed drawers rattling open at once, loneliness and fear and the burning city tumbling out into the aisles of him—and somewhere very far away, farther than the vaults, farther than the city, something that had not spoken in ten years gathered itself to make a sound.

What came was not a voice. It was barely a breath. It was one word, pressed thin as a leaf between the pages of the world, arriving not in his ears but in the steadiness of his own hand:

*Astrid.*

His name. Spoken aloud by no one, in a room where no one knew it, after eleven years of no one saying it.

The candle went out.

In the dark, all across the counting room, the other clerks paused—one heartbeat, two—as if the whole building had drawn a breath. Then Merrick's pen resumed. Pellia's tongs clicked. The night went on, ordinary and gray, and Astrid Vasquez sat at his desk with his heart slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out of a locked room, staring at a crate that contained, according to the ledger, nothing at all.

He relit the candle with fingers that shook and could not be steadied. He closed the ledger. He logged the silence for Vault Six, because procedure was procedure and procedure was the only prayer he'd ever been answered by.

But he did not call for the runners to take it down.

He told himself he would, in the morning. He told himself a great many things, in the careful professional dark, while outside in the alley below the receiving door, a courier with blood already drying on his lips came limping out of the rain toward the only light in the Gilded Thorn that burned past midnight—

the light above the window of the clerk who kept accounts for impossible things, and whose name, tonight, for the first time in ten years, the darkness itself had learned.
