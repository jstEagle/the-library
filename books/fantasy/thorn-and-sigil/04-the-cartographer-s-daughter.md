## 4. The Cartographer's Daughter

Wren Sable had three rules, and she'd carved them into the underside of her workbench where no inspector would ever think to look: *never sign your own work, never sell to someone twice, and never let anyone see your hands shake.*

She was breaking the third rule now.

The map lay under her loupe like a patient under a surgeon's blade—except no surgeon had ever felt her heart stop the way it stopped when she saw the ink. She had told herself she was imagining it. She had told herself that grief made forgers of everyone, that the bereaved saw their dead in crowds and doorways and, apparently, in pigment. But the loupe did not lie, and neither did the ink.

It was mixed with ox gall and iron-gall tannin and something else—a mineral wash she'd spent her childhood watching her father coax out of riverbed clay, the recipe he'd refused to write down because, he said, *a secret written down is a secret surrendered*. The color sat on the vellum with a particular weight, a faint blue-black bloom at the edges of each stroke where the wash pooled as it dried. Cartographers' guild ink sat flat. Church ink sat glossy. This ink *bloomed*.

Her father's ink. Her father's hand—the confident sweep of the river deltas, the little stylized waves he always put in open water even though every master cartographer in Vharenth had told him it was unprofessional, and he'd done it anyway because he said maps should remember they were pictures of living things.

Tobias Sable had been missing for two years. Not dead—Wren refused the word—but missing, the way a tooth is missing, the way a load-bearing wall is missing. He had walked out of their rented rooms in Lowbridge with his satchel and his good coat and a commission he wouldn't name, and he had kissed her forehead and said, *If anyone asks, you never learned anything from me.* Which was, she understood now, the closest he had ever come to saying goodbye.

And now his map was in the hands of a Thorn clerk who looked at her like a man doing arithmetic on how much trouble she was worth.

* * *

Astrid Vasquez had not wanted to come to the Drowned Quill. He had wanted to stay in his room with the map and the shutters closed and the memory of the courier's blood still tacky somewhere behind his eyes. But the map was drawn in an ink he didn't recognize on vellum he didn't recognize, and there was exactly one person in this quarter who could read both, and that person was Wren Sable, and Wren Sable charged in favors rather than coin, which meant Astrid owed her, which meant she could look at him however long she liked.

She was looking at him now across her workbench, and her hands were shaking, and he noticed, because noticing things was the one skill the Gilded Thorn had sharpened in him until it cut.

"You said you found this," she said.

"I said a courier brought it to me."

"A courier." She set the loupe down with exaggerated care. "A dying courier, if the blood is anything to go by—and it's real blood, by the way, I checked, don't ask me why I checked. A dying stranger pressed a map into your hands, drawn by a man who has been missing two years, and you brought it to me without mentioning any of this."

"I'm mentioning it now."

"Three days ago! You came here three days ago for forged pilgrim papers and you sat *right there*—" she pointed at the stool, "—and you watched me copy a bishop's seal while carrying my father's last known work in your coat, and you said *nothing*."

"What would you have done?"

Wren opened her mouth. Closed it. What would she have done? She would have burned it, probably. Or sold it. Or taken it apart stitch by stitch looking for the forgery, because a map that good had to be a forgery, because the alternative was that her father had been somewhere dangerous enough to die over and hadn't told her.

"That's not the point," she said, which was what people said when it was entirely the point.

"It's a fair point," Astrid said mildly, and she hated him a little for it.

He wasn't what she expected from a Thorn man. She'd dealt with Thorn clerks before—all of them polished, all of them pleasant the way a well-made knife is pleasant, all of them radiating the quiet certainty that whatever happened next, they would be fine. Astrid Vasquez had the polish but none of the certainty. He sat like a man waiting to be evicted from his own body. There was ink on his fingers—not her father's ink, just ledger-ink, the cheap kind—and he kept rubbing his thumb against them like he was trying to wipe away something that wasn't there.

"So," she said. "You know whose work this is."

"You told me last night. You were very clear."

"I told you whose *ink*. Now tell me why a guild clerk comes to a forger instead of taking a Thorn map to the Thorn archivists."

Something moved behind his eyes—quick, submerged, gone. "Because the Thorn doesn't have archivists for this. Because when I asked questions about it, my mistress started asking questions back. And because," he paused, choosing the words like a man crossing ice, "you're the only person in this city who might want the same thing I do."

"Which is what?"

"To know where he got the ink."

* * *

They argued for another hour. It was, Wren reflected later, almost restful—arguing with someone who argued properly, who conceded small points and held large ones, who didn't try to charm her or steamroll her but simply stood his ground like a fence post. She learned things about him in the argument the way you learn about a building from its scaffolding: he counted before he answered hard questions; he touched the ledger callus on his middle finger when he lied, which was nearly never, which frightened her more than lying would have; and when she mentioned her father, he went very still, the way men go still around things they've been taught are holy or forbidden.

In the end they struck terms, because everything in Wren's life came down to terms.

She would read the map fully—its routes, its marginalia, its cartographic flourishes—in exchange for his protection against whoever killed the courier, because if the courier had died for this map then its maker's daughter was already standing in the blast radius whether she liked it or not. In exchange, he would answer her questions truthfully, and she would know if he didn't, because she'd spent twenty-two years reading seals and seals were just lies with good posture.

"One condition," she said, as he rose to leave. "I work here. Not in some Thorn safehouse, not in an alley. Here. My bench, my tools, my light."

"The Thorn knows where you live."

"The Thorn knew where I lived yesterday too, and I'm still breathing. Whatever's coming for this map already knows my name, Astrid. Moving won't save me. Doing nothing will kill me slower."

He looked at her for a long moment, and she braced for the argument, and it didn't come. "All right," he said simply, and sat back down, and she realized with a small internal jolt that he had agreed because it was right, not because it was clever, and that she had no protocol whatsoever for dealing with that.

* * *

By lamplight, with the shutters barred and rain beginning against the glass, Wren read her father's map the way she'd been taught to read everything: twice, once for what it showed and once for what it hid.

The first pass was geography—the capital, the pilgrim roads, the mountain passes east of the Saltmarshes, rendered in Tobias Sable's immaculate hand. But the second pass was where the map began to speak in its other voice. The stylized waves in the open water: eleven of them, and eleven was wrong, because her father drew waves in threes. Three rivers marked with bridge symbols that appeared on no other map she knew. A compass rose whose north needle pointed not north but *up*—toward a symbol at the top margin so faint it was nearly invisible, a circle crossed by seven lines.

She knew that symbol. Every child raised within earshot of a temple bell knew it, the way children know lightning: the Sigil. The binding-sign. The mark the priesthood used for the old covenant, the one that chained something beneath the capital so long ago that the chaining had become a bedtime story, then a proverb, then nothing at all.

Her father had drawn the Sigil on a map of the king's own roads.

"Wren." Astrid's voice was careful. He'd been watching her face, and whatever it was doing had alarmed him. "What is it?"

"My father didn't get lost," she said, and her voice came out steadier than her hands. "Everyone said he must have run from debts, or run from the guilds, or run off with some woman. He didn't run. He was *sent*. Commissioned. Someone paid Tobias Sable—who never took a commission he couldn't verify, who made me swear off forging because 'a lie on paper becomes a lie in the hand'—someone paid that man to map a route to the thing the Sigil guards, and he knew it would get him killed, so he hid the true road inside the pretty parts. The waves. The bridges. The needle pointing up." She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "He used to say a map is a letter to someone who hasn't arrived yet."

Astrid was silent for a moment. When he spoke, his voice had changed—lower, rougher, like something dragged up from under floorboards. "The courier said a name. Before he died. A name I haven't heard spoken aloud in ten years, since my god went quiet."

"Vesperian," Wren said, because Astrid had told her that much the night before, and because saying it in this room, over this map, made it suddenly, horribly real.

"Yes." His jaw worked. "My god's name. From a stranger's mouth. On your father's map." He looked down at his ink-stained fingers. "Wren, I keep a ledger of impossible things for a living. Stolen years. Bottled grief. And in ten years of bookkeeping I have never once seen an entry that scared me until this week."

"Then we're both frightened," she said. "Good. Frightened people check their work."

That startled a breath of laughter out of him—short, rusty, like a hinge unused for years. She filed the sound away and pretended she hadn't noticed it, and he pretended the same, and between those two pretenses something fragile and unnamed settled over the workbench like the first snow of a season neither of them was ready for.

* * *

They worked until the candles guttered. Wren drafted travel papers while Astrid dictated details from memory—names, dates, toll-stamps from three provinces, all of it stored in his head with the terrible precision of a man who had spent a decade recording other people's secrets and keeping none of his own. She forged; he verified. Their hands passed documents back and forth across the bench, and each time their fingers brushed, both of them flinched a fraction, and both of them pretended not to notice, and the pretense became its own kind of courtesy.

"Your papers will hold at the city gates and the provincial checkpoints," she said near dawn, sanding the ink dry on the last page. "They won't survive a Thorn audit. Nothing survives a Thorn audit. That's rather the point of Thorn audits."

"Then we move fast."

"*We*?" She looked up sharply. "No. No, Astrid, I said I'd read the map, not walk it. I'm a forger. My whole skill set is staying indoors."

"And your father's whole skill set was making maps of places he'd never survive." Astrid gathered the papers, squared their edges against the bench—an accountant's habit, precise even now. "Whoever commissioned him will assume his daughter can read his work. They'll come here eventually. You know that better than I do."

She did know it. That was the unforgivable part. Two years of telling herself her father's disappearance had nothing to do with her, and the map on her bench said otherwise in her father's own blooming blue-black hand: *everything I touch, you inherit.*

"If we're traveling together," she said, because arguing about fate was pointless and arguing about logistics was not, "there are rules. One: you don't lie to me. Two: I don't lie to you. Three: when one of us says run, we run, and the philosophy happens later."

"Four," Astrid said. "Nobody signs anything."

Despite herself—despite the fear sitting in her chest like swallowed glass—Wren smiled. "You'd have made a decent forger."

"No," he said quietly. "I'd have made a terrible one. I can't stand keeping my own books."

It was the most honest thing either of them had said all night, and it landed in the room like a dropped glass, and neither of them bent to pick it up.

Dawn was graying the shutters when the knock came—three soft raps, patient, professional. Astrid's head came up. Wren's blood went cold, because she knew that knock. Everyone in the trades knew that knock. It was the knock of a man who had all the time in the world because the world belonged to his employer.

Through the frosted pane of the street door, backlit by the early light, stood a silhouette wearing the grey-and-gold of the Gilded Thorn, and beside it, fainter, the outline of a second figure who stood the way collectors stand when they have already decided the collection is complete.

"Astrid," Wren whispered. "How many exits does this room have?"

He was already moving, gathering the map, his face gone pale and set. "One," he said. "But I know a cistern."

The knock came again. Patient. Professional. Certain.
