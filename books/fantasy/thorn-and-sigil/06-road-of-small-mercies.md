## 6. Road of Small Mercies

Wren Sable had learned to measure people by the way they slept. Her father used to doze sitting up in his cart, hands loose around a cup of cold tea, one eye always half-open for the sound of hooves. Astrid Vasquez did not sleep at all so much as he lay down beside a fire and performed unconsciousness—spine straight even on bare ground, boots within reach of one hand, the other curled loosely near the knife at his hip as though sleep itself might try to rob him.

Three nights on the pilgrim road out of Thornhollow, and she had not once seen him truly rest.

"You're staring," he said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm assessing," Wren corrected. She sat cross-legged with her father's map across her knees, though there was nothing left to study in it that she hadn't memorized twice. The ink had gone still since the rockfields, lying flat and honest like any ordinary map, and she found she missed its small treacheries. "You sleep like a man expecting an audit."

"In my experience, audits come at inconvenient hours."

"You were a clerk."

"I *am* a clerk." He opened one eye. In the firelight it was the color of steeped tea, ordinary and watchful. "Clerkship is not a former condition. It's a diagnosis. There's no cure."

She snorted despite herself. This was how their conversations went now: barbed little exchanges, each one testing whether the other would flinch, neither of them quite willing to admit that they'd been traveling companions long enough to have a rhythm. Wren had spent her life forging other people's trust—seals, signatures, letters of safe passage—and she knew better than most that trust freely given was just forgery waiting to be discovered. But Astrid hadn't asked her to be anyone but what she was. He'd simply looked at her forged papers, said "the counterfeiter's mark is elegant," and walked on. It was, she suspected, the closest thing to affection either of them could afford.

The pilgrim road wound south through country that couldn't decide what it wanted to be—hedgerows giving way to gorse, gorse to grey stone hills dotted with wayshrines so old their gods had worn faceless. Other travelers passed them in ones and twos: penitents in rope belts, a tinker with a cart of dented pots, two sisters carrying a caged dove between them like something precious. All of them, Wren noted, gave Astrid's ledger-satchel a wide berth without knowing why. Guild work left a smell on a man, apparently. Or maybe it was just that he stood like someone who counted things.

"Coin check," Astrid said, sitting up at last. It had become ritual, this nightly inventory—the clerk's catechism. He untied the purse from his belt, and Wren watched his fingers stop moving.

"What?"

"It's lighter." He weighed the purse in his palm with the frown of a man reading a column whose sums didn't add. "It's lighter by—" he shook it once, listening, "—four marks. Maybe five."

Wren looked at the purse. Then at the ground around their campsite. Then at the low stone wall behind them, where a shadow detached itself from shadow and bolted.

"*Hey*—"

Astrid was already moving. For a clerk, the man could run; he cleared the wall in three strides and vanished into the dark beyond it, and Wren heard the unmistakable sounds of pursuit—footfalls, a squawked curse, the clatter of someone going over rocks. She gathered the map, the satchel, and the frying pan (priorities), and followed at a jog, because if Astrid got himself killed chasing a thief three days into their escape from the Gilded Thorn, Veyra's collectors wouldn't even need to bother finishing the job.

She found them in the hollow past the wall: Astrid, breath steaming, standing over a small figure backed against a wayshrine. The shrine was one of the faceless ones, its saint long since weathered smooth, and huddled in its niche was a child—ten years old, perhaps eleven, though hunger made age impossible to read. A girl, by the cut of the too-large coat she wore belted with twine. Hair chopped raggedly short. Eyes enormous and utterly unrepentant, glinting in the moonlight like coins at the bottom of a well.

In her fist, Astrid's coin purse. Around her neck, on a leather cord, an assortment of stolen treasures: buttons, a bird skull, half a tin locket.

"Give it back," Astrid said.

"No," said the child.

Wren bit the inside of her cheek. There was no negotiation happening here; the child had simply stated a fact about the universe, the way one stated that water was wet or that clerks were tedious.

"You picked my pocket while I was *awake*."

"Yeh breathe loud." The girl's accent was road-thick, all swallowed consonants. "Talked to yerself about interest rates for half an hour. Anyone could've took yer teeth."

Wren laughed. She didn't mean to—it escaped before she could forge it into something more dignified—and the child's eyes flicked to her, quick and calculating, reassessing the odds of the situation. Two against one, but the tall one was laughing, which meant the tall one wasn't holding a knife yet.

"Four marks," Astrid said, in the tone he probably used on suppliers who padded invoices. "That's food for a week. That's a ferry crossing. Do you understand what you've taken?"

"Do *you*?" The girl jingled the purse. "Five marks buys a lotta not-starving. I counted."

"She counted your money," Wren said, "by ear. While stealing it."

"While you lectured yourself about *interest rates*," the girl confirmed, with devastating precision. "Nobody guards their purse when they're talkin' numbers at theirselves. It's like hypnotizin' a chicken."

Astrid pinched the bridge of his nose. Wren watched him do it and understood, suddenly, exactly what she was looking at: a man discovering that his armor had a gap in it, and the gap was shaped like a feral child in a dead man's coat.

"The marks," Astrid said. "Now. And then we'll discuss the fact that you've clearly been following us since the ford."

The girl's chin came up. For a heartbeat Wren thought she'd bolt—she had the coiled readiness of every stray Wren had ever known, the weight balanced on the balls of the feet—but instead the child said, "Ferry's flooded anyway. Bridge master's chargin' double 'cause of the rain. Yer five marks wouldn'ta covered it." A beat. "So really I saved ye the trouble of findin' out."

"That's not—" Astrid stopped. Closed his mouth. Wren could see him running the arithmetic, because of course he was; the man audited everything, including insults. "The ferry at Marlow's Crossing runs on a chain, not a bridge. Floods don't stop it. They raise the toll because the current strains the chain, but the base fare—"

"So ye *do* know bridges." The girl grinned, gap-toothed and luminous, and Wren felt something twist unpleasantly in her own chest, because she recognized that grin. She'd worn it herself, at nine years old, in a cart full of forged seals, certain that cleverness was the only inheritance her father would ever leave her. "Which means yer thinkin' about it. Which means I'm in yer head now." She held out the purse—not returning it, Wren realized, but offering a trade. "Teach me the real fares. An' the fake names ye two are usin', 'cause *Mistress Copperpot*"—she nodded at Wren—"is the worst alias I ever heard, an' I've heard a lot."

"It's a perfectly serviceable alias," Wren said hotly.

"It's got *Pot* in it."

Her name was Bramble, she claimed, though she offered it the way she offered everything—on loan, revocable, subject to theft. She would not say where she came from. She said only that the sisters at the almshouse in Dunmarrow had given her the name when they'd found her "in a hedge, screamin'," and that she'd left Dunmarrow "before they could make me useful." When Wren asked what the sisters considered useful, Bramble looked at her with those coin-bright eyes and said, "Quiet," and Wren did not ask again.

They should have left her at the wayshrine. Wren knew it; Astrid knew it; Bramble, trotting along behind them the next morning at a distance of exactly fifteen feet, almost certainly knew it. A fugitive party of two was a needle in the empire's hayfield. A fugitive party of two plus a child who announced their approach by loudly cataloguing every edible thing along the roadside was a haystack on fire.

"We can't keep her," Wren muttered, on the second morning.

"I'm aware."

"She stole from you."

"Twice, if we count the dried plums."

"She'll slow us down. She'll draw eyes. Every collector between here and the capital will spot a child on our trail and know exactly what it means—that we're soft, that we can be leveraged." Wren kicked a stone off the road harder than the stone deserved. "Veyra would use her inside a day. You know that."

Astrid walked in silence for ten paces. Behind them, Bramble was teaching herself to whistle through a blade of grass, badly, with the dogged persistence of someone who had never once been talked out of anything.

"I know it," he said finally. "I've drafted the argument for leaving her at the next shrine. Three points, well-reasoned. I've also drafted the counterargument." He rubbed the back of his neck, a gesture Wren had come to recognize as his version of surrender. "The counterargument keeps winning."

"Because?"

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then, in the tone of a man reading a ledger entry he didn't want to exist: "Because when she grabbed the purse, I felt it. The lift. And you know what I thought? Not *stop, thief*. I thought—*good technique*. I thought, someone should teach her properly before she gets her fingers broken doing it wrong." He exhaled. "Ten years keeping books for the Thorn. Ten years pricing stolen things, including stolen years, including children, Wren—I've seen what we pay for a child with quick hands, I wrote the entries—and my first instinct toward her was *professional admiration*." His jaw tightened. "I don't want to be the kind of man whose first instinct is admiration for that."

Ahead, Bramble whirled around, walking backward. "Are ye talkin' about me? Ye're talkin' about me. I can hear ye whisperin'."

"We're discussing logistics," Astrid called back.

"Logistics," Bramble repeated, tasting the word. "That's a rich-person word for *arguin' about whether to feed me*." She spun forward again, grass-blade whistle shrieking triumphantly. "Well? Have ye decided? I vote yes. I'm very useful. I know which shrines have offerings left, an' which wells are poisoned, an' I can fit under carts."

"You can fit under carts," Wren repeated flatly.

"Watch me."

"I believe you. That's the problem."

It rained on the fourth night, a thin persistent drizzle that turned the wayshrine they'd sheltered in into a dripping cave, and Wren woke past midnight to find the fire rebuilt and Astrid awake, sitting with his back against the saint's faceless knees while Bramble slept in a heap against his side, her stolen treasures spread across his lap like an inventory awaiting entry.

He was, Wren saw, actually examining them. One by one. Holding each button and bone up to the firelight with the grave attention he gave to ledgers.

"You're cataloguing a child's loot," she whispered.

"I'm assessing value." But his voice had gone soft in a way she'd never heard it, and when Bramble stirred and mumbled, he went absolutely still, the way men go still around sleeping animals they're afraid of startling—or being trusted by. "Look at this." He passed her the tin locket, the half one. Inside, where a portrait should be, someone had scratched a crude sun. "It's junk. Tin plate, worth nothing. She's strung it like a relic."

"Everything she owns is strung like a relic. That's the whole point of pockets, when pockets are all you have."

"Yes." He turned the locket's twin halves over in his fingers. "I know."

And Wren understood that he did know—not as a clerk knew, but the way the knowing lived in people who had been unclaimed once themselves. Astrid Vasquez, raised by a guild that priced orphans by their aptitudes. Wren Sable, raised in a cart, taught that love was a signature you learned to counterfeit. Between them on the shrine floor, a child asleep against a near-stranger because he was warm and had not moved away.

Found family, her mother had once called such arrangements, drunk on cheap wine and bitterness. It starts as inconvenience and ends as the only thing you'd bleed for. Wren had filed the phrase away years ago as sentimental nonsense.

She was beginning to suspect it was a forecast.

The trouble arrived on the sixth morning, wearing pilgrim gray.

Bramble spotted them first—or rather, she smelled them, because she came pelting back down the road from her scouting range with her whistle abandoned and her face gone careful and blank, which Wren had learned was the child's version of screaming.

"Two men," Bramble said, sliding in beside them and matching their pace as though she'd been there all along. "Gray cloaks, boots wrong for prayin'. Walkin' the line of the road like they're readin' it." Her hand found Astrid's sleeve without seeming to notice it had done so. "One's got a guild signet on a cord round his neck. Thorn-work. I seen that flower before, on a fence in Dunmarrow."

Wren's stomach dropped. Collectors. Off the ledgers, out of the city, six days into clean flight—and the Thorn had already put bloodhounds on the pilgrim road. Unless they weren't tracking the map at all. Unless Veyra had simply guessed that a runaway clerk would take the one road every fugitive takes, because roads are habits, and habits can be audited.

"Off the road," Astrid said quietly. "There's a fold in the hills ahead, past the shrine with the leaning post. We cut cross-country and let them walk past."

"And if they've got a scent-hound?" Wren asked.

"Then we learn to climb faster than dogs."

But as they slipped into the gorse, Bramble tugging Wren's hem to guide her through paths only the small could use, Wren glanced back once at the gray figures on the road below—and saw the nearer one pause at the wayshrine where they'd slept, pick something from the mud, and hold it to the light.

Small. Round. Glinting.

A button. From Bramble's coat.

The collector closed his fist around it, and turned—not south, after the pilgrims, but directly into the hills. Toward them.

"Change of assessment," Wren breathed. "They're not tracking the map."

Astrid followed her gaze, and she watched the clerk in him do the terrible arithmetic: button, child, trail, trap.

"They're tracking us," he said. "All of us. And they just found their thread."

Behind them, oblivious, Bramble asked if the hills had rabbits.
