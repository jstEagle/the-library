## 8. Crown of Borrowed Breath

The king of Vhalen woke, as he did every morning now, in the space behind his own eyes.

It was a small country, that space. A room with no doors and one window that looked out through his own gaze at whatever *it* chose to look at. Aldric had mapped it in the first weeks—the way a prisoner maps a cell—with the same patient discipline he had once used to map the borders of his kingdom. The window was narrowest when the Sigil was busy: audiences, processions, the signing of decrees. It widened in the small hours, when the thing coiled down into the vault beneath the throne room and forgot, for a little while, that it was wearing him.

He had learned to count those hours. He had learned, God forgive him, to be grateful for them.

This morning the window opened onto the throne room.

Light fell through the high windows in long amber columns, catching the dust that no servant dared disturb—dust had become sacred in the palace, somehow, though Aldric could not remember decreeing it. Had he? He searched the ledgers of himself and found the memory waiting, smooth and plausible: yes, an edict, three months ago, on the sanctity of the royal precincts. He remembered writing it. He remembered wanting to write it.

That was the worst of it. Not the stolen hours, not the voice that moved his jaw like a puppeteer testing strings. The worst was that the memories were *his*—or close enough to his that he could no longer find the seam where the forgery began. The Sigil did not simply drive him; it edited him. Each morning there was a little more of King Aldric that had never been King Aldric at all, and each morning he trusted what remained a little less.

*Stand*, said something that was not a sound but arrived anyway, the way a headache arrives, from inside.

His body stood. His body had gotten very good at standing. Fourteen years on the throne, and the last eight months spent as a coat someone else wore to court.

Below the dais, the court assembled itself for morning audience. Chancellor Merev with his ledger of petitions. The ambassadors from Callowmere and the Salt Principalities, their silks chosen to offend one another. Serah Dane's absence at the door where she should have stood—he noticed it again, the way he noticed it every day, a wound he kept touching. His kingsguard had been reorganized in the spring. He could not remember proposing the reorganization. He remembered approving it. There was the seam, maybe. Maybe. Or maybe he had approved it and forgotten, the way kings forget things, the way all men forget.

*Begin*, said the thing inside him, and King Aldric began.

* * *

The first petition concerned grain tariffs in the eastern provinces. The petitioner was a broad woman with flour still in the creases of her knuckles—a miller's delegate who had ridden eleven days to stand before her king, and who now stood trembling, not from awe but from exhaustion, reciting figures about spoilage and road levies while the court's attention drifted toward the windows like smoke.

Aldric listened. That much was still his. Whatever they took—and they were taking it by the spoonful, patience first, then humor, then the specific love he'd had for the smell of rain on hot stone—they had never managed to take his listening. Perhaps because it cost them nothing. Perhaps because a king who listened made a better puppet.

The Sigil answered the miller through his mouth. "The levy stands," it said, in his voice, pitched to the exact note of weary reasonableness he had spent fourteen years perfecting. "Grain is the blood of the realm, good mistress, and blood must be taxed where it pools."

It was a fine sentence. He hated it. He would have said something clumsy and true instead—*the roads are bleeding you dry and I will send surveyors by month's end*—and the court would have murmured at his informality, and the miller would have gone home carrying his actual words like actual bread. Instead she bowed to a sentence, a polished nothing, and the chancellor recorded it, and somewhere in the eastern provinces the roads went on bleeding.

*Next.*

There were seventeen more petitions. Aldric learned early that if he let himself attend to each one fully—to feel each injustice land where injustices land, in the chest—he would have torn his own body apart against the inside of it within a week, and then where would Vhalen be? Wearing a king with no resistance left in him at all. So he had taught himself a trick that shamed him: he held the petitions at arm's length. He listened, and he grieved, and he did not let the grief become action, because his grief had nowhere to go and action was no longer his to command.

But he wrote. That was the one door they had left open, because closing it would have looked wrong. Kings sign decrees. The hand moved, the seal pressed, the wax cooled red as a wound—and inside the formal language, in the margins, in the spacing, in choices only a man who had drafted a thousand decrees would know how to make, Aldric hid himself.

Today's decree confirmed the appointment of three new customs officers at the river ports. Innocuous. Necessary, even—the Sigil was thorough about necessary things; it understood that a kingdom must run smoothly to be worth ruling. But Aldric chose the officers' names himself, from a list of forty, and he chose them so that read together—Ostrev, Malin, Corvel—their initials spelled O.M.C., and every clerk trained under old Chancellor Berrow would know that O.M.C. was the opening formula of the palace cipher, abandoned these six years, the cipher Aldric and Berrow had invented together over bad wine during the border winter. *I am still here. Read me.* And after the initials, a date written in the old style, which no living clerk used anymore except as a joke—and the date was the day Serah Dane had been knighted.

He did not know if anyone read them. That was the prayer of it. A decree goes out; a decree is copied; copies travel; some clerk, some archivist, some half-forgotten friend might one day spread the pages flat on a desk and feel the itch of pattern behind the words and begin, cautiously, to dig. Eight months of decrees. Eight months of messages folded into tax schedules and marriage dispensations and one memorably strange proclamation about the sanctity of dust. If even one had been read—

If even one had been read, someone would have come by now.

He put the thought away, carefully, the way he put everything away, because the Sigil fed on hope the way mold fed on damp—it found the warm wet places in a mind and bloomed there. Hope was dangerous. Hope was a lit window in a house you were trying to keep dark.

And yet.

* * *

In the hour after midnight, the window widened.

Aldric lay in the dark of the royal bedchamber—not sleeping; he suspected he no longer slept so much as waited—and felt the presence in him slacken and sink, pouring itself downward through stone, down to the vault, down to whatever it tended in the dark beneath the throne. Those hours were his. They were growing shorter. In autumn they had been four; by midwinter, two; this spring, sometimes barely one, and he hoarded them like a miser counting a purse that thinned each time he opened it.

He rose. His body obeyed him in these hours—that was the mercy and the mockery of it. He crossed to the window and looked out over his sleeping capital, the river running black and silver, the thousand lamps going down toward the docks like embers scattered on a hearth. Somewhere out there were people he had ruled for fourteen years. Somewhere out there were people he had failed for eight months.

"I am still here," he said aloud, to no one, to the glass. His breath fogged it. Small evidence. He was real; he breathed; the fog proved it. He had begun keeping proofs. A list, maintained in the dark, of facts about himself that the edits had not touched. *My mother's name was Isolde. I lost my first tooth falling from the orchard wall at Callow Hold. I am afraid of deep water. I knighted Serah Dane on the ninth of Thawmonth and she wept afterward and pretended she hadn't.* The list ran to two hundred entries now. Some nights he recited it like a rosary. Some nights he found an entry gone soft, doubtful, worn at the edges like a coin passed through too many hands—*did I lose that tooth at Callow Hold, or was that my cousin's story borrowed so long it became mine?*—and he would sit with the doubt until dawn, grieving a tooth.

Tonight, instead, he went to the desk.

They watched him work, sometimes. He could feel it—the faint pressure of attention, like weather. But midnight desk-work was expected; the Sigil wore his industry proudly; let the records show the king labored late for his realm. So he labored. He drew fresh parchment toward him and began a decree concerning the maintenance of the cisterns beneath the palace hill—an honest matter, actually; the cisterns were old and the masons had petitioned twice—but he wrote it slowly, and he wrote it wrong, deliberately wrong, loading it with the cipher's grammar. Old-style dates. Initials arranged in the opening formula. And buried in the technical language of water-mains and sluice-gates, a phrase no inspector needed: *the way beneath remains open to any who hold the old key.*

It was thin. He knew it was thin. Eight months ago he would have built the message in seven layers and burned the draft twice. Now he worked with a shaking candle and a mind he could not fully trust to keep its own secrets, and he wondered—this was the thought that came every night, punctual as pain—if the Sigil let him do this. If the cistern decree would be filed and forgotten precisely because the thing wearing him had glanced at it, weighed it, found it harmless, amusing even. A king scratching at the walls of his cell. Let him scratch.

Or perhaps it didn't notice. Perhaps he was smaller than he feared. There was a bleak comfort in that.

He sealed the decree with his own hand, pressing the great sigil of Vhalen into the wax—two crowns above a sword—and held his palm against the cooling seal a moment longer than protocol required, as if warmth alone could carry the message further than ink.

"Someone," he whispered. "Anyone. I left the door marked."

The candles guttered. Far below, through six floors of stone, he felt—faintly, the way one feels a storm still miles off—the thing in the vault turn over in its long attention, and settle again.

* * *

Morning audience, three days later, and the court had grown a new ornament: a delegation from the Thorn.

Aldric knew the Gilded Thorn the way a shepherd knows a wolf that has learned to walk like a sheep—officially a guild of brokers, licensed, respectable, dealing in commodities whose nature appeared in no ledger he had ever been permitted to audit. Their trade had flourished these past years. He had signed the licenses himself. He had signed them with hands that may or may not have been entirely his own, and the thought of it sat in him like swallowed ice.

Their envoy today was a silver-haired woman named Veyra, and she did not bow as deeply as she should have, and the court did not seem to notice, and Aldric noticed everything now; noticing was nearly all he had left.

"Your Majesty," she said, "the guild brings tribute, and a request." Tribute first: a reliquary of black wood, which a servant opened to reveal—Aldric's breath caught behind the puppet-strings—a vial of ordinary-looking light. "Bottled dawn," Veyra announced, "harvested from the last morning of the old century. For the palace chapel."

Applause, polite and hungry. The Sigil stirred in him with something like appetite, and Aldric felt it reach through his eyes to look at Veyra, really look, and he felt her look back without appearing to, two merchants appraising one another across a counter made of thrones.

Then the request: extension of the guild's charter to include, in Veyra's phrasing, "certain archival services on behalf of the crown." Verification of names, she said. Maintenance of records. Ensuring that the realm's books contained exactly what they ought to contain—no more, no less.

No more. No less. Aldric turned the words over in the dark behind his eyes and felt their edges.

*A name verified is a name owned*, he thought, and did not know whether the thought was his own or planted, and that not-knowing was the whole of his captivity distilled to a drop.

Through his mouth, warm and kingly, the Sigil granted the charter. Through his hand, it signed. And Aldric, watching the pen move, did the only thing left to a king in his position: he added a flaw. As the flourish left the final letter, he twitched the nib—one heartbeat's rebellion, a fraction of a second in which his will and the thing's will disagreed about a stroke of ink—and the signature came out imperfect, the crossing of the final *d* doubled, a tiny stutter no herald would remark and every clerk trained under Berrow would recognize as the mark of distress, the old signal, *under duress, verify all.*

It cost him. The Sigil's attention snapped around like a lighthouse beam, and for one terrible instant the window of himself narrowed to a slit, and he heard—not in words, in pressure, in cold—the thing considering him. Considering the signature. Considering whether a stuttered letter was accident or treason.

Then Veyra smiled up at the dais, and the moment passed, and the audience rolled on, petitions and bows and the great machine of the court grinding smoothly forward over the buried and the bound.

That night the cistern decree went out with the courier pouch, copied fair by clerks who never looked closely at what kings signed. Three days after that, a customs officer named Ostrev received orders he did not question, and a sealed copy traveled south on the pilgrim road in the satchel of a merchant who had bought it, among other papers, from a forger's stall.

And in the space behind his eyes, in the small doorless country where the king of Vhalen kept his two hundred proofs and his one lit window, Aldric allowed himself—for exactly ten breaths, counted, rationed—the luxury of believing that somewhere, someone was reading.

Then the presence rose from the vault, filling him like tide filling a channel, and the day's parade began, and the crown settled back onto borrowed breath, and the king smiled, because it was time to smile, and smiling was no longer something he got to choose.

Down in the dark beneath the throne, something ancient turned the name of a dead god over in its coils, and began, patiently, to plan a coronation.
