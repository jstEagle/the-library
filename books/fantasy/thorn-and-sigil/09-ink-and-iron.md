## 9. Ink and Iron

The collectors caught up with them on the third day out of the pass, in a country of gray slate and mean little pines where the wind came down off the ridgeline with an edge like a honed knife. Wren heard them before she saw them—the particular rhythm of hooves that meant riders who were not hurrying, because they did not believe their quarry could make them hurry.

She was mending the seam of her pack strap by the fire when she felt it: that prickle along the back of the neck that every forger learns young, the sense of being watched by someone whose job is watching. She looked up at Bramble, who had gone very still over the rabbit bones she'd been worrying, the way small animals go still when hawks pass over.

"They're here," Bramble said.

"I know."

Astrid was already moving, which was the thing Wren had learned to trust about him and the thing that still unsettled her most—he never seemed surprised by disaster, only resigned to it, as if he'd been keeping accounts on catastrophe for years and had always known the balance would come due. He kicked dirt over the fire, swept the ashes with a pine bough until nothing remained but a scorch the rain would forgive, and pressed the blood-inked map flat against his chest beneath his coat.

"Road," he said. "Off the road. There was a cut in the hillside half a mile back—we can lose them among the rocks if—"

"If," said Wren.

They ran. The wind tore at them from the left the whole way, as though the country itself were leaning into the chase. Behind them, faint but gaining, came the sound Wren had been dreading since the night they'd fled the Gilded Thorn: the soft chiming of the collectors' bridles. Veyra's people wore silver bells on their harnesses, one for each debt collected, and the Thorn's ledgers were long. Three bells each, at least. Six names between them, rung out into the cold air like a promise.

Bramble kept pace better than Wren expected, weaving through the scrub with the low, fluid economy of a creature built for exactly this—streets, alleys, escape. But the child's breath was going ragged, and the cut in the hillside was still ahead of them, and behind them the bells had stopped pretending to be distant.

Wren risked one look back.

Four riders. She knew the type from the guild's corridors—collectors weren't muscle, not really; they were auditors with weapons. The one in the lead wore a gray coat buttoned to the throat despite the wind, and his face had the terrible mildness of a man reading aloud from a document. In his hand, absurdly, he carried a ledger bound in green leather, and as she watched he opened it against his saddle and turned a page without slowing his horse.

"He's checking our entries," she gasped. "Astrid. He's *checking*."

"That means we're worth the ink." Astrid didn't look back. "Faster."

The cut in the hillside opened before them—a slot of shadow between two shoulders of slate, choked with fallen stone. They plunged into it, and for a hundred heartbeats it seemed possible, it actually seemed possible, the walls rising steep around them, the sound of the bells scattering and fading—

Then the walls stopped.

Not ended. Stopped. A wall of raw rock stood across the ravine where no wall should have been, unweathered, unbroken, its face smooth as poured wax. Wren put her hand against it and snatched it back. It was warm. Stone was never warm.

"Oh," she said quietly. "Oh, that's cheating."

"The Sigil's agents," Astrid said. His voice was flat, which she'd learned meant fear. "Veyra sold them the route. Or the shape of it. They're sealing the passes ahead of us." He turned, and Wren saw him do the arithmetic she was doing—the ravine mouth behind them, the riders entering it now, four mounted men against two exhausted adults and a child, nowhere left that wasn't ledgered.

Bramble tugged Wren's sleeve. "Back door," she whispered, and pointed up.

High on the right-hand wall, half-hidden behind a beard of dead ivy, there was a crack—not a way through, but a way *up*, a chimney of broken stone climbing toward the gray sky above the ridge. Climbing toward open ground and distance and maybe, maybe, enough of both.

"They'll see us," Wren said.

"They'll see us anyway." Astrid boosted Bramble onto the first ledge. "Go. I'm last. That's not chivalry, it's bookkeeping—if anyone falls, I fall on them slowest."

It was the worst joke she'd ever heard and she laughed anyway, breathless and ugly, hauling herself up after the child while the bells below swelled to a carillon. The stone was slick with rime; her fingers found holds by faith rather than sight. Ten feet up. Fifteen. Below her, Astrid began to climb, and below *him*, the gray-coated collector dismounted and drew something from his coat that was not a weapon—or was one, in the way that a signature is a weapon—and began to read aloud.

Wren did not hear the words clearly. She felt them. Each syllable landed on her skin like a stamp on hot wax, and where it landed, her name began to feel less true. Her hands remembered being called Wren. Her hands did not entirely agree anymore.

*Hold*, she told herself. *You are Wren Sable. You copy seals so well because you know what is real. Hold.*

Then three things happened nearly at once.

First: the reading stopped, mid-word, clipped off like a snapped thread.

Second: Bramble, wedged in the crook of a ledge above her, made a sound Wren had never heard from her before—not fear, but wonder.

Third: someone else was standing at the top of the chimney.

*

The woman was tall in the way that old trees are tall—not showy, just immovable. Gray-streaked hair cropped close to the skull, a jaw like the prow of a boat, and eyes the pale, washed-out blue of steel that has been quenched too many times. She wore no armor, only a long oilskin coat and a sword worn plain at her hip, scabbard scarred and oiled, the sword of someone who maintained a tool she no longer had any official reason to carry.

In her left hand she held a horn, its mouth still smoking faintly with a sound that had swallowed the collector's words whole.

"Well," she said, looking down at them. Her voice was rough gravel under snow. "Either you're the thieves the Thorn's been ringing bells about, or you're bait, and I've wasted my morning either way."

Below them, the collector had lowered his ledger. He looked up at the woman on the ridge with an expression Wren had never seen on a collector's face before, and it took her a moment to identify it, because it belonged to clerks and accountants and other careful people:

Recognition. And caution.

"Dane," the collector said.

"Master Quillon." She touched two fingers to her brow in something that was almost, but not quite, a salute. "Still auditing?"

"Still deserting?"

That word landed on the woman like a blow she'd been expecting for years and braced for anyway. Wren saw it hit—saw the tiny flinch around the eyes—and saw her refuse to fall for it.

"I resigned," Dane said. "There's a difference. Ask your mistress sometime what the difference costs."

Quillon closed his ledger with the soft finality of a door. He looked up the chimney—at Astrid clinging to the stone, at Wren, at Bramble—and then at the warm wall sealing the ravine's far end, and whatever calculation he performed, he performed it quickly.

"We'll take the long way," he said. "There is always a long way, Serah Dane. The Sigil builds roads faster than you can walk them." He swung back into his saddle. "And the king sends his regards."

He said it strangely—flat, mechanical, the way a puppet's mouth moves when someone else works the strings—and Wren saw the knight's knuckles whiten on the horn until the bone showed through.

The riders withdrew, bells receding down the ravine like a tide going out, and for a long moment nobody moved. Then Serah Dane sat down abruptly on a boulder, as if her legs had filed a formal complaint, and looked at the three of them with an expression caught somewhere between regret and appetite.

"Well," she said again. "I suppose you'd better come down before I decide whether saving you was a mistake."

*

They made camp that night in a hollow below the ridge, a fire small enough to hide and large enough to matter. Bramble ate like a small wolf and fell asleep mid-chew, curled against Wren's side with the shameless entitlement of the very young, and Wren found she lacked the heart to move her.

Astrid sat across the fire, turning the map's edge between his fingers, studying the knight with the wary attention he gave everything. "You knew the collector by name. He knew yours."

"Twenty years in the palace guard buys acquaintances in ugly places." She fed a stick to the fire. "Serah Dane. Formerly of the King's Vigil, First Company. Currently of nowhere in particular."

"The King's Vigil," Wren said slowly. "You guarded Aldric."

"I guarded the king." Something moved behind the quenched-steel eyes. "For eleven years. Until I stopped."

"Stopped guarding? Or stopped believing in what you were guarding?"

Dane looked at her for a long moment. "You're the forger. Sable's girl. The Thorn's files say you can copy anything." She said it without malice, merely as fact, and Wren realized with a chill how thoroughly they had all been read—that the green leather ledger probably held entries for every one of them, dates and values and dispositions, their lives itemized in another person's hand.

"Files lie," Wren said.

"So do kings." Dane leaned forward, elbows on knees, and the firelight carved deep lines into her face, lines that looked less like age than like weather. "I'll tell you something I've told no one, because telling it changes nothing unless you're the ones who can use it. Three years ago—I can give you the day, the hour—the king stopped blinking."

Astrid's head came up sharply. "What?"

"You think I'm mad. Everyone would." Her laugh had no humor in it, only rust. "But I stood post outside the royal apartments for eleven years. You learn a man's face the way a smith learns iron—every flaw, every temper, every place it bends. And one morning Aldric Vane walked out onto the balcony to review the honor guard, and his eyes went dry. Not blinking less. Not blinking *slower*. He simply... stopped. For the length of the ceremony, twenty minutes, his eyes never once shuttered, and the courtiers cheered, and I stood there with ice going down my spine thinking: *that is not the man I know. The man I know would be weeping or squinting in this wind.*"

"And you reported it," Wren guessed.

"I requested an audience with the Lord Marshal. I wrote it up—carefully, respectfully, *observed irregularity in His Majesty's ocular behavior*—because I knew how it would sound otherwise." She stared into the fire. "Two days later I was summoned before the court physician, who examined me for hysteria. A month later, my commission was reviewed. Three months later, I was offered a choice: resign with honors, or face inquiry. I resigned." Her jaw tightened. "Coward's arithmetic. I told myself I'd imagined it. I told myself that for three years, and I drank to help myself tell it, and last spring I sobered up because I couldn't stand the sound of my own excuses anymore. Then, six weeks ago, I heard rumors of a courier who died in the Thorn's alley with a map in his blood, and a clerk who vanished with it, and I started walking north."

The silence stretched. Bramble stirred against Wren's side and slept on.

"You followed us," Astrid said. It wasn't an accusation. It sounded more like a man confirming a figure in a column.

"I followed the story of you. There's a difference." She met his eyes across the fire. "I don't know what you're carrying, clerk, and I don't want to know tonight. But I know this: the thing wearing the king sent men to seal a mountain pass against three travelers. Whatever you're walking toward, it's afraid of you. And I have spent three years being afraid of a thing that everyone told me wasn't real." She reached over and drew her sword halfway from its scabbard—just the first inch, just enough that the firelight ran down the blade like water. "I'd like to stop being afraid. If you'll have me."

Wren looked at Astrid. Astrid looked at the map. And Wren thought about trust—about how she'd spent her whole life forging things because the real ones were always locked away from her, about how the only people she'd ever trusted were people who'd lied to her as skillfully as she lied to everyone else. This woman was offering the opposite of a forgery: the plain, ugly truth, unsealed and uncertified, worth exactly what it claimed to be.

"He asked you what the difference cost," Astrid said quietly. "Resigning instead of deserting. What did it cost?"

"Everything except the chance to fix it," Dane said.

Astrid nodded slowly, and Wren saw him arrive at the decision the way he arrived at all of them—with the weary certainty of a man balancing a ledger that had been unbalanced for years. "We leave at dawn. We walk fast, we sleep cold, and if you're the Sigil's creature, Bramble will bite you before you finish betraying us."

"She already tried," Dane said, glancing at the sleeping child with something dangerously close to fondness. "Last week. While I was scouting your camp. Sharp teeth, that one."

"She's thorough," Wren said.

They settled by degrees—Dane taking first watch without being asked, sitting upright at the hollow's mouth with her sword across her knees, facing north, toward the capital, toward the thing that had stopped blinking. Wren lay back and pulled her cloak around herself and Bramble both, and stared up at the ragged strip of stars between the ridgelines.

*A king who does not blink,* she thought. *A god who went silent. A map drawn by a father who knew he was dying.* Everything in her life lately was a document with a second layer, and she was tired of reading only what was written on top.

She was nearly asleep when she noticed it—so nearly asleep that at first she thought she'd dreamed it. But the habit of a lifetime is a hard thing to sleep through, and Wren Sable's habit was noticing ink.

The map. Astrid had laid it out beside him to study by firelight, and in the dying light its lines had shifted—subtly, the way watermarks shift when paper is tilted. The road they'd traveled was drawn wrong. Deliberately wrong. And along its margin, in flourishes she had taken for decoration, ran a line of tiny characters she now recognized with a jolt that stopped her heart:

Her father's shorthand. The cipher he'd used on his private surveys. The one only she had ever learned.

"Dane," she breathed. Then, louder, sitting up: "Astrid. Wake up. Look at the map—it's not finished. It's a *letter*."
