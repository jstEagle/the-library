## 10. The Market of Unmade Names

The town had no name, which was the first thing Astrid noticed, and the second was that this absence was deliberate. Cartographers left it blank. Pilgrim songs skipped the verse where it should have been. Even the road signs within a day's walk pointed to everything *around* it—Harrowmere to the east, the Salt Steps to the west—as though the place were a hole cut in the world and everyone had agreed not to look directly into it.

They called it the Undervale anyway, in the way people call things by what they refuse to write down.

It sat in a bowl of hills like water collected in a cupped palm: three tiers of market carved into the rock itself, lit by lanterns that burned without flame—bottled light, Astrid knew, sold by weight and measured in hours. The air smelled of tallow, wet stone, and something sweeter underneath, cloying as overripe fruit. He recognized that smell from the Gilded Thorn's deep vaults. It was the scent of memory going stale.

"You're making that face," Wren said beside him. She had her hood up and her father's map sewn flat against her ribs, beneath her shirt, where it rode with every breath. "The one where you're cataloguing everything and liking none of it."

"I'm cataloguing everything," Astrid admitted. "Liking none of it is new."

Bramble hung off his other arm like a burr, eyes enormous, drinking in the middle tier where a woman sold bottled griefs the way fishwives sold herring—rows of them glinting blue-black in their vials, each labeled in a hand so small you had to trust the seller about what was inside. A boy of maybe ten haggled over one, offering his own shadow, coiled at his feet like a sleeping dog. The grief-wife turned the shadow over with a stick, checked its edges, and shook her head. Too thin. Underfed shadows fetched nothing.

"Stay close," Astrid told Bramble, and meant it in the old guild way: *stay close enough that I can lie for you.*

"Wasn't going anywhere," Bramble said, already looking somewhere else entirely.

Serah brought up the rear, armored down to plain leathers but walking like the armor was still there, shoulders squared against a crowd that didn't care who she'd been. Her hand never strayed far from the sword wrapped in sacking on her back. "Where do we find passage?" she asked quietly. "And don't say the ferryman. I've seen what he charges."

"The ferryman is exactly where we're going," Astrid said. "Because what he charges is the only currency we have that can't be stolen."

He felt Wren go still beside him. "You said you had coin."

"I had coin. Bramble has most of it now." The child had the grace to look only slightly guilty. "And coin buys passage out of Harrowmere. Coin doesn't buy passage *through* the checkpoints past it. The Sigil's people are verifying names on the roads now—real names, ledger names. Every toll between here and the capital will want to know who you were before they let you be who you're pretending to be." He touched his own chest, where no ledger hung anymore, where the absence of one sat heavier than any book ever had. "There's one merchant in the Undervale who trades in the other direction. Who can make a name unfindable. But he doesn't take money."

"He takes memories," Wren said flatly.

"He takes memories."

"And you know this because—"

"Because I used to sell to him." Astrid started down the switchback stairs into the market's first tier, and the crowd swallowed them, and he let it. That was the one mercy of places like this: nobody looked at anyone. Looking cost something here too. "Before Veyra made me a keeper of ledgers, I was a runner. And before that—" He stopped himself. Before that was an orphanage with a shrine in its cellar, and a god who hadn't answered in ten years, and a boy who prayed so hard he sometimes gave away pieces of himself just to feel heard. "Before that isn't relevant."

Wren's look said she'd file that away for later, in whatever meticulous archive she kept behind her eyes. She'd been doing that since the temple. Since Vesperian's name had come out of him like a splinter working loose. She didn't ask questions anymore; she just watched him the way she watched seals she intended to copy—cataloguing pressure, angle, the small tremors that betrayed the original hand.

The ferryman's stall occupied the lowest tier, nearest the underground river that gave the Undervale its only exit east: a black waterway running through a tunnel toward the capital's hinterlands, navigable for those who could pay and forgettable for those who paid more. The ferryman himself was a narrow man with a scholar's stoop and a merchant's hands, soft-palmed, ink-stained to the second knuckle. He sat behind a desk that seemed absurdly domestic amid the stalls—a lamp, a ledger, a teapot—and when Astrid approached, the man looked up with the expression of someone greeting an expected appointment.

"Astrid Vasquez," the ferryman said. "I wondered when the Thorn would send you back."

"The Thorn didn't send me."

"No," the ferryman agreed, and there was something almost gentle in it. "I heard about your erasure. Terrible administrative accident. Happens all the time to people who take things that aren't theirs." He folded his hands. "You'll want passage east. Three adults, one child, no questions at the checkpoints. I can do that. The question is what you'll pay."

Astrid set both palms flat on the desk. "Name your price."

"Something true," the ferryman said. "Something with weight. Not a name—you've precious little of those left, and I don't deal in identities, that's vulgar work. Not a skill; skills are cheap, everyone's selling their grandmother's lullaby or the taste of their mother's bread. I want a memory with *consequence*. Something that changed you. Something whose removal leaves a shape behind." His eyes flicked, briefly, to Bramble, then back, and Astrid's blood went cold. "Not the child. I have standards. But you understand my meaning. The best goods are the ones you'd rather keep."

Behind him, Astrid heard Serah shift her weight—the sound of a woman deciding how many people she could fight in a cramped tunnel. Wren's breathing had gone carefully even, the breathing of a forger measuring a forgery.

"I have something," Astrid said slowly. "But I need to know what happens to it. Where does it go?"

The ferryman smiled, and it was the smile of a man who had answered this question ten thousand times and never once lied about it. "Into the market. Into a bottle, onto a shelf, into someone else's head if they pay enough. Your grief becomes another's souvenir. Your triumph becomes another's lesson." He poured tea into two cups, an oddly courteous gesture. "That's the trade, keeper. Nothing is destroyed. It's only ever *relocated*. Which is more than the Sigil can say for what it does."

Astrid thought of the ledger entry that had unsettled him a lifetime ago—one silence, gently used. He thought of all the silences he'd catalogued since, all the years and griefs and unspoken names, each one weighed and priced and shelved by his own careful hand. He had spent a decade believing that keeping the record of stolen things was a kind of stewardship. Standing here, on the buyer's side of the desk, he finally understood what the sellers felt.

He reached inward, the way he'd been taught to inventory himself, and found the memory he'd known he would find. It wasn't the largest thing he owned. It was, perhaps, the truest.

His ninth winter. The orphanage cellar. Kneeling before the crumbling shrine with frost on the stones, praying to a god who had already gone silent—or who had never been able to hear him at all—and feeling, for one impossible moment, *answered*. Warmth like a hand on his head. The sense—not words, never words—that he was seen, that he was kept, that the vast dark attention of something enormous had paused on him, one small cold child, and found him worth pausing for.

He had built his whole life on that moment. Every oath, every ledger line, every night he'd stayed honest when dishonesty was cheaper—all of it stood on that single foundation stone of being witnessed.

If he sold it, he would still *do* the right things. Habit would carry him. But he would no longer know why. He would be a man walking on a bridge whose supports had been removed, held up only by momentum.

"There's a memory," Astrid said. "Winter. I was nine. It's the reason I took the oath." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "It's yours if the price is right."

The ferryman's eyebrows rose a fraction—an appraiser recognizing provenance. "A first epiphany. Those are rare. Most people sell their first heartbreak instead; it's less load-bearing." He studied Astrid with genuine professional interest. "You understand what you'll lose?"

"I'll lose the reason," Astrid said. "I'll keep the habits."

"That's the bleakest thing anyone's offered me all season." The ferryman produced a small glass vial, stoppered with wax, and set it on the desk between them. "Touch this. Think of it. Let it pour. Don't fight the pour—it tears otherwise, and torn memories fetch nothing and leave worse holes."

Astrid picked up the vial. It was warm, which it should not have been, and he chose not to think about why.

*Astrid.* The voice came from inside his own skull, faint as light through shutters, and it was not his voice. It had been growing more distinct since the temple—Vesperian, bleeding through the anchor-bond, present the way weather is present. *You do not have to do this.*

"Yes, I do," Astrid murmured.

*Then let me hold it,* the god said. *While you cannot. A thing remembered by a god is not lost. It is only... archived.*

Astrid's breath caught. He thought of Veyra's ledgers, of his own careful columns, of the difference between losing a thing and entrusting it. He thought of a silent god who had apparently been listening after all—imprisoned, chained, but listening, all these years, to every prayer Astrid had ever sent down into the dark.

"Do it," he whispered.

He pressed the vial to his brow and thought of frost, and warmth, and the enormous pause of attention, and let it pour.

It did not hurt. That was the worst part. It felt like exhaling after holding breath too long—relief and loss indistinguishable. The warmth in the glass brightened to gold. And somewhere in the hollow where the memory had lived, something settled gently, like a coat laid over an empty chair: a sensation of being *held*, secondhand, borrowed, real.

When he opened his eyes, the ferryman was corking the vial and writing a label. Astrid read it upside down. *One epiphany, mint condition, owner unknown.*

Owner unknown. He supposed that was accurate now.

"Passage for four," the ferryman said briskly, all business again, sliding a wax-sealed token across the desk. "Boat leaves at moonrise. The pilot knows to ignore the checkpoint at Graylock Ford—your papers won't exist, and neither will you, officially. Congratulations. You're now the kind of cargo even the Sigil's clerks can't find." He paused, then added, quieter: "For what it's worth, keeper—I've bought ten thousand memories, and I've never once had a seller thank me. You're the first who didn't curse me either. That god of yours must still be teaching you manners."

Astrid walked away from the desk with the token in his fist and a hole in his chest shaped like a winter morning, and he could not remember what had filled it, only that something had, and that it had mattered, and that the mattering remained like heat lingering in a room after the fire goes out.

*It is safe,* Vesperian said softly, from the hollow. *I am keeping it. All of them, if you must spend them. Every piece of you that you pawn along this road—I will hold.*

"Why?" Astrid asked, under his breath, as they climbed back into the crowd. "Why keep them? What am I to you?"

The silence that followed was long enough to hurt. Then, faintly: *You are the last page of a very long book. I am trying not to lose my place.*

* * *

They had six hours until moonrise, and Astrid spent the first of them doing what he could not stop doing: reading the market.

It was habit older than his erasure. Eyes moving across crowds the way they once moved across ledger lines, sorting, pricing, flagging anomalies. And the Undervale's third tier—the tier above, where the expensive dealers kept their stalls behind curtains of woven shadow—was full of anomalies tonight.

He saw it from the balcony of a noodle shop, half a bowl untouched in front of him, Bramble asleep against Serah's shoulder below.

Veyra.

She stood at the stall of a map-dealer whose awning bore the crossed compass-needles of the Cartographers' Compact, and she was not browsing. She was *buying*. Two of her collectors flanked her—Astrid recognized the scarred one, Mott, who had chased them out of Thornhall—and between purchases she fed documents into a small brass brazier without ceremony, without hatred, the way a clerk feeds paper into an incinerator at day's end. Maps curled and flashed and went to ash. The dealer counted coins with shaking hands and kept producing more maps from under his counter, each one fetched up from some locked drawer, each one surrendered with the expression of a man watching his own children sold.

Every copy. She was buying every copy.

"Not just ours," Wren breathed. She'd followed his gaze and gone white. "Look—she's got a broker from the Salt Steps over there, and a pilgrim-scribe, and—" Her voice dropped to almost nothing. "She's buying the *route*, Astrid. Not our map. The road itself. Every rendering of the eastern passages, every chart of the river tunnels, every—my father drew variants. Redundancies. He always drew redundancies, he said a map with one copy is a map already lost, and she's—"

"She's erasing the way to the Sigil," Astrid finished. "Not chasing us. Making sure no one else can follow. No army, no rival, no rescue." He watched Veyra hold a document to the light, check it against something in her memory, nod, and feed it to the brazier. She wore the same rings she'd worn the day she erased him. She moved like a woman closing accounts at quarter's end. "We were never the prize. We were just the first draft of a problem she's solving wholesale."

"Then why hasn't she found us?" Serah asked. "This town is a crossroads. If she's buying every map, she knows the routes. She knows where we'd surface."

"Because she thinks we're already dead," Astrid said slowly, working it through, feeling it click like tumblers in a lock. "No. Because she thinks we're already *hers*. She erased me from the ledgers. In her arithmetic, a man who doesn't exist can't reach a throne room. She's not hunting us because, to her, there's nothing left to hunt." He smiled, and it felt strange on his face, feral and thin. "Being erased is the best cover I've ever had."

Below, Veyra paused.

Astrid went flat against the balcony rail, heart slamming, and pulled Wren down with him. Through the gaps in the rail's ironwork he watched the guildmistress turn from the map-dealer's stall, slow as a weathervane finding wind, and lift her face toward the noodle shop's balcony. Toward him. Her eyes—pale, patient, the eyes of a woman who had traded her soul for eternity and considered it a fair bargain—swept the crowd below, the stairwells, the shadows.

She couldn't see him. There was nothing to see. He was a gap in the ledger, a blank line, a name unwritten.

But she stood there a long moment, head tilted, like a listener hearing a note that shouldn't be in the song. Then Mott said something, and she smiled at whatever it was, and turned back to the brazier, and another map became ash.

"We leave at moonrise," Astrid said. "Earlier, if we can. And Wren—" He caught her arm. Her skin was cold. "Your father's variant maps. The redundancies. Do you know where they are?"

Wren stared at the brazier smoke rising through the market's throat, and when she spoke, her voice had gone quiet and certain, the voice of a woman reading fine print she'd memorized long ago and only now understood.

"They're not *somewhere*," she said. "They're *in* something. He hid them, Astrid. The flourishes—the little decorative compass roses, the sea-serpents in the margins—he always said a good forger hides the truth where everyone looks and no one sees." Her hand pressed flat against her ribs, against the map sewn there. "One of them is hidden in the map I've been carrying since Harrowmere. Which means Veyra can burn every copy in the world—" and here she almost laughed, a cracked, wondering sound, "—and it won't matter. As long as one of us is still carrying him."

Above the market, the lantern-light dimmed by degrees as the bottled hours burned low, and somewhere in the dark of the tunnel east, a boat waited that official records would never show carried four passengers who did not exist.

Astrid touched the hollow in his chest where his ninth winter used to live, felt the borrowed warmth there—*kept*, a god had promised, *archived*—and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own obituary, that before moonrise he would need to decide what else he was willing to pour out of himself to finish this road.

Because Veyra was erasing the route.

And the Sigil, he was suddenly certain, was already erasing something larger.

The thought arrived whole, cold, and absolute, the way the worst truths do: *it isn't buying up the maps to the throne room. It's buying up the maps to everything. When the coronation comes, there will be no roads left leading anywhere but to it.*

Moonrise was five hours away. Astrid stood, and woke Bramble, and went down the stairs toward the boat that did not officially exist, carrying a name no ledger held, a memory he could no longer see, and a map sewn against a forger's ribs that was, unknown to everyone but the dead man who had drawn it, the last original in the world.
