## 11. Bramble's Bargain

Bramble had three rules for stealing, and she had made them herself, which made them law.

One: never take from someone who was watching the thing you took. People guarded what they looked at. A purse left on a bedroll while its owner argued with his boots was fair game; a coin counted out slow in lamplight belonged to the light, not to her.

Two: never steal from anyone smaller than you. The world already took from small things. She wasn't going to be part of that.

Three: never take more than you could carry running.

She repeated them to herself now, crouched in the dark of the inn's stairwell like a spider in its own web, because tonight she intended to break rule three, and it helped to know exactly how far she was falling.

The town was called Marrowgate, though nobody seemed to know why, and it sat at the bottom of a valley like a stone in the cup of a hand. They'd been there two days. Two days of Astrid counting coins on the table with his mouth pressed flat, two days of Wren saying *we have enough* in the voice she used when they didn't, two days of Serah chewing the same heel of bread for supper so the others would have more and pretending she wasn't hungry, which was the worst lie Bramble had ever seen anyone tell, and she had watched liars for a living.

So: food. Real food. Bread that hadn't gone grey, cheese with a rind, maybe an apple if the gods were feeling generous, which they never were, but Bramble believed in checking anyway.

She knew the innkeeper kept a cold larder behind the kitchen, door latched with a hook-and-eye because Marrowgate trusted itself. She knew the cook went to the well at half-bell after midnight because she'd watched through two nights of cracked shutters, patient as frost. She knew the dog by the woodpile was old and mostly deaf and could be bought with the rind of anything.

What Bramble did not know — what no amount of watching from windows could have taught her — was that the men in the corner room below the stairs were not sleeping.

She heard them because the floorboards above their room had a loose one, a board that sang a little note under her knee, and she froze on it the way you freeze when a step betrays you, and in the silence that followed she heard the voice underneath.

"—at the ford, not before. He'll want to see the water first."

Bramble knew that voice. Not the man himself — she'd never seen his face — but the *kind* of it. Smooth as oiled leather, careful as a clerk. It was the kind of voice the collectors back in the capital used when they came down the guild rows at night, the kind that spoke softly because it never needed to speak twice.

She should have gone back to bed. That was the smart thing. That was the thing Astrid would tell her to do, with his flat mouth and his tired eyes: *small things stay small, Bramble, that's how they survive.*

But rule four — the rule she'd never written down because it wasn't really a rule, it was just the shape of her whole life — rule four said: *listen when people talk about other people.* Because in Bramble's experience, everything important that ever happened to anybody happened in rooms they thought were empty.

She eased off the singing board and lay flat against the stairwell wall, ear to the gap where the plaster had crumbled away from the lath, and she listened.

"There's no ford crossing," said a second voice, younger, uncertain. "The map shows—"

"The map shows what we let it show." The smooth voice again, and something in it made the hair on Bramble's arms stand up, because it wasn't showing off. It was bored. Bored the way a cat is bored with a mouse that has stopped running. "The cartographer drew them a road. We are simply standing at the end of it. Four riders at the willow bend, another pair behind the shrine wall. When the knight reaches for her sword — and she will, she's that type — the crossbows answer. The girl and the ledger-keeper we take breathing."

"And the child?"

There was a pause. Bramble held her breath so hard her chest hurt.

"The child is nothing," the smooth voice said. "A stray they picked up on the road. If she runs, let her run. If she doesn't—" A shrug lived inside the silence. "Strays are cheap."

Bramble stayed flat against the wall long after the voices stopped, long after the candle under the door guttered and died, long enough that her knees ached against the boards and the dark went from frightening to familiar, the way it always did if you sat in it long enough.

*A stray.*

She had been called worse. She had been called worse by people who meant it, who had chased her through fish markets with sticks, who had locked her in a cellar for three days once in Kettering for taking a wheel of cheese smaller than her own head. *Stray* was almost kind. Strays got fed sometimes. Strays got taken in by soft-hearted fools with ledgers and swords and secrets too big for them.

That was the problem. That was the whole entire problem, sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone.

Because Astrid wasn't a fool. Wren wasn't soft. Serah was the most dangerous person Bramble had ever met and also the one who cut apples into slices instead of giving them to you whole, like it mattered, like *you* mattered. And tomorrow — or the day after, whenever they left Marrowgate — all three of them were going to walk down to a ford that wasn't a ford, into a bend full of willows full of men with crossbows, because a stray child hadn't been worth listening to.

No. Because a stray child hadn't *spoken.*

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them shaking the way you notice weather, distant and unimportant, and then she noticed something else, which was that she was angry, and the anger was bigger than the fear, and that had never happened before. Fear was the biggest thing Bramble owned. Fear had raised her. Fear had taught her every rule she had.

But fear said *stay small,* and staying small meant letting them die, and the strange new anger said: *they cut your apple into slices.*

* * *

She didn't go to the larder. That was the first sign, later, that she'd become a different person: Bramble of the three rules walked past a cold larder full of cheese in the middle of the night and did not open it, because her hands were full of something heavier than hunger.

She climbed back up through the window she'd left unlatched — rule one applied to yourself too; never leave a way in that you weren't watching — and crossed the room she shared with Wren on feet that knew every quiet board. Wren slept curled tight as a fern, one hand even in sleep near the satchel with the map, and Bramble stood over her for a moment in the moonlight from the window.

Wren talked in her sleep sometimes. Mostly numbers. Once, heartbreakingly, *Papa, the scale is wrong,* in a small lost voice, and Bramble had lain awake the rest of that night understanding something about grown-ups she'd never understood before, which was that they were all just children who had gotten tall and learned to hide it worse.

In the next room, Astrid breathed slow and even. Serah did not breathe slow and even; Serah breathed like a soldier, shallow and ready, and Bramble had learned to tell the difference weeks ago, lying in haylofts between them.

Under her pallet, wrapped in oilcloth, in a hole she'd dug in the floorboards of every room she'd ever slept in out of pure habit, Bramble kept her treasure.

It wasn't much to look at. That was the point of treasures; the good ones never were. It was a brooch — a little tin thing shaped like a wren, wings folded, no bigger than her thumbnail, with one glass eye gone cloudy. She'd lifted it off a peddler woman in Harrowfen two years ago, back when she traveled alone, back when the rules were all she had. She'd stolen it because it was pretty and because stealing was breathing, and then she'd found out it was the only thing the woman had left of a daughter sold south to pay a debt, and Bramble had run three streets after the cart to give it back, and the woman had cried, and then — this was the part Bramble never told anyone — the woman had given it *back* to her. Pressed it into her dirty hand and closed her fingers over it and said, *You gave it home once. Keep it. Maybe it'll teach you the road home too.*

Bramble had carried it every day since. Through winters. Through the worst year, the year she didn't talk about. She'd slept with it under her tongue once, in a barn in the snow, because she was afraid of losing it more than she was afraid of swallowing it.

It was the only thing she owned that she hadn't stolen. Well. That she'd *stolen twice,* which amounted to the same thing, or better.

She dug it up now, and the oilcloth was cold, and the little tin wren sat in her palm catching the moonlight with its one clouded eye, and Bramble sat on the floorboards and cried without any noise at all, because noise was rule zero, the rule underneath all the rules, and some habits hold even when everything else is breaking.

Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, put the brooch in her pocket, and went to wake them up.

* * *

They didn't believe her at first. Of course they didn't. She saw it happen on their faces in the candle stub's light, all three of them, the same expression arriving at slightly different speeds: the polite patience adults keep for children's nightmares.

"Crossbows at the willow bend," Serah repeated slowly. "Behind the shrine wall."

"And a man with a voice like a clerk," Bramble said. "Smooth. Bored. He said the map shows what they let it show, and he said—" Her throat closed. She made herself finish, because the words were the proof and the proof was the price. "He said the child is nothing. A stray. Strays are cheap."

The room changed. She watched it change. Astrid's face did something complicated and terrible, and Wren went white around the mouth, and Serah — Serah stood up, and the floorboard under her boot sang its little note, and Bramble flinched before she could stop herself, and Serah saw her flinch, and something passed over the knight's face like a shadow of a bird.

"You heard all this," Serah said quietly, "through a floor. Alone. At night. From armed men."

"I hear everything," Bramble said. "It's what I'm for."

And then, because the moment was going to close over like water if she didn't move fast, she pulled the brooch out of her pocket and set it on the table between the candle and the map, and the little tin wren lay there with its clouded eye looking up at all of them.

"What's that?" Wren asked.

"My whole everything," Bramble said. "I want to trade it."

"Trade it for what?" Astrid asked, and his voice was gentle, which was worse than sharp, because gentle was what you used on strays.

"For horses. Tonight, before first bell. Out the north gate past the tannery, where the wall's broken, I know the gap, I've been out of it twice already." She pointed at the map, at the river, at the innocent blue line of the ford. "Not the ford. Upstream. There's a weir two miles north — I asked the stable boy about fishing, fishing's a good question, nobody guards an answer about fish. The weir's got a footwalk. We cross there and we're behind them, and their ambush is guarding an empty road."

Silence. The candle spat.

"That's a soldier's read of a position," Serah said at last, to Astrid, not to Bramble, like Bramble wasn't there, like the plan had come from the walls.

"It's a thief's read," Bramble corrected. "Soldiers fight. Thieves don't get caught. Different lessons."

And Astrid laughed — a short, surprised sound, like something breaking open — and then he picked up the brooch very carefully, the way you pick up something that might be alive, and held it out to her.

"We don't need your brooch, Bramble."

"Yes you do." She crossed her arms. "Because if it's free, it's charity, and charity runs out. But if it's a trade, it's a bargain, and bargains hold." She had learned that somewhere, in some winter, from some adult who'd cheated her, and she'd never known until this moment that she'd been keeping the lesson for exactly this night. "You take my treasure, you owe me safe. All the way. That's the deal. That's the *whole* deal."

Astrid looked at her for a long time. His eyes were the color of the ledger ink he wrote with, and Bramble had the sudden, vertiginous feeling of being *read* — entered into a book, given weight, given lines.

"A bargain, then," he said, and shook her grubby hand with his clean one, solemn as a coronation. "Your treasure for our lives. Witnessed."

"Witnessed," said Wren, and put her hand on top of both theirs.

"Witnessed," said Serah, and her hand came down last, heavy and warm, and Bramble's whole arm disappeared under three grown-up hands and she thought, wildly, *this is what being big feels like,* and her eyes were stinging again and she absolutely refused to let them.

Astrid tried to slide the brooch back across the table. Bramble slapped her palm over it.

"Nope. You keep it till we're over the weir. Then I might sell it back to you." She grinned, showing the gap in her teeth. "Might cost extra. Prices go up on dangerous roads."

* * *

They went out through the tannery gap in the grey hour before dawn, when the town smelled of wet hides and woodsmoke and the sky was the color of an old bruise. Bramble led, because it was her gap and her town now, hers by right of having watched it for two nights, and behind her came Serah with her sword wrapped in sacking so it wouldn't flash, and Wren with the map against her heart, and Astrid last, walking backward half the time, watching the streets they'd emptied.

At the top of the river path Bramble stopped and looked back once at Marrowgate, at the inn with its dark corner room, at the willow bend invisible beyond the roofs where men waited with loaded crossbows for a family that wasn't coming.

*The child is nothing.*

She touched the pocket where the brooch wasn't anymore — where it rode instead in Astrid's coat, next to the blood-inked map, a tin wren and a dying god's road traveling together like the strangest cargo in the world — and she found that the hollow where the treasure used to sit didn't hurt the way she'd expected. It felt, instead, like a door standing open.

Small things, she thought, starting down toward the sound of the weir. Small things fit through gaps. Small things hear through floors. Small things weigh less than you think right up until somebody puts them on the scale.

Behind them, faint in the grey distance, a horn sounded from the direction of the willow bend — confused, angry, too late.

Serah smiled like a knife coming out of a sheath. "They've found the empty road."

"Then run faster," Bramble said, and led them down to the water.
