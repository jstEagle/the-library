## 12. Where the Map Bleeds

The rain had followed them for three days, patient as a creditor, and by the fourth it found a way inside everything—boots, bedrolls, the seams of Wren's thoughts. She sat beneath the leaning shelf of rock that Serah called a mercy of the mountain and watched Bramble sleep curled against the knight's cloak like a burr in wool, and she unrolled the map across her knees for the hundredth time.

It was habit now. Ritual. She traced the roads with one finger without seeing them, the way her father used to trace the rim of his cup when he was thinking. The blood-ink had long since dried to a brown so dark it looked black, except where the courier's death had smeared it, except at the edges, where it seemed almost to breathe.

She had copied maps since she was seven years old. She knew ink the way priests knew scripture—every temper of gum arabic, every cruelty of iron gall eating through vellum over the years, every trick a forger could play on the eye. And she knew, with the certainty that lived below thought, below fear, that this map was her father's work. Not merely his style. His hand. The way he broke his north arrow slightly left of true, as if even compasses owed him an apology. The little flourish on the *h* of Harrowmere that looked like a bird taking wing if you squinted. The coastlines drawn with that particular tremor he'd never admit came from too much cheap wine and too little sleep.

But something else had been bothering her, some splinter under the skin of the thing, and tonight—rain drumming the stone, fire low, Astrid hunched over the shrine he'd built from river stones and stubbornness—the splinter finally worked its way out.

The mountains were wrong.

Not badly. Not in any way a guild clerk or a border officer would ever notice. But the ridge lines east of the capital were drawn in a hand heavier than the rest, pressed harder into the vellum, and they repeated. She counted. The same looping contour, the same double-hatched scree slope, appearing four times along the range like a phrase someone kept repeating because they needed you to hear it.

Her father had never repeated himself. He used to say a mapmaker who drew the same line twice was either lazy or lying, and he was neither.

Wren held the map closer to the firelight.

*Heavier hand,* she thought. *Pressed hard enough to emboss.* Her fingers found the ridges before her eyes confirmed them—raised lines, grooves cut deep, the kind of pressure you only used when you wanted a drawing to survive being folded, rolled, rained on. Survived. Not admired. *Read.*

"I need your knife," she said.

Astrid looked up from his shrine. In the firelight his face did what it always did—settled into that ledger-keeper's blankness, the mask of a man whose feelings were filed and cross-referenced. But his eyes went first to the map, then to her hands, and he understood before she explained, because that was the thing about Astrid Vasquez: he read documents the way other men read faces.

"The flourishes," he said quietly.

"Maybe." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Or maybe I'm chasing ghosts."

"You've been staring at that thing for three days like it owes you money." He passed the knife across the fire. "Chase it."

* * *

She worked by feel more than sight, the way he'd taught her. *Eyes lie when they're tired, Wrenlet. Fingers don't.* The blade tip whispering along the raised contours, angling to catch the light, and slowly—like a drowned thing rising—the second layer surfaced.

The repeated ridge-lines weren't mountains. They were letters. Or rather: they were instructions dressed as mountains, the way smugglers dressed contraband in coffins. A cartouche in the northeast corner, which she'd always taken for a decorative border, resolved into a spiral—a cistern spiral, she realized, her breath catching, the same spiral stamped on the old municipal seals of the capital's waterworks. Along the southern road, what looked like surveyors' marks became a sequence of numbers keyed to nothing on the map itself—which meant they were keyed to something off it. Distances. Bearings. A path that existed only in reference to another document.

And at the center, beneath the capital itself, where a cartographer would normally place a rose or a dedication, her father had drawn a tiny, exquisite vignette: a crown, and beneath the crown, almost invisible, a keyhole.

Wren sat very still. The rain filled the silence.

"He knew," she said. It wasn't meant to be aloud, but grief has never respected doors. "He knew he wasn't coming back."

Astrid said nothing. He was good at that—knowing when silence was company instead of absence.

* * *

She decoded through the night.

It took her hours to understand the numbering system, longer to accept how simple it was once she saw it—her father's old survey cipher, the one he'd invented to hide the locations of his best clients' caches, the one he'd taught her when she was nine as a game, *find the buried treasure, Wrenlet*, never telling her that someday the treasure would be this. Every flourish she'd ever loved in his work—the birds hidden in letterforms, the sea-monsters in the margins, the way he signed his maps not with a name but with a small compass rose missing its western point—every one of them was a word.

*The Sigil sleeps beneath the throne. The water remembers the way.*

The cisterns. Her hands shook so badly she had to set the knife down. Beneath the capital, beneath the king's own seat, the old aqueducts ran, and her father—who had mapped those tunnels twenty years ago for a patron whose name he'd burned from all records—had left the route folded into the map like a letter into an envelope.

There was more. Of course there was more. He had known she would be the one to find it; he must have gambled everything on that, the way he gambled at cards, badly and cheerfully and always somehow landing on his feet until the one time he didn't. In the margin, disguised as a scale bar, a final line:

*If you are reading this, you found me. I am sorry I made you look. Forgive me the hiding—I hid because they watch everything, and you were the only one I trusted to see. You always saw. Even at six, you saw the forgery in my own signature and laughed at me for a week.*

Wren put the map down.

She got up and walked to the edge of the overhang and stood in the rain, because the alternative was to do it in front of people, and Sables did not—wept alone or not at all, that was the house rule, though the house was gone now and the rule suddenly struck her as the stupidest thing anyone had ever decided.

The rain was cold and honest and didn't care about house rules.

Her father had disappeared fourteen months ago. Fourteen months of her turning over every explanation like stones: debt collectors, a jealous rival, a woman in Vhalorrest he'd never mentioned, simple cowardice—the theory she'd hated most and secretly believed longest, because it was the one that let her be angry instead of afraid. *He ran. He always talked about running. He just finally did it.*

And the truth was worse and better than any stone she'd turned. He hadn't run. He'd walked toward the thing everyone else walked away from, knowing—he'd *known*, he'd mapped the killing floor of the world and labeled it with love notes—and he'd spent his last months of freedom hiding a rescue plan inside pretty drawings, addressed to no one, trusting that the daughter he'd trained as a forger would one day hold the right paper up to the right light.

He hadn't even dared sign it. Because they watched everything. So he'd signed it the only safe way—with the broken compass rose, the western point missing, the flaw she'd mocked him for, the flaw she'd inherited in her own work without ever knowing why.

*West,* she thought. *Everything points west. Everything always pointed home.*

"Wren." Astrid's voice behind her, low. He'd draped something over her shoulders—his coat, still warm, smelling of ledger dust and lamp oil. He didn't say anything else. That was the mercy of him.

"He died for this," she said. Rain or tears; it didn't matter anymore. "Not dramatically. Not with a sword in his hand. He just... worked. Quietly, for years, on a map that could get everyone he loved killed, and then he let them take him, and he never—" Her voice cracked and she hated it and let it. "I spent fourteen months deciding he'd abandoned me. I built a whole person out of that. I built *me* out of that."

Astrid came to stand beside her, shoulder nearly touching hers, watching the rain stitch silver threads down the black face of the mountain.

"My god went silent ten years ago," he said at last. "I spent ten years deciding I'd been abandoned too." A pause. "Turns out he was in a cage. Turns out the silence was the loudest thing he ever said." He glanced at her sideways. "Seems to be a family trait, keeping love quiet until it can't hurt anyone."

"That's not a family trait," Wren said. "That's a disease."

"Mm." Almost a smile. "Serah keeps saying we should vaccinate."

Despite everything—a wet, cracked laugh escaped her, shocking them both.

* * *

Near dawn the rain stopped, and the world came out washed and ringing. Bramble woke first, as she always did, materializing at Wren's elbow like a cat that had heard a can open, and stared down at the map spread between them with the frank greed of the professionally curious.

"What's it say?"

"It says my father loved me," Wren said, "in survey coordinates."

Bramble considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "Mine didn't leave anything. Just lice." Then, brightening: "Can I have the sea-monster? The little one in the corner. When we're done with it."

"It's not a monster, it's a bearing marker."

"It's a monster if you believe in it." And she tapped the margin with one grimy finger, exactly on the coiled beast her father had drawn off the southern cape—and Wren froze.

Because the monster was new.

Not new-drawn. New-*revealed*. Under the dawn light, angled just so, a second creature ghosted beneath the first, fainter, done in the thinned blood-ink her father used for corrections—a leviathan shape coiling up out of the southern sea toward the land, toward the capital, its body forming a line that connected, when she traced it, to the spiral cartouche in the northeast. Water to throne. Cisterns to Sigil. The whole route, drawn twice: once for the eyes of the guild, once for the eyes who knew to look.

And beneath the leviathan, in letters so small she needed the knife-tip and the full morning sun to read them, one last instruction. The last flourish. The last word of the last letter of a dead man to the daughter who'd finally learned to see:

*Beware the third door. It opens inward.*

Wren read it three times. Then she rolled the map with great care, tied it, and turned to the others—Serah tightening saddle girths, Bramble braiding grass into the horse's mane, Astrid standing at the tree line with that stillness he wore when one of his god's memories was moving through him like weather.

"We ride today," she said. "My father left us a way under the capital. Through the cisterns." She swung up onto her horse, and her voice, when it came again, was steady in the way of forged things—stronger than the original metal, made so on purpose. "And he left us a warning about a door. Which means somewhere ahead of us, someone is going to offer us one."

Far off to the west, past the hills, the capital lay under a haze of morning smoke—too much smoke, even for a city that size, as if something beneath it had begun, very quietly, to burn.
