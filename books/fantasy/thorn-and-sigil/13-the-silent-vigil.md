## 13. The Silent Vigil

The mountain came down on them at dusk.

Astrid heard it before he understood it—a sound like the world clearing its throat, deep and wet and wrong—and then the pass above them was no longer a pass but a waterfall of stone. He had time to shove Bramble behind him and time to shout Wren's name and not much else. The world went white with dust and thunder.

When it settled, they were alive, which felt like an administrative error.

The road was gone. Behind them: a wall of shattered granite taller than the temple spires of Vael, choked with scree that would shift and slide if so much as a goat looked at it sideways. Ahead: another wall, this one older, where the trail had threaded between two leaning slabs of rock like a needle's eye. The slide had plugged the eye. They were in the pocket between—maybe forty paces of old road, walled in on both sides, open only to the sky, which was already bruising toward night.

"Everyone breathing?" Serah's voice came out of the dust, hoarse but level. She emerged from the gray like something carved, Bramble tucked under one arm despite the girl's furious wriggling.

"I can't breathe," Bramble announced, "because someone's squeezing me like a wineskin."

"You're welcome," Serah said, and set her down.

Wren appeared last, coughing, her satchel clutched to her chest with both arms—the map, Astrid knew. She always carried it against her body now, the way other people carried infants or knives. She looked at the walls of stone, then at the sky, then did the arithmetic aloud because she was Wren and could not help herself. "Three days to dig through either end without tools. We have food for two."

"We have water for one," Serah said.

Bramble, who had been quietly patting her pockets, went very still. "My canteen's gone."

The silence that followed had weight. Astrid looked at the child—at the sharp little face trying so hard not to show anything, because showing things got you hurt where she'd come from—and he saw her do the same terrible math he'd done a hundred times in the guild's back rooms, when a ledger didn't balance and someone would have to pay the difference.

"It fell during the slide," he said. "It'll be in the rubble. We'll find it when we dig."

She looked at him. She knew he was lying. He knew she knew. Something passed between them anyway—an agreement to let the lie stand, because it was a kind thing, and kindness was a currency they were all running low on.

They made camp in the lee of the western wall. Serah built a fire from the emergency kit she kept strapped inside her cuirass like a guilty secret—"a knight plans for disgrace," she said grimly, "even if she never plans for slides"—and they ate half-rations and drank what remained and spoke as little as possible, because speaking made thirst worse.

Astrid took first watch, because he always did. Sleep had become a country he visited less and less.

He sat with his back to the stone and watched the stars come out over the notch of sky above them, and he felt it begin.

* * *

It had been happening for weeks now—the bleeding-through. Small things at first. A smell of cedar smoke in rooms with no fire. A word in a language he didn't know rising to his lips like a burp. The sense, walking through a market, that he had walked through this market four hundred years ago when it was a riverbed.

But tonight the stone around him hummed.

He felt it through his spine first, a low vibration like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing, and he realized with distant, clerkish clarity that it wasn't the mountain humming. It was the mountain *remembering*. Old stone held old prayers; every pilgrim who had ever crossed this pass had left a little of their longing in the rock, and longing was a language, and somewhere beneath the language there was a listener.

*You are sitting on my hand,* said a voice that was not a voice. *I have been holding this mountain up for a very long time.*

Astrid did not scream. He was proud of that, later. What he did was grip his knife and say, very quietly, "Vesperian."

*You say my name like an entry in a ledger.* The voice was everywhere—in the firelight, in the dust, in the marrow of his teeth. It sounded tired the way oceans are tired: vast, patient, worn smooth by repetition. *Debit. Credit. One god, outstanding balance, ten years delinquent.*

"What do you want?"

*I want to show you.* And the stone opened like an eye.

* * *

He fell upward into memory.

He stood on a hillside under a sun that was not his sun, in a city of white towers and green terraces, and he knew—not thought, *knew*, the way you know your own hands—that the city was called Aurelith and that it was the most beautiful thing human hands had ever made. He knew it because he was seeing it through eyes that had loved it. Through eyes that were watching it burn.

The vision was total. Not the flickering fragments he'd suffered since the courier died—those had been torn pages shoved under a door. This was the whole book. He was *in* Vesperian, wearing the god's perception like a coat, and the coat fit horribly well.

He watched through those ancient eyes as the Sigil rose out of the earth beneath the palace—a thing of geometry and hunger, a pattern that ate meaning, coiling up through the foundations of the world like ivy made of mathematics. He watched it unmake the terraces. He watched it drink the names of the dead, and the dead forgot themselves mid-scream, becoming smooth-faced blanks that wandered the burning streets looking for something they'd lost and couldn't name.

And he watched Vesperian make the choice.

Not in battle. There was no battle. That was the horror of it—the thing the hymns had gotten wrong, the thing every priest of the Silent Order had sung falsely for generations. There was no war in heaven. There was a negotiation.

*You cannot be destroyed,* said the Sigil, in a voice like a ledger closing. *But you can be filed away.*

*Then file me,* said Vesperian. *But I set the terms.*

Astrid felt the terms being written even as the god spoke them, felt each clause land in his chest like a nail:

*A binding, not a death. Temporary—I will write temporary into the very structure of it, so that it frays on its own within a generation or three. You will hold me beneath your capital, yes, but you will not wear a king while you do it. You will not touch the minds of the living. And you will leave my people a way to find me. An anchor. A thread of my substance woven into mortal blood, so that when the binding weakens, someone can come and pull the knot loose.*

*Agreed,* said the Sigil.

And Astrid felt—felt with the god's whole ruined heart—the moment the Sigil lied. Felt the clauses twist as they were written into the binding-stone, *temporary* becoming *temporary at the discretion of the binder*, *an anchor* becoming *an anchor, and the anchor will be ours to use*. Felt the chains close not around Vesperian's body but around his heart, the literal heart, the organ the old stories said gods kept outside their chests like a man keeps his purse.

And then the god did the last thing he could do. As the dark closed over him, he gathered everything—all of it, the burning city, the broken terms, the shape of the lie—and he pressed it into the anchor. Into the bloodline. Into a single family of farmers in the hills south of Aurelith whose eldest daughter had once shared her bread with a stranger who smelled of cedar smoke.

*When you are ready,* he whispered into their blood, across the generations, *I will tell you everything.*

* * *

Astrid came back to himself on his knees in the dirt, hands flat against the stone, gasping as though he'd been underwater. The fire had burned low. Someone had put a blanket over his shoulders—Wren, probably; it smelled of ink. The others slept in a heap near the embers, Bramble curled against Serah's side like a burr.

The stars had moved. Hours, then. He'd been gone for hours and his body had knelt here the whole time, breathing, while he lived four hundred years of someone else's grief.

His hands were shaking. He watched them shake with a strange detachment, the clerk in him observing the discrepancy between the account and the record.

An anchor. Generations of them. His bloodline.

He thought about the orphanage at Corvess, the one run by the Order, where the priests had always been a little too interested in him. Where Mother Caslin had checked his eyes every year on his birthday—"just a habit, dear"—holding a candle close and looking for something. He'd assumed it was affection. It had been the closest thing to affection he'd known, and he had *missed it*, afterward, when they'd sent him at fourteen to apprentice with the Gilded Thorn, and the letters from the orphanage had stopped coming, and he'd told himself that was how growing up worked.

They hadn't stopped writing because he'd grown up. They'd stopped writing because the anchoring was complete. Because whatever the Order had been tending in his blood had ripened, and he'd been shipped—filed—to the one place in the world where a boy who could hear a silent god might be useful, or might be found, or might simply be kept where the guild's ledgers could watch him.

Every kindness had been a clause. Every coincidence, a contract term.

The rage came up through him slow, like groundwater rising in a cellar. It didn't feel like the hot rage of the young. It felt like the cold rage of an auditor finding fraud in accounts he'd personally balanced—fraud committed by everyone he'd ever trusted, in his own name, using his own life as the currency.

He had spent thirty years believing he was invisible by talent. That his carefulness, his competence, his gift for being overlooked were skills he'd honed, achievements of a self-made man. And the truth was that he'd been made *overlookable*. Designed for it. Bred into the margins of the world like a plant bred for shade, so that when the moment came, the anchor would be standing exactly where no one was looking—right beside the ledgers, right inside the guild, right under the Sigil's own network of eyes.

Even the courier. Even the dying man in the alley with the blood-inked map. Had that been chance? Had the Order—or what remained of it, or what Vesperian's fading will could still nudge—steered that man to that alley, to that door, to *him*? Had Astrid's entire life been a delivery route, and he the package?

"You're awake."

Wren sat down beside him without asking, the way she did everything—without permission, without apology. She had the map across her knees, and in the ember-light the ink seemed to move faintly, the way it had begun to lately, rivers shifting in their beds like sleeping men turning over.

"You were talking in your sleep," she said. "Not words. More like... handwriting. Your mouth was doing the shapes of letters." She paused. "Old letters. I've seen them in my father's reference folios. Pre-Imperial script."

"He's showing me things." Astrid's voice came out scraped raw. "Everything. The whole of it."

"And?"

"And I'm angry."

Wren snorted. "Congratulations. You've discovered the national pastime." But she said it gently, and she didn't move away, and after a moment she added, "Do you want to talk about it, or do you want to sit next to someone while you don't talk about it? Both are fine. I'm good at both."

He almost laughed. Almost. "He chose us. My family. Generations ago. Without asking. He put himself in our blood like a coin sewn into a coat lining, and he let us live our whole lives thinking we were just—people. Orphans. Clerks. Nobody." His fists clenched against his knees. "I prayed to him for ten years, Wren. Ten years, every night, at a shrine I built with my own hands, and he never answered. And now I know why. He wasn't absent. He was *waiting*. Letting the binding fray, letting the anchor ripen, letting me walk exactly the path he needed walked. My loneliness had a purpose. Do you understand what that does to a person? Every bad night I ever survived turns out to have been load-bearing."

Wren was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, she pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, eyes on the dying fire.

"My father left when I was nine," she said. "No note. Just gone, and my mother drinking herself blind with shame, and me learning to forge seals because a girl who can copy any signature can feed a household." She turned the map over in her lap. "I hated him for eleven years. And now I find out he drew this knowing he'd die for it. Knowing I'd carry it. He hid instructions in the flourishes, Astrid. In the *decorations*. He was talking to me the whole time, from the other side of his own death, and I couldn't hear him because I was too busy hating him to look closely." She shrugged, a small hard motion. "So I don't know if it helps. But being chosen without consent—it's not the same as being used. Sometimes it's just being loved clumsily. By someone with no better options."

"He had options. He's a *god*."

"He's a prisoner," Wren said flatly. "Prisoners write desperate letters. Haven't you read the guild's recovered correspondence? People in holes say terrible clumsy true things." She glanced sideways at him. "Besides. You're a ledger-keeper. Tell me honestly—if he'd asked your great-great-grandmother's permission, in person, explained the whole contract—what would she have said? Would anyone sane have said yes? *Let me hide my heart in your children's children so that someday one of them can die freeing me?*"

Astrid opened his mouth. Closed it. Because the answer was no, obviously no, and that was precisely the point, and Wren knew it, and the knowing sat between them like a third person at the fire.

"No one consents to being important," Wren said softly. "That's the trick of it. Importance finds you asleep and moves in."

Above them, in the notch of sky, the stars had begun to pale toward a false dawn that was still hours off. Astrid laid his palm flat against the stone wall, and this time he did it deliberately, and he felt the hum rise to meet him, patient as tide.

"I'm not forgiving you," he said aloud, to the mountain, to the god inside it. "Understand that. Whatever comes next—I do it because the king is a man in a puppet's skin and a child lost her canteen and my friend's father died drawing maps. Not because you wrote me into a story before I was born. If you want my help, you'll take it as a gift, freely given, or not at all. I keep clean books. Even now. *Especially* now."

The hum shifted. And in it, faint as a smile felt rather than seen, he heard:

*Clean books. Then let us begin the audit.*

The stone beneath his palm grew warm. Warm as skin. And along the wall of the cut, in the ancient pilgrim-graffiti scratched there by a thousand years of travelers, the old marks began to glow—one by one, line by line, until the entire cliff face was lit with script Astrid suddenly, terribly, could read.

It was a list. A ledger, scratched into the mountain by Vesperian's faithful in the years after the Binding—a census of anchors, generation after generation, names and dates, down and down and down.

To the very last line.

Where his own name was already written.

And beneath it, in fresher strokes, in ink that gleamed wet and red in the ember-light, someone else had added an entry of their own:

**RECEIVED — ONE ANCHOR. PAYMENT DUE AT THE THRONE.**

The signature was Veyra's.
