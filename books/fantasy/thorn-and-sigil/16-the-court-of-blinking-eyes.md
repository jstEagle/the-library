## 16. The Court of Blinking Eyes

The morning of the audience began, as all mornings now began, with the taste of iron.

Aldric woke in his own bed, in his own body, in a room he had slept in since boyhood—and found it strange. The canopy above him was embroidered with thorned roses, gold thread on wine-dark silk, and he could not remember choosing it. He could not remember *not* choosing it either. That was the cruelest arithmetic of possession: not the absence of memory but the presence of someone else's certainty laid over yours like wet linen over a face.

He lay still and counted what was his.

His name. He still had his name, though he had learned to keep it buried deep, below the level where the Sigil's attention swept like a lantern across water. *Aldric.* Not the king. The man who had once, at nineteen, climbed the palace wall drunk on summer wine to carve his initials into the oldest oak in the royal garden, and been caught by a gardener who laughed instead of reporting him. That man. That name. He held it the way a drowning man holds a stone that might be an anchor or might only be weight.

*They cannot take what they do not notice,* he told himself, as he told himself every morning. *Be small. Be furniture. Be a king-shaped nothing.*

Then the Sigil rose in him like a tide coming in, and Aldric went away.

He did not lose consciousness—that would have been mercy. He remained, pressed flat against the inside of his own skull, watching through eyes that were no longer entirely his as his hands dressed him in the regalia of a kingdom he still loved. The Sigil moved his fingers with terrible competence. It had studied him for years before it took him; he understood that now. It knew how he liked his collar pinned, which shoulder carried the ceremonial mantle better, how he preferred to hold his scepter slightly left of center so his wrist wouldn't ache by midday. It wore his habits like a tailor wears a client's measurements.

*You are well-fitted,* it said sometimes, in a voice that was not a voice, more a pressure against the walls of his mind, smooth and cold as river stone. *I have improved you. You were soft. You hesitated. I do not hesitate.*

*I hesitated because hesitation is where mercy lives,* Aldric answered, though he never answered aloud, never gave it the satisfaction of resistance it could punish. His rebellions were all interior now, small and silent as seeds underground. *You will learn that too late.*

The Sigil did not trouble itself to reply. It had stopped debating him months ago, the way a farmer stops arguing with weather.

* * *

The throne room at midmorning was a machine for manufacturing awe, and Aldric—Aldric's body—sat at its heart like the flywheel.

Sunlight fell through windows of stained glass depicting the nine kings of the Vessarine line, each one rendered in glory: crowned, sword raised, enemies kneeling. Aldric had always privately thought the glass made them look like saints pretending not to be frightened. Now he sat beneath them and understood the expression perfectly. Every king in that window had known moments when the crown weighed more than the man. None of them, he suspected, had known this.

The court assembled in its hierarchies. Ministers first, then the high clergy of the Concord—the state faith that had grown fat and strange in the decade since Vesperian went silent, its vestments shifting year by year toward colors Aldric did not recognize, its liturgy acquiring syllables that tasted wrong on the tongue. Then the ambassadors, the guild factors, the petitioners shuffling forward with their scrolls and grievances.

And then, near the back, among a knot of merchants claiming tax grievances from the eastern roads—a face.

Aldric's heart, the small stubborn animal of it, the part the Sigil had never fully colonized, lurched.

He knew that face. Not the face itself—he had never seen the man before—but the *quality* of it. Astrid Vasquez walked through the world the way Aldric had once walked through it: carefully invisible, competent, watchful, a person who had learned early that attention was a currency best hoarded. Aldric saw the ledger-clerk's posture, the ink-stained discretion of the hands, the way those gray-green eyes catalogued everything and confessed nothing. And beneath the disguise—the false merchant's beard, the caravan master's rough-spun cloak—beneath all of it, Aldric saw something else. Something that glowed faintly, the way coals glow under ash.

Vesperian's light. Or what remained of it. The god's silence had lasted ten years, but silence, Aldric had come to understand, was not emptiness. Somewhere out there, the god's anchor-walkers still carried embers.

*There,* thought the buried king, and the word rang through his hollowed halls like a bell through an empty cathedral. *There is one of them. One of the faithful. One who might still hear.*

The Sigil felt it too. Aldric sensed its attention sharpen, a needle turning slowly toward the back of the room, tasting the air the way a snake tastes breath. For one horrible moment Aldric feared it would simply reach out and snuff the stranger where he stood—one gesture, one decree, one "seize that man"—and the last hope of the kingdom would die between two columns of imported marble.

But the Sigil was patient in the way of things that believe themselves eternal. It wanted to know what the ember-man wanted. It wanted to follow him home. It wanted, most of all, to find the map—to find the route to the vault beneath the throne, the one place in the world where its own body lay coiled and vulnerable around a chained god's heart.

So it let the audience proceed. And Aldric, pressed flat against the inside of his own skull, watched the stranger approach the throne step by step, and did the only thing left to a man in his position.

He waited for his moment.

* * *

It came sooner than he dared hope.

The petitioners ahead of the merchants droned through their grievances: a boundary dispute, a miller's complaint about grain tariffs, a widow asking justice for a son conscripted and lost. The Sigil dispensed judgments through Aldric's mouth in a tone it had modeled on his own—warm, measured, faintly weary, the voice of a king who cares. It was a good imitation. It was even, in its way, a kind of tribute. But Aldric heard the hollowness underneath, the way you can hear the hollowness of a painted wall when you knock on it.

Then the merchant spokesman stepped forward—a broad woman with sun-cracked hands and an honest face—and began her recitation of tolls levied, tariffs doubled, roads made dangerous by collectors who wore the Thorn's colors and the crown's authority interchangeably.

And behind her, half-shadowed by a pillar, stood the ember-man.

Astrid Vasquez looked up.

Their eyes met.

It should have been nothing. Kings are looked at constantly; it is the occupational condition of crowns. A subject's gaze means nothing, holds nothing, risks nothing. But this gaze *knocked.* This gaze knocked on the painted wall of him and heard the hollow sound, and Aldric felt it like a hand pressed flat against his chest from outside his body.

*He sees me,* Aldric thought, and the thought was so loud, so violently his own, that for one incandescent instant it burned through the fog the Sigil kept poured over his mind like milk over a floor.

One breath. He had perhaps one breath.

The Sigil's attention was divided—listening to the widow's grievance, tracking the merchant's words, savoring the slow machinery of judgment. It had grown complacent. Ten years of unopposed rule will do that even to a thing made of patience. And in that division, in that sliver of inattention, Aldric moved.

Not his body. His body belonged to the thing wearing it. But his mouth—his mouth was still, at its deepest root, his. The Sigil controlled the tongue's grand gestures, the voice, the words of decree. It had never bothered to master the small muscles, the ones that shaped whispers rather than speeches. Why would it? Who whispered to a king?

Aldric shaped the word with his lips alone. No sound. No breath. Just the movement, small as a moth's wing, visible only to someone already looking directly at his mouth from twenty feet away in a crowded hall.

*Cis-tern.*

He watched the word land. Watched Astrid Vasquez go very still—the stillness of a man who has just heard a dead friend speak. Watched the clerk's eyes widen a fraction, flick up to meet the king's eyes again, and understand.

*Understand,* Aldric thought, pouring everything into the thought, into the shape of his lips, into the desperate telepathy of one human soul shouting across a possessed face at another. *Understand, please. Beneath the throne. The old cisterns. My grandfather sealed them; my father mapped them; there is a way in that the Sigil does not guard because it does not remember it exists. Come by water. Come beneath. I am here. I am still here. Hurry.*

The widow finished her petition. The Sigil turned Aldric's head, granted her relief with a graciousness it had learned from studying him, and moved to the next matter.

The moment closed like water over a dropped stone.

But something had changed in the room. Aldric felt it—the Sigil felt it too. A wrongness, small as a single false note in an orchestra, lingering in the air where the ember-man stood. The Sigil's attention swung back toward the back of the hall, hungry now, suspicious, replaying the last minute the way a gambler replays a hand he suspects was cheated.

And it found the stillness. Found the stare. Found the fraction of a second where a merchant's clerk had looked at the King of Vessar with recognition rather than awe.

*What did you see?* the Sigil asked the room, in the voice it used for interrogations, soft and reasonable and terrible. *What did any of you see?*

No one answered, because no one had seen anything. That was the horror of it. Only three beings in that hall knew a message had passed: the king, the stranger, and the thing that owned the king's flesh. And of the three, only the Sigil had the power to act.

It acted.

* * *

"Before we conclude," said the king's voice—Aldric's voice, warm and regretful, pitched to carry sorrow like a father announcing a necessary punishment—"there is a matter of internal security."

The hall stilled. Courtiers had learned, in the past year, to fear that particular warmth. It preceded arrests.

"The crown has tolerated, for too long, certain irregularities in the eastern trade roads. Smuggling. Sedition. The trafficking of forbidden texts." The Sigil paused, letting the silence do its work. "Effective immediately, all persons present at today's audience will be registered, questioned, and verified against the ledgers of record. Those whose names cannot be confirmed will be detained for further inquiry."

Murmurs rippled through the court—not outrage, Aldric noted bitterly, but the practiced calm of people who had survived purges before and knew the choreography. Stand still. Look bored. Pray your papers are in order.

Guards moved along the walls. Clerks appeared with rolls of vellum. The great doors at the far end of the throne room swung shut with a boom that Aldric felt in his borrowed bones.

And the Sigil, wearing his face, smiled his smile, and said in his voice:

"We are assured this will inconvenience no honest subject."

*Astrid,* Aldric screamed inwardly, with everything left of him. *Run. Get out. Don't be clever, don't stand your ground, don't finish whatever game you're playing—RUN—*

Through his stolen eyes he watched the ember-man move with the unhurried calm of a man who had spent his life being overlooked. Astrid Vasquez drifted toward the side exit used by household staff, produced papers at a checkpoint without fumbling, and was—Aldric's heart seized—waved through. For one moment, one blessed moment, it seemed the message had landed clean and the messenger had escaped clean, and hope, real hope, the first Aldric had permitted himself in ten years, bloomed in the ruined garden of his chest like a flower through pavement.

Then the Sigil's attention snagged on the merchant delegation.

The broad-handed spokeswoman. Her papers were perfect. Her story was perfect. Everything about her was perfect, and the Sigil, which had spent a decade learning to read humans the way Aldric once read ledgers, smelled perfection and knew it for forgery. Honest people were imperfect. Honest people fumbled. This woman handed over her documents like a soldier presenting arms.

"Detain her," the Sigil said, in Aldric's voice, almost gently. "Question her thoroughly."

She did not run. She argued—loudly, indignantly, with the genuine fury of a woman who might actually be innocent, which was either magnificent acting or magnificent bad luck. Guards took her arms. She spat at their feet. The court watched with downcast eyes, grateful it was not them.

And Aldric, trapped behind the king's face, understood with cold clarity what he was watching. The Sigil had not caught Astrid. It had caught the decoy. Which meant the caravan, the map-bearers, the whole ragged company of them had planned for this—which meant they were professionals, which meant they were *coming*, which meant—

Which meant the cisterns. Which meant beneath the throne. Which meant the vault where the Sigil's true body lay coiled around Vesperian's chained heart was about to have visitors.

Hope arrived, as hope always seemed to arrive in Aldric's long captivity, at the worst possible moment—flaring brightest precisely when the darkness gathered closest.

Because the Sigil had reached the same conclusion. And the Sigil did not wait for confirmation. The Sigil did not hesitate.

It never hesitated.

"Purge the eastern quarter," Aldric's mouth announced to his assembled ministers, and the words came out smooth and dreadful, a decree drafted in a heartbeat, signed with his seal, spoken with his sorrow. "Every lodging house, every warehouse, every cellar from the River Gate to the Tanners' Row. Search for the fugitives. Detain anyone harboring them. Anyone resisting detention is to be considered an enemy of the crown."

Ministers bowed. Secretaries scribbled. The machine of the state, oiled by ten years of quiet compliance, began to turn.

And somewhere beneath the throne room, beneath the marble and the mortar and the bones of the old kings, water moved through channels cut by masons three generations dead, carrying toward the vault the sound of footsteps that should not exist.

The Sigil felt them too. Felt them the way a spider feels the first tremor in the outer ring of its web. Aldric sensed its attention turn downward, inward, toward the dark beneath its own seat—and sensed, for the first time in ten years, something he had never dared hope to feel again.

Fear.

It was a small fear, tightly controlled, wrapped in layers of contempt. *Vermin,* it thought, using his mind's vocabulary against him. *Rats in the cellar. I will deal with them after the coronation preparations.*

But it was fear. And fear meant the web could break.

That night, locked in the gilded cage of his own skull while the Sigil reviewed troop deployments by candlelight, Aldric allowed himself one act of rebellion larger than a whispered word. He remembered. Deliberately, furiously, he remembered himself: the oak tree and his carved initials, the gardener's laugh, his mother's hands, the oath he had sworn at his coronation—not to the Concord, not to the crown, but to the *people*, every one of them, even the ones who would never know his name.

*Hold on,* he told the stranger in the dark beneath his feet, telling himself as much as anyone. *I am here. I have always been here. Come and find me.*

Far below, in the black water-ways of the old city, something answered—not in words, but in the language of moving water and steady footsteps and a lamp burning low.

They were coming.

And the purge had already begun.
