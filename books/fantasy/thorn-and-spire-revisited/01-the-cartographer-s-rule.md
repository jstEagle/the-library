## 1. The Cartographer's Rule

The first rule of cartography in Greyhollow was simple: never draw a map of anywhere real.

Felix Crane had learned it at their mother's knee, or rather, they had learned it from the silence that followed every question about her old work, the way she would go still as a snared bird and change the subject to the weather, to the price of tallow, to anything but the shape of the world. By the time Felix was old enough to understand why the neighbors crossed the street when she passed, why the tax collectors always seemed to know exactly which door to knock on, they had already taught themselves the corollary rules. Never draw coastlines you have not invented. Never name a river after one that exists. And above all, never let anyone see what your hands can do.

Twenty years of practice had made Felix very good at being no one.

They worked the night market in the Tannery Rows, where the lanterns were paper and the light came through them the color of weak tea, selling charms to people who could not afford to believe in them and bought them anyway. A luck-knot for the fishwife whose husband hadn't come home in three days. A stitched sigil for a midwife's apron, nothing so grand as protection—just *ease*, just a small untying of the world's habitual cruelty. Felix priced everything low and haggled against themselves, because the fastest way to be forgotten was to be generous, and the second fastest was to be dull.

Their stall was a folding table with a cracked leather roll of tools: bone styli worn smooth as river stones, inks mixed in thimbles, a scrap of grey felt to catch the spills. Everything ordinary. Everything deniable. The magic itself lived in Felix's fingers, quiet as breath, and it stayed quiet because Felix kept it that way, the way other people kept a bad tooth from aching by refusing to bite down.

"Charms," they said now, to a woman in a butcher's apron, "are nonsense. Superstition. I sell them because fools are born every market day."

The woman smiled the smile of someone who knew the ritual. She bought the knot for her daughter's wrist—a child who cried at night over dreams of falling—and pressed two coppers into Felix's palm with a warmth that made something in Felix's chest ache briefly, stupidly, before they folded the feeling away like a map along its creases.

That was the third rule, the unwritten one, the one that governed everything else: want nothing. Wanting left fingerprints. Wanting got people noticed, and noticed people in Greyhollow got visited by the Cartographers' Guild's enforcement men, who wore grey gloves and carried grey knives and asked questions in voices soft as sifting ash. Two decades ago there had been a purge. Felix's mother had survived it by becoming a woman who sold buttons. Felix had survived it by becoming no one at all.

It was working beautifully, right up until the stranger arrived.

* * *

He came out of the fog off the canal the way good trouble always does—unhurried, smiling, as though he had been expected somewhere else entirely and had simply decided Felix's stall was more interesting. He was tall and loose-limbed, with dark hair curling damp at his collar and eyes the warm brown of tea gone cold in a cup you keep drinking anyway because you like holding it. His coat was good wool, patched at the elbows with deliberate carelessness, and he moved through the crowd the way water moves through cloth—not pushing, just arriving everywhere at once.

He stopped at the table and looked at the charms without touching them.

"You're the one who draws," he said.

"I sell knots and stitches." Felix kept their voice flat, bored, a door closing politely. "Superstition. Fools born daily."

"Mm." He tilted his head, reading Felix the way Felix read ink—looking past the surface to what bled underneath. "You've got graphite under your nails. Silver-point, actually, if I'm not mistaken, though nobody uses silver-point anymore except people who need lines fine enough to lie with."

Felix's hands went still on the table. "You're mistaken."

"Often," he agreed cheerfully. "But not tonight." He leaned in, and up close Felix caught the smell of him—woodsmoke and old paper and something faintly green, like a forest after rain, which was absurd this deep in the Tannery Rows where the only green things grew in bottles. "My name is Wren. And I'd like to commission a map."

"No maps."

"Not of anywhere real." Wren produced a coin from his pocket and set it spinning on the table between them; it wobbled, gleamed, fell flat with a sound too loud for its size. "Somewhere neither of us has ever been. That's all. Somewhere invented. You do invent places, don't you? Everyone who draws does. Everyone who's ever been lonely has a country in their head."

Felix should have sent him away. Every rule they owned said *send him away*, and the rules had kept them alive through twenty years of Greyhollow winters, twenty years of being small enough to slip through the city's grasping fingers unnoticed. But the fog was coming off the canal in slow silver ropes, and the paper lanterns breathed their weak tea light, and Wren was looking at Felix with an expression Felix had not been looked at with in living memory—as though Felix were not a stall, not a service, not a pair of careful hands, but a *door*.

"What's it worth to you?" Felix heard themselves say, and hated how much it sounded like wanting.

Wren smiled like sunrise breaking over a harbor. "Everything I have, eventually. But tonight—" he set three silver marks on the table, an obscene sum for a doodle, "—tonight, this."

* * *

Felix told themselves it was the money. They were good at telling themselves things; they'd had two decades of practice.

They took out the good paper—the heavy rag stock they saved for work that didn't exist—and pinned it to the table with two brass weights shaped like sleeping cats. They took up the silver-point stylus, the one hidden beneath the felt, and they did what they had done ten thousand times in ten thousand safe ways: they invented.

A coastline first, because every country needs an edge. Then harbors, then hills, then a river branching like a crack in glass. Felix drew the way other people prayed, without words, the stylus whispering, and around them the night market went on—fishwives and knife-grinders, the cry of hot chestnuts, a fiddler mangling a reel two stalls down—and none of it touched the small bright country growing under Felix's hands.

Wren watched without speaking. He had pulled a crate over and sat on it backwards, arms folded across the backrest, chin down, and he watched the way people watch fires. It should have been unnerving. It was, instead, the most seen Felix had felt since childhood, and they hated how much they didn't hate it.

"There," Felix said at last, when the map was done: a bay called—Felix didn't decide the name, it arrived whole—*Vesperhold*. Hills behind it going gold in a sun that existed nowhere. A road leading inland toward mountains the color of bruised plums. "Invented. Imaginary. Worthless."

"It's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen," Wren said, and the terrible thing was that he sounded like he meant it, and the worse thing was that Felix believed him.

Then the ink moved.

Not the silver-point—that was dry, that was only metal dragged across rag—but the ink, the washes Felix had laid in for shadow, the sea they had tinted with a single stroke of diluted indigo. The sea *moved*. A slow swell traveled across the bay, lapped at the invented harbor, and receded. The gold on the hills deepened, spread, became evening. Along the road to the plum-colored mountains, something tiny and dark began to walk.

Felix did not breathe. Around them the market roared on, oblivious, chestnuts and fiddles and fog, while on the table a country that did not exist quietly began to.

"That's not possible," Felix whispered. Their voice came out strange, scraped thin. "I didn't—I don't—it's supposed to stay *still*."

"Is it?" Wren asked softly. But he had gone pale too, Felix saw; his knuckles were white where they gripped the crate's backrest. Whatever wonder was doing to Felix's face, fear was doing to his. "Felix Crane. Look at the road."

The tiny dark thing walking the road had stopped. The ink around it had gathered, pooled, drawn itself up into letters—small, precise, in a hand that was not Felix's, in a language Felix almost recognized the way you almost recognize a face across a crowded square.

Wren read it aloud, and his voice shook:

"*When the debtor comes to draw the door,*" he read, "*the last fire will know its name.*"

Silence. The fiddler finished the reel. Somewhere a gull complained over the canal.

"What did you call me?" Felix said.

Wren looked up, and in the paper-lantern light his eyes were not warm brown at all but amber, lit from within, lit like coals, lit like the far end of a very long tunnel with a fire burning in it. "I didn't," he said. "I've never met you before tonight. But I think—" he swallowed, and for the first time the charm in him cracked, and underneath was something young and frightened and achingly sincere, "—I think I've been dreaming about your maps my whole life."

On the table, the sea went on breathing. The tiny figure on the road stood waiting, patient as a debt, and the letters of the prophecy dried into the paper as though they had always been there, as though Felix had drawn them themselves, twenty years ago, in a life they couldn't remember.

Felix's hands, those careful, invisible, rule-keeping hands, were trembling.

And for the first time in twenty years, Felix Crane wanted something—wanted it so badly it felt like bleeding: to know what walked on that road, and why it knew their name.
