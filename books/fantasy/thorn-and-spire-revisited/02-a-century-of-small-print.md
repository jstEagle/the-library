## 2. A Century of Small Print

Wren had stolen books before. It was, in truth, the only honest work they'd ever loved.

The shop on Vellum Row kept no name above its door—only a brass bell gone green with age and a sign that read CLOSED in six languages, five of them dead. Wren knew the lock's temperament the way other people knew their mothers' moods: it wanted to be persuaded, not forced. A hairpin, a whispered apology, and the door sighed open onto dust that tasted like old prayers.

They hadn't come for anything in particular. That was the lie Wren told themselves as they moved between the shelves, fingers trailing spines like a lover trailing a wrist. They'd come because the night was too quiet after Felix left, because the ink on their own hands still smelled of somewhere neither of them had ever been, because the map they'd drawn together—the one where the rivers ran silver and uphill—was folded against their heart like a second heartbeat.

*Find me something true,* Wren thought at the shelves. *I'll steal it.*

The book found them instead.

It sat on the lowest shelf, wedged behind a dictionary of extinct birds, bound in leather the color of dried blood. No title. No author. When Wren pulled it free, the weight of it was wrong—heavier than its size, the way grief is heavier than its cause. The spine crackled. Dust rose in a slow gold spiral, and in the lamplight leaking through the shutters, Wren saw the date pressed into the first page.

*Compiled in the Year of the Ashen Bell.*
One hundred years ago. Give or take a winter.

A prophecy book. Greyhollow burned those when it found them; the cartographers' guild had a special bonfire for them, right outside the guildhall, where the smoke always smelled faintly of burnt sugar. Wren should have put it back. Wren had survived twenty-some years in this city by knowing exactly which doors not to open.

Their hands turned the pages anyway.

The paper was soft as moth wing. The script inside was cramped, urgent, the handwriting of someone writing fast before the words could escape. And then—

Wren stopped breathing.

*And the stranger with river-grey eyes shall ask the debtor to draw what has never been drawn, and the ink shall move of its own accord, and the debtor shall know wonder, and wonder shall be the hook by which the debtor is landed.*

There it was. *River-grey eyes.* Felix's eyes, exactly, the grey of rain deciding whether to fall. *Draw what has never been drawn*—the very words Wren had spoken not twelve hours past, leaning over a tavern table while Felix's pen hovered and trembled. *The ink shall move.* Wren had watched it move. Had felt the paper warm beneath their palm like something waking.

But it was the next line that made the floor tilt.

*The debtor, whose soul was sold before their first breath, whose parents paid in flesh what they could not pay in coin—*

Wren read the name.

Read it again.

It was their own name. Not Felix's. Theirs. Spelled out in century-old ink, letter by patient letter, sitting on the page like a bird that had always lived there and had only just now let itself be seen.

"No," Wren said aloud, and the dust motes flinched. "No, no, that's—that's a trick, that's a—"

But the letters didn't rearrange. The ink didn't blur. The name sat there, small and black and absolute, and beneath it the prophecy went on in its dry, unhurried voice:

*The debtor shall be seen at last, and being seen, shall be unmade or made whole, according to the choice of another. For the debt comes due when the last fire gutters, and the fire gutters now, and the one who holds the pen is the one who holds the price.*

Wren's hands were shaking so badly the pages rattled like teeth.

They did the only thing that made sense. They stole the book.

* * *

Felix's rooms above the charm-shop were exactly what Wren expected: small, immaculate, and arranged like a person trying to take up as little of the world as possible. Three cups, though Wren knew Felix lived alone. A bed made with corners sharp enough to draw blood. Maps pinned to every wall—but all of them maps of places that existed, safe places, mapped to death, mapped into obedience. Not one line of the impossible country they'd drawn together.

Felix opened the door already frowning, already checking the stair behind Wren for watchers, and Wren realized with a lurch that they'd never seen Felix afraid before tonight. Careful, yes. Invisible, absolutely. But fear was new, and it looked terrible on them, and Wren hated that they were the one bringing it.

"You're supposed to knock," Felix said. "You're supposed to be *normal* about this."

"Normal left." Wren set the book on Felix's table, opened it to the page, and stepped back as if it might bite. "Normal left about a hundred years ago."

He watched Felix read. Watched the exact moment the grey eyes reached the name—saw them stop, flick up to Wren's face, flick back down. Saw Felix's lips form the word silently, once, twice, like tasting something spoiled.

"That's you," Felix said. Flat. As if saying it plainly might make it less true.

"That's me." Wren's laugh came out cracked down the middle. "A hundred years before I was born. My name, Felix. In my grandmother's spelling, the one nobody uses, the one I haven't written since she—" They stopped. Pressed the heels of their hands against their eyes. "And it's your eyes. Your *words*, almost. *Ask the debtor to draw.* I said that. Last night I said nearly that, word for word, and I've never read this book, I couldn't have, it's been rotting behind a dead-bird dictionary since before my mother's mother drew breath."

Felix hadn't touched the page. Wren noticed that—noticed how Felix stood with their hands tucked under their arms, as though the book were a fire and they were determined not to warm themselves.

"Say the rest," Felix said quietly.

"What?"

"The part after my eyes. You stopped reading out loud. Say the rest."

Wren looked at the page. The words looked back, patient as stones.

"*The debtor,*" they recited, and their voice came out strange, borrowed, almost liturgical, "*whose soul was sold before their first breath, whose parents paid in flesh what they could not pay in coin.*"

Silence. The lamp hissed. Somewhere below, the night market was packing up its charms and its lies, and the whole city went on being Greyhollow, ignorant and loud, while two people stood over a dead man's handwriting and felt the ground open.

"Soul," Felix repeated. "Sold. Before your first breath."

"I don't feel sold." Wren heard how thin it sounded even as they said it. "I feel like me. I feel like—I don't know. Like a room where all the furniture is mine but there's one wall that doesn't match. There's always been a wall that doesn't match." They wrapped their arms around themselves. "Is that what that is? Is that the hole where something used to be?"

Felix finally touched the book—one fingertip, resting beside the name but not on it, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you didn't trust. "Prophecies are contraband for a reason," they murmured. "Not because they're false. Because they're *accurate*. The guild burns them because a city built on pretending nothing exists beyond the borders can't afford a book that says otherwise." Their finger traced the air above the final lines without landing. "*The debt comes due when the last fire gutters.* What fire? What debt? Who sold—"

They stopped.

Wren watched it happen: watched Felix arrive at the same sentence they themselves had been circling for hours, watched the color leave their face in stages, like a tide going out.

"*The one who holds the pen,*" Felix said. "That's me. I held the pen. I drew the map." Their voice dropped to almost nothing. "And the debtor is you. Which means whoever bought you—"

"—is coming to collect," Wren finished. "Or already has. Or has been waiting a hundred years with my name in a ledger, gathering interest."

The lamp guttered. Just a draft from the window, surely. Just a draft. But both of them looked at the flame anyway, and both of them saw it bend sideways as if listening, and neither of them said anything about it.

Wren sank onto the edge of Felix's too-neat bed, and the neatness of it suddenly broke their heart—the three cups, the sharp corners, the whole architecture of a person who had spent twenty years making sure no ledger anywhere contained their name. "You erase yourself," Wren said. "That's what you do. Brannoc told me once—you're a ghost with excellent penmanship. And now some book older than the guild has written us both down, and I can't—" Their breath hitched. "Do you know what it's like, spending your whole life making sure nobody sees you, and then finding out you were *seen* before you ever existed? That there was never any hiding? That the hiding was just—set dressing?"

Felix sat down beside them. Not touching. But close enough that Wren could feel the warmth of them, which was, tonight, worth more than touch.

"I know what it's like to find out your whole life was a transaction," Felix said. "If that helps."

"It doesn't."

"No," Felix agreed softly. "It doesn't. But we're in the same ledger now, you and I. Whatever bought you—it knows my name too. Or will." They looked at the book, and Wren saw something kindle in the grey eyes that hadn't been there before: not fear, or not only fear. Anger, banked and bright, like coals under ash. "Nobody writes me into their story without asking."

The lamp guttered again. This time it didn't recover. The flame shrank to a blue bead, swayed—and steadied, burning low and strange, the color of dragon-fire in none of the pictures Wren had ever been allowed to see.

On the table, the prophecy book turned its own page.

Neither of them moved. The paper whispered, settled, lay open to a fresh leaf of cramped, urgent script, and at the top of it, in ink that gleamed wet as a wound, were three words:

*THE FIRE GUTTERS.*

Felix stood slowly. "Tomorrow," they said, in a voice Wren had never heard them use—flat, decisive, the voice of someone opening a door they can never close again, "we go through my mother's things."

"Why?"

"Because if your soul was sold," Felix said, "someone in my family did the selling. And I want to know the price."

Outside, far beneath the city, so deep that no seismologist or priest or smuggler would think to check, something ancient shifted in its sleep—and the mountain, very softly, exhaled.
