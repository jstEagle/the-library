## 3. What the Parents Paid

The seal on my mother's effects had not been broken in twenty years, and it fought me like a living thing.

I knelt on the floor of the storage room above Brannoc's warehouse, lamplight guttering in its cheap glass housing, and worked my thumbnail along the wax that bound the cedar chest shut. The wax was black, shot through with threads of silver that caught the light and refused to let go of it. Official seals in Greyhollow were red. This was something older. Something that had been pressed into place by hands that knew what they were doing, and knew, too, that someday someone would come to break it.

My mother's hands, I thought. And then: no. Not hers. She would have used her teeth before she sealed anything this well.

The night outside was doing what Greyhollow nights did—pressing against every crack in the walls, smelling of river rot and frying onions and the faint copper tang of contraband magic leaking from a hundred hidden hearths. Somewhere below me, Brannoc's men were loading a barge with smuggled salt and unsmuggled prayers. Somewhere across the city, Wren was sitting in my rented room, guarding a book of prophecies that had spoken their name a century before they were born.

And I was here, alone, about to open the box my parents had died rather than let anyone open.

*Debtor*, the prophecy had called me. I had laughed when Wren first read it aloud, a laugh with no floor under it. Debtor. I had spent twenty years making myself unownable—no name in the census rolls, no face on any guild ledger, no signature on anything but false ones. I had erased myself so thoroughly that half the city's clerks believed Felix Crane was a ghost story told to frighten tax assessors. And all that time, apparently, there had been a debt with my name on it, accruing interest in the dark beneath the mountain.

You cannot outrun a creditor you don't know exists. That was the lesson. That was always the lesson in Greyhollow.

The wax gave with a sound like a sigh, or a small bone cracking.

Inside, the chest smelled of lavender gone to dust and something else—something mineral and cold, like the air off a glacier, or the breath of a cave that has never seen the sun. My mother's things lay folded with a precision she had never once shown in life: two dresses wrapped in oilcloth, a bundle of letters tied with string, a wooden bird I remembered from the windowsill of our house in the Ashfall Quarter, before the Ashfall Quarter stopped existing. And at the bottom, wrapped in a square of silk so fine it seemed embarrassed to be touching everything else—

A document.

I lifted it out with both hands, the way you lift a sleeping animal, or an accusation.

It was heavy paper, the color of old cream, and it was covered in writing that shifted when I looked at it directly. Not ink, exactly. The letters were dark as dried blood but they moved, slowly, the way worms move under a lifted stone—rearranging themselves, settling, watching me back. Around the border ran a script in a language I didn't know, though some animal part of me flinched from it anyway, the way a hand flinches from a stove it has never touched.

At the top, in letters that held still just long enough to be read:

**CONTRACT OF PASSAGE AND SURETY**

Below that, in smaller print—a hundred lines of smaller print, because there is always smaller print, and my whole life I had made my living in the spaces other people's small print left behind—I found the names.

My father's name. My mother's name. Written in full, which startled me; I hadn't seen them written anywhere since the funerals, since the state burned the records of everyone who fled the burning realms. And beneath theirs, in a space set apart, marked with a small symbol like a key turned sideways:

*The child, Felix. Born under the waning moon. Given in surety.*

Given.

I read the word four times. It did not improve with rereading.

* * *

Here is what I knew of my parents' escape, the story I was raised on, the story I had told myself in the dark for twenty years: They came out of Vessarin—the eastern realm, the one that burned—carrying nothing but me and each other. They crossed the Salt Waste on foot. They bribed, begged, and stole their way into Greyhollow, where magic was contraband and heretics were rendered down for lamp oil, and they built a quiet life out of silence and hard work until the fever took them both inside a single winter.

That was the story. Clean as a map with the dangerous country left blank.

Here is what the contract said:

They had not crossed the Salt Waste on foot. They had gone *down*. When the fires of Vessarin were at their backs and the roads were closed and the ports were watched, someone—my father's handwriting appears nowhere; the signature is my mother's alone, and it shakes—someone told them about the stair. The stair that isn't there. The way through the roots of the mountain, through the market of traded things, down to the court that has sat beneath Greyhollow since before Greyhollow had a name.

And the court does not grant passage for free. Nothing is free down there. Especially not passage upward, toward the light, carrying a child.

*In exchange for safe conduct through the Underways,* the contract read, *the undersigned cedes all claim to the soul of the aforementioned child, to be held in trust by the Undercourt until such time as the Seal-Fire permits collection.*

Until such time as the Seal-Fire permits collection.

I knew those words. Everyone in Greyhollow knew those words, or thought they did. The dragon-fire. The last of it in the world, coiled somewhere in the deep places, burning without fuel, and the old stories said it kept the Undercourt sealed—kept the things down there *down there*, kept the debts asleep, kept the mountain's heart shut like a fist. Cartographers weren't hunted for drawing unmapped realms, not really. We were hunted because the maps led down. Because somewhere in every honest atlas there was a blank space under the mountains, and blank spaces are doors, and doors are how the debt collectors get in.

My mother had signed it. Her signature shook, but she had signed it.

I sat back on my heels in the cold storage room and felt something in my chest rearrange itself—not breaking, exactly. Settling. The way a house settles after a storm, finding which walls will hold and which will have to come down.

Twenty years I had spent erasing myself, and I had thought I was hiding from the cartographers' guild, from the magistrates above, from the gray-eyed men who burned atlases in the public squares. All that careful work. All those false names. And the whole time, the truest record of me had been sitting in a box, in black wax and shifting ink, filed in the one archive no clerk could reach.

They hadn't sold me because they were cruel. That was the thing that kept arriving, unwelcome, patient as the tide. They sold me because the alternative was watching me burn with Vessarin, and love does arithmetic like that—love stands in a burning doorway and signs whatever is put in front of it. I could almost forgive them. I could feel the forgiveness trying to assemble itself somewhere in me, brick by stubborn brick.

But forgiveness is not the same as absolution, and the debt was still mine. The ink still moved. The court still counted me among its holdings, the way a miser counts coins he has never once looked at—until the day the vault door swings open.

And the vault door was swinging.

* * *

I took the contract to Wren.

I don't know why I didn't read the rest alone. I tell myself it was caution—that documents like this should only be handled with witnesses, that a second pair of eyes might catch what grief made me blind to. But that's a lie with good posture. The truth is I could not turn the next page by myself. Some things you need a hand to hold while your hands do the work.

Wren was waiting in my rented room with the prophecy book open on the table, a candle burning low between them and the window. They looked up when I came in, and whatever my face was doing made them stand.

"It's real," I said. "The debt. It's real, it's signed, and it's mine." I laid the contract on the table beside the prophecy book, and I swear on every false name I've ever worn—the two books leaned toward each other. Gently, the way plants lean toward light. The candle flame bent between them like it couldn't decide which to warm.

Wren went very still. "Do that again."

"I'd rather not."

"Felix." Their voice had gone quiet in the way it did when the wonder in them outweighed the fear, and I hated how much I loved that voice. "Put your hand on both of them."

I did. The contract's shifting ink stilled. The prophecy book's pages fluttered once, twice, and fell open—not to Wren's page. To a page further in, one we hadn't reached yet, and the old browned text there read:

*When the last fire gutters, the debtor shall descend, and the price of passage shall be asked anew. What was given may be taken. What was taken may be given. Choose, little coin, for the scale tips whether you will it or no.*

"Choose," Wren repeated softly. "Choose what?"

I looked down at the contract, at the clause I had not been able to read alone, the one near the bottom, in print so fine it was nearly a whisper:

*Should the Seal-Fire fail before collection, all debts held by the Undercourt shall come due at once, and the holder of this instrument may claim in full: the soul herein pledged, together with the standing, power, and dominion appertaining thereto within the Court Beneath.*

Appertaining. Dominion. Standing. Words like furniture in a house I'd never wanted to own.

"The fire's dying," I said. "You've felt it too—the tremors, the dreams, the way the charms in the night market have been burning brighter than they should. Everyone feels it and calls it weather. It's not weather. The seal is failing, and when it fails—" I tapped the clause. "Everything comes due. Every soul ever pledged. Including mine. And whoever holds the contract gets to choose what happens next."

"Who holds it?" Wren asked. "Who holds yours?"

I looked at the bottom of the page, at the countersignature I had skipped past three times because my eyes kept sliding off it the way eyes slide off a stranger's face in a crowd.

The Magistrate of the Undercourt. His mark was a circle bisected by a line, drawn in something darker than ink, and beneath it, in his own hand, a single line of notes—casual, almost friendly, the note a banker leaves on a late payment:

*Held in good faith. The fire fades. We will speak soon.*

We will speak soon.

I stood in my rented room with my whole life spread out on a table between a book of prophecies and a bill of sale, and I felt the betrayal finish its slow work and harden into something with edges. Not rage—rage burns off. This was colder. This was the resolve of a woman who has just learned the exact dimensions of her cage and has already begun measuring the door.

"They gave me away to save me," I said. "Fine. I can carry that. But I am not going *down* that stair as merchandise. If the court wants to talk, it can talk to me as a creditor. I'm owed too—twenty years of a life lived looking over my shoulder. Twenty years of being no one, on their account."

Wren's hand found mine across the table, over the moving ink, and the ink went still beneath our joined fingers as if even the contract understood witnesses.

"Then we need help," Wren said. "People who know the way down. People who owe you."

I thought of Brannoc, who owed me his life and hated owing anyone anything. Of Sister Ivetta, exiled for remembering rites the church had ordered forgotten—rites of binding, rites of unbinding. Of a pickpocket child named Quill who could read dead languages the way other children read street signs.

"I know exactly who owes me," I said, and blew out the candle, because in Greyhollow you plan in the dark, where the ledgers can't see you.

Outside the window, far beneath the city, so deep that most people would have sworn they imagined it, something ancient exhaled—and the tremor that shivered up through the floorboards felt, for the first time, less like a dying fire than like something clearing its throat.
