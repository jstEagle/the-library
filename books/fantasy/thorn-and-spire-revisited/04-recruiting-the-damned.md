## 4. Recruiting the Damned

*Felix*

Brannoc kept his boat in a flooded cistern beneath the Tanner's Ward, where the water ran black and slow and smelled of everything the city wanted to forget. You reached it by a door that wasn't a door—a seam in a warehouse wall that only opened for people who knocked in the rhythm of a smuggler's shanty, three quick and two slow, like a heart deciding to keep beating.

I had taught him that knock. I had also, four years ago, pulled him out of the Sump with a hook and a rope and no small amount of swearing, after the harbor watch put a crossbow bolt through his lung and left him to drown among the eel traps. He owed me. In Greyhollow, owing was the only currency that never devalued.

He opened the seam before I finished knocking, which meant he'd been watching the alley since dusk. Brannoc was a head taller than me and built like something the sea had tried twice to keep and failed, all scarred knuckles and a beard gone iron at the edges. His eyes went from my face to Wren's to the satchel under my arm, and his expression did the thing it always did when he was calculating how much trouble I was about to be.

"Crane." He stepped aside. "You've got your walking-into-doom face on."

"This is just my face."

"No," he said gravely, "your doom face has more apology in it."

The cistern was lit by lanterns hung on rusted chains, their light lying green-gold on the black water. His boat sat low in it, loaded with crates of contraband candlewax—ordinary enough—but I could see the second cargo lashed beneath the tarp: salted meat, rope, lamp oil. Enough for a long trip down, not up.

"You heard about the tremors," I said.

"Whole city's heard. My rats won't go below the third level anymore. Rats know things." He crossed his arms. "So. You're going under the mountain."

It wasn't a question. Word traveled fast in the dark places; I should have known my mother's sealed strongbox wouldn't stay secret longer than a day once I'd cracked it.

"I need you to get us there alive."

"And back?"

I didn't answer fast enough, and he saw it. Wren, standing close enough that our shoulders touched, said quietly, "We're going to get Felix's soul back. From the Undercourt."

Brannoc went very still. Smugglers are not brave men—they're careful men who have learned exactly when care stops working—and I watched him weigh it, the way he weighed every job, against the debt he carried and the life he'd built out of not being noticed.

Then he laughed, short and rough, and spat over the side into the water.

"The Undercourt. Right. Well." He looked at me, and there was something old and tired and fond in his face. "You fished me out when I was worth nothing to anybody. Reckon I can row you into hell and back." He held up one scarred finger. "But we do it my way below the third level. Nobody touches the merchandise in the memory market. And if any of you starts hearing your dead mother calling your name from a side tunnel—" his eyes flicked to me, apologetic, "—you let it call. You don't answer. That's how they stock the shelves."

Wren's hand found mine in the dark. Cold fingers. Warm grip.

"We'll need a priest," I said. "Someone who remembers the old rites. The kind that were banned before either of us was born."

Brannoc grimaced. "There's one woman. Ashfall district. They burned her chapel and she rebuilt it out of the ashes, which tells you most of what you need to know about Sister Ivetta."

* * *

*Ivetta*

They came to her at the hour when the ash-sifters worked the ruins of the Chapel of the Unwritten, raking the grey drifts for whatever might still burn. Sister Ivetta had learned to pray to the sound of rakes. It was, she had decided long ago, a more honest accompaniment than bells.

She knew Felix Crane by reputation only—the charm-seller from the night market whose wares actually worked, which in this city was either a miracle or a death sentence, depending on the week. She knew Wren not at all, but she watched the way the young man stood half a step behind Felix, watching them the way sailors watch weather, and she thought: *there is a man who has read his own ending and come looking for an edit.*

They laid the book on her table. The prophecy. A century old, its vellum soft as ear skin, and there in faded ink the words Wren had spoken last night verbatim, and beneath them a name that made Ivetta's old heart clench: *the debtor.*

"You want the Rite of Unbinding," she said. Not a question either. Everyone was asking questions tonight; someone had to answer them.

"If it still exists," Felix said.

"It exists." She turned her hands palm-up, scarred from the fire that had taken the chapel and, everyone assumed, her faith. It had taken neither. "That's the tragedy of exile, child. They burned the churches but the rites live in here—" she tapped her temple, "—where no magistrate's writ reaches. I remember the words. I remember the seals. I remember what must be given up." Her eyes went to Felix, and she softened, because she had spent forty years learning to see the shape of a wound before anyone showed it to her. "You already know, don't you. What it will cost."

Felix's jaw tightened. "I know what it costs if we fail."

"That's not the same thing, but it will do for now." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took down her traveling cloak from behind the ruined altar. The ash-sifters paused to watch her go. One of them, a boy of maybe ten, crossed himself with the old sign—the one that traced a flame instead of a cross—and she returned it without breaking stride.

At the door, she stopped them both. "One more thing. If you're going beneath the mountain, you'll need someone who can read what's written down there. The Undercourt keeps its ledgers in tongues that died before Greyhollow had a name. Contracts written in Old Vessic. Clauses in Ash-Tongue." She smiled, thin as paper. "And I'm too old to crawl through the memory market. But I know a child who isn't."

"A child?" Wren asked.

"A pickpocket," Ivetta said fondly, "who reads dead languages the way other children eat stolen plums. Come along. She owes me a sermon anyway."

*Quill*

Quill had been watching the fancy folk since they entered Ragpicker's Square, because that was her job, but she'd been watching the old nun harder, because the nun had once caught Quill lifting her purse and, instead of turning her in, had made her sit through four hours of scripture in a language nobody living spoke—and then paid her for it.

Nobody paid you for anything in Ragpicker's Square. That was the whole point of the place.

So when the nun came toward her corner with the two strangers trailing behind, Quill slid off her wall and waited, arms crossed, doing her best impression of a person who was not about to be offered something dangerous. She'd gotten good at that impression. It was how she'd survived eight years on streets that ate softer children whole.

"Sister." She bobbed her head, respectful-ish.

"Little Quill." The nun's voice was dry as her namesake. "How would you like to earn a month of bread?"

"Depends who I have to rob."

"Nobody. Read."

Quill frowned. Reading jobs were rare and weird. Rich collectors sometimes hired her to look at old things and say whether they were real, because apparently being able to read Ash-Tongue and Old Vessic and the trade-script of the drowned kingdoms made her valuable instead of strange. Her mother had called it a curse before the fever took her. The nun had called it a gift. Quill figured it was probably both, like most things.

The tall man—the one with the sad mapmaker eyes—held out a folded paper. "Can you read this?"

She unfolded it. It was a contract page, torn from something bigger, the ink brownish and mean-looking. She read the header and felt the hair stand up on her arms, because the header was in formal Old Vessic and it said:

*Deed of Soul, One (1), Infant Female, Crane.*

She looked up. The mapmaker was watching her with a face like a locked door, and the man beside them had gone pale, and the nun had gone very quiet.

"Yeah," Quill said, and her voice came out smaller than she wanted. "I can read it." She swallowed. "It says the soul belongs to the court under the mountain. It says—" she squinted, because the next part was in a cramped legal hand, clauses nested inside clauses like worms in a rotten apple, "—it says the debt comes due when the fire dies. And there's a word here I've only seen once before, in a book the Collector burned. It means *forever*, but it means it wrong. Like forever as a punishment instead of a promise."

Silence. Somewhere across the square, a rag-and-bone man rang his bell, and the sound fell into the hush like a stone into a well.

"How much do you know about what happens to children who go down there?" Quill asked, because she was a professional and professionals negotiated first.

"Nothing good," the mapmaker said honestly.

"Right." She chewed her lip. Then: "Bread for a month. New boots. And when this is done—if it's done—you teach me to draw maps. Real ones. Of places that aren't supposed to exist."

The mapmaker blinked. "Why maps?"

"Because maps are how you prove a place is real," Quill said, "and everybody in this square has been told their whole lives that they aren't."

The mapmaker looked at her for a long moment. Something moved behind their eyes—not pity, which Quill hated, but recognition, which was worse and better.

"Deal," they said.

* * *

*Felix*

We gathered that night in Brannoc's cistern, because it was the only room in Greyhollow where five people could speak the word *Undercourt* aloud without the walls growing ears. Lantern light on black water. The smell of wax and river-mud and Ivetta's incense, which she'd brought despite everything, because she said a crew that didn't smell like prayer was just a mob with weapons.

A smuggler who owed me his life. A priestess who remembered what the city wanted burned. A child who could read the language of my own damnation. And Wren, whose name was written in a book a hundred years old, standing beside me with our fingers hooked together like a knot that refused to slip.

I looked around at them—all of them, these damaged, stubborn, impossible people who had no reason under heaven to follow me underground—and felt something crack open in my chest that twenty years of careful invisibility had kept sealed.

Family, I thought. Not the kind you're born to. The kind you build, plank by plank, in the dark.

"All right," I said. "Here's how we get in."

Brannoc unrolled a chart of the mountain's roots—smuggler's cartography, all guesswork and rumor—and Ivetta began laying out the Rite of Unbinding in spilled salt on the deck, and Quill sat cross-legged with the contract page, reading clauses aloud in a dead tongue while the lanterns swayed.

And beneath us, far below the waterline, so deep the sound was less a noise than a pressure, the mountain exhaled again.

Longer, this time. Closer.

Like something counting down.

Like something saying *come.*
