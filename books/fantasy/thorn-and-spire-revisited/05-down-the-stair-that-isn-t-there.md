## 5. Down the Stair That Isn't There

Brannoc had smuggled a great many things down into the mountain's roots in his forty-odd years: salt-cured wyrm eggs, unlicensed prayers, a bishop once, though that had been an accident of tide and tide's worth of coin. He knew every stair that wasn't there the way other men knew their own hands.

But he'd never carried four people who mattered.

"Stay behind my left shoulder," he said, pausing at the seam in the cellar wall where the mortar didn't match. "The stair only exists if you believe it does, and it takes offense at hesitation."

"It *takes offense*," Quill repeated, delighted, her small fingers already twitching toward his coat pocket. He caught her wrist without looking. She grinned up at him, gap-toothed and unrepentant.

"The mountain is old and vain," Brannoc said. "Treat it like a rich man's wife."

Sister Ivetta pressed her palm flat against the mismatched mortar and murmured something in a language that predated Greyhollow's founding. The wall exhaled—a long, mineral sigh—and swung inward on darkness that smelled of cold iron and deep water.

"And you're sure this leads to the Undercourt?" Wren asked. They stood close to Felix, close enough that their shoulders touched, and Brannoc noticed how Felix leaned into it like a man leaning into warmth after years in the cold.

"I'm sure it leads *down*," Brannoc said. "Everything else is negotiation."

He went first. He always went first. It was the one honest thing about him.

* * *

The stair was not carved. That was the first thing he always forgot and relearned with each descent: the steps were grown, ribbed like the inside of some vast throat, slick with a moisture that was not quite water. Their lantern-light bent strangely here, pooling around their feet instead of reaching ahead, as if the dark were drinking it.

They walked for what felt like hours. The stair spiraled. Somewhere above them, muffled through leagues of stone, Greyhollow's bells rang none, which was impossible—Greyhollow's bells never stopped ringing; the city measured its guilt by them.

"We've passed out of time's parish," Ivetta said quietly, when Quill pointed it out. "Mind your prayers. They'll be heard down here by things that answer."

Felix walked with their hood up and their eyes down, and Brannoc watched them do it—the old habit, the erasure. Twenty years Felix had spent unpainting themselves from every ledger in the city, and now they were walking straight down into the one ledger no one escapes. There was a grim comedy in it. Brannoc didn't laugh. He owed Felix his life twice over—once from the wreck of the *Salt Vow*, once from the fever after—and debts were the only scripture he kept.

The stair ended at a threshold of black glass, polished smooth by centuries of feet, and beyond it the market opened.

* * *

Brannoc had seen it before, twenty years ago, when he'd come down to buy back three years of his own life he'd traded for passage out of the Sable Straits. He'd found the years, all right—three glass vials on a shelf, each one swirling with a different season of him, priced higher than he could pay. He'd settled for buying back one spring and leaving the rest, and he'd spent two decades wondering what he'd done with those missing summers.

It hadn't gotten easier to look at.

The market filled a cavern so vast the ceiling was a rumor. Stalls of black wood and bone stretched away in crooked avenues, lit by lamps that burned without flame—just a soft, hungry glow, like light being digested. And the wares—

Names, first of all. Whole stalls of them, written on slips of vellum, pinned like moths. Brannoc heard a woman ahead of them haggling over a name called *Marguerite*, testing it in her mouth, weighing whether it fit better than the nothing she currently answered to.

Memories next, stoppered in bottles that fogged and cleared with whatever was inside. A boy's first snowfall. A soldier's last order given. Someone's mother singing, sold three times over by the smell of it—one bottle had been opened for sampling and hummed faintly to itself.

And years. Years in jars, stacked like preserves. *Best before the body fails,* said one hand-lettered sign.

"Keep moving," Brannoc muttered. "Don't touch. Don't taste. Don't tell anyone your true name, and don't buy anything unless I say."

"What about selling?" Quill asked, eyes shining.

"*Especially* not selling."

The vendors watched them pass. Some had faces. Some had faces that had been purchased secondhand and didn't sit right. One creature wrapped entirely in gray silk turned its headless hood to follow Felix specifically, and Brannoc put his hand on the knife at his hip until it looked away.

"They know why we've come," Ivetta murmured.

"Then let them watch," Felix said, and their voice came out flat and steady, and Brannoc thought: *there it is.* Twenty years of being invisible, and underneath it all, steel.

* * *

It was Wren who stopped first. Brannoc heard the sharp intake of breath and turned to find them frozen before a stall of bottled voices—no, not voices. *Conversations.* Each bottle held two threads of light twined together, and one of them, near the front, flickered with a rhythm Brannoc recognized because he'd been hearing it for days: Wren's laugh, twined with Felix's, from the night market, the night they met. Priced at six coppers.

"That's—" Wren started.

"Don't," Brannoc said gently. "First rule of the market: everything here is real, and nothing here is yours. If you start claiming things, the market starts claiming back."

But Felix had gone still in a different way. Brannoc followed their gaze down an aisle marked with a sign in dead script—Quill translated under her breath, "*Childhoods, assorted*"—and saw Felix walking toward it like a sleepwalker, like a man walking toward his own grave to check on the flowers.

The stall was modest. Dusty. Shelves of shallow boxes, each labeled in a clerk's careful hand. And third shelf from the top, between a childhood full of fear (priced high) and a childhood full of boredom (priced low), sat a box labeled:

*CRANE, FELIX — b. Greyhollow, Lower Terraces. Contents: one (1) childhood, ages 0–9. Condition: surrendered in full by sellers. Provenance: payment rendered.*

*Sellers.*

Not *seller*. Plural.

Felix lifted the box with both hands, the way you lift something you already know is heavier than it looks. Inside, folded in tissue paper, lay small things: a wooden bird with a chipped wing. A drawing of a house with too many windows. The smell rose out of the box—bread, rain on hot stone, someone's lullaby sung off-key—and Brannoc watched Felix's face do something he had never seen it do in all the years he'd known them.

It came apart.

"They sold all of it," Felix whispered. Not to anyone. To the box. "Every year. Every—there's nothing left. This is everything I was before they ran, and they *priced* it." Their thumb moved over the label. "*Payment rendered.* I was payment. I wasn't even—I was *change* they got back."

Wren's hand closed over theirs. Quill, for once, said nothing, and stood very close, and glared at the market with the ferocity only a child can muster at injustice.

Ivetta bowed her head. "This is how the court feeds," she said softly. "Not on souls whole—that's the Magistrate's privilege alone. On the pieces. The parents pay with the child's years, and the child grows up hollow and calls it caution. Calls it privacy. Calls it *being careful*." She looked at Felix with terrible kindness. "You did not learn to erase yourself, child. You were erased. You simply became expert at the shape they left."

Brannoc had carried men out of burning ships. He had held them while they screamed for people already drowned. He knew what to do with that. But this—this quiet, dust-and-tissue-paper grief—he had nothing for. So he did the only thing he could.

He took the box from Felix's hands, tucked it inside his coat, over his heart, and said, "We're taking it with us. Consider it salvage. Smuggler's privilege."

"You can't steal from the market," Ivetta began.

"Watch me," Brannoc said. "I've never once paid full price in my life."

Something in the dark laughed—not cruelly, but the way a merchant laughs when a mark amuses him. All down the avenue, the hungry lamps brightened a fraction, and the crowd of vendors seemed to lean in, and the temperature dropped as though the mountain itself had drawn breath to speak.

At the far end of the market, past the stalls of names and years and childhoods, a gate of dragon-bone stood half-sunk in the rock, and before it, waiting with the patience of something that had already collected its fee, stood a herald in robes the color of extinguished coals.

"The Court Beneath," the herald announced, in a voice like paper tearing, "receives the debtor."

Beside Felix, Wren went white as prophecy paper.

Brannoc's hand found his knife again, and he stepped forward, because he always went first.
