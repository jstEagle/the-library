## 6. The Court Beneath

The stair ended where no stair should end: in a doorway of black glass, tall as a cathedral door, set into living rock that breathed.

Wren felt the breathing before they saw it. The mountain inhaled, slow and vast, and the air that rushed past them smelled of cold iron and old honey and something else—something like a library left closed for a hundred years. Dust and paper and power going quietly to rot.

"Everyone breathe," Brannoc said behind them, which was absurd, because none of them were breathing. His hand rested on the hilt of a knife he had sworn three times not to draw. "And everyone remember: we're guests. Terrible, doomed guests, but guests."

Quill peered up at him from beneath her too-large hood. "Guests get fed. Do you think they'll feed us?"

"Quill." Ivetta's voice was gentle and graveled, a riverbed voice. She had anointed each of them at the last landing with ash and oil, murmuring rites so old they predated the city's ban on magic by a thousand years. "Child, whatever is offered to you down here, do not eat it. Do not drink it. Do not say *thank you*."

"Why not thank you?"

"Because thanks is a contract," Wren said quietly. They hadn't meant to speak. They found, since reading their own name in a book older than their grandmother's grandmother, that words came out of them carefully now, weighed like coins. "Everything down here is a contract."

Felix stood at the front, one hand flat against the black glass. In the torchlight their face was unreadable—the face of someone who had spent twenty years practicing unreadability—but their fingers trembled against the surface, and Wren watched the glass fog where they touched it, then clear, then fog again, as though the mountain were tasting them through their skin.

"Felix," Wren said.

"I know." A breath. "I'm deciding whether to knock."

"You don't have to knock. It knows you're here. It's *always* known you're here."

Felix turned. In the dark, their eyes caught the light strangely, the way a cat's do, or a coin's. "That's what frightens me."

Then they pushed, and the door swung inward without a sound, and the Undercourt received them the way a spider receives a fly: beautifully, patiently, with every courtesy.

* * *

The court beneath the mountain was not what Wren had imagined, and they had imagined it constantly for six days—for six days they had dreamed of it, woken from dreams of it, found its name inked into the margins of their own handwriting without remembering writing it. They had imagined fire and screaming and chains. Instead there was velvet.

A hall stretched away from them longer than any street in Greyhollow, floored in carpet the color of dried wine, walled in stone carved with scenes Wren could not look at directly—figures giving things away, figures having things taken, always smiling, always smiling. Chandeliers hung from a ceiling lost in shadow, burning with a light that was warm but gave no heat, and beneath that light sat the court.

They sat at long tables on either side of the aisle like wedding guests, dozens of them, scores, dressed in fashions from every century Wren had ever studied and several they hadn't. Silks gone out of style when Greyhollow was a fishing village. Armor polished to mirrors. Robes stitched with symbols that made Quill make a small strangled sound and press her lips together, as if she'd swallowed a word. None of them moved. None of them blinked. Their eyes followed the five intruders down the aisle with the smooth, synchronized attention of a field of sunflowers turning toward the sun.

"They're not alive," Ivetta murmured. "Or not only alive. Look at the shadows—they cast two each. One belongs to them. One belongs to whoever they were before they sold themselves here."

Wren looked, and wished they hadn't. Every seated figure had a second shadow, thinner, wrong-angled, crouched at its owner's feet like a dog waiting to be fed.

At the far end of the hall rose a dais, and on the dais a chair of dragon-bone—Wren knew it was dragon-bone the way you know a face in a crowd, instantly and with dread—and in the chair sat a man.

He was unremarkable, which was the most frightening thing about him. Grey-haired, pleasant-faced, dressed in a magistrate's robes of dove grey, with the kind of face you'd trust with your keys or your children or your secrets. He was drinking tea. He set the cup down as they approached, and the sound of porcelain on porcelain rang through the hall like a bell through a coffin.

"The debtor arrives," he said, "with friends. How rare. How touching." He smiled, and his teeth were all exactly even. "Sit. Please. You've walked a long way down, and the mountain does hate to be hurried."

Chairs appeared. No one carried them; they simply were there, drawn up around a low table as if the court had been expecting tea for five for a century. Which, Wren thought, sitting because refusing seemed more dangerous than complying, perhaps it had.

Felix did not sit. Felix stood, arms crossed, in the posture of someone negotiating a lease, and Wren loved them so fiercely in that moment that it frightened them more than the court did.

"Magistrate," Felix said. "You have something of mine."

"Do I?" The Magistrate poured tea from a pot that steamed though the fire beneath it had been cold for years. "Let us consult the ledger." He opened a book beside him—a slim volume, bound in grey leather, and Wren's stomach dropped, because they knew that binding. It matched the prophecy book sitting in Felix's satchel. Same binder. Same age. Same hungry paper. "Ah. Yes. Here. *One soul, whole and unworn, sold by Vessa and Tomas Crane, in consideration of safe passage out of the realm of Ashfall for themselves and issue.* Signed—" he glanced up, delighted, "—well. Signed by you, technically. In advance. Your parents had a lovely hand, but yours is the signature that matters. Infants sign such honest signatures, don't you find? All loops and no lies."

"You're saying a baby signed this."

"I'm saying the contract doesn't care who held the pen." He closed the ledger gently, reverently, the way one closes a Bible. "But I am not a monster, Felix Crane. I am a magistrate. And magistrates believe above all things in fair dealing. So." He folded his hands. "Here is my offer."

* * *

The offer came wrapped in kindness, and that was its poison.

"You have spent twenty years erasing yourself," the Magistrate said, and his voice softened into something almost tender. "Twenty years of being no one. Unlisted. Unmapped. Unremembered by landlords and taxmen and neighbors. Do you know what I see, when I look at that life? I see a person paying interest on a debt they never chose, in a currency of loneliness. That is *injustice*, Felix Crane. True injustice. And I despise injustice."

Wren watched Felix's jaw tighten. Watched their hands, scarred from years of charm-work, curl slowly against their sides.

"So take it back," the Magistrate said. "Not stolen from me—*returned* to you, with all arrears paid. Twenty years of compound interest, Felix. Do you know what a soul accrues, kept whole and untouched in a vault beneath a mountain? Power. Not the gutter-magic of charms and smugglers' tricks. Real power. Enough to walk back into Greyhollow in daylight and be *seen*. Enough to unmake every law that made you hide. Enough to redraw the maps they burn people for drawing." He leaned forward, and his even teeth gleamed. "Reclaim your soul, and inherit my seat when I am done with it. The court would be yours. The ledgers would be yours. You could tear up every contract ever signed in this room—including, if you wished, your own."

Silence. The chandeliers burned. Somewhere far above, Wren thought they could feel Greyhollow trembling, faintly, like a sleeper dreaming of falling.

"And the cost," Felix said. Not a question. They were too careful for it to be a question.

"The cost." The Magistrate sighed, genuinely regretful, and turned his pleasant face to Wren, and Wren's blood went cold. "There is a darker clause in the prophecy your friend carries. Perhaps you've read ahead? Page forty-one. *When the debtor stands before the seat, the marked one shall open the way.*" He spread his hands apologetically. "Prophecies are terribly rigid things. If you reclaim your soul and take my seat, the way must still be opened. By the marked one. From the inside." He smiled at Wren with real warmth. "You'd be joining the court, young Wren. Permanently. As our honored guest. Forever is not so long, down here. The tea is excellent."

"No," Felix said.

"You haven't heard the alternative."

"I don't need to."

"You do," the Magistrate said gently, "because the alternative is the same clause, with nothing purchased in exchange. Refuse my offer, leave empty-handed, and the prophecy fulfills itself anyway—the fire gutters, the debts come due, and the marked one opens the way regardless. The only question, dear Felix, is whether you get anything at all for Wren's fate. Justice or charity. Those are your options. I've been very generous with both."

Wren sat very still. Down the length of the hall, a hundred second-shadows stirred at their owners' feet, and a hundred pairs of borrowed eyes turned toward them with something horribly like sympathy.

Felix looked at Wren. Just looked. And everything unsaid between them—six days of it, the moving ink, the map of somewhere neither had been, the way Felix had said *you're the first person who's ever seen me* with their forehead pressed to Wren's shoulder—all of it passed across their face in a single naked moment before they buried it again.

"Give us a night," Felix said. "To decide."

The Magistrate considered. Sipped his tea. "One night. The guest chambers are prepared—they have been prepared for a hundred years, actually; I do hope the linens aren't stale." He rose, and the entire court rose with him, a rustle of silk and steel and old obedience. "Do think carefully, Felix Crane. I am offering you justice. No one above has ever offered you that. It would be a shame—to waste it on love."

As they were escorted down a side corridor, Quill tugged Wren's sleeve and whispered, barely audible:

"He never asked how we got past the stair. He never asked about Sister Ivetta's rites. He already knows everything." Her eyes were huge in her small face. "Wren—he *wants* us to refuse. I read page forty-one twice. There's a third option written in the margin. Someone wrote it tiny, in the margin, a hundred years ago."

"What does it say?"

Quill's mouth went white around the words.

"It says: *the fire can be fed by a willing hand.*"
