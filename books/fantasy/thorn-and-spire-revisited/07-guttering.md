## 7. Guttering

Sister Ivetta had knelt before dying fires before. She had held the hands of the plague-struck in Vellamark, had sung the last rites over a cathedral as it burned with everyone she loved inside it, and she had learned this much: a fire that is ending does not go quietly. It argues. It flares and gutters, flares and gutters, spending its final strength in bursts of furious light, the way a dying man grips your wrist too hard.

The dragon-fire beneath the mountain argued like a sermon.

She stood at the edge of the Seal-Chamber, where the stair of smuggled stone let out into a cavern vast enough to swallow Greyhollow whole, and she watched the flame that was not a flame—or not only a flame. It rose from a basin of black iron wide as a millpond, and it burned in colors fire had no business wearing: gold shot through with veins of green, then blue as deep as a winter dusk, then a red so old it seemed less a color than a memory of color. The heat came off it in slow tides, pressing against her face like a hand, and every tide carried a sound underneath it—a low, rhythmic thrum, patient and enormous, like the heartbeat of something sleeping rather than something burning.

*That*, Ivetta thought, *is what we broke when we taught the world to fear dragons.* Not the beasts themselves. The beasts were only the keepers. This was what they kept: the first fire, the one lit before the realms were mapped, the one that burned debt clean instead of kindling it. Every covenant ever sworn beneath an open sky had been witnessed by a spark of this. Every oath. Every promise. And now it was down here in the dark, guttering, while the city above slept on, dreaming its tidy dreams of ledgers and law.

"It's worse than it was," Brannoc said behind her. He had gone quiet on the descent—quiet for Brannoc, which meant he still spoke, but only about distances and exits and how many guards patrolled the upper galleries. A smuggler's catechism. "Two days ago you could stand here without sweating through your shirt."

"Fire that is failing pulls its heat inward," Ivetta said. "Conserves itself." She did not take her eyes from the basin. "This isn't pulling inward. Look at the edges."

He looked. So did Wren, who stood close enough to Felix that their shoulders touched, and Quill, who had climbed onto a shelf of fallen masonry to see better, because children are wise about sightlines. At the rim of the black iron, where the flame met metal, the fire was *crawling*. Not dancing—dancing was alive, dancing was joy—but crawling, dragging itself up the inner wall of the basin and falling back, again and again, like something trying to climb out of a well.

"That's not dying," Quill said. Her voice was small in the cavern, but it carried; children's voices always do in big empty places. "That's starving."

Ivetta closed her eyes and let herself listen—not with her ears but with the old training, the discipline the Church had burned out of everyone else and exiled her for keeping. She let the fire's rhythm enter her the way a tide enters a sea cave, and she listened to what it was saying, because fire speaks, if you have the patience and the grief to hear it.

It said: *not enough. Not enough. Not enough.*

And underneath that, fainter, worn almost smooth by repetition: *taken. Taken. Taken.*

Her eyes opened. Her stomach had turned to river-stone.

"Felix," she said. "Come here. Tell me what you feel."

Felix came. Of course they came; twenty years of erasing themselves had made them a creature of careful motion, and even now, even here, they moved like someone crossing a frozen pond. They stopped at Ivetta's shoulder and stared into the basin, and Ivetta watched their face do what faces do when the body understands a thing before the mind consents to it: a small contraction, a flicker, the look of a person hearing their own name called from a great distance.

"It's pulling," Felix said slowly. "Not from me—I'm not—it's pulling from *near* it. From the room. Like a draft. But there's no draft."

"No," Ivetta said. "There's no draft. There's a mouth."

* * *

She made them withdraw to the gallery above the chamber, a smuggler's ledge behind a curtain of hanging root and mineral lace, because what she had to say was not for the ears of whatever patrolled these depths—and because she needed, selfishly, a moment not to be watched by the fire while she said it. She sat on the cold stone and put her back against the wall the way she used to sit in the cloister, and she folded her hands, and she was aware of how theatrical it must look, the old priestess arranging herself to preach. She did it anyway. Some habits are load-bearing.

"I served nine years in the Temple of the Open Flame," she began, "before they exiled me. Do you know what my heresy was? I asked where the offerings went."

Quill snorted. Brannoc shifted his weight. Wren and Felix were very still.

"The Church taught that the tithe of flame—every candle, every pyre, every burning offered in covenant—rose to heaven. Pretty story. But I kept the accounts, and accounts do not lie, and the numbers never balanced. Flame went in. Nothing came out. And the older the temple, the hungrier the dark beneath it grew." She looked at each of them in turn. "I thought I had found corruption. Embezzlement. Some bishop selling the people's devotion. It took me thirty years and one very long exile to understand it wasn't theft at all. It was *feeding*. There are things in the deep places of the world that cannot make fire. They can only eat it. And the oldest of them learned, long ago, that the most nourishing fire of all is not wood or oil or candle-wax."

Wren's voice came thin from the shadows. "Covenant."

"Covenant," Ivetta agreed. "Oaths. Debts. Promises. Fire fed on meaning burns hotter than any fuel, and it burns *forever*, because meaning renews itself every time it is remembered. The dragon-fire down there is the original hearth—the flame every promise was ever sworn beside. And it is guttering not because it is old, and not because it is failing." She heard her own voice harden, and welcomed it; anger was cleaner than the grief underneath. "It is guttering because it is being *drained*. Someone has been drinking from the first fire of the world, sip by sip, century by century, and building himself a court out of what he swallowed."

Felix said, quietly, "The Magistrate."

"The Magistrate." She nodded. "You felt him in the market of traded things—that whole place runs on his appetite. Memories, names, years, souls. Do you wonder why he deals in them? A man who eats ordinary food stays hungry. A man who eats *meaning* grows monstrous, and then he grows hungry for the largest meaning there is. And the largest meaning in this world, the fire everything else was lit from—" she gestured downward, at the floor, at the whole sleeping weight of the mountain, "—is down there. And he has been feeding on it since before your grandmother was born."

"But the seal—" Wren began. "The prophecy says the fire seals the court. If it dies, the debts come due. That's what everyone believes. That's what *you* believed."

"I believed what I was taught, and I was taught by liars, as were we all." Ivetta's hands tightened in her lap. "Think, child. Why would the first fire of the world be a prison? Who would build such a thing? No. The fire is not the lock on the cage. The fire is the *warden*—no. Worse. Better." She struggled for the true shape of it, the truth she had been circling for thirty years. "The fire is the creditor. Every soul sold to the Undercourt is a debt written against that flame. As long as it burns, the debts are *held*—watched, weighed, redeemable. When it dies, the ledger passes to whoever holds the court. To him. He hasn't been waiting for the fire to fail. He has been *eating toward* it. Each soul he consumes, each year he swallows, brings the flame lower, and each finger's width the flame falls makes him stronger. It is not a countdown running out. It is a meal nearly finished."

The silence that followed had weight. Ivetta felt it settle on all of them like snow.

Above them—far above, through half a mile of living rock—Greyhollow felt it too.

* * *

They felt it as a tremor first, a shiver in the wine in the cups of the night market, a rattle of shutters in the Salt Quarter. Then the lamps behaved strangely—burning low and colorless in streets where no wind moved, flames leaning all one direction, toward the mountain, like grass bending before a herd of invisible cattle. Old women who remembered their grandmothers' superstitions put salt in their doorways. The cartographers' guild, those pious destroyers of unmapped things, convened an emergency session and blamed seismic activity, aquifer collapse, anything with a name that fit in a ledger.

But in the Temple of the Open Flame—the rebuilt one, the respectable one, the one that had exiled her—Ivetta would have wagered her last relic that the high altar candle was doing exactly what the dragon-fire below was doing: crawling. Climbing its own wick. Starving in full view of a congregation trained not to look.

Here below, the signs were less deniable. As they crouched in the gallery, the tremors came in waves, and each wave matched the rhythm of the flame's thrumming—except the rhythm was *quickening*. The great slow heartbeat was racing, the way a heart races when the body knows it is losing blood.

"It's accelerating," Felix said. Their voice had lost its careful flatness; there was something raw under it. "Yesterday the pulses were—slow. Now—"

"Now he's near the end of the meal," Ivetta said. "A drinker at the bottom of the cup tilts it higher. The closer the flame comes to dying, the faster he draws, because the last of it is the strongest—the oldest covenants, the deepest vows. And when the last of it goes—" she made herself say it plainly, because plainly was all she had left to give, "—the debts come due, all of them, everywhere, at once. Every soul ever sold. Every name ever traded. And the holder of the ledger becomes, in law older than law, the owner of everything owed. He will own the dead and the unborn. He will own the *word* 'mine.'"

Quill, from her perch, asked the question with the terrible directness of children: "Can we feed it back?"

Ivetta looked up at her. "What?"

"If stuff was taken out," Quill said, picking at the mortar between stones, "can't you put it back? He ate it. It's got to still be *in* him. My gran says nothing's ever really gone, it's just somewhere you can't reach."

And Ivetta felt something move in her chest that she had not felt since the cathedral burned—an ember of the thing that had once been called hope, before exile had banked it down to ash and duty. Because the child was right. The child was *doctrinally* right, in a way no canon had ever managed to state. Stolen fire does not cease to exist. Swallowed centuries do not dissolve. Everything the Magistrate had drunk was still inside him, and a thing that holds everything stolen is, by the oldest arithmetic in the world—

"—a hoard," Brannoc breathed, catching it a half-second after she did. Smugglers know hoards. "He's not a judge. He's a *dragon*."

"No," Ivetta said, rising, and her knees cracked and she did not care, and her voice came out of her with thirty years of exile behind it and none of the caution that exile teaches. "Dragons guard fire. He only eats it. Which means he can be made to give it back—which means the flame is not dying, it is being *robbed*, and robbery, thank every forgotten saint, is a crime with a remedy."

Below them, through the root-curtain, the light in the Seal-Chamber surged—a great convulsive flare, gold and green and that ancient red—and in the flare's heart Ivetta saw, or dreamed she saw, a shape: vast, coiled, winged, pressed flat against the inside of the fire like a moth against glass. Watching them. Waiting.

The flame spoke, in the language of heat and tide, and for the first time in a hundred years it did not say *not enough*.

It said: *soon*.

Ivetta turned to the others, and the tremor ran through the mountain again, harder, rattling dust from the gallery ceiling, and far above them Greyhollow's bells began to ring—all of them, unstruck, all at once.

"We have until the fire dies," she said. "Which is sooner than anyone thinks. Come. We're going to introduce the Magistrate to his creditors."
