## 8. The Price of Taking It Back

The Magistrate's vault was not a room so much as a held breath.

They came to it through corridors that had forgotten they were corridors—walls of black stone veined with something that pulsed slow and amber, like light trapped in resin. The air tasted of old coins and colder promises. Brannoc went first with his smuggler's lantern hooded to a sliver, Quill pressed close at his heel, and Ivetta walked behind them all murmuring the names of saints who had been dead so long even the Undercourt had forgotten to fear them.

Felix walked in the middle, because Wren had put them there.

"Debtor walks in the center," Wren had said, with a crooked attempt at humor that didn't reach their eyes. "It's in the small print somewhere. I'd know. I've read enough of it."

Now, at the vault door—a slab of basalt carved with a ledger so vast its columns ran off the edges of the stone like rain down glass—Felix felt their own name pull at them from the other side. Not heard. *Felt.* The way a compass needle feels north. The way a scar feels weather coming.

Their soul was in there. Twenty years of it, sitting on a shelf in the dark, priced and patient.

"You don't have to touch it," Ivetta said quietly. She had stopped beside them, her exile's robes hanging loose around a frame gone thin as candle-wax. "Knowing where it is has always been enough for most people. Most people turn back here."

"I've spent twenty years being *most people*," Felix said. "I'm done."

The vault door had no handle. It had a scale, instead—a balance etched into the stone, one pan empty, one pan holding a groove shaped precisely like a hand. Felix understood without being told. Everything in this place was transaction. Everything had a price, and the price was always paid in kind.

They set their hand in the groove.

The stone drank their warmth like a drunkard drinks wine, and the door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, because sound, too, could be sold, and someone long ago had evidently bought this silence outright.

* * *

Inside, the vault was full of shelves.

Not gold. Not jewels. Nothing so crude. Shelves of glass and bone rising into a darkness the lantern couldn't reach, and on them, arranged with a librarian's terrible tenderness: everything anyone had ever traded away. A jar of laughter, still fizzing faintly against the glass. A ribbon tied around what could only be someone's courage—it moved when you looked away from it, restless as a caged bird. Vials of first snowfalls. Boxes of mothers' voices. An entire shelf of Sundays, each one labeled in a dead script that Quill read aloud in a whisper before Brannoc gently put a hand over the child's mouth.

"Don't," Brannoc murmured. "In here, naming a thing is halfway to buying it."

And there—third row, eye level, exactly where a person would be forced to look—was Felix.

A crystal no larger than a heart, resting on a cushion of grey velvet gone dusty with decades. Inside it, light. Not white light, not any color Felix had a word for, but the light of a specific afternoon in a burning realm twenty-two years gone: their mother's hands cupped around a flame, their father's voice saying *it's only until we're safe*, and then the light itself, scooped out of a child's chest like the yolk from an egg, poured into a contract, sealed, shelved.

Priced. There was a tag. Of course there was a tag. Someone had written an appraisal on it in ink that shimmered: *Soul, human, unclaimed, 22 yrs accrued interest. Note: debtor approaching maturity. Value appreciating.*

"They priced me," Felix said. Their voice came out very flat, which was how they knew they were close to screaming. "Like a barrel of wine. Like contraband."

"That's exactly what you are," said a voice from the shelves. "Contraband. The finest kind."

The Magistrate did not appear so much as *arrive*, the way debt arrives, the way winter arrives—suddenly everywhere at once. He wore a coat stitched from promissory notes, and his face was kind the way a trap is kind, padded and patient. Behind him, far down the vault's throat, the amber veins in the walls pulsed faster. Hungrier.

"You came all this way," he said warmly. "So let's not pretend you're here to negotiate. You're here to *take*. And oh—" he spread his hands, and every shelf in the vault seemed to lean toward Felix, "—what there is to take."

* * *

Later, Felix would try to describe what happened when they lifted the crystal from its velvet, and fail, because language was built by people who had never held their own soul in two hands.

It started as warmth. Then it became memory—not remembered but *relived*, all of it at once, every year the court had been accruing interest on their name. They were five and learning letters by firelight. They were nine and drawing maps in the dust because paper cost too much. They were twelve and invisible in the night market, nineteen and careful, twenty-five and lonely, and through all of it ran a current of power that had never once been theirs to feel—because it had been *here*, waiting, compounding, growing fat in the dark.

Twenty years of borrowed strength flooded home.

Felix gasped, and the gasp became light. It poured out through their skin, through their eyes, through the seams of the world. They could see the vault's every shelf in perfect detail. They could see through the mountain, through Greyhollow above, through the streets where they had spent two decades making themselves small—see every ward, every hidden thing, every unmapped corner of every realm, and know, with total and effortless certainty, that they could map it all. Name it all. *Own* it all.

They could see the Magistrate's true shape beneath his kindly coat: a knot of stolen centuries, thin as smoke and just as greedy.

And they could see, with equal clarity, exactly how to unravel him. One gesture. One flex of a power that was, after all, rightfully theirs. He had fed on the dragon-fire; they could feed on *him*. Take back every year he'd stolen, every soul he'd shelved, and become—

Become what?

The thought arrived wearing Felix's own voice, and it sounded so reasonable.

*You've been small your whole life. Erased. Careful. Invisible. And now you never have to be again. No more selling charms in alleys. No more flinching from cartographers' guild men. You could redraw the world. Who would dare hunt the person who holds the map?*

Who indeed.

The power sang. It was not evil—that was the trap of it, Felix realized dimly. It was simply *vast*, and vastness does not need to tempt you with wickedness. It tempts you with competence. With safety. With the end of fear. Every terrible thing they might do arrived pre-justified, wrapped in the warm logic of *never again will they make me hide.*

Somewhere far away, the dragon-fire guttered. Felix felt it flicker and thought, with a calm that should have frightened them: *I could hold the flame myself. I wouldn't need the fire. I would BE the fire.*

"Felix."

Small voice. Small hands.

Quill stood in front of them, and the light pouring off Felix was bending around the child like water around a stone, and where it touched, it burned. Quill's sleeve was smoking. Their eyes were streaming. They had walked straight into a conflagration of godhood to stand between Felix and the rest of the vault, and they were holding up both hands—not to shield themselves.

To reach for Felix's wrists.

"You're going all *shelvy*," Quill said, and their voice cracked in the middle. "Your edges. You're going all—I can see the shelves through you. Felix. *Felix.* You're doing the thing where you disappear, except now you'd take the whole sky with you."

"I'm not disappearing," Felix heard themselves say, from very far away. "I'm finally *here*. Can't you feel it?"

"Yeah." Quill's small fingers closed around Felix's wrist, and the burn must have been agony—the skin there was already blistering—but the child did not let go. "I feel it. It's like standing next to a really rich man. Everything he says sounds true and none of it is."

That landed. Somewhere under the flood, something that was still Felix—still the person who sold charms to people who couldn't afford luck, who drew maps for strangers because loneliness was worse than risk—felt the words go in like a splinter.

"He'll keep you safe," the power whispered. "Keep them all safe. Isn't that what family is for?"

"No," Quill said, and now they were crying, and pulling, hauling on Felix's arm with their whole slight weight, feet skidding on the vault floor. "Family's the ones who tell you when you're being a *bastard*. Brannoc says that. He says it about himself all the time. Felix—you're looking at us like we're *prices*. I can feel it. Stop looking at us like prices."

*Prices.*

The word went through Felix like a blade finding the seam of a seal.

Because that was what the Magistrate had done. That was the whole architecture of this place—everything loved reduced to a tag, an appraisal, a value appreciating. Their mother's desperation, priced. Their childhood, priced. And here they stood, twenty seconds into godhood, already pricing the people they loved: *useful, loyal, expendable if necessary.*

The same arithmetic. Their arithmetic now.

"No," Felix said aloud.

The power surged, wounded, persuasive: *Think of what you could—*

"*No.*"

They did not push the power away. That was the trick of it, the terrible trick, the one Ivetta had tried to warn them about in the corridor: you could not refuse a soul, only choose what it was *for*. So Felix chose. They took all that vastness—all that borrowed, compounded, interest-swollen glory—and aimed it, not outward at the world, not upward at the throne waiting behind the Magistrate's smile, but *downward*, into the single small point of contact where Quill's blistered hands gripped their wrists.

Into the child. Gentle. Only gentle. Healing the burns they had caused, and nothing else, and nothing more.

The light collapsed inward like a star remembering modesty, and Felix Crane came back to themselves kneeling on a vault floor, hollowed and shaking and utterly, ordinarily *small*—and had never in their life been so grateful for it.

Quill fell into them, sobbing, hands bandaged in the moment before they'd finished blistering. "You went away," the child kept saying. "You went away and you came back. Nobody comes back."

"Somebody pulled hard enough," Felix said into their hair.

Behind them, unhurried, the Magistrate began to applaud.

* * *

"Oh, *beautiful*," he said, and his kindness had finally slipped enough to show the machinery underneath, all gears and appetite. "Do you know how many debtors have stood where you're standing? Hundreds. Thousands. And every one of them either fled or fed. You are the first to *touch it and put it down*." His smile widened, and the amber veins in the walls pulsed in time with it. "Which means you're the first worth keeping. The contract matures tonight regardless, Felix Crane. When the last fire dies—and it is dying even now—you come due. All of you do." His gaze slid past Felix, past Quill, to Wren standing pale in the doorway, and something in his face sharpened into hunger. "Every clause. Every penalty. Unless—"

"There's no unless," Felix said. They rose, legs unsteady, soul-light still leaking faintly from the cracks in them like heat off a cooling forge. In their right hand, unnoticed until now, the crystal sat—empty. Hollow. Whatever had filled it had poured into them and been refused, and the vessel was just glass.

Just glass, shaped exactly like a heart.

Felix looked at it. Then at the Magistrate. Then at the far end of the vault, where the walls throbbed amber, and beyond them, deep in the mountain's roots, the last dragon-fire fought for every breath.

"Actually," Felix said, closing their fist around the empty crystal, "there is one unless. But it isn't yours to offer."

Far above, Greyhollow's bells stopped ringing all at once—which was somehow worse than the ringing—and in the sudden silence, everyone in the vault heard it:

The sound of the last fire, beginning to scream.
