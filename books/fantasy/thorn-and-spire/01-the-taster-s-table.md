## 1. The Taster's Table

There are two ways to learn a poison. The first is from a book: the bitter almond of cyanide, the honeyed rot of aconite, the way hemlock makes the legs forget their business while the mind watches, politely horrified. The second is from a spoon.

I learned mine the second way. Most of us do. The Crown does not employ tasters who have only read about dying.

The banquet tonight was in honor of the Ambassador from Vell — a nation whose chief export is olive oil and whose chief import, if you believe the rumors that reach even my ears, is other people's secrets. Nine courses. Which meant nine chances for someone to improve the ambassador's evening and ruin mine, or the reverse; at these tables it's often difficult to say which death would inconvenience the Crown more.

I sat three chairs down from Her Majesty, in the taster's alcove with its little table and its little silver spoons, dressed in grey so that no one would be tempted to look at me. Invisibility is part of the uniform. A taster who gets noticed becomes a symbol, and symbols get poisoned out of spite rather than strategy, which is wasteful for everyone.

Course four was the pheasant. I took my portion, chewed, swallowed, and waited the prescribed ninety seconds while the footman watched me with the bored expression of a man who has seen forty girls sit in this chair and only remembers the one who didn't get up.

The pheasant was fine. The pheasant was *more* than fine. Underneath the cherry glaze there was a whisper of something green and metallic, faint as a rumor — gallowleaf, I'd have said, or its cheaper cousin, hedgebitter. Dosed not to kill but to sicken. Enough to lay the ambassador low by morning, enough to make him miss his meeting with our Chancellor, enough to shift one clause of a trade treaty half an inch in Vell's disfavor.

Not my department. My department is whether it kills. It wouldn't. So I nodded to the footman, the pheasant went out, and somewhere between the cheese and the candied violets a man across the table began to sweat and go grey around the mouth and excuse himself with tremendous dignity.

No one looked at me. No one ever looks at me. That is the arrangement: they poison the food, I drink the poison, and the kingdom continues to believe itself loved.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving poison every night of your life: your body keeps a ledger too.

Mine has entries going back eleven years. Small doses, building like sediment — nightshade, arsenic, the distilled tears of whatever grows on the walls of enemy cellars. Each dose teaches the blood a little lesson, and the blood takes notes. By now I could probably drink the ambassador's entire wine cellar and feel nothing worse than a mild disappointment in the vintage.

They call it immunity. The courtiers say it like a blessing: *she can't be touched*. What they mean is: she cannot be *harmed*, therefore she may be *used* without cost. There is no entry in any royal account book for what the doses take out of me, because the Crown's ledgers do not record expenses it has decided are free.

I know ledgers. Better than anyone in this city suspects, which is precisely the point.

* * *

By the time the last course was cleared I had been dismissed — tasters are dismissed early, before dessert, in case dessert is ambitious — and I walked out through the servants' corridors with my grey dress and my grey face, down stairs that smelled of tallow and old fish, out into the honest dark of Saltquay where nobody curtsies because nobody cares.

The counting-house of the Guild of Impossible Things sits behind a chandlery that hasn't sold a candle in years. You enter through the cellar, past barrels of pickled eels that are exactly as legitimate as they look, and knock twice, pause, once, pause, twice more, and then Wren lets you in and complains about the state of the docks as though you personally arranged the tide.

"You're late," Wren said, because I was four minutes late, because banquets run long when ambassadors start sweating.

"The pheasant needed supervising."

"Hedgebitter?"

"Something in that family. Cheap. Vell will blame their own kitchens, which was presumably the idea." I hung up my cloak. "Is he in?"

"Where else. He never leaves. I think he's growing roots. Sable says—" Wren lowered his voice with the reverence of a man delivering scripture, "—the autumn books need closing."

Sable Fenwick, master of the Guild of Impossible Things, keeper of relics and memories and at least two last dragon-eggs (one of which turned out last year to be a very good forgery, an embarrassment we do not discuss), believed with his whole crooked heart that double-entry bookkeeping was a form of prayer. Every impossible thing the guild moved — a saint's fingerbone, a dead man's childhood, a vial of snow from a summer that never happened — went through my columns twice. Once for what it was. Once for what it cost.

I lit the lamp in the back room, opened the strongbox, and set the autumn ledger beside the private one, because a guild that trades in stolen things must keep two truths about every object: the truth we tell the world, and the truth we tell ourselves. The world's truth goes in the public book. Ours goes here, in my handwriting, in ink mixed with a little salt so it can't be lifted and forged. Sable calls me paranoid. Sable also sleeps with a knife under each pillow, so his opinion on paranoia carries limited weight.

I worked. This is the hour of the day I actually like, I'll admit it — the quiet, the arithmetic, the small clean satisfaction of numbers agreeing with each other, which is more than can be said for people. Item by item:

Three vials of memory, harvested from a drowning survivor, sold to a widow in Gilt Row who wanted to remember her husband better than he deserved. Entered twice.

One reliquary finger, provenance dubious, returned to the temple it was stolen from — for a fee, because virtue should still turn a profit.

One crate of Ashen Order vestments, white wool, ordered anonymously, destination unknown. That one I flagged with a red thread. The Order doesn't buy from smugglers. The Order burns smugglers. Someone inside the white cloaks wanted a purchase that left no trail, which meant someone inside the white cloaks was planning something that would leave a great many trails.

And then, near the bottom of the private ledger, an entry in Sable's own hand — older ink, older year, carried forward quarter after quarter like a debt no one will settle:

*One ember, unspent.*

No price. No buyer. No description beyond those three words, just the notation renewed in the margin each season in Sable's cramped script: *still held. Still held. Still held.*

I'd asked him about it once, two years ago, over bad brandy. An ember? Whose? Kept where?

Sable had gone very still, the way he does — a big man made suddenly all edges — and said, "Some entries are less inventory than promise, girl. When it moves, you'll know." And then he'd drunk his brandy and changed the subject to the weather, which in Saltquay is always a rich topic of complaint.

So I did what I always did with it. Carried it forward. Balanced around it. One ember, unspent, value: withheld. It bothered me the way a loose tooth bothers a tongue — I worried at it without meaning to, and every quarter it stayed exactly as loose as before.

Tonight it bothered me more than usual. Maybe it was the pheasant, the little metallic ghost of hedgebitter still sitting in my blood alongside everything else, reminding me that I was a woman built entirely out of other people's poisons. Maybe it was the red thread on the Ashen vestments. Maybe it was simply that I am thirty-one years old and I spend my evenings being poisoned for a crown that pays me in silence and my nights doing sums for a guild that pays me in secrets, and some animal part of me has begun to keep its own tally, in a column I don't let myself read.

*Still held,* I wrote, and blotted it, and told myself the small cold feeling in my chest was just the dose working through.

That was when the knocking started at the street door. Not the rhythm — not twice-pause-once, none of it. Just fists, hammering, and then a voice I didn't know, ragged and wet-sounding, shouting a word that made Wren come out of the front room with his knife already drawn:

"Delacroix! Is there a Delacroix in there?"

My name. Not my guild name — my *name*, the grey-dress, silver-spoon, royal-table name, the one that exists nowhere in Saltquay except in my own careful head.

Wren looked at me. I looked back at him, and neither of us said anything, and outside, the fists kept beating on the door like a heart that couldn't decide whether to stop.
