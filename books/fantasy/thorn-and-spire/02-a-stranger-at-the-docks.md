## 2. A Stranger at the Docks

The counting-house smelled of brine and tallow, which is to say it smelled like money that had been laundered twice. I sat behind the desk in the back room with a lamp turned low, reconciling the week's takings against the week's lies, and for one hour — one blessed, unremarkable hour — nothing tried to kill me.

I want to be clear about how rare that was. Most nights my body was the Crown's property: poured into court silks, marched to banquets, seated at the King's left hand where the dishes came to me first. The poisoners of Vael had grown inventive lately. Tuesday it was arsenic in the quince preserve, Wednesday nightshade folded into the wine like a lover's apology, Thursday something new and oily that made my tongue go numb for an hour while the court watched me chew and swallow and smile. They call me the King's Taster as if it were an honor. What they mean is that I am a lock built out of meat, and every banquet is someone trying to pick it.

So yes. An hour with nothing but arithmetic was nearly a holiday.

The Guild of Impossible Things keeps its ledgers the way other institutions keep their sins: carefully, and in duplicate. The true books live in my head and in this room; the false ones go to the tax assessors, who believe we are a modest trading concern dealing in salted fish and, occasionally, salted fish. My pen scratched. Candlelight guttered over columns of figures. Somewhere above, gulls argued about a dead mackerel.

That was when the door came off its hinges.

Not opened — *removed*, slammed inward with such force that the lamp jumped and a shelf of false ledgers shed its dignity across the floor. And in the doorway stood a man who had been on fire.

I don't use that word lightly. I've seen burn victims in the guild's line of work; accidents happen when you smuggle alchemist's reagents past customs men with more curiosity than sense. But those men are burned. This man had been *consumed* and had somehow kept walking. His coat was char to the elbow. One side of his face had gone smooth and waxy, the ear melted down to a curl. He smelled of cinders and something under the cinders — hot iron, old blood, a sweetness like a struck match held too close to your nose.

He looked at me, and his remaining eye did something worse than beg. It *recognized* me.

"Door," he rasped, and I moved without deciding to, dragging the broken door up and wedging it back into its frame because panic makes you stupid and stupid, tonight, meant buying seconds.

"Who sent you?" I asked. "Which handler? Fenwick doesn't run couriers through the front—"

"It knows its own blood."

Four words, and they made no sense at all, which by guild standards put them somewhere in the middle of a normal sentence. He was already crossing the room, leaving scorched prints on the floorboards, and then his hands were around a scroll case — oilskin-wrapped, sealed with black wax — and pressing it into mine, folding my fingers shut over it with hands hot enough that I felt them through the leather.

"Take it," he said. "It wouldn't open for me. Wouldn't burn either." A sound came out of him that might have been a laugh if laughter could bleed. "Wouldn't let me look. It's waiting for someone else. It knows its own blood, Taster. It's been *waiting*."

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, which was true, and also the kind of lie I'm best at: the kind where you tell the truth slowly enough that it buys you time.

"You will." His good eye drifted toward the ruined door. Whatever he heard on the other side, I heard it a moment later — boots. Many. Moving in step, which no smuggler does and no dockside thief bothers with. Soldiers' cadence. Worse: *quiet* soldiers' cadence, the kind drilled into men until marching becomes breathing.

"Ashen," he whispered, and the word dropped the temperature of the room five degrees.

Everyone knows the Ashen Order the way everyone knows a scar. White cloaks, gray faces, a doctrine written in ash: *that which burned once must never burn again.* They'd spent a hundred years being a footnote in history texts — zealots who tended cold hearths and preached against wildfire — and in the last two years they'd become something with funding, and reach, and lists. I knew because the Guild kept a ledger entry for them too, filed under *threats, institutional*, right between *the Crown's secret service* and *my mother*.

"They can't have it." His grip tightened on my fingers until the bones ground. "No one can have it. That's the mercy. Promise me you'll—" He coughed, and the cough came out gray, ash sifting from his lips onto my desk, onto my careful columns of figures. "Promise me."

"I don't even know what *it* is."

"Good," he said, and smiled with half his face. "Then it chose right."

The boots stopped outside the door.

What happened next happened fast, the way the worst things do. The courier shoved me — hard, both palms, toward the floor hatch behind the desk, the one that drops into the salt-cellars — and then he turned to face the door and pulled something from inside his ruined coat. Not a weapon. A lamp, of all things, a little ship's lamp with the glass fogged white, and he set it burning on the desk beside my ledgers as the door burst inward a second time.

White cloaks. Four of them, then six, filling the frame shoulder to shoulder, faces gray with ash-paste in the old style, swords drawn but held low, because the Order prefers to take people alive. I got one glimpse of their leader — tall, patient eyes, the calm of a man who has done terrible bookkeeping all his life — and then the courier said, clearly and almost gently, "*Burn bright,*" and threw himself backward into the lamp.

I will not describe it. Some things belong in ledgers and some things belong nowhere at all. Suffice to say the light went white, the heat went everywhere, and the last thing I saw of that poor man was him standing upright in the heart of it with his arms spread wide, holding the doorway, holding the line, giving a stranger he'd never met three extra seconds.

Three seconds is a long time when you spend them falling.

*

The salt-cellars run beneath the whole dock quarter, brick throats older than the city above them, and they smell exactly as you'd expect a century of pickled herring to smell. I landed badly, rolled, came up with the scroll case clutched to my chest like a child and my heart going like a drum corps.

Above me, muffled through the floorboards, the fire roared and then didn't. Boots crossed the ceiling. A voice — calm, unhurried, terribly reasonable — gave orders. *"Sweep the cellars. She'll run for the water. They always run for the water."*

They knew about me. Not guessed — *knew*. Which meant someone had told them, which meant there was a leak in the Guild, or the Order had grown eyes inside the palace, or both, and every possibility was worse than the last.

I did not run for the water.

Instead I went the other way, deeper, into the dark where the bricks sweated and the air tasted of iodine and old mistakes. I know those tunnels the way I know ledgers — every junction, every collapsed branch, every spot where the tide comes sighing up through the grates twice a day. I put my hand on the wall and walked blind and quiet, and the whole time the scroll case pressed against my ribs like a second heartbeat, warm. Warm when everything around me was cold. I noticed that. I noticed it the way you notice a toothache: unwillingly, and with a growing certainty that it was going to be a problem.

Because here is the thing I couldn't stop thinking, wading hip-deep at last through a flooded passage with my skirts bunched in one fist and my life rolled up under my arm: the courier hadn't come to the counting-house by accident. Handlers route couriers. Someone had known Orsen Delacroix, King's Taster and keeper of impossible books, would be behind that desk at that hour. And a dying man had pressed a mystery into my hands and called me — what had he called me? Not *Taster*. Something else. Something the fire had taken with him, or maybe something he'd never finished saying.

*It knows its own blood.*

Whose blood? Mine? I am the daughter of nobody, raised on nobody's coin, bred — and I use that word with its full ugly weight — *bred* for one purpose: to swallow death on schedule and keep smiling. There is no bloodline in me worth knowing. There is only immunity, purchased by generations of whatever was done to my family before the Crown got polite about it.

And yet the case was warm. And yet the white-cloaks had swept in on my address within minutes of that man catching fire halfway across the city.

I surfaced near the fish-market stairs as the bells rang the small hours, soaked to the waist, smelling of herring and terror, and I stood there in the dark doing what I do best: double-entry. On one side, the safe column: go home, burn the case unread, report nothing, be tomorrow morning the same numb instrument the Crown sharpened nightly. On the other side: four words from a dead man, warmth against my ribs, and the memory of a voice saying *sweep the cellars, she'll run for the water* — meaning they would keep looking, meaning the safe column had just quietly emptied itself, meaning I was already in the second ledger whether I signed it or not.

A taster's whole art is knowing what's poison before you swallow it.

I unwound the oilskin. Black wax, unbroken, marked with a sigil I didn't recognize — a spiral, drawn as though it were falling inward forever. The wax was warm too. Under my thumb, faint as a pulse, the seal *shifted*, softening, the way wax does near a flame.

Somewhere behind my ribs, something answered it.

I told myself it was my heartbeat. I am very good at telling myself things.

Then I broke the seal — because I am, despite everything, still a woman who reads the books she keeps — and the parchment unrolled itself in my hands like a thing unfolding from sleep, and a voice that had waited a hundred years to speak drew breath.
