## 4. Ledgers of Impossible Things

There are three rules to keeping the ledgers of the Guild of Impossible Things, and I have broken all of them, though never on the same night.

Rule one: count everything twice. Rule two: trust no one with a clean face. Rule three — and this is the one they carve into the counting-house door, or did, before I had it planed off and replaced with something about discretion — *write nothing you cannot burn.*

I have spent forty years not writing things down. It is a peculiar skill. Most people think bookkeeping is the art of recording. It is not. It is the art of deciding what the record is allowed to know. A ledger is a confession with good posture, and mine has been standing up straight for four decades while lying through its teeth.

That is what I was doing the night Orsen Delacroix came back from the salt-cellars smelling of blood and brine and destiny: lying to her, one careful entry at a time.

* * *

She arrived past midnight, wet to the knees, holding a rolled map the way you hold a sleeping snake — respectfully, and at arm's length, and with the growing suspicion that it was awake. She set it on my desk between the inkwell and the candlestick, and said, "It talked to me."

"Maps do that," I said. "Particularly old ones. They're mostly gossip."

"It said *heir.*"

Ah. Well. There it was.

I poured her a glass of the good brandy — the bottle I keep for funerals and auditors — and watched her drink it in two swallows like a soldier instead of sipping it like a lady, which told me more about her childhood than any dossier ever had. Then I took the map, weighed it in my hands as if appraising it, and lied to her face with the smooth professionalism of a man who has done it ten thousand times.

"Curious piece," I said. "I'll have it authenticated."

"You knew," she said. Not a question. Orsen Delacroix does not waste punctuation on questions when she already knows the answer. "You knew what this was. Your face did the thing it does when you're calculating."

"My face does many things."

"It's doing one now."

I put the map in the iron cabinet behind my chair — the one with the lock shaped like a closed mouth — and turned the key, and gave her the smile I reserve for clients who are about to be overcharged. "Go home, Orsen. Sleep. In the morning this will be an interesting curiosity and nothing more."

She went. She always goes when I tell her to, which is the most dangerous habit she has, because she goes *believing me*, and I have never once deserved it.

Then I sat alone in the counting-house with the lamp turned low, and I opened the first ledger.

Not the first ledger chronologically — that would be too easy, and nothing about this guild has ever been easy. The First Ledger. Capital letters. Bound in hide that no tanner will admit to having cured, written in ink that shifts color depending on how ashamed you are of what you're reading. I am told that to a truly innocent reader it appears blank. I would not know. I have never been innocent enough to test it.

I unlocked the iron cabinet, and I read the entry I have been dreading for thirty years.

* * *

You should understand what the Guild of Impossible Things was, before it became what it is.

We were not always smugglers. Smuggling implies the goods belong to someone. For six hundred years, nothing in this world belonged to anyone — the dragons saw to that. They held everything in common the way a storm holds the sea: entirely, and without paperwork. We were their clerks. Their stewards. When a dragon needed a hoard moved, we moved it. When a dragon died and left an egg to a friend — yes, dragons had friends; imagine — we delivered it, unopened, uncounted, because rule one applies only to things that can be owned, and dragons cannot be owned, and neither could their fire.

The oldest entries are not in any human script. They are scorched into the pages. Dragon-claw cursive, the archivists call it, when they call it anything. Every guildmaster for six centuries has been able to read it, or has pretended to, which amounts to the same profession.

And then came the war, and after the war came the bargain, and after the bargain came the ledgers' long slow lie.

The bargain. Everyone learns a version of it in school. *The dragons were defeated; the fire was quenched; peace reigned.* What the schoolbooks omit — what the Ashen Order has burned every library to keep omitted — is that the fire was not quenched. It was *entrusted*. Placed in an iron cradle at the Emberhold and guarded by a bloodline bred specifically to hold dragonfire in trust without burning. Tasters, the crown calls them now. Poison-eaters. The family whose bodies were designed, generation by generation, patient generation by patient generation, to be a vault with a heartbeat.

Delacroix.

I turned to the page. I have turned to it perhaps two hundred times over the years, the way a man touches a bruise to check whether it still hurts. It always hurts.

There it is, in claw-cursive older than the kingdom itself:

*House Delacroix. Fire-sworn. Keepers of the cradle. Let their blood hold what our wings cannot carry. When the last ember dims, the debt comes due.*

Fire-sworn. Not fire-*bound*. Not fire-*owned*. Sworn. A vow freely made, by a family that chose, once, to be the lock on the door — and was then quietly converted, by kings and ash-priests, into the thing locked inside it.

Every year, the Crown summons a Delacroix to court and feeds her poison for a living, and calls it honor. Every year, the Ashen Order sharpens its white cloaks and calls the guardianship a crime to be ended. And every year, some guildmaster — me, currently, God help the position — reads this page and says nothing, because the alternative is telling a young woman that her entire life has been a misfiled entry.

* * *

Here is the arithmetic of my silence, laid out honestly, since honesty costs me nothing at three in the morning with no witnesses:

If I tell her, she will go to the Emberhold. Of course she will. Orsen Delacroix has the finest mind I have ever employed and the self-preservation instincts of a moth with a grudge. She will walk into the one place in the world where both the Crown and the Order are waiting, and she will do it believing she is walking toward answers, when in fact she is walking toward everyone who wants her dead or domesticated.

If I don't tell her, the map will. That voice in the parchment — Veyra, executed a century ago for cartography the Order deemed heretical — will tell her everything eventually, in that patient dead woman's way of hers. And then Orsen will learn that I knew, and she will learn it from someone who isn't me, and whatever we have built between us — employer and employee, which is a thin word for what it actually is — will be another casualty of the ledger.

So I keep silent. And I hate it. And I do it anyway. This is called stewardship, in the trade. In the dark, at three in the morning, it is called cowardice, but cowardice with excellent margins.

I sat there until the candle burned down to a thumb's width, reading backward through the First Ledger, following the Delacroix name down the centuries like a thread through cloth. Fire-sworn, fire-sworn, fire-sworn. Entries in dragon-script, entries in the old high tongue, entries in the cramped hand of my predecessor Prentiss, who died at his desk with his pen still loaded. And then — this is the part that made me close the book and sit with my eyes shut for a long time — an entry in my own hand, dated twenty-six years ago, the week a girl named Orsen was born.

I had written: *A daughter to the Delacroix line. The debt stirs. Watch her.*

Watch her. As if she were a shipment. An item awaiting valuation. I was younger then, and dutiful, and I genuinely believed that watching was a kindness — that keeping track of her was a form of protection. Twenty-six years later I employ her, poison-proof her against her own government, teach her double-entry bookkeeping, and tell her nothing, and I still call it protection. The vocabulary improves. The entry stays the same.

* * *

Toward dawn I heard her moving upstairs — she keeps a room above the counting-house, ostensibly for convenience, actually because neither of us sleeps well apart from the ledgers — and I hurriedly locked the First Ledger away and arranged my face into something innocuous. When she came down, dressed for travel with a pack already slung, I knew two things simultaneously: that the map had been talking to her all night, and that I had approximately four minutes before she walked out of my life and into the story I had spent thirty years trying to keep her out of.

"The pilgrim road," she said, studying me. "East. Toward the wheat country." A pause, precise as a knife going between ribs. "Unless you'd rather I wait here while you authenticate things."

"Orsen—"

"Sable." She almost smiled. "You've been kind to me. In your way. Whatever your way is." She picked up her pack. "I'll send word."

"Don't," I said. "Word gets read."

"Then I'll send something impossible," she said, and left, and the door clicked behind her with the small final sound of a lock turning in a ledger I hadn't finished writing.

I stood there in the empty counting-house surrounded by forty years of meticulous omissions, and I did the one thing I swore I'd never do. I pulled the First Ledger out, laid it open on the desk to the Delacroix page, and reached for a pen.

Rule three: write nothing you cannot burn.

I wrote: *She knows the road. I let her go. If the debt comes due, let it be entered under my name.*

The ink went down black and stayed black, which the archivists say means the ledger agrees with what you've written, and which I choose to interpret as the closest thing to absolution this guild has ever issued.

I was still blotting the page when I heard the horses on the cobbles outside — many horses, moving quietly, in the disciplined formation of men who have been taught that noise is a sin. Through the shutters, gray in the false dawn, came riders in white cloaks, and at their head a man carrying a warrant sealed with ash.

They were looking for her.

They had come to me.
