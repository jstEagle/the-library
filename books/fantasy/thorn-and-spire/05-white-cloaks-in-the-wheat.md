## 5. White Cloaks in the Wheat

The pilgrim road out of Vellamere smelled of dust and someone else's incense, which I have always found to be the two great constants of organized religion. I walked it with a rolled-up map in my satchel that talked to me, a purse of guild coin I was technically embezzling, and the growing suspicion that I had traded one set of people trying to kill me for another, larger, better-funded set.

Progress, of sorts.

"You're sulking," said the map.

"I'm walking," I said. "Sulking is when I stop."

"Your shoulders are up around your ears. In my day we called that sulking."

"In your day," I said, "they executed cartographers for drawing accurate things, so forgive me if I don't take your counsel on emotional wellness."

Veyra made a sound like wind through a dry reed bed, which I had learned was her laugh. A hundred years dead and still funnier than most living men I'd dined with. The parchment rustled against my hip where it lay wrapped in oilcloth, and I felt — this is going to sound mad, but by then madness and I were on familiar terms — I felt something almost like warmth from it. The map liked being near me. It had said *heir* twice now, and each time the word had settled into my chest like a swallowed coal.

I did not want to be anyone's heir. Heirs inherit debts, and I already kept ledgers; I knew precisely how those worked.

The wheat fields began an hour past the city walls, golden and endless, stitched through with the little wayshrines the faithful built for a god whose name changed depending on which village you asked. Pilgrims passed me in twos and threes, hooded, sandal-sore, singing off-key. I envied them their honest blisters. My own feet hurt too, but mine came with the added flavor of paranoia.

"They've found us," Veyra said, without preamble, somewhere around the third wayshrine.

I didn't break stride. "How do you know?"

"The same way you know rain before it falls. Something in the air has changed its mind about us." A pause. "Behind. Four riders, maybe five. They're not hurrying, which is worse than hurrying. Men who hurry are chasing. Men who don't are herding."

I glanced back the way one glances at scenery: casual, incurious, a woman admiring wheat. Two figures on the road behind us, white cloaks gone grey-gold in the field light, riding easy. Ashen Order. Of course. The Order never traveled in ones and twos unless they wanted to look harmless, and they never looked harmless on purpose.

"There's a drainage cut ahead," Veyra said. "On the old survey — left side, past the shrine with the broken bell. It runs under the road toward the river."

"And then what? I swim away while you shout encouragement?"

"And then you stop being clever at me and start being fast."

I adjusted my stride toward the broken-bell shrine, heart tapping out a rhythm against my ribs that no taster's training could steady. Poison I could handle. Poison sat still in a cup and waited its turn. Riders with ash-grey sigils did not wait their turn.

The shrine was small, moss-eaten, its bronze bell cracked down the middle like a bad egg. Beyond it, half-swallowed by bramble, the drainage grate gaped black. I crouched as if tying my bootlace and slid the map's roll free of my satchel, holding it against my stomach under my cloak.

"They've stopped," Veyra murmured. "Dismounting. Orsen — they're not coming for the map alone. They're spreading out through the wheat. They mean to walk you down."

"How do you know what they—"

"Because I helped draw the Order's first patrol charts, child. Before they hanged me for the second ones."

There was no time to unpack that. I went through the grate on hands and knees, into cold and dark and the smell of a century of runoff, and crawled until my elbows screamed, and came up in a culvert ditch on the far side just as the first shout went up behind me.

Not a shout of discovery. A shout of command.

They'd known exactly where I was all along.

* * *

I ran.

I am not, I'll admit, built for running. Tasters are built for sitting at long tables looking serene while their bodies quietly wage war on their behalf. But terror is an excellent tutor, and the wheat gave me cover as I crashed along the ditch-line, seed heads whipping my face, the map clutched to my chest like a screaming infant I couldn't put down.

"Left!" Veyra barked. "Left at the stone — there's a shepherd's track, it bends back toward the river willows—"

I went left. Behind me, wheat flattened in converging lines. Three hunters on foot now, moving through the grain like knives through dough, and the horses circling wide to close the far end of the field. Herding. She'd been right. They weren't chasing me; they were funneling me.

"They know the ground," I panted.

"They wrote the ground. Orsen — listen to me. When they take you, they will not kill you quickly. There is a thing they use, a dart-paste, made from widow's-nettle and grave-iron. It stops the lungs gently, over an hour, so the body can be examined afterward without damage." Her voice did something I hadn't heard from her yet. It gentled. "They'll want to study why you didn't burn, heir. Whatever happens: do not let the paste stay under your skin."

"Wonderful. I'll be sure to file a complaint."

The shepherd's track bent as promised, toward a smear of willow-green that meant water, and for three blessed minutes I thought I might actually make it. Then the fourth hunter stepped out of the wheat directly into my path, and I learned what a white cloak looked like up close: bone-colored wool, the ash-circle sigil stitched in silver thread, and beneath the hood a face young and calm and terribly sorry about everything.

"Orsen Delacroix," he said. Not a question. He held something in his hand — a short blowpipe, no longer than a stylus. "Grand Ashen sends her regards. This won't hurt."

"It always hurts," I said, "that's rather the point of it," and turned to run, and the dart took me in the shoulder anyway.

The world went soft at the edges. That's the only honest description. The wheat blurred to honey, the willows smeared sideways, and my legs informed me they were resigning. I went down into the grain with a strange, floating clarity, watching my own hands open and close.

*Widow's-nettle,* Veyra had said. *Grave-iron.* Paralytic paste, gentle, slow, meant to leave the specimen intact.

And here is the thing nobody in the Ashen Order could have known, because I barely knew it myself: I had spent eleven years drinking death in thimblefuls every night before banquets. Arsenic and nightlock, salts of lead and powdered stone, the whole apothecary's litany, each dose a little stronger, each morning waking a little more immune. My body did not merely tolerate poison. It *studied* it. It kept accounts. Somewhere in my blood was a ledger of every toxin ever offered to me, balanced nightly, carried forward, compounding like interest.

The paste hit my bloodstream and my ledger opened.

Heat first — not the numb spreading cold they'd promised, but heat, climbing from the wound outward, as though someone had lit a lamp behind my collarbone. The paralysis crested, held for one long terrible heartbeat—

—and broke.

I gasped, rolled, sat up in the wheat while the hunter stared at me with his calm face cracking straight down the middle, and something moved under my skin that I can only call recognition. The poison hadn't beaten me. It had *joined* me. Widow's-nettle and grave-iron, broken down, catalogued, and — this was the part that frightened me — *used*. Warmth pooled in my palms. The seed heads nearest my hands curled and browned, just slightly, just at the tips, as if a breath of August had passed through October.

"Oh," Veyra said softly, from inside my cloak. "*Oh*, heir."

The hunter recovered faster than I did. He dropped the blowpipe and drew steel, and I did the only sensible thing available to a poisoned woman on her knees in a wheat field: I grabbed a fistful of dirt and threw it at his eyes.

It should have been pathetic. A handful of soil, thrown badly, from below. But the grains left my fingers hot, unnaturally hot, and he flinched — a full flinch, blade wavering — and that bought me the second I needed to drive my shoulder into his knees.

We went down together. His knife scored my forearm; my head found his nose; and then we were grappling in the wheat like drunks outside a tavern, and I felt his breath go strange, and I realized with distant horror that the paste residue was still on my skin, on my *hands*, and that touching him was enough. His body was meeting my blood's ledger the way all poisons met it — and losing.

He stopped struggling. His eyes, very close to mine, went from fury to confusion to something older than both.

"*Delacroix,*" he whispered. Blood in his teeth. "The fire-sworn line. We were told the line was ended. Grand Ashen said—" A wet, disbelieving laugh. "She said we were putting out the last of it. She never said the last of it had a *name*."

Then his eyes fixed on something over my shoulder, and whatever he saw there — wheat bending against the wind, perhaps, or nothing at all — he crossed himself the old way, thumb to sternum, the way farmers cross themselves against storms, and died in the grain with the ash-circle pressed into the dirt beneath him.

I knelt over him for a long moment, shaking, my burned-hot hands hovering above the wheat because I no longer trusted them to touch anything.

"What did I do?" I asked. My voice came out small. "Veyra. What did I just do?"

"You survived," she said. "You survived the way your grandmothers survived, and their mothers before them, all the way back to the cradle of fire itself." The parchment trembled against me. For once she didn't sound ancient or knowing. She sounded afraid. "Get up, Orsen. The other three heard him fall."

"I killed a man with my *hands*—"

"You survived a man who was killing you, and I will grieve him properly later, and so will you, but *later*." Steel rang somewhere in the wheat, a hunting horn's flat cousin. "Up. River willows. Now."

I got up. I ran. And the whole time I ran, I watched my own hands — ordinary hands, ink-stained, ledger-callused — glowing faintly at the fingertips like coals breathing under ash.

The fire-sworn line. *We were told the line was ended.*

Which meant someone, a hundred years ago, had tried very hard to end it.

And had failed by exactly one Delacroix.
