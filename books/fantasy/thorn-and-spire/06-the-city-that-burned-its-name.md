## 6. The City That Burned Its Name

Cindermark smells like a hearth that someone forgot to bank, and I mean that as a compliment. After three days on the pilgrim road — three days of wheat, fear, and a dead hunter's face saying *Delacroix* like it was a curse and a key at once — the smell of warm ash felt almost maternal.

The city sits atop its dead the way a grieving widow sits atop a coffin: deliberately, and with her best dress on. Every street is paved with fused glass where dragonfire once struck. Every rooftop wears tiles the color of old embers. And down in the foundations, they say, there lies a dragon the size of a cathedral, buried whole, its bones serving as the city's cellar beams. The Cinderfolk built their homes on the grave of the thing that burned them, which is either the most pragmatic thing I've ever heard or the most vindictive, and after thirty years of swallowing poison for people who smile at me over banquet tables, I found it hard to tell the difference.

I came in through the ash-gate at dusk, hood up, coins counted, Veyra's map folded against my ribs where my heart could keep an eye on it.

*You are limping,* said the voice from the parchment, faint as smoke off a candle.

"I am walking with dignity."

*You are limping with dignity. There is an ash-shrine two streets ahead. The priests will feed you. They always feed strangers — it is the one commandment their order never managed to break.*

She'd been quieter since the wheat field. Not silent — I sometimes wished for silence — but subdued, the way a person gets after watching you kill someone with your own throat. Because that was what I'd done, more or less. The Ashen hunter's paralytic had gone into my wine-skin, and I had gone into it without hesitation, without even the good manners to die, and something in me had taken the poison apart like a child dismantling a clock. The hunter had died with my family name on his lips and a look on his face that I have not yet managed to file away anywhere.

*The priests here,* Veyra said now, *knew my work. They knew the map. If any souls left in this world remember how the war truly ended, it will be them.*

"And how did it end? You've been dodging that question since the docks."

A pause. Parchment does not breathe, but hers seemed to.

*Badly,* she said. *And then, somehow, mercifully. Ask them. It should come from mouths, not from me. You would not believe a ghost.*

"I don't believe you now."

*Good,* she said. *Distrust is the heir's first inheritance. Now walk. Your limp is getting worse.*

* * *

The shrine was a low stone building with no door, only a curtain of woven grey cloth, because — the priest later explained — doors are for keeping things out, and ash-priests are commanded to let everything in: weather, beggars, grief, dogs, and apparently poisoned novelists with contraband cartography.

The priest who rose to meet me was old in the way that river stones are old, smooth and grey and impossible to guess at. Her name was Mother Sere, and she took one look at my hood, one look at my hands — which, despite scrubbing, still carried a faint scorch-mark across the knuckles from the wheat field — and said:

"Sit. Soup first. Questions after. That is the law here, and unlike most laws, we actually keep it."

I sat. I ate. The soup was barley and smoked root and tasted like being seven years old in some other life, and I want it noted for posterity that I cried into it, briefly and quietly, and that Mother Sere pretended not to notice, which is the highest form of courtesy the poor can offer each other.

When the bowl was empty she refilled it herself, and only then did she sit across from me and say, "You're fire-touched. Third generation, if the mark on your knuckles means anything. Maybe fourth."

My spoon stopped halfway to my mouth.

"I'm a taster," I said carefully. "For the Crown. It's a poisoner's immunity. Nothing more."

Mother Sere smiled, and it was not an unkind smile, but it was the smile of a woman about to take away the last comfortable lie a person owns.

"Child," she said, "there is no such thing as poisoner's immunity. There never was. No breeding program of kings can make a body shrug off arsenic and basilisk-venom and whatever the Order put in that wineskin. What your blood carries is older than any crown in existence." She leaned forward. "You've come asking about the war. So let me tell you how it ended, since everyone else tells it wrong."

* * *

Here is the story Mother Sere told, by the light of a brazier that burned without wood — ash-priest trickery, or something less explainable — while Veyra's parchment lay between us on the table, listening, I would swear, the way the dead listen when their names come up.

"There were dragons, and there were men, and for four hundred years they made war on each other with a thoroughness neither side could sustain. Dragons burned cities; men poisoned wells and stole eggs. Both lines were dying. And in the end, it was not a victory anyone won. It was a bargain struck in a place very much like this one — on a grave, over ashes.

"The dragons agreed to sleep. To withdraw from the world, egg by egg, wing by wing, until none remained awake, so that the war would starve. But fire cannot simply be unmade, child. Dragonfire is not a thing dragons carry. It is a thing they *are*, and it must live somewhere.

"So the fire went into trust. Into us."

Her eyes, pale as salt, fixed on mine.

"The last free flame sleeps at the Emberhold — you know this already, I think; your map knows it. But scattered through every kingdom of men, in certain bloodlines, there runs a portion held in trust. Fire-bearers. Fire-tasters. Families sworn, generations ago, to hold dragonfire in their veins until such time as the dragons wake and call it home. Your body does not resist poison, Orsen Delacroix. Your body is *occupied*. Every toxin you've ever swallowed has been met by something far older and far angrier than any alchemist's spite."

The brazier popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the city, and the whole of Cindermark sighed with it — ten thousand rooftops breathing like the flank of a sleeping beast, which, I supposed, in a sense they were.

"They didn't tell us," I said finally. My voice came out flat and strange. "The Crown. My handlers. Thirty years of banquets, thirty years of dying a little every night, and not one of them ever said *you're doing this because you were born to it.* They let me think I was livestock. A useful animal. Bred and fed and kept."

"What would you have done," Mother Sere asked gently, "if they'd told you?"

"Run."

"Yes. And the bargain fails if even one bearer runs. That was the price of the peace — the collateral, if you like. The bearers could never be told, because told people choose, and the whole architecture of the peace rests on no one choosing." She spread her hands. "You were not lied to out of contempt, child. You were lied to out of cowardice. Which is worse, I grant you, but it is at least a different flavor."

I looked down at my hands. At the burn-scars layered under the skin, invisible, thirty years deep. Every banquet. Every cup raised in toast while my own pulse counted down the seconds until the cramping started, the cold sweat, the nights I spent curled around a chamber pot thanking whatever gods there were that I'd survive to eat breakfast and do it again tomorrow.

They hadn't bred a shield-maiden. They hadn't even bred a servant. They'd bred a *vessel*, and filled it nightly to keep the contents fresh, and called it an honor, and pinned a medal on me, and I had *thanked* them.

"You're crying again," Mother Sere observed.

"I am leaking with dignity."

"That's the spirit."

* * *

Later — much later, past midnight, sitting alone in the shrine's guest alcove with the grey curtain stirring at the entrance — I unrolled the map across my knees and spoke to it before it could speak to me.

"You knew."

*I suspected,* Veyra said. *Then I confirmed it, a century ago, and they executed me for confirming it. The Order has always understood what the Crown merely suspects: that the bearers are the bargain's collateral. Kill the bearers' knowledge, kill the bearers' choice, and the fire stays safely locked in bodies that don't know they're cages.*

"My mother didn't know. Her mother probably didn't know. How many generations of my family swallowed poison on purpose, believing it was duty?"

*How many believed it was love?* Veyra asked softly. *That is the crueler question. The Order doesn't hate the bearers, Orsen. They pity them. They think they are freeing you.*

"By killing the last fire."

*By killing the last fire, yes. Extinguish the Emberhold, and the trust is void. The fire in your blood goes dark and quiet forever, and no bearer ever has to be poisoned, or hunted, or chosen, or anything at all. They will offer you that, when they catch you. Peace. An ending. And they will be sincere.*

I sat with that. Outside, Cindermark slept on its grave, and beneath the paving stones, beneath the cellars, beneath the fused glass streets, something vast and patient kept its bones still, holding up a city that had killed it, because that was the bargain, and bargains are kept by whoever agreed to them first.

Grief is a strange thing in a taster's body. I have spent my life detecting poisons in other people's cups, identifying them by aftertaste, cataloguing them like old acquaintances. And sitting there, I recognized the taste in my own mouth at once: bitter root, cold iron, slow burn. Grief, and under it rage, and under the rage something I did not have a name for yet — something that warmed the back of my throat like a swallow of strong liquor, like a coal breathed upon, like—

Like an ember. Unspent.

The guild ledger entry surfaced in my mind, in Sable's precise hand: *one ember, unspent.* I had assumed it meant the map. I had assumed it meant the Emberhold.

I was beginning to suspect the ledger had been keeping accounts on me all along.

"Veyra," I said. "When the fire wakes — when the dragons wake and call the trust home — what happens to the vessels?"

Silence. Long enough that I thought the parchment had finally frayed into ordinary sheepskin.

*No one knows,* she admitted. *It has never happened. The bargain says the fire returns. The bargain says nothing at all about what remains of the ones who carried it.*

"Comforting."

*I never promised comfort. I promised a road.* The ink-lines of the map shifted in the dark, roads rearranging themselves like a hand pointing onward. *And the road leaves Cindermark at dawn. Rest while you can, heir. The Order is closer than you think.*

"How close?"

Below us, faint but unmistakable, a bell began to ring — the ash-bell, the one Mother Sere had told me rang only for one reason. Not fire. Not flood.

White cloaks, at the gate.
