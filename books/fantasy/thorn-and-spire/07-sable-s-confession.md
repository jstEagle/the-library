## 7. Sable's Confession

There are three ways to run a smuggling guild, and I have tried all of them.

The first is fear. It works beautifully until the night someone decides they'd rather be feared than frightened, and then you're dead in your own counting-house with your own ledger-weights scattered across the floor like bad dice. The second is greed, which works until the money runs out or someone counts faster than you do — and Orsen Delacroix counted faster than anyone I have ever known, which should have been my first warning about her.

The third way is love. Nobody tells you that love is the worst of the three, because love makes you keep things you should burn, protect people who would be safer hating you, and — when the white cloaks finally come up your stairs with their ash-blessed irons — trade away everything you spent forty years building, for the chance that one stubborn woman might live long enough to curse your name properly.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. A good confession, like a good ledger, begins with the opening balance.

* * *

They came at dusk, which was insulting. Every competent inquisitor knows dusk is when a guildmaster's defenses are at their best; we are creatures of twilight by trade. These ones came anyway, six of them in white that had never seen honest labor, and their Grand Ashen walked in through my front door without breaking it, which told me he'd already bought my doorwarden and probably my cook.

"Sable Fenwick," he said. He pronounced it correctly, which was worse than getting it wrong. "We've been reading your guild's history. Fascinating material."

"Flattery will get you nowhere," I said. "I'm immune to it. Occupational hazard."

He smiled the way priests smile — patiently, as though eternity were on his side of the argument, which historically it usually is. He set something on my table between us. Not an iron. Not a writ. A book. My book — no, not mine. *The* book. The first ledger, bound in dragonhide that hadn't been legally available for a hundred years, its pages soft as old skin.

"You keep excellent records," he said. "So did your predecessors. We only want one thing from them."

"And here I thought you'd come for the wine cellar."

"The map, Fenwick. The map to the Emberhold. And the girl carrying it."

I looked at the first ledger and felt every year of my age settle into my knees like silt into a harbor. Because here is what the Ashen Order didn't know, what even Orsen didn't know, what I had kept folded inside forty years of careful bookkeeping like a pressed flower nobody was meant to find:

The map could be surrendered. Maps are just ink and vellum. But the girl — the girl was not cargo. The girl was the *entry*.

And if I gave them the map without telling them that, she might live. If I told them what she was, she'd die in an iron cradle before midwinter, and the last fire in the world would go out with her blood still warm.

You see the arithmetic. I've always been good at arithmetic. It's the sums that cost you something that teach you what you're made of.

* * *

Here is what I traded, so the record is straight, since apparently I'm the only one left who cares about straight records:

I gave them the route she'd take along the pilgrim road — the wheat-country stretch, where the road narrows and a rider can't turn. I gave them Cindermark as her likely shelter, because Orsen is clever and clever people go to cities built on graves, thinking the dead will hide them. I gave them the guild's safehouse signs, three of them, and the name of our factor in the salt-trade, and I watched each item leave me like blood leaving a body, one measured spoonful at a time.

In exchange: the Order's written assurance that the Guild of Impossible Things would be dissolved rather than purged. That my people would walk out with their hands empty and their heads attached. That the map, once recovered, would be burned in a public ceremony and the matter closed.

I believed him. That's the part that shames me most, writing it down. Not the betrayal — betrayal is just commerce conducted in a different currency, and I've never pretended otherwise. What shames me is that I believed a man whose entire faith is built on the proposition that some things must be destroyed *completely*, and who looked at a living girl and called her a thing.

Grand Ashen Vael put out his hand to shake. I took it. His palm was cold and dry, like holding a page.

"She'll be treated honorably," he said.

"I know," I said. "That's what frightens me. Your people treat fire honorably too. They smother it."

* * *

They left at moonrise, and I sat alone in the ransacked counting-house, because my people had sensibly fled and I had sent them off with severance pay and instructions to forget my face, and there was nothing left to do but the one task I had avoided for forty years.

The first ledger sat on my table where Vael had left it. He hadn't taken it. Of course he hadn't. To him it was bait, a lure to confirm what I'd give up. To me it was the oldest thing the guild owned, older than the guild's name, older than its sins.

I poured a drink I didn't taste and opened it to the first page, and I read it aloud, because a confession read silently doesn't count. Everyone knows that.

"*Entry the first.*" My voice sounded strange in the empty room, rusty, like a gate in winter. "*Received into trust: one ember, unspent. Held for the line of Delacroix, fire-sworn, until such time as the dragons wake and call it home.*"

There it was. Her name. On the first page, in ink gone brown as old blood, a century before Orsen was born, a century before I was born. *Delacroix, fire-sworn.* Her family hadn't stumbled into taster's service. They had been *bred* for it — no, worse, they had *agreed* to it, some grandmother of hers standing in front of a dying dragon and swearing her descendants' bodies as the vault where the last fire would be kept in trust. Every poison Orsen swallowed for the crown, every night she poisoned herself to stay useful, and underneath it all her blood was already doing the real work: keeping a banked flame alive in the dark, generation after generation, without one of them knowing.

The crown thought it owned her immunity. The Order thought it was hunting a courier. And the whole time, the girl herself was the Emberhold — the true one, the walking one — and the iron cradle in the mountains was just the decoy the dragons had built to draw the eyes of men.

I turned the pages. Entries in a dozen hands across a hundred years. *One memory, bound in vellum, cartographer Veyra, held against her return.* *Three eggs, cold, unviable — stored, not discarded; the guild does not discard.* *One promise, outstanding, interest accruing.*

Interest accruing. On a promise. We were smugglers of relics and memories and the last eggs of a dying race, and under all of it, in the oldest book, we were *bankers*. Trustees. The guild hadn't been founded by thieves at all. Thieves came later, the way moss comes to a ruin. The founding members had been servants. Sworn ones. And somewhere in the third generation, a Fenwick — my ancestor, my namesake, my particular shame — had decided the trust was more profitable as inventory.

I read until my candle guttered, and then I lit another and read more, and what I was really doing, I suppose, was composing the speech I would deliver to Orsen Delacroix when I found her again. *I knew. I always knew. I let you poison yourself for a crown that was stealing your inheritance, because telling you would have painted a target on you, and I told myself the target was already there, and I told myself a great many things, because I am a keeper of ledgers and ledgers forgive nothing but record everything.*

She would not forgive me. Orsen forgives almost anything except being managed, and managing her was the only thing I had ever done for her.

* * *

Near dawn I found the last entry in Veyra's hand, dated the week before they executed her. The cartographer who drew the map, whose memory lived in its vellum, waiting a century for the right blood to read it. She had written it small, in the margin, the way people write the things they don't want committees to see:

*"If you are reading this, keeper, then the heir has come or is coming. Do not lie to her. Do not manage her. The fire chooses, and it has chosen badly before — it chose us, and look what we did with it. Give her the truth entire and let her be terrible with it. That is the whole of your duty. Everything else is theft."*

I sat back. Outside, the gulls were arguing over the docks, and somewhere down the coast a woman with a talking map was walking into a trap I had drawn for her with my own steady, meticulous hand.

Do not lie to her. Too late. Do not manage her. Far too late. Give her the truth entire —

Well. There was still time for that part. There was, if I rode hard and bribed faster, possibly enough time for exactly that part.

I closed the first ledger and held it a moment, feeling its weight, the weight of a century of small cowardices and one large one. Then I did the only thing left in my power to do honestly: I tore out the page with her name on it — *Delacroix, fire-sworn* — folded it into my coat over my heart, and went to saddle the ugliest horse in the guild's stable, a creature so mean-tempered that even the Ashen Order had declined to requisition her.

Let them have the map. Let them chase ink while the true Emberhold walks the pilgrim road with a head full of a dead cartographer's voice and no idea what she's carrying.

Let them catch her, if they can, before I do. And God help them — God help all of us — if what Veyra wrote in the margin was true, and the fire has finished choosing.

Because I had read enough of the old entries to know what happens next, and the next entry in the first ledger was going to be written in somebody's blood.

I only prayed it wouldn't be hers, and I knew, as I kicked that hateful horse toward the wheat country, that prayer has never once balanced a book.
