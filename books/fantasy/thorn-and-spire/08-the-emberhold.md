## 8. The Emberhold

They did not bind my hands. That was the first insult, and I have been insulted by professionals.

"You won't run," the Grand Ashen had said, not unkindly, the way a physician tells you the fever will break on its own. "You'll want to see it."

He was right, which was worse. Three weeks of flight — through Cindermark's ash-fields, over the salt passes where Veyra's voice had grown thin as winter light — and here at the end of the road I walked willingly into the black throat of the mountain because some traitor part of me *did* want to see it. The last fire. The thing every ledger entry, every smuggled relic, every dying courier's blood had pointed toward.

The Emberhold did not look like a fortress. It looked like a wound that had learned to dress itself. The mountain's flank was shored with iron ribs the size of cathedral beams, rusted orange at the joints, and between them the rock glowed faintly, like a banked hearth seen through a shuttered window. White-cloaks lined the approach in silent ranks, their hoods back, their faces gray with something I recognized from taster's tables: the discipline of people who live near a poison so old they've stopped calling it one.

"Careful of your step," the Grand Ashen said, gesturing me forward across a bridge of black iron. "The floor remembers heat."

I stepped carefully. I resented him for making me grateful.

*Orsen.* Veyra's voice came faint from inside my coat, where the map lay folded against my heart. It had been growing quieter for days, each word arriving with more static, more gaps, like a letter read aloud by someone forgetting the language as they spoke. *It's colder than I remember. When I drew this place, the light came up through the floor.*

"When you drew this place," I murmured under my breath, "you were alive."

*I was a great many things.* A pause, long enough that I thought the memory had frayed through. *I was also wrong about several of them. You'll want to keep that in mind, heir. Everyone who comes to the Emberhold is certain they understand it.*

The Grand Ashen walked beside me without looking at me. He was older than I'd guessed when his hunters dragged me from the wheat — sixty, perhaps, with the lean, dried-out look of a man who has spent decades near heat and given most of his moisture to it. His name, I'd learned from the white-cloaks' careful address, was Serel Vane. He carried no weapon. He didn't need one; he had certainty, which cuts deeper.

"You count things," he said abruptly, as we passed beneath an archway carved with scales — not the coin-weighing kind, but the other kind, vast and overlapping, each scale big enough to roof a cottage. "That's what the guild taught you. Ledgers."

"I keep accounts," I said. "Among other crimes."

"And do you know what the first account ever kept in this place was?" He didn't wait for me to guess. "A debt. One debt, entered once, never closed. Come. It's time you saw the collateral."

* * *

The cradle chamber was round, and cold, and that was the horror of it. Everything about the approach had promised heat — the glowing rock, the iron ribs, the archway of scales — and then we descended a stair of fused glass into a room where my breath fogged, and there it was.

The last dragon-fire burned in an iron cradle the size of a ship's hull, suspended on chains thick as my waist, and it was *dying*. Not roaring, not even burning properly — guttering, the way a candle gutters when the wick is drowned, a low blue-gold shimmer coiled at the bottom of the iron like a snake too tired to strike. The light it gave was barely enough to see the chains by. Frost crept along the cradle's rim.

I had expected awe. What I felt was grief, sudden and specific, the way you grieve a stranger's face in a coffin because it looks like someone you loved.

"Oh," I said. Just that. My voice cracked on it.

"Yes," Serel Vane said quietly. "Everyone says that. Kings said it. Queens. The Order's founders stood exactly where you're standing and wept, and then they did their duty anyway." He moved to the cradle's edge and laid one hand flat against the iron, gently, the way you'd touch a sleeping animal you intended to put down. "Do you know why it's called a cradle, Delacroix? Not a cage. Not a vault. We didn't build it. It was built before us, by people who understood what they were holding."

"A bargain," I said. Cindermark's ash-priests had taught me that much. "Human fire-tasters, bred to hold dragonfire in trust."

"In trust." He turned the phrase over like a coin he suspected was clipped. "Your family held it in trust for four hundred years, and in four hundred years the trust bought nothing but wars. Every time the fire ran strong, someone found a way to spend it. Kingdoms rose on it. Kingdoms burned on it. Your ancestors drank poison nightly so the crown could hold the threat of it over every border it coveted." His eyes found mine, dry and level. "You know what you are. You've known since the wheat field, since you swallowed a paralytic meant to stop a horse and felt it warm you instead. You are the trust's collateral, Orsen Delacroix. The last of it. And the trust is failing."

The fire guttered as he spoke, and I swear — I would swear it on any ledger you like — the whole room dimmed with it, as if the mountain itself were losing blood.

*Heir.* Veyra's voice, very thin now, coming not from the map but from somewhere behind my own eyes. *Don't listen to the arithmetic. He counts only costs.*

"He's counting honestly," I said aloud, before I could stop myself. "That's what frightens me."

Serel Vane raised an eyebrow. "The map speaks?"

"The map remembers." I drew it out and unfolded it, and its ghost-lines glimmered faintly in the dying light, roads and ranges drawn by a dead woman's hand. "Veyra. Cartographer. Executed a century ago for drawing this place too well."

"I know her work." Something moved behind his dryness — respect, maybe, or its fossil. "She understood. She drew the truth and it killed her, and the truth is this:" he turned back to the cradle, and his voice dropped into the register of a man reciting scripture he no longer believed but couldn't put down, "extinguish it, and it ends. Not just the fire. The *wanting*. No kingdom will covet it, because it will not exist. No child will be bred immune to poison, because there will be no poison worth tasting. No Delacroix will sit at a table night after night swallowing death so that men in silk can threaten each other with a flame none of them can hold." He looked at me. "No war can ever burn again. Tell me the flaw in that accounting, taster. You're supposed to be good at finding flaws."

And here is the shameful thing, the thing I set down now because a ledger that hides entries is worthless: I looked for the flaw, and for a long moment I could not find one.

I thought of the banquet halls. The subtle bitterness under the wine, the third course that tasted faintly of almonds, the king watching me over his goblet with that small attentive cruelty, waiting to see if I'd sweat. Fourteen years of being a shield made of meat. I thought of Sable Fenwick, wherever he was — whether he'd sold me or mourned me, and I genuinely did not know which — and of the ledgers full of impossible things: relics pried from tombs, memories bottled and sold, the last dragon-eggs traded like pearls. All of it, all of it, grew from that guttering blue-gold coil in the iron. Cut the root, kill the trade. Kill the wanting.

"No war can ever burn again," I repeated.

"No war can ever burn again," he agreed.

"It's a good line," I said. "You've rehearsed it."

"I've believed it longer than I've rehearsed it. Those are different disciplines." He gestured at the frost creeping along the cradle. "Look at it, Delacroix. It's dying regardless. The question is only whether it dies *used* — whether some crown or guild or clever girl with dragonblood drains the last of it and buys another century of the same war on credit — or whether it dies finished. Whether we let the whole rotten inheritance end cleanly, once, with witnesses." He spread his hands. "I am offering you the one thing no one has ever offered your family. An exit. Put your hand on the iron. The fire knows its own blood — it will come to you, and you can let it go out *choosing*, rather than be extinguished by neglect. There's a dignity in it. More dignity than you've ever been granted."

*Heir.* Veyra again, and her voice was fraying badly now, words dropping out mid-thought, a hem unraveling. *The cradle isn't — the cradle was never a cage, it was a — a* — a long silence, terrible, like hearing a woman forget her own sentence a century after speaking it — *I'm sorry. I had it. I had the whole of it. The fire dims and I dim with it, we were bound, we were always—*

"Veyra." I pressed the map to my chest. "Stay with me."

*Count for me,* she whispered. *You're good at counting. Count what remains.*

So I counted. It's what I do. I stood in the cold before the dying of the world's last wonder and I ran the figures, because figures are honest even when everyone wielding them lies.

Asset: one ember, unspent. Liability: fourteen years of swallowed poison, a courier burned alive on a dock, a cartographer executed, a guildmaster possibly betrayed, a species — whatever dragons were now, whatever slept under Cindermark's grave-city — reduced to collateral and cinders. And the proposed settlement: extinguish the asset, forgive all debts, ensure that no one, ever again, feels what I felt at that banquet table.

It balanced. That was the seduction of it. Serel Vane's arithmetic *balanced*, and I stood there hating him and hating the crown and hating my own treacherous body that warmed at the sight of the fire like a dog hearing its name, and I understood that if I let myself feel anything but the numbers, I would say yes. Ending everything has a logic to it that no argument survives, because it doesn't argue. It just waits, patient as frost, for you to get tired.

"There's a flaw," I said finally.

His expression didn't change. "Tell it."

"You're settling the account," I said, "without asking the creditor." I lifted the map. "Dragons chose to make this bargain. Chose — that's what the ash-priests said, that's what Veyra drew, that's what your own first ledger says if you can still read it. And everything your Order has done for four hundred years has been designed to make sure no one with standing can ever be asked again. You don't want to end the war. You want to end the *negotiation*." My voice shook, and I let it. "Extinguish the fire and you don't free anyone. You just make sure the last word spoken in the conversation is yours."

For the first time, Serel Vane looked tired. "And if the creditor is beyond asking? If all that's left is this—" he touched the iron, and the fire flinched under his palm, guttering lower, lower, and somewhere inside my ribs something guttered with it, "—then the account closes itself, Delacroix. Tonight, perhaps. Look."

I looked. And the frost had reached the center of the cradle's rim, and the blue-gold coil had shrunk to a thread, and Veyra's voice had gone so quiet I had to strain to catch the last of her:

*Orsen. Listen. The cradle was a — it was a promise, not a prison. Fire kept is fire owed. It was always going to choose — it was always going to need someone who — oh, I've lost it. I've lost the middle of it. Keep the ending for me, heir. Whatever you decide — keep the ending—*

And the map in my hands went dark, all its ghost-roads gone, and the silence where a century-dead woman had been talking to me for three weeks was the loudest thing in the mountain.

Grief hit me like a wave hitting a seawall. I stood in it. Then I wiped my face with the back of my hand, because I am a professional, and looked at the thread of fire in the iron cradle, guttering toward a darkness that would take Veyra's ending with it, and every dragon's choice, and my own.

Serel Vane watched me with those dry, patient eyes. "Now," he said softly. "While there's still something left to save. Hand on the iron, taster. End it kindly."

Behind us, far up the glass stair, came a sound like the mountain clearing its throat — boots, many boots, and a voice I knew, ragged and shouting, calling my name.

Sable Fenwick had come. Which meant either rescue or reckoning, and knowing Sable, both.

I put out my hand toward the cradle.
