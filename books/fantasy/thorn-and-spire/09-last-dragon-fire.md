## 9. Last Dragon-Fire

The iron cradle sat at the heart of the Emberhold like a wound that had learned to be furniture.

I had imagined it a hundred ways on the road here — a brazier the size of a cathedral, a forge big enough to swallow armies, some theatrical conflagration worthy of a century of whispered dread. What I found instead was small. A bowl of blackened iron on a stone plinth, holding a flame no larger than my two cupped hands. It guttered. It hesitated. It burned the way a candle burns in a room where someone has just died — stubbornly, and without hope.

The Grand Ashen stood beside it, white cloak spotless in a hall of ash, which I had to admit was a kind of discipline.

"You see," he said, "how little it takes to end it."

I saw. That was the problem. I had spent my whole life being shown things by people who wanted me to draw their conclusions for me. The Crown showed me poisoned goblets and called it duty. The Guild showed me ledgers and called it trust. Now this man showed me a dying flame and called it mercy.

"Extinguish it," he said, "and no war can ever burn again. No dragon wakes. No city falls. No child learns the word *drakefire* except as history. You of all people should understand, taster. You have spent your life swallowing ruin so that others need not taste it. This is the same work. One last sip."

He had rehearsed that speech. I could tell because it had the smoothness of something said aloud many times in an empty room, and I am an expert in empty rooms.

Behind me, Veyra's voice came thin off the map in my satchel, fraying at the edges like a hem worked loose. *"Orsen. Heir. Listen to what he is not saying."*

What he was not saying was this: the fire was not dying because it was old. It was dying because they had starved it. A century of iron and ordinance and careful, pious suffocation. The Emberhold's own records — I had read them on the road, because I read everything; it is the one vice I have never managed to quit — showed the flame burning tall as a man three generations ago. They had not inherited a guttering candle. They had made one, and then built a religion around calling it natural.

"Grand Ashen," I said, "I've been poisoned by professionals. Kings, cardinals, a duchess with a grudge and a very good apothecary. Do you know what they all had in common?"

"I suspect you'll tell me."

"They never once pretended it was kindness. Poisoners are honest that way. It's only the people who order the poison who need it dressed up as mercy." I stepped closer to the cradle. The heat of the little flame touched my face, and something under my skin turned toward it like a plant toward light — slow, involuntary, ancestral. "You want me to choose between keeping it and killing it. Hoard or smother. Those are the only two doors you've built."

"The only two there are."

"No," I said. "Those are the only two you can imagine. Which is a different thing entirely, though I understand how a man in a clean cloak might confuse them."

* * *

Here is what I knew that he did not, because Sable Fenwick kept secrets even from his ledgers, but Veyra did not keep them from me:

The fire was not treasure. It was not weapon. It was *seed*. The bargain struck at Cindermark, the one the ash-priests had described with such grief — human fire-tasters bred to hold dragonfire in trust — had never meant a vault. Trust has two ends. They had spent a hundred years polishing one end and pretending the other didn't exist.

My blood was the other end. Delacroix blood, fire-sworn, bred down through generations of tasters who swallowed poison nightly so that somewhere in the line, in the marrow, in the deep quiet places under the skin, a vessel would survive to matter. My immunity was never the Crown's gift. It was the dragons' loan. And loans, as any guildsman knows, come due.

*"Drink it,"* Veyra said, and her voice cracked halfway through the word like ice on a pond. *"It knows its own blood. It has always known. Drink, heir, and I can finally—"*

She didn't finish. She couldn't. The memory bound into the parchment was tied to the flame; as the fire dimmed, she dimmed. I had watched her lose words over the weeks of travel. First the names of rivers. Then the sound of her own laughter. Then, on the last night before the Order took us, the color of her mother's kitchen. A cartographer executed for drawing a true map, and death had not finished with her — they had let her linger a century in vellum just long enough to guide her killer's heirs' enemies home.

If the flame went out, she went with it. If I hoarded it, she went with it too — hoarded fire feeds nothing, not even ghosts. There was exactly one door left, and it was the one the Grand Ashen's imagination couldn't build because it required him to believe the world could be given away rather than taken.

I pulled off my gloves. Taster's habit — bare hands for the important work. My fingers were steady. I noted that with professional interest, the way I noted everything: pulse calm, breath even, pupils undilated. Whatever else I was, I was still the best poison-handler in the kingdom, and I intended to be excellent at this too.

"Orsen." The Grand Ashen's voice dropped into something less rehearsed. "Don't."

"There it is," I said. "The honest sentence. Took you all night."

I bent over the cradle and drank the fire.

* * *

I don't know how to describe it without lying, so I will lie carefully and tell you it was like drinking light.

It went down my throat and did not stop at the stomach. It went *through* — through the walls of me, through whatever membrane separates a person from the animal warmth of her, down into the place where my grandmother's grandmother's grandmother had banked a coal and told her daughter: *keep this, keep this, keep this.* A hundred years of tasters, each one swallowing the Crown's poisons and passing the ember deeper, each one thinking she was only surviving. None of them knew they were carrying. All of them were.

The flame left the cradle empty. Iron, cold in the space of a heartbeat, and above me the great vaulted dark of the Emberhold seemed to lean in, listening.

And Veyra —

Veyra came home.

I felt her the way you feel rain starting: not a single drop but a change in the whole air. Her memory poured off the parchment and into the current of the fire and into me, and for one impossible moment I was two women. I was Orsen Delacroix, kneeling on cold stone with soot on my lips. And I was Veyra, young, laughing in a kitchen the color of egg-yolks, ink on her fingers, drawing a coastline from memory because the coast itself was already burning — and I understood, suddenly, wholly, why she had bound herself to the map. Not defiance. Not scholarship. She had drawn the way to the fire because someone had to remember where hope was kept, and she volunteered, the way my grandmothers volunteered, generation after generation, to swallow.

*"Thank you,"* she said, from inside my own heartbeat. *"Oh — thank you. I thought I would be alone in there forever."*

"You were never going to be alone," I said out loud, to a hall full of enemies, and meant every word.

Then the Grand Ashen struck me across the temple with the iron poker, and the moment ended.

* * *

I went down hard. The world went to sparks — real ones, now, mine, swimming behind my eyes. Through the ringing I heard him say something about abomination, about the last blasphemy, about how the fire belonged to God and to ash and to silence, and I thought, with the detached clarity of a woman who has been hit before: *he has killed the cradle's keeper, the cartographer, and now he means to kill the cup, and he calls himself the merciful one.*

His boot drew back to kick, and that was when the doors of the Emberhold burst open.

Not soldiers. Not the Order. Thieves.

Sable Fenwick came through the smoke first, silver-haired, immaculate even now, wearing the expression of a man arriving late to a dinner party he had personally catered. Behind him — gods, behind him — the guild. Dock-rats and forgers, smugglers and lockmen, the whole improbable congregation of the Guild of Impossible Things, armed with everything from crossbows to fish-gaffes to, I swear on the ledger, a soup ladle wielded by a woman named Brackish Tess who I once watched forge a document so perfect a cardinal wept.

They had come for me. Sable, who traded my location to save the world, had evidently decided the world could wait its turn.

"Hold the line!" Sable's voice cracked like a whip-crack of pure accounting fury. "Nobody touches the bookkeeper!"

The Grand Ashen turned from me to face them, and I will grant him this: he did not hesitate. White-cloaks poured from the galleries, and the hall became a single terrible arithmetic — bodies against bodies, the guild buying seconds at the price of themselves. I crawled toward the wall, blood in my eye, fire in my veins, watching men and women I had double-entered for years fall among the white cloaks.

And Sable — Sable fought his way *toward* me. Not away. Toward. An old man against an Order, moving like the debt was personal, because it was.

He reached me. He put himself between me and the Grand Ashen, and he spread his arms the way a counting-house door spreads when the last clerk stands behind it.

"Sable," I gasped. "Get *down*—"

"Orsen." He was smiling. Of course he was smiling. "I read your ledgers for eleven years. Best numbers I ever saw. Never once fudged." The Grand Ashen's blade came up. "You're the finest entry this guild ever recorded. Don't you dare write me off as a loss."

The blade came down.

I screamed — and the fire answered.

It came up out of me the way a sneeze comes, involuntary and total, a bloom of gold that filled the hall and threw every fighter, every cloak, every gaffe and ladle backward like leaves in a gale. When my vision cleared, the Grand Ashen lay against the far wall, alive, smoking, disarmed, staring at me with the expression of a man whose theology has just been audited and found fraudulent.

Sable was on the stones. Breathing, barely. His blood on the floor in a shape I refused to look at directly, because I am a bookkeeper, and I know what a final entry looks like, and I was not ready to make this one.

"Tess!" I shouted. "Take him! Everyone out — *now!*"

They moved. The guild does not need to be told twice; it needs to be told once, by someone whose figures are trusted. They carried Sable. They carried their wounded. They carried me, half-walking, half-burning, out through the shattered doors into a night that smelled of snow and cinders.

Behind us, the Emberhold stood hollow. The cradle empty. The last dragon-fire nowhere in the world — except here.

Except in me.

Veyra stirred along my bones like a cat settling in a warm window, and said, softly, in a voice that was becoming my voice: *"Now we go and tell the kings."*

Yes. I thought. Now we do.

I had survived every poison they ever gave me. It was time to return the favor.
