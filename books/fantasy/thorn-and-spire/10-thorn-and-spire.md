## 10. Thorn and Spire

The throne room of Verretholme smelled the same as it always had: beeswax, lilies, and beneath both, faint as a sin, arsenic. I had spent nine years learning to taste that note in everything — in wine, in bread, in the air of rooms where important men made important decisions. Nine years of swallowing what would kill anyone else and smiling about it.

I stood before the Ironrose Throne without kneeling, and the whole court held its breath like a man who has just realized his cup was poured by someone else.

Queen Isolde looked older than she had at autumn's start. The Emberhold had fallen; her Grand Ashen had come home in a box with a white cloth over his face; and somewhere between those two facts, her government had discovered that the woman who tasted her food could now, by every report, breathe light.

"Delacroix," she said. My family name, spoken aloud for the first time in my hearing. Not *taster*. Not *girl*. "You have something of ours."

"There it is," Veyra murmured inside my skull, dry as old ink. *"Of ours." A century of trust-breeding and they still open with possessives.*

I let the silence stretch until it creaked. Courtiers shifted. Somewhere near the pillars, a young lordling's sword-hand trembled, and I noted — the way I note all figures — that half the guards in this room had been reassigned from the eastern garrisons, which meant the Queen expected violence and hoped to avoid witnesses.

"Your Majesty keeps excellent books," I said. "So do I."

I drew the ledger from my satchel. The Guild of Impossible Things' great book, its spine cracked, its pages soft as moth-wing, and on the first page, in Sable Fenwick's careful hand and in the hands of every keeper before him going back four hundred years, the record of everything the world had decided not to remember. Dragons served. Bargains kept. Delacroix, fire-sworn, generation upon generation, entered like livestock into a breeding inventory.

I opened it to the marked page and turned it so the whole court could see.

"Four hundred years ago, your ancestors made an arrangement. Dragonfire, held in trust. Tasters bred to carry the immunity, so the Crown could keep the last ember safe from itself. Every one of my grandmothers swallowed poison on your behalf. Every one of them died of it eventually, because immunity is not infinity — it is a loan drawn against the body." I turned another page. "Here is the interest schedule, Your Majesty. You never read it. You never asked who paid."

"You survived," Isolde said.

"I did. And I brought back the principal." I laid my hand flat against my own sternum, where the fire sat warm as a swallowed sun. "The question is what happens now. You have three options, and I have already priced all of them."

The Queen's jaw tightened. She had been raised on ledgers herself; I could see her doing sums against her will.

"One: you order your archers to loose, and you learn whether arrows stop dragonblood. Spoiler — I've been shot twice since Cindermark. Two: you ask me to hand the fire over, put it back in an iron cradle, appoint a new Order to starve it. That road ends where the last one did, with everything guttering down to nothing and a cartographer's ghost screaming inside the metal. Three—"

"Three," Isolde said, "is whatever you want."

"No." I smiled, and felt Veyra smile with me, and knew it was not a pleasant sight. "Three is what *we* want. Plural. It's a novel concept; bear with me."

*Careful,* Veyra said, but gently. *Don't salt the field before planting.*

I breathed. The fire moved under my ribs like something turning over in sleep.

"The fire will be shared," I said. "Sworn freely, by choice, to those who choose it. Not owned. Not hoarded. Not extinguished. There are people — there are *dragons*, Your Majesty, waking now in the ash-fields of Cindermark and the deep vaults under the Grey Teeth — who will choose their allies, not be chosen for. Your part is simple: you sign, or you don't. If you don't, we proceed without you, and history records which crown stood aside."

"And if I say yes?" she asked quietly. "What does a queen become, in a world where fire picks its friends?"

"An honest one," I said. "For the first time in four hundred years."

She studied me for a long moment. Then, to the shock of every courtier in the room, the Queen of Verretholme laughed — short, tired, real. "My taster," she said, "has better arithmetic than my entire chancellery."

"My taster," I corrected, "*was.*"

* * *

They signed in the great hall, on a table cleared of state dinners, with the ledger open between us. I wrote the heading myself, in the hand I'd learned copying guild entries by candlelight:

**Inheritances Returned.**

Underneath: *One ember, spent freely. One debt, discharged. One name, restored.*

Isolde pressed her seal into wax the color of dried blood, and then — and I confess I did not expect this — she offered me her hand, palm up, the gesture of equals swearing a mutual oath rather than a subject taking a king's.

"I poisoned you for nine years," she said.

"You ordered it done. The cooks were innocent; I checked their ledgers too." I took her hand. "We're even when the last entry clears. Give it thirty years."

Dragons woke slowly, the way spring does — one thaw, then suddenly everywhere. The first rose out of Cindermark's grave-city on the third morning after the signing, wings shedding centuries of ash, and instead of burning anything it landed in the market square and waited, with terrible patience, to see what people would do. What people did was argue, then panic, then bring it soup. The ash-priests wept. The dragon ate the soup. From such small absurdities, peace is built.

Others followed. In the north, a wyrm the color of glacier ice chose a shepherd girl who shared her lunch. In the south, over the Guild's own docks, a sea-drake settled onto the counting-house roof and refused to leave, and Sable's successor — a reformed smuggler named Quill, who cried more easily than any master the guild had ever had — started keeping a separate ledger just for fish.

Ah. Sable.

They buried him at the turn of the season, on the hill above the counting-house, under a stone that read only *He Kept the Books*. The guild argued for weeks about what should be carved underneath, and finally agreed on nothing, which is how he would have wanted it — every keeper adding their own line, year after year, until the stone became a palimpsest. I gave him the first addition: *Forgave himself last, and least.*

Veyra faded through that winter, by degrees, the way a memory does when it stops being needed. On the last night I heard her clearly, she was reading over my shoulder as I entered figures — new columns, sworn allies, returned relics, dragon-names given freely — and she said, in my own voice entirely now:

*"Good cartography maps what is, heir. Better maps draw the road to what might be. You've done the second thing."*

Then she was quiet, and I was alone in my own head, and found that I no longer minded.

* * *

On the anniversary of the courier's death — the burned man who had pressed a map into my hands and called me by blood — I walked the docks at dusk. The counting-house windows glowed gold. Above it, the sea-drake slept in a spiral, breathing fog. In my satchel rode the ledger, and in the ledger, under **Inheritances Returned**, the newest entry still wet:

*A map, fulfilled. One heir, self-made. Account open.*

A girl was waiting by the pilings. Sixteen, maybe. Burn-scarred hands, the particular hollow look of someone who has recently swallowed something that should have killed her and lived. Word travels fast among people like us. We had always existed, it turns out — scattered, unrecorded, immune by accident or by old blood, tasting our way through hostile kitchens and courts. Now they came looking for the ledger.

"You're Delacroix," she said. Not a question. Her voice shook. "They say you keep accounts for impossible things."

"They say correctly." I sat down on the pilings beside her, and the drake above opened one eye, interested. "What's your name, and what can't anyone else count?"

She told me. And it was impossible, and wonderful, and it went into the book — because some inheritances aren't fire. Some are just the truth, finally written down, in a hand steady enough to hold the weight.

Above the harbor, the first stars came out, and below the water, far down in the dark where old things sleep, something vast turned over and began, patiently, to dream of waking.

The ledgers were open. The year had just begun.

And I, Orsen Delacroix, last taster of Verretholme, first keeper of the returned — went inside to do the books.
