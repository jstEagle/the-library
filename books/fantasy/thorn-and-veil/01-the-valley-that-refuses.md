## 1. The Valley That Refuses

The ink ran pale at the edge of the world.

Yara Dunmore watched it happen for the fourth time that morning, his brush hovering above the vellum as the line he'd drawn — a confident, black stroke marking the ridge called the Gray Shoulder — faded from iron-dark to the color of weak tea before his eyes. He dipped the brush again. Redrew. Watched it fade again, slower this time, like a man drowning in shallow water.

"Stubborn," he said to the valley, because there was no one else to say it to, and because after nineteen years of mapping the place he had earned the right to address it like an acquaintance who owed him money.

The valley did not answer. It never did. But it went on refusing him in its own silent way, and Yara had come to understand that this refusal was, in its fashion, a kind of conversation.

He sat on a shelf of granite halfway up the Gray Shoulder, legs dangling over a drop that would kill a careless man, with his instruments laid out around him on a cloth as precisely as a surgeon's tools: the brass theodolite with its cracked leveling bubble; the plumb line weighted with a river stone; three compasses, each made by a different hand in a different year; the ink horn; the brushes; the roll of cured vellum across his knees. Below him the valley unrolled in the long green light of late summer — the Aldern River stitching silver through fields of barley, the village of Lowmark with its smoke rising straight up in the windless morning, the orchards, the millpond, the little stone bridge he had drawn so many times he could render it from memory with his eyes shut.

It was, by any honest measure, a beautiful map waiting to be finished. And it was, by any honest measure, unfinished, and would remain unfinished, because the valley would not let itself be known all the way to its edges.

Yara had discovered this at nine years old, with a child's charcoal and a child's certainty. He had climbed the eastern rim to draw the boundary stream, and found that every time he set the stream to paper, the paper forgot it. Not smudged — forgotten. The charcoal lines simply lost their conviction, paling until they were ghosts of themselves, until the stream on his page was less real than the stream under his boots. He had cried about it, once, briefly, and then — being the sort of child who turned grief into method — he had begun to experiment.

Nineteen years later, the experiments continued.

He tried the compasses now, one after another. The oldest, brass-cased, bought from a tinker who swore it had pointed true through two wars, swung lazily and settled pointing at nothing in particular — or rather, pointing at everything, its needle describing a slow, indecisive arc, like a man trying to bow to an entire room at once. The second compass held steady but lied, insisting north lay somewhere over the western rim, which Yara knew for a fact was wrong, because he had walked to the western rim forty times and taken the sun's height at noon every single visit. The third compass — his own work, its needle cut from a sewing needle magnetized against his grandmother's old lodestone — did something worse than lie or waver. It trembled. It stood on its point of balance and shivered, fine as a struck harp string, as though it could feel something at the valley's edge that it had no vocabulary to describe.

Yara closed the lid on that one first.

"Instruments fail," he murmured, writing it in the margin of his field book in his small, exact hand. *Instruments fail. Ink fades. Charcoal forgets. Observation holds only while observer remains within sight of the thing observed.* He paused, then added, because honesty was a discipline like any other: *Conclusion unchanged after nineteen years. The valley is not unmapped because no one has tried. The valley is unmappable.*

And then, beneath that, smaller, in the cramped script he used for thoughts he wasn't sure he wanted to keep: *Or I am doing it wrong.*

* * *

He ate his lunch on the granite shelf — bread, hard cheese, a pear from his own scrawny tree — and let himself do the thing he only allowed himself up here, alone, where no one from Lowmark could see his face while he did it. He looked outward.

Beyond the valley's rims, the world rose in blue ridges, range behind range, fading into haze the color of diluted milk. Somewhere out there was the King's Road. Somewhere out there were cities he had never seen except as rumors brought back by the autumn caravans: Vellaster with its hundred bridges, the salt markets of Corrinne, the great library at Ambresford where, the caravan men said, they kept maps of the whole world drawn on skins the size of sails. Somewhere out there was the mountain — not the Gray Shoulder, not any of the friendly worn hills of home, but the Mountain, the one the old songs mentioned only sideways, the one Maren the herbwoman would not name even when she was deep in her cups, saying only, *There are courts and there are courts, lad, and you want nothing to do with the kind that sits under stone.*

Yara had drawn all of it anyway. That was the secret shame of his life, or one of them: the drawer beneath his bed at home, locked, filled with maps of places he had never been. Vellaster rendered from a caravan man's drunken description, corrected three times when the man came through again and contradicted himself. The coast, extrapolated from a sailor's tattoo. The Mountain, drawn eleven different ways, each version crossed out and begun again, because rumor was a terrible surveyor and fear was worse.

He was thirty-one years old and he had never been farther from his own door than the Gray Shoulder, and some mornings — most mornings — this felt like wisdom. This morning it felt like a cage with a very good view.

Because here was the truth he circled without landing on, the way his third compass circled without settling: the valley refused to be mapped, and Yara Dunmore had built his entire life around the attempt, and if he ever admitted the attempt was futile, he would have to admit something else too. That the futility was comfortable. That the wall at the valley's edge — whatever it was, whatever caused the ink to fade and the needles to tremble — was also a wall around him, and he had helped maintain it, stone by careful stone, year by contented year.

*Restless contentment,* his grandmother would have called it, if she'd lived to see it. She'd had a phrase for everything. *You're like a dog, Yara, that guards a gate it's afraid to walk through.*

He finished the pear and got back to work, because work was the answer to everything, or at least the postponement of every other question.

* * *

The afternoon he spent walking the rim southward, pacing distances with a knotted cord and taking sightings back toward the church spire at Lowmark, and everywhere he went the valley performed its quiet treachery.

At the southern rim, where the land stepped down in terraces to a hanging meadow, he measured the meadow at four hundred and twelve paces across. His field book said four hundred and six. He remeasured. Four hundred and twelve. He checked his cord against the church spire sighting, ran the trigonometry twice in the margins, and got four hundred and six both times. The meadow was two different sizes depending on how you asked it, and the meadow did not care, and somewhere below, the barley went on ripening as though the ground itself were not lying to him.

He laughed — one short, sharp bark of a laugh that startled a hawk off the crag above him. Nineteen years. Nineteen years of this. Any sane man would have quit at year five, or ten, or fifteen, would have settled for a good-enough map with a decorative border and a cartouche reading *Here Ends the Known World*, and gone home to marry and plant turnips.

But then, sanity was a matter of degrees, and Yara had always suspected his ran a few degrees shy of the valley's own.

What kept him at it — what had always kept him at it — was not really the maps. Or not only the maps. It was the principle of the thing. A world that could not be known was a world that could not be trusted, and a world that could not be trusted was a world where anything might be taken from you without warning, without reason, without even the courtesy of a rule. His parents had taught him that much, in the way of parents who die early and leave their lessons written in absence: know your ground. Know it exactly. Because the day you don't is the day it shifts under you.

They had died when he was seven. Fever, the herbwoman said, though Maren had been young then herself and Yara had sometimes wondered, in the way of children, whether adults simply chose the nearest word for things they didn't understand. He remembered almost nothing of them. A voice humming. Hands. The smell of lamp oil and crushed mint. His mother's face — he had loved her, he must have, but her face was a blank in his memory, a smooth oval where features should have been, and this bothered him more than he ever said aloud, because what kind of cartographer loses the map of his own mother's face?

He shook the thought off the way you shake off a fly, and climbed down from the southern rim as the light began to go long and gold.

* * *

By the time he reached the valley floor, the shadows of the western hills had swallowed the barley fields whole, and the first stars were pricking through above the Gray Shoulder. He took the riverside path home, past the millpond where frogs were tuning up for the evening, past the orchards heavy with early apples, and let the familiar dark settle around him like a coat.

This was the hour he loved best, and he knew it, and knowing it did not spoil it. The valley at dusk was the one version of the valley that never argued with him. In the dark, distances collapsed into suggestion. The river became a sound instead of a measurement. Everything softened into the kind of truth that didn't need to be exact.

His house stood at the valley's northern edge, half a mile from the last cottage in Lowmark, close enough for company and far enough for silence. He had chosen it for that distance, or told himself he had. It was a low stone house with a slate roof and a garden going pleasantly to seed, and inside it were his worktable, his locked drawer, his shelves of field books — twenty-two of them now, each spine dated in his precise hand — and the great map of the valley, six feet by four, pinned to the wall of the main room, which he had been correcting for nineteen years and which was still, in the corners, in the margins, at the edges, wrong.

He lit the lamp. He hung his wet cloak. He set water on for tea, and while it heated he stood in front of the great map with his arms crossed, the way a general stands before a campaign table, and studied the enemy.

The center of the map was beautiful. He could admit that without false modesty; modesty was for men with something to hide. The Aldern River ran through it in a line of perfect confidence, every bend surveyed, every ford named. The villages, the roads, the bridges, the mill, the orchard boundaries, the little symbols for wells and shrines and standing stones — all of it drawn in ink that had stayed black for two decades, all of it *true*, verified and reverified, the plain record of a plain country.

And then the edges. The edges were the wound in the thing. Where the map approached the valley's rims, his lines grew hesitant. Fainter. Some of the outermost contours he had redrawn so many times the vellum had gone soft and gray there, furred like old cloth, the paper itself tired of holding what it could not hold. In the northeast corner, where the boundary stream of his childhood still defeated him, there was simply a region of pale wash, shapeless, annotated in his smallest handwriting: *Ink will not take. See field books IX, XIV, XVII–XXII. Cause unknown.*

Cause unknown. Nineteen years, and those two words were the sum of his learning.

He thought, not for the first time, about leaving. About rolling the great map, locking the drawer, shouldering his pack, and walking out along the King's Road to see whether the rest of the world's ink behaved better than his. He thought about it the way a man thinks about swimming a cold river: knowing he could, doubting he should, aware that the far bank would change him in ways the near bank never would.

Because that was the other thing, the thing under the thing. The valley's refusal wasn't only aimed at his instruments. He had noticed it years ago, quietly, and never written it down anywhere, because writing it down would make it real: the valley refused *him* gently, too. Kept him. Held him the way a hand holds a moth — not cruelly, but completely. When he stood at the rim and looked out at the blue ranges, something in his chest pulled backward, some gravity deeper than fear, whispering that beyond the edge there was nothing he needed, that here was enough, that here was safe, that here —

Here was where he belonged, said the pull, in a voice that sounded almost like his own, and Yara had learned to distrust any voice that flattered him into staying.

He poured his tea. He sat at his worktable and opened field book XXII to today's entry and wrote, in order, the meadow discrepancy, the compass readings, the fading ink, the trembling needle. Then he sat back and looked at the last line, the one about the needle, and read it over three times.

*Trembles continuously at the rim. Not wavering — trembling. As if afraid. As if listening.*

He had written *as if* twice, which was once more than scientific rigor allowed, and he left both instances standing, because tonight he had no better words and no patience to find them.

Outside, the moon was coming up over the eastern rim — a thin new moon, a paring of silver, the first night of its newest turning. Yara noticed it idly, the way you notice weather, and gave it no more thought than that. He counted moons the way farmers did, for planting and for the caravan seasons, and if this one marked some beginning, some clock starting quietly in the dark hills, he had no instrument that could have told him so.

He banked the lamp, checked the bolt on the door — a habit, though no one in Lowmark had locked a door in living memory — and went to bed in the small room above the worktable, where he could hear, if the wind was right, the river talking to itself all night long.

He was nearly asleep when the sound came.

A knocking. Faint, irregular, from the direction of his front door — but that couldn't be right, because no one came up this road at night, and besides, the knocking was wrong somehow, too soft, too slow, the knocks spaced like the beats of a heart that was having to remember how.

Yara lay still in the dark, listening, telling himself it was a branch, the shutter, the river.

The knocking came again. Patient. Small.

He got up, took the poker from beside the hearth because courage is easier with iron in your hand, and went down the stairs into the dark.
