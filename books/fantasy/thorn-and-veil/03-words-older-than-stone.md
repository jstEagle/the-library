## 3. Words Older Than Stone

The boy slept in my chair.

I had given him the bed, of course — or tried to. He would not take it. He had stood beside it for the better part of an hour, mud still drying in the creases of his neck, looking at the blanket as though it were a document written in a language he could not read, and then he had walked to my chair by the window and folded himself into it like a letter being returned to its sender. So the chair was his, and I sat up through the small hours at my worktable with a candle burning down to a stub, drawing nothing.

Drawing nothing was itself remarkable. In thirty-one years I had not once sat before blank vellum with no intention behind my hand. But every attempt to render the boy — his face, his hands, even the shape he made in the chair — came out wrong. Not badly drawn. *Wrong.* My ink ran pale where it should have run black, as if the paper were drinking it faster than I could pour, and the lines wandered off true. I had burned three sheets before I understood what my hands already knew: that some subjects refuse to be mapped, and this child was one of them.

Outside, the valley did its nightly work of refusing. The wind came down from the rimwall and died at the tree line; the river forgot its own name somewhere past the mill; and over it all hung that quality the valley had, the one I had spent my life circling and never once named aloud — the sense of a held breath. A room someone had left on purpose, closing the door behind them.

The boy woke once, near the coldest hour, and spoke.

Not to me. To the window, perhaps, or to whatever stood beyond it that only he could see. The words were low and rounded and utterly without meaning to my ear, five syllables rising and falling like water finding its level, and when they ended he looked at me — directly, deliberately, the way a messenger looks at the person for whom the message is intended — and waited.

"I don't understand you," I said.

Something moved across his face that was not quite disappointment. Recognition, maybe. As if he had expected nothing else and had simply needed to confirm it.

He slept again after that. I did not. When the light came up gray through the frost on my windows, I dressed, banked the fire, and went to find Maren.

* * *

Maren Ossel lived a mile downriver, past the last working field, in a house that leaned eastward the way old trees lean away from the prevailing wind. She was the nearest thing our valley had to a scholar, which is to say she was the only person in it who had ever admitted to wanting more than the valley contained. Sixty years of herbs and tinctures and bonesetting had taught her hands patience, and sixty years of listening at the edges of things had taught her ears suspicion.

She was in her garden when I arrived, cutting winter sage with a knife that had gone silver-thin from decades of sharpening. She took one look at my face and one look at the small figure trailing me — because the boy had refused to stay behind, had planted himself at my heel like a shadow with opinions — and set down the knife.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you."

"Maren—"

"Yara Dunmore, you have the look of a man who has been awake all night arguing with himself and losing. Sit."

We sat. She poured something dark and bitter into three cups, and the boy wrapped both hands around his and drank without hesitation, which told me something about how long it had been since anyone had offered him anything warm.

Then Maren crouched in front of the boy, bringing herself down to his height the way you'd approach a skittish animal, and said, gently: "Where did you come from, little one?"

He looked at her. And he answered.

The words poured out of him — a flood this time, not the five syllables of the night, but whole sentences, urgent and tumbling, his hands moving too, shaping shapes in the air that seemed to mean something alongside the sounds. It lasted the length of a held breath. Then he stopped, chest heaving, and looked between us with the terrible patience of someone who has said everything important and is waiting for the world to catch up.

Maren had gone very still.

"Maren?" I said.

"Say something else," she whispered to him. Not in our tongue — in his. Or rather, she made the attempt: two syllables, rough and uncertain, shaped by a mouth that had not spoken them in fifty years.

The boy's eyes went wide. He scrambled forward on his knees and gripped her wrists, and the words came again, fast, and Maren closed her eyes and listened the way you listen to music — letting it move through you rather than trying to hold it.

When he finished, she opened her eyes, and I saw that they were wet.

"That's not possible," I said.

"No," Maren agreed. "It isn't." She eased herself onto the bench beside me, and her hands, those patient hands, were trembling. "Yara. Do you know what I did before I married Ossel? Before I came up this valley?"

"You were a hedge-healer on the far side of the pass. You've told me. You gathered simples for the mining camps."

"I gathered simples," she said, "and I listened. Camps are full of every kind of tongue. And there was an old woman there, a digger's widow, who used to sing to herself in the evening. Songs nobody knew. I asked her once where they came from, and she told me — laughed when she told me — that they were older than the kingdoms. Older than the roads. They were the speech people used before kings learned to write their names on other people." She nodded toward the boy. "That is what he speaks. Or the bones of it. Fifty years of forgetting, and I caught maybe one word in ten. But I caught enough."

"What did he say?"

Maren was quiet a long moment. The garden ticked around us, frost releasing its grip on the sage leaves as the sun found them.

"He asked whether we knew," she said slowly, "whether we had been told. He asked it the way you'd ask about weather. As if it should be obvious to everyone." Her jaw tightened. "And then he said — I think this is right, Yara, I think I have this right — he said *they count.*"

"They count?"

"The speakers of that tongue. That's the word she used, the widow. *Counted.* Not counted the way you'd count sheep. Counted the way a moneylender counts. Owned, entered in a book, owed." Maren turned to look at me fully, and I saw something in her face I had never seen there in twenty years of knowing her: fear. Old fear, worn smooth by handling, the way a stone worn smooth by a river. "She said her grandmother's people sold themselves, or were sold — the story had rotted by the time it reached her — and that the debt never dies. It sleeps. And it passes down."

The boy was watching us speak, tracking our faces, understanding none of it and all of it. His hands had gone quiet in his lap.

"Maren," I said carefully, "you're telling me a campfire story."

"I'm telling you what an old woman sang, and what a mute child confirmed this morning by asking us if we'd been told." She stood, abruptly, joints cracking, and began moving among her drying racks with unnecessary energy, touching bunches of herbs she clearly wasn't seeing. "You want me to tell you it's nothing. A lost language, a stray boy, a sad tale from the hills. And I would like to tell you that. I have liked, these sixty years, living in a valley where the worst thing that happens is a bad harvest and my own stubborn knees."

"But."

"But he didn't wander here, Yara." She stopped, her back to me, her hand flat against the doorframe of her drying shed. "Nothing wanders here. Nothing crosses the rimwall by accident — you know that better than anyone, you who've spent your life mapping the refusal of this place. He came here *on purpose*. Someone sent him, or something drove him, and either way—" she turned, and her voice dropped to nearly nothing, "—either way he crossed out of a world we don't map into the one place under the sky where nothing can follow him. Think about that. Think about what has to be chasing a child before hiding in a valley that refuses magic seems like the safe choice."

The words landed in my chest like stones dropped into deep water.

Because I had thought it. All night, watching him sleep in my chair, I had thought it: *he didn't come here despite what this valley is. He came here because of it.*

"How do you know he can't be followed here?" I asked. "If your widow's tales are true — if something owns him—"

"Because the valley doesn't allow debts," Maren said simply. "Whatever wall stands at our borders, whatever my grandmother's grandmother put up their prayers against, magic cannot live here. And if this counting, this owning, this ledger-keeping — if it runs on anything beyond bread and blood and cruelty, then it stops at the tree line like everything else does." She looked at the boy, and her voice gentled in a way that frightened me more than the rest of it. "In here, little one, whatever holds your leash goes slack. Out there—" she glanced at me, "—out there it pulls."

The boy rose. He walked to me — not to Maren, who had spoken his tongue, but to me, the stranger whose words meant nothing to him — and he took my hand in both of his small ones and turned it palm-up, studying it with the focus of a craftsman. Then he pressed his own palm against mine, held it there a moment, and released.

I don't know what he meant by it. I know that my hand stayed warm the rest of the day.

* * *

We stayed until afternoon. Maren fed us barley soup and questioned the boy in her broken fragments of the old tongue, and he answered as much as he could, patiently, the way you'd speak to a deaf aunt — repeating, gesturing, simplifying. Most of it dissolved between them like sugar in rain. But pieces surfaced, and each piece Maren fished out of the current made her grayer.

"There's a turning," she told me quietly, while the boy examined her shelves of jars with the reverence of one who understands collections. "He keeps saying it. Turning, turning. I think it means the moon. He holds up his fingers — see, like that — and he can't make the number. His fingers won't go high enough, or he doesn't know how to say it. But he counts anyway, on his other hand, tapping." She mimicked it: tap, tap, tap, a small finger striking her knee in rhythm. "Like something winding down."

"Winding down toward what?"

"I don't know." She met my eyes. "But he says the word *owned* the way you'd say *raining*. Present tense, Yara. Not something that happened. Something happening."

I walked home in the blue hour with the boy's hand tucked in mine — he had taken it without asking, somewhere on the path, and I had let him — and I felt the valley close around us the way it always did, that held breath, that shut door, and for the first time in my life the feeling of safety it gave me felt less like peace and more like the pause between lightning and thunder.

At my door I knelt to unlace his mud-stiffened boots, and he let me, watching me with grave attention. And as I worked the knot free he spoke again — softly, just once, the five syllables from the night before, the ones he'd said to the window.

I looked up. "What does that mean?"

He touched my chest. Then he pointed, out the window, eastward — eastward, where beyond the rimwall and the ranges and a thousand miles of unmapped country, the old songs said there stood a mountain.

Then he drew his finger slowly across his own throat, and looked at me, and waited for me to understand.

I did not sleep that night either. I lit every lamp I owned and pulled down every folio, every fragment of rumor-geography I'd ever inked from travelers' hearsay, and I searched them for the mountain while the moon rose thin and new over the rimwall — a sliver, barely born, beginning its first night of counting.

Behind me, in my chair, the boy watched it through the glass. And once, very quietly, so softly I might have imagined it, he tapped his finger against the armrest.

Tap.

Counting.
