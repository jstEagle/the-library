## 4. The Mark Beneath the Skin

The boy slept like something buried.

Yara had watched him through the small hours from the doorway of the back room, a candle guttering in his hand, and the image would not leave him: not a child sleeping but a child *stowed*, folded away somewhere deep beneath his own skin, waiting to be collected. The boy lay on Yara's spare cot with his knees drawn up and his fists curled under his chin, mud still dried in the creases of his neck, wearing one of Yara's old shirts that hung off him like a sail off a mast. He had eaten three bowls of barley stew without looking up, as though someone might take it from him mid-bite, and then he had walked to the cot on his own, lain down on top of the blanket rather than under it, and gone still.

Not asleep-asleep. Yara knew sleep. He had slept beside strangers in waystations across half the valley's trade roads, drawn their faces from memory afterward, catalogued the way exhaustion rearranged people. This was different. The boy's breathing stayed shallow and even, too even, the rhythm of someone listening. His eyes moved behind his lids — quick, darting movements, like a man reading by candlelight who keeps checking the door.

*He is in there,* Yara thought, and did not know why the thought frightened him so much. *Whatever he is, he is in there, behind glass.*

He set the candle on the shelf and went to his desk, because that was what he did when the world refused to make sense: he made sense of it in ink. He drew the boy's face from memory while it was fresh — the sharp little chin, the burn-scars on both forearms, old ones, healed crooked, the kind you got from grabbing things you'd been told not to touch. He drew the eyes and found he could not get them right. Not because they were unusual in color or shape, but because of what was *in* them, which ink had no symbol for. A boy who had looked at Yara's house the way a drowning man looks at a shoreline.

Then he turned the page and began, out of habit older than thought, to sketch a map of where the boy might have come from. The valley's eastern rim, past the slate quarries, past the standing stones that shepherds avoided after dusk. His pen moved easily for a moment — and then, as always at that latitude of paper, the lines went thin and gray, the ink fading as if the paper were drinking it. The same failure he'd known all his life. The valley's edge, where his instruments spun and his maps went blind.

Except tonight, for the first time, Yara wondered if the blindness was not in the land at all.

He looked up at the doorway. The boy stood there.

Yara hadn't heard him cross the room. There should have been floorboard-creak; there was always creak; the house announced every visitor whether he wanted it to or not. But the boy simply *was*, standing barefoot in the doorframe with the candlelight pooling around his ankles, watching Yara with those unreadable eyes.

"You should be sleeping," Yara said, and felt immediately foolish — the boy hadn't spoken a word of common tongue since he'd arrived babbling that impossible language at his doorstep. Maren would come at first light; she knew more old tongues than anyone in the valley. Until then they had been communicating in gestures, in stew, in the universal language of two wary creatures deciding, slowly, not to bite each other.

The boy didn't answer. He crossed the room instead — silently, silently, that wrongness again — and climbed onto the chair across the desk from Yara, kneeling on it so his face was level with the map. He studied the faded lines where the valley ended. Then he reached out one finger and pressed it against the blank space beyond the boundary.

And spoke.

It was only a word, or what Yara took to be a word — low and resonant, two syllables that seemed to arrive not through the ears alone but through the floorboards, through the desk wood, through Yara's own breastbone. The candle flame leaned sideways though there was no draft. On the wall, the shadow of the boy's outstretched finger did not match the finger; it stretched longer than it should, thinner, ending in a point like a quill nib.

Yara sat very still. Every instinct honed by thirty years of solitary precision told him to stay calm, observe, record — and underneath that instinct, older and less civilized, another voice said plainly: *run.*

The boy withdrew his hand. The shadow shrank back to its proper shape. He looked at Yara, and his expression was not fear, exactly. It was apology. It was the look of someone who has shown a stranger something broken about themselves and expects to be sent away for it.

"No," Yara heard himself say. "You're not — I'm not sending you anywhere."

The boy tilted his head. He didn't understand the words, Yara realized. He understood the tone. Perhaps that was all either of them had tonight.

Yara pulled a fresh sheet toward him and, slowly, deliberately, began to draw. Not the valley. Not anything real. He drew a simple picture — a house, a door, a stick figure standing inside the threshold with its arm extended inward, palm open. Invitation rendered in cartographer's line-work. He turned it around on the desk.

The boy looked at the drawing for a long time. Then he did something that cracked Yara open in a way he could not have predicted: he reached across and touched the stick figure's open palm with one finger, carefully, as though the ink might be warm.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of rain that wouldn't fall. Maren arrived before Yara had finished the porridge, letting herself in with the ease of forty years' friendship, her basket of herbs swinging, her sharp old eyes finding the boy at the table in an instant.

"So it's true, then," she said. "Half the village says you've taken in a ghost. Other half says a thief."

"He's neither." Yara ladled her a bowl. "He came to my door at dusk yesterday, covered in mud, speaking—" He stopped. How to describe it? "A language I've never heard. And I've copied dictionaries of dead tongues for the atlas appendices. This wasn't any of them."

Maren sat down heavily. She ate in silence for a while, watching the boy over the rim of her spoon. The boy watched her back, unblinking, unafraid — and that itself was strange, Yara thought. Children who had survived whatever this child had survived usually flinched from new adults. This boy assessed. Weighed. Like a customs officer at a border.

"Say something for me, love," Maren said gently, in the coaxing tone she used on sick animals and stubborn farmers alike.

The boy looked at Yara. Yara nodded — he didn't know why, some permission being asked and given — and the boy opened his mouth and let fall a short phrase of that other speech. It sounded, Yara thought, the way riverbed stones sounded under water. Round. Old. Weighted.

Maren's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

She set it down with exaggerated care, the way people do when they are afraid their hands will shake. "Again," she whispered.

He said it again.

The herbwoman closed her eyes. Her lips moved, shaping sounds, testing them, discarding them. When she opened her eyes they were wet and fierce at once, and she looked at Yara the way she looked at a wound that had gone bad beneath clean skin.

"That's not a language of any kingdom," she said. "That's not a language of any kingdom *because it's older than kingdoms.* My grandmother sang fragments of it — lullabies she claimed came from before the walls went up, before the First Partition, words her grandmother said the old folk used when they bargained with the earth directly. Nobody speaks it anymore." She paused, and her voice dropped. "Nobody *can* speak it anymore. Because everyone who carried it was owned. Bought. The tongue died out down here because the speakers kept getting sold upward — or downward, depending how you count."

"Bought," Yara repeated. The word sat wrong in his stomach. "Bought by whom?"

Maren didn't answer. She was looking at the boy again, and now her gaze traveled downward, slow and deliberate, from his face to his throat to his collarbone — where the neckline of Yara's borrowed shirt gaped wide, as it did on all children, showing the hollow of his chest.

"Maren?"

"Turn around, love," Maren said softly to the boy. "Let me see your chest. Just your collar. Nothing to be afraid of."

The boy glanced at Yara once more — that checking, that asking-permission — and then obediently tugged the shirt's collar aside and turned.

There, below his left collarbone, in the hollow above his small heart, was a mark.

Yara had assumed, in the candlelit hours of the night, that the shadows on the boy's skin were dirt. Mud caked everything else; why not there? But mud washes off, and this had not washed, because it was not mud. It was a sigil — a seal of fine dark lines sunk *into* the skin, not scarred over it, the way frost flowers form inside the surface of a windowpane. Intricate. Deliberate. A circle of interlocking script in that same pre-kingdom tongue, ringed around a central glyph like a keyhole, and threaded through the whole design a single unbroken line that coiled outward from the center and simply... stopped, at the edge of the mark, the way a road stops at the border of an unmapped country.

The skin around it was pale and smooth. No swelling. No scab. Whatever had put that mark there had done it cleanly, expertly, the way a bookbinder stamps a patron's crest into leather.

"It's a contract," Maren breathed. Her voice had gone flat and hard, which Yara had heard perhaps twice in his life, both times over graves. "A bill of sale, written into the flesh. That's the old script — that's *their* script, the brokers' script, the courts below the mountains used it before anyone above ground admitted they existed. The ring of words is the terms. The keyhole is the claimant's seal." Her finger hovered above the boy's chest without touching, tracing the unbroken coil. "And that line — that's the tether. It runs from the mark to wherever the debt is held. It doesn't end here. It ends *there*. In the boy, and out of the boy, like a leash with no visible far end."

The kitchen was very quiet. Rain finally began against the windows, soft as sifted flour.

"What does it mean," Yara said, and his own voice sounded foreign to him, low and rough, "practically? What has been done to him?"

Maren looked at him for a long moment, and Yara saw her deciding — saw the whole calculation pass behind her eyes, the same calculation she made before telling families the true name of the sickness killing them.

"I told you my grandmother's lullabies were in that tongue. What I didn't tell you is why she learned them. She learned them from her sister, who was marked like this one. Sold as an infant to settle a village's debts. The mark isn't decoration, Yara. It's ownership. The child's body walks around free, but the *soul* — the soul is collateral. Held elsewhere. Pledged. And when the contract comes due—" Maren's jaw tightened. "When it comes due, the holder collects. The body stays. Walking, breathing, eating, sleeping. Empty. My grandmother's sister lived sixty years like that. The village called her quiet Wren. She'd smile at festivals because smiling was left in the body. But nobody was home."

Yara became aware that he had risen from his chair. He didn't remember standing. He was across the room, and his hand was hovering near the boy — not touching, the way Maren's hadn't touched — and there was a sound in his own ears like blood moving fast.

"Can it be undone?" he asked.

"I don't know."

"There must be someone who knows. Someone who's seen these marks. Someone—"

"Yara." Maren said his name the way she dosed bitter medicine, precisely and without mercy. "Sit down. Listen to me. Marks like this don't happen here. They haven't happened here in living memory. Which means whoever sold this child brought him from outside — or sold him *to* outside, and he ran, and ran, and ran, until his feet carried him to the only place in the world where the thing holding his soul can't easily reach." She gestured around the kitchen, at the walls, at the fog-wrapped window, at the whole gray valley beyond. "Here. To the valley that refuses. Think about what that means. Something hunted that boy across every mile between here and wherever he was sold, and the one direction it couldn't follow was *toward us*."

Yara looked at the boy. The boy was still turned, still holding his collar aside, patient as an exhibit — and that patience was the worst part, Yara thought. The terrible practiced patience of a child who has been inspected many times and has learned that stillness makes inspections end faster.

"Pull your shirt closed, lad," Yara said quietly. "You don't have to show anyone anything ever again unless you choose to."

The boy blinked. He didn't understand the words. But he understood — Yara was beginning to understand that he always understood — the change in the voice. He let the collar fall and turned around, and climbed back onto his chair, and picked up his spoon, and went on eating his porridge as if the last five minutes had been about weather.

But his eyes stayed on Yara. Watching. Measuring. The customs officer at the border, deciding whether the traveler carrying him was friend or freight.

* * *

That afternoon, Yara did something he had never done in thirty years of methodical, orderly life.

He cleared his desk of everything except a single sheet of the good vellum, ground fresh ink, and began to draw the boy. Not his face this time. All of him. A full-figure rendering, precise as any survey, and beside each detail he wrote observations in his cramped field-hand: *scars, both forearms, cauterized, old. Gait silent — abnormally so. Speech: unknown tongue, archaic. Mark: sigil, sternal, contractual.*

He worked until the light failed. He worked because the alternative was sitting with the fury, and fury was not a thing Yara Dunmore permitted himself; fury was imprecise; fury smudged lines. But the fury was there anyway, sitting in his chest like a second heartbeat, growing with every careful stroke of the pen. Some stranger — some *institution*, because that mark had not been made in rage or madness, it had been made with craft, with ledgers, with the calm of people who do such things daily — had taken a child's soul the way a landlord takes a harvest, had stamped him like cargo, had leashed him like a beast, and had then presumably filed the paperwork and gone to supper.

And the boy had run. Barefoot, burned, mute with terror, through mud and night and whatever distance lay between here and the broker's door, following some instinct older than language toward the one place in the world that magic couldn't enter — and had pounded on the door of a lonely mapmaker whose compass, Yara remembered with a chill, had spun wild the moment he first touched the child.

Spun wild. At the valley's edge his needle merely trembled. Against this boy it had whirled like a dying bird.

*Why?* Yara set down his pen and stared at the finished drawing. *What are you, little one, that the instruments scream at your touch?*

A knock at the outer door — Maren's particular knock, two raps and a pause — broke the silence. Yara rose to answer it, and behind him, softly, the boy slid off his chair and followed, staying just at Yara's heel, close enough to touch.

Outside, the fog had thickened into something almost solid. Maren stood on the step with her basket clutched in both hands, and she was not alone. Behind her, half-lost in the mist, waited two of the quarrymen from the far settlement — men Yara had never seen walk this far east in his life — and their faces, in the gray light, wore expressions he recognized from his own mirror that morning.

Fury. And beneath it, worse, fear.

"Yara," Maren said, and her voice shook despite her obvious effort to steady it. "We need to talk about that mark. Because Bram from the quarry camp — his grandfather kept papers, old ones, forbidden ones, from the time before the veil. And Bram says—" she swallowed— "Bram says there's a ledger entry. A sale recorded. And it names this valley as the place the debt will be collected."

She stepped closer, fog curling around her shoulders.

"And it gives a date, child. Thirteen turnings of the moon. Twelve remain."
