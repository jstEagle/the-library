## 5. What the Ledger Says

The sigil would not stay still.

Maren had seen that once before, forty years gone, on the wrist of a drover's wife who came to her hut asking for something to quiet dreams. The mark there had been a simple thing — a circle, a line through it, the kind of glyph a hedge-witch might scratch into a doorpost against ill luck. But it had moved. Slowly, the way a snail moves, so slowly you could watch an hour and swear it hadn't shifted and then find it two fingers from where you'd last looked. The woman had died in winter. Maren had told herself many stories about what else it might have been.

She was not going to tell herself stories tonight.

"Hold the lamp higher," she said.

Yara Dunmore did as she asked without argument, which was itself remarkable. She had known him since he was a boy with ink-stained knuckles and a habit of correcting people's pronunciations of valley places, and he argued the way other men breathed. But tonight his mouth stayed shut, and his hands were careful, and when the boy flinched at the lamp's heat Yara turned it away a little, shielding the child with his own arm without seeming to notice he'd done it.

The boy sat stripped to the waist on Maren's stool, small and thin-boned, his ribs like the strakes of a half-built boat. Mud still clung in the creases of his neck despite the basin of water they'd given him. He did not shiver, though the night was cold enough that Maren's own joints sang. He simply sat, watching her with those dark, patient eyes, the way livestock watch a storm come over the ridge — not afraid, exactly. Waiting to learn whether this was weather worth fearing.

Maren pressed her thumb gently beneath his collarbone, where she had seen the shine of something wrong when she'd helped him out of his ruined shirt.

There it was.

Not a brand — she'd thought at first glance it was a brand, but brands scar, and scars are dead things, and this was not dead. It sat in the skin like a coin sunk in wax: a seal the size of her thumbnail, its lines too fine for any iron her village smiths could hold. A ring of characters circled a central figure — a shape like a mountain seen upside down, its peak driven downward into darkness instead of rising toward sky. Around the inverted peak ran script in no alphabet she could name, and yet —

And yet she knew three of the letters.

That was the thing that made the back of her neck prickle. She was ninety-one years old, or near enough that she'd stopped counting honestly, and she had gathered languages the way some women gather buttons: off coats, out of drawers, most of them missing their mates. Her grandmother had been born before the kingdoms, back when this land had names no king had written down, and the old woman had sung counting songs in a tongue the priests called superstition and beat out of the children whenever they caught them at it. Maren had learned the songs because they were pretty, and forgotten them because she was young, and remembered them again — fragments, only fragments — when she was old and everything lost comes back wearing the face of regret.

Three letters. *Veth. Or. Senn.* She sounded them in her head like stones dropped down a well, listening for the bottom.

*Veth* meant debt. Or burden. Or the weight a thing carries — her grandmother had used it for the yoke on oxen, and also for grief, and also, once, laughing, for Maren herself as a fat infant. Old tongues were like that. They didn't separate what the new ones insisted on separating.

*Or* was harder. It meant *under*, but it also meant *belonging-to*, the way a river belongs to its bed. Ownership, but the intimate kind. The kind that doesn't let go.

*Senn.*

Her grandmother had never said *senn* above a whisper. It appeared in one song only, the lullaby about the mountain that ate the moonlight, and when Maren had asked as a child what senn meant, her grandmother had stopped singing, put both hands flat on the table, and said: *It means the ones below. And we do not say it loud, because loud is how they hear.*

Maren took her thumb off the boy's skin and stepped back.

"Well?" Yara's voice came out cracked. He cleared his throat. "You recognize it."

"I recognize pieces." She lowered herself into her chair by the hearth — her knees made their usual complaint, and for once she was grateful for the delay it gave her. "Before I say more, I'll ask the boy something. Don't translate. Don't guess. Just let him answer however he can."

Yara frowned but nodded.

She crouched — slower than she used to, the floor arriving eventually — until her face was level with the boy's. Up close his eyes were not simply dark; they had depth to them, like wells you lean over and cannot see the water in. She raised her hand, palm open, and spoke the old word carefully, shaping it the way her grandmother had shaped it, the vowels round, the consonants soft as loam:

"*Veth?*" she asked. Debt? Burden?

The boy went very still.

Then his mouth opened and sound came out — not words, not quite. A low, humming tone, three notes descending, and though Maren could not have translated it if you'd held a knife to her, she understood it the way you understand a door being closed. *Yes,* it said. *But don't ask me to show you.*

Beside the lamp, Yara made a small wounded noise, as if the sound had struck him physically.

"He answered," Maren said quietly. "In the old way. With tone, not tongue." She straightened, joints protesting again, and this time she let them complain aloud so she'd have something ordinary to hold onto. "Yara Dunmore, fetch my strongbox. Under the bed. Mind the trap on the left side — I keep meaning to remove it and I keep not dying, so it stays."

He fetched it. An iron-bound chest no bigger than a bread box, older than he was, older than her, brought down through four generations of women who kept things kings would have burned. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark: a book.

Not a book, precisely. Pages sewn into a cover of grey hide that no tanner in any market would claim, because no animal wore that hide. Maren's grandmother had called it the ledger, though nothing in it recorded debts of money. It was a record of bargains — the old kind, the pre-kingdom kind, the kind struck between people and powers when the world was younger and both sides still believed the other would keep terms. Half the pages were blank now. The rest were written in a hand that changed every few generations, because each keeper added what she'd witnessed, and witnessed bargains grew rarer as the centuries thickened with law and church and crown, all of them built, if you believed the oldest entries, specifically to make such bargains unnecessary. Or impossible. Depending who you asked.

Maren unwrapped it on her table and opened it to the ribbon-marked place, and there, copied in her grandmother's careful hand beside an entry four hundred years old, was the character *senn*.

And beside it, drawn in faded ink, the inverted mountain.

The lamp guttered. Outside, wind moved through the herb rows with a dry rattle, and somewhere far off a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"Read it," Yara said. "All of it."

"It's not a storybook, lad. It's a warning label." But she read. Her voice, which had quavered over the boy's silence, went steady now, the way it always did when she worked — the body remembering it had a job.

"The entry is called *Of the Court Beneath the Mountain*. Not *a* court. *The*. In the time of my grandmother's grandmother, there were powers that kept courts as men do — with seats and laws and officers — except their laws were written in flesh and their coin was not silver." She touched the inverted mountain. "This is their seal. Their seat lies under the mountains east of here, past the valley's edge, deeper than mines go. They call themselves the Under-Crown's court, and what they deal in—" she paused, and found she had to make herself say it— "is soul-collateral. Souls taken as surety against obligations. Sold outright, sometimes, by sellers who wanted something badly enough. Bought, held, hung up like lamps in a long hall, burning for the court's light until the debt matures or the holder claims them."

"Hung up," Yara repeated. His face had gone the color of the lamp-pale ink he complained about. "Like — preserved?"

"Like pickled," Maren said bluntly. "A soul apart from its body keeps. That's the whole appeal, from their side. No rot, no escape, no death to complicate collection." She turned the page. "Here's the part that matters. When a soul is sold — truly sold, sealed with this mark — the contract matures on a fixed date. Thirteen turnings of the moon from sealing. On that date, the debt comes due, and the court collects. The body walks to them, or is walked, and the soul is rejoined to it behind their gates, and that person belongs to the Under-Crown until the end of whatever passes for time down there. There is no appeal. There is no statute. There is no power above ground that has ever broken one of these contracts, because—" she tapped the page— "the contracts are written in the same language as this mark, and in that language, a bargain freely sealed *cannot be unsealed*. Not by force. Not by mercy. The words won't permit it. It's the one law older than all the others."

Silence filled the hut the way water fills a hull.

Yara looked at the boy. The boy looked back at him, and then — this was the part Maren would remember all her life, she was sure of it, the way you remember the exact angle of light on a day the world changed — the boy reached out and took Yara's ink-stained hand and held it, and made the descending hum again, softer this time. Three notes. But different notes. Not *yes, but don't look.*

Something more like: *I know. Sit down anyway.*

Yara sat.

"How long," he said. His voice was very controlled, the control costing him visibly, a man holding a door shut against a flood with his shoulder. "How long since the seal was set?"

"I can't know the sealing date from looking. But—" Maren counted backward through the days since the boy had arrived, mud-caked and mute at Yara's door, and felt the arithmetic settle in her stomach like a swallowed stone. "If he was sold recently — and he must have been, the mark is fresh, weeks old at most — then the moon overhead tonight is the first turning. Which leaves twelve."

"Twelve moons."

"Twelve turnings. One year, near enough. And the thirteenth moon will rise full over whichever ground he stands on, and the contract will mature, and the court will collect. Distance means nothing to them. Borders mean nothing. Walls mean—" she glanced at Yara and stopped herself, because the valley's edge, the boundary his instruments failed at, was a wall of a kind too, and she saw him arrive at the same thought a heartbeat later and go pale all over again. "Very little," she finished.

"Who sold him?" The question came out of Yara flat and hard. "A child can't sell his own soul. Someone sold it *for* him. Who has the right?"

"That's in the mark itself, likely — seller's name, terms, all of it, written fine as spider silk. But I can't read it all. Nobody above ground reads it whole anymore. What I can tell you is what the old entries say about *why* children get sold." She closed the ledger gently, as if the thing inside might be sleeping. "Because a child's soul is clean collateral. Unencumbered. No oaths sworn, no debts owed, no gods claimed. A grown soul is a tangled ledger already. A child's is blank paper — the most valuable paper there is, to a buyer who values blankness. And the price they pay for it, the seller's price—" she shook her head. "Whatever the seller wanted, they got it. That's how these bargains work. They're honored to the letter. It's the spirit that gets eaten alive."

Yara stood abruptly, knocking the stool back, and crossed to the window, and stood there with his fists on the sill, staring out at the dark where the valley lay — his valley, mapped corner to corner by twenty years of his stubborn feet and instruments, every stream and stone given a name in his neat cartographer's hand. Maren watched his back and understood, better than he probably did himself, that the map he was really staring at was the one in his chest, the one where every fixed point of his life was suddenly drifting.

"We take him to a priest," Yara said finally. "The abbey at Fordham has lawyers trained in canon contract law. If anyone can void a—"

"Lad." She said it gently. "The abbey's canon lawyers couldn't void a marriage contract between two consenting farmers if the ink smudged. This is older than their God's paperwork. You'd be carrying the boy to men who will, at best, write to their superiors, and their superiors will write to theirs, and meanwhile twelve moons become eleven." She rose and came to stand beside him at the window. "There's one other thing in the entries. About redemption."

He turned. "Redemption."

"A clause. Rarely usable, the text says, but it exists — the same law that forbids unsealing permits substitution. A soul may be redeemed from contract by an equal claim. Another soul, freely offered, of equal standing." She watched him absorb that, watched him start — she could see it happening, the fool, the brave fool — to do the arithmetic of himself, and she cut it off before it finished. "Don't. Not tonight, and maybe not ever, because 'equal standing' in that language doesn't mean willing. It means equivalent. Clean, unencumbered, blank-paper. And your soul, Yara Dunmore, is many things, but it is not blank. Whatever you are, whatever made this valley the strange hole in the world that it is—" she tapped his chest once, lightly, over his heart— "there's writing on you. Old writing. So don't volunteer for a trade you may not legally be."

His jaw tightened. "Then what do we do?"

"What we always do with problems too large for the room they're in." She turned back to the table, began wrapping the ledger in its oilcloth. "We learn everything. We find someone who reads that language whole — and there will be someone, because brokers exist, and brokers read their own contracts. We find out who sold this child and why, because the seller may have left a door open — sellers always leave themselves loopholes, greed is a sloppy lawyer. And we do it quietly, because—" she paused, oilcloth half-folded, and looked at the boy, who had begun, silently, to help gather the scattered herbs off the table, sorting them by touch into little piles with the deft, sad competence of a child who had worked for his keep wherever he'd come from. "Because whoever sold him knew what they were doing. And people who know what they're doing tend to check on their investments."

Outside, the wind dropped all at once, the way it does when the night decides to listen.

And in the silence that followed, the boy froze over his little piles of herbs, head cocked, eyes going wide and fixed on the dark window — and Maren saw the skin beneath his collarbone catch the lamplight and gleam, and the gleam was moving, the fine lines crawling like something turning over in its sleep.

Twelve turnings, she thought. And something below had just turned its attention upward, toward a mud-caked child in a herbwoman's hut at the edge of a valley that refused the world.

The dog started barking again, far off, and did not stop this time.

Yara was already reaching for his boots.
