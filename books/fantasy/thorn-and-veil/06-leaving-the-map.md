## 6. Leaving the Map

The boundary was not a wall. That was the first lie his own maps had told him.

Yara had drawn it a hundred times over thirty years — a fine dotted line running the length of the northern ridge, annotated in his careful hand: *limit of instruments, 4th of Harvestmoon; limit of ink, 9th of Frostfall; limit of nerve, undated.* He had always assumed that if he ever walked to it, he would find something. A stone marker left by the first settlers. A change in the soil's color. A hedge grown wild and deliberate. Boundaries were made of things. That was what boundaries *meant* — that someone had stood at an edge and said *here, and no further*, and had driven a post or raised a cairn to prove the saying serious.

There was nothing. There was a stand of grey birches like every other stand of grey birches, and between two of them, a gap wide enough for a cart, and beyond it the land simply went on — meadow falling away into a river valley he had never surveyed, hills rising blue-grey in the morning haze, a road, faint as a scar, winding toward all of it.

The boy stood at his side with his hand fisted in Yara's coat hem, and did not pull.

They had been walking since before dawn. Maren had seen them to the edge of her herb-garden at first light and pressed a bundle into Yara's arms — dried yarrow, a twist of salt, a jar of honey for wounds, "because you'll both be bleeding eventually, it's only polite of me to say so now" — and then she had held the boy's face between her palms for a long moment and spoken to him softly in that old tongue, the one that made Yara's teeth ache to hear, and whatever she'd said had made the child nod once, solemn as a judge.

"Twelve turns," Maren had said to Yara at last, gripping his wrist hard enough to leave marks. "Count them. Don't trust any road that promises to be shorter than it looks. And Yara—" She had hesitated, which she almost never did. "When you cross, don't fight it. Whatever comes back to you, let it come. Fighting it is how people drown."

He hadn't understood. He thought he had, the way a man thinks he understands drowning until the water is in his mouth.

Now he stood at the line his dotted ink had claimed to know, and there was no wall, and the world on the far side of the birches looked exactly like the world on this side, and his hands were shaking so badly he could not hold his compass steady.

It was the compass that frightened him most, in the end. Not the boy's silence, not the sigil beneath his collarbone, not even Maren's warnings. The compass. For three days since the child had arrived mud-caked and mute at his door, the needle had behaved like a living thing — spinning when the boy wept, trembling when he slept, straining north-northeast toward the mountain with the persistence of a dog pulling a leash. Yara had wrapped it in wool and buried it in a drawer and still felt it through the wood, a small frantic heartbeat in the house.

This morning, standing at the valley's edge, the needle pointed straight and true and utterly wrong: not north, but *out* — out past the birches, past the meadow, into the country Yara had spent his whole life declining to map.

"It doesn't want me here," he said aloud, and heard how foolish it sounded, and said it again anyway. "It never has."

The boy tugged his hem. When Yara looked down, the child was watching him with those dark, patient eyes — eyes that had watched, Yara now understood, a great deal worse than a cowardly cartographer dithering at a tree line. The boy raised one small hand and pointed through the gap in the birches. Then he pointed at himself. Then he took Yara's finger in his fist, turned, and pulled.

One step. Two. The birch shadows slid across their faces like water.

And the world came back.

* * *

Later — much later, when he could bear to think about it in words instead of only feeling it behind his ribs — Yara would describe it as color. But even that was a coward's description, because it had not been color alone.

It began as sound. All his life the valley had been quiet in a way he had mistaken for peace: birdsong present but thin, wind moving without voice, his own footsteps landing in the grass with a softness that had always seemed right and proper. Now, one step past the birches, sound *arrived*. The meadow grass hissed and whispered in a chorus of ten thousand separate voices. The wind came down off the far hills carrying the smell of rain that had fallen yesterday on slopes he couldn't see, and the smell itself had texture, weight, direction. A lark somewhere above him sang and the song went up and up and did not fade so much as climb, as though the sky were tall enough to hold it, and Yara stopped dead in the grass because he realized he had never in his life heard a bird finish its phrase. In the valley they had all trailed off, unfinished, like thoughts interrupted.

His vision followed. The greens of the meadow split apart into forty greens he had no names for — green like bottle glass, green like the underside of leaves after rain, a green so dark it was nearly black and seemed to move when he wasn't looking directly at it. The light itself had grain to it, a golden sediment suspended in the air, and motes drifted through it that were not dust; they moved *against* the breeze, in slow deliberate spirals, like insects thinking, like letters arranging themselves.

And underneath everything, humming in the ground, in the air, in his teeth and the wet marrow of him, was the other thing. The thing he had no word for and suspected no language had a word for, except perhaps the boy's, except perhaps the tongue older than stone.

Magic. Magic as weather. Magic as gravity. Magic as the simple fact of the world, so thickly present that Yara wondered how anything here managed to move at all, swimming as it must through a sea of it.

He fell to his knees.

Not gracefully. Not dramatically. He went down like a man whose legs have been stolen, hands slapping flat in the wet grass, and the grass under his palms was *alive* in a way grass should not be — he could feel it growing, actually feel the slow green push of it, feel the mycelial dark beneath the soil threading and trading and whispering, the whole meadow one vast conversational organism, and he retched bile onto the roots of it and knelt there shaking.

"Don't fight it." Maren's voice, in his memory, calm as a woman salting a stew. "Whatever comes back to you, let it come."

Because that was the horror of it, the thing he understood now, kneeling in a foreign field with his heart slamming like a trapped bird: this was not new. Nothing here was new. This was *familiar*. The flood of sense, the grain of the light, the hum in his marrow — some deep-buried part of him recognized all of it the way a body recognizes its native language after decades abroad. His blood knew this country. His bones had been born in air like this.

Which meant the valley had not been protecting him from the world.

The valley had been protecting the world from knowing what he was. Or protecting him from knowing. Or — and this thought arrived cold and complete, like a blade laid flat against the nape of the neck — the valley had been built around him the way a scab builds around a wound, and he had spent thirty years gratefully mapping his own bandage.

"Master Yara."

He looked up. The boy was crouched in front of him, small and grave, and he was holding out his hand — the same gesture, Yara realized dimly, that Yara had offered him on the first night, kneeling in the doorway with the compass spinning wildly between them. *I am not going to hurt you. Come out when you're ready.*

"I'm all right," Yara croaked, which was a lie so bald that the boy simply kept his hand where it was, waiting, with the patience of the very young and the very old, until Yara took it.

The touch steadied him — or rather, gave the flood somewhere to go. Through the boy's small warm fingers the roaring world seemed to pass *through* Yara rather than pile up against him, and he breathed, and breathed again, and slowly the vertigo resolved into something else. Something adjacent to exhilaration. He was still terrified. He was also, unmistakably, alive in a way he had never been, and some traitor corner of his mind was already reaching for charcoal and paper, already composing the meadow, the layered greens, the climbing lark-song, the motes that moved against the wind.

*You are drawing,* he told himself sternly. *You are on your knees in another kingdom's dirt and you are composing.*

But that was the truth of him, and it was, he supposed, the only rope he had. Fear and craft braided together. He got his feet under him. He stood.

* * *

They walked.

The road took them down out of the birches along the shoulder of the meadow, and Yara made himself do the work, because work was ballast. Every hundred paces he stopped and sketched — quick, dirty thumbnails in the cheap little book he'd brought for the purpose, recording bearings by sun and shadow since his compass was useless for anything but pointing at the mountain like a hound scenting meat. He noted the fork where the road split, the leaning milestone carved with a script that predated any alphabet he knew (the boy touched it gently, the way one touches a sleeping animal). He drew the river below, wide and brown-green, and the ferry crossing with its rope strung slack between posts, and the smoke of a chimney or two in the middle distance that meant farms, that meant people, that meant the world was populated by strangers who might ask questions he could not answer about a mute child branded with a contract mark.

The strangest thing — stranger than the singing grass, stranger than the light with grain in it — was how *well* he drew here.

In the valley his maps had always fought him. Ink ran pale; lines came out hesitant; distances refused to agree with each other no matter how carefully he chained his measurements, as though the land itself declined to be pinned down and subtly shifted its proportions whenever he looked away. Thirty years of that. Thirty years of redrawing, of annotating uncertainty in margins, of lying awake wondering whether he was a poor surveyor or whether the valley simply would not be known.

Here, his pencil obeyed him. Better than obeyed him — the third sketch he made, a rough plan of the road bending along the hillside with the ferry below, came off the page looking *more* true than the scene itself, the proportions perfect in a way no eye could verify, the bend of the river rendered with a certainty he did not possess. He stared at it for a long moment. Then he held it up beside the actual landscape, and the landscape seemed, briefly, embarrassed. As though it had considered rearranging itself to match.

He put the sketchbook away very carefully, the way a man puts away a knife he has just discovered is sharper than he thought.

The boy watched him do it, and something flickered across the child's face — not fear. Recognition, maybe. Or appetite.

They crossed at the ferry near midday. The ferrymen were ordinary enough — two brothers, broad and sunburnt, charging an honest toll of two coppers — but the elder brother's eyes lingered on Yara a moment too long as he paid, and the man said, mild as milk, "Haven't seen valley-folk cross in years. Thought your lot didn't hold with the rest of us."

"We're going to the mountain," Yara said, because a lie required more invention than he currently possessed.

The brothers exchanged a look. It was brief, but Yara had spent a lifetime reading terrain, and faces were terrain. What passed between them was not surprise. It was something closer to pity, or the particular wariness of men who have ferried other travelers toward that mountain before and knew how many had come back.

"The mountain keeps its ledgers well," the younger brother said, pushing them off from the bank. "Mind you don't add to them."

The river carried them, and the river was worse than the meadow.

Because the river remembered — he felt it the moment the boat slid out over the deep channel, a cold pressure against his newly opened senses, like pressing his ear to a door behind which a thousand conversations were happening at once. Every drop of water in it had passed over stones and roots and drowned branches and the bodies of things, and none of it had been forgotten; the current was less a flow than a recitation, endless and murmurous. For one dizzy moment Yara fancied he caught a fragment of it — a flash of image, a woman's hands, glowing faintly, cradling something small and screaming — and then it was gone, swept downstream, and his own heartbeat was the loudest thing in the world.

He gripped the gunwale and did not look at the boy, and the boy, mercifully, did not look at him.

On the far bank the road climbed toward hills that were genuinely new country, unmapped by anyone whose maps Yara had ever smuggled home in traders' packs. He stood a moment at the water's edge with the sketchbook heavy in his satchel and the compass straining northeast in his pocket, and he allowed himself, finally, to look back.

The valley was invisible from here. Of course it was. He had always known it must be — the traders' accounts, the pale ink, the instruments failing — but knowing and seeing were different animals. From the ridge above the river there should have been a view of it: the long green trough of it, the town, Maren's garden, his own house with its slate roof. Instead there was only a fold of hills like any other fold, unremarkable, forgettable, a blankness so complete it looked deliberate. As though someone had taken an eraser to the world and rubbed until the page showed through.

*The veil was for—*

The thought arrived and stopped, snagged on something, a word missing from the middle of it like a tooth knocked from a jaw. Yara frowned. He had been about to think something. He was certain he had been about to think something important.

"Come on," he said to the boy, and turned northeast, toward the mountain, which was visible now on the horizon — a black shape too symmetrical to be natural, wearing a crown of cloud that did not move with the wind. The compass hammered against his hip like a second heart.

They walked until the light went long and gold, and camped in the lee of a hedgerow, and Yara discovered that building a fire here was different too — the flame burned taller than it should, brighter, and leaned toward the boy like a plant toward the sun, and when the child slept, curled against Yara's side with his breath slow and even, the fire settled into a low contented purr.

Yara sat watch with the sketchbook open on his knees.

He did not draw the day. He found he couldn't — every time he tried to render the ferry or the milestone or the layered greens of the meadow, his hand wanted instead to draw something else, something he had no memory of deciding to draw: a shape like a valley, ringed and sealed, and around it a border being drawn, line by careful line, in a hand that was almost his own but not quite. Older. Quicker. Certain.

He watched his own fingers lay down the border stroke by stroke and felt, for the first time since the boy had knocked at his door, that he was not the one holding the pencil.

Far off to the northeast, the mountain's cloud-crown caught the moonlight, and somewhere beneath it, he did not doubt, a ledger lay open with a line item twelve turns from maturity. And somewhere behind him, back across the river, in a valley that refused to be remembered by the world, the blank space where his house should be sat on the hills like an unwritten page — waiting, he understood now, for him to come home and fill it, or for the world to find out what had been written there before he was born.

The fire purred. The boy slept. And in the margin of the page, in handwriting that was almost his, the almost-map finished itself, and at its center, where a cartographer would put a name, his hand wrote a single word in that old tongue older than stone —

and Yara, reading it upside down by firelight, felt the syllables slide into his blood like a key into a lock he had carried all his life without knowing,

and the word was *home*.
