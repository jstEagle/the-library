## 7. The Road of Small Mercies

The valley let them go the way a fist lets go of something it has held too long — slowly, finger by finger, with an ache left in the shape of what it carried.

Yara felt that ache for three days. It sat behind his sternum like a stone swallowed wrong, and every morning he woke expecting to find himself back in his own bed, back inside the borders he had spent thirty years drawing and redrawing, back where the compass needle lay quiet and the ink dried true. But each dawn found him on the far side of the ridge, in a world that hummed.

He had no word for the humming at first. Then, on the second night, camped beneath a stand of birches whose leaves turned silver without any wind he could feel, he understood: it was magic. Not the wild, storybook kind — not fire from fingertips or storms called down — but the low, constant thrum of a world where everything was *connected*, where a river knew its own name and a crow could remember your face across generations. The valley had none of this. The valley was a held breath. Out here, the whole world exhaled.

And the boy — Tam, Yara had begun calling him, though the name was his invention, a placeholder stitched over a silence — the boy did not seem to hear the humming at all. He heard something else. On that second night among the birches, Tam stood with his bare feet in the moss and his head tilted, listening to some frequency beneath the world's, and when Yara asked what he heard, the boy answered in that old tongue, three soft syllables like water moving under ice.

"I don't understand you," Yara said, as he said every time. "I'm sorry. I don't."

Tam looked at him then — really looked, the way Maren had looked at the sigil under his collarbone, with the patience of someone reading a text everyone else had forgotten how to read — and shook his head once. Not refusal. Correction. As if to say: *you do understand me. You just haven't admitted it yet.*

Yara banked the fire and did not sleep well.

* * *

By the fourth day they had learned the shape of each other's company.

Tam rose before dawn. Always. He would be sitting cross-legged at the edge of their little camp when the sky went grey, watching the east with the stillness of a heron, and he would not move until the sun cleared whatever hills or trees stood in its way. Only then would he turn to Yara and make a small gesture — two fingers touched to his lips, then extended outward — which Yara eventually understood meant *good morning*, or possibly *the day is permitted to begin*. He never learned which. It didn't seem to matter. Rituals outlive their meanings; that was rather the point of them.

For his part, Yara talked. He talked because the silence between them was too large to carry unbroken, and because he suspected — without evidence, purely on instinct — that the boy understood more common speech than he could produce. So Yara narrated. He named the birds. He explained why he boiled water even when it looked clean. He told stories about the valley, about Maren and her bitter teas, about the way his mother's loom had sounded through the floorboards of his childhood — and then stopped telling that particular story, because he noticed it made his chest hurt in a way unrelated to leaving home.

Tam listened the way stones listen: completely, without interruption, giving nothing back but weight.

They ate what the land gave and what Yara's coin bought. His purse was thinner than he'd estimated — cartography paid poorly in a valley that needed no maps — and by the fifth day he began doing the thing he should have thought of sooner.

He drew.

The first village they came to sat at a crossroads where three dirt tracks met a river ford, and the village had a problem: the ford moved. Every spring flood rearranged the gravel bars, and every year someone's cart went into the deep channel, and every year the villagers argued about where the safe line ran. Yara watched the argument from the edge of the tavern yard for less than an hour before he took out his kit — paper, pens, the good iron-gall ink Maren had pressed on him at parting — and sat down on a barrel.

He drew the ford. Not as it was, but as it behaved: the sweep of the current, the way the gravel bar built on the upstream side of the big stone and scoured away below it, the deep channel curling like a question mark past the willow. He marked the safe line in red. He marked the place where the channel had been five years ago in faded grey, and the year before that fainter still, so the whole drawing showed not a river but a *habit*.

The miller found him there. Read the map upside down for a long moment. Then turned it around, read it again, and said, "Where did you learn rivers do that?"

"Rivers are honest," Yara said. "You just have to watch them longer than a single season."

The miller fed them both, gave them a corner of the loft to sleep in, and pressed two coppers into Yara's hand besides, which Yara tried to refuse and could not. Word traveled ahead of them after that. At the next village, a woman was waiting with a dispute over an orchard boundary that three generations of neighbors had failed to settle; Yara settled it in an afternoon by finding, in the oldest man's memory and the youngest tree's shadow, a line both could live with. At the third, they mended his boot sole and refilled his ink without being asked.

"You're buying passage," Tam said.

Yara dropped his pen.

It was evening. They were walking the last mile toward the orchard village, dust gold in the slant light, and the boy had simply said it — two words, rough-edged, accented in a way Yara couldn't place, as if common speech were a coat he'd once owned and outgrown. He said nothing else. He didn't look at Yara. He kept walking, hands in his pockets, as if he hadn't just broken nine days of silence with a sentence of perfect, ordinary sense.

"That's right," Yara managed finally. His voice came out strange. "That's exactly right. Maps for meals. Ink for lodging." He walked a few steps. "How long have you been able to—"

Tam shrugged, eloquent in the universal language of shrugs, and said nothing more for two days.

But something had changed between them, and both of them knew it. The boy was not mute. The boy was *rationing*. Whatever had been taken from him — voice, soul, self, Yara still didn't know the full shape of the theft — had left him careful with what remained, a man spending his last coins one at a time. And now he had decided Yara was worth spending on. One word here. A sentence there. A gesture that needed no translation at all: on the sixth night, when Yara shivered through his watch, Tam silently added his own blanket to Yara's without a word, and sat the rest of the night awake beside him, shoulder almost touching shoulder, watching the dark so Yara didn't have to.

Yara pretended to sleep and let him.

* * *

The road bent north on the eighth day, and with the north came the mountain.

It had been visible since the second morning — a blue-grey shape on the horizon, more suggestion than fact — but each day it grew more insistent, and by the eighth it dominated the skyline like a judgment. Snow at the crown. Forest to the knee. And somewhere inside it, if Maren's decipherment was right, a court of ledgers and lanterns where a child's soul hung waiting for the moon to finish its counting.

Twelve turns remaining, Maren had said. That had been eleven days ago. Eleven nights Yara had lain awake doing arithmetic he hated.

"How does it work?" he asked Tam that evening, because the question had been building in him all day and he could no longer afford politeness. "The debt. Do you feel it? Is there a — a tether? Can you hear it?"

Tam considered him for a long moment. Then he held up one hand, fingers spread, and pointed at each finger in turn — one, two, three, four, five — and then closed his fist slowly, and opened it again, and looked at Yara with an expression of such plain grief that Yara had to look away.

Counting. The boy was counting. Five fingers, five turns gone already, perhaps — or five remaining, or five until something worse. He lived inside the arithmetic the way a prisoner lives inside a wall, tapping it daily to check whether it had thinned.

"We'll make it," Yara said. "I've measured distances my whole life. I know what roads cost. We'll make it with turns to spare."

Tam's mouth did something that was almost a smile and mostly wasn't. Then he reached into the satchel Yara carried — reached without asking, which startled them both — and pulled out the folded map Yara had drawn on their first morning out, the one charting the road from the valley's edge to the mountain's foot. He smoothed it on his knee. He tapped the mountain. Then he dragged his finger slowly, slowly down off the edge of the paper, into the blank margin below, and tapped the empty space twice.

Yara stared at the blankness. "What's down there?"

Tam tapped the blank space again. Harder. Then he looked up, and his eyes were wet, and he said — in the old tongue, the words spilling out of him like blood from a reopened wound — something long and desperate and utterly unintelligible, except for the shape of it, which was unmistakably the shape of *you don't understand, you don't understand, nobody understands.*

Then he snatched the map, folded it with violent precision, and handed it back. And he would not speak again that night, nor the next morning, and when Yara tried to apologize he turned his face away with the terrible dignity of the very young.

That afternoon, Yara sat by the roadside and did the thing he should have done on the first day. He took out a fresh sheet and drew the region around the mountain from memory — not the memory of having seen it, but the memory of every rumor, every merchant's tale, every fragment Maren had ever repeated, every half-remembered line from the old pre-kingdom texts she'd shown him. He drew the tollhouses people whispered about. The ashwood. The veiled market some swore appeared only to those already lost. He drew them all in dotted lines, conjecture made visible, and labeled the whole sheet in his neatest hand: *THINGS SAID, NOT SEEN.*

When he showed it to Tam, the boy went very still.

His finger came down on the veiled market — the one Yara had placed half in jest, a smudge of dots southwest of the mountain — and did not move. His breathing changed. And when he raised his eyes to Yara's, there was no anger in them anymore. There was recognition, sharp and frightened, the look of a man who has seen his own house drawn accurately by a stranger.

"You know it," Yara said quietly. "Don't you. You've been there."

Tam nodded once. Then he took the pen from Yara's hand — gently this time, asking permission with the taking — and beside the dotted market he drew a small symbol. A circle, crossed by a vertical line. Yara had seen it before. He spent a cold moment hunting his memory and found it: carved into the lintel of Maren's herb-shed door, worn nearly smooth, older than the shed, older perhaps than the valley.

"What is it?" he asked. "What does it mean?"

Tam pressed the pen back into his hand, closed Yara's fingers around it, and said his longest sentence yet — six words, halting, each one hauled up from somewhere deep:

"Sell nothing. There. Buy nothing."

* * *

They struck north along the river road on the eleventh day, and the world began to change around them in ways Yara's instruments recorded and his understanding refused.

The villages thinned. The fields gave way to scrub, the scrub to forest, and the forest grew strange — trees with bark like hammered lead, clearings where the grass grew in perfect concentric rings, a stream that ran uphill for twenty paces before thinking better of it. Yara mapped obsessively, filling sheet after sheet, and noticed that his drawings had begun to feel less like records and more like arguments: the land resisted being fixed on paper, shifting its details between his first sighting and his second, as if it resented witnesses.

And they were not alone on the road. That was the other change.

Travelers had been sparse but friendly in the lowlands. Here they were sparse and *watchful* — carters who fell silent as Yara and Tam approached, a tinker who took one look at the boy and made a sign against evil before catching himself, a pair of pilgrims who walked wide around them and murmured to each other in voices pitched just below hearing. Once, passing a shrine at a crossroads — a crude stone figure with its face chiseled away — Yara saw fresh offerings: bread, a copper coin, and a lock of hair tied in red thread. An offering to something. Or a payment to it.

"To whom?" he asked the tinker that evening, at a fire both parties shared out of mutual necessity. "The shrine. Who's it for?"

The tinker was a lean man with a burn-scarred jaw, and he answered carefully, watching Tam where the boy sat apart, cleaning Yara's pens with meticulous care. "Depends who's asking. Some say the Old Court. Some say it's for the brokers, so they pass you by." He spat into the fire. "Some say it doesn't matter who you pay, because somebody always holds the ledger."

"And if the debt isn't yours?" Yara heard his own voice go hard. "If the mark is on someone who never agreed to anything?"

The tinker looked at him for a long moment. Then at Tam. Then back. Something shifted in his scarred face — pity, maybe, or the memory of pity once felt and long since spent.

"Then you're on the wrong road, friend," he said softly. "Or the right one. Never met anyone going toward that mountain for a happy reason." He rolled into his blanket. "There's folk in these woods who might help you. And folk who'll strip the boots off your feet and the name off your tongue. Learn the difference fast."

Yara lay awake long after the tinker slept, listening to the forest hum its endless note, feeling Tam's silent vigil beside him, and turning over the boy's warning — *sell nothing, buy nothing* — like a stone in his hands, looking for its edge.

In the morning they would reach the edge of the ashwood, where the maps went blank and the rumors began. In the morning, Yara would discover that the difference the tinker spoke of was narrower than a blade — and that the figures dropping from the trees on either side of the road, ropes in hand and faces wrapped in grey, had already learned everything about them worth knowing.

One of them, when they came, looked straight at Tam's collarbone — at the sigil no cloth quite hid — and smiled.

"There's a price on that mark," the man said. "Question is who collects."
