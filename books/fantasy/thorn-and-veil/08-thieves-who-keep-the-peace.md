## 8. Thieves Who Keep the Peace

Sef had counted eleven wagons on the Ash Road in her time, and she had robbed ten of them. The eleventh had carried plague, and she'd let it pass with a courtesy she still regretted.

This morning's prize was a cartographer and a child, which was either the easiest take of her career or the last mistake of it, and she wouldn't know which until the ropes were off or the blood was dry.

She watched from the crook of a blackened alder as the man came up the road. He walked like someone reading — eyes down, then flicking to the treeline, then down again, cataloguing. A map case rode his shoulder the way other men wore swords: close, habitual, like a limb. Behind him came the boy, small and silent, keeping exactly one pace back, which was the distance of a dog that had been kicked by everyone who'd ever fed it.

*Cartographer,* the tinker had said in the low town, three days past, over watered beer and a purse Sef hadn't asked where he'd gotten. *Valley man. First time out. Got a boy with him that brokers would skin their own mothers for.* He'd grinned through the gap in his teeth. *Thought you'd want to know before someone less friendly did.*

Less friendly. As if Sef's people were known for their gentleness. She'd let the insult slide because the coin was good and because the tinker was right about one thing: word traveled faster than wheels, and anything worth taking on the Ash Road was worth killing for too.

"Steady," she breathed into the alder's shadow. Around her, nine shapes hung motionless in the branches, grey-wrapped, rope coiled. Bram was in the oak across the road, and if he so much as shifted a leaf she'd have words for him later. Wren was nearest the bend, crossbow leveled at the cartographer's chest, because Wren believed in efficiency and Sef had never fully cured her of it.

The man reached the bend. Stopped. And here was the thing Sef would remember afterward, the thing that made her later decisions feel less like mercy and more like recognition: he didn't startle. He looked at the road ahead, at the way the ruts deepened where nothing heavy had passed, at the absence of birdsong in trees that should have been loud with it — and she saw him *understand*, the way a man understands weather. His hand went not to a weapon but to the map case, and he drew the boy behind him with the other arm, slow, deliberate, the way you'd shield a candle from wind.

"He knows," Wren murmured beside her, though Wren wasn't supposed to talk during a hanging. "Look at him. He knows."

"Everyone knows," Sef said. "Knowing doesn't help."

The cartographer knelt, unhurried, and set his palm flat against the road. Reading it, Sef realized. Reading the dust. Then he stood, turned to face the trees on both sides — *both* sides, which meant he'd guessed the shape of the trap entire — and said, in a voice pitched to carry:

"We have little worth stealing. But I'll draw you a map of anywhere you name, accurate to a boot's width, in exchange for safe passage."

Silence. Somewhere above Sef, Doss exhaled through his nose, which was Doss's version of laughter.

"A cartographer," Wren called down, "who thinks maps are worth more than what's in the strongbox."

"On the Ash Road?" The man's voice stayed level, but Sef heard the effort of it, the fine tremor under the calm like ice over moving water. "I think everything is worth more than what's in a strongbox."

It was a good answer. It was also, Sef thought, an answer given by a man who had no idea what kind of thieves he was talking to, and that was the moment she should have whistled the drop and been done with it — taken the map case, taken whatever coin he had, sent him weeping down the road toward the tollhouse where the brokers would weigh him and find him wanting.

Instead she looked at the boy.

The boy had come out from behind the man's leg. Not bravely — there was nothing brave in the set of those thin shoulders — but the way a creature comes out when it has learned that hiding changes nothing. He stood in the middle of the road in his mud-caked coat, and he looked up into the trees, into the grey-wrapped shapes, and his eyes found Sef's alder as if the wind had told him where to look.

And the collar of his coat had slipped. Just enough. Just enough to show the edge of the mark beneath — the sigil burned into the soft flesh above the collarbone, the contract brand, the ledger-line that every soul-broker's hound could smell at a mile and every honest thief could read at twenty paces.

Sef had seen that mark twice before. The first time had been on a girl her band had pulled, half-dead, from a broker's caravan outside Permitted Streets, and they'd gotten the brand cut out of her and the girl had lived, mostly, in the way that things live after being weighed. The second time had been on a man they'd caught selling children to the tollhouse at Gray Ford, and they'd hanged him with the same rope he'd used on his cargo, and Sef had slept well that night and most nights since.

"Well," she said aloud, to the road, to the boy, to the morning. "That changes the arithmetic."

She dropped.

She landed between the cartographer and the treeline, boots cracking frost, and heard her band come down around her in the whisper of rope and the soft thud of nine bodies choosing the ground. Wren landed last, crossbow never wavering, its bolt aimed with love and precision at the space between the man's eyes.

"Captain," Wren said.

"I see him."

"You saw the mark."

"I saw the mark." Sef circled the pair slowly, hands loose at her sides, and watched the cartographer watch her. Up close he was younger than the road had made him look, with ink stains worked so deep into his fingers they might have been born there, and eyes the flat grey-green of valley slate. He put himself between her and the boy without seeming to decide to — the body doing what the mind hadn't voted on yet. She liked that in a man. It was the only honesty some of them had.

"Name," she said.

"Yara Dunmore." A pause. "Of the valley."

"The valley." She let the word sit there like a stone in a stream. Everyone knew the valley; the valley was the hole in every map, the place where the brokers' ledgers went blank and the hounds lost scent and the whole rotten machinery of bought souls simply — stopped. Nobody came *out* of the valley. It was like meeting a fish who claimed to have grown up on a mountain. "And this?"

"Tam." His arm tightened fractionally around the boy. "He's mine to look after. That's all anyone needs to know."

"That's the least of what anyone needs to know." She nodded at the sigil, and the boy flinched and pulled his collar up, and something in Sef's chest went cold and hard and old. "You know what that is."

"It's why we're on this road."

"It's why you're still breathing." She turned to her band. "Ropes down. All of you. We're not taking them."

Wren's crossbow dipped an inch, no more. "Captain, the tinker said—"

"The tinker said brokers would pay gold for that mark. The tinker also sells hair tonic that makes your beard fall out." She held Wren's stare until the crossbow came the rest of the way down. "We don't sell children, Wren. We don't sell *anyone*. That's the whole point of us. That's the only point of us."

Doss spat, philosophically. Bram, dropping from his oak with a creak of old joints, said nothing at all, but he was looking at the boy with an expression Sef had learned to read years ago — the look of a man seeing a debt he couldn't collect and didn't want to.

* * *

They made camp early, in a hollow off the road where the ashwood's dead trees stood like a congregation of grey saints, and Sef sat the cartographer down across the fire from her with tea in a tin cup and questions stacked behind her teeth.

Tam had fallen asleep almost before the fire was lit — collapsed, really, sideways against Yara's shoulder mid-chew, the way children sleep when their bodies have decided for them. Yara had gone very still when it happened, as if afraid movement might undo it, and had remained a statue ever since, holding his tea in one hand and a sleeping boy with the other.

"You've not had him long," Sef said.

"Two months." The man's voice came out rough. He cleared his throat. "He arrived at my door in the rain. No note. No rider. Just — him. Mud to the knees and speaking a language I'd never heard."

"Maren's tongue," Bram said quietly, from across the fire. Several heads turned; Bram rarely spoke, and when he did it was usually worth the interruption. "Pre-kingdom speech. My grandmother knew fragments. Said the ones who spoke it whole were owned — body to one master, soul to another." He looked at the sleeping boy. "Older than any court. Older than the mountain, maybe."

"You know the mountain," Sef said. Not a question.

"I know what everyone knows. Court Beneath the Mountain. They buy what can't be seized and keep what can't be bought." Bram's scarred hands flexed once around his cup. "I know their tollhouses. I know their enforcers. I know what my brother looked like after they finished weighing him." He said it flatly, the way men say things they've said so many times inside their own heads that the saying costs nothing anymore. "That's how I know."

The fire popped. Above them the dead ashwood creaked in a wind that smelled of coming winter.

"So," Sef said, and leaned forward, elbows on knees. "Here's how it is, valley man. My band rides this road because it's the artery. Everything the brokers move — souls, contracts, the paper and the flesh both — moves along it eventually, going to market or coming back with interest. We hit their caravans. We burn their tollhouse records when we can reach them. We cut brands out where cutting works and hang the sellers where cutting doesn't." She turned the tin cup slowly in her hands. "We're thieves. I'll grant the word. But ask yourself who taught this country that justice was something you could purchase, and then ask yourself whether the law that grew from that seed deserves the loyalty of anyone with a working heart."

Yara was quiet a long moment. "In the valley," he said finally, "we have no courts at all. Disputes are settled by whoever the neighbors trust. I always thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I always thought that was just because we were small. That law was what you graduated to, when you grew big enough to afford it."

"And now?"

"Now I'm standing in a country where a child's soul has a price and a paperwork trail, and I'm starting to think the valley was the graduation."

Sef laughed — short, surprised out of her. "Careful, cartographer. Talk like that gets you recruited."

"Is that what this is? Recruitment?"

"This is arithmetic." She nodded at the boy, at the sigil rising and falling with his breath. "Thirteen turns of the moon, yes? That's what the mark says, if you can read it. Twelve left now. After that, the debt matures, and the mountain sends for him, and no amount of running puts enough road between a contracted soul and its owner. You can't outrun a ledger. Ledgers don't get tired."

"I know." The flatness in his voice told her he'd done that math long ago, sleeplessly, many nights. "So I'm taking him to the mountain. To return what was sold."

Sef stared at him. Around the fire, the band had gone quiet in the particular way of people pretending not to listen while listening to every word.

"To the— You're walking *toward* it. Voluntarily."

"I'm told there's a gate. I'm told debts can be contested. I'm a cartographer, Captain. I don't fight well and I don't lie well, but I can find any door that exists, and I'm beginning to suspect doors are what this comes down to." He met her eyes, and underneath the fear and the exhaustion there was something load-bearing, something that had already decided. "I need to cross the ashwood. Past the tollhouses. Through country I've only ever drawn from rumor. Every guide I've asked for has refused, fled, or tried to rob me." A ghost of a smile. "You've done the third. Perhaps you'll consider the first."

The band was definitely listening now. Wren had stopped even pretending to clean her crossbow.

"It's suicide," Sef said.

"Probably."

"You'd be asking nine people to walk into the mountain's shadow carrying the single most hunted child in the living world, past enforcers who kill first and weigh second."

"Yes."

"And what do we get?"

Yara looked down at the sleeping boy. When he answered, his voice had changed — quieter, stripped of negotiation, just the bare fact of it laid on the firelight between them.

"You get to hurt them," he said. "Every mile we go, we're taking something they've already bought and paid for. Whatever happens at the end — however it ends — every mile is theirs to chase and ours to steal." He looked up. "I thought that was your trade, Captain. Stealing back what shouldn't have been for sale."

Nobody moved. The wind moved, sifting grey ash through the dead branches overhead, and somewhere far off a hound gave voice — one long note, miles away, probably nothing, probably a farmer's dog, and every person at that fire heard it anyway.

Sef looked at her band. At Wren, whose brother had died in a broker's cellar with his name struck from every record so thoroughly that even his grave was anonymous. At Doss, who never spoke of the tollhouse at Gray Ford and drank hard whenever it was mentioned. At Bram, who had already decided — she could see it in the set of his shoulders, had probably decided somewhere between the oak tree and the fire, maybe the moment he'd seen the mark.

Twelve turns of the moon. Nine thieves. One madman with ink on his hands and a child who spoke the language of the owned.

"Wren," Sef said. "Get the fast camp struck. We ride at first light."

Wren grinned like a knife coming out of a sheath. "Toward the mountain, Captain?"

"Toward the mountain." She drained her tea and stood, and pointed a finger at Yara Dunmore across the flames. "But understand me, cartographer. This isn't rescue and it isn't charity. If it turns out you're leading us into a broker's net — if that mark is bait and you're the hook — I will hang you myself with the same rope I'd use on the men who branded him. Swear it true."

"I swear it true," Yara said, and Sef, who had heard a thousand oaths and believed perhaps four of them, found herself believing this one — not because of the words, but because of the way his hand settled, without thinking, on the sleeping boy's shoulder. Like a man swearing on the only scripture he had.

Above the ashwood, the moon rose — one turn begun, twelve remaining — thin and white and patient as a coin waiting to be spent.

And far down the dark of the Ash Road, behind them, the hound bayed again. Closer this time.

Bram stood, head cocked, listening, and his face in the firelight had gone the color of the dead trees.

"Captain," he said. "We need to talk about what's following us."
