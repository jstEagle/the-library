## 9. Justice Outside the Law

The fire was built small, the way Sef liked them. A big fire invited company, and company, in the hills east of the Toll Road, meant either tax collectors or worse.

She sat with her back to a fallen oak, boots crossed at the ankle, knife across her knees where it always was after sundown. Around her the band had arranged itself in the loose geometry of people who trusted each other's blind spots: Bram and Osla arguing over dice, Wren asleep sitting up with her bow still strung, the twins sharing a single blanket and a single dream by the look of their breathing. And at the edge of the light, the cartographer and the boy.

The cartographer — Dunmore — sat like a man who had never learned how to be outdoors overnight. His spine kept trying to find a wall that wasn't there. The boy was folded against his side, small as a bundle of kindling, watching the flames with an expression Sef hadn't yet learned to read. He didn't blink much. She'd noticed that on the road today. He watched things the way a hawk watches, or the way something watched through a hawk.

"Your watch is second," Bram called to her, mid-toss of the dice. "Unless you're planning to lecture all night instead."

"I'm planning both."

Osla groaned. "She's going to lecture."

Sef let them have their noise. It was good noise, the sound of people who felt safe enough to complain. Then she looked across the fire at the mapmaker and said what she'd been turning over since the tollhouse dust had settled off their boots.

"You asked me today why we didn't rob you."

Dunmore's hands went still on his satchel. "I didn't ask. I said you were thieves who changed your minds."

"Same question wearing different clothes." She turned the knife once, flat side catching orange light. "And I'll answer it properly, because you're going to keep asking it until you do. You think we spared you out of charity. Charity runs out. What we did, we did because of what's written under that boy's collarbone, and if you're going to walk into the mountain carrying him, you ought to understand what you're walking against."

The boy looked up at the word *collarbone*. He understood more than he spoke. That was becoming clear.

"So talk," Dunmore said. There was frost in it. Good. She preferred men who argued; men who agreed with her too quickly were usually planning to leave in the night.

* * *

"Centuries ago," Sef began, "there were no courts worth the name. There were elders, there were moots, there were villages that settled their own blood-feuds or burned down trying. Justice lived where the hurt lived. If Osla stole Bram's pig, they settled it between themselves or their families did, and everyone knew the terms because everyone had to live inside them afterward." She nodded at the pair, who paused their dice to grin at each other with entirely unearned innocence. "That's the part people forget. Old justice wasn't kinder. It was *closer*. You couldn't hide behind it. Whatever you decided about your neighbor, you woke up next to him the next morning."

"And then?" Dunmore said. Flat. Testing.

"And then kings got ambitious, and priests got clever, and somebody worked out that justice could be *sold*. Not given away — sold. You write down rules, and you say: only the crown's court may interpret these rules, and interpretation costs silver. You take a thing every person carries in their gut from birth and you make it a licensed trade." She spat into the fire, mostly for rhythm. "First they sold judgment. Then they sold the right to hear grievances. Then they sold protection, which is to say they sold you freedom from the men they'd armed. Every layer was a fee. And underneath all the fees, the oldest bargain of all: the right to decide what a person *is*. Whether he's free or bound, guilty or owed, whole or owned."

Wren had opened one eye. Even asleep-sitting-up, she listened better than most did wide awake.

"The soul-brokers came later," Sef went on, quieter now, because this part deserved quiet. "Or maybe not later. Maybe they were always the point, and the courts were just the shopfront. Either way, somewhere along the way, the crowns discovered that a debt could be attached to a person so deep no bailiff could reach it. Attach it to the soul itself. Sell it. Trade it. Weigh it at tollhouses and ledger it in books no living debtor has ever read. And the courts? The courts wrote the contracts. The courts enforce them still. Your lawful king, mapmaker, keeps broker-tollhouses on his roads and calls the tolls *fees*."

Dunmore's jaw had gone tight. "I've paid those tolls."

"Everyone has. That's the elegance of it. The robbed pay rent on the robbery." She let that sit. "Now here's the part that will stick in your teeth. When the law became a thing sold, some of us decided we weren't buying. We went outside it. We took back the old justice — the close kind, the kind you can't hide behind — and we enforce it ourselves. No writs. No fees. No crown."

"That's called lawlessness," Dunmore said.

"It's called taking responsibility for your own hands," Osla cut in from the dice game, not looking up. "You've clearly never had to."

"Osla," Sef said mildly, and Osla went back to her dice, unrepentant.

"No," Dunmore said, leaning forward now, and Sef felt the real argument arrive, the one that had been building in him since the ambush. "I mean it. I've seen what happens when men appoint themselves the law. I've mapped border valleys where three 'justices outside the law' ruled within a day's ride of each other and hanged each other's cousins. Without law above you, you're just strength with a vocabulary."

"Yes," Sef said. "Sometimes we are. I won't lie to you around my own fire. We've made mistakes that would curdle milk. Bram hanged a man once for a theft his brother committed — found out after, buried him proper, and carried the rope ever since. Show it to them, Bram."

Bram didn't move. But he reached up, slow, and drew from his collar a length of worn rope, knotted, black with years of handling. He held it where the firelight could find it, said nothing, and put it away again.

"That's our court," Sef said. "That knot. Every judgment this band makes gets tied into that rope by whoever made it, and we all carry knowing it. When we err, we don't issue a pardon. We carry the man we wronged in our heads until we die, and we tell his story to new recruits so they flinch sooner next time." She shrugged. "Is it clean? Nothing is clean. Is it *accountable*? Ask yourself, mapmaker, when did a crown court last hang its own error around its neck and wear it home?"

Dunmore was silent for a long moment. The fire popped. Somewhere up the slope a nightbird called twice and thought better of a third.

"You're describing vengeance with bookkeeping," he said finally.

"I'm describing memory with consequences," Sef answered. "Yours has ledgers too. Yours just pretends the ink is holy."

* * *

It was the boy who shifted the shape of the night. He untangled himself from Dunmore's side, crawled forward into the full ring of firelight, and sat down cross-legged in front of Sef like a small magistrate taking his bench. The whole band went quiet. Even the twins stopped dreaming in unison.

He pointed at Sef. Then at the dark hills beyond the fire. Then he pressed his fist against his chest, over the mark beneath his collarbone, and made a motion — dragging, deliberate — as though pulling something out of himself and laying it on the ground between them.

Then he waited.

"He's asking," Dunmore said slowly, "what you'd do. If it were yours. If someone held your claim."

"That right?" Sef asked the boy directly.

The boy nodded once. Precise. Terrible in its precision.

Sef considered him. Around a fire, in the hills, with her band watching and a rope-knot of old griefs hanging in every silence, she decided the boy had earned the same honesty she'd give any sworn hand.

"If it were mine," she said, "and I were strong enough, I'd go take it back. And if I weren't strong enough, I'd find the ones who were, and I'd owe them until it was done, and I'd hate owing, and I'd pay anyway." She tapped her own chest, mirror to his gesture. "Nobody sells me. Nobody sells anybody while I'm standing. That's not law. Law's whatever the richest man in the room says it is, and tonight the richest thing in this room is a debt on a child. So no. Not law. Just us."

The boy studied her for a long moment. Then he did something he had not done before, not once in all the days since Dunmore's door: he smiled. Small, crooked, gone almost before it arrived — but a smile, aimed straight at her, and Sef felt it land somewhere the knife never guarded.

"Careful," Dunmore murmured. "He'll follow you home."

"He can't," Sef said, but her voice had gone rougher than she intended. "We don't keep houses."

"He followed you into an ambush without a word," Dunmore said. "Seems keeping isn't required."

* * *

Later, when the band had banked the fire and the twins had claimed first sleep, Sef found the mapmaker awake, sketching by ember-light — of course he was; the man drew like other men breathed. She crouched beside him, out of earshot of the sleeping boy.

"You still think we're lawless," she said. Not a question.

"I think you're honest outlaws," Dunmore said. "Which is rarer than honest judges, but not the same as being right." He turned the sketch toward her: the camp, rendered in quick sure lines, each sleeper named in his small precise hand. "But I'll grant you this. In every courtroom I've ever drawn into a map, the building was marked with a little tower and everyone pretended the tower made it fair. Tonight I watched a woman explain her mistakes by showing a rope." He looked up. "My valley taught me that law is a wall. You're teaching me walls are just maps someone else drew."

"Now you're learning cartography," Sef said, standing, satisfied enough to leave the argument unwon. "Everything's territory. Question is who holds the pen."

She was three steps toward her watch when the boy stirred in his blankets and sat bolt upright, eyes open and fixed on the northern sky — on the thin sliver of moon just clearing the ridgeline, one turn gone, eleven to run. And in the dead language older than any kingdom, soft as ash falling, he said a single word that none of them knew, and Sef, who had heard brokers chant over weighed souls and had never once been afraid of words, felt the cold walk down her spine and settle in her boots.

Dunmore was already reaching for his compass.
