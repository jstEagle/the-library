## 10. First Turning

The moon had worn itself thin and then swollen full again, and Yara had counted every night of it against the marks he scratched into the doorframe of whatever shelter they'd found — a notch for each turning of the sky above them, a habit so ingrained from his mapping years that he did it half-asleep. Twelve notches now. Twelve turns remaining, if Maren's arithmetic held, and Maren's arithmetic always held.

He woke before dawn on the thirtieth morning out with cold in his bones and the boy's small weight against his side, and for a moment he simply lay there beneath the lean-to's tarpaulin and watched his breath fog and listened to the world decide whether it would rain.

They were camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower two days east of the River Vell, on a stretch of road that appeared on none of Yara's maps because it had not existed when he'd last surveyed the region — or perhaps it had, and the valley's blind ink had simply refused to record it. He was still adjusting to that. The world outside the valley was not larger than he'd drawn it; it was *stranger*. Roads wandered like drunkards. Rivers forked where no fork should be. On their ninth night, Sef's outriders had sworn to him that a hill they'd camped against had moved three miles west by morning, and when Yara checked his instruments — real instruments now, brass and glass and needle, none of which failed out here — the hill's shadow agreed with the outriders.

He had begun keeping two journals. One recorded what was. The other recorded what changed. The second journal filled faster.

Beside him, the boy slept with his fists curled under his chin the way children do when they are defending something small and precious even in dreams. He had done that since the first night, since Yara had wrapped him in a blanket that smelled of woodsmoke and road dust and the boy had not flinched. It had taken eleven days for the flinching to stop entirely. Yara had counted that too.

The boy still had no name that anyone could pronounce. His true name lived somewhere under his collarbone, branded into the sigil Maren had read aloud in her herb-smelling kitchen what felt like another life: *sold, sealed, payable upon the thirteenth turning.* Until the debt was paid or broken, the name itself was collateral, locked away behind the Court Beneath the Mountain, and so the boy went through the world as a silence shaped like a child.

But he was less silent than he had been.

Yara had noticed it first around the second week — small sounds, not words, but *approaching* words. A hummed note when Sef's fiddler played. A click of the tongue meaning *look there*, directed at birds, at strange rock formations, at Tam's ridiculous hat. And once, waking from a nightmare he would not describe, a sound like the beginning of a scream that the boy had caught between his teeth and swallowed, and had looked at Yara afterward with an expression of such furious concentration that Yara understood: the boy was practicing. Reclaiming his mouth one syllable at a time, the way a man relearns walking after a long illness.

Maren had warned them about this, in her letter sent by hawk to the crossroads shrine. *The tongue will come back crooked,* she'd written. *Common speech will feel like borrowed clothes. Be patient. Be gentle. And do not weep in front of him when it comes — children can smell grief, and grief tells them they've cost you something.*

Yara intended to obey all three instructions. He suspected he would fail at the third.

* * *

They broke camp in grey light. Sef's band had grown comfortable with the pair of them — or as comfortable as outlaws ever let themselves become. Tam had taken to carrying the boy on his shoulders during marches, announcing landmarks in a voice pitched for theater: *Behold, the mighty stump! Behold, the moderately impressive boulder!* The boy never laughed — Yara wasn't sure the machinery of laughter still worked in him — but he had developed a way of pressing both palms flat against Tam's head when he wanted to go faster, and Tam obliged, and the column behind them learned to smile without making noise about it.

Sef herself rode beside Yara at the front, as she usually did, though "rode" flattered her mount, a grey gelding with opinions.

"Two days to the Vell," she said. "Then the tollhouse."

Yara's hand found his journal strap and tightened. "You're certain it's on our road."

"I'm certain everything worth taking is on someone's road. That's how roads work." She glanced at him sidelong. "You knew this was coming, cartographer. You agreed."

"I agreed," Yara said carefully, "that we would look at your intelligence. Looking is not raiding."

"It's not *not* raiding either." Sef shrugged. "Bram swears the tollhouse at Vell Ford weighs souls. Weighs them, prices them, ships them south in iron boxes no bigger than bread loaves. If there's a ledger in that building with your boy's seller named in it, you want that ledger. Don't pretend otherwise. I've watched you draw maps of places you've never seen with your jaw set like that."

Yara didn't answer. The truth was that he *did* want the ledger, had wanted it since the night Maren read the sigil and said the word *seller* — because a sale required a seller, and a seller required a reason, and every reason he could imagine ended with a person in his own quiet valley who had done an unspeakable thing to a mute child. He had turned that thought over so many times its edges had worn smooth, and still it cut him.

Ahead, the boy walked beside Tam's horse with his hands in his pockets, kicking a stone along the road with the seriousness of a craftsman. He had gotten good at kicking stones. It was, Yara reflected, exactly the sort of skill a child develops when the world gives him nothing else to master.

"Look at him," Sef said quietly. "A month ago he wouldn't make eye contact. Now he kicks rocks like he owns the road."

"People heal," Yara said. "Slowly. In pieces."

"Mm." Sef was silent a moment. "My little brother was like that. After the magistrates took our farm." She said it flatly, the way people say things they have said to themselves many times. "Never came all the way back either. Some piece of him stayed in the courthouse, in the paperwork. I've spent twenty years trying to burn down paperwork."

"You burn courthouses."

"Paperwork," she repeated, with the ghost of a grin, "is a broad category."

* * *

They made the river country by late afternoon of the next day, and Yara felt the change before he saw it — a softness in the air, a green insistence, willows leaning over water the color of old pewter. The Vell ran wide and slow here, and on its far bank, half-hidden in reeds, stood the tollhouse: a squat stone building with a tower, a chain across the ford, and lanterns already lit against the dusk.

Sef called a halt in a hollow a mile back and laid out her plan on the ground with twigs and pebbles while the light died. Yara listened with half an ear and watched the boy with the other half, and what he saw made his chest ache in a way he could not name.

The boy was sitting apart, on a root, looking at the river. Not at the tollhouse — at the *river*. His lips were moving. Barely. Yara drifted closer without seeming to, the way he'd learned to, and caught the shape of what the boy was mouthing. Syllables. Over and over. Practicing.

He couldn't make out the word. But he recognized the posture. It was the same posture Yara himself used at night, hunched over the changing-world journal, rehearsing sentences about hills that moved and rivers that forked — the posture of a person trying to say something the world was not built to hold.

Dinner was barley and dried plums and Tam's endless commentary. The boy ate sitting pressed against Yara's side, which had become his custom, and afterward, when the fire had burned down to a red heart and the outlaws had scattered to their watches, Yara spread his blankets and the boy folded himself into them without asking, as he always did. The first few nights Yara had wondered whether he should discourage it. He had stopped wondering around the tenth night. Whatever childhood the boy had been robbed of, no one was going to rob him of this too.

The moon rose. Full again. One turn complete.

Yara lay awake beneath it doing the arithmetic he hated: twelve turns left. Roughly twelve months, if a turning meant a month, and Maren seemed sure it did. Three hundred and sixty-odd days to find a court beneath a mountain that appeared on no map, breach gates designed to admit only honest desire, and break a contract written in a language older than kingdoms. Against that: one cartographer whose maps sometimes came true, one outlaw captain with a grudge and thirty knives, one disgraced mage they hadn't found yet, and one small boy who could not speak.

It should have been despair. Instead, watching the moonlight move across the sleeping face beside him, Yara felt something harder and more durable than hope settle into him, like ballast. He was beginning to understand that he would not survive failing this. That some part of him had already decided the boy was his to protect, permanently, and that the decision had been made somewhere below thought, below fear, in the place where a person's actual life gets decided.

The boy stirred. Frowned. Opened his eyes.

For a moment he stared up at the branches and the moon beyond them, and his mouth worked — that careful, practicing motion — and then, in a voice rusty and small and cracked from disuse, he said:

"Home."

One word. Common speech, rough-edged, mispronounced at the end, *home* said almost like *holm*, like a word crossing a border and picking up an accent on the way. But unmistakable. His first word in the language of the living, spoken not to ask for food or water or help, not to warn of danger, not to name any of the hard things around them.

He had chosen *home*. And he was looking at Yara when he said it.

Yara's whole body went still, the way it did when he stood at the valley's edge and felt the boundary hum. He did not trust himself to speak. He did the thing instead that he had learned to do with the boy: he opened his arm, and the boy burrowed into it, and Yara pressed his cheek to the crown of that tangled head and breathed, slow and controlled, until the burning behind his eyes subsided into something he could carry.

*Do not weep in front of him,* Maren had written.

He didn't weep. He came close. He held it the way you hold a map in wind — both hands, elbows tight, knowing that a single gust could take it and being willing to be torn rather than let go.

"Yes," he managed finally, his voice rougher than he wanted. "Yes. We'll get you home."

The boy's hand found the front of Yara's shirt and gripped it, and after a while his breathing lengthened into sleep again, and Yara lay staring at the moon and understanding, with terrible clarity, what Maren had been warning him about. Grief tells children they've cost you something. But tenderness does too. Every kindness deepened the debt the world would come to collect, because the world had already shown them its collections department: iron boxes, soul-ledgers, courts beneath mountains.

To love the boy was to raise the stakes of losing him. There was no way around it. There was only forward.

* * *

He must have slept, because he dreamed — and the dreams had been strange all month, stranger since he'd crossed the valley line. This one was of ink. A sea of it, black and patient, and standing in the middle of the sea a figure holding a lantern, and the figure turned toward him and had no face, only the sigil, the contract mark, glowing on the skin where a face should be. And the figure spoke with a voice like a pen scratching:

*Everything drawn can be owned.*

He woke before dawn with the word still hanging in the air of the dream and the boy already awake beside him, watching him with those grave dark eyes.

"Bad dream?" Yara asked.

The boy considered the question with the seriousness he gave all questions. Then he shook his head — slowly, and then, puzzlingly, nodded — and pointed at Yara's chest.

At Yara's collarbone.

Where, Yara realized, his own skin had prickled all night, faint and warm, in exactly the place the boy bore his brand.

The boy said nothing. He couldn't. But his expression said enough: *I felt it too.*

Across the hollow, Sef was already saddling the grey, and Bram was checking the edges of things that had edges, and the mist was coming off the river in long white banners, and two days away a squat stone building waited with its lanterns and its ledgers and its iron boxes no bigger than bread loaves.

Yara put his hand flat against his own collarbone, over the prickle, over nothing he could see, and felt the warmth pulse once, gently, like a second heartbeat answering the first.

Twelve turns remained. And something, somewhere, had just begun counting *him* too.
