## 11. The Broker's Tollhouse

The tollhouse sat where the cart-road narrowed against the cliffs of the Greywater gorge, squat and stone-faced as a toad, its chimney breathing a thin gray smoke that smelled wrong even at half a mile downwind. Sef had crouched in the bracken above the road for the better part of an hour, watching the comings and goings, and everything she saw confirmed what she'd already decided.

Three guards on the gate. Two more on the roof, crossbows racked beside them. A wagon had come up from the south at midmorning, curtained and heavy-axled, and it had left again empty — which meant whatever it carried in had been small, and valuable, and not the kind of thing you weighed with scales meant for grain.

"Souls," she said under her breath, tasting the word like old gristle.

Beside her in the ferns, Wren shifted. "You're sure about this, cap?"

"No," Sef said honestly. "But I'm sure enough."

She was never sure, not entirely. That was the price of keeping a company of outlaws who answered to no crown and no charter: every choice was hers alone, made in the dark before dawn or the bracken before noon, and if it went wrong there was no law to blame but her own judgment. She'd learned to live with that the way a sailor learns to live with the sea — not by trusting it, but by respecting how easily it could kill her.

This time, though, the decision had come easy, and that itself was strange.

It was the boy who'd decided it, though he didn't know it. Three nights past, camped in the ruins of an old shepherd's bothy, Sef had woken to find him standing at the edge of the firelight, staring east toward the gorge with an expression she couldn't read — or rather, an expression she could read too well. She'd seen that look on freed captives before, on men who'd spent years in a debtor's pit and still woke reaching for walls that weren't there. The boy couldn't speak — not their tongue, barely any tongue at all — but his eyes went to the tollhouse road the way a dog's eyes go to the kennel it was beaten in.

He wasn't afraid of it. He was *drawn* to it. And that frightened Sef more than any fear would have.

So here they were: nine of them in the bracken, watching a toad-shaped building breathe wrong smoke, waiting for the sun to swing behind the western crags and put the gorge road in shadow.

She looked back along the line of her people, laid out in the ferns like beads on a string. Wren, steady as ever. The twins, Ferro and Cass, grinning at each other in the way that always preceded property damage. Old Tam with his crowbar and his bad knee. Dree, who could pick a lock by feel in the dark. Bram at the rear, big and quiet, checking the straps on his shield for the third time because Bram checked everything three times. And further back, half-hidden, two figures who had no business being anywhere near a fight.

Yara Dunmore, the mapmaker, with the boy pressed close against his side.

Sef still hadn't entirely made up her mind about the cartographer. Nine days on the road together, and he remained the most contradictory man she'd ever traveled with. He flinched at violence — actually flinched, like a horse at a whip — and yet he'd walked into a burning tollhouse ambush without hesitation once the plan was explained. He complained constantly about the danger, catalogued every risk in that dry, precise voice of his, and then did the thing anyway. He distrusted her outlaws on principle, called them *vigilantes* and *lawless* with the disapproval of a magistrate, and yet he'd sat up two nights running drawing maps for them free of charge — roads, patrols, garrisons, water sources — with an accuracy that had made Wren whistle low and long when she first unrolled them.

"He draws like the ground owes him money," Wren had said.

And the boy — the boy trusted him completely. That counted for more than anything else, in Sef's arithmetic. Children knew. Children had always known. You could lie to a magistrate, lie to a priest, lie to your own mother, but you couldn't lie to a child who'd been sold, because they'd learned to smell intent the way wolves smelled blood.

The boy caught her looking and stared back, unblinking, those dark eyes older than his face. Then he did the thing he'd started doing lately: he touched two fingers to his collarbone, right over the mark beneath his shirt, and looked at her with a question in them.

*Is this where it ends?*

"Not today, little one," Sef murmured. "Today we take something back."

The sun slid behind the crags. Shadow poured down the gorge wall like water finding a channel.

Sef stood up out of the bracken and gave the signal — two fingers, a flat sweep — and her company came off the hillside like weather.

* * *

The thing about tollhouses, Sef had learned over eleven years of robbing them, was that they were built by accountants and defended by bored men. The brokers who ran them thought in ledgers: so many guards per shipment, so many silver bits per guard, calculated to the minimum profitable margin. They never budgeted for the unexpected, because the unexpected didn't appear in last quarter's columns.

Ferro and Cass hit the gate guards first, out of the shadow of the cliff with slingshot stones to the helmet and boot to the ribs before either man finished shouting. Dree was through the postern door four heartbeats later — the lock was old, and locks were honest in a way people weren't; they told you exactly how much trouble they'd be. Tam went in behind her with the crowbar raised, mostly for the noise of it, because noise was half the battle. A raid that sounded like twenty men needed only nine.

Wren took the roof ladder with a drawn blade and came back down shaking her head. "Roof boys ran for the treeline. Didn't even loose a bolt."

"Cowards or smart men," Sef said. "Same thing, usually."

The interior of the tollhouse was nothing like she expected, and exactly like she should have expected.

She'd robbed tax collectors' way-stations before — strongboxes, coin, seals, liquor. This was not that. The main hall had been converted into something between a countinghouse and a chapel. Long tables ran its length, and on the tables, instead of coin, were objects. Small ones. A tin whistle. A wedding ring. A child's milk tooth threaded on a cord. A lock of hair gone gray. Each one tagged with a lead seal stamped with the broker's sigil — a circle bisected by a vertical line, like an eye shut forever — and each one humming, faintly, with a wrongness Sef felt in her teeth rather than heard.

"They're tokens," Yara said behind her. His voice had gone thin. "Pledges. Collateral." He stood in the doorway with the boy's hand crushed in his, and his face had the gray look of a man reading his own death warrant written in someone else's hand. "A soul can't travel. It has to be *anchored* somewhere while the debt runs. These are anchors."

"You know this how?" Sef asked sharply.

"I don't." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger. "I know it the way I know north. I just — know it."

That was another entry in Sef's growing ledger of things-that-didn't-add-up-about-the-cartographer. She filed it away. There were more urgent matters. Like the fact that the boy had let go of Yara's hand and was walking down the line of tables, slow, touching nothing, his face lit by some terrible recognition.

At the third table he stopped.

He reached out and picked up a token — a small wooden bird, carved crude and painted blue, the kind of toy a father whittles for a son on a winter night — and held it in both palms, and made a sound Sef had never heard from him. Not speech. Not quite weeping. Something between, like wind through a broken shutter.

Yara crossed the room in four strides and gathered him in, and the boy clutched the bird to his chest and shook.

"It's his," Yara said hoarsely. "From before. From wherever they took him from."

"Then the records are close," Sef said, "because brokers don't leave pledges lying loose. There'll be a registry." She turned. "Dree! Back office. Find me the ledger room."

"Already found it, cap." Dree's voice came from behind a barred door at the hall's far end, followed by the crack of a lock surrendering. "And you're going to want to see this. All of you."

* * *

The ledger room was cold, windowless, and lit by a single oil lamp Dree had found. Shelves lined the walls floor to ceiling, and every shelf was filled with ledgers — hundreds of them, bound in leather the color of dried blood, spines stamped with dates going back further than Sef cared to count.

Bram stood in the middle of the room holding one open, and he wasn't moving, and Bram not moving was like a river not flowing.

"Cap," he said quietly. "You need to read this."

She took the ledger from him. It was the current year's register, and it was organized the way everything broker-made was organized: ruthlessly, precisely, without a single wasted entry. Each page held a contract. Name of debtor. Name of seller. Nature of pledge. Terms. Maturity. And at the bottom of each entry, in red ink, a valuation — a number assigned to a human soul, rounded to the nearest copper, the way you'd value a pig or a plot of turnips.

Sef had known what brokers were. She'd hated them since she was fourteen years old and watched them carry her father out of their tenement in chains, sold by a landlord to cover six months' rent. But seeing it written down — seeing the whole architecture of it laid out in neat columns, the sheer *industry* of it, decades of it, souls processed like timber — made something go hot and quiet behind her ribs.

"We burn it all?" Ferro asked from the doorway. He said it hopefully, the way he said everything destructive.

"No," Sef said. "Not yet. We take the current registers and we burn nothing until we've read them." She flipped pages. "Every name in here is a trail. Every seller is a lead. We've spent years hunting brokers on the road — this is the road itself, written down."

Wren was already pulling volumes, stacking them into a satchel. Tam pried open a locked drawer and found it full of lead seals and stamping dies — the machinery of authenticity. Dree went shelf by shelf with a thief's efficiency, checking for hidden compartments, and found one behind the second-year ledgers: a slim book, separate from the rest, bound in black leather with no date on the spine.

"That one's different," Sef said.

"Everything about it is different," Dree agreed, handing it over.

It was the exceptions ledger. Where the main registers recorded the trade, this one recorded its corrections — disputed contracts, failed collections, pledges that had been contested or reclaimed. Sef turned pages of dense entries, most of them dry administrative notes, and then stopped.

Because there, halfway down a page dated eight months past, was an entry written in a different hand than the rest — larger, more hurried, the ink blotted as if the writer's hand had shaken:

*Contract 4471. Pledge: soul of male child, age approx. five years. Seller declared residence: the Veiled Valley. Broker of record declined comment on method of acquisition. Note: acquisition irregular. No witness signature obtained. Flagged for review.*

*Review closed by order of the Under-Crown. Contract stands. Maturity: thirteenth turning of the moon from date of sale.*

Below it, in the margin, someone had added a single line in pencil, faint, almost erased:

*Who sells a child from a valley that doesn't exist?*

Sef read it twice. Then a third time, slower, feeling each word settle into place like stones dropping into still water.

"The Veiled Valley," she said aloud.

Behind her, she heard Yara stop breathing.

She turned. The cartographer stood in the doorway with the boy's wooden bird still clutched in one white-knuckled fist, and his face had gone the color of the ledger paper.

"That's my valley," he said. "That's — the valley doesn't trade with anyone. The valley doesn't *let anything in*. No one comes out to sell. There are no brokers within a hundred miles of—" He stopped himself. Started again, quieter, as if arguing with the words as he said them. "There is no one in that valley who could have done this."

"Somebody did," Sef said. She held up the ledger, turning it so he could see the entry. "Somebody walked out of your valley carrying a five-year-old boy, walked him to a broker, and sold him. Somebody who knew the valley well enough to leave it, and knows the outside world well enough to find a broker and strike a deal." She let that hang in the lamplit air. "How many people does that describe?"

"I don't—" Yara's voice cracked. He looked down at the bird in his fist, then at the boy, who had come to stand beside him and was looking at the ledger with an expression Sef couldn't bear to examine too closely. Not shock. Not surprise. A terrible, patient familiarity, like a wound being re-opened just to confirm it was real. "Maren might know. The herbwoman. She knew the old tongue. But even she—"

"Even she what?"

"She told me the valley keeps itself clean. That's why magic won't stay there. That's why my instruments fail at the border. We tell ourselves it's because the valley is *honest*." He laughed, and there was nothing funny in it. "We tell ourselves we're the one place the world's rot hasn't reached."

"And now you know better," Sef said. Not cruelly. There was no kindness available that wouldn't be a lie, and Sef had never lied to her people, and whether she liked it or not — and she was beginning, uncomfortably, to — this man and this boy were becoming her people.

"Yes," Yara said. "Now I know better."

The boy tugged at Yara's sleeve. When Yara looked down, the boy pointed at the ledger — at the words *the Veiled Valley* — and then pointed at himself, and then, slowly, deliberately, he pointed at Yara. His dark eyes moved between the page and the cartographer's face with a question in them so naked it hurt to look at.

*Someone from your home did this to me. Who?*

"I don't know," Yara whispered. "I swear it, I don't know. But I'm going to find out."

Sef believed him. That was the worst part. She believed him completely, and belief didn't help, because the entry wasn't going to change. Somewhere back in that clean, honest, unmappable valley, someone had taken a sleeping child by the hand and walked him out into the wide world and sold him to a court beneath a mountain. Someone with a name Yara probably ate dinner across from. Someone whose face hung in his memory next to all the other faces of home, indistinguishable, innocent, until now.

"There's one more thing," Dree said from the shelves. Her voice was odd — tight, careful. "Cap. The broker of record on this contract. It lists who handled the sale."

"Read it," Sef said.

Dree read it. The name meant nothing to Sef. But Yara made a sound like a man taking a blade between the ribs, quick and involuntary, and when Sef turned, he was staring at the page with his lips moving around the name, silent, testing it, the way a man tests a tooth he suspects is rotten.

"You know them," Sef said.

"I know everyone in the valley," Yara said. "Four hundred souls. I've mapped every field they farm and every path they walk." He closed his eyes. Opened them. "I know them."

He didn't say the name aloud. He tucked the ledger under his arm instead, carefully, like something fragile and dangerous, and walked out of the room with the boy's hand in his, and neither of them looked back.

* * *

They fired the tollhouse on the way out — the buildings only, the ledgers already secured in satchels and saddlebags. Sef stood in the yard and watched the wrong-smelling chimney catch light along with everything else, and felt no satisfaction in it. Usually a burn like this felt like balance restored, a column struck from the brokers' books. Tonight it felt like opening a wound to see how deep it went.

They rode east through the gathering dusk, putting miles between themselves and the smoke. Sef dropped back to ride beside Yara, who sat his borrowed horse like a sack of meal and stared at the road ahead without seeing it.

"The name in the ledger," she said, keeping her voice low, under the hoofbeats. "You don't have to tell me tonight."

"Don't I?"

"No. But hear me, cartographer, because I'll only say this once." She let her mare match his pace, close enough that their knees nearly touched. "In eleven years of keeping justice outside the law, I've learned one thing truer than all the rest. People aren't betrayed by enemies. Enemies can't reach deep enough. It's always the friendly face, the shared table, the neighbor who waters your garden while you're away. That's who sells a child. Because only a friend knows the price a child will bring."

Yara's jaw worked. In the last light, his face looked haggard and older than his years, a map with new lines drawn hard across familiar country.

"And when we find them?" he asked finally. "What then? What do you do to someone like that, in your justice outside the law?"

Sef was quiet for a long moment. The road unspooled ahead of them through the purple dusk, toward the mountain they still couldn't see, toward a debt still ticking down moon by moon.

"Depends what we find when we dig," she said at last. "Sometimes there's greed underneath. Sometimes fear. Sometimes somebody held a knife to *their* family and chose, the way people choose, badly and humanly." She shrugged. "My justice makes room for all of it. But make no mistake, Yara Dunmore — whoever walked that boy out of your valley is going to answer for it. To us, or to the court below, or to you. One way or another, everybody's ledger gets read."

Ahead of them, the boy rode wrapped in Bram's cloak on Wren's saddle, the little wooden bird held against his chest, his eyes fixed on the eastern horizon where the mountains were beginning to show themselves — dark teeth against the last red of the sky.

And Sef, watching him, thought of the penciled note in the margin of the exceptions ledger, and found herself asking the same question the some unknown clerk had asked, and hating that she had no answer for it either.

*Who sells a child from a valley that doesn't exist?*

The answer was riding somewhere behind them, in a valley that had just stopped being safe, and Sef had a feeling that before this road ended, they would all wish they'd never learned his name.
