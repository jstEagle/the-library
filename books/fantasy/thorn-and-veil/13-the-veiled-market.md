## 13. The Veiled Market

The boy had been dreaming of the market for three nights before he understood that it was not a dream.

In the dream, there was always fog the color of old milk, and lanterns hanging from nothing at all, and voices that came wrapped in cloth so they sounded like they were being spoken through walls. In the dream, his chest did not ache. That was how he knew it was a dream, because when he woke, the hollow place inside him where his name used to be would open its mouth again and swallow everything warm.

But on the third morning, waking beside the dead fire with Bram's absence sitting heavy on all of them like snow on a roof, he found a thread of fog clinging to his sleeve.

Real fog. Wet. Smelling of iron and honey.

He sat very still and looked at it until Sef kicked dirt over the coals and said, *Up, sprout. We move before the light does.* She always talked like the world was listening in and might betray them. Maybe she was right. He had learned that much about the wide country beyond the valley: the world listened. The world kept accounts.

He did not know why his feet knew the way. That frightened him a little, but only a little, because most things about himself frightened him only a little now, the way you stop flinching from a bruise you have had so long it feels like part of your body. His feet knew the way down out of the gully, past the split boulder shaped like a sleeping dog, along a streambed where the water ran backward whenever no one watched it. His feet knew, which meant something in him knew, which meant the market had been in him all along, folded up small behind the place where his voice had been taken, waiting for the road to come close enough to unfold.

He tugged Yara's sleeve. Yara was walking with his head down, drawing in his mind the way he did, his fingers twitching against his thigh as if holding an invisible pen. The boy liked watching Yara draw real maps better than anything except eating, but the invisible maps made him sad in a way he could not have explained: a man drawing a country that wasn't there yet, wanting it into being. It seemed lonely. It seemed brave. Both at once, like most true things.

Yara looked down. The boy pointed back the way they had come, then pointed the other way, off the track entirely, toward the low gray hills where the fog stood like a wall someone had forgotten to finish building.

Sef saw it first. She put her hand on her knife without seeming to move at all. "We're not going in there."

"He wants us to," Yara said.

"I don't care what he wants. I care what *it* wants."

The boy looked at Sef and tried to make his face say what he could not: that the market did not want anything the way wolves wanted things. The market only traded. Wanting was what happened after, when you walked away carrying your new purchase and found you could not remember why you had ever thought you needed your old thing, whatever the old thing had been.

Then, because words came hard and slow and cost him something every time, he spent one.

"Names," he said.

His voice cracked on it, thin as winter ice. Everyone went still. It was only his fourth word since the valley, counting *home*, and they treated his words like coins dropped on stone — everyone looking to see if they rang true.

Sef crouched to his height. Up close her eyes were the brown of river water over stones, quick and unreadable and, he had decided weeks ago, kind underneath, though she would gut anyone who said so. "Names," she repeated. "What about names?"

He touched his own chest, over the hollow place. Then he pointed at the fog.

* * *

They argued for a while in their grown way, which meant saying the same things louder, but in the end the fog decided for them, because the fog began to walk.

That was the second time he knew the dream was not a dream: the fog moved aside to let them pass, folding open like a curtain drawn by hands he could not see, and behind it the hills were gone and there was a road instead, pale and smooth as the inside of a shell, going down between banks of white flowers that turned to follow them with their whole faces.

He led. Behind him came Yara, whose breathing had gone shallow and fast — the boy knew the sound of Yara being terrified and refusing to show it; it was the same as Yara being excited and refusing to show it, only quieter — and behind Yara came Sef and Dagny and little Wren-who-was-not-little, the three who remained of the eight who had set out. They carried their grief for Bram quietly, the way you carry a full bucket, careful not to spill.

The road smelled of iron and honey. The boy remembered that smell from before, from the life that lived behind the locked door in his head. Remembering hurt, so he did it anyway, because Maren had told him once — no. Someone had told him once. A voice like bread crusts and dried lavender. *Remembering hurts less than forgetting, little bird. Forgetting goes on hurting forever.* He held onto the sentence even though he could not hold onto the voice.

The flowers stopped following them when the road reached the market.

There was no wall around the market. There didn't need to be one. The market simply ended, the way a sentence ends, and beyond its last stall there was nothing at all, a whiteness so complete that looking at it made his eyes feel like they were being washed.

Lanterns hung from nothing. He counted them out of habit — he counted everything, because numbers were safe, numbers had never been sold — and got to forty-one before the numbers began to swim and he gave up. Under the lanterns stood stalls of black wood that seemed to grow up out of the ground rather than be built, and at each stall sat or stood a trader, and every trader wore a veil.

Not one veil. Many. Some wore veils over veils over veils, layered gauze falling to the ground so they looked like ghosts wearing wedding dresses. One trader near the entrance wore a veil stitched all over with tiny silver bells, and when she moved, the sound was not ringing but whispering, hundreds of tiny whispers saying *yes, yes, yes* in a language he almost understood.

And everywhere, everywhere, the goods.

He had expected, without knowing he expected it, bottles and jars, smoke in boxes, moths in cages. There were some of those. But most of the wares were stranger and plainer. On one table lay rows of names written on slips of paper so old the paper had gone soft as cloth. At another stall, names hung from hooks like smoked fish. A trader with six veils was measuring a length of ribbon onto which memories had been woven — the boy could see them flickering in the threads: a red door opening, snow falling upward, a woman laughing so hard she had to sit down on a kitchen floor.

A man stood at that stall, arguing softly with the trader, turning the ribbon over and over in his hands. As the boy passed, the man chose a small piece and paid for it with a tooth, and the moment the tooth left his mouth he smiled with the gap showing, wept without any change in his face, and walked away lighter, humming.

"That one bought back his daughter's christening," murmured a voice near the boy's ear, a voice like paper. He jumped. A trader had drifted alongside him, veils stirring in a wind that touched no one else. "He sold it years ago to pay a gambling debt. Memory keeps, you know. Better than wine. Better than love, some say." The veils tilted, considering him. "You've been here before, little ledger."

*I haven't,* he thought, hard.

"Mm," said the trader, in the tone of a shopkeeper hearing a child deny touching the jam, and drifted away.

*Little ledger.* The words followed him down the aisle. He touched the mark beneath his collarbone, through his shirt, the sigil that Yara called the contract, the brand that itched worse the closer they came to the mountain. Was that what the brokers had made him? Not a boy with a debt, but a page in a book? A line of writing that walked around and ate porridge?

Yara's hand settled on his shoulder. Yara's hand was always finding his shoulder lately; the boy had begun to expect it there the way he expected his own heartbeat. "Stay close," Yara said quietly. "Touch nothing. Trade nothing. If anyone speaks to you, look at me and I'll answer."

*You can't answer this,* the boy thought. But he nodded, because Yara needed the nod more than he needed to believe it.

Sef had gone strange and stiff, the way she did in lawful cities. Her eyes moved constantly, pricing exits. Dagny muttered under her breath, counting the veiled figures and getting a different number every time, and growing angrier about it. Wren alone seemed unafraid; Wren was examining a stall of bottled lullabies with the expression of a woman judging horses.

"It's the same as any market," Wren said finally. "Look. Haggling. Queue-jumping. That one's watering her stock." She nodded at a trader sprinkling drops from a vial onto wilting memories laid out like herbs, and indeed the memories brightened, greened, became vivid again. "Only the coin's different."

"The coin's never different," Sef said. "It's just sometimes harder to count."

* * *

The boy did not know which stall he was looking for until he was standing in front of it.

It stood apart from the others, deeper in, where the lanterns burned a color he had no word for — the color of the inside of a closed eyelid. The trader there wore only one veil, plain gray, and sat perfectly still behind a table that was bare except for a scale. Old brass, two pans, the kind Yara used for weighing ink powders. But the pans did not hold weights. One pan held darkness. The other pan held nothing at all, and still the beam swung slowly, slowly, comparing them.

The boy stopped. His heart was going hard. Behind his collarbone, the sigil had begun to warm, a coal wrapped in skin, and he felt it the way you feel weather changing.

The veiled trader lifted her head.

"You brought it back," she said. Her voice was neither old nor young. It was the voice of someone reading aloud a letter written by someone else. "How thoughtful of you. We do misplace so many small things."

Beside him, Yara went rigid. "It," he repeated. "You'll want to choose another word."

"Will I?" The trader tilted her head, and the veil shifted, and beneath it — nothing. Not emptiness, exactly. More like the moment between lightning and thunder, stretched out and given a shape. "And what will you do about it, mapmaker? You are far from your valley. Farther than you know." She turned the veil back toward the boy, and he felt seen all the way through, felt read, felt the way a book must feel. "Thirteenth turn coming. The mountain is sharpening its pens. And here you are, walking him through my market like a lamb through a wool merchant's fair."

"We need a gate," Yara said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice he used for dangerous facts. "A way under the mountain that isn't the front door. They say names can buy that here. Ways in. True roads."

"Everything is for sale here," the trader agreed pleasantly. "That is the entire point of the place. The question is never whether we sell. The question is what you can pay." She gestured at the empty pan of the scale. "Sit, if you like. Browse. But understand the rules, because the rules are the only honest things in this market, and I will tell them to you once.

"We deal in what people carry that cannot be seen. Names. Memories. Years. Regrets — regrets fetch a poor price, too many people are selling. First loves fetch well; they're rare, most folk keep those till the end. Promises fetch highest of all, but only promises freely broken or freely kept; the ones in between are worthless." The veil rippled, which he understood, somehow, was laughter. "And payment is always subtraction, mapmaker. Never addition. We do not add to you. We take away. Whatever you buy, you buy with a piece of yourself, and the market chooses which piece, and you will not know what is gone until you reach for it and find the shelf empty.

"You may leave whenever you wish," she added, gently. "No one here will stop you. No one here has ever once stopped anyone. That is the horror of the place, if you want my opinion, and no one does."

The boy looked at the scale. At the pan of darkness. And deep behind the locked door in his head, something stirred and pressed its ear against the wood, listening, and he realized that he had not come to the market by accident at all. His feet had known the way because the market had called them, yes — but also because somewhere in him, in whatever the brokers had left behind when they took his name, there was a memory of this table, this scale, this voice.

He had been here before. Something of his had already lain in these pans.

He reached out — Yara's hand shot out to catch his wrist, but slow, too slow — and touched the empty pan.

The beam tipped. Just slightly. Toward nothing.

"There it is," the trader said softly, almost tenderly, the way you speak over a cradle. "Still on the books. Still owed." The veil turned toward Yara now, and the lantern-light guttered, and all down the aisles of the market the whispering bells went quiet at once, forty-one lanterns and more bending their flame toward the boy as if the whole market had leaned in to hear.

"You want a gate under the mountain," the trader said. "Very well. That is for sale. But the price, mapmaker—" the veil inclined, courteous as a curtsy, terrible as an avalanche, "—the price is a memory. And not just any memory. The market will choose. Shall we see what you can spare?"

Yara's hand tightened on the boy's shoulder. When he answered, his voice was steady, and that steadiness frightened the boy more than the empty pan had.

"Show me the scales," Yara said.
