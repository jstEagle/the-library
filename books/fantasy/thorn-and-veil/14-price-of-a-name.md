## 14. Price of a Name

The market had no fixed shape.

Yara understood that much by the time they reached it — or rather, by the time it permitted them to arrive. For two days after the boy had begun walking in his sleep, turning always the same direction at every fork and crossroads, the company had followed him through country Yara could not map. Not because his instruments failed, as they did at the valley's edge — but because the land itself declined to be recorded. He would sketch a ridge at dusk and find, come morning, that the ridge had moved a half-mile east, politely, as if embarrassed to be caught in one place. Rivers braided and unbraided. A stand of birches appeared twice on opposite sides of a hill, identical down to the lightning scar on the seventh trunk.

"It doesn't like being written down," Sef said, watching him crumple his fourth attempt. "Places like this exist because people forget them. You put them on paper, you start remembering, and then everyone starts finding them, and then they're just — places."

"Then how do we leave?"

"You don't," Sef said cheerfully. "You get let out."

The boy led them out. On the third morning he stopped at the lip of a hollow where fog pooled like milk in a bowl, took Yara's hand — which he almost never did — and stepped down into the white.

The market assembled itself around them as they descended. It was not that stalls stood waiting; it was that Yara's eyes kept discovering them, one at a time, each new sight crowding out the last so that the whole never quite added up. Canopies of grey silk. Counters of driftwood and bone. Traders in veils of every weight and weave — some gauzy enough to show the ghost of a mouth beneath, some so heavy and dark they seemed less worn than inhabited. No voices called out wares. Instead, when Yara passed, each vendor turned toward him with the slow attention of sunflowers following light, and he felt himself *appraised* — not his purse, not his blade, but something further in.

He kept the boy close. He kept his hand on the strap of his satchel, where the maps lived. And he kept telling himself that the cold crawling up his spine was only the fog.

"They deal in three things here," Ilvane murmured. She had come with them from her exile's cottage, wrapped in a traveling cloak that made her look smaller and older than she ever allowed herself to seem indoors. "Names. Memories. And futures — though no one with sense trades in futures. The exchange rates are obscene."

"And we need—"

"The gates." She said it before he could, gently, the way one says a diagnosis. "Yes. The mountain has seven approaches, but six are lies. The court lets rumor spread about them precisely so that thieves and desperate fathers waste their lives knocking on stone that opens for no one. The true gate — the Gate of Teeth — its location isn't secret. It's *unrememberable*. People can be standing in front of it and walk away without knowing they've seen it. The only way to carry the knowledge is to buy it back into your head. And the price—"

She didn't finish. Ahead of them, a trader had risen from behind a counter of black wood, and the veil lifting from that face was like a page turning.

* * *

The booth sat apart from the others, at what might generously have been called the market's center, though center implied a geometry Yara couldn't confirm. Its awning was stitched from what looked like pages — hundreds of them, torn and layered, some still bearing legible words. The trader beneath it was tall, sexless beneath layered robes, and wore a veil of plain white linen through which no features showed at all. Where a face should have pressed against the cloth, there was simply smoothness, as if the veil were draped over an absence.

It gestured to a pair of stools. It did not speak.

"I'll do the talking," Ilvane said, low. "Yara — whatever it asks, do not answer quickly. These creatures read hesitation like a ledger reads ink. Hesitate too little and they think you don't value what you're selling. Hesitate too much and they raise the price, because now they know exactly how much it hurts."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave poorer than you came. They take a token memory regardless. An hour ago, probably. Something small. You won't miss it until you do."

Yara sat. Up close, the trader smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any smell. From somewhere beneath the counter it produced a scale — brass, ancient, its two pans hung from chains fine as hair. In one pan it placed a smooth river stone. The pan did not move. The stone simply sat there, weighing nothing, waiting to weigh something.

Then the trader spoke, and its voice was the strangest thing yet: familiar. It sounded like Yara's own voice, pitched slightly wrong, the way a voice sounds in memory.

"*What do you seek?*"

"The mountain's gate," Ilvane said. "The true one. We wish to purchase the knowledge of its approach."

A pause. The veiled head tilted toward Yara, though it had no visible eyes to tilt with.

"*Not you,*" it said — still in Yara's voice. "*Him.*"

Ilvane's jaw tightened. "I'm brokering—"

"*Knowledge of the gate must live in the skull that carries it.*" The trader set both hands flat on the counter. "*And the payment must be drawn from the same skull. That is the law of this market, older than your kingdoms, older than the court below. What is bought with another's coin belongs to another. So. Boy who draws the world—"*

Yara realized his hands had gone cold on his knees.

"—*what will you pay?*"

* * *

They negotiated in the strange silence of the place, Ilvane translating intent into terms while the trader answered in Yara's stolen voice, each word landing in his chest like a knock on a door he hadn't known was there.

Memories, it explained — in Yara's voice, describing Yara's life back to him — were weighed on three axes. Vividness. Uniqueness. Love.

"Vivid memories are common," Ilvane recited, her broker-face firmly in place, though her knuckles were white where they gripped her staff. "Everyone remembers their worst wound, their first kiss, the day their father died. Unique memories are worth more — the taste of a fruit no one else has eaten, a conversation no other soul witnessed. But love-weighted memories — those are the currency of kings. A memory soaked in genuine love weighs more than a decade of ordinary days. The traders hoard them. They're the closest things mortals make to souls."

"And what does the gate-knowledge cost?" Yara asked. His voice came out steadier than he felt.

The trader considered him. The white veil did not move, and yet he had the distinct sensation of being *read* — turned over like a page, every line examined.

"*You have few such memories,*" it said at last, and there was something almost apologetic in the borrowed timbre. "*Your childhood was sealed away from you. Your mother—"*

"Don't," Yara said.

The trader stopped. Waited. The courtesy of it was unbearable.

"She died when I was four," Yara said. "In the valley. I remember—" He closed his eyes. He had spent thirty years guarding these fragments the way a miser guards coins too few to spend: her hands, flour-dusted, pressing dough flat. Her voice humming something without words. The warmth of a kitchen and the smell of cedar smoke. Her face—

Her face was—

His eyes opened. The fragments were all there. Hands. Voice. Warmth. Cedar smoke. And where her face should have been, where it had always been, there was a soft blankness, like a watermark in paper, like a region of the map marked *here be nothing*. He had assumed, all his life, that grief had worn the image away naturally, the way rain wears stone. Everyone lost faces. Everyone said so.

But the trader's veil tilted, and in Yara's own voice it said, softly: "*Ah. Someone has already purchased it. Long ago. The transaction left a residue — grief without object, mourning without a mourner's face. That is why you draw coastlines so obsessively, cartographer. You have been trying to redraw her this whole time.*"

The stool seemed to drop away beneath him. He heard Sef swear softly somewhere behind him; heard the boy make a small sound, wordless, urgent. Ilvane's hand landed on his shoulder and gripped hard enough to hurt.

"That's not possible," Yara managed. "Who would—"

"*That question is not free,*" said the trader. "*Nothing here is free. But note it well: the residue remains. Grief is the part of a memory that cannot be sold separately. It stays with the seller forever. You have been paying interest on a debt you never took out.*"

Yara looked down at his own hands. Mapmaker's hands. Ink-stained, calloused, steady on the pen. Thirty years of drawing coastlines. Thirty years of a valley that refused to be mapped, and a man who mapped it anyway, compulsively, tenderly, as if the valley were a patient and he a physician who could not name the disease.

He had thought it was obsession. It had never once occurred to him that it might be love.

"What do you want from me?" he asked quietly. "If her face is already gone. What's left to trade?"

The trader produced, from beneath the counter, a small clay jar sealed with wax. It set it on the counter between them, next to the scale, and did not explain it. It didn't need to. Yara understood vessels. He had spent his life putting things inside borders.

"*The knowledge of the gate weighs this much,*" the trader said. "*We require payment of equal mass. Her face is sold — but her voice remains. Her hands. The warmth of her kitchen. The humming. The cedar smoke. Sell us the rest. Every fragment. And the residue — the grief itself — goes in the jar with them. Cleanly. You will remember that you had a mother. You will remember that you loved her. You will feel nothing when you say so.*"

Silence. Somewhere across the market, a curtain swayed in a wind Yara could not feel.

"No," said Sef, from behind him. Flat, immediate, captain's-final. "No. Find another way."

"There isn't one," Ilvane said. Her voice had gone very quiet, which Yara had learned meant she was frightened. "This is the only door. I told you all what this market was. I told him."

"You told him it would cost *a* memory. One. Not—"

"Not everything I have left of her?" Yara said.

Sef rounded on him. In the grey light of the page-stitched awning her face was fierce and young and angry. "You don't even know what you're carrying until it's gone. I've seen men sell memories here. Little ones. The color of a wedding dress. The name of a dog. They laugh it off for a day and then they wake at night reaching for something that isn't there anymore, and they can't even say what they're reaching for, and that hole *never closes*, Yara. Never. You'd be trading the last real thing you own for directions."

"For *him*," Yara said.

The boy was standing beside the counter now, looking up at the trader, and Yara saw that the child had gone very still — the particular stillness of prey that has decided not to run. Then the boy did something he had never done in all their months together. He climbed onto the empty stool across the counter, folded his small mud-brown hands on the black wood, and waited, solemn as a magistrate, as though the transaction were his to conduct and he had merely been permitting the adults their ceremony.

"*He understands,*" said the trader in Yara's voice, and there was something in it that might, in a kinder instrument, have been gentleness. "*Better than you do. He knows what it is to have pieces of yourself held elsewhere. He is asking you not to become him.*"

"I know what I'm asking," Yara said.

He looked at the boy. The boy looked back. And Yara found, to his astonishment, that he wasn't afraid — or rather, that the fear had arranged itself into something usable, the way fear does at the moment a decision stops being a decision and becomes a fact. Twelve turns remained. Bram was dead. The hounds of the brokers had their scent. There was no version of the road ahead that did not pass through this booth.

Besides. Some traitor part of him whispered. *Besides, you've already lost her face. How much worse can an empty room be?*

He knew the answer even as he thought it. The room wouldn't be empty. The room would be swept. Swept rooms don't echo.

"Terms," he said aloud.

* * *

They were simple, as such things always are. The trader uncapped the clay jar and set it open on the counter. It placed the river stone in the scale's empty pan, and the pan sank slowly until it hung level with the weighted one, and Yara understood that the stone now weighed the gate — the whole impossible, unrememberable truth of it, compressed to a pebble's mass.

"*Place your hands on the stone,*" said the trader. "*Think of her. All of her. Hold nothing back — a withheld fragment voids the sale and doubles the price. When the scale tips, it is done, and it cannot be undone by anyone, anywhere, ever.*"

"Yara." Sef's hand on his arm. "There are seven turns left. Seven moons. We could find another way, we could—"

"Sef." He covered her hand with his own. "You keep the peace outside the law because someone has to. This is my version of it. Let me do mine."

Her grip tightened. Then, with visible effort, she let go — and stepped back, and gave him, of all things, an outlaw's salute: two fingers to the brow, mocking and sincere at once. "Cartographer," she said. "Draw us a way in."

He turned to the boy last. Knelt, so they were eye to eye. The child's face — that thin, watchful, unsold face — searched his with an intensity far past his years, and then he reached out and pressed his palm flat against Yara's chest, over the heart, and held it there a long moment. A blessing or a measurement. Yara honestly could not tell. Perhaps, from the boy, they were the same thing.

"I'll come back with more than I left with," Yara told him. "That's a promise. Write it on me if you like."

The boy's mouth twitched. Almost a smile. He stepped down off the stool and went to stand with Sef, and took her hand, and did not look away.

Yara placed both hands on the river stone.

It was warm. That was the last thing he noticed clearly — that the stone was warm, warmer than brass had any right to be, warm as a kitchen, warm as cedar smoke, warm as—

*Flour-dusted hands pressing dough flat.*

The scale trembled.

*Her voice, humming something without words, and the hum rising now, filling the booth, filling the market, every veiled trader turning toward the sound the way the drowned turn toward light—*

The pan began to sink.

*Mother. Mother, I'm sorry. I'm sorry I'm selling you to buy a door. I hope wherever you are, you understand that doors matter, that some nights a door is the only mercy there is—*

*—the warmth of the kitchen, cedar smoke, her laughing at something his father said, her leaning down, her face coming closer, closer, the face he had carried for thirty years without knowing he was carrying it, the face arriving now like a ship long overdue, oh, there she is, there she is, there she—*

The scale tipped.

* * *

The silence afterward was total. Even the market's ambient hush seemed to hold its breath.

Yara opened his eyes. His hands rested on a cool river stone. The scale hung level. The clay jar stood capped and sealed on the counter, and something moved faintly inside it, the way a moth moves inside a lantern glass, and he looked at it with mild, detached curiosity, the way one looks at a stranger's luggage.

"Well?" Sef's voice, tight. "Do you know it? The gate?"

Did he? He cast inward and found — yes. There. A new certainty sitting in his mind like a landmark: the Gate of Teeth, eastern face of the mountain, approached through the Hollow Stair, admitted by named desire, guarded by honesty. Whole and detailed and *his*, paid for and owned.

"I know it," he said. "Every step."

"And your mother?"

"My mother?" He frowned, genuinely puzzled, sorting through the word. "She died when I was small. I've told you that. I don't really — there's not much to tell. She died. I loved her, I suppose. Children do." He heard himself say the words and felt nothing at all, and the nothing was vast and clean and terrible, a freshly swept room, a coastline with no features drawn upon it. He noticed that his eyes were wet and had no idea why, and that frightened him more than anything the trader had done. "Why are you looking at me like that?"

Sef didn't answer. Behind her, Ilvane had gone pale as the trader's veil. And the boy —

The boy crossed the space between them in three strides and threw his arms around Yara's neck, and buried his face against his shoulder, and wept — silent as always, shoulders shaking, gripping fistfuls of Yara's coat as if holding shut a wound. As if grieving on behalf of a man who could no longer locate the loss inside himself.

Yara patted the child's back awkwardly. "Hey. Hey. I'm fine. I'm right here."

The boy pulled back just far enough to look at him. And with one muddy finger, deliberate, trembling, the child drew a shape in the air between their faces — a circle, then two dots, then a curved line beneath. Over and over. Insistent. A child's first drawing. The first thing every child in every kingdom learns to draw.

A face.

Yara stared at it. Something stirred in the swept room of him, far away, like wind moving in a house whose doors were all locked. He felt the grief-residue shift in his chest — the debt the trader had named, the interest still accruing — and for one vertiginous second he nearly understood what he had paid, what was sealed in the jar, what was being carried away in a stranger's clay vessel to sit on some shelf in a market that did not exist.

Then it passed. Of course it passed. Everything passed. Nothing stayed; that was the whole point of the transaction.

"Thank you," he said gently, to the boy, meaning the hug. He rose, rolled his shoulders, and turned to the company with the crisp clarity of a man with a destination. "Eastern face. The Hollow Stair. Four days' hard travel if the weather holds. We should move — Sef, your people have been camped too long as it is. Half of them think this mission is a grave with extra steps, and they're not entirely wrong, and if we linger they'll start saying so out loud."

He was right, and they all knew it. The company gathered itself, checked straps, counted arrows. Ilvane settled the final courtesies with the trader in a language Yara was surprised to realize he didn't recognize. The veiled figure inclined its head to Yara — in respect or pity, the veil declined to say — and lifted the clay jar, and tucked it away beneath the counter among a hundred identical jars, each holding something someone had loved enough to be worth selling.

They walked out the way they had come in, down aisles of grey silk, past vendors who turned to follow them with that slow sunflower hunger. At the edge of the fog, Sef fell into step beside Yara and said, quietly, without looking at him:

"When this is done — when he's safe — we're coming back here. You and me. And we're buying it back. Whatever it costs."

Yara smiled at her, kindly, puzzled. "Buying what back?"

Ahead, the boy had stopped at the top of the hollow, silhouetted against the ordinary morning, and he was looking back at Yara with an expression Yara couldn't read — an expression, had he still possessed the face to compare it to, that would have reminded him achingly of someone.
