## 15. Second Turning

The second moon came up fat and copper over the ridge line, and Sef watched it rise from the back of a dead man's horse, counting.

Two turns gone. Eleven left. She had started this journey doing that arithmetic in her head the way other people said prayers, and now she caught herself doing it in her sleep, waking with her fingers already moving against her thigh like an abacus with one bead too many.

Behind her, the camp was making noise again.

She let it build for a while. That was a captain's trick she'd learned from the woman who'd taught her the trade, back when Sef had been nothing but a cutpurse with quick hands and a quicker mouth: when your people are angry, let them be angry where you can hear it. Anger you can hear is anger you can steer. It's the quiet ones who put knives in tents.

So she sat on the ridge with the copper moon climbing and listened to her band come apart at the seams, and she catalogued the voices the way she'd catalogue a market crowd before a job.

Doss, first and loudest. Doss was always first and loudest. *"Eleven moons to cross half the world and walk into a mountain, and for what? For a marked brat and a mapmaker who draws pictures?"* A scatter of laughter that didn't quite land. Doss's laughter never did; it was a tool he carried badly, like a sword too heavy for his arm.

Kettle's voice, low and reasonable, which was worse. Kettle could argue a wall into thinking it was a door. *"...not saying we abandon them on the road. Saying we point them at the mountain and wish them well. There's a difference between mercy and suicide, and I'd like us all to still be alive to know which one we picked."*

Ashka didn't say anything. Ashka never said anything until the end, and then she said one sentence that decided things. Sef had seen her do it three times in six years, and all three times the band had done what Ashka said, including the time Sef had privately thought Ashka was wrong.

And under all of it, quieter than any of them, the sound of Bram whittling. He always whittled when the band argued. The little shavings falling off his knife sounded, from a distance, like patience itself.

Sef gave the moon another ten minutes. Then she stood, rolled her shoulders until they cracked, and went down to referee.

* * *

The fire had burned down to its red bones by the time she walked into the light. They were arranged around it the way they always arranged themselves — Doss standing because sitting meant conceding, Kettle cross-legged with his hands folded like a clerk, Ashka leaning against the wagon with her arms crossed and her face doing nothing at all. Bram on his stump, knife moving. And apart from all of them, at the edge of the firelight where the warmth ran out, the mapmaker and the boy.

Yara Dunmore sat with his maps spread across his knees even now, even here, charcoal stub in his fingers, working. The man drew the way other men breathed. Sef had watched him sketch a ford onto a scrap of paper two days ago and then watch the river rearrange itself to match, and she still hadn't decided whether that terrified her or made her want to follow him anywhere. Both, probably. Both was usually the honest answer.

The boy — Wren, they'd taken to calling him, because he was small and quiet and watched everything from high branches of attention — sat pressed against Yara's side with the contract sigil hidden under his collar and his eyes on the arguing outlaws the way a deer watches dogs through a fence. Not afraid, exactly. Counting exits.

"Captain," Doss said, when he saw her. Not *Sef*. Captain, with the word loaded like a crossbow. "Good. Maybe you can tell us what I've been telling these fools for an hour."

"That you talk too much," Sef said, and sat down on the flat stone that was hers, the one nobody else touched, and held her hands to the fire. "Go on, though. Say it again. I want to hear if it sounds better the third time."

"It doesn't sound better because it isn't better." Doss threw his arms wide. "Two moons, Sef. Two moons we've spent playing escort to a child the Under-Crown owns on paper, and every mile east there are more of the broker's men, more tollhouses, more hollow-eyed bastards in grey who don't bleed right. We lost the Tollbrook job because of this detour. We lost the Ferrow crossing. We're eating trail ration and sleeping in ditches, and for what? Tell me what we get."

"You know what we get," Bram said, without looking up from his whittling. "We get the same thing we always get."

"And what's that?"

"A soul that stays free." Bram blew a shaving off the little wooden bird taking shape in his hands. "Cheapest thing going. Nobody pays for it. That's why nobody else bothers."

"That's poetry," Doss snapped, "and poetry doesn't fill a belly."

"No," Bram agreed mildly. "But it fills a reason, and a reason will fill a belly faster than coin, because coin runs out and reasons don't."

Kettle raised one hand, the way he did, asking permission to be sensible. "Bram talks pretty and Doss talks loud, so let me talk plain." He turned to Sef, and his face was genuinely pained, which was the worst part about Kettle — he never argued from malice, only from arithmetic. "Sef. I love this band. I have loved this band since you pulled me out of the debtor's pit in Carrowmere with my hands still bleeding. Everything we are, everything we've built — justice outside the law, no soul sold, no debt collected, none of it — that's worth dying for. But it has to be worth *living* for too, or it's just a nice way to be buried. Eleven moons to the mountain. Even Ilvane — and she's a court mage, she knows what's under there — even Ilvane said the odds were—"

"Ilvane said the odds were bad," Sef said. "She also said she'd hold the gate when we asked her to. Funny kind of cowardice."

"She said she'd consider holding the gate."

"She said it while sharpening a staff the length of a fence post. I know considering when I see it."

A few of them laughed — not Doss, but a few — and Sef felt the room tilt a degree her way, and hated how much she cared about the tilting. This was the part of command nobody sang songs about: not the charge, not the speech, but the slow nightly work of keeping eleven stubborn hearts pointed the same direction when every one of them had a different idea of north.

"Here's what I know," she said, before Kettle could regroup. She stood, because some arguments you take seated and some you take standing, and this one needed her full height. "I know that six years ago we wrote our law on the inside of the wagon lid, and the first line is *no soul is property*, and I have never once heard anybody propose we amend it. Second thing I know: that mark on that boy's collarbone is the same mark we burned off Tam Vessick's daughter four years ago, and every one of you stood in the rain and helped me do it, and not one of you asked what we'd get out of it. So don't stand here tonight and ask me what we get. Ask me what we are."

"We're nine people," Doss said. "Nine. Against a court that's been buying souls since before the kingdoms had names."

"Nine," Sef agreed. "Which is eight more than the boy had when we found him."

"And if it costs us all nine?" Kettle, quietly. "Who keeps the law then, Sef? Who's left to keep anything? You'd spend the whole band on one child, and then the brokers go back to weighing souls in their tollhouses with nobody to burn them down, forever. Is that justice? Spending the future to save the present?"

It was a good argument. Sef hated him a little for how good it was, and loved him a little for the same reason, and knew that half the band was leaning toward him in the dark places of themselves where they kept their fear.

She opened her mouth to answer —

— and Ashka spoke.

"One sentence," Ashka said, as she always did, from the wagon, arms still crossed, eyes on the fire. Everyone stopped. Even Doss. Especially Doss. "I followed the captain out of Carrowmere because she went back for a dog. A dog. Half-dead, worthless, bit her on the way out. I remember thinking, this woman will come back for anyone, and that is the only kind of person worth following." She unfolded her arms. "I'm still following. Whoever wants to ride west, the wagon's yours. Take the grain. Take the tent. But you'll be leaving the law behind, because the law is going east."

Silence. The fire popped. Somewhere up the valley a nightbird called twice and quit.

Then Bram set down his knife, held up the finished carving — a small wooden wren, wings folded, absurdly delicate for his big scarred hands — walked to the edge of the firelight, and crouched down in front of the boy.

"For you," he said. "Heard that's your name now. Seemed fitting."

Wren took it with both hands, the way children take things that might be taken back, and looked up at Bram, and something passed between them that Sef couldn't have described under oath and didn't try to.

"Well," Doss said at last, disgusted, dropping onto his bedroll like a felled tree. "One sentence. Every time. One sentence and I'm outvoted by a woman who hasn't bought a round in six years."

"You're not outvoted," Sef said. "You're outvoted one to seven. It's different math but the same result."

Doss grumbled something into his blanket that was probably insubordination and possibly a compliment, and the band began, in the way of bands everywhere, to pretend the fracture had never happened — Kettle banking the fire, Ashka checking the wagon's axle out of pure habit, the argument folding itself away into the place where bands keep the arguments they'll have again next week.

Sef stayed by the coals a moment longer. Yara hadn't moved through any of it. He sat with his maps and his charcoal, and only when she glanced over did she realize he'd been listening to every word with the particular stillness of a man hearing his own death debated as a line item.

"You could still go west," she said quietly. "Take the wagon. Take the grain. Nobody would think less—"

"Yes, they would," Yara said. Not bitter. Just precise, the way he was precise about everything, coastlines and kindness alike. "And they'd be right to. Besides." He looked down at Wren, already asleep against his side, the wooden bird clutched to his chest, and something moved across the mapmaker's face that Sef recognized, because she'd worn it herself since the day she'd pulled a bleeding cutpurse out of a pit and called it a band. "Besides. I've been reading your law on the wagon lid. First line. No soul is property." He tapped the map on his knees — and Sef saw, with a small cold thrill, that it wasn't a map of roads at all. It was the mountain. The whole massif, drawn in fine obsessive lines, and beneath it, sketched fainter, deeper, a network of galleries and halls that no living surveyor could have charted. "I've been thinking about what a soul actually weighs," Yara said. "Where they'd keep one. How the rooms would have to be shaped."

"You're drawing the inside of the Under-Crown's court," Sef said slowly. "From rumor. From *guesswork*."

"I'm drawing what I believe is there." He looked up at her, and in the firelight his eyes had the flat shine of a man standing on a ledge he can't remember stepping onto. "And lately, Captain, I've been wondering whether that's the same thing as drawing what *is* there."

Sef looked at the map a long moment. Then at the sleeping boy. Then east, past the black shoulder of the hills, to where the third moon's journey had already begun somewhere below the horizon, patient as a debt collector.

"Get some sleep, mapmaker," she said. "We cross the Remembering River tomorrow. And if half the stories about that water are true—" she paused, and found she didn't want to finish the sentence, so she finished a different one. "Sleep anyway."

Above them, the second turning rode the sky, copper and complete, and began, imperceptibly, to wane.
