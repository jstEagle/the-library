## 16. The River That Remembers

The river appeared on no map I had ever drawn, which by then should not have surprised me, but did.

We had come down out of the high scrub country for two days, following a line of willows that Sef swore marked water, and on the morning of the third day the trees opened like a curtain and there it was: broad, slow, and wrong in a way my eye caught before my mind could name it. The water ran silver-grey, the color of old pewter, and it moved without sound. Not quietly — *soundlessly*. A river that wide should have muttered over its stones, should have gossiped at its banks. This one flowed like poured glass.

"Well," said Sef, reining up beside me. "That's the Lorn."

"The Lorn isn't on any map."

"No," she agreed. "It wouldn't be." She swung down from her horse and stood at the bank, arms folded, studying the current the way a gambler studies an opponent's hands. "It remembers everything that ever touched it. Cross it careless and it'll show you things you'd rather it kept."

"Show how?"

"You'll see." She glanced back at the column behind us — Dree and Oskar with the pack animals, Ilvane riding with her hood up against a sun that seemed to avoid her personally, and the boy on his own small pony, watching the water with an expression I had learned to read as closely as contour lines. "We cross single file. Don't stop in the middle. Whatever you see, don't stop."

The boy slid off his pony and came to stand at my stirrup. He looked up at me, then at the river, and made the small gesture he used for questions — fingers spread, palm tilted, like a man weighing something invisible.

"I don't know," I told him honestly. "Sef says it shows the past."

His hand changed. He tapped his own chest, twice, then pointed at the water. A question I understood all too well: *Will it show mine?*

Twelve turns had become nine since Maren read the sigil under his collarbone, and somewhere along the road between the tollhouse and here, the stolen life had begun leaking back into him. He dreamed now — I heard him sometimes in the dark, weeping without sound, the way he did everything without sound, though whether the silence was the debt's doing or something older in him I couldn't say. In the mornings he would look at me with those eyes too grave for his years and touch the places dreams had been, as if checking pockets.

"It might," I said. "If it does — if you see something hard — you look at me instead. Can you do that? Eyes on me, and we walk, and whatever the river says, it can't hold us."

He considered this with the seriousness he brought to everything, then nodded once.

* * *

We crossed at midmorning, when the light lay flattest on the water, because Ilvane said the river's visions burned brightest at dawn and dusk and none of us needed more of them than necessary. Sef went first, then Oskar, then Dree leading the packs. I took the boy's hand and we went fourth, Ilvane behind us, her boots whispering through the shallows like someone walking through a memory she'd rather not revisit.

The water came up past our knees. It was cold, but the cold was strange — not the clean bite of snowmelt but something older, colder, like touching the inside of a locked room. And then the visions began.

They rose off the surface the way heat shimmers rise off summer stone, except these were pictures, whole and moving, laid over the silver flow like paint on glass. I saw a wedding — strangers' faces, garlands, a bride laughing — playing itself out in the current around my legs. Three steps farther and it was a funeral, same family, greyer faces. A child learning to walk along this very bank, falling, rising, falling again. A man standing where I stood, decades ago, staring at the water with an expression I recognized because I was wearing it too.

The river didn't show me these things so much as it *replayed* them, faithfully, indifferently, the way a cracked bell repeats its one note. Everything that had ever happened within reach of its water was still happening there, faintly, forever.

I kept my eyes forward. I felt the boy's hand tighten in mine and I squeezed back: *eyes on me.* He was doing well. His jaw was set, his gaze fixed on the back of my coat, and only once did his attention flick sideways — toward a vision near the far bank, small and bright — before he wrenched it back.

Then, ten paces from the middle, the river found *me*.

* * *

It began as warmth. That was the first wrongness — the water had been cold as a crypt, and suddenly it was warm around my calves, warm as bathwater, warm as —

— as a kitchen. I was in a kitchen. Not remembering it; *in* it, the way you are in a place, with floorboards under feet that were suddenly much smaller than my feet, and steam on a window, and a woman humming. I knew the tune. I have never known the tune, not in forty-odd years of living, and I knew it instantly, completely, the way you know your own heartbeat.

The woman turned. She had flour on her hands and dark hair escaping its braid and she was singing to someone — to a bundle in a basket on the table, and the bundle was me. I knew it the way you know things in dreams, without evidence, without argument. The baby in the basket had my eyes. It had, God help me, my exact frown, the one Maren always said I'd been born wearing.

And the woman leaned down into the basket and lifted me up, and her hands —

Her hands *glowed*.

Not reflected light. Not lamplight through a window. Her hands gave off their own soft gold, the way embers give it, from underneath the skin, and where her fingers touched the blanket they left brief fading prints like frost in reverse. She held me up and sang the tune I had never known and always known, and she pressed her glowing forehead to my small forehead, and she whispered something the river would not let me hear — the words blurred, smudged, as if a censor's thumb had smeared them across the glass — and outside the kitchen window, beyond the garden, beyond the wall, there were mountains. Mountains I did not know. Mountains that were not the valley's mountains.

*I was not born in the valley.*

The knowledge arrived not as thought but as fact, the way you know you've been bleeding before you look down. Every year of my life I had believed — had built myself upon the belief — that I was valley-born, valley-bred, that the valley's stubborn magiclessness was my native weather. My parents had died there. My mother's grave was on the hill above the house; I had mapped that hill down to the individual stones. But the woman in the river's memory was not buried on any hill I had ever walked, and her hands glowed, and the mountains through the window belonged to some other country entirely.

The vision widened. The kitchen dissolved outward and I saw — briefly, horribly briefly — a road at night, and horses breathing steam, and the woman running with me wrapped against her chest, her hands cupped behind my head and shining like twin lanterns in the dark, lighting her way because she dared not carry a lamp. Behind her, far behind, something followed. I never saw what. I saw only her face over my head, turning back and back and back, and her mouth shaping words the river refused to give me, and then —

A door. A threshold. A line of pale stones set in the earth, and beyond them a valley sleeping under a veil of nothing, and her voice — her actual voice, breaking through the smear at last, one sentence, torn out of context and given to me whole:

*"The veil was for you."*

Then cold. Real cold, the river's cold, slamming back around my legs like a dropped lid, and I was standing in midstream with the boy's hand crushing mine and my own breath coming in ragged white plumes as though I'd been running.

"Yara." Sef's voice, sharp, from the bank. "Yara. Move."

I moved. I don't remember the last ten paces. I remember coming out of the water onto the southern bank with my boots squelching and my heart going like a hammer on sheet iron, and the boy standing in front of me, both hands on my face now, searching my eyes with a ferocity that was almost angry — because I had told him *eyes on me*, and I had broken faith with the river and with him both.

"I'm sorry," I said. My voice sounded borrowed. "I'm sorry. I saw —"

He waited. He was very good at waiting. It may have been the first thing anyone ever stole from him, or the first thing he ever owned; I couldn't tell anymore.

"I saw my mother," I said.

Something crossed his face — recognition, sympathy, a grief too large for his body — and then, unexpectedly, he pointed. Not at me. At himself. Then at the river. Then he made the gesture I had come to know best of all his silent vocabulary: he drew one finger slowly down the center of his chest, tracing the path of the sigil beneath his shirt, and looked at me with a question that needed no language.

*Did yours glow? Did you come from somewhere else too? Are we the same kind of lost?*

"I don't know," I said, which was becoming the truest sentence I owned. "I don't know what I am."

Behind us, Ilvane stood hip-deep in the Lorn, motionless, her hood back, letting the river show her whatever it showed exiles. When she finally waded out, she did not look at me — but she passed close, close enough that only I could hear, and said, quietly:

"Now you understand why I asked to ride last."

* * *

We made camp early that afternoon in a hollow above the south bank, because Sef said no one crosses the Lorn and marches twenty miles the same day; the river takes something out of you even when it gives nothing back. Dree built a fire. Oskar checked the pickets. The ordinary machinery of the band ground on around me, and I sat apart with my journal open on my knees and could not make my hand draw.

That had never happened before. Not once in my life. There had been days I drew badly, days I drew angrily, days I drew maps of places I hated to keep from screaming about them. But my hand had never simply refused. Now it hovered over the page like a bird that has forgotten what wings are for, because every line I might draw led back to the kitchen, to the glowing hands, to the mountains through the window, and I did not know what country to put them in.

The boy came and sat beside me. He didn't look at the blank page. He looked at me, and after a while he reached over and touched the journal, then touched my chest, then pointed south — toward the mountains we were already walking toward, the mountain, the court beneath it, the ledger with his soul written in it.

Then he did something new. He took my hand, turned it palm-up in both of his small ones, and studied it intently, turning it over, pressing the pads of my fingers, as though checking whether *my* hands glowed.

They didn't. They were just hands, ink-stained and rope-scarred, a cartographer's hands, a valley's hands. He frowned at them anyway, deeply, as if the absence of light were itself a clue.

"Not everyone's hands glow," I told him. "Maybe hers were — maybe she was something. Maybe I'm something's son and nothing myself." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Listen to me. Forty years mapping other people's borders and I can't find my own origin on a map."

He released my hand and, with the grave deliberation he brought to everything, picked up my pencil from where it had rolled against my knee and put it back in my fingers. Then he closed my hand around it.

*Draw,* the gesture said. *You're the one who draws.*

So I drew. Not the route ahead — Sef had that in her head, and rightly distrusted paper. I drew the kitchen. Or tried to. The window, the steam, the basket, the woman's shape with the flour on her hands, and where her hands were I pressed the pencil until the graphite shone faintly, grey-gold, the closest I could come to light on paper. Around it all I sketched the mountains I'd glimpsed through the glass, working from a memory three seconds long and forty years deep, and as the ridgelines took shape under my hand a cold feeling crawled up my neck that had nothing to do with the river.

Because I knew them.

Not from life. From *work*. Those peaks — the long saddle, the twin horns, the notch like a bitten ear — I had drawn them before, or drawn near them, years ago, compiling rumor-maps of the far kingdoms from traders' tales. They matched a range on a chart I'd made at nineteen, copied from a fur-trapper's drunken description of a pass he swore no honest man used. I had labeled it, in my neat adolescent lettering, *UNVERIFIED — LIKELY FABLE.*

Likely fable. My mother had run through it with me in her arms and her hands alight, and I had dismissed it at nineteen as a trapper's fancy.

"Yara." Sef crouched beside me, and her eyes went to the sketch, and stayed there. She was quiet a long moment. "Where's that?"

"I don't know exactly. Northeast, I think. Past the Ashwood, if the Ashwood is where I think it is."

"The Ashwood's burned," she said flatly. "Burned years back. Nothing crosses it now but ash and things that eat travelers." She looked from the drawing to my face, and whatever she saw there made her jaw tighten. "You've gone grey as the river. What did it show you?"

I told her. All of it — the kitchen, the flight, the glowing hands, the sentence the river had finally surrendered. She listened the way she listened to everything, utterly still, and when I finished she was silent for the space of ten heartbeats.

"The veil was for you," she repeated. "Meaning somebody built it around you. Meaning somebody was hunting you before you could walk." She shook her head slowly. "Same somebody who buys souls under a mountain, I'd wager my knife. You're not traveling with that boy, Yara. You're traveling *with* him. Two debts on one road."

"He doesn't owe anything. He's a child."

"So are you, far as they're concerned. Debts don't ripen by age, they ripen by moon." She stood, brushing off her knees, and looked north, where the land climbed toward passes I had only ever drawn in dotted lines. "Get some sleep. We move at first light, and we move fast. There's a stretch ahead — the pass through the Ashwood's edge — where I've never liked the ground. Too open. Too quiet." She paused at the edge of the firelight. "And Yara — if the river showed you true, then whoever's hunting that boy has been hunting you longer. Keep your eyes open tonight. Both of us, we're the ones with prices on our heads."

She walked away into the dark, and I sat with the fire and the boy asleep against my shoulder and the sketch drying on my knee — a kitchen, a woman with burning hands, a range of mountains I had once called fable.

Far to the north, past the black teeth of the pass, something was already waiting. I felt it the way the valley had taught me to feel the absence of magic: not as a sound, but as a silence where a sound should be.

In the morning, we would walk into it.
