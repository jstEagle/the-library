## 17. Hollow Men of the Pass

Sef counted the pass twice before she believed it the first time.

The Greytooth Pass was a throat of stone between two shoulders of mountain, narrow enough that six riders abreast would be four too many, and it should have been empty. It was never empty — that was the trouble with passes. Everything that wanted to go from one side of the world to the other had to squeeze through them like a coin through a miser's fist, and everything that wanted to take from travelers knew it too. Sef had ambushed a tax convoy in this same pass three years ago, back when her band was nine strong and hungry and stupid in the way young bands are stupid. She remembered the echo of their own arrows off the walls, the way sound doubled and trebled in the narrows until it sounded like an army.

Now she stood at the mouth of that throat with eleven people, two of them children in all but name — Yara Dunmore with his ink-stained fingers and his maps that came true, and the boy who didn't speak but watched everything the way a hawk watches a field.

And the pass was empty.

"Say it," Bram said beside her. He'd been chewing the same strip of dried venison for an hour without eating any of it, a habit he fell into when his gut was talking louder than his mouth. "You're doing the thing where you count things twice and don't tell anyone why."

"The pass is empty."

"I noticed."

"It shouldn't be." She nodded up at the ridgeline, where the late-afternoon sun was already sliding behind the western peaks, dragging its shadow down the rock face like a curtain being drawn. "This time of year there are pilgrim trains coming down from the High Shrines before the snows close it. Merchants. At minimum, shepherds moving flocks to winter pasture. We've seen one shepherd in two days, and he was running."

Bram spat out the venison strip, finally, and tucked it away. "Running from what?"

"That's the question I keep asking, and the pass keeps not answering."

Behind them, the rest of the band made camp in the lee of a fallen slab of granite — small fires, no smoke if they could help it, the horses picketed close. Wren was teaching the boy knucklebones, or trying to; the boy kept winning, which unsettled Wren more than losing would have. Ilvane sat apart as always, hood up, hands folded around something invisible. And Yara was drawing. He was always drawing now. He'd spread one of his strange living maps across a flat stone and was working on it with a stylus of blackened bone, and even from twenty paces Sef could see the ink *moving*, settling into place like water finding its level.

She still hadn't decided how she felt about that. A man whose drawings became real was either the most valuable ally she'd ever carried or the most dangerous cargo, and the difference between those two things was usually just which direction you were standing when it went wrong.

"We go through at first light," she said. "Full dark before we're in the narrows, so anything watching has to watch blind. Single file. No fires tonight after moonrise."

Bram grunted. "And if the empty pass isn't empty?"

"Then we find out why the shepherd was running."

* * *

They found out at the third hour past midnight, when the boy started screaming without sound.

Sef was half-asleep against her saddle when it happened — the boy bolt upright by the dead fire, mouth wide open, both hands pressed flat against his chest as though holding shut a door. No sound came out of him. There never was sound. But every horse in the camp screamed for him, all seven at once, a chorus of terror that bounced off the canyon walls and came back tripled.

"On your feet!" Sef was moving before her mind caught up, knife out, scanning the black mouths of the ravines above them. "Wren, get the boy behind the rocks. Ilvane—"

"I feel them." The exiled mage's voice came out flat and cold, stripped of all its usual careful distance. "Coming down the eastern ravine. Six. Maybe eight." A pause. "They aren't alive, Captain. Not properly."

"Hollow men," Bram said quietly.

Everyone went still. Even the wind seemed to stop.

Sef had heard the stories the way everyone had heard them — told in taverns by men who claimed a cousin who'd seen one, described as bogeymen, as rumor, as the kind of story you told to make children stay away from broker roads. Soul-broker enforcers. Men who had sold so much of themselves over the years — pieces of their courage, their grief, their names, whatever the brokers required as toll — that there wasn't enough left inside to hold them upright. What walked was the shape of a man with nothing in it, animated by contract and compulsion, obedient until the last scrap of soul was spent and then simply falling down in the road like an emptied coat.

Nobody had ever claimed to survive meeting more than one.

"How many did you say?" she asked Ilvane.

"Eight. Moving fast. They don't tire, Captain. They don't maneuver. They come straight at what they're sent for." Ilvane rose, and pushed back her hood, and for the first time since they'd collected her from her exile Sef saw her look genuinely afraid — not of dying, Sef realized, but of failing. "They're sent for the boy. Possibly for Yara. They will not stop, and they will not negotiate, and they will not break off. You understand? Whatever else happens tonight, understand that."

"Then we don't fight fair," Sef said. "We fight like thieves."

She started giving orders, and the band moved, because that was the whole difference between her people and the lawful world: when Sef said move, they moved, no magistrate's seal required, no fee assessed, no delay while some clerk decided whether their lives were worth the paperwork.

The plan was simple because plans made in terror must be simple. Give ground. Funnel the enemy into the narrowest throat of the pass where eight couldn't come abreast. Let Ilvane spend her hoarded power in one place instead of ten. Keep the boy moving, keep him covered, and get everyone through to the far side before dawn.

It was a good plan. It was even, mostly, working — right up until the hollow men stopped being where Ilvane said they were.

"They've split," she said suddenly, her head snapping toward the western ravine. "I miscounted. There were never eight coming down the east. Four east, four west. They were herding us toward the narrows." Her voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "Captain — the narrows is where they want us. It's a trap shaped like our own good sense."

* * *

Later, Sef would try to remember exactly how it went, and she never quite could. Terror does that; it takes the memory and files it under bruises.

What she remembered: firelight, or the lack of it. The moonless dark of the pass floor, and then shapes emerging from it that were darker than dark, moving with a wrongness that hurt to look at — like watching puppets worked from strings you couldn't see, except the strings were contracts and the puppeteer was a court beneath a mountain. They looked like men the way a ruin looks like a house. Their faces were the worst part. Not skull-bare, not monstrous — just *unfinished*, features worn smooth as river stones, as though someone had sanded away everything that wasn't strictly necessary for walking and killing.

What she remembered: Wren screaming a warning. Arrows loosed into the dark and striking flesh without eliciting so much as a grunt, because these things had traded away pain years ago, sold it piece by piece to pay their own debts. An arrow through the chest meant nothing to a man with no heart left worth stopping.

What she remembered: Yara Dunmore, the mapmaker, the soft-handed valley-man who flinched at loud voices, planting himself in front of the boy with a bone stylus clutched in his fist like a dagger, and shouting words Sef didn't know, and the ground beneath the lead hollow man *shifting* — actually shifting, stone flowing like taffy, because somewhere in the dark Yara had drawn it so, and here outside the valley his drawings obeyed him.

What she remembered most, though, what she would carry to her own grave whenever it came for her:

Bram.

He'd taken the western four alone. That was the shape of it — the trap had split them, and someone had to hold the western ravine mouth or the whole band was pinched between two fists, and Bram had looked at her across the firelight and said, "Go. I've got the left door," in the same tone he used for saying he'd take first watch, and she'd gone, because there was no time and because she trusted him, and trust was the whole architecture of everything they'd built together outside the law.

When she fought her way back — three minutes later, five, she'd never know — the left door was held.

Bram stood in the mouth of the western ravine with his back against the rock and his long knife in one hand and a broken arrow in the other, and around his feet lay two of the hollow men, crumpled like dropped clothing. He'd figured out what none of them knew: they could fall. They weren't unkillable, they were *unwilling* — no, worse, they were *contracted*, and enough damage broke the contract, let whatever was left of them finally lie down. Two of them had paid that price against his blade.

The other two were inside his guard.

"Bram!" Her voice tore out of her. She was running. She would always be running, in the memory, and never arriving.

He saw her. Of course he saw her. And he smiled — Bram, who never smiled in daylight, who said smiling cost a copper a time and he wasn't paying it — and he threw himself backward off the ledge into the ravine, taking both hollow men with him.

The sound they made going down was nothing. That was the horror of it. Forty feet of falling rock and bodies and it barely echoed at all, as though the pass itself declined to remember it.

By the time Sef scrambled down the scree to the ravine floor, Ilvane's light following her like a reluctant dog, it was over. The hollow men were down and still, and the compulsion had gone out of them entirely — they looked like what they were now, which was clothes. Empty coats of men, lying among the stones. One of them had Bram's knife buried in its chest to the hilt.

Bram was propped against the ravine wall, and he was breathing, which for a moment made her stupid with relief, and then she saw the wound.

One of them had gotten its hand into him before the fall. Not a blade — a hand, fingers that had once belonged to a man who'd sold everything soft in himself, and where they'd touched him below the ribs the flesh was gray and spreading, like frost creeping across a window. Contract-touch. She'd heard of it. A hollow man's grip didn't cut; it *collected*. It took payment directly, from the nearest account, and the nearest account was Bram.

"Sef." His voice was already thin. "Don't waste Ilvane on me. I've seen this. In Tolliver's Crossing, years back — a drover got touched. Three days. There's no — there isn't a version of this where I don't—"

"Shut up." She knelt in the scree and took his face in her hands, and his skin was cold, cold as the hollow men, cold as payment. "Shut up and let me decide what we waste."

"Sef." He almost laughed. "You're counting things twice again. Tell me why, this time."

She couldn't answer. That was the joke of it, the cruel joke she'd carry: all her life she'd built a justice that needed no permission, no seal, no clerk's approval, and now the only law she answered to was the oldest one, and it wasn't negotiable, and there was no ambush she could set for it.

Ilvane crouched beside them, laid two fingers over the gray spreading frost, and closed her eyes. When she opened them she shook her head, once, small. "I can slow it. Hours, not days. Enough to hear what he wants said."

"Then do that," Sef said. "Do exactly that."

They carried him up to the camp because he asked to see the sky, and the sky gave them nothing — cloud cover rolling in off the peaks, no stars, no moon. The thirteenth moon was still weeks away but the night felt like it was already leaning over them, patient, holding out its hand.

The boy came and sat by Bram without being called. He took Bram's ruined hand — the clean one, the untouched one — and held it, and did not cry, because he couldn't, because his tears had been sold along with everything else, and somehow that was the worst thing Sef had seen all night, worse than the hollow men, worse than the gray frost. A child who had paid so much that even his grief was collateral.

Bram looked at the boy a long time. Then at Yara. Then at Sef.

"You're going to win this," he said. Not a question. "I can tell. You've got the look. The stubborn look. The one that gets people killed and then gets them home anyway." His breath rattled. "Listen. My pack. Don't let them bury it with me. There's something in it I've been carrying thirty years and never had the nerve to open, and I think — I think it was never mine to keep. I think I was carrying it to give away. To somebody." His eyes drifted, unfocused, and settled on Yara Dunmore with an expression Sef couldn't read and would think about for a long time afterward. "Maybe him. Maybe whoever. Just — don't burn it. Promise me the pack doesn't burn."

"I promise," Sef said.

"Good." He exhaled, long and slow, like a man setting down a load he'd carried up a very tall hill. "Justice outside the law. That's what you called it, that first night, when you talked me into this mad life. Best thing I ever heard. Still true." His grip tightened on her hand, faintly. "Keep it true. Cost's real. That's the whole lesson. The cost is real, and you pay it anyway, and that's what makes it justice instead of a bargain."

He died an hour before dawn, with the gray frost having reached his heart, with the boy still holding his hand, with the clouds finally breaking just enough to show one hard white star over the eastern ridge — as if the sky, too, insisted on marking the debt.

* * *

They burned him at sunrise, high on the ridge where the wind could take him, because Bram had always said burial was for people who expected to stay put. Sef spoke the words herself, the old outlaw words, the ones that predated every court and every crown: *We kept the peace you were denied. Go and keep it somewhere warmer.*

No fees. No license. No magistrate's blessing. Just eleven people — ten now — standing in the wind, owing a debt that no ledger anywhere would ever record, and therefore, Sef thought, the only honest debt left in the world.

Afterward, she found Yara sitting apart with Bram's pack in his lap, unopened, his hands resting on the flap without moving to lift it. The mapmaker looked older than he had a week ago. Everyone did. But there was something else in his face too, something lit from underneath, and when he looked up at her Sef recognized it with a jolt, because she wore the same expression every time she looked at the boy.

Resolve. The kind that costs something. The kind that's paid for.

"He said not to burn it," Yara said quietly. "He looked at me when he said it."

"Then maybe it's yours to open."

Yara's hands trembled slightly as he loosened the buckle. Inside the pack, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, was something flat and rectangular, and when he peeled the cloth back Sef saw the edge of paper — old paper, foxed and brittle, and drawn on it in faded ink, lines and contours and a cartographer's neat, confident lettering.

But it was the handwriting that made Yara go white.

Because Sef had spent enough nights watching him work to know his hand — the sharp downstrokes, the peculiar hooked capitals — and the writing on that fragment was his. Exactly his. And Yara Dunmore was staring at it as though staring at a ghost, and when he finally spoke, his voice came out cracked and strange:

"This is my handwriting. From when I was a child." He swallowed. "I have never drawn this map. I've never *seen* this place."

He turned the fragment toward the rising sun, and Sef leaned in despite herself, and saw what the faded ink depicted: a mountain, rendered in a child's careful strokes, with a gate at its base like a row of teeth —

— and beneath it, in that impossible familiar hand, a single word.

*Home.*
