## 18. What Bram Carried

They buried Bram at dawn, in the way of the road: no coffin, no marker, only a cairn of grey stones that Sef built herself, stone by stone, her jaw set so tight it seemed carved from the same material. The pass wind came down off the heights and pulled at the smoke of the small fire they'd lit to warm the ground before the digging. The boy stood at the edge of the little gathering with his hands folded, and when Sef placed the last stone he did something I had not seen him do before — he bowed. Deeply. The bow of one who understood exactly what had been taken.

I did not bow. I could not make my body move that far. Grief had settled into me as a kind of stiffness, a rigging tightened too hard, and I stood there like a mast while the others bent around me.

Bram had died the way he had lived, which is to say loudly, badly positioned, and for other people's benefit. He had stepped into the gap between the hollow men's blades and Wren's back — Wren, the youngest of Sef's band, a girl who still laughed at her own jokes — and he had held that gap for eleven seconds. Eleven seconds is nothing. Eleven seconds is the space between two heartbeats stretched until it becomes a country. In those eleven seconds the enforcers' hooks found him twice, and where the hooks touched, his light went out of him in visible threads, silver-grey, like wool unravelling from a sleeve. I saw it. We all saw it. That is what soul-brokers do when they kill: they do not take the whole of you at once. They pull a thread and let the rest collapse.

Afterward, one of them lay dead among the rocks and the other had limped off into the high scree trailing its own unravelled light, and Sef had stood over Bram's body and said nothing at all, which from Sef was louder than any eulogy.

Now the cairn was done, and the band mounted up, and I looked once more at the mound of stones and thought: *I never learned what he carried.*

It is a strange thing, the guilt of the living. It does not choose sensible objects. Mine chose Bram's saddlebags.

* * *

We made camp that night in the lee of an old watchtower, half-swallowed by the hillside, its stones furred with lichen the colour of old bronze. Ilvane had gone ahead toward the mountain roads — she traveled apart from us now, appearing and vanishing like weather — and the company numbered seven where it had numbered eight. Seven people, one mute boy, and a silence where Bram's voice used to be. He had been the one who sang off-key on the marches. He had been the one who complained about the food and then ate most of it. He had been the one who, on my second night with the band, sat beside me and said, "Cartographer, eh? Draw me somewhere warm," and laughed at his own wit until he coughed.

Sef distributed his effects the way she did everything: quickly, without ceremony, before grief could grow roots into the objects themselves. His blade went to Wren, who took it with both hands and would not look at anyone. His cloak went to the firewood pile — it was burned through at the shoulder anyway. His dice went to Ferro, who rolled them once, softly, against his palm, and put them away without looking at the result.

The saddlebags Sef dropped at my feet.

"You were closest to him at the end," she said. "You sort them."

"I barely knew him."

"You knew him eleven seconds longer than the rest of us needed to." Her voice was flat, but not unkind. Flat was how Sef said things that cost her. "Sort them. Whatever's in there, we should know if it's worth anything on the road."

So I knelt by the fire with Bram's saddlebags in my lap, and the company drifted to their bedrolls, and the boy sat near me as he always did, close enough that his shoulder nearly touched mine, watching my hands with that grave attention he gave to everything — as though the world were a text written in a language he was patiently teaching himself to read again.

The first bag held the ordinary archaeology of a life on the road. A tin cup dented into a shape like a bitten apple. A whetstone worn to a smile-curve. Three apples, going soft. A carved wooden bird, crude, the paint long flaked away — a toy, perhaps, or a gift someone had given him and he had never given back. A bundle of letters tied with string, addressed in a woman's hand to *Bramble, if you're alive, and Bram, if you're reading this over your own corpse.* I untied the string just enough to see that there were forty of them, maybe fifty, all kept, all worn soft at the folds from rereading, and I retied it and set it aside with a care that surprised me. There had been a wife. There had been a house. There had been a whole country inside this loud, unlucky man, and I had ridden beside him for weeks and never once asked.

Under the letters, wrapped in oilcloth, was the thing that stopped my breath.

* * *

It was a map fragment. A piece of heavy rag-paper, torn along one edge, water-stained along another, perhaps a hand's width across and slightly longer than that. The ink had faded to the brown of dried blood. And the hand that had drawn it —

I knew that hand.

I want to be precise about what I mean by *knew*, because knowing one's own handwriting is not like recognizing a face. A face you might misremember. But a hand — the particular forward lean of the letters, the way the *r* ends in a small upward flick rather than a loop, the habit of drawing north arrows as a single barbed line rather than a star, the tiny tick-marks used for elevation instead of hatching because hatching takes too long when you are twelve years old and impatient to be done — these are not things a person forgets. These are things a person *is*. My hand had changed since childhood, of course; decades of professional work had smoothed it, disciplined it, taught it economy. But underneath the discipline, the boy's habits persisted, the way a river keeps the shape of the valley it cut when it was young.

This was my handwriting. Not similar. Not reminiscent. *Mine*, from a specific season of my life — I would have said thirteen, fourteen years old, the period when I had begun mapping the valley in earnest, when my father had given me my first good pen and I had worn through three nibs in a month.

And I had never drawn it.

I turned the fragment over. Blank. Turned it back. Studied it until the firelight swam. It showed a stretch of river — no, not a river. A line that *wanted* to be a river but curved wrong, doubled back on itself in a way rivers do not, unless the map was not of our world at all. Around the water-line were annotations in that impossible script, the pre-kingdom tongue Maren had named, the language the boy spoke in his sleep — except rendered in *my* letterforms, my abbreviations, my private shorthand for *here the survey fails* (a small circle with a slash) and *unverified* (two dots beneath). Symbols I had invented alone, in my room, for maps no one else ever saw.

There was a scale bar. There was a compass rose — my barbed north arrow. And in the lower corner, where a cartographer signs and dates his work, there was a signature:

*Y.D.*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, pressed hard enough to tear the paper, as though the writer had been afraid or angry or both:

*the third turning shows the door*

I read it four times. Five. The words did not rearrange themselves into sense. They sat on the page like stones at the bottom of clear water — perfectly visible, perfectly unreachable.

"Yara."

I hadn't heard Sef come back. She stood over me, arms crossed, firelight carving her face into planes and shadow. She was looking at the fragment, not at me, and her expression had gone very still in the way I had learned meant danger.

"What is it?" she said.

"It's my handwriting."

"I can see it's a map."

"No." My voice came out strange, thin. "It's my handwriting. From when I was a boy. The abbreviations — I invented those. No one else uses them. I've never shown them to anyone." I held the fragment up between us. "I have never seen this map. I did not draw this map. And it is in my hand."

Sef took it from me — carefully, by the edges, the way one handles evidence — and studied it for a long time. The fire cracked. Somewhere up the slope, Ferro's horse stamped and blew.

"Soul-brokers keep records," she said at last. "Everything goes in a ledger. Every sale, every debt, every name." She turned the fragment over, back, front. "And every hunter carries copies of what he's hunting. If the court has wanted you since you were born — if what Ilvane says is true —"

"Then Bram was carrying a file on me." The words tasted like iron filings. "He was one of them?"

"No." She said it fast, and with certainty, and I realized she had already worked through this in the hours since the burial, already turned the ugly possibility over and crushed it. "Bram's been with me nine years. I know what's in a broker's blood, cartographer. It's patience and appetite and nothing else. Bram couldn't wait for soup to cool. He wasn't one of them." She handed the fragment back. "But brokers don't always travel as brokers. Sometimes they travel as *collectors*. Someone who finds a thing and holds it until the owner comes to claim it. Or—" she hesitated, and Sef hesitating was its own kind of alarm— "or someone who found a thing and didn't understand what he'd found, and kept it because it was beautiful."

"And the hollow men attacked us for it."

"The hollow men attacked us because we're close to the mountain and they're close to their wages. But maybe they smelled it on him. Maybe that's why they came down the pass at all." She crouched, forearms on knees, and looked at me straight on. "Question isn't how Bram got it. Question is: who drew it? Because you say you didn't."

"I didn't."

"Then either someone forged thirteen-year-old you down to your private shorthand — which means someone knows you better than you know yourself — or you drew it and don't remember." She let that sit. She was good at letting things sit; it was how she gutted people. "Which one frightens you more?"

I didn't answer. Both answers frightened me equally, and equally deeply, and they frightened me in different directions, like two winds trying to tear a sail from opposite sides.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat by the banked fire with the fragment on my knee and the boy asleep against my side, his weight warm and absolute, and I interrogated the piece of paper as though it could be made to confess.

Compulsion is a strange instrument. It doesn't feel like being forced. It feels like being *right*. Every hour I spent with the fragment, the certainty grew in me that I could complete it — that the torn edge implied the rest of the sheet the way a chord implies its resolution. I knew where the doubled river went. I knew that if I rotated the fragment thirty degrees counterclockwise, the water-line resolved into a shape I had seen before: the outline of the valley as it appears from the eastern ridge, the basin I had grown up in, except drawn from *outside*, from above and beyond, from a vantage I had never occupied because I had never left.

A map of home, drawn by a child who had never been outside it, from a perspective no child could have had.

Unless the child hadn't been entirely in the valley when he drew it. Unless some part of him had been elsewhere — or some part of elsewhere had been in him.

*The veil was for you.* My mother's voice, traded away to a veiled trader for the mountain's gates, returning now in fragments like shards working up through soil after rain. I had given up her face. I had kept, apparently, her voice, and it came to me now unbidden, saying words I did not yet understand, and beneath the words a memory that was not quite a memory: ink, and lamplight, and my father's hands closing over mine on the pen — not guiding, *stopping* — and his voice saying, gently, terribly: *Not that page, Yara. Never that page. Some places must stay blank to stay safe.*

I had forgotten that. I had forgotten it so completely that encountering it now felt less like remembering than like being *shown* — as though the forgetting itself had been a wall, and something on the other side was patiently taking it down, brick by brick, and had been doing so since the day a mute child knocked on my door.

The boy stirred against my shoulder. In sleep his lips moved, shaping syllables of the old tongue, and I watched them form and — this was the thing that finally drove the cold all the way through me — I found I could almost follow them. Not translate. *Follow*, the way you follow a melody you've never heard but somehow know. The language older than kingdoms, and it sat in me like a key sitting in a lock that had always been there, waiting.

The fragment trembled in my grip. I made myself look at the inscription again.

*the third turning shows the door*

Three turnings had passed. We were deep in the fourth. If the words were a date, it was past. If they were a prophecy, it was unspent. And if they were neither — if they were a *note to self*, a message a boy had left for the man he would become, hidden inside a dead man's saddlebags because no safer hiding place existed in all the world than the memory of a man who had been made to forget —

Then whoever I had been at thirteen had known what was coming. Had known about the debt, the moon, the child. Had known that the man he became would need a door, and had drawn the way to it in the only ink that could survive the erasing of himself: his own hand, his own habits, buried in the keeping of strangers on the road.

I looked down at the sleeping boy. His brow was furrowed; whatever he dreamed, it grieved him. His stolen memories had begun to surface — Sef told me he cried out at night now, soundlessly, tears sliding sideways into his hair — and each dream seemed to return to him a piece of the life the court had taken. Soon, Ilvane had said, he would remember everything. Including, perhaps, the day of the selling. Including, perhaps, the face of the seller.

Including, perhaps, the name of the person from my own valley whose name we'd found in the tollhouse ledger.

The fire settled. Sparks rose and died. And as I sat there holding my own childhood in my hands — a hand I did not remember making, pointing at a door I did not remember learning — I understood that Bram had not been carrying a map at all.

He had been carrying a letter. And it was addressed to me, and it was overdue, and the third turning had already come and gone.

Which meant the door was open now. Or closing.

Or — and this was the thought I could not put down, the hook of it sinking past reason into the meat of me — which meant the door had opened *three turnings ago*, on the night the hollow men first caught our scent, and everything since — Bram's death, the ambush, the pursuit through the ashwood — had not been the court hunting us at all.

It had been the court hunting the *map*. And I had been riding beside it, sleeping beside it, mourning over it, for weeks.

The boy woke suddenly in the grey before dawn, sat bolt upright, and gripped my wrist with both hands. His eyes were wide and wet and fixed on something beyond me, beyond the tower, beyond the hills — and when he spoke, it was in the old tongue, urgent, repeated, his fingers pressing my skin at each word as if to drive it in.

I did not know what he said.

But my hand — my traitor, my childhood's hand — was already reaching for the fragment, already turning it thirty degrees counterclockwise, already tracing the doubled river to the place where the water-line broke open into a shape like a mouth.

And under my breath, in a language I had never been taught, I heard myself answer him.
