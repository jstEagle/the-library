## 19. Third Turning

The dreams come like water finding a crack.

I do not know the word *dream* in their tongue. In the old tongue — the one that lives under my skin now, the one that comes out of my mouth when I am not careful — there is no word for it either, because the people who spoke it first believed that what you see with your eyes shut is not a different thing from what you see with them open. It is all the same country. You are only walking in different weather.

So: I walk in different weather now. And in that weather, I remember.

* * *

The first dream is small, and I almost lose it before I wake.

I am standing in a kitchen. Not Yara's kitchen, which smells of ink and cold ash and the river-stones he boils his tea over. A different kitchen. There is bread rising under a cloth on the sill, and the cloth has red thread woven through it in a pattern of interlocking hooks, and someone is singing.

I know the song. That is the terrible part. I have never heard it in my waking life — not once, not from Maren, not from Sef's rough-voiced company around their fires — but my mouth knows it the way my mouth knows swallowing. The melody sits behind my teeth like a stone warmed by the sun. The words are in the old tongue. They say:

*Lay down your name upon the shelf,
the night will keep it for itself.*

I wake with my lips moving. Sef's lookout hears me and calls down softly, asking if I'm ill, and I shake my head because I cannot answer, because answering would mean saying words, and the words in my throat right now belong to a woman with flour on her hands whom I cannot see.

Weeping without sound is a skill I learned long ago. It was the first thing the mountain taught me, though I did not know then that the mountain was teaching. When they took my voice they took the noise of grief along with it, and left the grief itself sitting inside me like water in a sealed jar. So I weep the way rain weeps against a window from the far side. Nothing comes out. Everything runs down.

Yara sleeps badly these days. He is always half-rising, checking the map fragment Bram carried, checking the sky. But this morning he is deep under, breathing slow, and I am glad, because I do not want him to see my face while the woman with flour on her hands is still standing behind my eyes.

I do not know her name.

That is the cruelest arithmetic of it. I can remember the exact weight of her hand on the top of my head. I can remember that she smelled of yeast and rosemary and woodsmoke, that she hummed when she worked and sang when she thought no one listened, that the red-threaded cloth belonged to her mother and her mother's mother and that she let me hold one corner while the bread rose because holding it was luck. I remember everything except the one word I need most.

They did not take my memories. I understand this now, three turnings into the journey, watching the dreams come back like a tide returning to a shore it had abandoned. They took something smaller and worse. They took the *handles*. Every memory in my head is a door with its handle torn off — I can stand in front of it, I can press my face to it, but I cannot make it open onto anything as simple as *this is my mother*, or *this is my village*, or *this is my own name*.

The old tongue is coming back instead. Word by word, dream by dream. As if they poured my language out of me and filled the space with silence, and now that the seal on the jar is cracking, both things are flooding home at once — and they do not fit together. The words fit the memories. The memories fit the words. Only I don't.

* * *

The second dream comes two nights later, in the ruins below the pass where we shelter from sleet.

This time I am older in the dream. Five, maybe six. I know because the table reaches only to my chest, and in the other dream the table had been a mountain I stood beside.

There is a man at the table. He has my hands.

I stare at this across the whole width of the dream — the way his fingers drum the wood, the way he catches a fly out of the air and releases it through the door rather than killing it, the crooked knuckle on the third finger of the left hand that I have looked at every day of my remembered life on my own hand without ever knowing why it made my stomach hurt. It is his knuckle. It was always his knuckle. I inherited a broken bone I never knew broke.

He is arguing with someone I cannot see. His voice is low and hard and afraid, and the fear in it is worse than anger, the way a cracked bell is worse than a silent one. The unseen person speaks in a smooth voice, a broker's voice, a voice like oiled leather, and I cannot hear the words but I hear the shape of them, and the shape is an offer.

My father — and the word rises in me now, whole, unbearable, *father* — my father puts both hands flat on the table and says one sentence clearly enough that six years of sleep cannot erase it:

*"Not his soul. Take mine instead."*

And the smooth voice laughs — kindly, which is the worst thing a broker's laugh can be — and says, in the old tongue, words I now translate even in the dream, words I carry up through waking like a fish pulled gasping from deep water:

*A debt may be paid in coin, in labor, or in blood. But a soul sold must be redeemed by a claim of equal weight. Bring me such a claim, little man, and the boy walks free.*

Then the dream turns over, the way dreams do, like a page, and I am in the dark of the mountain again, and there are lanterns hanging everywhere in rows, and each lantern flickers in a rhythm, and after a long time of watching them I understood, in the way you understand things in dreams without being told, that each lantern was keeping time with a heartbeat somewhere above, and that one day a lantern would go out, and somewhere above, someone would stop.

I woke with Sef's blanket twisted around me like a bandage and the whole camp awake around the fire, because I had been making a noise none of them had ever heard me make.

It was not crying. Crying has sound. This was the sound a house makes when the wind finds a gap it never found before — a low, hollow, structural keening, coming from somewhere in me that should have been sealed. Bram's death had opened the gap. The dreams were pouring through it.

Sef crouched in front of me and did not touch me, which I loved her for, because touching me just then would have been like touching a spiderweb. She waited until the keening stopped on its own, the way weather stops, and then she said, "Bad ones?"

I nodded.

"Of him?" She meant Bram.

I shook my head, and then — because lying to Sef felt like a sin of a different order than any I had committed before — I shook my head again, uncertainly, and pointed at my own temple, and then at the dark east, where the mountain was.

She understood. I watched her understand. Her face went through three expressions very fast: pity, fury, and calculation. With anyone else that last one would have chilled me. With Sef it was a comfort. It meant she was already thinking about how to kill whoever had done this to me.

"Sleep," she said. "Tomorrow we walk."

But I did not sleep. I sat up until the grey hour watching Yara, who was also awake, hunched over the map fragment with a stub of charcoal, drawing and erasing and drawing again. He thinks I do not notice things. Everyone thinks I do not notice things, because noticing requires speech in their minds, and I have none. But I noticed that his hand trembled when he drew the doubled river. I noticed that he had started whispering answers to me in the old tongue in his sleep, words no valley-born cartographer should own, and that he woke from those whispers looking like a man who has found a stranger's clothes in his own wardrobe, fitted perfectly to his body.

He is remembering too. He does not know yet what he is remembering. I think I might know. I think the mountain showed me lanterns, and one of those lanterns, in the row, burned a color I recognized — a pale gold, like lamplight through honey, like the light in a kitchen with red-threaded cloth on the sill — and it hung apart from the others in a alcove of its own, guarded, precious, labeled in the broker's smooth voice with a word I could not read then and can almost read now.

Almost.

* * *

Between the dreams, the days go on, and this is its own strange education: learning that grief does not stop the world. Sef argues routes with Denna. The horses complain about the sleet. Oskar burns the porridge, twice, and swears at it in two languages, and Vess laughs at him, and the laughing is ordinary and warm and completely indifferent to the fact that I spent the night watching my father sell himself to save me and fail.

I used to think that was what the mountain had stolen — my voice, my years, my name. Now I understand it stole the ability to be ordinary. Grief with no voice becomes a permanent condition. The others grieve Bram loudly and then, visibly, in stages, put him down; I watch them do it with something like envy. Their sorrow has handles. They can pick it up and set it aside. Mine is a door with no handle, and I must stand in front of it forever.

Except.

On the third morning after the second dream, we come down out of the pass and the land opens ahead of us into a wide grey valley, and in the middle of the valley stands a city — walled, ordered, smoking gently from a hundred licensed chimneys — and Sef spits and calls it by a name full of contempt, and Yara looks at it the way he looks at blank spaces on maps, hungry and wary at once.

I look at the city and my knees nearly give.

Because I have drawn it.

Not seen it. Drawn it. In the dream between the second and third turning — a dream so thin and bright I had dismissed it as nothing, a scrap, a leaf blown past — I sat at a high desk in a room lined with shelves of ledgers, and I drew, with a stylus of black glass, a perfect map of a walled city with a hundred chimneys, and my hand knew every street, because the map was not a copy of the city. The city was a copy of the map. That is what the court beneath the mountain does. That is what the smooth-voiced brokers do. They draw the world first, in contracts and ledgers and lines of ownership, and then the world, poor obedient thing, grows up to match the drawing. Every street permitted. Every chimney taxed. Every soul weighed.

I grip Yara's sleeve. He looks down at me, surprised — I rarely reach for anyone — and I point at the city, and then, because pointing is not enough, because the dream is still hot in me like a coal, I take his charcoal out of his hand, kneel, and draw on the flat stone at the roadside.

I draw the walls. I draw the gate with its twin towers. I draw the great square, the tiered courts, the licensing hall, the taxhouse, the bell tower whose bell only rings for sanctioned events. My hand moves without hesitation. I have never been here. I could not find this city on any of Yara's maps. But my hand knows it the way my mouth knows the lullaby.

When I finish, the road is very quiet.

Yara picks up the stone with both hands, the way you pick up something wounded, or holy. He compares it to his own sketch of the approach roads and his face goes white and then grey and then settles into the expression I have come to dread and rely on more than any other: the expression of a man who has stopped pretending the world is the size of his maps.

"How," he says. Just that. Just *how*.

And I open my mouth, and the answer comes up from wherever the dreams have been storing it, in the old tongue, three words, and this time I know what they mean, because knowing and speaking arrive together for the first time since the mountain closed its hand around my throat:

*I built it.*

No. Not quite. The words are subtler than that, and crueler. What I said, exactly, in the grammar of a language older than kingdoms, was: *my hands remember building it.* Which means the mountain did not only take my childhood. It *used* it. Somewhere in those lost years, in the dark, among the ledgers and lanterns, small hands — my hands — drew the cities of the lawful world, and the lawful world grew up to match.

Sef reads the stone over Yara's shoulder. Then she looks at the real city on the horizon, and then back at the stone, and I watch a decision move through her like weather.

"They'll search everyone at the gate," she says. "Licensed travelers, licensed goods, licensed tongues." Her eyes come down to me. "And they'll have a description of the boy by now. Brokers talk to toll-keepers; toll-keepers talk to city clerks; clerks report to the crown, and the crown reports downward, to *them*."

"Then we don't use the gate," Yara begins.

"There's no other gate. That's the point of a permitted city." Sef chews this over. Then, slowly, she smiles, and it is not a kind smile, and I have never been gladder to see anything. "But gates are for things that enter honestly." She taps my stone map, right on the wall's western curve, where I have — without deciding to, without knowing why — drawn a seam in the stones, a place where the masonry doesn't match, a builder's flaw. "Boy. Is that real?"

I look at the seam. My hands remember it. A wall I helped design, flawed on purpose — by me? by the child I was? — and I realize, with a feeling like the ground tilting, that some part of me left doors in the world the mountain made me build. Small ones. Handleless. But doors.

I nod.

"Then tomorrow," Sef says, rolling the stone map closed and tucking it into her coat like a stolen thing, which I suppose it is — a stolen thing returned — "we enter the City of Permitted Streets as smugglers of our own lives. And boy—" she crouches to my height, and her voice drops to the register she saves for truths, "—if your hands remember how to build their city, I'm thinking your hands might remember how to unbuild it. Hold onto that. We'll want it under the mountain."

That night, the third turning comes full, and the moon stands over the permitted city like an eye that has finally finished opening, and I dream one last dream before morning.

In it, my mother — the word arrives whole now, *mother*, and I hold it carefully, like a bird with a broken wing that might still fly — my mother stands in the kitchen doorway with flour on her hands and looks at me with an expression I finally have the handles for: it is the expression of someone watching a debt approach their child and knowing the exact date it comes due. And she sings the shelf-song one more time, and adds a verse I have never heard, and the verse says:

*But names laid down may be reclaimed
by any hand that loves the name.*

I wake before dawn with the verse burning in me, and I go to Yara, who is awake as always at the grey hour, and I take his charcoal, and on the back of Bram's fragment, in the margin, in the old tongue, in letters I have not written since before the mountain, I write the verse out entire.

Yara reads it. Reads it again. And then he lifts his head, and his eyes are the eyes of a man who has just realized that the map he has been searching for was folded inside him all along.

"That's not a lullaby," he says quietly. "That's a clause. That's a *contract clause*." His hand closes slowly around the charcoal. "A name laid down may be reclaimed by any hand that loves it. Boy — do you understand what your mother wrote into your cradle-song?"

I do not answer. Outside, the permitted city wakes to the ringing of its sanctioned bell, and we begin to walk toward it — toward the seam in the western wall, toward the lawful world that grew from my own stolen drawings, toward whatever my hands remember next.
