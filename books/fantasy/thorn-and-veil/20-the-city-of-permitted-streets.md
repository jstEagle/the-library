## 20. The City of Permitted Streets

The city announced itself a full day before we reached it.

We crested a ridge of grey limestone in the late afternoon and there it was below us, spread across the river plain like something poured rather than built — Vessamare, Sef called it, the City of Permitted Streets. I had drawn it once, years ago, from a merchant's drunken description: a smear of rooftops and a river. The reality was worse than any smear. It was orderly. That was what struck me first, before the walls, before the towers — the terrible, meticulous order of it. Fields in rows so straight they might have been ruled onto the earth. Roads that met at perfect angles. And rising above everything, thin as needles, poles hung with lanterns of white glass, hundreds of them, each one burning with a light that did not flicker.

"What are those?" I asked.

Sef stood beside me with her arms crossed, her face wearing the expression she saved for things she despised on principle. "Witness-lanterns. They burn on soul-light — rendered down, refined. Cheap fuel, if you don't mind what it's made of." She spat over the ridge. "They say a lantern can see a face and remember it. Every street, every gate, every market square. You walk into Vessamare, you are *known*."

Beside me, the boy had gone very still. He had been still more and more often since the Ashwood, as if the closer we drew to the mountain, the more he could feel whatever thread ran between his chest and the dark beneath it pulling taut. His hand found my sleeve without his looking at it, the way a man's hand finds a rail in fog.

"We don't have to go through," I said. "There must be a way around."

"There is." Sef turned down the slope, already walking. "Nine days around, through the Salt Fens, and we've got five. Or one day through the city, if you can stomach being counted." She glanced back at me, and there was no mockery in it, which from Sef was its own kind of courtesy. "You wanted to see how the other half keeps order, mapman. Here's your chance."

I told myself she was wrong about my reasons. I told myself it was only arithmetic — five days against nine, a boy's debt maturing like rotten fruit, no time to spare. Both things were true. But underneath the arithmetic there was something else, a cartographer's hunger I didn't like to name: I wanted to see it. A city where every street was licensed, every door numbered, every soul entered into a ledger. I had spent my life drawing places that refused to be drawn. Some part of me needed to look at the opposite thing and understand it.

I took the boy's hand and we went down into the light.

* * *

The gate of Vessamare was not a wall with doors in it. It was a building — a long, low hall of pale stone through which every road passed, and inside it, the counting happened.

There were perhaps forty clerks behind forty desks, and above each desk a witness-lantern burned with its patient, unblinking light. Travelers queued in lanes marked by brass rails, shuffling forward one at a time to state their name, their business, their destination, and their *worth*. That last one puzzled me until I watched a farmer ahead of us answer it: he named his acreage, his stock, the contents of his strongbox, and the clerk wrote it all down and stamped a slip of paper and the farmer pressed his thumb into an inkpad and then onto the paper, and that was that. He was permitted. He walked out the far side into the city carrying his own small contract.

"Entry tax," Sef murmured. "Exit tax. Market tax, road tax, window tax — they tax windows here, by the pane. Everything's got a fee because everything's got a *value*, and everything's got a value because the court taught them how to weigh it." She said it flatly, but I heard the old anger under it, banked coals she carried everywhere. "Keep your hood up. Lanterns remember faces; clerks remember names. Give them neither."

We joined the queue. It moved with the smooth, horrible patience of water finding a channel. No one pushed. No one complained. A woman two places ahead of us was quietly weeping while she answered the clerk's questions, and the clerk waited with perfect courtesy for her to finish sobbing before asking the next question, and the lantern above them both went on burning, and no one did anything at all.

"Where do the lanterns get their light?" I asked Sef, though I already dreaded the answer.

"Souls," she said. "Rendered ones. The court sells the dregs — souls too worn out to hang as lanterns down below, too dim to be worth keeping. They ship them up in iron casks and the city burns them in the streets." She watched my face as I understood it. "Every lamp in this city is somebody. Most folk just try not to think about whose."

The boy made a sound. Small, low, in the back of his throat — the noise he made instead of words when words were too far away. I put my hand on the back of his neck, under his collar, where the sigil sat hidden against his skin, and I felt him breathe again.

When our turn came, the clerk looked up. He was young, with ink-stained fingers and the pleasant, emptied-out face of a man who has been trained to be a surface only. Above him the witness-lantern leaned — I would have sworn it *leaned*, the flame bending toward us like a plant toward sun.

"Names," said the clerk.

"Travelers," said Sef. "Pilgrims, bound for the mountain shrines. We've paid the shrine-road toll at Corvess." She set coins on the desk with the ease of long practice, and among them, I noticed, a stamped slip that had not existed ten minutes ago. I decided not to wonder where she'd gotten it.

The clerk examined the slip, examined us. The lantern's flame stood perfectly upright now, straining, quivering. For a moment I thought the whole machinery of the city would turn its attention on us — three travelers with one soul between them mortgaged to the dark, and a fourth soul, mine, that apparently should not exist at all.

Then the clerk yawned, stamped our permission, and waved us through.

It was only outside, in the shadow of the wall, that Sef exhaled and muttered, "That lantern saw something. Lanterns don't strain unless they're hungry."

"Hungry for what?"

"For anything with a soul worth weighing." She pulled her hood lower. "Walk fast, mapman."

* * *

Inside, Vessamare was beautiful the way a ledger is beautiful.

Every street was straight and clean and signed. Every shop displayed its license in a gilt frame beside the door — licenses for baking, licenses for hammering, licenses for the selling of bread baked under license. Children wore brass tokens on cords around their necks certifying their existence. Beggars were absent, or rather, they had been assigned places: I saw them sitting in marked alcoves, each with a number painted above his head, alms given by dropping coin into slots so that no hand ever touched another hand.

And everywhere, everywhere, the lanterns. White glass globes on their black poles, at every corner, outside every door, their light steady as held breath. People walked beneath them without looking up. I could not stop looking up.

"It's the mountain," I said quietly, as we crossed a square where a fountain played and a lantern hung over the water and its reflection lay trembling on the surface like a drowned star. "Don't you see it? This is what the court looks like when it gets big enough to build streets."

Sef stopped. She looked around the square — the licensed vendors, the numbered beggars, the tokens, the lamps — and something moved across her face that was almost pain.

"Aye," she said. "That's the lesson, isn't it. Everyone thinks the court beneath the mountain is some other place. Some hell with different rules. But it's just this." She kicked the base of a lantern pole, gently, almost affectionately, the way you'd kick a sleeping dog you hated. "This is what happens when weighing becomes the whole of the law. Down below they keep the souls in ledgers and hang them up when they're ripe. Up here they keep the *lives* in ledgers and call it peace. Same hands wrote both books."

The boy tugged my sleeve. He pointed across the square.

A crowd had gathered, quiet and orderly, around a raised platform where a city official stood reading from a scroll. Two bailiffs flanked him, and between them knelt a man in a baker's apron, his hands bound, his head bowed. The official's voice carried clearly across the square, trained and pleasant:

"—found guilty of the unlicensed practice of memory-work, contrary to Statute Eleven, and sentenced accordingly. The offender's memories of the unlicensed period will be extracted and remitted to the civic archive, where they may be purchased by interested parties at standard rates—"

"They're taking his memories," I breathed. "As a *fine*."

"As revenue," Sef corrected. "Look at the crowd. Nobody's horrified. Half of them are wondering what a baker's memories go for these days." Her jaw was tight. "In the fens and the high country, when a man does wrong, we sit him down in front of everyone he's harmed and he hears it from their mouths and they decide together what he owes. It's slow and it's ugly and sometimes it fails. But nobody *profits* from it. The moment justice turns a profit, it stops being justice and starts being harvest."

On the platform, the extraction began. It was done with a small silver instrument and a great deal of ceremony, and the baker did not scream — he simply grew quieter, section by section, like a house being emptied room by room, and when they led him away he walked steadily and looked at nothing, and the crowd dispersed satisfied, and the lantern above the platform burned a little brighter.

I know it did. I stood there and watched it happen, and the flame was brighter afterward, and no one else seemed to notice, and I understood that I was looking at the machine entire — the court and the city, the mountain and the street, one apparatus running from the deepest dark to the brightest day, grinding souls into light and calling the light civilization.

The boy's hand slipped into mine. He was shaking.

"Home," he said. Just that. Just the one word he owned. But he said it looking back the way we had come, toward the open country and the valley beyond it, and I knew what he meant. Not a place. A *kind* of place — anywhere a person wasn't weighed.

"We'll get there," I told him, and hated how much it sounded like a promise I had no right to make.

* * *

We lodged that night in a licensed inn, because an unlicensed bed was a crime in Vessamare, and Sef judged — correctly — that the safest hiding place in a surveillance city was inside its own paperwork. Our names went into the register. False ones, but written down, stamped, filed. Somewhere in the city's archives, three travelers who did not exist now officially existed, and Sef found this funnier than I did.

I couldn't sleep. I sat by the window of our rented room with my journal open on my knees and the white lantern-light coming through the glass, and I drew.

I hadn't meant to draw the city. My hand simply started moving — the square, the platform, the lanterns, the queue at the gatehouse, the numbered alcoves, the fountain with its drowned star. And as I drew, the strange gift — curse — whatever Ilvane would later help me understand — woke in my fingertips, that faint warmth, that sense of the ink wanting to be true. I felt the city settle onto the page the way wet cloth settles onto a body. For a moment the lines of my map and the lines of the streets were the same lines, and I could feel the whole grid of Vessamare lying under my pen like a net lying over a fish.

And beneath the net, faintly, I felt something else. Threads. Hundreds of them, thousands, running up out of the city and away southward, converging, all bending toward one distant point like iron filings toward a lodestone. Soul-threads. Debts. Every contracted soul in the region, it seemed, paid its interest along lines that passed through — or terminated at — the mountain.

Including one that ran directly out of the boy's sleeping chest, through the floor, through the earth, south.

I sat very still, pen lifted, heart hammering, and did the thing I am apparently incapable of not doing: I mapped it. One line, carefully, in red ink, from the point where the boy slept to the southern horizon. Then I sat staring at it, because a map is a claim, and I had just drawn a line that said *this belongs to me, this journey, this debt, this child* — and somewhere, I was suddenly certain, in a cathedral of ledgers far beneath the mountain, something had felt the tug.

The boy stirred in his sleep. He made his small wordless sound, and then, half-dreaming, he spoke — not *home* this time, but a string of syllables in that older tongue, soft and pleading, the voice of someone arguing with a figure in a dream. Arguing, or begging.

And then, clear as a bell, one word I somehow understood without knowing how:

"*Coming.*"

He said it the way you'd report the weather. *The debt is coming.* Or maybe — and this was the version that kept me awake until the lanterns outside paled with dawn — he said it the way a messenger says it. As if something on the far end of that red line had answered him in his sleep, and he was only passing the message along.

I closed the journal. Outside, Vessamare was waking to its bells and its queues and its licensed morning, and beyond the southern walls, past the plains and the passes, the mountain waited with its books open.

Five days. We had five days left, and a favor to collect from a queen's exile — if the exile proved willing, and if the road let us reach her, and if the thing at the end of the red thread didn't come to meet us first.
