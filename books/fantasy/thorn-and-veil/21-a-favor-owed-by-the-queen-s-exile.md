## 21. A Favor Owed by the Queen's Exile

The city of Vell had three gates, and Sef hated all of them.

The north gate was for merchants, and its guards weighed every cart like a coffin. The south gate was for pilgrims, and its guards weighed every pilgrim harder. The east gate was for the licensed — letters of passage, seals of trade, writs of worth — and it was the only one that mattered tonight, because Ilvane the Gray had been granted one thing when they stripped her of her title and cast her out of the court: the right to live in Vell. Not to leave it. Not to practice her art. Just to exist, on paper, in a narrow house on Cordwainer Street with her windows painted over and her name struck from every register except the one that said *exiled*.

"You're certain she'll see us," Yara said. He walked beside her with the boy's hand in his, wearing the borrowed gray cloak Sef had given him three days ago, looking like exactly what he wasn't — a lawful man on lawful business.

"I'm certain she'll open the door," Sef said. "What happens after the door is anyone's guess."

"And if she refuses?"

"Then we've lost a night and gained a story." Sef kept her eyes moving. Vell at night was all lantern-wardens and ledger-clerks, every street corner stamped with the city's seal, every beggar carrying a permit to beg. She despised the place the way a hawk despises a cage — not because it was cruel, but because it was *tidy* about it. "Exiles don't get many visitors, Yara. Visitors remind them of doors."

The boy tugged Yara's hand and signed something quick — *cold, cold, the streets here are cold* — and Yara signed back without looking down, the two of them fluent in a language Sef had watched grow between them like ivy. Twelve turns gone. Four behind them, nine ahead, and the moon overhead was a fat silver coin spending itself toward the thirteenth.

Cordwainer Street smelled of tallow and old rain. The house at its end was narrower than the others, squeezed between a chandler's and a wall, and its windows were indeed painted over — but Sef noted, with the small satisfaction of a professional, that the paint had been applied from the inside. Whatever Ilvane the Gray had given up, she had not given up watching.

Sef knocked. Four beats, then two, then one — the old rhythm, the one the queen's own household had used a lifetime ago, back when Sef had been nobody and Ilvane had been somebody and neither of them had known it.

The silence behind the door went on long enough that Yara shifted his weight.

Then the door opened the width of a knife, and a voice like dry paper said: "That knock is twenty years dead. Who taught it to you?"

"A woman who owes me a favor," Sef said. "Or owes me the memory of one. I've never been sure which."

The door opened all the way.

* * *

Ilvane the Gray was not what Sef remembered, and was exactly what she remembered, which is a thing that happens to everyone you meet twice across a gulf of years. The woman who had once worn the queen's silver chain at her throat now wore a housecoat the color of dishwater, and her hair — once black as a magistrate's ink — had gone the gray of her name. But her eyes were unchanged: pale, precise, the eyes of someone who had spent her life measuring things that did not wish to be measured.

She looked at Sef first. Then Yara. Then the boy, and Sef saw it happen — saw the measuring stop, saw something behind those pale eyes go still and cold and *afraid*.

"Come in," Ilvane said, "before the wardens see you. And wipe your boots. I have spent twenty years keeping this floor clean of the court's dirt, and I will not have it tracked in now."

The house inside was smaller than its outside, which Sef knew was a mage's trick and hated her for it anyway. Shelves crowded the walls, but they held no grimoires. They held jars — hundreds of them, labeled in a hand so fine it looked printed — and each jar held something Sef did not want to look at too long: a moth with too many wings. A knot of rope that untied itself when she wasn't watching. A coin that showed a different face each time it was turned over. Small magics, bottled. The permitted residue of a forbidden life.

"Sit," Ilvane said. "No — not you, captain. You'll stand. You always stand. You stood at the queen's shoulder for six years and I don't suppose exile taught you to sit."

"You remember that," Sef said.

"I remember everything. It's the curse of my profession." Ilvane turned to Yara, and her voice changed — softened by a degree, the way a blade softens when it's turned from wood to flesh. "You're the cartographer. The one from the valley that refuses."

Yara blinked. "You know of the valley?"

"I know of everything the court knows, and the court knows more than it says." She sat down across from him, folding her hands. "The Veiled Valley. The blank space on every royal map. Do you know why your home is a blank space, cartographer?"

"Because magic can't cross its boundary," Yara said. "I've spent my life trying to map it. The instruments fail. The ink runs pale."

"Yes. And why do you suppose the most powerful court beneath the sun permits a hole in its map the size of a duchy?" Ilvane's pale eyes didn't blink. "Because it's not a hole, man. It's a *seal*. And seals have makers, and makers have reasons, and I suspect you've begun to guess whose hands made yours."

Sef watched Yara's face do something complicated and painful. The boy, sitting close against his side, signed something small — *don't be sad* — and Yara took a breath and didn't answer the question at all.

"We're not here about me," he said. "We're here about him." He turned gently, and lifted the boy's collar enough to show the sigil beneath — the contract-mark, the brand that Sef's whole band had learned to read this season, the mark that turned a child into a ledger entry. "His soul was sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain. The debt matures on the thirteenth turning of the moon. Nine turns remain. We mean to take him to the mountain and break the contract."

Ilvane was quiet for a long moment. In the silence, one of the jars on the shelf — the one with the self-tying rope — gave a small, restless clink, as if it too were listening.

"You mean to walk into the Under-Crown's hall," Ilvane said at last, "and *break a contract*."

"Yes."

"With what army?"

"Thirty-one outlaws," Sef said. "Give or take the ones who've decided we're all mad."

"With what magic?"

"With him." Yara's hand rested on the boy's head. "His maps come true," the boy signed, and Ilvane's eyes flicked to the moving fingers, and Sef saw the mage go very still — the stillness of a woman catching a scent she'd buried twenty years ago.

"His maps come true," Ilvane repeated softly. "Of course they do. Born outside the veil, are you, boy? Or inside it?" She didn't wait for an answer. "It doesn't matter. Not tonight. What matters is that you are asking me to commit the crime I was exiled for refusing to commit — no. Forgive me. That's not true, and I don't lie in my own house." She stood, walked to the shelf, and took down a jar of plain salt, turning it in her hands like a rosary. "I was exiled for refusing to *weigh* souls. What you're asking is worse. You're asking me to help unweigh one. To take something the court has entered in its ledger and strike it out."

"Can it be done?" Sef asked.

"Everything can be done," Ilvane said. "That is the tragedy of magic. The question is what it costs, and who pays, and whether the ones who pay are the ones who agreed to the price." She set the salt down. "The gate beneath the mountain admits only those who can name their true desire. That's the first lock, and it's the cruelest, because most people don't know their own hearts, and the gate *knows*, and it takes its toll in honesty. Past the gate is the Hall of Ledgers. Past the hall is the Under-Crown. And between you and all of it are the broker-enforcers, the hollow men, and nine turns of moon in which any one of a thousand things can go wrong."

"You've been there," Sef said. It wasn't a question.

"I built half of it," Ilvane said, and the words fell into the room like stones into a well. "I was the court's mage of weights and measures for eleven years. I designed the scales that weigh a soul. I wrote the clauses that let them sell a child's name to pay a father's debt. I was very good at my work." Her voice didn't shake, but her hands, around the salt jar, went white. "And then a woman came to the court to redeem her daughter's soul — a farm woman, with a pig and two chickens as her whole estate — and the court weighed her offering and found it light by three ounces of soul-stuff, and I was ordered to take the difference from the *mother*. Not her soul. Her *memory of the daughter*. So that the debt would be paid and the mother would stand in the hall, weeping, not knowing why she wept." She set the jar down with a click. "I refused. I unsealed the daughter's lantern myself, in the hall, in front of the Under-Crown, and I walked out of the mountain alive only because killing me would have required admitting what I knew. Exile was cheaper. The court is always counting."

The fire popped. The boy had gone very pale, and Yara had put his arm around him, and Sef found she was standing the way she always stood — weight forward, hand near her knife, though there was nothing in the room to fight.

"So you know the way in," Sef said. "The locks. The wards. The clauses."

"I know the way in the way a surgeon knows the body she once cut open." Ilvane looked at her, and for a moment Sef saw the woman she must have been at court — the pale eyes gone hard as assay-marks. "And you know why I should say no. Helping you is treason against the only institution that still acknowledges I exist. If they learn of it, they won't exile me twice. They'll weigh *me*."

"Then don't help us for free," Sef said.

Ilvane's eyebrows rose a fraction. "Captain."

"You're a woman of accounts. So let's keep one." Sef stepped forward, into the firelight, and made her voice the flat, level voice she used for dividing plunder and for dividing blame. "You owe me nothing and I owe you nothing, and that's the cleanest ground there is to build on. But I carried a message for you once, twenty years ago, from a woman in the queen's household, and I never delivered it, because by the time I could, you were already gone. I've carried it ever since. I don't know what it said. I never read it. But I still have it." She reached into her coat and drew out the letter — small, sealed with plain wax, softened and darkened by two decades against her heart. "I'll give you that tonight, free, because it was never mine to keep. And in exchange, you give us your knowledge of the mountain. And then — *then* — we're even, and we start a new account."

Ilvane took the letter without a word. She turned it over once. Her thumb moved across the wax seal as if reading braille, and Sef watched twenty years move behind the pale eyes like weather.

"This is Tamsin's hand," Ilvane said quietly. "The queen's taster. She's dead."

"I know. Eleven years now. Fever."

"She never learned I'd gone. She wrote this thinking I was still at court." Ilvane held the letter a moment longer, then set it on the table between them, unopened. "And you never read it."

"Wasn't mine to read."

"No," Ilvane said. "It wasn't. And that, captain, is the most expensive thing anyone has ever given me in this room." She looked at the letter, and then at the boy, and then at Yara, and Sef saw the scales behind her eyes tipping — the woman who had spent her life weighing things weighing, at last, herself.

"Here is my price," Ilvane said. "Not gold. Not passage. Not protection — you'll have none to spare by the end, I promise you that." She straightened, and the dishwater housecoat suddenly looked less like a rag and more like a robe, and Sef understood she was seeing, for one moment, the mage of weights and measures in full. "A favor. Owed by me, to be named by you — no. The other way. A favor *I* will one day ask of *you*, unnamed, to be called in when I choose, at a time of my choosing, and you will answer it. All of you. The captain, the cartographer, the child. Whatever I ask, when I ask it."

"That's not a price," Sef said slowly. "That's a hook."

"It is exactly a hook," Ilvane agreed. "I learned contract-craft from the best and worst court in creation. I know what an unnamed favor is worth: everything, because it could be anything. That is why I name it. If I asked for gold, you'd pay it and forget me. This way, you won't." Her pale eyes moved across the three of them. "Refuse, and I'll wish you luck and bolt my door, and you can go die under the mountain with my blessing. Accept, and I'll draw you the mountain's throat from memory — every gate, every ward, every clause I wrote and every clause they wrote after I left. Choose."

The fire crackled. The rope in its jar clinked, restless.

Yara looked at Sef. Sef looked at the ceiling, at the painted-over windows, at the hundred small bottled magics of a woman who had traded everything for one act of mercy and had been paying for it ever since. She thought about Bram's empty saddle. She thought about the boy signing *home* by firelight. She thought about every debt she'd ever collected and how the collecting always cost the collector something the ledger never recorded.

She thought about what an unnamed favor could become, in the hands of a woman who had designed the scales of souls — and who had, once, at terrible cost, refused to use them.

"We accept," Sef said.

"No," Yara said, and Sef turned. The cartographer's face was drawn, but level. "We accept — with a clause. You get your favor, unnamed. But not from the boy. Whatever you ask, when you ask it, it can't be asked *of him*. He's paid enough to other people's ledgers. He doesn't go into ours."

Silence. Ilvane studied the cartographer for a long moment, and then — Sef would have sworn to it — the corner of her mouth moved, the ghost of a smile, the first in twenty years perhaps.

"A clause," she said. "Spoken like a man who has read contracts he hates. Very well. The favor is mine to name, but never of the child. Agreed and witnessed." She extended her hand — not to Sef, but to Yara — and Yara took it, and Sef felt the air in the room change the way it changes when a seal is pressed into wax.

"Then witness this too," Ilvane said, and her voice dropped, and the firelight seemed to lean in. "Before I draw you the mountain, you should know what you're carrying into it. The court doesn't just want the boy. The court has wanted the boy's *kind* for a very long time — and there are those beneath the mountain, cartographer, who have been waiting nine turns of the moon for a soul like his to walk through the gate of its own free will." She turned to the shelf, took down a blank jar, and began, with a fine-nibbed pen, to draw. "Because your maps come true, and theirs do too, and the Under-Crown has spent nine turns preparing a map of its own."

She looked up at them across the small, crowded, watching room.

"Now," said Ilvane the Gray, "let me show you what we're walking into."
