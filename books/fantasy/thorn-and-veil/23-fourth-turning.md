## 23. Fourth Turning

The moon came up wrong.

Yara noticed it first as a smudge on the horizon while he was mending a strap on his pack, and then again an hour later when it cleared the ridgeline of the Ashwood's far edge — a hair too wide, a shade too copper, like a coin held too long near a fire. He stood with the leather strap forgotten in his hands and watched it climb, and something in his chest tightened in a way he could not name. Four turnings now since they had left the valley. Half the debt paid down by mere distance and days. Eight more before the thirteenth moon rose whole — or failed to — over whatever ground they would be standing on when it did.

Behind him, in the hollow beneath the leaning stone where they had made camp, the boy slept. He slept badly these nights. That was new. For the first two moons of their journey the boy had been a still sleeper, curled tight as a fern frond, silent even in dreams. But lately he whimpered. Lately he turned and turned, and once — three nights past — he had sat bolt upright at midnight and looked straight through Yara as if Yara were a window, and said nothing, because he could say nothing, and gone back down into sleep like a stone dropped into deep water.

And Yara himself was dreaming things he could not account for.

He finished the strap without looking at it. His fingers knew the work; his mind was elsewhere, standing at the edge of a room he had no memory of ever entering.

* * *

It had started with the smell of bread.

That was the absurd part. Not light, not voices, not some grand vision — bread. Three nights ago, on watch, he had caught the smell of baking bread where there was no oven within fifty miles, and for one unguarded moment he had been *somewhere else*: a kitchen with steam-fogged windows, flour on every surface including his own small hands, and a woman laughing low in her throat as she pulled a loaf from the iron stove. He had felt warmth on his face. He had felt — this was the part that undid him — that he was *small*, that his feet did not reach the floor from the stool he sat on, that the world was sized for grown bodies and he was newly arrived in one of his own.

Then Sef had said his name across the fire, and it was gone. All of it. Except the smell, which clung to him for an hour after like smoke to wool.

He knew what it was. There was only one thing it could be. In the Veiled Market, to buy the way through the mountain's gates, he had laid a memory on the scale — his mother's face, the trader had said, weighing it like a coin of doubtful mint — and watched it wrapped in grey silk and carried away into the veiled dark. He had walked out of the market knowing he had lost something vital and being unable to remember what it was, which was the cruelest trick of the trade: grief with its object amputated, mourning with no corpse to mourn.

But the memory was coming back.

Not whole. Not the way it went. It came back in pieces, in smells and angles and the texture of moments, like a map recovered piecemeal from a flood — a corner here, a coastline there — and each piece arrived at night, uninvited, and each piece fit strangely against the life he remembered living.

Because here was the thing he could not stop turning over, the stone in his boot: the kitchen in the fragment was not in the valley.

He had lived his entire remembered life in Dunmore Vale. Born there, so far as anyone knew — old Tam Weller used to swear he'd delivered him, though Tam swore he'd delivered half the valley — raised in the croft below the surveyor's mark, taught letters by the miller's wife and numbers by whoever would tolerate his questions. The valley had no kitchens with fogged windows and iron stoves of that make. The valley baked on hearthstones, in low rooms, with woodsmoke that stung. The kitchen in his fragment had glass windows. Glass, in a house that smelled of yeast and clean linen, and beyond the glass —

Beyond the glass there had been mountains. Wrong mountains. Mountains with snow on them in high summer, arranged in a order he did not know, and between him and those mountains a garden, and beyond the garden a wall —

A veil.

The word arrived in his head with the force of a struck bell, and he set down the strap and pressed the heels of his hands against his eyes and breathed.

*The veil was for you.*

No. That wasn't a memory. That was — where had that come from? A voice, a woman's voice, warm and tired and fierce all at once, saying words he had never heard spoken in his life: *the veil was for you.* He tested it the way he tested any suspect source. Was it a dream? Dreams faded. This did not fade. It sat in him with the density of fact. Was it something Ilvane had said? He ran back through the exile mage's careful, guarded conversations and found nothing — she spoke of the valley's wall, yes, of magic walled out, but never in a voice like that, never with that particular tenderness, that tone of a woman explaining to a child why the world must be kept away from him.

*The veil was for you.*

He opened his eyes. The moon had climbed another hand's width. Copper, patient, halfway to full.

"Who are you?" he asked the moon quietly, and of course it did not answer, and he hated himself a little for asking.

* * *

The boy woke near dawn, the way he always did — all at once, eyes open, body still, as if sleep were a country he crossed out of at the border rather than drifted from. Yara had learned to read the boy the way he read terrain, by long attention to small signs, and this morning the terrain was troubled. The boy sat up and looked at Yara and then looked at the moon, low and pale-copper in the greying west, and his hand went to his collarbone, to the sigil beneath the skin, the way it did when the debt pressed close in his dreams.

"Bad night?" Yara asked, uselessly, as he always asked, knowing the boy could not answer in any tongue the waking world shared.

The boy shook his head. Then, after a moment, he nodded. Then he made a sound — not a word, not yet, though *home* had come a moon ago and other words were assembling themselves behind his teeth like birds deciding to land — a sound of frustration, and pressed both palms flat against his own chest.

Yara crouched to his level. "In your dreams?"

The boy nodded hard.

"What do you see?"

The boy's mouth worked. He pointed — at his own eyes, then outward, at the world — then swept his arm in a circle that took in the camp, the ridge, the sky, and finally closed his fist slowly, as if closing a hand around something small and stolen. Then he looked at Yara with an expression that Yara had last seen on the face of a man in the Broker's Tollhouse, staring at the ledger page that named him.

Something taken. Something seen. Something held shut.

"I know," Yara said. "I'm trying to get it back."

The boy studied him for a long moment. Then he reached out and touched Yara's temple, lightly, with one finger, the way Maren used to touch herbs she suspected of hiding deeper properties than their labels confessed.

Yara went very still. "What do you see?" he asked again, and heard how his voice changed on the second asking.

The boy tilted his head. Drew back his finger. And then, deliberately, he mimed — clumsily, but unmistakably — the act of wrapping: arms circling, circling, gathering something vast and invisible and folding it closed, over and over, until his arms could fold no further, and then he looked at Yara and pointed at him.

The cold that went through Yara then had nothing to do with the hour.

"You've dreamed that," he said. "You've dreamed me. Wrapped." He thought of the fragment — the kitchen, the glass, the wall beyond the garden — and of the voice, *the veil was for you*, and of the way his compass needle had spun wild the first time he touched this child, as if the boy were not a point on any map but a hole in the very fabric that maps describe. "Why would you dream that?"

The boy shrugged, the shrug of someone who does not know why he knows what he knows, which was the boy's permanent condition and, Yara was beginning to fear, his own.

They broke camp in silence. Sef's band had split into two companies after the Second Turning — six had gone north with Doss to keep faith with their own code, and five remained, and of those five only Rill and old Vetch were awake at this hour, striking the fire and packing with the economy of people who had fled from enough things to be good at it. Sef herself stood at the tree line with her spyglass aimed south, toward the mountain that was still four turnings distant, and she did not look around when Yara came to stand beside her, but she said:

"You didn't sleep."

"Neither did you."

"I don't have the luxury of interesting dreams." She lowered the spyglass. "You've got the look of a man whose map has stopped matching the ground."

Yara almost laughed. Trust an outlaw to find the exact phrase. "Is it that visible?"

"To me it is. I've spent twenty years reading faces for the lie in them. Yours isn't lying. Yours is frightened of something true." She slung the spyglass away. "Out with it or carry it alone, cartographer. Both are allowed. Only one of them is useful."

So he told her — briefly, because most of it had no words yet — about the fragments returning. The kitchen. The mountains that were wrong. The wall beyond the garden. And the voice, and what it said, and how the boy had dreamed him wrapped in folds of nothing.

Sef listened the way her band did everything: without interruption, without comfort, without flinching. When he finished she was quiet for the space of ten breaths, looking south.

"In the tollhouse," she said at last, "the ledgers named the boy's seller. You never told me who it was. You went white as paper and burned the page."

"I don't want to talk about that."

"I know. But I want to talk about this." She turned to face him fully, and her eyes in the early light were the grey of a blade's spine. "Every soul-broker ledger I've ever seen or burned had a section at the back we call the quiet pages. Debts the court doesn't collect. Debts it *watches*. Souls that were sold and then bought back and hidden somewhere the court can't reach, accruing interest all the while. Twenty years of raiding broker houses, and in every single one, the same entry appears in the quiet pages, written in the same hand. An account kept open for a soul called—" she paused, watching him— "the Valley Child."

The wind moved through the Ashwood's black trunks. Somewhere behind them the boy was showing Rill how to fold a map along its creases so it opened one-handed, a small domestic sound, unbearably ordinary.

"That's a story you tell recruits," Yara said. "To scare them."

"It's a story I tell myself," Sef said, "because I've read the entry. It doesn't say who holds the debt. It says who *holds the collateral*. One line. 'Asset veiled. Location known to holder only.' And the holder's mark is a sigil I've seen exactly twice in my life. Once in the deepest ledger in the Tollhouse. Once—" she tapped her own collarbone, mirroring the place where the boy's brand sat— "on the seal of a letter I stole off a dead courier nine years ago. A letter addressed to a house in a valley that isn't on any map."

Yara's mouth had gone dry. "Whose mark?"

"That's the question I've been carrying for nine years, and I'd hoped you'd answer it, because the letter was written in a hand I've since seen in a dozen official charters, and every charter it matches belongs to the office of Royal Cartographer." She let that sit between them. "Your trade, Halloran. Your guild. Your ink."

"My mother died birthing me," Yara said, and heard how it sounded: recited, rote, a sentence worn smooth from handling, a boundary stone he had never once thought to survey beyond. "My father was a surveyor. He drowned when I was six. Everyone in the valley will tell you the same."

"Everyone in the valley," Sef said gently, "lives inside a lie built to keep something hidden. Would they even know what they're telling you?"

*The veil was for you.*

He did not say it aloud. He could not. It felt too much like handing someone a live coal.

"We make the mountain in four turnings," he said instead. "Ilvane comes with us to the gate. Ask her. She was court mage; she'll know what the Royal Cartographer's seal means."

"I intend to." Sef shouldered her pack and turned back toward the road, and then paused. "Halloran. If it turns out the valley you love is a cage, and you're the thing it was built to cage — what then?"

"I don't know," he said honestly.

"Good," she said. "Men who know in advance frighten me."

* * *

They walked south through the morning, and the fragments kept arriving.

He had expected, after the Market, that losing his mother's face would mean losing her entirely — that the scale had weighed the whole woman and left him with a gap shaped like a person. Instead what he had lost was stranger and more surgical: her *face*, specifically, and everything attached to seeing it. Her voice had survived, he realized now, because voice was never part of what he'd surrendered — he simply had never had occasion to notice, since a voice without a face floats free as weather. And now the voice was coming back to him with terrible clarity, unmoored, speaking sentences he had no context for.

*Hold still, little star. Let me look at you.*

He stumbled on a root and Rill caught his elbow and asked no questions, which was Rill's particular mercy.

*The veil was for you.*

*They will come for what you are. So we will make a place where what you are cannot follow. We will make it so thorough, my love. We will make it so complete. And when you are grown —*

When you are grown *what*?

That was where the fragments tore. Every time, the same tear, the same missing seam, right at the place where a mother's explanation would become a mother's plan. As if the trader in the Veiled Market had not merely taken her face but had taken, with it, the ending of her — the resolution of the sentence that would have made sense of everything. He found himself hating the veiled traders with a slow, mineral hatred, colder and more durable than any he had felt toward the brokers. At least the brokers were honest about what they stole.

At noon they halted in the shadow of a fallen ash, and Yara sat apart and did the thing he trusted most in the world: he drew.

He drew the kitchen from the fragment. Glass windows, iron stove, flour-dust hanging in a shaft of light. He drew the wrong mountains beyond the garden. He drew the wall — and his pen hesitated over the wall, because in the fragment the wall was not stone. It was a shimmer, a heat-haze standing upright, a curtain of *not-quite*, and his hand knew how to render it the way his hand knew riverbends, from somewhere deeper than memory. He drew the veil. And under it, because a map is a confession of what the mapper believes, he wrote the only caption that was true:

*Built, not found.*

Maps record. Maps do not invent. If the veil appeared in his fragmentary memory as a constructed thing, with a maker, then it had a maker, and the maker had said — in a voice he owned like his own heartbeat — that it had been built *for him*. Which meant either that his mother had walled off an entire valley to hide a child, which was madness, power beyond reckoning—

—or that the valley had already existed, and she had *used* it, and the ones who made it had let her, and the debt of that favor was the kind of debt the Court Beneath the Mountain kept in its quiet pages, accruing interest across a childhood, waiting for the asset to walk out from behind the wall on his own legs.

He looked at the drawing. The ink was doing what his ink did now, out here beyond the valley — settling, thickening, taking on the faint authority of truth. The little shimmer-wall on the paper seemed to ripple.

"Cartographer." Vetch's voice, low, at his shoulder. The old outlaw was looking not at the map but at the trees — at the way the light between the trunks had begun to move wrong, pooling and sliding like oil on water. "Your ink's awake. And so is something else. We've got company or we've got omen, and I've stopped believing in omens."

Yara covered the map with both hands as if shielding a candle flame. The rippling stopped. Under his palms he could feel the paper humming faintly, like a struck string fading.

Four turnings. Four more moons until the thirteenth. And every fragment that returned to him made the road ahead less simple, not more: because he had set out to save a boy from a court that bought souls, and now it appeared that he himself was a soul the court had never stopped hunting — an asset, veiled, location known to holder only — and the holder's mark belonged to his own trade, and his own mother's voice had built the walls of his whole life around him like cupped hands, and cupped hands can hold, or they can conceal.

That evening, when they made camp, the boy came and sat beside him at the fire and leaned against his side, the way he had begun to do, trust expressed in weight. And as Yara stared into the flames, the final fragment of the day surfaced — not the kitchen this time, not the garden, but a single image, brief as a lightning strike: a table strewn with charts, a hand holding a pen, a seal being pressed into wax, and the hand —

The hand glowed.

Softly, steadily, from within, gold at the fingertips, glowing the way Ilvane's hands had glowed the first time she cast before them on the road — and Yara looked down at his own hands, ordinary, ink-stained, mapped with scars, and understood with absolute certainty that the glowing hand had been his mother's, and that somewhere in the world there was a sealed letter bearing her sigil, addressed to a house in his valley, and that Sef had stolen it nine years ago and had never told him what became of it.

"Sef," he said, across the fire. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. "The letter. The courier's letter with the cartographer's seal. Nine years ago."

She looked up. Read his face. And he saw the outlaw captain weigh, in real time, how much truth the mission could bear.

"It's in my saddlebag," she said. "I've kept it because I couldn't open it. It's warded — Ilvane took one look at it in the spring and said it would take her a night with her tools, and she's been avoiding it ever since. She said—" Sef hesitated— "she said some seals are addressed to the blood, and shouldn't be broken by strangers."

The fire cracked. Above the black trees, the copper moon climbed, halfway home.

"Tomorrow," Yara said. "We break it tomorrow. Whatever it says — whoever wrote it — I'm done being a blank space on my own map."

And in the dark beyond the firelight, where the boy lay listening with his eyes open, something stirred — the sigil beneath his collarbone warming like a banked coal, as if the contract itself had heard, and understood, and was afraid.
