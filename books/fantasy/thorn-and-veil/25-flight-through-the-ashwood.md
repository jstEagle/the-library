## 25. Flight Through the Ashwood

Sef had run from better soldiers than these and slept soundly after. She had slipped a king's levy at Ford Anwen with nothing but rain and nerve. But she had never been hunted by something that did not need to eat, or sleep, or stop.

"They're behind us," Bram's ghost would have said, if ghosts could talk, and Sef hated herself for hearing his voice in it anyway. Three days dead and still he was the loudest member of her band.

She crouched on a ridge of blackened stone and looked back the way they'd come. The Ashwood spread below her like the skeleton of a country — ten thousand trees burned to charcoal in some old war nobody sang about anymore, their branches bare and glassy, clicking together in the wind like teeth. The ground was ash to the depth of a man's ankle in the hollows. Every footprint they left was a signature. Every broken twig, a confession.

And there, far back along their trail, moving between the charred trunks without disturbing so much as a fleck of ash: the hounds.

There were six of them that she could count, though counting them felt wrong, the way counting your own heartbeats feels wrong when you're afraid. They were not dogs. Dogs had weight. These moved like ink poured uphill, long and low and jointed in too many places, and where they passed, the ash did not stir. They were reading the trail the way Yara read a map — patiently, thoroughly, with an appetite for what they found.

"Soulscent," Ilvane had called it, back at the river camp, before the attack, before Bram. "The brokers breed them from nothing, which is why they want so badly. A thing made of hunger only knows how to be hungry." She'd said it lightly, the way she said everything, as if the world were a lecture she'd already sat through twice.

Sef spat into the ash. "Then we give them a scent worth choking on."

* * *

The company had shrunk to six. Sef; Yara, who walked like a man carrying a house on his back and refused to put it down; the boy, Fen, silent as ever but for those small impossible words; Ilvane, disgraced court mage, walking backward more often than forward because she insisted on watching the rear; Doss, her best tracker, who had not spoken since they buried Bram under a cairn of stones that wouldn't hold a name; and Wren, sixteen and furious about it, who had taken Bram's axe and hadn't let go of it since, even to sleep.

They moved by day now, which Sef hated. Daylight was for armies and honest men. But the hounds hunted by soul, not sight, and Ilvane had explained — in that maddening classroom voice of hers — that souls showed brightest in darkness. "We are lanterns to them," she said. "At night we burn. In daylight we're only smoke."

"So we travel as smoke," Sef said, and gave the order, and hated giving it, because daylight meant exposure, and exposure meant arrows, and somewhere behind them rode the hollow men — the brokers' enforcers, men whose eyes had gone pale and empty as boiled eggs, who did not charge or shout or do anything so human as hate. They simply came.

Doss found the game trail first, threading up through a fold of hills where the fire had never reached — a green seam stitching through the black. It was narrow enough that they went single file, and Sef took the rear, because that was where captains belonged when the devil was behind you.

It was there, walking last, that she heard it: the click of glassy branches, far off, then closer. Not wind. Wind didn't keep time.

"Off the trail," she hissed. "Now."

They scattered into the rocks above the green seam just as the first hound flowed up the path below them. Up close it was worse than at distance. It had no face, exactly — a smoothness where a face should be, and set into that smoothness, a single aperture like a keyhole, and through the keyhole Sef could see light. Warm light. Lamplight. The color of every hearth she'd ever sat beside.

It was carrying someone's home inside it. That was the trick of them, she understood suddenly. That was the bait. You looked at the hound and you saw everything warm you'd ever lost, and you wanted to go to it, and going to it was dying.

Wren made a sound — a small, involuntary sob — and the hound's keyhole swung toward the rocks like a compass needle finding north.

Ilvane moved faster than Sef had ever seen her move. She snatched a handful of ash, whispered something old and ugly into it, and threw it. The ash hung in the air a moment, glittering, then fell as a fine gray snow over the whole slope — and the world went quiet. Not silent. Quiet, the way a room is quiet when everyone in it has stopped breathing. The hound stood swaying, its keyhole dimming, dimming—

"Soulsmoke," Ilvane breathed. "A mask. It won't hold long. Run."

They ran.

* * *

They ran until dusk, and then they ran past dusk, because stopping felt like drowning, and at full dark they finally collapsed into a hollow beneath a shelf of stone where the fire had never come and the trees still lived — a little island of pine and shadow in the sea of charcoal. Sef set watches and forbade fire, and no one argued, which told her how frightened they all were. Her band argued about everything. Arguing was how they stayed a band.

Yara sat apart, as he always did, with his maps spread across his knees and a stub of charcoal in his fingers, working. Even now. Especially now. Sef had watched him draw since the tollhouse, watched the strange bright truth of it — the way his lines became briefly real, a drawn bridge holding weight for a hundred heartbeats, a drawn wall turning aside an arrow. He was drawing the Ashwood now, she saw. Drawing it fast, and frowning at it.

"You should sleep," she said, settling beside him. Captains checked on their people. That was the whole job, some days.

"I will." He didn't look up. His hand kept moving. "I'm trying to understand how they track us. If I can map it—"

"Map what? Hunger?"

"Everything can be mapped." He said it simply, without pride, the way another man might say water is wet. "That's the whole secret of my trade, Sef. People think maps are about places. They're not. They're about relationships. Where things stand relative to other things. What touches what. What lies between." He tapped the charcoal against the page. "The hounds follow our souls. Fine. Then our souls have a position. And anything with a position can be drawn. And anything I can draw—" He stopped himself, and she watched him swallow whatever the rest of the sentence was, like a man swallowing a coin he wasn't ready to spend.

"And anything you can draw becomes true," Sef finished for him. "For a while."

"For a while," he agreed quietly.

She looked at him a long moment. Here was the thing she'd never say aloud, the thing she'd known since the tollhouse, since the first time she saw his lines turn to living stone: Yara Dunmore was the most dangerous person in her company, and he didn't know it yet, and the day he learned it fully would either save them all or end the world, and she honestly couldn't guess which.

"Get some sleep, cartographer," she said instead. "Dead men draw terrible maps."

He almost smiled. Almost. "You've seen my work tired, then."

"I've seen yours awake. It frightens me enough."

* * *

The hounds found them again two hours before dawn.

No warning. No cry. One moment the hollow was safe and dark, and the next Doss was screaming the alarm from the north watch, and there were three of them coming down the slope like spilled oil, keyholes glowing, and behind them on the ridgeline — torches. Hollow men. A dozen at least, standing in a line, not advancing, just watching, the way a fisherman watches a net close.

"South!" Sef roared. "Everyone south, move, MOVE—"

They broke cover. The pines ended within fifty yards and the ash began, and running through ash was like running through deep water, each stride sinking, each breath a mouthful of gray. Wren stumbled. Sef hauled her up by the collar without breaking stride, and the girl's face was ash-streaked and wild, and she was gripping the axe, always the axe.

Behind them, the hounds weren't slowed by the ash at all. Of course they weren't. They didn't touch the ground. They skimmed it, the way guilt skims a conscience.

"Ilvane!" Sef shouted. "More smoke!"

"I'm empty!" the mage called back, ragged. "I made it from ash and prayer and I am out of both!"

Then Yara was beside her, running with the map case banging against his hip, and his face had the look it got when he forgot to be afraid — the look she was starting to recognize as the most dangerous thing about him.

"The ridge," he gasped. "West ridge. There's a gorge. It's on my survey — dry gorge, forty feet deep, shale walls. Hounds are made of hunger, yes? So feed them something else."

"What in the nine hells does that mean?"

"It means I need ninety seconds and everyone else needs to be downhill when I'm done!"

She trusted him. That was the miracle of the last weeks — she, Sef of the Free Companies, who trusted no one further than knife-range, trusted this soft-handed valley man with her people's lives. She bellowed the order, and they bent their flight westward, toward the black spine of the ridge, and Yara dropped to one knee in the ash with his paper flat on a stone and began to draw.

Sef covered him, axe out, facing back the way they'd come. The hounds came on. The nearest one was thirty yards out, twenty, and its keyhole was wide open now, blazing, and inside it she could see — gods help her — a kitchen. A specific kitchen. Her mother's kitchen, twenty years gone, bread on the board, steam on the window. The hound wore her childhood like a lure wears a hook.

"Fifteen seconds," Yara said through his teeth.

Ten. The hound gathered itself, elongating, joints unfolding like a nightmare opening a letter—

"Now! Downhill, ALL OF YOU—"

They flung themselves down the western slope, tumbling, sliding, and behind them Yara cried out — a short sharp sound, effort or pain — and the world changed.

Sef looked back once, and what she saw she would tell around outlaw fires for the rest of her life. The gorge was there — real, deep, walled in gray shale — but it was *moving*. The walls rippled like the surface of a struck drum. And rising out of it, drawn up out of the paper and the earth alike, came a second pack of hounds: six of them, identical to the hunters, keyholes glowing — but these glowed with a different light. Cold light. Starlight. Empty light.

Yara had drawn a trap baited with nothing. He had mapped hunger a shape to chase, and given it a belly full of void.

The real hounds hit the false ones at the lip of the gorge, and the sound they made was not a sound any animal had ever made — it was the sound of two appetites annihilating each other, a shriek like a nail dragged across the sky. Char-black bodies locked together and tumbled down into the drawn gorge, and the shale walls closed over them like water, and the shrieking cut off as if a door had shut.

Silence. Real silence, this time.

On the southern ridge, the hollow men stood in their torchlit line. For a long moment none of them moved. Then, as one, they turned and walked away into the dark, unhurried, unangered — and somehow that was worse than pursuit. Because they weren't leaving. They were reporting.

"They'll bring more," Ilvane said softly, coming up beside Sef. "Something bigger. The masters don't send hounds twice after a failure."

"Then we don't fail twice." Sef turned and found Yara on his knees in the ash, white-faced, nose bleeding freely, hands shaking so hard he could barely roll up his map. She crouched beside him. "What did it cost you?"

He wiped his nose, looked at the blood on his palm with distant interest. "I don't know yet," he said honestly. "Ask me tomorrow."

"Cartographer." She gripped his shoulder, hard, because tenderness would have embarrassed them both. "That was the single ugliest, cleverest thing I have ever seen a man do with paper. Don't do it again unless I ask."

"Understood," he said, and then, ruinously, with the ghost of a grin: "Unless it's cleverer."

Despite everything — Bram's cairn behind them, the hollow men ahead, the thirteenth moon swelling fat and nearly full in the east — Sef laughed. It came out of her like a bird startled off a corpse, brief and startling, and Wren stared at her as if she'd seen rain fall upward.

"Move," Sef said, standing, wiping her eyes with the back of her wrist. "We make the far edge of the Ashwood by dawn or we die famous."

* * *

They made the far edge by midmorning, half-dead, wholly exhausted, and it was Fen who stopped them.

The boy had been strange all morning — stranger than usual, which Sef hadn't thought possible. He kept drifting to the front of the column, then falling back, then drifting forward again, his head cocked like a bird's, listening to something none of them could hear. When the trees finally thinned and the burned land gave way to open moorland rolling gold toward the mountain's distant shoulder, Fen planted himself in the middle of the trail and would not move.

"Fen." Yara knelt to him. "What is it?"

The boy pointed — not at the mountain, not at the road. At a side path, barely a path at all, winding down into a shallow wooded vale where the mist lay thick as wool. And then he spoke, in that old tongue that raised the hair on Sef's arms, three soft syllables, and looked up at Yara with an expression of such urgent pleading that Sef felt it in her chest like a hooked line.

"He says—" Yara hesitated. Sef watched him wrestle with it. "He says someone is waiting. Someone who knows the mountain's gates." His eyes came up, and they were wide. "Sef — he says the market is here. The veiled market. The one from his dreams."

Ilvane went very still. "That's a story," she said. "A traders' tale. A market that isn't there unless it wants to be."

"Aye," Sef said slowly, looking at the mist-choked vale, at the boy who had led them true through every horror since the river, at the mountain waiting beyond with its thirteenth moon ticking down like a blade being sharpened. "And we're a story too, aren't we? An outlaw, a mapmaker, and a stolen child, walking into the one place on any map with no roads leading in."

She rolled her shoulders, loosened her axe in its loop, and stepped off the trail after Fen, into the mist.

"Keep your hands visible," she said over her shoulder, "your names to yourselves, and your memories in your skulls where they belong. If anyone offers you a bargain — *anyone* — you wake me first."

The mist closed behind them like a door.
