## 27. Fifth Turning

Yara did not sleep.

He sat by the banked fire with Bram's effects spread before him like an autopsy — the whetstone worn to a crescent, the carved wooden bird the man had never explained, the letters from a sister in a town whose name Yara had already forgotten because grief does not hold on to trivia. And beneath all of it, weighted down with a river stone so it would not curl, the map fragment in his own handwriting.

*His own.* That was the part his mind kept circling like a hound that would not lie down. The strokes were his — he knew them the way a man knows his own gait, the slight leftward lean of his north arrow, the way he crossed his *t*s with a single impatient slash. He had drawn this. He had drawn it as a child, before he could have known what it depicted: a stretch of mountain country he had never walked, rendered with a confidence no child should possess, and annotated in a hand too small and too certain with words he did not remember writing.

*The gate has teeth. It eats what you want most.*

He turned the fragment over for the hundredth time. Blank on the back. Of course blank. The paper offered nothing; it only accused.

The fire ticked and settled. Across the camp, Sef stood her watch with her arms folded, a silhouette against the grey ash-trunks of the wood, and beyond her the boy slept — actually slept, deeply, his breath slow and even, his face slack in a way it had never been in all their weeks together. Whatever the dreams had been taking from him at night, they had given something back tonight. Or perhaps he was simply exhausted past dreaming. Children were owed that much, Yara thought. Exhaustion deep enough to drown even grief.

Five moons. Five turnings since the needle spun wild over a mud-caked boy at his door. Seven remained, and somewhere beneath the mountain a ledger waited with the boy's name in it, and the ink on that page was patient in a way no living thing should be.

Yara looked at his hands.

They were ink-stained to the second knuckle, as they had been since he was old enough to hold a pen. They had made forty-one maps of a valley that refused to be mapped, and every one of those maps had been a kind of prayer — *stay, stay, stay* — though he had not known until recently what he was praying against. His mother's voice came back to him sometimes now, surfacing in fragments like drowned things rising: *the veil was for you.* He still did not know if she had said it as comfort or apology. Perhaps there was no difference, for parents.

Bram was dead because of him. Not because of the boy — the brokers would have hunted a soul-contract anywhere, through anyone — but because Yara had been the one who chose the pass, who read the terrain, who said *this way* with the authority of a man who trusted his own eyes. Bram had died in ground Yara had chosen. That was a debt no ledger under any mountain held, but it weighed more than all of them.

And yet.

And yet the boy had said *draw.*

Yara unrolled a fresh sheet of the good paper — Ilvane's paper, bought in the veiled market with coin Sef's people had liberated from a tollhouse, and he tried not to think about how many small thefts were braided into everything now, how far outside the law his life had traveled while his back was turned. He ground ink. He set his compass. And then he stopped, because the old habits rose up in him like reflexes, and he saw them clearly for the first time.

A cartographer begins with boundaries. That was the first law of the craft, older than ink: define the edge, and everything inside becomes knowable, ownable, taxable. Every map he had ever loved was an instrument of possession. *Here is the valley. Here is its border. Here is what lies within your reach.* Kings fought over such lines. Courts collected souls along such lines. The contract mark under the boy's collarbone was itself a kind of map — a territory survey of a self, drawn by someone who considered a child's soul a parcel of land.

What if the first law was the flaw?

Yara set down the compass and picked up the pen bare, without its cage of brass, and began to draw without edges.

It went against every instinct he owned. His hand shook. There was no frame, no neat rectangle declaring *all within these lines is true*, no scale bar, no title cartouche waiting to name and claim. Instead he started at the center — started with the boy, though he drew no figure, only a point, and around the point he wrote not coordinates but debts. *One soul, sold. One debt, maturing. One claim, outstanding.* He wrote the shape of the obligation itself, the way you might draw a river by drawing its current rather than its banks.

The ink behaved strangely.

Not the pale wash of the valley, where pigment died on the page. Not the eager magic of the outer world either, where his maps became briefly true terrain and Sef's outlaws had learned to look at him like a loaded weapon. This was something else. The lines he drew seemed to *listen*. When he wrote *sold*, the word darkened, as if pressed deeper into the paper than mere ink could go. When he wrote *outstanding*, the letters seemed to lean forward, hungry.

"You're doing it again," Sef said quietly. She had drifted over from her watch without him hearing, which frightened him more than he let show. "That thing where the paper starts breathing."

"Maps are arguments," Yara said. His voice came out rougher than intended. "I've always thought that. A map says: the world is thus. I just never asked who the argument was *for*."

"And now?"

"Now I'm asking what happens when the world argues back." He turned the sheet toward her. In the firelight the central point seemed almost to pulse. "This is the boy's contract. Not a copy — the *shape* of it. What was taken, what's owed, when it comes due."

Sef studied it the way she studied terrain before a raid, eyes moving fast, cataloguing exits. "You can see the terms?"

"I can see the structure. Not the words. The words are under the mountain, in the ledger." He hesitated, then said the thing that had been forming in him all night, the thing that felt less like an idea than a vertigo. "But Sef — a contract is a document. A document is a text. And a text—"

"—is a map," she finished. Her jaw tightened. She understood faster than he'd expected, or perhaps she'd understood for days and had been waiting for him to catch up. "You're saying you could redraw it."

"I'm saying I don't know yet. But Bram carried a piece of my own childhood handwriting across half the world to put it in my hands, and I refuse to believe that was only grief playing tricks." He looked toward the sleeping boy. "Someone wanted me to see this. Someone believed I could do more than look."

Sef crouched beside him, close enough that he could see the new scar the hollow men had given her along her forearm, still angry-red. "Ilvane will tell you it can't be done," she said. "She'll say the court's contracts are woven into the foundation of things, older than kingdoms, that even the Under-Crown doesn't rewrite them, only collects on them."

"She may be right."

"She usually is. That's what makes her unbearable." Sef was quiet a moment. The fire popped. Somewhere off in the black trees a nightbird called once and fell silent, as if ashamed of the noise. "When we buried Bram," she said, "I told the band the truth. That following you might get us all killed, and that I'd keep going anyway. Dalla called me a fool. Corren didn't say anything, which from Corren is a eulogy." She looked at the strange breathing map. "I keep going because in thirty years of thieving I've watched courts sell justice a hundred times and never once buy it back. If your ink can tear up a contract — any contract — then it's worth Bram's life. It'd be worth ten of mine."

The weight of that settled onto Yara's shoulders like wet wool. He thought of telling her not to spend herself so freely on a maybe. He thought of the tollhouse records, the seller's name from his own valley, the betrayal still coiled and waiting in that fact like a snake in tall grass. He thought of his mother's glowing hands, glimpsed in a river's memory, holding an infant who did not belong anywhere — and of the Under-Crown's patience, seven turns deep and counting.

Instead he said, "Then help me test it."

* * *

They worked until the sky went from black to bruise.

Yara drew, and Sef challenged. That was the method they fell into, and it was a better method than any he'd learned in forty years alone with his instruments, because Sef attacked every line like it had wronged her. When he wrote *the debt matures on the thirteenth turning*, she said, "Prove it. Where's the moon in your map? A date isn't a place." So he drew the moon's arc, thirteen stations of it, and found that when he reached the thirteenth the ink refused to hold — bled outward, blurred, as if the moment itself declined to be fixed on paper. *The future resists cartography,* he noted in the margin, and then stared at that note for a long time, because it meant the contract's power lived in the future, in the *not-yet*, and the not-yet was the one country no one had ever successfully mapped.

Which meant it was unmapped territory.

Which meant, by the oldest law of his craft — the true one, the one underneath all the others — it belonged to whoever drew it first.

His heart was hammering when he understood that. He made himself breathe. He made himself write it plainly, in his own steady hand, so it would stop feeling like blasphemy: *A contract is a map of a soul's future. The court holds the original. But originality is not sovereignty. Whoever surveys the territory first may name it.*

"The boy's soul," he said aloud, testing the words, "is a country. Sold to a crown that never surveyed it properly — that bought it sight unseen, from a broker, on paper. And a sale without survey—" He laughed, short and disbelieving. "In every kingdom I've ever mapped, a sale without survey is voidable. It's the first principle of land law. You cannot sell what you have not seen."

Sef was staring at him. "You're talking about unmaking it. In a courtroom."

"I'm talking about unmaking it with a pen." He looked down at his hands again, and this time they did not feel like a dead man's tools. They felt like what they had always been, though he'd spent a lifetime pretending otherwise: instruments. "The court will demand I argue by its rules. Its ledgers, its clauses, its equal claims. Fine. Let it. Because its rules have a hole in them wide enough to drive a river through, and the hole is this: the Under-Crown thinks in documents, and I think in *terrain*. It has never once left its mountain. It has never walked the ground it owns. It holds ten thousand souls and couldn't draw a single one of them from memory."

The camp was stirring around them now. Dalla feeding the fire, Corren checking blades with his ritual patience, Ilvane emerging from her tent with her silver-grey hair loose and her eyes already narrowed at the smell of working ink — she always woke suspicious, as if the day were trying to sell her something.

"What," she said, "have you two built in the dark?"

Yara turned the sheet toward her. To her credit, she did not scoff. She knelt, read it twice, and went very still in the way he had learned meant her mind was running ahead of her mouth.

"This is the contract's architecture," she said at last. "Where did you learn its geometry? Even I've only seen fragments, and I served the court for twenty years."

"Bram's map showed me the gate. The rest I inferred." He met her eyes. "Tell me why it won't work. You'll do it anyway, so do it now, while I can still change the design."

Ilvane was quiet long enough that Dalla and Corren drifted closer, pretending to tend the fire. Then she said, "Three reasons. First: the court reads its contracts aloud before enforcing them, and the reading binds. If the debt is spoken in full before your counter-map exists in more than draft, the ink sets forever. Second: the gate admits only those who name their true desire, and it takes honesty as toll — you'll arrive already spent, already poorer than you know. Third." She paused, and something almost gentle moved through her severity. "Third: a counter-claim requires a claimant with standing. The court will ask by what right you speak for the boy's soul, and *love* is not standing, cartographer. Lawful courts do not recognize it. You'll need a claim the ledger itself must honor, and I do not know what that looks like. Neither do you."

"No," Yara agreed. "Not yet."

"But?"

"But I've got seven turns, a pen that makes things true, and a boy who told me to draw." He rolled the sheet carefully, tied it with cord, and slung it inside his coat against his chest, where it lay warm as a second heartbeat. "I've spent my whole life mapping places that refused to be known. One more impossible territory won't break the habit."

Across the fire, the boy had woken. No one had noticed him sitting up — he moved like that, quiet as weather — and now he looked at Yara with those old, old eyes, and touched his own collarbone, and then pointed at the rolled map against Yara's chest.

"For me?" he signed, in the small precise language of his hands that Yara had grown fluent in without noticing when it happened.

"For you," Yara said. "Every line."

The boy considered this with grave seriousness. Then he raised one hand and, slowly, deliberately, made the sign Yara had taught him weeks ago and never once used back — the one that meant *together*, palm to palm, fingers laced.

Sef cleared her throat and became very interested in the fire. Corren studied his blade. Ilvane smiled the way a glacier might smile, which is to say barely, and only at the edges.

Yara laced his fingers through the empty air across the flames, completing the sign, and felt something settle in him that no map had ever given him: not certainty, nothing so clean as that, but direction. North. Forward. Down, eventually, into the dark under the mountain, with a pen in his pocket and a war in his margins.

"We move within the hour," Sef said, rising, her voice snapping the camp into motion. "Ilvane — how far to the gate?"

"Two days, if the pass holds. Three if the mountain disagrees with us." Ilvane pulled her hood up against the morning cold. "And it will disagree with us. The gate has teeth, remember. It eats what you want most."

Yara's hand went involuntarily to the map fragment in his pocket — his own childish handwriting, the warning he'd written before he could possibly have known. *The gate has teeth. It eats what you want most.*

He looked at the boy. At Sef. At the ragged, loyal, unlawful family gathered around a dying fire in a burned wood, five moons gone and seven remaining, and understood with cold clarity exactly what he wanted most in all the world.

He only hoped the gate would settle for honesty instead.
