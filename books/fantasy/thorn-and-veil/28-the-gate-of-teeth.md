## 28. The Gate of Teeth

I had walked to the mountain three times in my life before this, and each time I had turned back at the tree line. Not from cowardice — though I will not pretend cowardice played no part — but because the gate asks its question, and I had not yet been honest enough to answer it.

Now I had no choice left but honesty. It is a strange thing, to be driven toward the one thing you have spent thirty years avoiding. The court exiled me for refusing to weigh souls; the road has spent five moons teaching me that refusal was only the first of many truths I owed.

We came out of the Ashwood at dawn, or what passes for dawn beneath that permanent bruise of cloud the brokers' fires have painted across the sky. The ash had thinned over the last day, giving way to grey scree and then to a slope of black stone so smooth it looked poured rather than quarried. And there it stood.

The Gate of Teeth.

The maps call it something else — the Undermount Approach, the Court's Threshold, names written by cartographers who never came close enough to feel it. But everyone who has stood where I stood calls it what it looks like: a mouth. A cavernous arch of jagged stone, each tooth taller than a man, set into the mountainside like the jaw of something that fell asleep mid-meal and never woke. Between the teeth, darkness. Not shadow — darkness, the kind that eats lantern light and gives nothing back.

Yara stopped walking when he saw it. I watched him do the thing he always does when confronted with something new: his hand went to his satchel, fingers finding the worn spine of his sketchbook, and his eyes began measuring. Height of the teeth. Width of the gap. Angle of the slope. He could not help himself. The world only became real to him once it was committed to paper.

"Don't draw it," I said quietly.

He glanced at me. "Why not?"

"Because the gate will know. It reads intention the way your maps read terrain." I nodded toward the boy, who stood slightly ahead of us all, as he had since the Ashwood, chin lifted toward the mountain like a man greeting an old enemy. "And because some things should be seen before they are owned."

Sef came up on my left, arms crossed, her scarred jaw working. Behind her, what remained of her band — six now, where there had been twelve at the tollhouse — fanned out in a loose crescent, hands near weapons out of habit even though we all knew weapons would mean nothing here. Bram's absence walked with us like a seventh companion. No one had said his name aloud in two days, but everyone kept glancing at the space where he should have been.

"That's it?" Sef asked. She already knew it was. She wanted someone to say otherwise.

"That's it."

"It looks hungry."

"It is," I said. "But not the way you think. Come. I'll tell you what to expect, and then it will do no good whatsoever, because the gate does not care what you expect. Only what you want."

* * *

I made them stop a hundred paces out, close enough to hear it — and there is a sound, a low resonance like a held breath, like the moment before a confession — and I told them everything I remembered from my years in the court above, before my fall, when I had been permitted to study the old texts.

"The gate predates the court," I said. "That matters. The Under-Crown did not build it; the court found it, the way you find a spring or a fault line, and built around it. The texts call it the First Honest Thing. Before there were ledgers, before there were contracts, before anyone thought to put a price on a soul, there was this — a door that would only open for truth."

"And if you lie?" one of the outlaws asked. Dalla, the young one, who had not slept properly since Bram died.

"You cannot lie to it. That is the trap people don't understand. You can refuse to answer — the gate will simply wait, and you will grow old waiting, or turn back. But if you speak, you speak true. Every word. The gate strips pretense the way water strips paint." I looked at each of them in turn. "It will ask you what you desire. Your true desire. Not the noble version. Not the version you'd tell at a fire with friends. The raw thing underneath. And once you name it, you cannot unname it. Everyone who hears it will know it. And more importantly—" I hesitated, because this was the part I had turned back from, three times, thirty years ago. "More importantly, *you* will know it. Some people go their whole lives without admitting what they want. The gate takes that shelter from you. Forever."

Silence, except for the mountain's held breath.

"And the cost?" Yara asked. His voice was careful, the way it always was when he suspected the ground under him was thinner than it looked. "You said each answer costs honesty. What does that mean?"

"It means honesty *is* the cost. There is no tithe, no blood, no coin. The gate takes nothing from your pockets. It takes the lie you've been living inside. For most people, that's worse than blood." I turned fully to him, because he deserved to hear this plainly, from me, before the stones heard it first. "For you, I think, it may be worst of all. You've built your whole life on a map that leaves out the middle of itself. The valley. Your birth. The reason magic runs off you like rain off oiled cloth. The gate does not care about the veil your parents wove. It will ask what you want, and somewhere underneath all your careful lines and margins, the true answer is going to come up like a body from a lake."

His face went very still. I had learned to read him over these months — the cartographer's stillness, the surveyor's pause before committing ink to paper — and I knew that look. He was triangulating. Taking bearings on himself.

"And yours?" he asked. "What did you turn back from, thirty years ago?"

I almost smiled. He was the only one of them who had noticed that my warnings were confessions wearing a coat.

"I'll show you," I said. "Watch me answer first. Then you'll know it can be survived."

* * *

We crossed the last hundred paces in single file, boots silent on stone that swallowed sound. Up close, the teeth were worse — veined with something pale and slow-moving, like light trapped under ice, and warm. The air between them breathed against our faces, in and out, patient as tide.

I walked to the threshold alone.

The darkness between the teeth stirred. I felt it attend to me — there is no other word — felt the gate's attention settle on me like a hand on the back of the neck. And then the question came. Not in sound. It arrived the way memory arrives, whole and undeniable, as if it had always been inside me and the gate had merely knocked it loose:

*What do you desire?*

Thirty years of prepared answers rose in me and dissolved. I desire justice. Lie. I desire to atone. Lie. I desire to undo what I did. Closer, but still a coat over the thing beneath.

I closed my eyes. I let the lake give up its dead.

"I want to matter again," I said, and my voice came out cracked and small in that vast stone throat. "Not good. Not forgiven. I weighed souls for eleven years and told myself I was softening the machine, and I wasn't, I was oiling it. I want to stand somewhere — anywhere — and have my choices count again. I want to be a person whose presence changes the weight of a room. I traded that away a coin at a time, and I want it back, and I would burn the whole court to get it, and part of me — the honest part, the part this gate sees — *enjoyed* the weighing. I liked knowing exactly what things were worth. That's what I desire. To matter, and to stop pretending I didn't love the arithmetic."

The silence after was total. Behind me, I heard Sef exhale slowly through her nose. I heard Yara say nothing at all, which was louder.

The darkness considered. Then, like a jaw relaxing, the space between the two nearest teeth widened — just enough. A passage. An invitation.

I walked through, and the dark closed behind me, and I stood inside the gate's antechamber — a hollow the size of a chapel, lit by that same pale vein-light — and waited for the others, listening to them cross the threshold one by one.

* * *

They came in this order, and I will record it because the order mattered.

Dalla went second. She was shaking before she reached the threshold, and her answer came out in a flood, half-swallowed: she wanted to go home, she wanted her mother's kitchen, she wanted to have never followed Sef out of that burning village, and she hated herself for wanting safety more than she had ever hated the brokers. The gate opened for her. She came through weeping silently and would not meet anyone's eyes, and I understood that too. The gate had taken her shelter, and now we all lived in the house her truth had built.

Then the twins, Corrin and Vess, who answered together — the gate allowed it, reading their desires as braided, one rope — they wanted each other to survive, whichever of them didn't. The dark held them a long moment, testing the seam of that shared wish, and then opened. They came through holding hands like children.

Old Hollis, who had barely spoken since Bram fell. His answer was quiet and terrible: he wanted to die well, in front of witnesses, doing something that counted, because he had watched his whole generation die stupidly, drunk, in ditches, unmourned. The gate opened almost gratefully, as if it had been waiting years for an answer with no coat on it at all.

Then Sef.

She stood at the threshold longer than any of us. I watched her shoulders square, watched her do the thing she does — take the blow on purpose, choose the harder path because the easier one insults her — and then she said, clear and carrying, in a voice pitched deliberately so every one of us would hear:

"I want them to need me. Not follow me — *need* me. I want to be the reason they're alive, because if I'm the reason they're alive then losing me costs something, and I am so tired of being the kind of person who can be lost cheaply." A breath. "And I want Yara to succeed, and I hate that it's not first."

The gate opened. She walked through with her chin high and her eyes wet and dared any of us to comment. None of us did. But I saw Yara look at her — really look, the way he looks at terrain he intends to render faithfully — and I saw her see him looking, and neither of them flinched, and something passed between them that had no name yet but had, at least, stopped pretending to be nothing.

Then the boy.

He went to the threshold without hesitation, without fear, and I realized with a lurch that he had been waiting for this — that perhaps he had known about the gate before any of us, that the old tongue he spoke might be older than the gate itself, that this door might recognize in him something like kinship. The darkness gathered around him tenderly, the way frost gathers on a window.

*What do you desire?*

He could not speak it. His voice had been sold; that was the whole wound we carried. But the gate did not require speech. I felt his answer arrive in the chamber behind us like a change in pressure, like weather:

*I want to be a person again. I want my name back. I want to remember my mother's voice, and I want the man who draws maps to keep being my family whether the debt is paid or not.*

Every one of us heard it. Yara made a sound I have never heard a grown man make — small, involuntary, like a rib cracking inward — and pressed the back of his hand to his mouth.

The gate opened wide for the boy. Wide as grief. Wide as welcome.

Which left Yara.

* * *

He stood before the threshold for a long time. The vein-light pulsed slow around the teeth, and the mountain breathed, and the rest of us waited in the antechamber, watching his back the way you watch a diver from a cliff.

"He doesn't have to rush," Sef murmured beside me.

"He's not rushing. He's drafting." I shook my head. "He's trying to find the phrasing that tells the truth without telling *everything*. And it won't work. The gate doesn't grade answers, Captain. It weighs them."

"Like the court weighs souls?" There was an edge in her voice. She had never quite forgiven me my eleven years, and she was right not to.

"No," I said. "The opposite. The court weighs what you're worth to it. The gate weighs what you're worth to yourself. It's crueler and kinder at once. Watch."

At the threshold, Yara finally spoke. Even across the distance I caught the words, because the gate carried them to all of us — that is part of its cruelty, or its mercy; the truths are communal, shared like bread.

"I want to belong somewhere," he said. "That's all. That's the whole of it. I've mapped every place I've ever been because mapping is how you claim a thing without asking permission, and I never asked permission for anything because I was afraid of being refused. I want a door that opens when I knock. I want to be from somewhere." A pause, long enough that the vein-light dimmed and brightened twice. "And I'm afraid the answer is the valley. I'm afraid I'll trade anything — the boy, the mission, all of you — to get back behind the veil and be the man I was before I knew what I was."

The darkness went utterly still.

Then it did something I had never seen it do, in all the texts, in all the accounts. It laughed. Not in sound — in pressure, a great slow pulse of amusement rolling through the stone, rattling grit from the teeth overhead. And the question came again, gentler this time, almost kind:

*Is that true? Is the valley what you desire?*

And Yara Dunmore, standing in the mouth of the First Honest Thing, with his sold boy behind him and his outlaw companions watching and his own origin waiting for him under the mountain like a knife in a drawer, was silent for ten full seconds.

Then: "No. No, it isn't. I want to belong — but not *there*. Not behind a wall built to hide me. I want to belong *here*. In the world. With them. With him." I could not see his face, only his shoulders, and his shoulders were shaking. "I want to stop being a map of myself and start being the territory. That's what I desire. God help me, that's what I desire."

The gate opened for him wider than it had opened for any of us — wide enough to drive a cart through, wide enough that the vein-light flared along every tooth like a grin — and Yara walked through into the antechamber looking hollowed-out and scoured and, for the first time since I had met him, entirely present. A man with no coat left to remove.

Sef clapped him once on the shoulder, hard, which from her was an embrace. The boy took his hand and did not let go.

I looked past them all, through the far side of the antechamber, where the vein-light funneled down into a descending stair cut into the living rock — a stair that spiraled down and down into the dark, toward the cathedral of ledgers, toward the hanging lanterns that had once been souls, toward the Under-Crown and its arithmetic.

The gate had taken our lies. All of them. We stood together in the truth like swimmers in cold water, gasping, alive.

"There it is," I said quietly, nodding at the stair. "The way down. From here, the gate protects no one. Everything below answers to the court."

Yara stepped toward the stair first. Of course he did. He had drawn the shape of this descent in a hundred margin-sketches over five moons; now the territory finally matched the map, and the mapper went to meet it.

"One thing," Sef said, catching his arm. Her voice was low, meant for the three of us — him, the boy, me. "Whatever's down there offers you something. It offered Bram's contact something, once. It knows exactly what to put on the table." Her eyes flicked to me, briefly, and I knew she was thinking of my eleven years, of how easily the court had once bought me with the currency of mattering. "Don't drink."

"I won't," Yara said.

And the terrible thing — the thing I carried down the first hundred steps with me, turning it over like a stone with a worm under it — was that he believed it. He truly believed it.

The stair swallowed us one by one, and far below, faint as a heartbeat, we began to hear the sound of pages turning.
