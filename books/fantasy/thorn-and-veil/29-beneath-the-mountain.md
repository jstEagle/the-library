## 29. Beneath the Mountain

The gate did not open so much as admit us, the way a throat admits a swallowed thing.

One moment we stood in the Gate of Teeth's shadow, the black basalt maw still humming with the truths we had paid to pass it — Ilvane's confession that she had loved the court once and hated it longer; Sef's flat declaration that she wanted her dead back alive and knew it was impossible; my own answer, which I would not repeat aloud even now, even to myself. The next moment the stone had folded around us like a page turning, and there was no gate behind us anymore. Only descent.

Stairs. Of course stairs. A court built on debt would make you pay for every step.

They spiraled down into the mountain's heart, cut from rock so dark it seemed to drink the light of our lanterns rather than reflect it. The boy — Tam, though he had not yet earned that name back; I only gave it to him because *boy* felt like an insult to someone who had suffered this much — walked close against my side, his small hand fisted in the hem of my coat. He had not spoken since the gate. None of us had. There is a silence that comes after paying honesty's price, and we carried ours down into the dark like stones in our pockets.

"Count the turns," Ilvane murmured behind me. She had been counting since the gate, lips moving, keeping our bearings the way she kept everything: precisely, and with visible resentment at having to do it. "Seven hundred steps to the antechamber, if the old maps hold."

"The old maps," Sef said, "were drawn by people who came back?"

"People who were *released*," Ilvane corrected. "Which is worse. Nothing leaves this place by accident."

I touched the satchel at my hip where my rolled maps slept — the true ones, the ones that became terrain when I drew them. Six blank sheets remained. Six chances to remake the world, if my hand held steady enough and the ink lasted. In the valley, ink ran pale at the boundary. Here, I suspected, ink would run toward whatever the court wanted it to run toward. I had brought three bottles. I resolved not to think about which of them might already be compromised.

The air changed first. Before we saw anything, before the stairs ended, the air grew thick with a sound I could not at first name — a vast, layered whispering, like wind through wheat, like ten thousand pages turning at once, like breathing that was almost but not quite synchronized. Tam stopped on the stair. His grip on my coat went white-knuckled.

"What is that?" Sef whispered.

Ilvane did not answer immediately, and her silence told me more than any answer could have. She had been here before. She had stood on these stairs and heard this sound and gone down anyway, all those years ago, when she was young and certain that weighing souls was a science and not a sin.

"It's them," she said finally. "The souls. They're not stored, Captain. They're *kept*. And kept things make noise."

* * *

The stairs opened without warning into space.

I have spent my life drawing places I have never seen, and I thought I understood scale. I had drawn the Ashwood as a smear of charcoal two fingers wide and known it for a hundred miles of burned and hungry ground. I had charted the River That Remembers from rumor and made its bends believable. But nothing in my craft had prepared me for the cathedral.

It was a cavern, yes — but *cavern* is a word for holes, and this was architecture. The ceiling arched up beyond the reach of our light, ribbed with veins of pale mineral that glowed with a cold, patient luminescence, like moonlight that had been bottled and forgotten. Pillars rose from the floor in ranks, each one carved — no, I realized as we descended the final flight, each one *bound* — with chains of ledger-paper, thousands upon thousands of pages wrapped around the stone like ivy grown over a corpse. The whispering came from everywhere and nowhere. From the paper. From the pillars. From the dark above.

And hanging in that dark, suspended from heights we could not measure, were the lanterns.

That is what they looked like at first. That is what I let myself believe they looked like, for the length of perhaps ten heartbeats, because the alternative was to fall to my knees on the last step and refuse to go further. Soft-glowing shapes, hundreds of them, thousands, drifting at every level of the vast dark like a sky turned inward — warm gold, cool silver, some flickering, some steady, some so dim they were barely embers of themselves.

Then one of them drifted closer, following some current of air I could not feel, and I saw what hung inside the glow.

A face.

Small. Closed-eyed. Peaceful, in the way that the drowned are peaceful. A child's face, or the shape memory makes of a child's face, suspended inside a cage of light and script — contract-language, the same script that was branded under Tam's collarbone, circling the soul like a birdcage circles a bird. As I watched, the little face turned, dreaming, toward the sound of our footsteps. Its mouth moved. No sound reached us. But the whispering of the cathedral swelled, just slightly, just there, and I understood with a horror that went through me like cold water through cloth:

They were talking. All of them. All the time. Praying, or pleading, or simply saying their own names over and over so they would not forget them. And the cathedral heard every word, and kept every word, and filed every word away in the ledgers bound around the pillars, and none of it changed anything at all.

"Oh," said Sef, very quietly. She had seen a great many terrible things in her years outside the law — she had told us so around a hundred fires — and I had never once heard her voice do what it did just then. "Oh, that's — that's not justice. That's not even theft. Theft ends."

"The brokers call it *custody*," Ilvane said. Her face was gray in the lantern-light. "The court calls it *preservation*. A soul in the open world decays, they say. Grieves. Changes. Here it is kept exactly as it was sold. Forever." She paused. "I weighed four hundred of them before I understood what I was weighing. Four hundred, and I told myself each one was a number."

Tam made a sound.

It was the smallest sound he had ever made — smaller than his weeping in dreams, smaller than the breath before his first word, *home*, all those turnings ago. It was the sound of someone recognizing their own future. His hand left my coat and pressed flat against the brand beneath his collarbone, through the cloth, and his eyes were fixed upward on the drifting lights with an expression I will draw someday, if I live to draw again, and it will be the truest map I ever make.

"No," I said. I crouched to his level, took his shoulders, put my face where his eyes could find nothing else. "No. Look at me. We are not leaving you here. Whatever happens below — whatever they offer, whatever they show us — you walk out of this mountain with your soul in your chest. Do you hear me? That is the whole of the plan. Everything else is decoration."

His eyes searched mine. Then, slowly, he raised one hand and tapped my chest — right over the heart, twice, the way he'd done since the road, his private grammar for *I believe you* — and then pointed up, at the lanterns, and made the smallest gesture with his fingers: *them too.*

This child, who had lost his voice and his name and nearly everything else, standing at the mouth of hell, worried about the others.

"Yes," I said, and my voice cracked on it. "Them too. If we can. Them too."

* * *

We went down into the cathedral proper along a causeway of black stone that crossed the open dark, and I made the mistake, halfway across, of looking down.

Below the causeway, far below, the floor of the cavern was not floor. It was shelves. Ranks and ranks of shelving carved into living rock, and on the shelves lay books — no. Not books. Ledgers, each one tall as a man, spined in iron, and from each spine hung a thin chain of light that rose through the dark to one of the drifting lanterns above. Every soul had its book. Every book was open. Even at this distance I could see the pages moving, turning themselves, recording — what? Dreams? Memories leaking through the contract-script like water through a cracked jar? The whole history of a stolen life, annotated in real time by the thing that had stolen it?

And between the shelves moved figures. Tall, gray-robed, faceless in the gloom — clerks, I supposed, or something the court used instead of clerks. They walked the aisles with the unhurried gait of people who have done one task for centuries, pausing here and there to touch a ledger, adjust a chain, trim a wick of light. Once, one of them paused directly beneath us, tilted its hooded head as if listening to something in a page, and made a small notation with a stylus that gleamed wetly, like bone.

"They can't see us," Ilvane said, but her hand was tight on her staff, and I noticed she stayed to the center of the causeway. "Not yet. We're unregistered. Uncontracted." A bitter breath of a laugh. "In this place, being nobody is the only protection there is."

"Then let's stay nobody until we find the reading room," Sef said. She had her knives out, which I suspected was less strategy than comfort. "Where does the Under-Crown keep the actual contracts? The originals. The boy's original has to be here somewhere, and if Yara's going to pull his cartographer's tricks, he'll want to see the ground he's redrawing."

"I know where it is," Ilvane said. "That's the problem. I know where it is because I worked there. The Hall of Instruments. Central aisle, deepest shelf, under seal of the Under-Crown's own signet." She looked at me, and in the cold light her eyes were very old. "Yara. Before we go another step. You should understand what you're about to attempt. The contracts aren't paper down here. They're law given weight. To alter one isn't forgery. It's heresy against the oldest legal structure in the world. The court doesn't punish it. The court *can't comprehend* it. And things that cannot be comprehended get erased."

"Good," I said.

She blinked. Sef grinned, sharp as her knives.

"You want me to explain—"

"No need. I've spent forty years mapping a valley that refuses to be mapped. I have some experience with systems that erase what they can't explain." I looked out over the cathedral — the drifting lights, the whispering dark, the endless patient shelves — and felt something settle in me that I recognized from the boundary of my valley, all those months ago: the moment when fear stops being a wall and becomes a compass. "Every map is an argument. Whoever draws the territory owns the truth of it. This court has been drawing the same map for a thousand years — soul as property, debt as destiny, everything owned, everything filed. Well." I patted the satchel at my hip. "I'm holding six blank sheets and a bottle of ink, and I intend to file a counterclaim."

The whispering swelled.

I felt it before I heard it — a change in the pressure of the air, the way a valley changes before a storm. All around us, high in the dark, the lanterns had begun to drift. Slowly. Purposefully. Hundreds of those soft-glowing cages turning like compass needles until every dreaming face in the cathedral pointed down at the causeway, at us, at the small boy standing between a thief and a traitor and a man with too much ink.

And the whispering resolved, for one terrible instant, into words. Thousands of voices, one word, breathed from every direction at once:

*Mine. Mine. Mine. Mine.*

Not a threat. Worse. A lesson. The cathedral teaching us its grammar, showing us the only sentence it knew.

Then the lights drifted apart, the whisper dissolved back into its vast rustling sea, and from the far end of the causeway — from a doorway I would have sworn had not existed a moment before — came footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. Accompanied by the soft, continuous sound of pages turning.

A figure emerged onto the causeway: robed in white so fine it seemed woven from the ledger-pages themselves, its face hidden behind a mask of polished iron shaped like an open book. It stopped twenty paces from us. When it spoke, its voice was gentle, courteous, and utterly without warmth, the voice of a toll-keeper greeting a traveler who has no choice but to pay.

"Unregistered guests," it said. "How unusual. The Under-Crown bids me tell you that your arrival has been anticipated for—" a pause, delicate as a turned page, "—some time. Since before the boy was born, in fact. Since before the valley was walled." The iron mask tilted toward me, and though it had no eyes, I felt seen in a way that made my skin crawl. "Cartographer. Your parents' account is still open. It will be a pleasure, at last, to discuss it with the family."

Behind me, Tam's small hand found mine and gripped it hard.

The masked figure turned, robes whispering, and began to walk — and the way ahead of it lit, step by step, deeper into the dark, deeper than the shelves, deeper than the lanterns, down toward whatever throne sat at the bottom of a cathedral built entirely of other people's names.

"The Under-Crown," it said over its shoulder, "will see you now."
