## 31. Sixth Turning

The moon was a coin bitten nearly through.

Sef watched it from the ridge above the Ashwood, where the burned trees stood like the ribs of some enormous animal that had died on its feet. Six turns gone. Seven nights left. Below her, in the black fold of the valley, forty-one fires burned in a pattern that would have looked random to anyone who hadn't spent twenty years reading campsites the way priests read scripture. It wasn't a camp. It was a promise, laid out in embers: *we are still here, and we mean to be heard.*

"You're doing the thing again," Wren said beside her.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stare at the moon like it owes you money."

"It does owe me money." Sef didn't smile. She hadn't smiled properly since Bram, and everyone in the band had stopped waiting for one, which was its own kind of grief. "Everything up there owes all of us money. That's rather the point of tonight."

Below, the band was moving. Not drilling — drilling made noise, and noise made witnesses — but moving with the loose, overlapping purpose of people who had done this before. Vetch was walking the fire-line with a horn lantern, checking the pitch-soaked faggots they'd spent three days hauling up from the tar-flats. The twins were fitting arrowheads to shafts by feel, their faces lit green by foxfire lamps so no honest light would betray them to watchers on the high passes. And at the center of it all, spread across four flat stones, lay the map.

Yara's map. The one he'd drawn in the tollhouse at Second Turning and refined every night since, working by candle stub while the rest of them slept — or didn't sleep — around him. The Court Beneath the Mountain rendered in his precise, patient hand: the switchback roads, the gate of teeth, the cathedral of ledgers, and, marked in red ink that seemed faintly warm to the touch, the great sealed doors of the Under-Crown's hall.

It was a good map. It was also, Sef had come to understand, more than a map. When Yara drew a thing here, in this world where magic ran thick as sap, the world leaned toward his lines like a plant toward sun. The map did not describe the mountain. The mountain was learning to agree with it.

That was why they were still here. That was why forty-one fires burned in a pattern instead of scattering into the dark like sensible criminals.

"Report," Sef said, as Vetch crested the ridge.

"Tar-loads are set along the western scree, all six stations. Twins have eyes on the gate road — nothing moving but wind. Ilvane sent word down an hour ago." He paused, and his jaw worked. "She says the descent went clean. They're inside."

Inside. The word landed in Sef's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She waited for the splash and never heard it.

"And the favor," she said. "She named it yet?"

"No. Still owed." Vetch shifted. "Cap. You've read her letter twice now. Want to say what's in it, or shall we all keep pretending you're not chewing glass?"

Sef looked at him. Twenty years they'd run together, through tollhouse raids and broker hunts and one catastrophic winter in the Salt Fens that had taught them both to eat things they refused to name afterward. If anyone had earned the truth, it was Vetch.

"Ilvane says the diversion will work," she said quietly. "And she says it won't matter unless someone holds the gate open from the outside when they come back up. The Gate of Teeth admits those who name their true desire. It doesn't necessarily let them leave." She turned the folded letter over in her fingers. "She's holding it from within. Her unnamed favor, spent on a door. But doors need two hands, Vetch. Someone has to be here when the mountain tries to bite shut."

"Then we hold it," Vetch said simply. "All of us. That's the plan. Was always the plan."

"The plan," Sef said, "was that when I gave the order to scatter, the band scatters. Every soul for the hills, rendezvous at the Fenmark in spring if any of us live. That was the plan we agreed to at Fourth Turning, when half the company wanted to walk away and I let them think I'd let them." She folded the letter closed. "The plan is void."

"Because?"

"Because I'm not giving the order."

* * *

They gathered at the central fire an hour before midnight, forty-one of them — no. Thirty-nine. Sef counted twice and made herself say the number honestly, even inside her own skull. Thirty-nine, because Bram was ash under a cairn in the Hollow Pass, and because Dalla had taken eleven others east at Fifth Turning when the argument finally came, the one Sef had known was coming since the day they'd seen the contract mark on a mute child's collarbone.

Dalla had stood in front of her with the fire between them and said, *This isn't our war, Sef. Souls and courts and kings' old debts — we steal from brokers because brokers are soft targets, not because we're saving the world. Walk away while walking's cheap.*

And Sef had said, *You're right. It isn't our war.* And then, because honesty was the only currency she'd ever respected: *But it's our door. We found the boy. We fed him. Bram died on that pass with a broker's iron in him, and if we run now, Bram ran for nothing.*

Eleven had gone with Dalla. Sef had watched them out of sight without hatred, because fear was not treason, and because a band that punished leaving became the very thing they hunted. But she had counted thirty-nine ever after, and the number sat in her chest like a splinter worked too deep to draw.

Now she stood before the fire with the map weighted down at her boots and thirty-eight faces looking up at her, and she did the thing she hated most in the world, which was talk.

"Seven nights," she said. "In seven nights, if Yara Dunmore can do what he believes he can do, a court that has bought and sold souls for longer than there have been kingdoms stops existing. Not falls — stops. Unmakes itself, the way a bad map gets unmade. You've seen what his drawings do. Imagine one drawn over the whole rotten ledger of the place."

A murmur. Old Pell, gray-braided and missing two fingers to a broker's hound, spat into the fire and said what they were all thinking: "And if he can't?"

"Then the thirteenth moon rises whole, the debt comes due, and somewhere beneath us a child becomes a lantern hanging in a cathedral full of lanterns. And the brokers go on. And we go on. And every night for the rest of our lives we get to decide whether we're thieves or something better." She let that sit. "I promised you at Fourth Turning I'd give the word to scatter when the risk outweighed the prize. I'm giving it now. Anyone who wants the hills, goes tonight, with my blessing and a share of the strongbox. No shame in it. Dalla's eleven carry none, and neither will you."

Silence. Wind moved through the dead ribs of the Ashwood, and far off, something howled — not a wolf; they all knew the difference now, God help them; the broker hounds sang in harmonics no throat should manage.

Wren stood first. Then Vetch. Then the twins, rising together the way they did everything together. Then Old Pell, groaning about his knees, and then the whole ring of them, thirty-eight outlaws standing up one by one around a fire in a burned forest, and nobody reaching for a pack.

"We're not staying because it's smart," Wren said, grinning at last, and Sef felt something crack open in her chest that might, in better light, have been a smile. "We're staying because we're family, and families don't scatter when the house is on fire. They throw water."

"We haven't got water," said one twin.

"We've got tar," said the other.

"That'll burn the house down faster—"

"*Metaphor,*" the whole band roared, and just like that it was done, decided not by orders but by standing, which was the only law Sef had ever trusted.

* * *

Later — past midnight, past the deep blue hour when even the hounds went quiet — Sef walked the line alone. It was an old habit from her soldiering days, the ones she rarely spoke of: a captain who inspected the watch before battle slept better than one who didn't, and a captain who didn't sleep at all needed whatever help she could get.

At the third station, Old Pell was awake, naturally. Old Pell slept maybe two hours a night and claimed sleep was a habit for people with fewer regrets.

"Can't lose her, you know," he said, without preamble, watching the bitten moon.

"Lose who?"

"The girl." He nodded toward the dark south, toward the hidden market roads where Ilvane had descended into the mountain two days ago with Yara and the boy, down through the Gate of Teeth, into the cathedral of ledgers. "Ilvane. Court mage, exiled queen's favorite, woman who could've lived fat in any city in the world and chose instead to spend thirty years refusing to weigh souls until they threw her out." His voice was rough. "You know why she agreed to this? For a favor unnamed. A *favor*. That's not payment. That's a woman buying herself a reason."

Sef said nothing. The fire cracked. Somewhere below, the tar hissed softly in the cold.

"My wife weighed souls once," Pell went on, quieter. "Not for brokers. For the temple, back when temples thought that was holy work. Told me once she could feel each one, like holding birds. Some struggled. Some had gone still." He looked at Sef with his one good eye. "When she couldn't bear it anymore, she didn't buy herself a reason. She just stopped. There's a difference, Captain, and I've spent forty years trying to name it."

"And?"

"And I think it's this: stopping is for yourself. Going on is for somebody else." He poked the fire. "Ilvane's going on. So are you. So am I, damn my knees. Get some sleep or don't, but stop pacing like a caged thing. You'll wear a trench in my station."

Sef clapped his shoulder and moved on.

At the fifth station she found Wren awake too, sitting with her back against a stone, whittling nothing in particular. Beside her lay a small bundle wrapped in oilcloth, and Sef knew without asking what it was: Bram's effects, the ones they hadn't buried with him. The broken bow. The luck-knot his sister had tied for him at fourteen. And the map fragment — the impossible one, in Yara's childhood hand, the one that had started all of this unraveling. Wren kept it close. Everyone in the band had noticed. Nobody had asked.

"He'd have loved this," Wren said. "Bram. A real fight, at last. Something worth the losing."

"He'd have complained magnificently the entire time."

"Oh, entirely. He'd have made it an art form." Wren's knife stilled. "Cap. Tomorrow night. When it starts — when the sky comes apart and the mountain answers — where will you be?"

"At the gate," Sef said. "Holding the door."

"Not in the fight?"

"There is no fight I'm fit for tomorrow. There's only the door." She crouched, took Wren's chin gently, made the girl meet her eye. "Listen to me. Whatever happens, when the gate opens — if it opens — you take whoever's left and you go east to the Fenmark. That's not an order you may disobey with a speech about family. Family means somebody survives to bury the rest and tell it true."

"And you?"

"I'll be last through," Sef said. "Or I won't come through at all."

Wren held her gaze a long moment, then nodded once, the way soldiers nod, which is to say: *I hate this, and I'll do it.*

* * *

Dawn came up gray and thin over the Ashwood, and with it came the hounds.

Sef heard them before the sentries did — she'd learned their song in the Hollow Pass, learned it the worst way, learned it the night Bram fell — and she was moving before the first harmonic shivered the frost off the scorched branches. By the time the twins' signal-arrow whistled up from the gate road, she was already at the map-stones, already pulling Yara's careful linework free of its weights, already shouting the word she'd sworn she wouldn't need until tomorrow:

"Stations! Stations, all — they're early!"

They came up the western road in a column of gray riders, and behind the riders loped the hounds themselves, lean as famine, heads low, singing that wrong-moon song. Broker enforcers. Hollow Men, Pell called them — men whose eyes had been paid away piece by piece until almost nothing looked out anymore. Twelve riders. Four hounds. Enough to burn a camp and salt the ashes.

Enough, Sef understood with a cold, clarifying certainty, to make sure no diversion ever happened. The court knew they were here. The court had always known.

"Vetch — tar-line, west scree, on my mark. Twins, arrows at the riders' horses, not the men — hollow men don't bleed right, but horses still spook like horses. Wren—" she caught the girl's arm, "—the bundle. Take it and go. Now. East. This is the order you don't get to refuse."

"But the gate—"

"Will be held. Go."

For one heartbeat Wren's face did everything at once — fury, fear, love — and then she slung the bundle across her back and vanished into the treeline, and Sef turned to face the gray column coming up through the ash, thirty-eight outlaws at her back and seven nights short of a miracle.

The lead rider raised a hand. The column halted. And from beneath the rider's hood came a voice like dry paper, like a ledger page turning, like something that had sold its voice long ago and was using the receipt:

"Sef, called Halloran's captain. The Court offers terms."

Sef rolled her shoulders. Drew the long knife that had ended three brokers and one bad marriage. Behind her, thirty-eight people she had chosen and who had chosen her stood ready among the dead trees, and the bitten moon hung pale in the morning sky like a wound that refused to close.

"Tell the Court," she called back, "that we don't deal. We *map*."

Above the ridge, the tar caught early — someone's nerve, someone's torch — and the western scree went up in a wall of black smoke and orange flame, and the Sixth Turning began.
