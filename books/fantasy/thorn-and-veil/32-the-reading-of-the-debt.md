## 32. The Reading of the Debt

The chamber where debts were read was not a room so much as a throat.

Yara felt this the moment the herald led them down from the audience hall — a long descending corridor of black stone, ribbed with arches like cartilage, the air growing warmer and wetter as they went, until it seemed they were being swallowed rather than escorted. The boy's hand found Yara's sleeve and gripped it. Sef walked close behind, her boots silent on stone that seemed to drink sound. Even Ilvane, who had spent years in these courts before her exile, kept her eyes forward and her mouth shut.

At the corridor's end, the throat opened into a hollow shaped like nothing Yara had ever mapped. It was round, vast, and utterly without ornament — no pillars, no banners, no throne. Only tiers of benches carved from the living rock, curving up and around like the inside of a shell, and at the center, on a plinth of polished obsidian, a single object:

A book.

It was not large. That was the first cruelty of it. After the cathedral of ledgers above, after the hanging lanterns of souls that had made his stomach turn for days, Yara had expected the contract itself to be monumental — a tome chained to an altar, guarded by things with too many teeth. Instead it sat alone on its plinth, no bigger than a farmer's almanac, bound in leather gone dark with age, its corners worn soft as an old dog's ears. A book that could be held in two hands. A book that had ended lives.

The herald — a thin figure in grey whose face Yara still could not fix in memory, though he had looked directly at it three times now — gestured them to the benches nearest the plinth. *The parties to the debt,* the gesture said. *Sit.*

They sat. Bram's absence sat with them; Yara felt it like a cold spot in the row, an empty place where a broad shoulder should have been.

Above them, far up in the curved dark, other figures took their places on the higher tiers. Clerks. Notaries. Creatures Yara had no names for, robed in colors that hurt to look at directly, the way a bruise hurts. They arranged themselves with the unhurried precision of people who had done this ten thousand times and would do it ten thousand more. Somewhere among them, Yara knew, the Under-Crown watched. He did not try to find it. He had learned, in the audience hall, that looking for the Under-Crown was like looking for the center of a whirlpool: you knew it was there by what circled it.

A bell rang once. The sound came from everywhere and nowhere, and it did not fade so much as withdraw, pulling back through the stone like water into sand.

Then the reading began.

* * *

The reader was a woman — or something wearing the shape of one — called the Lector of Standing Debts. She rose from the second tier, descended to the plinth, and opened the book with both hands, the way one opens something fragile and old. Her voice, when she began, was not beautiful. It was worse than beautiful. It was *exact*. Every word arrived at precisely the weight the words demanded, no more, no less, and Yara — who had spent his life listening to language, who had transcribed the speech of valley elders and border smugglers and once, memorably, a dying river-pilot's last directions — felt within minutes that he was hearing language used as an instrument of surgery.

"*In the year of the Deep Court's reckoning four-one-nine,*" she read, "*there was entered into this ledger a sale.*"

The boy went rigid against Yara's side.

"*The seller: one party of the overland, resident of the veiled valley, name recorded under seal.*" Yara heard those words — *under seal* — and felt something twist behind his ribs. The seller's name existed. It was written in this book. It was simply not for him to hear. Not yet. Perhaps not ever. The court kept its sellers the way a leech keeps the wound it feeds from: hidden, attached, indispensable.

"*The goods:*" — and here the Lector paused, and the pause lasted four full seconds, and in those four seconds Yara understood that the word *goods* had been chosen deliberately, weighed against *soul* and found heavier, because *goods* did not flinch — "*one soul, juvenile, unencumbered, presented for valuation on the night of the winter dark.*"

*Unencumbered.* Yara stared at the obsidian plinth until his reflection stared back, warped and thin. Unencumbered. As if the boy had been a field, clear of stones. As if he had not been six years old. As if whoever carried him to whatever broker had handled the sale had not had to carry him at all — had perhaps only needed to hold out a hand, and the boy, trusting, had taken it.

He thought of the sigil beneath the collarbone, the brand Maren had shown him a lifetime ago in a valley kitchen, twelve turns of the moon ago. He thought of how the boy had not cried when Yara first touched it. How he had only watched Yara's face, reading it, the way mute children learn to read faces — as if the whole world were a page and everyone in it a writer who might, at any moment, write something cruel.

"*Valuation rendered: the soul is judged small but sound,*" the Lector continued. "*Its quality is noted as resilient. This notation shall bear upon the terms of redemption hereafter.*"

*Resilient.* Yara wanted to laugh, and could not, and wanted to weep, and could not do that either. Resilient. The court had tested the boy's soul — of course it had; everything here was tested, weighed, assayed — and had found that it bent and did not break, and had written this down not as praise but as *data*, a note bearing upon terms, a variable in an equation. Even the boy's strength had been converted into ledger-language. Even his survival had been priced.

Sef, beside him, had gone very still. Ilvane had her hands folded in her lap, knuckles white. The boy had turned his face into Yara's sleeve, hiding, the way he hid during thunderstorms, during the hounds, during Bram's burial. But he was not asleep. Yara could feel the tension in the small body like a drawn bowstring. He was listening. However much it cost him, he was listening to strangers read aloud the story of what had been taken from him, in a hall full of creatures who considered the taking lawful.

"*The consideration paid:*" the Lector read, and recited a sum in a currency Yara did not know — units of something called *veiled silver*, which Ilvane had told him once was minted from grief, literally, pressed from the substance of mourning under great pressure. "*Paid in full at the hour of sale. No lien remains upon the seller. The seller walks free.*"

There it was again. *Walks free.* Whoever had sold this child — whoever in Yara's own valley, in the place his parents had built to be safe, had carried a child across the veil and traded his soul for silver pressed from someone else's sorrow — that person had been paid, discharged, released. The transaction was clean. The court's records glowed with the cleanliness of it. And somewhere beyond the wall his parents had raised, that seller was still walking free, still breathing valley air, still perhaps passing Yara on the market road, nodding to him, calling him *mapmaker* in a friendly voice.

Yara filed the fury somewhere deep and structural, the way he filed everything, the way a river files its rage in the shape of the canyon it cuts.

"*Now,*" said the Lector, and turned a page, and the sound of the turning filled the whole hollow chamber like a door opening in a tomb, "*the terms of maturity.*"

* * *

She read the terms slowly. She was required to, Yara understood — or perhaps she chose to, the way a headsman chooses his pace. The debt matured upon the completion of the thirteenth turning of the moon since the date of entry. Upon maturity, the debtor — the soul itself, the boy, named in the book not by any name anyone used but by a designation: *Asset Nine-And-Forty, Veiled Provenance* — was to present himself for collection. Failing presentation, collection would be enforced. Enforcement, the Lector read, "shall proceed by such means as the Court deems proportionate," and the phrase *proportionate* was left undefined, and Yara saw Ilvane close her eyes briefly at the sound of it, because she knew, and he was beginning to know, what the court considered proportionate when it came to souls that resisted.

The moon was nearly full above them, though they could not see it. Down here, under the mountain, under miles of stone, the moon was a rumor, a term in a contract, a countdown. Six nights. Five, if you counted the way the court counted, from sundown to sundown, and the sun had set hours ago.

The Lector read on. Conditions of custody pending maturity — the boy was to remain, the book said, "in the keeping of any party, provided such keeping does not constitute concealment from lawful collection." Yara's jaw ached and he realized he had been clenching it. So their entire journey — the tollhouse raid, the ashwood, Bram dead in the pass, the hounds, all of it — was, in the court's grammar, a question of whether *keeping* shaded into *concealment*. They had been walking a legal knife-edge for seven months without knowing the blade had a name.

Then the Lector reached the clauses of redemption, and everything changed.

Or rather — Yara corrected himself, sitting in the cold light of what came next — everything failed to change, which was worse.

"*The debt may be discharged before maturity,*" the Lector read, "*by redemption. Redemption requires an equal claim.*"

Yara leaned forward. Beside him, Sef leaned forward. Even Ilvane, who must have known what was coming, leaned forward slightly, as if her body could not help hoping.

"*An equal claim is defined thusly:*" the Lector's voice did not slow, but somehow each word grew heavier, the way stones grow heavier the deeper you carry them into a mine. "*A claim of equal standing to the claim of sale. The claimant must hold title to a soul of equal valuation, freely held, unencumbered, and must surrender that title wholly and irrevocably in exchange for the release of Asset Nine-And-Forty.*"

The silence afterward had texture. It had grain and weight. Yara sat in it and felt it settle over him like snowfall.

*A soul for a soul.*

Of course. Of course it was that. He had known, in some part of himself that he had kept locked and unlit through seven months of road and grief, that the court would not accept gold, would not accept service, would not accept a map of its own halls drawn true enough to shift its walls. The court dealt in one currency only. It had always dealt in one currency only. You did not buy back a soul with anything less than a soul, because to permit otherwise would be to admit that souls had a price above themselves, and the entire architecture of the court — the ledgers, the lanterns, the tiers of notaries rising into the dark like the rings of some vast and patient tree — rested on the axiom that nothing was above price, least of all the thing being priced.

An equal claim. A soul of equal valuation, freely held, unencumbered.

Yara ran the inventory the way he'd run inventories his whole life, quick and merciless, a surveyor's habit.

Sef? He glanced sideways. Sef's soul — if the court even recognized outlaw souls as inventory, and it did, it weighed them at tollhouses like hers, he'd seen the scales — Sef's soul was scarred, encumbered, pledged in a dozen small ways to the band that followed her. Not freely held. Not equal. And he would sooner cut off his own drawing hand than ask.

Ilvane? Ilvane's soul had already been partially claimed — that was what exile meant, here; exile was a lien. She had told him as much on the road, in the flat voice she used for truths that cost too much to say with feeling. Unequal. Encumbered. Ineligible.

Himself?

Here Yara made himself stop and actually think, because despair is most dangerous when it arrives ahead of the facts, and he was a man who trusted facts the way other men trusted gods.

His soul. What did the court know of his soul? He was born of magic — Ilvane had confirmed it, the wall's architect's son, the reason the valley refused magic, the leak in the dam his parents had spent their lives patching. His soul was, by any assay the court could run, extraordinary. Unusual provenance. Veiled origin. If anything, his valuation would exceed the boy's — a child's soul, *small but sound*, against a soul that had soaked in magic before it could walk.

So the clause fit him. Technically. Arithmetically. An equal claim, or better than equal. He could stand before the plinth and say: *I hold title to my own soul, freely held, unencumbered, and I surrender it for the boy.*

Except.

Except that he did not hold title to his own soul. Not here. Not in this book's grammar. He understood that now, sitting in the throat of the court, understanding arriving with the slow horrible clarity of a map drawn in terrain that refuses to match it. Because he had learned, seven months ago and again in the audience hall and again tonight in the phrase *veiled provenance*, that the court had been hunting him since before he could speak. His parents had built a wall around a valley to hide him. The brokers had never stopped looking. Which meant that somewhere in these ledgers — in some older book, some deeper tier — there was already an entry with his name under seal. Already a claim. Already a file. A soul that was *already claimed* could not be *freely held*. A soul that was the object of a standing hunt could not serve as redemption for another soul, because the court would simply be trading one asset it intended to collect for another asset it already owned.

He was not a buyer in this market. He had never been a buyer. He was stock. He had walked into the cathedral of ledgers believing himself a rescuer, and the ledgers had known better the whole time.

Yara became aware that the Lector had stopped reading and was looking at him.

Not at the boy. At him. The face he could not remember, the eyes that would not stay in memory, fixed on him with an expression that was not malice — malice would have been almost kind — but something more like an accountant's patience. The expression of a system that has already run your numbers and is waiting, politely, for you to run them yourself and arrive at the same total.

"*The Court notes,*" she said, and her speaking voice was different from her reading voice, looser, almost conversational, which made it worse, "*that parties to a debt sometimes believe the clause of redemption offers them a door. It does offer a door. Doors are not the same as destinations.*" She closed the book — gently, respectfully, the way you close the eyes of the dead — and stepped back from the plinth. "*The reading is complete. The parties have six nights. The Court wishes the parties to understand that it takes no pleasure in enforcement.*"

Somewhere above them, the Under-Crown's presence shifted, the way air shifts before weather. Yara did not look up. He was looking at the book on its plinth, small and soft-cornered and absolute, and he was thinking about doors and destinations, and about the difference between a clause and a key.

* * *

They were given a cell. Of course they were given a cell — the court was scrupulously hospitable to its assets. Three rooms off a side gallery, warm, dry, provisioned with food that tasted of nothing at all, food designed by someone who understood that flavor was a form of hope and hope was not on the menu. A guard stood outside, or something shaped like a guard, patient as furniture.

Sef paced. Ilvane sat with her back to the wall, eyes closed, doing something with her breath that Yara suspected was older than any kingdom. The boy sat on the cot beside Yara and did not hide his face this time. He watched Yara with those eyes that read the world like text, and what he read there, Yara did not want to imagine.

"There's a way," Sef said finally, stopping mid-stride. "There's always a way. We've come too far and buried too many for there to not be a way."

"The clause is clear," Yara said. His voice sounded strange to him, flattened, like ink diluted past usefulness. "Equal claim. Freely held. Unencumbered. I've run it every direction. There's no one in this mountain who qualifies, and the one person whose soul might outweigh the boy's—" he stopped. He could not say *mine* out loud yet. Saying it made it real, and it was not yet real enough to say, only real enough to feel, sitting on his chest like wet slate.

"You don't know that yours is claimed," Ilvane said quietly, without opening her eyes. "You're inferring. Inference is a kind of mapping, Yara. Sometimes maps are wrong."

"They're wrong," Yara agreed. "That's why I redraw them."

He said it lightly, and then he heard himself say it, and something moved behind the flatness of his despair — something small and mineral and stubborn, the thing that had kept him drawing maps of a valley that refused to be drawn, the thing that had carried him across the boundary line when every instrument he owned told him to turn back.

Maps are wrong. So you redraw them.

The clause said *equal claim*. It did not say where the claim must come from. It did not say the claimant must be a person. It did not say the claim must pre-exist the reading. It said: *a soul of equal valuation, freely held, unencumbered, surrendered wholly and irrevocably.* Legal language. Ledger language. And ledger language, Yara thought, staring into the dark beyond the cell's lamp, was just another territory — surveyed, bounded, defended by its precise definitions — and every territory, no matter how carefully fenced, had margins. Had blank spaces. Had edges where the ink ran pale and the surveyor's certainty gave out.

The court had read him its law tonight, all of it, every clause, in a voice exact as a blade. It believed that in doing so it had closed every door.

But Yara Dunmore had spent thirty years learning what the court had never learned: that a map is not the territory, and a law is not the world, and somewhere between the letter of any document and the truth of any place there is always — *always* — unmapped ground.

Six nights.

He looked at the boy, and the boy looked back, and neither of them said anything, because the boy couldn't and Yara, for once, didn't need to.

"I'll need paper," Yara said to the guard-shaped thing at the door. "And ink. And as much of both as you can spare."

The thing regarded him for a long moment. Then, with the terrible courtesy of a system that has already run your numbers, it brought him paper.
