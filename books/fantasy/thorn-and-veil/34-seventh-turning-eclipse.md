## 34. Seventh Turning: Eclipse

### YARA

The mountain had teeth. He had known it since they first saw it from the pass — a black crown of stone against a bruised sky, its gate a mouth, its ridgelines molars worn smooth by centuries of wind and worse. But standing before the Gate of Teeth now, with the thirteenth moon climbing toward its eclipse, Yara understood that the mountain's true teeth were not stone at all.

They were ledgers.

He could feel them through his boots, through the soles of his feet, through whatever strange cartographer's sense he had carried all his life without knowing what it was. Beneath them, deep in the mountain's hollow heart, thousands of souls hung like lanterns in a cathedral of debt, each one burning with the light of someone who had been bought, sold, weighed, and filed away. The Under-Crown was down there, reading.

And above them, the moon was dying.

It rose swollen and white over the eastern shoulder of the world, and even as Yara watched, the shadow began to eat it — a bite taken from the lower edge, slow as ink spreading through wet paper. He had drawn eclipses before. He had charted their paths across oceans he would never sail, calculated their hours to the minute, and never once had he imagined standing beneath one while it counted down the last turn of a child's soul.

"Yara." Sef's voice, low. She stood at his shoulder with her arms crossed, her scarred knuckles white where she gripped her own forearms. "Say it again."

"The gate opens at totality." His throat was dry. "Ilvane says the court reads its debts by eclipse-light. When the moon goes dark, every lantern below flares, and the gate unseals for one hour. No more."

"And after that?"

"After that," Yara said, "it seals again, and everything we came for stays down there forever."

The wind off the pass smelled of ash and old iron. Behind him, the outlaws were checking blades and oiling bowstrings in near silence — twenty-three of them left of the thirty who had ridden out of the tollhouse country, and every one of them wearing the particular stillness of people who have decided to be afraid later. Tam moved among them with Bram's hatchet hanging from her belt. She hadn't spoken since the pass. She didn't need to.

The boy pressed close against Yara's side. He had not let go of Yara's hand since dusk, and his small palm was slick and cold, but when Yara looked down at him the boy did not look afraid. He looked *ready*, in the way that children are ready — absolutely, without any of the doubt that ruins adults — and it broke something open in Yara's chest.

"We could still run," Yara said quietly, kneeling so they were eye to eye. "Sef has routes south. We'd be gone before the shadow finished."

The boy shook his head once. Then he lifted his other hand and pointed, deliberately, at the mountain. And then — slowly, carefully, the way he did everything with language now, like a man carrying water across ice — he spoke.

"You. Me. Home."

Three words. They cost him visibly; his voice cracked on the second and he had to breathe before the third. But he held Yara's gaze the whole time, and Yara felt the words land somewhere deeper than hearing.

"All right," Yara whispered. "Together."

Above them, the moon was half-eaten. Below them, the Gate of Teeth began, very faintly, to glow.

### SEF

Twenty years of outlawry had taught Sef that no plan survives contact with anything — an enemy, a river, a bad night's sleep. So she had built this one to break on purpose, in the right places.

She crouched at the lip of the ridge above the gate road, watching her people take position, and ran the arithmetic of sacrifice one more time. It never balanced. That was the thing about plans; you dressed them up in tactics until they looked clean, and underneath they were always just a list of who might die so that who else might not.

"Bram's post is yours," she said to Tam, without looking at her. "Fire lines on my signal. Not before. If the hounds come up the western switchback, you fall back to the scree and you *stay* falling back. You don't get brave. Bram got brave."

Tam's jaw worked. "Yes, Captain."

"And if we're not out by moonrise-after — if the gate seals with us inside—"

"It won't."

"It might." Sef turned and made herself meet the girl's eyes. "Then you burn the toll-records in the valley on your way home and you tell the story true. Every part of it. Even the parts that make me look like a fool."

For a moment Tam's composure wavered, and Sef saw the sixteen-year-old underneath the soldier. Then it hardened again. "You'll be telling it yourself," Tam said. "You always say dead captains make lousy narrators."

Sef laughed despite everything — a short, rough sound that scattered into the wind. Gods, she'd miss that girl if it came to it. She'd miss all of them, this ragged parliament of thieves she'd built out of deserters and orphans and one mad cartographer, these people who kept justice outside the law because the law had sold itself centuries ago and nobody inside it seemed to remember the price. Whatever happened tonight, the ledger of the world needed people willing to write outside its margins. She liked to think Bram would have agreed.

The eclipse was three-quarters done now. The light on the mountain had gone strange — copper and grey, shadows lying wrong, the whole world looking like one of Yara's maps come half to life. Speaking of which.

Yara stood apart from everyone, near the gate, a roll of fresh paper under his arm and charcoal-blackened fingers flexing at his sides. He'd barely slept in two days. She'd watched him work by firelight, drawing and redrawing, muttering measurements of a place he'd only glimpsed through a gate that admitted truth — and she had understood, watching him, that she was looking at a weapon the courts had never accounted for. A man whose maps became real. A man who could redraw the world.

The law would call him a heretic. The court would call him a thief of assets.

Sef called him a friend, which was rarer and worth more.

"Moon's nearly gone," called Wren from the high watch. "Shadow's at the last bite."

Below them, the Gate of Teeth blazed — veins of pale light crawling across its surface like frost racing over water. The mountain groaned, a sound felt in the chest rather than heard, and the great doors began to swing inward on a corridor of descending amber glow.

"Now," Sef breathed. Then, louder, with twenty years of command behind it: "*Now!*"

Torches bloomed along the ridge. Tam's fire lines went up on the southern slopes, exactly on signal, a crown of flame meant to look like an army arriving. Somewhere below, horns began to scream — the gate-guard, confused, outnumbered by smoke and noise. And Sef drew her sword, kissed the flat of the blade the way her mother used to kiss her forehead, and led her people down toward the open mouth of the mountain.

### ILVANE

The favor came due at the worst possible hour, which was, of course, the only hour favors ever chose.

Ilvane stood alone on the causeway before the Gate of Teeth, staff planted, silver hair whipping in the thermal wind, and watched the eclipse complete. The moon went out like a snuffed candle. Darkness fell whole and sudden, and in that darkness the gate's light turned from amber to a deep arterial red, and she felt the court notice her.

It had been eleven years since she stood on this stone. Eleven years since she had refused, before the assembled Under-Crown, to weigh a farmer's daughter on the soul-scales — refused, and been stripped of rank and name and driven out into the lawful world with nothing but her staff and her conscience, which weighed more than any soul she'd ever declined to measure. Eleven years, and here she was again, because a stubborn mapmaker and a thief with a good heart had asked for help she couldn't refuse without ceasing to be herself.

They thought she owed them a favor. She had let them believe it. But the truth was simpler and more shameful: she had spent eleven years waiting for someone to come ask.

"Hold the gate," Yara had said. "However long it takes. Don't let it seal with us inside."

"I know how gates seal," she had answered. "I helped design the wards on this one, before I knew what I was warding."

Now she raised her staff, and the old words rose with it — words she had sworn never to speak again in service of this place, though she found they tasted different in defense against it. Light gathered at the causeway's four corners. She drove the staff down and the ward-lattice ignited, a dome of pale geometry snapping into being around the gate itself, sealing it *open* — wedging the jaws of the mountain apart against its will, against the will of everything beneath it.

The response was immediate. Pressure rolled up out of the depths like a fist closing, testing the lattice. Ilvane braced, teeth gritted, and held.

Something moved in the red-lit corridor beyond the gate. Tall shapes, unhurried. Enforcers — the hollow men of the pass, but greater, elder, robed in ledger-leather. They stopped at the threshold of her wards and regarded her with faces like blank vellum.

*Exiled one,* said a voice that was many voices, dry as turning pages. *You were cast out for mercy. Return to exile. This does not concern you.*

"It concerns me entirely," Ilvane said, and her voice did not shake, which surprised her pleasantly. "Eleven years ago I refused to weigh one soul. Tonight I am refusing all of them. Consider it a correction of the record."

*The record,* the voices said, *is ours.*

"No," said Ilvane, and poured more of herself into the lattice, feeling it drink her strength like a thirsty field drinks rain. "The record," she said, "has been rewritten."

Far above, the eclipsed moon hung black-ringed and terrible, corona blazing around its rim like a wound in the sky. And through her straining wards, down the long throat of the mountain, Ilvane felt four small figures begin to descend — a thief, a mage-in-exile's old student, a woman who should have died twice already, and a mapmaker walking hand in hand with a child whose soul burned brighter than any lantern in the dark below.

*Hold,* she told the lattice. *Hold, hold, hold.*

The pressure doubled. Her knees bent. Blood threaded warm from her nose.

She held.

* * *

### YARA

The descent was a descent through an account book.

That was the horror of it, Yara thought as they went down — stairs cut from black stone, walls lined floor to ceiling with shelves, and the shelves packed with ledgers. Millions of pages. Every transaction the court had ever recorded, going back further than kingdoms. He ran his fingertips along a spine as he passed and read, in the strobing red light: *One winter's luck, purchased. Seller: a widow, Rynmark. Price: her late husband's memory of her face.* Another: *Courage, extracted in installments.* Another: *A childhood, entire, mint condition, seller unknown.*

And between the shelves, hanging from hooks of cold iron, the lanterns.

Souls. He had imagined fireflies, candles, embers. They were none of those things. They were small human lights, each one shaped faintly like the person it had been torn from — a girl mid-laugh, an old man bent over a plow, a soldier reaching for someone out of frame. They glowed softly, patiently, the way people wait who have forgotten they were waiting for anything. Some flickered weakly. One or two had guttered almost to nothing.

Beside him, the boy made a sound Yara had never heard from him — a low, wounded hum, hands pressed to his own chest, staring at the lanterns with recognition. Of course. He could feel them. He was one of them, or nearly; his own soul hung somewhere ahead, tagged and shelved, waiting for the eclipse to finish its accounting.

"Don't look away," Yara said gently. "Look at all of them. Remember all of them. That's why we're really here."

Sef moved point, blade bare, eyes tracking every junction. Wren covered the rear. The diversion thundered distantly overhead — muffled horns, the crackle of Tam's fires, the deep concussion of the gate fighting Ilvane's wards. Time bled away. The eclipse would not last.

At the bottom of the final stair, the corridor opened.

The chamber was vast beyond sense — a cavern dressed as a counting-house, pillars carved as columns of figures, and in its center a dais of black glass where the scales stood. Soul-scales. Ilvane had described them; the reality was worse. They were beautiful. Balanced to a hair's tolerance, pans of hammered silver, chains fine as spider silk. Above them, suspended in a cage of iron filigree, hung one lantern brighter than all the rest — a small fierce light shaped unmistakably like a laughing boy.

The boy made his wounded-hum sound again and took one step forward, and the lantern flared in answer, and Yara felt the pull of it in his own ribs, the way a compass feels north.

"That's far enough."

The voice came from everywhere — from the shelves, the scales, the walls of figures. The darkness at the chamber's heart gathered, folded, and became the Under-Crown: a figure robed in ledger-pages, crowned with a ring of tiny keys, a face like an unfinished contract, features suggested rather than committed.

*Mapmaker,* it said, and Yara heard the smile in it. *You've brought me a debtor, a bandit, and a dead woman's son. How enterprising. The eclipse has minutes remaining. Shall we conclude the reading?*

Yara stepped forward, put himself squarely between the scales and the boy, and unrolled the paper he had carried against his heart for two days and nights — the map he had drawn by firelight, the map of the court itself, the map that was already beginning, very quietly, to become true.

"No," Yara Dunmore said, as the last sliver of eclipsed moon began, high above, to return. "Before you read the debt — I have an amendment to file."
