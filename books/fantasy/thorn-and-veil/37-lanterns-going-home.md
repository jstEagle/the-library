## 37. Lanterns Going Home

The silence after the Under-Crown's fall was not silence at all.

It was the sound of ten thousand ledgers closing at once — a whisper like wind through a canyon of paper, like the last breath of a library burning in slow motion. The great cathedral beneath the mountain had gone dark where the court's power had held it lit, and in that darkness the lanterns began to sway.

Yara stood among them with ink still wet on his fingers and the map of the court dissolving off his page into the living stone, and he watched the souls hang in their glass-and-brass housings and swing, gently, as if waking from a long sleep.

They had hung there for centuries. Some of them for longer than centuries. He could feel it now that the contract-web was unraveling — feel the weight of each one the way a cartographer feels the weight of an unnamed coast, the pressure of a thing that exists but has never been permitted to exist *out loud*. The lanterns were not lamps. He had known that from the moment he descended into this place, had felt it in his teeth. They were people. Folded small. Filed away. Interest accruing on the light of them.

And now the filing system was dying.

* * *

The first lantern cracked on its own.

It hung near the eastern wall, a small thing, no bigger than a fist, its glass fogged with age. A sound came out of it — not breaking glass but something softer, the sound ice makes when a river decides to be water again. Then a seam of gold split down its side, and what spilled out was not fire.

It was a woman. Or the shape of one, made of the same light that lives behind closed eyelids. She unfolded slowly, joints remembering themselves, and when she was whole she looked down at her own hands and turned them over and over, and she did not weep, though her shoulders shook. She simply breathed. Yara understood, watching her, that breathing was the whole of it — that after however many years of being weighed and shelved and owned, breath itself was the miracle.

Then another lantern opened. And another.

It moved through the cathedral like dawn moving across a valley floor — not all at once, but inevitably, following the channels Yara's map had cut through the court's architecture. Where his inked lines had redrawn the walls, the contract-threads that fed each lantern had been severed, and every severed thread ended in a small golden rupture, and every rupture let someone out.

The outlaws' diversion above ground still thundered faintly through the mountain — Sef's horns, Ilvane's held gate, the crash of broker enforcers against a wall of borrowed storm — but down here the battle had become something else entirely. Down here it was a homecoming with no one left to stop it.

Yara walked the aisles of hanging light with his heart hammering against his ribs, doing the only work he knew how to do. He read the tags. Every lantern bore one — a lead seal stamped with a ledger number, a debt date, a name reduced to a reference. He took out his knife and cut them, one by one, and as each seal fell away the lantern it belonged to brightened, as if the name itself had been a hand pressed over a mouth.

"Vessin Corrane," he read aloud from one tag, before cutting it free. "Sold by his brother. Debt matured two hundred years ago." The lantern above him flared and burst, and a young man made of light stood blinking in the aisle, looking around at the endless shelves of the dead-ledger cathedral with an expression Yara would carry to his own grave: not joy. Not yet. Just the stunned, animal disbelief of a prisoner hearing a door open and not trusting it.

"Go up," Yara told him, and his voice cracked on the second word. "The gate is held. Go up and keep going until you see sky."

The man went. His footsteps made no sound, but the air bent around him as he passed, grateful.

There were so many. Yara cut seals until his knife arm burned and his voice went hoarse from reading names aloud — because that mattered, he discovered; the names mattered. A lantern whose name was spoken aloud opened faster, wider, more completely than one merely cut loose. The court had known this. It was why they filed people under numbers. A number could be owned forever. A name, once spoken by a free mouth, started becoming itself again immediately, and things that are becoming themselves cannot be collateral.

So he spoke them. Hundreds of names. Thousands. His throat raw, his eyes streaming — from the ink-smoke, he told himself, only the smoke — he moved down the aisles reading the fragments the tags preserved and giving each soul back the sound of what it had been called.

* * *

He found Tam's lantern an hour before dawn, or what passed for dawn beneath a mountain — the moment when the cathedral's high shafts dimmed from black to grey and the freed souls drifting upward toward the gate glowed brighter against it, like stars coming out in reverse.

It hung in the central vault, apart from the others, on a chain of black iron thick as Yara's wrist. Of course it did. A debt maturing on the thirteenth turning would be kept close. Kept ready. The Under-Crown had intended to drink this one at moonrise.

The lantern was larger than the rest, and older in its making — the glass etched with the same pre-kingdom script Maren had traced in the mud-caked boy's speech, the same words older than stone. And inside it, instead of the steady gold of the other souls, something moved. Something restless. A light that paced the curve of its prison like a child pacing a window.

Yara's hands shook so badly he could barely hold the knife.

Because the tag on this one was different too. Not lead. Gold. And instead of a number it bore a single line in the court's precise, beautiful, hateful hand:

*Name withheld pending collection.*

They hadn't even written his name down. They had taken a child's soul and refused to record what it was called, because a recorded name might have been spoken, and a spoken name might have been remembered, and a remembered name is the beginning of every rescue ever attempted.

"No," Yara said out loud, to the empty vault, to the dead court, to whoever had written that line. "No. I know his name."

He didn't. That was the terrible truth of it. Twelve moons of road and river and ashwood, twelve moons of the boy sleeping against his side and eating from his hand and learning the word *home* one syllable at a time — and Yara had never once heard his true name, because the boy had none to give. It had been in here the whole time. Pacing. Waiting.

But he knew *a* name. The one he'd given without thinking, somewhere on the Road of Small Mercies, some cold night when pointing and grunting failed them both. The name the boy had turned toward every time since, the way a plant turns toward the only light offered.

Yara sheathed his knife. This seal would not be cut. He understood that suddenly, completely, the way he understood contour lines — not with effort but with recognition. Gold answered to claim, and claims answered to words, and the only words that could open this lantern were the ones the Under-Crown feared enough to withhold: a true name, freely given, by someone with the right to give it.

He put his palm flat against the glass.

The light inside stopped pacing. Came to the wall nearest his hand. Pressed back.

"I can't give you your name," Yara said quietly. "I never had it. But I have this one, and I've carried it for you all this way, and if you'll take it—" His voice broke and he let it. There was no one left down here to perform steadiness for. "If you'll take it, it's yours. It always was. I was only keeping it warm."

The lantern warmed under his palm like a cheek.

"Tamsin," Yara said.

Not Tam. Tamsin — the full shape of it, the name he'd reached for that first night without knowing why, the name that had been waiting in his mouth since a mute child stood dripping in his doorway speaking a language older than any kingdom. Perhaps the valley had known. Perhaps the part of him that magic had been walled out of had recognized what walked through his door anyway, kin calling to kin across the veil his parents built.

The gold seal did not crack. It *melted*, running down the chain like morning frost off iron, and the black links rusted through in the space of three heartbeats, and the lantern sank into Yara's waiting arms, light as a bird, and then it opened.

* * *

What came out was not a woman-shape or a young man unfolding. What came out was small, and fast, and it hit Yara in the chest hard enough to stagger him, and it was not light at all — or not only light. For one impossible moment he held the boy's soul against his heart and it was warm the way a sleeping child is warm, and it hummed the way a held breath hums before laughter, and it smelled, absurdly, unbearably, of the herb-garden behind Maren's cottage and the woodsmoke of Yara's own hearth.

Then it dove into the boy standing beside him, and Tam cried out — *cried out*, the first voiced sound Yara had ever heard him make, a raw cracked note like a hinge opening after years — and folded double, and the light poured into him through his chest and his throat and his open, astonished eyes.

Yara caught him before he fell. Held him up. Felt the tremor move through the small body like a flood moving through a dry riverbed, filling every channel at once.

"Tam—"

The boy lifted his head.

His eyes were different. Yara saw it instantly — the flat watchful wariness that had lived in them since the door at dusk, the look of a creature that had learned no expression was safe, was *gone*. What replaced it was bottomless and bright and terrified and eleven years old.

And then Tam spoke.

"My name," he said, and his voice was rough as an unopened road, ruined from disuse, shaking, *alive*. "My name is Tamsin of the Veilward Line. My mother gave it to me at the river. My father wrote it in the margin of a map." He swallowed, and tears went down his face, and he did not wipe them away, and Yara realized with a jolt that the boy was quoting — reciting something learned before his voice was taken, held in the soul that had just come home, a message sealed in the one place the brokers could not reach it. "She said: *if it is ever taken from you, say it again, and mean it, and it will find its way back.*"

"Say it again," Yara whispered.

Tam straightened. Drew a breath that filled his whole body, that seemed to fill the whole vault, that made the last few hundred unbroken lanterns tremble on their chains in answer.

"TAMSIN."

The name went up through the mountain like a bell struck underground. Yara felt it pass through his bones, through the stone, through whatever remained of the court's dying architecture — and everywhere it passed, lanterns flared. All of them at once, hundreds, thousands, the whole buried constellation of stolen souls answering a child's voice the way iron filings answer a magnet, and the ones already freed paused on their ascent and turned, all those faces of light, looking down at the small boy in the cartographer's arms who had said his own name and meant it.

"It found its way back," Tam said. Smaller now. Just a boy again, exhausted, weeping, gripping Yara's coat with both fists. "You kept it warm."

"You kept yourself," Yara managed. "I only drew the road."

Above them, muffled by a mile of stone, the sounds of battle changed pitch — the horns shifted to a new call, Sef's retreat-and-rally signal, three rising notes. The diversion was ending. The gate would not hold forever even with Ilvane paying her unnamed favor, and the mountain around them was beginning, very faintly, to groan as a court without contracts reconsidered whether it needed ceilings.

"We have to go," Yara said. "All of us. Now."

Tam looked up at the thousands of lanterns still swaying, still opening, still pouring their people out into the aisles — the old, the young, souls sold by brothers and creditors and kings, souls sold yesterday. So many. Too many to outrun the mountain's grief.

"They'll follow the light," Tam said. His voice was hoarse but certain, and there was something in it Yara had never heard before: authority, inherited from wherever his mother had learned it, from whatever the Veilward Line had been. "Souls go toward open sky. Always have. Open the roof, mapmaker."

Yara stared at him. Then at the vault's ceiling, a hundred feet of living stone above the central chain. Then at his own ink-stained hands.

His maps became true terrain. He had redrawn the walls of this cathedral once already tonight.

He pulled out his book with hands that were steady now, steadier than they had ever been in his life, and flipped to a clean page, and began to draw — fast, furious, joyful strokes, a shaft, a chimney, a wound of open sky cut through the crown of the mountain, and he named it as he drew, because naming was the whole of the craft and he finally, finally understood that:

"*The Lanterns' Road,*" he said, "*from the vault to the stars.*"

The ceiling opened like a flower.

Stone parted along his inked lines without violence, folding back in slabs the size of ships, and cold night air fell into the cathedral — real air, mountain air, carrying woodsmoke from Sef's fires and the first grey suggestion of dawn — and through the gap poured the light of the thirteenth moon, whole and eclipsed and passing, exactly as the debt had scheduled, over a court that no longer existed to collect it.

And the souls rose.

All of them. A river of gold going up through the wound in the world, past Yara and Tam standing hand in hand in the vault, past the empty chains, up through the Lanterns' Road and out into the open dark, scattering toward valleys and cities and graves and homes they had been sold away from lifetimes ago. Yara watched them go and felt the thing he had been building toward his entire life without knowing it: not a map. A release.

Tam tugged his sleeve. Pointed up.

"Home," the boy said — his first common word, given back now with its full meaning attached — and then, with the ghost of the smile Yara had spent twelve moons earning: "Our home. You're drawing it next."

Yara looked at the blank page waiting in his lap, and at the boy, and at the road of fading light climbing toward the stars, and he picked up his pen.

"Yes," he said. "Yes, I am."
