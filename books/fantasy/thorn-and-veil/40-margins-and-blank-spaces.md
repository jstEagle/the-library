## 40. Margins and Blank Spaces

The atlas took three years to finish, and the last page was the hardest.

Not because it was complicated. Because it was blank.

Yara sat at his desk in the house at the valley's edge — the same house, the same crooked door, the same window that caught the evening light and held it like a cupped hand — and looked down at the empty rectangle where a map should have been. The paper was the good stock now, the kind Sef's people traded up from the coast, thick enough to survive ink and time. He had cut it to size weeks ago. He had ruled its borders in fine lines, drawn the compass rose in the corner with the care of a man writing a love letter. And then he had stopped.

The pen hovered. It had been hovering for an hour.

Outside, Tam was teaching Bram's dog — the one they'd brought home from the pass, gray-muzzled now and slow — to sit for a dried apple instead of meat. The boy's voice carried through the window, patient and clear, the voice of someone who had spent years not speaking and had decided never to waste a word again. *Sit. Sit. Good.* The dog sat. The dog had always been going to sit; it simply enjoyed being asked.

Yara smiled, and the pen came down.

He did not draw a map on that final page. He wrote, instead, in his careful surveyor's hand, the words he had been turning over since the night the lanterns went home:

*This space is left intentionally unmapped.*

*Some places belong to those who live there, not to those who draw them. A map is a claim as much as a record, and I have spent my life learning what claims cost. What follows in this atlas is offered as gift, not as seizure: roads for travelers, rivers for boatmen, borders only where the people who cross them have agreed they exist. Where you find blankness here, know that it is not ignorance. It is respect.*

*The blank spaces are not empty. They are full of everyone who never asked me to name them.*

He read it back twice. Then he signed it — *Yara Dunmore, cartographer, of the Valley That Refuses* — and crossed out *cartographer* and wrote beneath it, after a moment's thought, *neighbor*.

The ink dried gold-brown in the last of the light. Somewhere below the floorboards, in the cellar where he kept his instruments, the old brass compass that had once spun wild at a mute child's touch hung on its nail, needle resting easy, pointing north out of habit rather than conviction. He had not needed it in three years. He kept it anyway. Some tools you retire the way you retire a sword: sharpened, oiled, and honest about what they were for.

* * *

The printing was done in the city of Permitted Streets, which had, in the years since their passage, become slightly less permitted and slightly more street.

That had been Ilvane's doing, mostly. She had taken up residence there after the mountain came down — or rather, after the mountain *opened*, which was the more accurate and far stranger thing. The Court Beneath had not been destroyed. Courts like that could not be destroyed; they could only be emptied, and emptiness, Yara had learned, was its own kind of architecture. The ledgers still hung in that cathedral dark, but they were blank now, every soul unhooked from its chain, every contract unraveled to its first innocent thread. The Under-Crown had gone with them, dissolving into the general dark like salt into water, and what remained below was quiet in the way a courtroom is quiet at midnight: full of echoes, empty of judgments.

Ilvane called it *the largest library in the world, currently misfiling itself*. She went down twice a year with lamps and assistants and catalogued what she found, and she had made the city sign — actually sign, in front of witnesses, which for that city was a revolution — a charter guaranteeing that no ledger written below would ever again be enforceable above. The queen's seal was on it. The exile's seal was beside it. Between the two of them, they had redrawn the oldest map of all: the one that said who owned whom.

"You could have done this yourself," Ilvane had said to him once, over tea in her cluttered tower room, gesturing at the charter with a biscuit. "You're the one whose maps come true. You could have willed the whole thing into being with a steady hand and a bad night's sleep."

"That's why I didn't," Yara said. "A world I draw is just a bigger version of the court. Everything where I put it, staying there because I said so." He turned his cup. "I'd rather build places that don't need me."

She had looked at him for a long moment with those court-trained eyes that missed nothing and forgave less. "Your parents would be proud," she said finally. "They built a valley to hide you. You built a world that doesn't need hiding."

"They built a wall," Yara said. "I'm trying to build doors."

* * *

The valley had changed, and it had not.

That was the thing about the veil, once Yara had taken it down and rebuilt it — and rebuilding had been the work of a full season, himself and Ilvane together, walking the boundary line with pens and chalk and, on the worst days, each other's arms to lean on. The old veil had been a refusal: *magic cannot follow*, a sentence shouted at the world for twenty years. The new warding was an invitation with conditions. Magic could come in. It simply could not *buy* anything inside. No contract written beyond the line held force within it. No broker's hound could track a soul across it. No debt matured on valley soil, no matter how many moons had turned elsewhere.

Maren had tested it personally, the week it was finished, by attempting to sell her own memory of a bad marriage to a trader who wandered up from the south. The words left her mouth and fell flat as dropped stones. She had laughed until she cried, then hugged Yara hard enough to crack a rib, then gone home and told everyone within earshot that the Dunmore boy had finally amounted to something, though in her opinion he still needed feeding.

People came, now. That was the strangest harvest. Exiles and runaways and folk whose names had been sold or stolen or simply worn away by hard living — they heard of a valley where debts died at the border, and they walked in carrying everything they owned, and they stayed. The village at the valley's heart had doubled. There were new roofs on the east side, and a second well, and a schoolhouse where Maren taught letters and Tam taught, of all things, the old tongue — the pre-kingdom speech older than any kingdom, which it turned out half the refugees' grandmothers had sung to them without knowing what it was. Words older than stone, kept alive in lullabies by people who never knew they were keeping anything alive at all.

Tam taught standing on a stool, because he was still small for his age, and he taught with his whole body, drawing the shapes of the words in the air. The children loved him. They had given him a title — *the Speaker* — which embarrassed him so thoroughly that he pretended to hate it, and therefore it would probably stick forever.

His true name, the one that had come back to him with his soul among the hanging lanterns, he shared only rarely. It was not a secret, exactly. It was simply his, the way the valley was the valley's, and he gave it the way Yara had learned to give maps: as a gift, when it was wanted, and never as a claim.

* * *

Sef came twice a year, riding in with whatever company remained of the band that had once ambushed a cartographer and a child on the road of small mercies and ended up changing the shape of the world instead.

The band had not disbanded, precisely. Justice outside the law had found, to everyone's surprise, that the law had grown some justice of its own — slowly, grudgingly, in the way of large institutions dragged forward by small persistent hands. Ilvane's charter. The blank ledgers. The courts of three kingdoms now employed, of all things, *advocates for the unsouled*, a phrase that would have been meaningless nonsense five years ago and was now a salaried position with a stamp and everything. Sef claimed this proved the world was ending. She claimed it with deep satisfaction, over ale, every visit.

"Used to be we hunted brokers because nobody else would," she said, on her most recent visit, feet on Yara's table, uninvited and welcome. "Now there's paperwork about it. Forms. *Ombudsmen.*" She pronounced the word like something she'd scraped off her boot. "We're practically respectable."

"Terrible," Yara agreed.

"We'll have to turn to banditry proper. Very disappointing. Kesha will be devastated."

Kesha, who had joined two years ago after fleeing a tollhouse raid gone wrong, was at that moment in the yard helping Tam fix the dog's fence, laughing at something, her sword hung on the fencepost with the casualness of a tool rather than a threat. Sef watched her through the window and said nothing for a while, and Yara let the silence be, because some silences were maps too, showing exactly where a person stood.

"They'd stay, you know," Yara said eventually. "Any of them. The valley takes everyone."

"I know it does." Sef turned her cup — she drank tea in his house, having learned early that he didn't keep ale, and had never once complained, which from Sef was a declaration of profound affection. "That's why we ride on. Somebody has to be out there reminding the world the valley exists. Can't have paradise turning into a rumor." She grinned, quick and sharp as ever. "Besides. Outlaws who keep the peace. Someone's got to do it badly enough that the professionals get embarrassed into doing it well."

She left three days later, at dawn, with Kesha and six others, heading south toward reports of a broker cell that had survived the fall of the mountain and was trying to rebuild in the ashwood country. Before she went she stood at the gate a moment, looking back at the valley — at the open border, marked now not by a wall of absence but by a ring of standing stones carved with the warding script, each stone bearing a single word in the old tongue that Tam had chosen and Maren had checked three times.

"What does it say?" Sef asked, nodding at the nearest stone. "The word on them. You never told me."

"*Welcome,*" Yara said. "*With limits.*"

Sef laughed all the way down the road.

* * *

On the evening the atlas was finished, Yara walked the boundary path alone.

It was a habit now, three years old. Once a season, he walked the whole ring of stones, checking the script, touching each one, listening to the hum beneath the ground that meant the warding held. It always held. But he walked anyway, because the walk was not really about the warding. It was about remembering what the line used to mean to him — the edge of the known world, the place where his instruments failed and his ink ran pale and his courage ran out — and how different the fear felt now, when crossing it cost nothing and returning cost less.

He reached the northernmost stone as the moon rose.

It was nearly full. Not quite. One night short, maybe two — he had stopped counting the turnings long ago, but some part of him still tracked the moon the way a sailor tracks weather, automatically, against his will. Thirteen turns had been a death sentence once. Now it was just a number the sky passed through on its way to other numbers.

He stood a while in the blue dusk, thinking about the boy asleep in his house a mile behind him, and about the woman whose face he had traded away in a veiled market and never gotten back, and about the strange arithmetic of loss — how he had given up his mother's face and gained, somehow, a family larger than any bloodline. Grief and gratitude braided, Sef had called it once, watching him look at Tam across a fire. She hadn't meant it kindly. She had meant it truly, which was better.

The fragments still came sometimes. His mother's voice, surfacing in dreams like a fish rising in dark water: *the veil was for you.* He knew now what she had meant. He knew about the glowing hands that had held him as an infant outside the valley, about the hunt that had never stopped, about two frightened parents who had built a fortress of silence around their son and paid for it with everything. He had forgiven them somewhere along the road beneath the mountain, though he couldn't have said when. Forgiveness, he had learned, was less a decision than a destination you noticed you'd arrived at.

And he had made his peace with the trade. The market-traders had offered, once, to return the memory — word had traveled even to the veiled stalls that the mapper of the unmapped had unmade the great ledger, and the veiled ones were, above all things, traders who respected a good transaction reversed. He had declined. His mother's face belonged to whoever wore it now, and grief spent was grief honored. Besides, he had her voice. It was enough. Some margins you leave blank on purpose.

He touched the northern stone. It hummed under his palm, warm as a sleeping animal.

"Still holding?" said a voice behind him, and there was Tam, breathless from running the path, the dog loping at his heels. Fifteen now, tall as Yara's shoulder, with the serious eyes of someone who remembered being sold and had decided, deliberately, every day, to be gentle anyway.

"Still holding."

"Good." The boy came to stand beside him, and for a while they watched the moon climb over the mountains that had once seemed like the end of the world and were now, merely, neighbors. "Is it done? The atlas?"

"It's done. Last page tomorrow, if the light's kind. Then it goes to the printer, and then—" Yara shrugged. "Then it belongs to everyone. Which is the point."

"The blank spaces and all?"

"Especially the blank spaces."

Tam nodded slowly, the way he did when he was deciding whether to say the next thing. He usually said it. "There's a place I want to show you someday," he said. "South past the ashwood, where the river forks. Nobody lives there. Nobody's ever mapped it. When I was—" he paused, choosing, "—when I was theirs, I saw it in dreams. It's real. I know it's real." He looked up. "I want to be the one who shows it to you. Not the one who draws it. The one who *shows* it. So it stays theirs, whoever they turn out to be."

Yara looked at the boy — at the child the court had bought and the world had failed and the road had returned, standing on the border of the valley that refused and now, instead, invited — and felt the familiar fullness in his chest that he had long ago stopped trying to name, because some things were better left in the margins.

"There's a page in the atlas for exactly that," he said. "Page four hundred and twelve. Blank. With your name in the corner."

Tam blinked. "My name?"

"Cartographer emeritus," Yara said gravely. "It means the one who leaves the space empty on purpose. Highest honor in the profession. I invented it this morning."

The boy laughed — he laughed easily now, sound filling sound, no silence anywhere in him — and the two of them turned back down the path toward the lights of the village, toward the house with the crooked door, toward the ordinary miraculous evening waiting for them there. Behind them the stone hummed its single word to the dark. Above them the moon climbed, whole and unhurried, keeping its own count now, owing nothing to anyone.

In the morning, Yara would write the dedication page — *for the ones who live in the blank spaces* — and send the atlas out into a world that had learned, slowly and imperfectly and not everywhere yet, but learning, that a map could be a door.

But that was tomorrow. Tonight there was supper, and a dog to feed, and a boy who talked, and a valley holding all of them gently in its open hands.

The needle of the compass in the cellar pointed north, out of habit, and hurt no one, and was not consulted, and slept.
