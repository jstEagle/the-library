## 1. The Ledger of Ash

The fog came off the Thornfell marshes the way it always did at dusk—slow, patient, like something that had learned to wait. I'd learned to wait with it. Nine years on gate watch, and the fog and I understood each other.

The smuggler's boat broke the surface line of the reeds a half-hour past full dark, running no lantern, which was either stupidity or craft, and the way she cut through the black water said craft. I doused my own fire and moved to the willow stand where I'd spent so many cold nights that the ground knew the shape of me.

Routine. That was the word I held onto. Intercept, search, confiscate, report—or don't report, because the garrison at Highmoor had stopped caring about one person's ledgers years ago. A routine interception, and by dawn I'd be alone again with my tea kettle and my silence, which was exactly how I liked it.

I want that noted. I *liked* it.

The boat grounded in the shallows below the old ferry stones, and a figure stepped out—slight, hooded, moving light on their feet the way people do when they've been chased before. I let them get three steps onto the mud, close enough that the fog wrapped them, far enough that the water would slow any sprint.

"Gate law," I said. "Stand where you are."

They froze. Then, infuriatingly, they laughed—a short, breathless sound, like they couldn't quite believe it. "You're real," they said. "The gatekeeper. I thought you were a story the smugglers told to scare each other."

"Story's got it wrong. I'm not a keeper. Just a warden who draws pay from nobody." I came down the bank with my staff leveled, though we both knew I wasn't going to use it. "Cargo. Open it."

They pushed back the hood, and I made a mistake I'd make again: I looked at their face first instead of their hands. Sharp-boned, dark-eyed, younger than the voice suggested. A scar through one eyebrow, old and clean. They watched me watching them with an expression I recognized because I wore it every day—the look of someone deciding how much of themselves to show.

"Wren Vale," they said, offering it like a hand.

"I didn't ask for a name."

"You didn't ask for anything. You just started ordering." They crouched and flipped open the boat's cargo hatch themselves, which was either surrender or confidence. "Go on then, warden. Do your duty."

The crates held salt fish, iron nails, dyed wool—and beneath the false bottom of the third crate, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age, a book.

Not a book. A ledger, bound in leather so dark it was nearly black, its corners capped in tarnished silver. It was heavy in a way paper shouldn't be, the way things are heavy when a lot of people have died near them. The clasp bore a sigil I knew better than my own name, because I saw it every day: the thorn-and-wolf, the mark of the gate covenant, broken these hundred years and more.

"This isn't cargo," I said.

"No," Wren Vale agreed quietly. "It's not."

* * *

I should have taken it and turned them over to Highmoor. That was the procedure. Procedure was the spine I'd built my whole invisible life around—do the task, file the paper, keep your head down, want nothing. Nine years of wanting nothing.

Instead I sat across my own hearth from a smuggler while they drank my tea and told me about the ledger.

"It came out of the Ashwood vaults," they said. "Before the purge, when the last of the wolf-clan archivists went into exile, they hid everything they could carry. Most of it's been lost or burned. This surfaced in Vell's Landing two months ago, and the moment I saw what it was, I wasn't taking it to a buyer."

"What is it?"

"The Prophecies of Ash. Written a century ago, by the archivists who watched the covenant break." They set the cup down. "Warden. There's no gentle way to do this. Look at tonight's date. Then look at the page."

"Stop calling me warden. My name is Cyrus."

"Cyrus Zhou." They said it evenly, no flourish, and that was somehow worse. "Yes. I know."

My name. In their mouth, in this room, attached to knowledge I had never given anyone. I kept my face still because stillness was the only discipline I'd ever mastered, but something behind my ribs had begun, very quietly, to come apart.

They opened the ledger to a ribbon-marked page and turned it toward me.

The script was old gate-tongue, the formal hand they'd taught us to recognize and never read, and it took me a long moment to parse it. The ink had faded to the color of dried blood. The entry was short. Prophecies usually are; they can't afford decoration.

*On the night the fog comes early and the river runs low, the warden at Thornfell will be named in this book. Cyrus Zhou, child of the broken line, born of the keepers who shattered the covenant. On this night the last heir stops hiding.*

I read it twice. Three times. The fire popped. Outside, the fog pressed against the shutters like something listening.

"That date," Wren said softly, "is tonight."

"This is a trick." My voice came out flat and strange, someone else's voice. "A con. You found my name somewhere—garrison rolls, a tithe list—and you wrote—"

"The ink is a hundred years old, Cyrus. I've handled enough forged documents to know aging work when I see it, and this isn't it. And there's this." They reached into their coat and produced a second thing wrapped in cloth: a small stone disc, cracked, etched with the thorn-and-wolf. "It came with the ledger. The archivists' seal. It warms when it's near the bloodline of the keepers. It has been cold as riverstone since Vell's Landing."

They set it on the table between us.

It began to warm. I felt it before I touched it—a faint heat rising off it like breath, like something waking. When I finally put my fingers against it, the crack in its center seemed to pulse, once, in time with my heart.

The floor did not move. That was the terrible part. The room stayed exactly where it was, the fire stayed lit, my tea went cold in its cup, and none of it cared that the shape of my entire life had just been peeled up like wet paint. Nine years of being nobody. Of choosing the far watch, the empty nights, the reports written small so no one would look too closely. Not modesty. Armor. Because some part of me, some part I'd never examined because examining things was how you got found, had always known there was something to hide from.

"My parents," I heard myself say. "They never told me anything. They just—left. Went east when I was fourteen and didn't send word after the third winter."

"The keepers' children were scattered on purpose. After the covenant broke, anyone of the gatekeeper lines was a target—for the crown, who wanted them dead, and for the clans, who wanted them used." Wren's voice had gone quiet, careful, the voice of someone handling something fragile. "Your family ran further than most."

"And you? Why does a smuggler carry a dead people's scripture across a haunted border?"

Something crossed their face then—not guilt, exactly. Grief wearing guilt's coat. "Because I grew up on the other side of that gate. In the exile camps. I've watched three generations of wolf-clan children be born stateless and die hungry, waiting for a gate that only opens for blood or love, whatever that means, according to a prophecy nobody living can finish reading." They turned the ledger gently toward the final page—the only other marked page—and I saw that it was not empty. It held a few lines, and the last of them was smudged, unfinished, as if the writer had been interrupted mid-sentence by something that mattered very much.

"So I stole it," Wren said. "And I ran. And everyone who wants this book—the crown's inquisitors, the clan elders, the buyers in Vell's Landing—they all followed me here. To you. To the one place the prophecy says it all ends."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to. My legs simply informed me we were done sitting.

"If the inquisitors are following you," I said, "then they'll be here by—"

The fog outside went white. Not the soft grey white of marsh mist—the hard, sourceless white of witchlight, blooming beyond the willows, and under it came the sound I had spent nine years listening for and praying never to hear: horses, many, moving fast, and bells. Inquisitor bells, tuned to ring only in the presence of unbound covenant-magic.

Which meant me. Or the book. Or both.

Wren was already on their feet, already reaching for the ledger, and when our eyes met across my little table, in my little house at the edge of the world, I understood that whatever I chose in the next ten seconds would be the first honest choice of my life.

Arrest them. Take the book, bar the door, hand them both to the king's men in the morning, and go back to being nobody. Safe. Invisible. Dead, in every way that counted.

Or.

"There's a tunnel," I said, grabbing my staff and my father's old cloak off its peg, the cloak I'd never once worn, "under the hearthstone. My father built it. He never told me where it goes—but I think, tonight, I'm going to find out."

Wren Vale smiled, and it was not a comforting smile. It was the smile of someone stepping off a ledge and daring the world to catch them.

"After you, heir of Thornfell."

The witchlight flared against the windows as the hearthstone rolled aside, breathing up cold air from underneath the world.
