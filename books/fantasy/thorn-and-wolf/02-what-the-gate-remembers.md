## 2. What the Gate Remembers

**CYRUS**

The tunnel mouth opened behind the third shrine on the Pilgrim's Stair, exactly where the ledger's marginal sketch said it would be. Cyrus had walked past that shrine four hundred times in nine years of gate duty and had never once noticed the seam of worked stone beneath the lichen.

*You were never looking*, the ledger seemed to say. *None of you were.*

"Down," Wren whispered, and the darkness took them both.

Cyrus went first, because it was their gate, their mountain, their fault—whatever it was—and because standing still meant thinking about the page they'd torn free and burned at the last waystation. The page with their name. The page that was still ash in their memory, letter by letter: *the keeper unnamed shall stand at Thornfell when the thirteenth moon returns, and the gate shall know its blood.*

Tonight had been a thirteenth moon. They'd checked twice.

The stairs descended farther than any cellar had a right to. Cyrus counted two hundred steps, then stopped counting, then lost the count entirely when the walls began to change—rough quarry stone giving way to fitted blocks, the blocks giving way to carvings worn nearly smooth. But not quite smooth. Under Wren's shuttered lantern, faces emerged from the rock: men and women standing in a line, hand to hand, a human chain rendered in granite.

"What is this?" Wren asked quietly.

"I don't know." A lie. Or half of one. Because Cyrus did know, in the way you know your own heartbeat without ever having looked at it. "I think—I think these are my people."

They reached a landing where the tunnel split three ways, and above the center passage hung a carved lintel: an arch, a thorn tree, and a wolf passing through them both. Below it, in Old Script that Cyrus had only seen in sealed archives, a single word.

*Vashten.* Keepers.

Wren's lantern hand went very still. "Your name," she said. "In the ledger. Zhou."

"The gatekeeper families took common names after the Breaking," Cyrus heard themselves say. It came out flat, recited, like something learned young and never examined. "Zhou. Ashkari. Ondu. Merrow. Four lines sworn to hold the crossings closed until—" They stopped.

"Until what?"

"I don't remember. Nobody remembers. That's rather the point of us."

The silence that followed had weight. In it, Cyrus heard water dripping somewhere far below, and Wren breathing, and their own heart doing something unsteady.

"You've guarded a door your whole life," Wren said, "and never asked what it was for."

"It kept things out."

"It kept everything *in*." Her voice cracked on the word. She caught it, smoothed it, but Cyrus had spent nine years reading smugglers' faces across a customs table and knew what a slip cost her. "We'll come back to that."

They chose the center passage, under the wolf and the thorn.

* * *

**WREN**

The clans called the exile years *the Long Hunger*, and Wren had been raised on its arithmetic the way border children were raised on prayers: eleven thousand wolves crossed the night the gates sealed. Nine hundred survived the first winter. Fewer than six thousand remained now, in a territory too small for a tenth of them, ringed by realms that had forgotten they existed—or worse, remembered perfectly well.

She'd told herself she wouldn't tell the gatekeeper any of this. The gatekeeper was leverage, a key with legs, a means to an end the clan mothers had paid her dearly to reach. Telling him about the hunger was handing a knife to the family that had forged the blade.

But he'd stood in front of that lintel with his hand pressed flat against the carved wolf like he was greeting kin, and something in Wren's chest had come loose.

So in the long dark between landmarks, walking behind his surefooted silhouette, she talked.

"There's a valley north of the Ashfang range," she said. "Good grass, good water, no game—we killed the last of the deer thirty years ago. The children there eat moss-bread in winter. Real bread twice a year, if the traders are honest, which they aren't." Her boots found the next step. "The clan mothers keep a census. Every pup born gets a mark; every death takes one away. My mother used to read me the numbers like bedtime stories. Eleven thousand became nine. Nine became seven. You learn to hear those numbers as people you might have been."

Cyrus hadn't spoken in a while. When he finally did, his voice was careful. "The ledger said the covenant broke. It didn't say who it broke against."

"Against us. Obviously." Bitterness came easy and she let it. "Your four families sealed the crossings and wrote the terms so the other side couldn't survive them. Then your kings burned your own histories so even the jailers forgot they were jailers. Convenient."

"That's not—" He stopped. Started again. "I believe you. That's the worst part. I believe every word, and I've been standing at that gate for nine years feeling proud of myself."

Something in Wren wanted to press the advantage, twist the guilt into compliance. Something older and more tired just wanted him to keep believing her. She chose the tired thing. It frightened her more than the inquisitors did.

"They're close," she said instead. "Behind us. Two hours, maybe less. Your Captain Vess doesn't travel slow."

"You know Vess?"

"She interrogated me once. I bit her." Wren smiled at the dark ahead of them. "It seemed polite."

He laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly strangled, like laughter wasn't in his job description. Wren found she wanted to hear it again and hated wanting things.

* * *

**CYRUS**

The chamber, when they found it, took Cyrus's remaining certainty apart and spread the pieces on the floor.

It was round, vaulted, older than anything the king claimed to own. Four alcoves ringed a central dais where a block of black stone sat like a sleeping animal—the covenant-stone, Cyrus understood without being told, the way you understand your own name. And on the walls, in paint that had somehow refused a century of dark, ran a frieze: the four families, hand in hand around the arch, and beyond them—

Beyond them, figures with wolf-shadowed silhouettes, standing on the *other side* of the arch. Not enemies. Counterparts. The frieze showed them trading: grain for pelts, iron for song, a child held up over the boundary line to be blessed by hands on both sides.

"This isn't a wall," Cyrus said. His voice sounded foreign to himself. "It was never a wall. It was a *door*. We were supposed to open it."

"And your kings preferred a border," Wren said. She stood at the frieze's edge, arms crossed, not touching it—as if she didn't trust herself to. "The covenant needed all four families to hold it. Break one line, starve one side, and the whole thing cracks. Somebody very clever figured that out a century ago."

"Somebody very clever," Cyrus repeated. He touched the black stone. It was warm. It should not have been warm; nothing down here had been warm in a hundred years. Beneath his palm, faint as a second pulse, something answered.

And in that answering pulse he felt the ledger's truth settle into his bones like silt finding a riverbed: *the gate shall know its blood*. Not a threat. An inheritance. The last heir of four murdered lines, standing in the house nobody had told him he owned.

"Cyrus." Wren's voice, low. Not her smuggler's purr—something plainer underneath it. "There are torches in the passage we came by. Vess is faster than I thought."

He turned. She had her knife drawn—not aimed at him, aimed at the dark behind them—and she was watching him with an expression he'd spent nine years learning to read across a customs table and had never once seen on her face until tonight.

Fear. For him, or for what he was becoming, or possibly for the terrible fact that she had told him true things she could have withheld.

"How much time?" he asked.

"Minutes."

Cyrus put his hand back on the warm stone and felt the covenant stir like a thing remembering it had a name. "Then we run," he said. "And Wren—when we get where we're going, you tell me the rest. All of it. The parts you almost didn't say."

Her jaw tightened. "Why?"

"Because you chose honesty back there, and I intend to make it expensive."

For one heartbeat she looked like she might laugh or cut him. Then torchlight bloomed in the passage mouth, and together they ran toward the ruined covenant-stone above, where the prophecy waited with its cruel arithmetic—and where, neither of them yet knew it, the price would finally be named.
