## 3. The Price of the Covenant

Wren had stolen a lot of things in her life. Ledgers, cargo, a watch off a sleeping magistrate once, though she'd returned that one out of spite rather than conscience. But she'd never stolen anything as heavy as hope, and she was carrying it now, up the switchback trail to the covenant-stone with Cyrus Zhou walking two paces behind her like a person who didn't know how to walk beside someone.

She'd tried to give them room for three days. Room to grieve the life they'd lost—the quiet gatehouse, the nameless routine, the beautiful anonymity of being nobody. But grief made Cyrus slow, and slow got people caught, and so Wren had stopped being gentle somewhere around the second river crossing and started being honest instead.

"You're brooding," she said now, not turning around.

"I'm thinking."

"You're brooding *loudly*. I can hear it over the wind."

Cyrus caught up. The trail narrowed here, hugging the cliff above the treeline, and they walked shoulder to shoulder because there was nowhere else to be. Below them, the valley of Thornfell spread out in autumn gold, and beyond it—just visible through the haze—the black seam of the gate itself, sealed these hundred years, humming faintly to anyone who knew how to listen.

Wren knew how to listen. She'd grown up on the other side of it, in the exile camps where the wolf-clans had been penned since the breaking of the covenant, starving politely, dying impolitely, waiting for a keeper who never came. Her mother had died still believing one would. Wren had stopped believing at fourteen and started smuggling at fifteen, which was the same thing wearing different clothes.

"The stone's just past the ridge," she said. "Old meeting-place. My grandmother swore oaths on it before the clans were driven out."

"And you're sure the ledger meant this place?"

"I'm sure the ledger meant *you*, and you're sure the ledger meant tonight, and neither of us is sure of anything else." She glanced sideways at them. "That's usually how prophecy works. It gives you exactly enough rope."

Cyrus said nothing, but their hand drifted toward hers on the narrow path—a small thing, half an inch of contact, knuckle against knuckle—and stayed there the rest of the climb. Wren counted that half inch as the most valuable thing she'd ever carried across a border.

* * *

The covenant-stone stood in a clearing like a broken tooth: twelve feet of granite split down its center, the halves leaning apart, moss-choked, carved all over with script so old the letters had gone soft-edged as river stones. Wren had seen it once as a child, from a distance, when her clan still remembered the way. Now she touched it and felt the cold come up through her fingers like something alive and patient underneath.

Cyrus knelt first. Of course they did. They knelt the way they did everything—carefully, completely, as if kneeling were a discipline and not a posture. They brushed the moss from the base of the stone and found what Wren had promised them was there: the gatekeeper's mark, a thorn wrapped around a wolf's tooth, cut deep enough to survive a century of weather.

"It's my family's," Cyrus said quietly. "I've seen it on my grandmother's ring. I thought—I always thought it was just jewelry."

"Nothing is just anything anymore."

They were reading the script together, Cyrus sounding out the old glyphs while Wren translated the ones she knew from camp-songs, when the wind changed.

It came down off the ridge wrong—too warm, too deliberate, carrying woodsmoke and steel oil. Wren felt it before she understood it, the smuggler's instinct arriving ahead of thought, and by the time she turned she could already see them: four figures in grey coming down the far side of the clearing, moving with the unhurried certainty of people who had been told exactly where to be.

Inquisitors. The king's own. And leading them, tall and silver-collared, with a face like a closed door—

"Inquisitor Halloway," Cyrus breathed.

"You know him?"

"He interrogated me for six hours after the ledger theft. He let me go. I used to think he'd made a mistake." Cyrus looked at Wren, and their voice went flat and terrible. "He didn't make a mistake. He followed me. He wanted me to lead him here."

* * *

There was no time to run and no cover worth running to, so they did the only thing left: they stood at the stone and waited, backs to each other, Wren facing the treeline and Cyrus facing the ridge, and let the inquisitors come to them.

Halloway arrived first, as leaders do, and he arrived talking, which Wren had learned to distrust more than violence. "Keeper Zhou," he said pleasantly. "You've saved me a great deal of trouble. All I needed was for you to understand what you are. The rest—" he gestured at the stone, at the gate beyond it, at the whole wounded architecture of the covenant "—the rest was always going to happen."

"What do you want?" Cyrus asked.

"The same thing the prophecy wants. The gate opened. It has been sealed too long; the king's scholars agree the imbalance between realms is souring the land, the harvests, the weather. The crown requires the Thornfell gate *open*. What the crown does not require"—and here Halloway smiled, thin and surgical—"is a free crossing. A gate under keeper's control is a door. A gate under the king's control is a wall with a hinge."

"So open it yourself."

"I can't." Halloway spread his hands, almost apologetic. "Only a keeper can. That's the cruelty of your inheritance, Keeper. Your ancestors broke the covenant, and the breaking wrote terms into the stone itself. We've read them. Every scholar who's touched this script has read them. The gate opens only for a keeper's blood or a keeper's love. Blood spilled upon the stone, or love freely given and bound to the crossing. Those are the terms. There has never been a third option."

Wren heard it and felt the ground tilt. Not the words—she'd guessed the shape of them from the ledger, from the songs, from every story her mother told about the last night of the old world. What tilted her was watching Cyrus hear them.

Because Cyrus went still. Completely, terribly still, the way water goes still before it freezes, and Wren—who had spent three days learning every version of this person's face—watched the stillness resolve into a decision.

"No," Wren said aloud, before Cyrus said anything at all. "No, whatever you're about to—"

"My blood opens it." Cyrus turned to face her, and their voice cracked straight down the middle. "A keeper's blood. Mine. It's the clean term. No politics, no king holding the hinge, no clans owing anyone anything. The gate opens, the covenant reforges, the exile ends, and I—" They stopped. Swallowed. Started again, smaller. "And I don't have to choose between the name I was born to and the person I—"

"Don't." Wren stepped forward, into the space between Cyrus and the stone, as if her body could block the sentence. "Don't finish that. Don't you dare finish that."

"Wren—"

"*I saw you.*" It came out of her louder than she meant, louder than was safe with four armed men listening, and she didn't care. "Six years I moved through that border town and nobody saw me—everyone saw the cargo, the lies, the useful stranger. Then you stopped me on the road with your hand on my wrist and you looked at me like I was a *person*, and I have been falling ever since, and you think I will stand here and watch you bleed out on a rock to spare me a hard conversation? You think that's love? That's surrender dressed up as nobility and I will not—"

Halloway raised a hand, and his three inquisitors leveled crossbows, and none of it mattered, because the argument had already left the ground and gone somewhere weapons couldn't follow.

Cyrus reached out and put their palm flat against the covenant-stone, against the thorn-and-tooth mark, and the stone answered. Warmth bled up through the granite, ancient and slow, and the old script began to glow along its channels like embers remembering fire. The gate in the distance hummed louder. The air between the halves of the stone shimmered.

"It knows me," Cyrus whispered. "It's been waiting. It's been waiting a hundred years for someone of my blood to touch it and offer."

"Then we both offer or neither of us does." Wren put her hand beside theirs on the stone, close enough that their fingers overlapped the carving, hers and theirs tangled over the thorn-and-tooth. "Two hands. One offering. If the terms want love, then give it love—not a corpse, not a martyrdom. Give it the real thing, standing up, both of us alive, refusing to pick which one of us the world gets to keep."

"That's not how the terms work—"

"Then we break the terms." Wren's voice dropped low and fierce. "Your family broke a covenant once, Cyrus. Apparently it runs in the blood. Break this one better."

The glow surged. The stone groaned. Somewhere below, the seam of the gate split light down its length, and Halloway's composure finally fractured—he shouted orders, the crossbows came up again, and the clearing filled with the sound of the world deciding what it was going to allow.

And Cyrus, hand locked with Wren's on the waking stone, blood-warm and terrified and more themselves than they had ever been, said the thing that would carry them into whatever came next:

"Then witness it. Both of us. Living."
