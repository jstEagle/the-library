## 1. The Silent Tithe

Salt doesn't weigh much until you've carried it over three ridgelines with a bad knee and a worse conscience. Then it weighs like sin.

I'd been hauling salt through Thornfell for six years now, which is the kind of sentence that sounds like a confession and functions as one. Salt out of the flats at Greyharrow, up through the pine dark of the fells, down to the villages that couldn't afford the crown's tariff. Smuggling, if you asked a magistrate. Charity, if you asked me. Nobody asked either of us much anymore. The crown had stopped asking questions and started issuing lists.

The mule was named Duchess, which she resented, and she carried most of the weight, which she also resented. We understood each other the way two tired things understand each other: poorly, but without lies.

It was the third evening of the crossing when I came to the shrine.

You could miss it if you weren't looking — that was rather the point of it. A shelf of grey stone tucked into the hillside where the smuggler's path bent around an old birch, half-swallowed by moss, crowned with a lintel so weathered the carving on it had gone soft as melted wax. It might once have been a face. It might once have been a hand. Nine years of rain will do that to a god.

Veyra. The Quiet One, they'd called him, back when quiet meant peace instead of absence. God of song, of thresholds, of the moment between one breath and the next. God of mine, once. God of a choir of thirty-one voices who were singing his name at high noon on a bright spring morning when he simply — stopped. Mid-prayer. Mid-*word*, if I'm honest, because I was holding the lead line and the word was *come* and I never got to finish it.

Thirty-one voices, cut off like a door closing. And then nothing. No thunder, no judgment, no explanation. Just silence rolling down off the mountain like an avalanche made of nothing at all, and thirty-one singers standing in a chapel with their mouths still open, waiting for a god who wasn't there.

I set down my pack at the shrine because I always do, even though I tell myself every year that this will be the year I walk past.

The tithe was wordless. That's not piety; that's cowardice dressed up as discipline. A proper offering to Veyra would have been sung — a line of hymn, a bar of grace, anything given voice. But I hadn't sung in nine years. Not a note. Not in the bath, not drunk, not alone on a ridge with no one but Duchess to hear. Singing was how the gift worked, and the gift was how my choir died, or at least how they died *near* me, and I had decided — in the way you decide things at twenty-four, with blood in your ears and ash on your hands — that if I never sang again, nothing else would ever fall silent mid-word.

So I paid him in silence. A pinch of salt from the top of the sack, poured into the stone basin where rainwater had gathered. Salt for preservation. Salt for tears, if you wanted to be poetic about it, and Veyra had always liked poetry. He'd been a god who wept easily, according to the old stories. A god who heard a good harmony and had to sit down.

"Still here," I said to the basin. Words were allowed. Words weren't the gift. "Don't know why."

The rainwater didn't answer. It never did. But I stood there longer than I needed to, the way you stand at a grave of someone you're angry at, because leaving too quickly would mean admitting you came for comfort, and comfort wasn't something I'd earned.

Nine years. Long enough for the guilt to stop screaming and settle into something quieter and more permanent, like tinnitus. Long enough to learn that survival isn't a choice you make once. It's a choice you make every morning, and every morning it gets a little easier to make, and that ease is its own kind of wound.

I shouldered the pack. Duchess sighed the way only mules and old soldiers can.

"Come on," I told her. "Two more days to Marrowgate, and then we're rich enough to be miserable indoors."

She did not find this funny. Mules rarely do.

* * *

I smelled the smoke before I saw the village.

That's the thing about Thornfell in autumn — everything smells faintly of woodsmoke anyway, hearth-fires and leaf-burn, so it takes a while for your nose to sort the ordinary smoke from the other kind. The other kind has fat in it. Wet thatch. Something sweeter underneath, like resin, like pitch, like the accelerant the crown wardens favor because it burns hot and fast and leaves nothing worth burying.

I left Duchess tied in the alder copse and went up the ridge on foot, because I'm a fool, and fools look.

Cinderhollow sat in the valley below like a dropped toy. Forty roofs, maybe, a mill, a well, the kind of village that exists on maps only as a smudge. And in its center square, where any village keeps its shrine, there was fire.

Not the whole village. Not yet. Just the shrine — a proper one, timber-framed, carved, the kind communities build over generations, each family adding a beam or a bell or a painted panel. It went up like it had been waiting all its life for this, flames climbing the eaves in orange sheets, and around it stood six crown wardens in their grey-and-gold, watching with the professional patience of men doing a chore.

The Edict of Ash had come down from Verreholm eight months ago. Every shrine to the old gods to be purged. Every image of Veyra especially — though the edict hadn't named him, everyone knew. You don't hunt the gifted and leave their god's face on the walls. That would be like burning the birds and sparing the nest.

A warden pulled a painted panel from the fire's edge — some saint's face, some singer's, blackening at the corners — and held it up to the light like a man appraising a fish, then fed it back into the flames.

There were villagers watching. Of course there were. An old woman with her arms wrapped around herself. Two children behind her skirts, the small one crying in the flat, exhausted way of children who have learned this particular lesson already. A man — the miller, maybe, flour still dusting his forearms — who took one step forward and was stopped by nothing at all, by the mere arithmetic of six armed men and forty unarmed ones.

Nobody moved. Nobody spoke. The fire crackled and ate, and the wardens checked their list, and the village watched its god burn, and I watched the village.

From the ridge. Behind a birch tree. With salt on my hands.

Here is what I want you to understand about that moment, because I have turned it over nine hundred times since and it hasn't gotten cleaner: I could have done something.

Not much. I'll grant that. Six wardens, one smuggler, no weapon but a skinning knife and a mule with opinions. But I had a voice. I had the gift — buried, rusted, sworn off, but *there*. I felt it the way you feel an old break before rain, a deep ache under the breastbone, something turning over in its sleep. One note, pitched right, shaped by nine years of suppressed longing into something with edges — I could have cracked every arrow shaft in their quivers. Could have shattered the warden-captain's sword in its scabbard. Could have done *something*, made them look up at the ridge, made the children see that someone, somewhere, had objected.

Instead I did the math. Six against one. A cargo of salt that fed four villages through winter. A face that would go on a list — and the lists, by then, were not metaphorical. People whose names appeared on the Edict rolls stopped writing letters. Their houses developed new owners. Their dogs came to the roadsides to wait for them, patiently, for weeks.

I did the math, and the math said hide, and I hid, and the fire burned, and I memorized the old woman's face because some part of me knew I would need it later, for the nights, for the ledger I kept in the dark behind my eyes where I recorded every debt of cowardice with interest accruing.

The wardens mounted up. One of them laughed at something — I heard it clear across the valley, laughter carrying the way it does in cold air — and they rode north toward the pass, unhurried, men with their day's work done.

The villagers began pulling beams from the fire. Salvage. That's what grief looks like in poor country: you save what you can carry and you pretend the saving was the point.

I stayed on the ridge until full dark, which was stupid, because cold kills faster than conscience, and then I went down and got Duchess and walked the opposite direction from Cinderhollow, because walking toward it would have meant looking at faces, and I had used up all my looking for one day.

I told myself the usual things. *One man can't stop an edict.* True. *The gift is gone, rusted shut, useless.* Mostly true. *If Veyra wouldn't lift a finger for his own choir, why should I lift one for strangers?* That one I'd told myself so many times it had worn smooth as river stone, and it was also the worst lie of the three, because Veyra hadn't lifted a finger because Veyra was *gone*, and I was not gone, I was right here, breathing, choosing, every single morning, to keep being the man who hid.

Duchess stopped at the fork where the smuggler's path branched — left along the river, right up toward the high caves. She stopped and turned her head and looked at me with one wet dark eye, and I swear on whatever's left of the gods that the look was reproachful.

"Don't," I said. "You weren't there. You don't get a vote."

She flicked an ear. Somewhere upslope, wind moved through the pines with a sound almost like — no. Not almost like. I'd spent nine years training myself out of hearing music in wind. Wind was wind. Water was water. Silence was silence, and I lived in it the way a snail lives in its shell, carried it everywhere, called it safety.

We took the right-hand path, up toward the caves, because the river route passed a warden waystation and I'd seen enough grey-and-gold for one lifetime. The cave system above the treeline was an old smuggler's rest — dry chambers, a hidden entrance, soot-blackened stone where decades of runners had warmed their hands. I knew it the way you know a scar.

The snow started an hour after sundown, fat and silent, the kind of snowfall that erases the world politely, flake by flake. Good traveling weather, for my trade. Bad for everything else.

I was maybe two hundred yards from the cave mouth when Duchess stopped again, dead still, ears forward, and this time I heard it too.

Singing.

No — not quite. Humming. A thread of melody coming from inside the cave, thin as wire, wavering, half-lost under the wind. It should have been impossible. Nobody used these caves in autumn; the passes were closed; the smuggler season was over. And yet.

And yet the melody reached me across the snow, and something in my chest — something I had bricked over, mortared, painted a nice respectable sign on reading *NOTHING IN HERE BUT SALT AND REGRET* — something in my chest *turned over*.

Because I knew the tune.

It was a Veyran grace. A table blessing, a child's prayer, the first hymn every choir-child learned. *Keep us through the night, keep us through the threshold, hold our voices in your keeping till the morning comes.* I had sung it ten thousand times. I had led it the morning my world ended. And no one had sung it aloud in Thornfell for nine years, because singing it aloud was exactly the kind of thing that got your name on a list.

The humming wavered. Broke. Started again, weaker, slurring at the edges — the sound of someone freezing, spending the last of their warmth on a prayer because a prayer was all they had left.

I stood in the falling snow with the reins going slack in my hands, and every rule I'd built, every vow, every morning's careful choice, stood in a row behind my teeth like a choir waiting for the downbeat.

"No," I said out loud, to myself, to the snow, to the brickwork around my heart.

The humming faltered again — and beneath it, so faint I could have imagined it, so faint I *did* spend three full seconds convincing myself I'd imagined it, I heard something else. Under the melody. Under the wind. Down deep, the way you hear a river under ice.

A voice, vast and ragged and impossibly far away, screaming a single unfinished word.

*Come.*

Duchess stepped forward. Traitor. Or maybe just the only honest creature on that mountain.

I followed her into the dark of the cave, toward the voice in the snow, and I have never in my life been more afraid of what I might find — or of how badly I wanted to find it.
