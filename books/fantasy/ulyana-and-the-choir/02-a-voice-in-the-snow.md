## 2. A Voice in the Snow

There are three rules to smuggling salt through Thornfell in winter, and I had learned all of them the expensive way. One: never take the high pass after the first snowfall, no matter how short it looks on a map drawn by someone who has never actually been there. Two: never trust a mule that doesn't complain — a quiet mule is a dying mule. And three: if you hear singing in the mountains of Thornfell, you walk the other way, because either it's a ghost, or it's a warden lure, or it's something worse, and none of those options end with you keeping your salt.

I was breaking rule one when I heard the singing.

The storm had come down off the peaks like a debt collector, sudden and personal, and I'd abandoned the pass for a smuggler's cave I knew from my younger, stupider years — a fissure in the limestone above the treeline that went back far enough to hold a fire and a man's regrets without either getting wet. I'd cached rope and pitch there once, nine years ago, for purposes I no longer let myself think about. The cache was long gone, probably, but the cave would keep me from dying stupidly, which at my age counts as ambition.

The mule — a gray, opinionated creature named Duchess who complained constantly and was therefore in perfect health — planted her hooves at the cave mouth and refused to go further. That should have been my first warning. Duchess had good instincts about trouble; she'd once refused to cross a bridge an hour before it washed away, and she'd held it over me ever since, metaphorically speaking, insofar as mules hold anything.

Then I heard it. Under the wind. Under the hiss of snow against stone.

Humming.

Not a warden's whistle-lure — those are sharp, military, designed to sound like lost travelers, and they always land their notes too neatly, like soldiers marching in song. This was ragged. Human. A girl's voice, thin as thread, humming a rising line over and over again, the way you hum when your teeth are chattering too hard to do anything else. Humming to stay awake. Humming to stay *alive*.

Every sensible part of me said: walk away. You have salt. You have a mule. You have nine years of carefully constructed nothing, a life built like a dry-stone wall, no mortar, just weight upon weight, and you know exactly what happens to walls when something starts singing at them.

But the sensible parts of me have always been outvoted by the stupid ones, and the stupid ones remembered that humming is what people do when they're freezing, and that half-frozen people don't last the night up here, and that if I walked away I'd spend the rest of my life hearing it anyway.

So I did what any self-respecting coward does. I told myself I was only going to look.

* * *

She was deeper in the cave than I expected, past the first bend where the limestone opened into a chamber big enough to stand in, and she'd done something impossible before I even saw her face.

The ice was melting.

Frost coated every surface of that cave — it coats everything above the treeline in Thornfell, breath freezes on the walls in lacework, and the floor was glazed where meltwater had refrozen in sheets. But around the girl, in a rough circle maybe six feet across, the stone was bare and dark and *wet*, steam curling off it faintly, like spring had been convinced to make a single exception. Ice on the ceiling above her wept in fat drops. She sat in the middle of it wrapped in a soaked wool cloak, knees to chest, shivering so hard the humming came out in pieces, and every piece of it pushed the cold back another inch.

I stopped so fast Duchess walked into me.

Because I knew that trick. Not the shape of it — hers was small, instinctive, a candle to what I'd seen — but the *mechanism*. Song-magic. Voice given to the world instead of kept in the chest, the world listening and answering. Nine years since I'd felt it. Nine years since the last time I'd heard it, mid-hymn, in a mountain chapel, right before the god we were singing to fell silent and took eleven voices down with him into whatever silence gods fall into.

I'd buried that gift so deep I'd told myself it was dead. Told myself so often I'd almost believed it. And here was a girl of maybe sixteen thawing ice with a lullaby, casual as breathing, and every buried thing in me turned over in its grave and *knocked*.

"You're leaking," I said. My voice came out steadier than I deserved.

Her head snapped up. Wild eyes, dark hair crusted with frost, a cut on her cheekbone gone white at the edges with cold. She scrambled backward until the wet stone hit her spine, and her humming — this was the remarkable part — her humming didn't stop. It changed. Went lower, went somewhere behind her ribs, and the temperature in the cave dropped like a door opening onto winter itself.

"That's far enough," she said. Her lips were blue. Her voice cracked on both words.

"I'm not a warden."

"Everyone says that." The frost crept toward me across the floor, glittering, deliberate. "Wardens say it. Tax men say it. The man in Hallowford said it right up until he grabbed my arm." Something moved under her skin then, some pressure looking for an exit, and the ice sheet nearest me groaned and split with a sound like a rifle crack.

"Fine," I said, and I did the only thing that has ever reliably made me less threatening: I sat down, slowly, in the snow at the cave mouth, well outside her circle of summer, and started unlacing my pack. "If I were a warden I'd have brought friends, and friends would be standing behind me getting frostbite while I chat. It's just me and a mule. Her name's Duchess. She'll want payment in oats if you're going to point weather at her."

The frost hesitated. Curious. Cold is curious; it's one of its better qualities.

"You're bleeding," she said, nodding at my hands, which had split along old scars in the cold the way they do every winter and which I had not noticed because I am very good at not noticing things.

"Salt work."

"Salt smuggler."

"Salt *logistics consultant*."

That startled something out of her that might have been a laugh, quickly strangled, like she wasn't sure laughing was allowed anymore. The frost pulled back an inch. And because I am a fool with a soft spot roughly the size of Thornfell, I dug in my pack, found the waxed bundle with my emergency rations — hard cheese, black bread, a twist of dried cherries I'd been saving for a day I'd earned them — and slid it across the stone toward the edge of her warmth.

"Eat," I said. "Then tell me why a girl with a talent that could buy her a house in Marrowgate is sitting in a cave above the treeline, melting ice with her throat, instead of anywhere with a roof."

She looked at the food the way starving people look at food, which is to say she pretended not to look at it at all. Then she dragged herself forward, snatched it, and ate like the dead rise — bread first, then cheese in enormous bites, then the cherries, which stopped her entirely. She held one cherry up and looked at it.

"My grandmother dried these," she said, and her voice did something young and breaking. "Before the wardens came."

And there it was. I'd known it since the frost. Wardens don't hunt girls who can carry a tune; wardens hunt girls whose tunes *do things*. King Aldric's Edict had rolled over Thornfell like weather over the last two seasons — lists of names, burned shrines, the gifted dragged south in iron-collared caravans, and nobody talked about what happened at the other end of those caravans because nobody came back to say. I'd spent nine years making myself invisible precisely so that no list would ever find my name on it.

"What's your name?" I asked.

A long pause. The wind filled it.

"Ulyana."

"And what are you running *from*, Ulyana?"

She swallowed the last of the bread. When she answered, the humming had stopped for the first time, and I swear the cave got colder for it, like her voice had been the only thing holding the season politely outside.

"Wardens. Three of them. Maybe four days behind me." She hugged her arms around her knees. "They killed my grandmother. They burned our shrine. And they're hunting me because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter, in the tone of someone testing whether a floor will hold their weight. "Because I hear him."

"Hear who?"

The word, when it came, hit me like ice water poured directly down the spine.

"*Veyra.*"

* * *

I want to be clear about what happened inside me in that moment, because from the outside I imagine I looked like nothing at all — a smuggler sitting in a snowy cave mouth, chewing on nothing, wearing the blank expression of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like.

Inside, nine years of careful masonry came down.

Veyra. The silent god. The god of the small prayers, the hearth-prayers, the god whose shrines stood at crossroads and wellsides because he wasn't a god of thunder or harvest or war but of the moment when a tired person says *let this be enough*. My god. I'd sung for him for six years with a choir of twelve — twelve voices braided into one prayer-line, and it had worked, that was the obscene thing, it had actually *worked*, we could feel the prayers travel, feel them received. And then one morning in his own mountain chapel, mid-hymn, mid-*word*, he went silent. The prayer-line snapped back through us like a cut cable. Eleven singers died with the backlash. I survived because I was carrying the lead line alone at that moment, apart from the braid, and the snap broke something in me instead of stopping my heart.

Afterward I'd sworn the oath every survivor swears and means and breaks: never again. No song-magic. No choir. No god. I'd ground my gift down to nothing through sheer refusal, starved it, buried it under salt routes and ledger books and a mule, and for nine years it had stayed buried, and I'd called that peace.

Now a half-frozen girl in a stolen cave said Veyra's name like she knew him, and every stone of that wall shifted, and underneath, faint as a pulse in a wrist, something of mine *stirred*.

It was terror. There's no honest way to dress it up. Terror, and underneath the terror, worse, *longing* — the specific longing of a man who has missed something he swore he hated. My chest ached the way a missing limb aches. Somewhere below my sternum, in the place where the lead line used to live, something turned over and reached upward like a root finding water, and I wanted — gods help me, I *wanted* — to hum. To answer her. To sing one note and see if the world still listened.

I did not sing. I gripped my pack strap until my knuckles cracked, and I said, in the flattest voice I own:

"He's dead. Or gone. Whatever he was, he's not hearing anyone now. I'd know."

Ulyana stared at me. Frost-blue eyes, fever-bright. "How would you know?"

"Because I was there," I said, "when he stopped. And I've spent nine years being very certain about it."

Something passed over her face then — recognition, or pity, or that awful tenderness the young sometimes aim at the broken, which is worse than contempt. She crawled forward to the edge of her melted circle, close enough that I could see the cut on her cheek had started bleeding again, and she spoke low, urgent, the way people speak when they think time is shorter than it is.

"He's not silent," she said. "*He's screaming.* Down under the world. I've heard him since I was small — everyone thought it was dreams, thought I was touched — but he screams, and lately it's gotten louder, and the wardens aren't hunting gifted girls for the Edict, they're hunting *me specifically*, because I'm the only one who hears him and they don't want—"

Outside, Duchess screamed.

Not complained. *Screamed* — the full-throated terror-bray of a prey animal that has smelled predators, and it cut Ulyana off mid-word, and we were both moving before conscious thought caught up. I snuffed my little pitch-flare with my boot. Ulyana killed her humming like pinching out a wick, and the warmth died around us so fast my breath went to fog instantly, and the cold rushed in like it had been waiting politely at the door all along.

Torchlight, down-slope. Two points of it, maybe three, bobbing through the snowfall, coming up the switchbacks toward the cave. And threaded under the wind, faint but unmistakable, a whistle-lure — sharp, military, landing every note too neatly.

Warden callsign. *Come out, little bird.*

Ulyana's hand found my sleeve in the dark. Her fingers were still freezing. And she whispered, so quietly I nearly lost it under the wind, words that I would spend a long time afterward wishing I hadn't heard, because they made walking away impossible, and she must have known that, and I have decided to forgive her for it:

"They're not here for the Edict," she breathed. "Tamsin Zhou. They're here for *us*."

I hadn't told her my name.
