## 3. Wardens at the Waystation

The waystation had a name once, probably. Something dignified, like *Haven's Rest* or *Traveler's Hope*, painted on a sign that some enterprising smuggler had since pried loose to sell as firewood. What remained was a stone shell squatting in the mountain pass like a drunk uncle at a wedding—still standing, technically, but only out of spite.

I loved it immediately.

"Two nights," Tamsin said, shouldering through the door and kicking snow off his boots. "Then we move. You're still limping."

"I'm *striding*. Striding with character."

"You're favoring your left ankle and pretending the wince is a smile."

He was right, which I resented. The fall down the scree two days ago had turned my ankle into something purple and opinionated, and every step reminded me of it. But I'd been raised on stories where the heroine never complained, so I complained constantly, just quietly, mostly to myself, sometimes out loud when I wanted to watch Tamsin's jaw tighten.

It was one of my few entertainments. He was not an entertaining man. He was a tall, gray-tempered smuggler who spoke in the clipped syllables of someone rationing his own words, and who had looked at me—half-frozen, humming, full of impossible claims about screaming gods—with the expression of a man reading a letter he already knew would ruin him.

He'd come anyway. That counted for something. I hadn't decided what yet.

The waystation's great room held a dead hearth, three broken tables, and a loft above with straw that smelled of mice but at least didn't smell of wolves. Tamsin built a fire with the efficiency of a man who'd done it ten thousand times and enjoyed none of them. I dragged our bedrolls near it and lay down with my ankle propped on my pack, and outside, the wind came down the pass like something with intent.

"Tell me again," Tamsin said, "why they're hunting you."

"Because I hummed."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the whole answer. I hummed. In Drenmark, at the winter market. Just a little thing my mother used to sing, three notes, nothing." I watched the fire instead of him. "A warden heard it. And then there were six wardens, and then there was a bell rung backward—you know how a bell sounds wrong when it's rung backward? Like the world hiccupped?—and the innkeeper's daughter told me there was a back door, and I ran, and I've been running for eleven days." I paused. "Twelve. I lose count when I'm frightened."

"And the god," Tamsin said. "Veyra. You hear Veyra."

The way he said the name—it wasn't a question so much as a wound being pressed.

"I hear him screaming," I said. "Under the world. Under everything. Like a voice through a wall, if the wall were the size of the sky." I finally looked at him. "You don't believe me."

"I believe you hear *something*." He fed another stick to the fire without looking up. "I knew Veyra. Nine years ago. And Veyra isn't screaming anywhere, because Veyra is gone. Whatever's left is echo, or madness, or a trap." His jaw did the tightening thing. "I've seen gifted voices call things up out of mountains that wear your dead mother's face. The scream might be real. It might also be bait."

"And if it's real?"

"Then it's worse."

That was the most he'd said all day, and it exhausted him visibly, like a man lifting something too heavy for show. He wrapped himself in his cloak by the far wall, facing the door, because of course he slept facing the door, and within minutes his breathing went slow and even, though I suspected the evenness was performed. Smugglers, I was learning, performed even their sleep.

I lay awake listening to the wind and, beneath the wind, faint and terrible and constant, the screaming. It had gotten louder since I'd found Tamsin. I didn't tell him that. Some truths are gifts you give people gradually, or never.

* * *

I woke because the birds stopped.

Not flew away—stopped. Mid-song. One moment the pass was full of small feathered gossip, and the next it was silent in the specific, pressurized way of a held breath, and my body understood before my mind did: *someone is coming.*

I sat up. My ankle screamed. Outside, through the frost-furred window, the morning fog was peeling off the slopes, and in it—shapes. Dark shapes, moving in formation, fanning across the track below the station in the practiced geometry of men who had done this many times and found it profitable.

"Tamsin." I didn't whisper; whispering wastes the seconds between sleeping and dying. "Tamsin, wake up, there are—"

He was already awake. Already moving. Of course he was. He crossed the room in three strides and flattened himself against the wall beside the window, angling his head to see without presenting anything worth shooting, and I thought, absurdly, *he even hides beautifully*, before terror caught up with me and made my hands shake.

"Wardens?" he asked.

"Six. Maybe more in the fog."

"Crown colors?"

"I can't see colors in fog, Tamsin, I can barely see *shapes*—"

But then the fog thinned for a moment, the way fog does, maliciously, precisely when it wants to show you something, and I saw them clearly: gray cloaks, black badges, and at their center a banner I knew from a hundred village walls. A silver crown over crossed keys. The Edict of Ash, stitched in thread the color of dried blood.

Tamsin exhaled through his nose. It was the closest he came to swearing.

"Back way?" I asked.

"Flooded. Spring melt. We'd be chest-deep in freezing water and visible for half a mile."

"Front way?"

"Six wardens."

"So we're agreed the options are terrible."

"We're agreed the options are terrible." He pulled a knife from his boot, considered it, put it back. "They haven't rushed us yet. They're waiting for the rest of their patrol. Which means we have maybe five minutes to become very small, very quiet people in a building with no cellar."

"The loft."

"The loft has one ladder and no exit."

"The loft has *straw*, Tamsin, and I am an excellent hider, it's practically my second gift after the singing that ruined my life—"

The front door opened.

Not broken—opened. Which was somehow worse. A warden stood in the frame, young, wind-burned, sword drawn but low, and behind him in the fog the others spread into a crescent, and the youngest one's eyes went wide when he saw us standing there like idiots in the middle of the room, because we *were* standing there like idiots, our entire five-minute plan having consisted of the words *the loft* shouted in panic.

"Well," I said, because my mouth has always operated independently of my survival instincts, "this is awkward."

"Hold where you stand," the young warden said, in the official voice of someone reciting something. "In the name of King Aldric, by edict of the—"

"Yes, yes, ash and keys and the king's eternal health," I said. "Do you know, every single one of you says it exactly the same? Do they teach you that in warden school, or do you practice in front of mirrors?"

"*Ulyana.*" Tamsin's voice had dropped into a register I hadn't heard from him before—not loud, but *full*, like a struck bell. "Stop talking."

"She's stalling," the young warden said, and there was something almost admiring in it, which I chose to take as a compliment. "Sergeant! She's stalling!"

The sergeant came forward through the door. Older. Gray at the temples, a face like a closed ledger. He looked at me the way you look at a goat you've been sent to retrieve, then at Tamsin, and something in his expression shifted—the look of a man doing arithmetic and arriving at an unexpected sum.

"Zhou," he said. "Tamsin Zhou."

The name landed on Tamsin like a thrown stone. I felt him go still beside me—not frozen, exactly, but *gathered*, the way water gathers before it goes over the edge.

"Don't know him," Tamsin said.

"Nine years ago I stood guard at the cordon around Veyra's chapel while your choir died in there." The sergeant's voice was flat as slate. "I heard it happen. Every man on that cordon heard it. Forty voices singing, and then—" his mouth worked, finding a word and rejecting several, "—*stopping*. Not fading. Stopping. Like a door shut on a room." He drew his sword fully now, and the others followed. "The Edict lists names, Zhou. Yours is first. Has been first for nine years—we just couldn't prove you were alive. And now you're standing in a smuggling den with a girl whose description matches a fugitive from Drenmark who can wake *dragons*." He almost smiled. "The captain will want you both breathing. Mostly."

And here is the thing I will carry to whatever grave I get: I panicked.

Not bravely. Not cleverly. Not in any way that a ballad would improve upon. I panicked like a cornered animal, which is what I was, and what came out of me was not a song but a *sound*—three notes, the same three notes my mother sang, ripped out of me raw and terrified and *loud*, and the magic in them, the thing I'd spent eleven days running from and my whole life hiding, woke up like a struck match in a dry barn.

The sound hit the wardens' swords.

I don't have a better word for it. The notes went through the air the way cold goes through a window, and where they touched steel, the steel *rang*—every blade in the room, drawn or sheathed, sang one high agonized chord—and then shattered. All of them. At once. Six swords came apart into falling rain of bright fragments, hilts clattering to the floorboards, and the silence afterward was so complete I could hear the pieces still settling, ticking against each other like cooling iron.

Every face in the room turned toward me with the same expression: the look of men who had just watched a girl break six swords by making noise, and who were rapidly recalculating everything they'd believed about the world.

"Oh," I said faintly. "That's new."

The sergeant recovered fastest, because sergeants are selected for it. He didn't reach for a weapon he no longer had. He reached for the horn at his belt.

Tamsin moved.

I had known him for two days and in that time I'd catalogued perhaps four expressions on his face, none of them warm. But what crossed it as he stepped between me and the sergeant was not any of those. It was older. It was grief wearing speed like a coat.

"No horn," Tamsin said.

The sergeant swung the horn arm up anyway, and Tamsin took him by the collar and the belt—clean, economical, a movement with no anger in it at all, which was somehow the worst part—and drove him into the hearthstone. Once. There was a sound I won't describe because I still hear it at night, and then the sergeant was on the floor and not moving, and the horn rolled free across the boards, untouched, and Tamsin stood over him breathing hard with blood on his knuckles that wasn't his own.

The young warden made a noise. Small. Young.

Tamsin turned toward him, and I saw the moment stretch—I saw the calculation happen behind his eyes, the old mercenary arithmetic, *witnesses, witnesses mean pursuit, pursuit means death*—and I watched it fight something else, something that had cost him nine years and everything he'd loved, and for one horrible instant I genuinely did not know which way he'd go.

He went the other way.

"Out," Tamsin rasped at the young warden. "Take them—" he flicked a hand at the others, dazed, disarmed, one of them weeping openly into the wreck of his sword-hilt, "—take them and walk south. Tell your captain you lost us in the fog. Tell him whatever keeps you alive. But the horn doesn't sound, and you don't follow past the river, or the next thing that breaks will be you."

They went. Wardens don't run, exactly—it's against doctrine—but they exited with tremendous purpose, stumbling over each other in the doorway, and the last one, the young one, looked back once. Not at me. At Tamsin. With an expression I couldn't read then and still can't, though I think about it often.

Then the fog swallowed them, and we were alone with the fire and the broken swords and the sergeant who wasn't getting up.

* * *

Tamsin cleaned his hands at the snowmelt bucket for a long time.

I sat on my bedroll with my ankle throbbing and my whole body shaking in delayed installments, watching him scrub at skin that was already clean, and I understood—I understood with a horrible clarity—that the blood wasn't what he was washing off.

"You killed him," I said. Not accusing. Just placing the fact in the room, the way you'd set down something heavy.

"He'd have sounded the horn."

"I know."

"You broke six swords with three notes and your hands are shaking. Mine aren't." He kept scrubbing. "That's what I hate. Not the killing. How *easy* it comes back. Nine years I didn't—nine years I built a life where I was a man who carried salt and paid shrines and never—" His voice snagged on something and he mastered it, brutally. "My choir sang. Forty of them. And when Veyra went silent mid-prayer, the song didn't stop, Ulyana. *We* stopped. Every one of them, mid-note, like candles pinched out. And I survived because I was holding the low line and the low line was too quiet to matter." He finally looked at me, and his eyes were the gray of the fog outside. "So I swore I'd never sing again. Never lead. Never kill. Two oaths. I broke both before breakfast."

"The sergeant would have brought fifty more down on us."

"I know that too." He wrung out the rag. "Knowing doesn't make it cleaner. Nothing makes it cleaner. You just do the necessary thing and then you carry it, and eventually you carry enough of them that you stop counting, and *that's* the moment you should be afraid of, and I passed it nine years ago and lied to myself that I hadn't."

I should have said something wise. I was eighteen and terrified and my ankle hurt; wisdom was not available. What I said was:

"I broke their swords. That's the first time I've ever done anything with it except hide. And it felt—" I searched. "*Good.* That's the part that frightens me. Not that I can. That I liked it."

Tamsin came and sat down across the fire from me, heavily, an old man in a young body, and for a long moment neither of us spoke. The wind worried at the roof. Somewhere under the world, Veyra screamed on, patient as stone.

"They'll report it," he said finally. "Broken weapons, a dead sergeant, a girl whose voice shatters steel. By tomorrow every warden in Thornfell will have our names and our direction. The hunt isn't starting anymore, Ulyana. It started. Today. This room." He stared into the fire. "There's no more hiding. For either of us."

"Good," I said, and meant it, and hated that I meant it.

His head came up sharply.

"There's a deserter road through the high passes," I said. "Old smuggler talk—my father ran it, before. It avoids the cordon stations entirely. And there's a man on it, they say, a warden captain who tore off his own badge rather than burn a village of children. If we're going to be hunted, I'd rather be hunted *toward* allies than away from them."

Tamsin looked at me for a long time. The firelight moved on his face and I watched the war behind his eyes—the man who wanted to bury himself back in salt and silence fighting the man who had just, for the first time in nine years, sung something true.

"You're going to get us both killed," he said at last.

"Probably," I agreed. "But think of the songs."

Something happened to his mouth then. It was brief, and grudging, and gone almost before it arrived, but I saw it, and I hoarded it like stolen gold.

It might have been a smile.
