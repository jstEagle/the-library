## 4. What the God Left Behind

The chapel found us before I was ready for it to.

I'd been dreading this stretch of the Thornfell high road for three days, ever since Ulyana first said the words *Veyra's mountain* with that particular tilt of her chin, the one that meant she'd already decided and my objections were a formality. The smuggler paths wind up through black pines and scree, and every switchback brought me closer to a place I had spent nine years arranging my entire life around never seeing again. You can do that, if you're disciplined. You can build a whole self out of avoidance, mortar every gap with routine, salt routes and ledger lines and the careful wordless hum you make instead of singing. It holds. It held.

Then the trees opened, and there it was.

* * *

Veyra's chapel had never been grand, even in the god's day. That was the point of it. While the great cathedrals of the lowlands raised their gilt spires and charged admission to grace, Veyra's people built a hall of grey mountain stone with a roof of slate and lichen, wide doors that were never locked, and no altar — just a ring of flat stones worn smooth by nine hundred years of kneeling singers. The god didn't want your gold. He wanted your voice, given freely, once, at whatever hour you happened to need him most.

Now the doors hung off one hinge like a drunk leaning on a fence. Snow had drifted through the broken east wall and lay in blue shadows across the floor. The bell tower still stood, but the bell was gone — I knew where it was; half of Thornfell's farmhouses owned a piece of it, melted down into plow blades and kettle hooks after the Silence, because a dead god's bell is just bronze and grief doesn't keep its value — and the empty arch framed nothing but sky, which felt obscenely generous. Sky, all that sky, as if nothing had ended here.

Ulyana walked in ahead of me without hesitation, boots crunching on drifted snow, and stopped in the middle of the singer's ring. She turned a slow circle, head back, mouth slightly open, like someone tasting the air.

"It's warm," she said.

"It's a ruin."

"It's *warm*, Tamsin." She crouched and pressed her palm flat against the snow-dusted stone. "Feel it. The rest of the mountain's been freezing us solid since dawn and this room is—" she frowned, hunting for the word, "—*listening*."

I did not feel it. Or rather, I felt exactly what I had spent nine years teaching myself not to feel: a faint pressure behind the sternum, like a hand laid gently over a wound to check if it's still bleeding. I set down my pack, made a show of rummaging for tinder, and got a small fire going in the old hearth because my hands needed something stupid to do.

"You know what this place is," she said. Not a question.

"I grew up forty miles from here. Everyone knows what it is."

"That's not what I asked."

I fed the fire a knot of pine. "It's a chapel where a god used to live. Now it's a chapel where he doesn't. There are hundreds like it."

"There aren't." She came and sat across the fire from me, cross-legged, chin on her fists, watching me with those pale eyes that saw far too much for a girl who claimed to be sixteen and moved like something much older wearing a borrowed winter coat. "There aren't hundreds like it. I've been to dead shrines all the way from the coast. They're *empty*. This one isn't. Something's still here. Something's still—" she pressed her fist against her own chest, "—*screaming*."

"There it is," I said.

"There what is?"

"The screaming. You've been saying it since the cave. 'I hear Veyra screaming.' I thought it was frostbite talking, or terror, or a girl who'd been hunted so long she'd started inventing gods to keep her company." I looked at the fire instead of at her. "So let's say I believe you. Let's say the god I watched die is somehow still alive somewhere under the world, screaming. What exactly would you have me do about it? Shout down a well?"

"Maybe listen first."

"To what?" My voice came out sharper than I intended, sharp enough that it cracked the quiet of the chapel, and the echo came back wrong — came back *warmer*, the way sound behaves inside a full house rather than an empty one. We both heard it. We both pretended we hadn't. "Ulyana. Listen to me carefully, because I'm only going to say this once, and then I'm going to sleep, and tomorrow we're going down the west ridge toward Marrowgate and away from all of this."

She waited. She was good at waiting. It was one of the many infuriating things about her.

"Nine years ago I stood right there—" I pointed at the western edge of the singer's ring, at a flat stone I could have identified blindfolded, because it had been *mine*, "—and sang the Evening Unfolding with eleven other people. Twelve voices. We weren't priests. We were farmers and a smith and two sisters who ran the mill and a boy of fourteen named Col who couldn't hold a tune in a bucket but whose heart was so loud the god heard him anyway. That's how Veyra worked. He didn't care about skill. He cared about *truth*. You gave him your voice honestly and he carried it somewhere. Where, nobody knows. Prayers go somewhere. That was the whole arrangement."

The fire popped. Outside, wind worried at the broken wall.

"And then?" Ulyana said softly.

"And then, mid-hymn, mid-*note* — the fourth line of the third verse, I remember because I was carrying the descant and it's a bastard of a line — he went silent. Not quiet. Silent. Like a door closing in a house you thought was open all the way through." I kept my eyes on the fire. "You have to understand what song-magic is. It isn't casting. It's *conduction*. The god is the wire and you're the current, and when the wire vanishes mid-phrase the current has nowhere to go. Eleven voices hit a wall of nothing at full throat. Do you know what that does?"

"No."

"Neither do I, entirely. I know Col fell over. I know the mill sisters' noses bled. I know old Marta, the smith, who could sing sparks off cold iron, screamed for six days afterward and then never spoke again. And I know that when the silence finished moving through all of us, it took something. Different things. Col lost his joy — not his happiness, his *joy*, they're not the same thing, and I've watched him try to laugh for nine years and it comes out like coughing. Marta lost her words. The mill sisters lost—" I waved a hand, "—each other, mostly. They live in the same village and haven't spoken since.

"I lost my music. Not my voice — I can talk, obviously, I'm doing it now, at length, you may have noticed. I lost everything *underneath* the voice. Every gifted singer has a second hearing, a place where music lives before it becomes sound. Mine went dark. Nine years ago, in the space between two notes, it went dark and stayed dark, and what was left was just... a man who talks." I finally looked at her. "Eleven people. Mid-hymn. Because their god decided, or was made unable, or *forgot*, I don't know which, to be a wire anymore."

"And you swore it off."

"I swore it off. All of it. Wordless tithe at roadside shrines — salt and silence, never a note. I taught myself to hear music as physics, vibration, air, nothing holy. And when the Edict came down and the wardens started burning shrines, I told myself it wasn't my war. Let the crown hunt the loud ones. I'd done my loudness. It cost eleven people. I paid."

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. Snow slid off the ruined roof with a soft sigh.

"You know what I heard in all of that?" she said at last.

"A cautionary tale."

"*A man describing a wound.*" She leaned forward. "Tamsin. You didn't lose your gift when Veyra went silent. You buried it. There's a difference, and the difference matters, because buried things don't die — they wait. Your second hearing didn't go dark. You nailed it shut and called the darkness proof." She tapped the floor with her knuckles, twice, deliberately. "And this room agrees with me."

"The room is a ruin."

"The room is *warm*." Her voice dropped, and for the first time since I'd fished her half-frozen out of that smuggler's cave, she looked frightened — not of wardens, not of hunger, but of whatever she was carrying. "Every night since I was small I hear it. Under everything. Beneath the world like a sound under a floor. I thought everyone heard it and was polite enough not to mention it. Then the wardens came for me and I learned nobody hears anything, and I learned the thing I hear has a name, because I said it once, out loud, in a tavern, and a warden went white as milk and reached for his iron." Her pale eyes fixed on mine. "*Veyra.* He didn't die, Tamsin. He was *taken*. Silenced. Stuffed somewhere deep and shut away, and he's been screaming into the dark for nine years, and last winter, in the worst of the snow, in a cave where I was dying, something of him reached up and found the only ear left in the world that was listening."

The fire burned. My hands, traitors, had gone cold despite it.

"If that's true," I said slowly, "then the god abandoned my choir mid-hymn not by choice. He was *pulled away* mid-prayer. And eleven people broke against the silence of a kidnapping."

"Now you're hearing it."

"Don't." I stood up, too fast, and the old stone rang faintly under my boot — that warmth again, that horrible attentive warmth. "Don't do that. Don't hand me a story where my guilt gets retroactively cancelled. Nine years I've lived with the arithmetic: I sang for a god who failed us, so I quit the god. If he was dragged away screaming, then I spent nine years cursing a victim, and worse — I spent nine years hiding while the thing that took him started eating *everyone else's voices*, one shrine, one village, one Edict at a time. Do you understand what that makes the last nine years? Not survival. *Desertion.*"

"Yes," Ulyana said simply. "It does."

I stared at her.

"You wanted absolution," she said. "I don't have any. But I'll tell you what I have instead, and it's better. I have the screaming. It hasn't stopped. It won't stop until somebody finishes what he was saying when they took him." She rose too, small and stubborn in the firelight, and pointed at the ring of stones, at my stone, at the flat grey circle worn smooth by centuries of knees. "Veyra's prayers don't vanish, Tamsin. They *go somewhere*. Everyone knows that. So ask yourself — a god silenced mid-word, nine years ago, in this room — where did the unfinished prayer go?"

And the chapel answered.

Not in words. Ruins don't talk, I want that on record. But the fire flared though I hadn't fed it, and the pressure behind my sternum — the hand-on-the-wound pressure I had spent nearly a decade pretending was scar tissue — pressed once, firmly, unmistakably, like a knock. And underneath the wind and the snow-sigh and the girl's breathing, so faint I could have drowned it in denial if I'd hurried, I heard it:

A single held tone. Low. Patient. Coming up through the mountain itself.

The second hearing I'd nailed shut rattled in its coffin.

Ulyana watched my face and saw whatever she needed to see there. "Tomorrow," she said quietly, "we don't go down the west ridge."

I looked at the hearth. At my stone. At the door hanging off its hinge, still open, after all these years, because even ruined, even robbed of its bell and its god, the chapel refused to learn locked doors.

"We'll see," I said, which is what cowards say instead of yes, and what the guilty say instead of *help me*.

Above the empty bell arch, snow began to fall through the sky, slow and silent, each flake landing without a sound — and every one of them, I could have sworn, landed *in time*.
