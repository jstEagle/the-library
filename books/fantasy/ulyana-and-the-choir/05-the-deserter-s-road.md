## 5. The Deserter's Road

There are three ways to leave the Wardens of the Crown. The first is death in service, which comes with a flag and a hole in the ground and your name read aloud by a man who never liked you. The second is disgrace, which comes with a trial and the same hole but no flag. And the third—the one nobody tells you about until you're standing in it—is to tear the badge off your own chest on a cold morning in front of eleven men who used to call you Captain, and walk into the mountains with nothing but the shirt underneath and whatever you can carry.

I chose door number three. I don't recommend it. The shirt was thin.

My name is Brannic Hale. For nineteen years I wore the grey-and-gold, and for nineteen years I told myself the same lie every warden tells himself at some point: *the Edict protects people from worse.* Then came Hallowmere, and two children in a cellar with their mother's hands over their mouths, and an order signed by a man whose eyes didn't move right when he spoke, and I discovered that there is a limit to how long you can hold a torch over a family's door before your arm gets tired of being someone else's.

So now I'm walking the deserter's road, which isn't a road at all, really—it's a rumor with a path worn down its middle. Smugglers use it. Runaway gifted use it. Once, so the stories go, a tax collector used it, though nobody believes that part.

I'd been on it four days when I found them.

* * *

You learn to read camps from a distance if you've spent two decades hunting them. This one was wrong in every particular. A proper fugitive camp hides its fire; this one had it burning cheerful as a festival. A proper camp posts watches; this one had a girl asleep against a rock with her mouth open, and a big man sitting up doing watch-duty with his eyes shut, which is a technique I have personally arrested people for inventing.

And there was a dragon.

Not a large one—though "not large" for a dragon still means the size of a granary—and old, old the way cliffs are old, her scales gone the grey of woodsmoke, one wing folded like a grandmother pulling a shawl tighter. She was watching me before I knew she was there. That's the thing about dragons they don't put in the ballads: they see you first. Always. You are never sneaking up on a dragon. You are merely approaching one slowly enough to be permitted.

"Evening," I said, because my mother raised me polite and then died before she could see what I did with it.

The big man stood up fast, and I recognized him even before he reached for the knife. Tamsin Zhou. Salt-smuggler, ghost, and—if half of what we'd scraped together in the warden archives was true—the last surviving member of Veyra's choir. Nine years dead, all of them. Nine years of a god going silent mid-prayer and taking forty voices with Him, and one man walking out of that mountain with his gift intact and his soul apparently left behind as payment.

His name sat at the top of a list I'd helped draft. That fact sat in my stomach like a swallowed stone.

"You're warden," he said. Not a question. He'd seen the way I carried myself, or maybe just the way I'd assessed his camp in under three seconds, because that's not something you unlearn either.

"I was."

"Were." His knife hand hadn't relaxed. "What happened to 'was'?"

"Hallowmere happened," I said. "Two children. Orders to burn the house with them inside. I declined."

"People decline orders all the time. They usually get demoted, not exiled."

"I also tore off my badge and hit my lieutenant with the torch."

Something moved at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. Recognition, maybe—the look of a man finding another animal with the same scars.

"And now you've walked four days through the snow to find us," he said, "out of the goodness of your heart."

"No," I said honestly. "I walked four days through the snow because the deserter's road runs through here and I didn't know you were on it. Finding you is bad luck for both of us. But since I have—" I shrugged, which in the cold hurt more than it sounds. "I've got news you'll want, and you've got a fire I'd very much like to stop dying next to."

Behind him, the girl had woken up. Ulyana. Fifteen, maybe sixteen, wrapped in three mismatched blankets, with the kind of face that made wardens write *unremarkable* in reports right up until she opened her mouth and put a valley to sleep. She was looking at me the way you look at a snake that has wandered into the kitchen—not screaming yet, but keeping count of the exits.

"The dragon decides," she said.

Everyone looked at Ysolde. The old dragon regarded me for a long moment with eyes like banked coals, and then made a sound deep in her chest that I felt in my back teeth rather than heard, and settled her head back down onto her forelegs.

"She says you smell honest," Ulyana translated. "Also tired. Also like a man who hasn't eaten anything but pine bark in two days."

"Pine bark is underrated," I said.

Tamsin lowered the knife by degrees, the way a man lowers something he doesn't trust to stay down. "Sit," he said. "Talk. And Brannic—if this is a trap, know that I've survived worse than a warden with frostbite."

"That's the spirit," I said, and sat down before my knees could file a formal objection.

* * *

Here's what I told them, once my hands had stopped shaking around the tin cup Tamsin pressed into them—tea, weak, but hot, and I could have wept:

Three weeks ago, the crown issued a new edict. Not the general hunt—that had been running since autumn, wardens kicking in doors across Thornfell, dragging out anyone whose voice did things voices shouldn't. This one had names. A written list, sealed with the king's own signet, distributed to every garrison from the coast to the passes. The Edict of Ash, the men were already calling it, because soldiers name things quickly and badly.

Forty-one names. I'd seen the original in Captain-General Voss's tent, because rank has privileges and one of mine had been reading things I wasn't supposed to.

The first name on the list was TAMSIN ZHOU.

Not alphabetically. Not by region. First. At the top, in a different hand than the rest—a heavier hand, pressed hard enough to tear the page slightly, as though whoever wrote it had wanted the ink to bite. Underneath, in the same hand, a single instruction: *Alive. The voice must be delivered whole.*

"The rest of the list is arrest and assessment," I said. "Yours is different. Whoever wrote that line doesn't want you questioned. They want you *delivered*. Like cargo. Like—" I paused, because the word arrived before I was ready for it. "Like provisions."

Nobody said anything for a while. The fire cracked. Somewhere downslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

"Veyra's choir," Ulyana said quietly. She was hugging her knees, chin on top, eyes fixed on me. "Forty voices, taken at once. If something eats voices—"

"Don't," Tamsin said.

It wasn't sharp. That was the strange part. It was quiet, and flat, and final, the way a door closes when the person behind it has decided the conversation is over whether the room agrees or not. He got up and walked to the edge of the firelight and stood there with his back to us, and I watched a man try to be a wall.

Ulyana looked at me. I looked at her. Between us, without a word passing, we agreed to let him be a wall for a few minutes. You learn that in the wardens too—when to press and when to let the silence do the work. Most confessions happen in silence. Men can't stand it. Give them thirty seconds of nothing and they'll tell you where the bodies are just to fill the air.

It took him closer to a minute. Then he came back and sat down, and said, in a voice sanded smooth by nine years of practice: "Tell me the rest."

* * *

The rest was this: the list was growing. Voss had new pages coming in weekly, names gathered from parish records, from schoolmasters, from neighbors paid in copper to report the child who hummed the well dry or the grandmother whose lullabies cured fevers. And the assessments weren't assessments anymore. Gifted taken in were shipped east, toward the capital, in wagons with barred windows, and the wagons came back empty, and the men who guarded them had started drawing lots to avoid the duty, though none of them would say why.

"There's something wrong with the king," I said. "I know how that sounds. Every drunk in every tavern says the king's gone mad. But I stood honor-guard in the throne room twice last year, and both times Aldric didn't blink for the entire ceremony. Not slowed down. *Didn't.* And his eyes—" I turned my cup in my hands. "You know how a lamp looks when the oil's nearly gone? Still burning, but the light's gone thin, and something's moving in the flame that isn't flame? It was like that. Like looking at a lit window in a house where everyone's dead."

"Something wearing him," Ulyana said. Not a question. She said it the way you'd say *it's raining*—a fact observed, unpleasant, ordinary.

"You've seen it?"

"I hear it." Her arms tightened around her knees. "Under everything. Under the ground, under the prayers, under the quiet. Something feeding. Brannic—I hear Veyra too. Screaming. He's not gone. He's *held*."

Tamsin closed his eyes. Just for a moment. Just long enough.

I should say something here about what it does to a man, hearing that. Nineteen years in the wardens teaches you to sort claims fast: true, false, mad, useful. What Ulyana said should have landed in *mad*, filed away with the prophetesses and the men who claimed the moon owed them money. Instead it landed somewhere lower and older, in the place where I keep Hallowmere—because I had stood in that throne room, and I had watched a king not blink, and I had signed orders in his name that no sane king would sign, and some part of me had known, had *known*, the way you know a house is wrong before you can say why, that the signature was real and the hand behind it was not.

"Then here's where I land," I said. "I can't unmake the Edict. I can't reach the king. I'm one man with a stolen horse and a warrant out for my own arrest—yes, I'm on the list too, near the bottom, under *sedition*, which I confess I enjoyed seeing in writing. But I know the warden garrisons. I know which captains hate the burnings, which ones are just scared, and which ones would sell their mothers for a promotion. There are more like me than Voss thinks. Scared men follow orders. But scared men with permission—" I looked at Tamsin. "Scared men with permission become something else entirely."

"Permission from whom?"

"From proof. From a name on a list that says *alive, delivered whole*. From whatever's under the capital that eats voices. You want the army to stand down? Armies don't stand down for songs. They stand down when the reason for marching dies in front of them, publicly, where everyone can see it." I spread my hands. "I can't give you that. But I can find the men who'll help you get close enough to try."

Tamsin studied me for a long time. Firelight moved on his face and he let it, and I thought: *he's deciding whether I'm worth the risk of hoping again,* and I thought: *gods help us both if he decides yes, because hope in that man will be a dangerous thing, the kind that's been underground nine years and comes up hungry.*

"You understand what you're asking," he said finally. "You're offering to help build a rebellion out of smugglers and children and a dying dragon."

"A rebellion needs all three," I said. "Smugglers know the roads, children are why we bother, and dragons—" I glanced at Ysolde, who had opened one coal-bright eye at the word *dying* and closed it again with the air of a matriarch declining to dignify gossip. "Dragons are morale."

That got a sound out of him. Half a laugh, strangled at birth, but a sound. Ulyana grinned like the sun coming out over a battlefield, and I understood suddenly why half the wardens on her trail had filed reports describing her as *dangerous beyond parameters*—not the voice, the grin. Grins like that make men brave, and brave men are hell on paperwork.

"One condition," Tamsin said.

"Name it."

"When we take the capital—if we take the capital—there are trials. Public ones. Voss, the captains who burned Hallowmere, all of them. No pyres, no midnight knives, no matter what they've done. If the thing on the throne taught us anything, it's what happens when power stops answering to law." His jaw worked. "I buried a choir because something above the law got hungry. I won't build a new world out of the same material."

I thought about Voss. I thought about the lieutenant whose nose I'd broken, who'd laughed at Hallowmere, who'd held the torch steady while the mother screamed. Every bone in my body, nineteen years of it, wanted those men dead in a ditch.

Every bone in my body had been wrong before.

"Trials," I said. "Witnesses, evidence, sentences that fit. Justice outside the law until we can put the law back on its feet." I put out my hand. "That's a bargain I can soldier for."

He took it. His grip was careful, controlled, the grip of a man who knows exactly how much force lives in his hands and rations it like water. "Welcome to the choir, Captain."

"It's not a choir," Ulyana said. "It's five people and a dragon."

"Five people and a dragon," Tamsin agreed, and there it was again, that almost-smile, that dangerous thawing thing. "Give it a month."

* * *

We broke camp before dawn, because the deserter's road belongs to the dark and the wise surrender it early. I took point, partly because I knew the next twenty miles and partly because walking first meant I didn't have to watch Tamsin Zhou carry a sleeping child on his back while a dragon the age of kingdoms limped along behind him like a dog too proud to admit its hips hurt.

Somewhere past the second ridge, with the sky going the color of a bruise healing, Ulyana fell into step beside me.

"You tore off your badge," she said. "Did it hurt?"

"Funny thing. No. Nineteen years wearing it and it came off like it was never sewn on at all. The pin had rusted through." I kept my eyes on the path. "The hurting part was after. When my hands wouldn't stop shaking. When I realized I'd spent nineteen years being the wolf and calling it shepherding."

She considered that with the terrible seriousness of the young. "Tamsin says guilt is just love with nowhere to go."

"Tamsin sounds like a man who's had a lot of love with nowhere to go."

"He has." She glanced back at him—at the broad shoulders, the bowed head, the man carrying a child through the snow toward a war he'd sworn off nine years ago. "But it's going somewhere now."

Ahead of us, the road bent south, toward the lowlands, toward Marrowgate and the free-town and whatever strays the Edict was driving toward us like leaves before a fire. Toward a list with a name at the top in ink that had torn the page.

I pulled my collar up against the wind and walked faster, and for the first time since Hallowmere, my hands were steady.
