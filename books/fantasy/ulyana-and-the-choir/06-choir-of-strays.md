## 6. Choir of Strays

Marrowgate smelled like fish, tar, and freedom, which is to say it smelled like a place that had never once been asked for its papers.

We came down off the deserter's road in the grey hour before dawn, when the fog off the Marrow estuary was thick enough to chew and the gulls were arguing about who'd been robbed worse. Tamsin walked ahead with Pip asleep against his chest—the drummer boy hadn't spoken since Hallowmere, but he'd stopped flinching at Tamsin's hands somewhere around the second night, and I'd learned to read that as progress. Brannic brought up the rear with his hood up and his warden's stride carefully folded away into something more civilian. He still scanned rooftops like he expected them to report on him. Old habits. Bad ones, now.

"You're grinning," Tamsin said without turning around.

"I'm breathing sea air."

"You're planning something."

I was. But all I said was, "I hear drums."

He heard them too, then—faint, arrhythmic, coming from under the fish market's raised walkways. Not a performance. A heartbeat keeping itself company.

* * *

The boy under the market was maybe eleven, sitting cross-legged on an upturned crate with a pair of bone sticks and an overturned fish barrel, playing patterns into the wood so soft you felt them in your teeth before your ears. When we crouched at the edge of his little kingdom of shadow, he went rigid, sticks frozen mid-air, eyes going from my face to Tamsin's to the sword-shaped outline under Brannic's cloak.

"Easy," I said. "We're not wardens."

His chin came up, defiant in the way of children who have already decided how they'll die and would prefer it be interesting. Then his gaze snagged on something—my throat, maybe, or the way Tamsin had shifted slightly forward, putting himself between the boy and Brannic without seeming to notice he'd done it—and some of the defiance cracked into something rawer.

He pointed one stick at me. Then at his own chest. Then he tapped out a quick pattern on the barrel: three fast, two slow, three fast.

"I don't speak drum," I admitted.

"He says he can't talk," Tamsin translated quietly. "And that we should say what we want or leave."

Brannic snorted. "The drum thing too?"

"The drum thing especially." Tamsin lowered himself to the crate across from the boy, unhurried, making himself small the way big men can when they've practiced. "Someone cut your tongue?"

The boy's jaw tightened. He tapped: two slow beats. Then he touched the brand-scar on his own wrist—a warden's mark, half-grown-over, the kind they gave gifted children to track their hum until the Edict turned tracking into hunting.

Pip, Tamsin had named him by then—Pip, because the boy had answered to nothing else, tapping twice whenever Tamsin said it, which everyone correctly took as consent.

"He's been living here how long?" I asked.

Tamsin asked with a tilt of his head. The answer came back as a shrug and a rhythm on the barrel that was either "a season" or "since the ice broke," and honestly could have been both.

"We're gathering people," I told him. "People the Edict wants dead. We're going to keep each other alive, and then we're going to do something about the thing doing the wanting." I held out my hand, palm up, the way you offer anything to anything wild. "You don't have to decide now. You can just stop being alone tonight."

Pip looked at my hand. Looked at Tamsin. And Tamsin—who had spent nine years perfecting the art of not being anyone's anything—gave the smallest nod, like a door opening one inch.

Pip put down his sticks and took my hand.

* * *

The twins found *us*, which should have been my first warning.

They were waiting outside the rooming house where Brannic had rented us beds under a false name, leaning against the wall like they'd grown there—two girls of maybe seventeen, identical down to the chipped front tooth on the left side of identical smiles. Red hair cropped short for running. Matching coats, matching boots, matching way of looking at you like you were a song they were deciding whether to finish.

"Tamsin Zhou," said one.

"Sings," said the other.

"Killed a choir," said the first.

"Buried one," corrected the second, and something crossed Tamsin's face like weather.

"How—" I started.

"You hum in thirds," they said together, and shivered pleasantly. "Everyone in Marrowgate with ears and the wrong blood knows there's a man walking the docks who harmonizes with the foghorns without meaning to. We've been listening for two days trying to work out if you were bait."

"And?" Tamsin asked.

"You're terrible bait," said the first. "Bait doesn't look that tired."

"Bait doesn't carry children," said the second.

Their names were Sorrel and Sage, though they refused to tell me which was which, and after a week I understood why: it wasn't a game, it was armor. If the wardens caught one, the records wouldn't know which twin to burn. Their gift was harmony so precise it did things—Sorrel could hold a note that made locks forget themselves; Sage could sing a counter-melody that made fear sit down and wait politely. Together, standing close enough that their shoulders touched, they could make a roomful of people believe anything for exactly as long as the chord lasted.

"They'll want us separately," Sage told us on the first night, while she braided and unbraided Sorrel's hair in an endless loop. "That's the trick of twins. You only have to catch one and the other comes free."

"So we don't get caught," Sorrel said simply, and smiled her chipped smile, and I thought: *yes. Exactly that.*

* * *

Old Maren we found by accident, unless you believe the storms send invitations, which after knowing her I no longer discount.

A squall came up the estuary our third evening—nothing serious, just the sky clearing its throat—and the fishing fleet was scrambling for harbor when we saw a woman standing on the breakwater with her arms out and her face turned up into the rain. Seventy if she was a day, thin as a gull's leg, hair like storm-cloud wool, and she was *singing*. Low, wordless, a sound like rigging rope in wind. And the squall bent around her. Curved away from the boats like water around a stone, and set itself down harmlessly out past the shoals where it sulked like a scolded dog.

"She's not hiding it," Brannic breathed beside me. "In the open. With the whole town watching."

But nobody in Marrowgate was watching, is the thing. The fishermen hauled their lines and cursed the weather and nodded to her on their way past like she was a fence post that occasionally performed miracles, and she nodded back, and that was that. Free-town. The Edict hadn't reached here yet—not really, not past rumor—and the town had apparently decided that a woman who turned storms was between herself and the sky, and none of the harbor's business.

She found us afterward, drying off in a tavern called the Drowned Rat, and sat down at our table uninvited with the air of someone who has paid rent on every table in creation.

"You're collecting," she said. Her voice was gravel and honey. "Gifted. For a run south."

"We're not—"

"Girl." She looked at me, and her eyes were pale as weather glass, and they went through me like light through water. "You've got a voice in you that wakes things that ought to sleep. You've got a man over there who's been sitting on his own gift so long it's gone to scar tissue. You've got twins who think being two is safety, and a boy who speaks in thunder because men with badges took his words. And you've got a deserter who hasn't decided yet whether he's running from the crown or toward something better." She accepted the ale Brannic slid over without looking at him. "You're collecting. Question is whether you know what for."

"Do you?" I asked.

"I know what the crown does with voices." She drank. "I sang at Verreholm once, sixty years back, for old Queen Isolde's coronation. Sang the weather fair for a week. And I watched the court eat the sound of it and ask for more, and I thought then: that hunger isn't a court's hunger. Courts are greedy. This was hungry like a mouth is hungry." She set the mug down. "So no. I won't join your choir, child. But I'll walk with you as far as my bones allow, because I'm old, and old women get to spend their last coin however they like, and I mean to spend mine loudly."

"That's joining," Sorrel said.

"That's accompanying," Maren corrected, and stole Sage's bread.

* * *

Here is what I learned that week, in the fog and the fish-smell and the lamplit rooms above the Drowned Rat: a choir assembles itself the way a fire catches. Not all at once. By proximity. By one dry thing leaning near another until neither remembers who warmed whom first.

Pip taught Sorrel his rhythms, and she built chords around them, and by the fourth day he was smiling—smiling—with his whole face, silent and blazing, while Sage kept time against the wall and the room forgot to be afraid.

Maren told stories that were half history and half warning, and Brannic listened to all of them with the expression of a man taking inventory of his sins, and somewhere in there he started laughing at her jokes, which I counted as a second defection.

And Tamsin—

Tamsin resisted.

I want that recorded accurately, because later, when people wrote songs about him, they'd skip this part. He resisted. Every time the strays drifted toward him the way cold hands drift toward a stove, he stepped back. When Sorrel asked him to teach her the third-harmony technique the foghorns had revealed, he said, "Ask Sage." When Pip tapped out a question—*will you stay?*—Tamsin looked at those two slow beats for a long moment and then busied himself with the firewood like the question had been about kindling.

"He thinks he kills choirs," I told Maren one night, watching Tamsin stand watch alone on the landing, shoulders squared against a doorway like doors had personally wronged him.

"Mm," said Maren. "And what does he think killed his choir?"

"Himself. Leading them. Singing lead when the god fell."

"And what actually happened?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Because I'd heard the story by then, in the chapel ruins, told in a voice like a man reading his own autopsy, and the honest answer was: *a god went silent mid-prayer, and nine singers died holding a note for something that stopped existing.* Not his fault. Not anyone's fault. Fault implied agency, and Veyra's silence had swept through that mountain chapel like an avalanche sweeps a road—indifferent, total, blameless in the way disasters are blameless.

"He knows that," I said finally. "Knowing and believing aren't neighbors."

"No," Maren agreed. "But they share a wall. Sometimes you just have to knock hard enough."

"What are you proposing? That we knock?"

"I'm proposing," Maren said, "that a man who buries a choir is a man who intends to dig it up someday, or he'd have burned the map. Watch his hands when they sing. He conducts. In his lap. Little movements, like a man praying in a language he's sworn off. You can't help it, girl, if you were made to hold the line. You can only decide whether you'll do it running or standing."

* * *

It came to a head on the sixth night, over nothing. Over soup, actually.

We'd pushed the tables together in the Drowned Rat's back room—all seven of us, because Maren had officially become "not joining" in the same way tides are "not wet"—and Sorrel had the idea that we should practice. Just lightly. Nothing that would ripple past the walls. A round, something simple, so that if the worst ever came we'd know each other's ranges, know who could cover whom, know the shape of the thing we might have to be.

"It's a bad idea," Tamsin said.

"It's soup," said Sorrel.

"It's a rehearsal."

"It's *soup*," Sage agreed, "with singing near it."

"You don't rehearse," Tamsin said, and his voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man walking on ice he knew was rotten, "unless you intend to perform. And I am telling you, as someone with experience, that performing together means dying together, and I will not—"

He stopped. Because Pip had put down his spoon, picked up his sticks, and tapped out a rhythm on the table. Two slow beats. The one that meant, in the small language he'd built among us: *stay. listen. stay.*

Then the boy looked Tamsin dead in the eye and tapped it again. And again. Patient as tide.

The room held very still. Even the fire seemed to lean in.

"Pip," Tamsin said hoarsely, "you don't know what you're asking."

Two slow beats.

"I buried them. All nine. I dug the graves myself in frozen ground, and I swore on—"

Two slow beats. Softer now. And then, deliberately, the boy laid one stick down, and pressed his palm flat over his own heart, and held Tamsin's gaze with the terrible directness of children, and tapped once—one soft beat, right over the sternum.

*I'm asking anyway.*

I don't know what broke in Tamsin then. Something small, I think. Something that had been holding up something large. He looked around the table—at Sorrel and Sage with their matched breath held, at Maren with her weather-glass eyes full of something ancient and kind, at Brannic who had torn a badge off his own chest for strangers' children, at me, at Pip—and I watched nine years of careful silence come apart not with a bang but with a long, shaking exhale, like a man surfacing.

"I don't lead," he said finally. Roughly. "I can't. If we do this—if any of you insist on this madness—we do it *together*. No lead line. No one man out front for the world to shoot at. Everyone carries the song or no one does."

"That's not how choirs work," Sage began.

"It's how this one works," Tamsin said, "or it doesn't work at all."

And Maren laughed, low and rolling, like distant thunder approving of itself. "Boy," she said, "that's the most leading thing you've said all week."

* * *

So we sang.

Nothing grand. A harbor round, old and anonymous, the kind fishermen use to keep oars in time—four bars, folding back on itself, everyone entering in turn and no voice above another. Sorrel first, finding the note. Sage sliding beneath her, a floor of sound. Maren adding a drone like bedrock. Brannic—I swear on whatever gods are left—he actually sang, badly, quietly, in a voice like a hinge, and nobody laughed, because courage counts even off-key.

Pip kept the pulse on the tabletop, soft as rain.

And I came in last, because Ulyana always came in last in those days; I didn't trust my own voice not to wake something, and I wanted to see the others safe inside the chord before I risked adding mine. But when I did—when I let a thread of the true voice out, just a filament, the one that thawed ice and stirred sleeping things—I felt the round *take* it. Absorb it. Pass it along. My dangerous sound, woven into six other sounds, became just music. Became safe. Became *ours*.

Across the table, Tamsin was crying. Silently, without drama, the way men cry who learned young that grief gets no stage. His hands were in his lap, moving—conducting, Maren would have said, except there was nothing to conduct, we needed no conducting, and I understood suddenly that he wasn't leading us. He was leading *them*. The nine graves on the mountain. Finishing, in his fingers, the prayer their deaths had interrupted.

Nobody spoke when the round ended. Nobody had to. Pip broke it eventually by tapping out dinner, and Sorrel declared the soup a triumph, and Maren drank someone's ale without apology, and the moment dissolved into ordinary noise the way good moments do—thoroughly, and leaving sediment.

* * *

I woke near midnight to the sound of hooves.

Not many. Four horses, maybe five, moving fast along the harbor road with that particular cadence of riders who are in a hurry and armed and *official*—and below them, faint but unmistakable, the high reedy whine of a warden's tuning-fork being struck and passed hand to hand. Listening for hum.

I was out of bed and down the stairs before my boots were fully on, and the others were already assembling in the taproom's dark—Brannic at the window with his jaw gone white, Sorrel and Sage pressed shoulder to shoulder, Maren calm as deep water, Tamsin with Pip's wrist gripped in his hand and his eyes finding mine across the dark room with terrible steadiness.

"How many?" he whispered.

"At least four. Heading for the gifted quarter." I swallowed. "Tamsin, the gifted quarter is where the dyers live. Where the harbormaster's daughter lives. There are *children* down there who hum when they're happy and don't even know it's a death sentence yet—"

The fork-note sounded again, closer now, and under it I heard something else, something that dropped my stomach through the floorboards: a voice, amplified by the fork, ringing flat and official off the warehouse walls.

*"By edict of King Aldric, second of his name: all persons found harboring the gifted are guilty of treason. All gifted persons will present themselves for assessment. Resistance will be met with fire."*

Fire.

The word landed in the taproom like a coal on dry wool. I saw it catch behind every face—saw Sorrel's hand close around Sage's, saw Brannic reach for a sword he'd sworn off, saw Maren turn her face toward the window with an expression like a storm deciding.

And I saw Tamsin look down at Pip, at the boy whose tongue had been taken by the last people who wore those badges, and I watched the choice happen behind his eyes—the old choice, the invisible choice, the one he'd made at nineteen over nine frozen graves.

Watched him refuse it.

"Get the strays' names from the dyers' guild list," he said quietly, and his voice was the one from the chapel story, the one that used to hold a choir together, and God forgive me but hearing it again made me feel *safe*. "Ulyana—you're with me. Brannic, the tunnels under the fish market—Pip can draw them for you. We're getting them out *before* the forks find their doors."

Outside, the hoofbeats turned down toward the waterfront.

And somewhere in the gifted quarter, faint and frightened, someone began to hum.
