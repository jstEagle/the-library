## 7. Marrowgate Burns

I want it on the record that I said no.

Not a polite no. Not a diplomatic, let-me-sleep-on-it no. I said *no* in three languages, one of them dead, and I meant every syllable. I had spent nine years perfecting the art of being nobody—smuggling salt through Thornfell's back ways, keeping my ledger clean and my throat shut—and then a girl who hummed glaciers apart and a warden captain with his badge torn out like a bad tooth decided, between them, that Marrowgate was where we'd stop running.

"We need supplies," Brannic had said, in the reasonable voice of a man who has never once been hunted.

"We need to not be on a list," I'd said. "My name is first on that list. It's practically engraved."

"You're already on it," Ulyana pointed out, with the merciless clarity of the young. "Hiding in the hills doesn't un-engrave you. At least in Marrowgate there are people like us."

People like us. Nine years ago those words would have meant something warm. Now they meant *targets*, plural, clustered conveniently for the crown's convenience.

But I went anyway. That's the thing about saying no around Ulyana—it's less a refusal than a delay.

* * *

Marrowgate smelled of fish brine, tar, and rain that never quite finished falling. A free-town wedged into the cliffs above the Greywash, all crooked streets and crookeder bargains, governed by nothing sterner than the harbor master's mood. The gifted quarter clung to the eastern terraces like lichen: painted doors, wind chimes made of broken bell-bronze, children who could whistle gulls out of storms.

The old woman found us within an hour of our arrival, which told me everything about how well we were hiding.

She came down the terrace steps leaning on a driftwood staff, small as a salt-cured apple, with white hair braided into ropes thick as ship's cable and eyes the grey-green of deep water. She looked at Ulyana first, then at me, and her mouth did something complicated.

"Tamsin Zhou," she said. "The salt smuggler." She spat, ceremonially, into the gutter. "Veyra's last lead singer, selling seasoning."

"Word travels," I managed.

"Word sings, boy. Word always sings." She thumped her staff. "Maren. Storm-singer, retired-ish. You'll stay in my house, because your alternatives are worse, and because I've been waiting nine years for someone from that choir to walk past my door so I could tell him he's a coward."

"Maren—" Brannic started, hand drifting toward his knife out of pure professional habit.

"He is," she said, not looking away from me. "Aren't you?"

I'd have liked to argue. I'd rehearsed arguments for nine years—the pragmatism of silence, the arithmetic of survival, one voice against a kingdom. But standing there in the drizzle with her looking at me like I was a hymn left unfinished, none of my arithmetic would come.

"The house," I said instead. "You offered a house."

Her laugh was thunder rolled very small. "Coward *and* a negotiator. Fine. In."

* * *

Maren's house stood at the top of the gifted quarter, three stories of weather-grey timber with a widow's walk that had clearly never hosted a widow and a kitchen that smelled permanently of kelp tea. She fed us fish stew and interrogated us with the efficiency of a customs officer.

Pip sat beside me, drumming soft rhythms on the table edge with two fingers—his only speech, the stuttering heartbeat language he'd kept when the fever took his words. The twins, Sorrel and Sable, harmonized under their breath while they ate, a habit that made the candle flames lean toward them like eavesdroppers. Ulyana talked with her hands almost as much as her mouth. Brannic watched the windows.

"So," Maren said, refilling my bowl without asking. "The Edict of Ash. You've seen what it does."

"I've seen shrines burned," I said carefully. "Villages emptied."

"I've seen neighbors taken." She set the pot down harder than necessary. "They don't burn the gifted quarter here yet, because Marrowgate pays its tithes and looks the other way. But the wardens come through every month now. Counting heads. Listening at doors." Her eyes went to the twins. "And the harbor master keeps a list of names, because even free-towns learn to kneel eventually."

"Then we leave tomorrow," I said. "North, through the tunnels. There's an old smuggler network under the eastern cliff—my routes. It runs all the way to the high passes."

"And go where?" Maren asked. "Every road south of Thornfell has a checkpoint. Every town has a list. You're carrying four gifted children and a deserter, salt man. Where exactly are you taking them that the crown's long arm doesn't reach?"

"Nowhere," Ulyana said quietly. Everyone looked at her. She was staring into her stew. "That's the point, isn't it? There isn't anywhere. So either we keep running until we drop, or we stop being people who run."

"That's a lovely sentiment," I said. "It pairs beautifully with a shallow grave."

"Tamsin." Brannic's voice, low. He nodded at the window, where the lamplight of the lower terraces had begun to flicker strangely—not with wind.

I felt it before I understood it. A pressure in the air, like the moment before lightning, except wrong—too hot, too dry, tasting of pitch and iron. My skin remembered this feeling from nine years ago, from villages I'd passed at a distance while telling myself the distance was mercy.

"Bell," Maren said.

Down in the harbor quarter, the fog bell began to ring. Not the steady toll of tide-marking. The ragged, urgent clangor of alarm.

* * *

We reached the street as the first torches crested the western stairs.

There must have been forty of them—wardens in ash-grey cloaks, moving in the disciplined files Brannic knew too well, and behind them men with firepots, and behind *them* something worse: a warden-priest in a mask of hammered tin, carrying a censer that swung with a sound like a struck tooth. I knew that censer. Every gifted child in Thornfell knew it. It didn't detect song-magic. It *ached* for it—a hollow instrument that whined when voices came near, guiding hunters by hunger.

"They're not here for a tithe collection," Brannic breathed. "This is a purge. They hit the quarter at night so no one can scatter to the boats."

"Why now?" Sorrel whispered. "Marrowgate pays—"

"Because we came here," I said, and the truth of it landed in my stomach like swallowed stone. Forty wardens, a tin-masked priest, a coordinated night action in a free-town that had knelt for years. They hadn't come for Marrowgate's quiet singers. They'd come following the trail of a girl who woke dragons and the man whose name topped the Edict. "Gods below. We brought this."

"No," Ulyana said sharply. "*They* brought this. Don't you dare carry their work for them."

The first firepot arced over the terraces and burst against a painted door—one with wind chimes, one I'd noticed that morning because a child's chalk drawing of a dragon decorated its frame. Flame climbed the timber like it had been waiting years for permission.

After that, the night stopped being a place and became a noise.

* * *

"The tunnels," I said, and my voice came out flat and cold and useful, the smuggler's voice, the survivor's voice, and I hated how grateful I was for it. "Under the eastern terraces. There's a gate in the salt cellar of the chandlery—I have the key. It runs beneath the quarter and comes out at the sea caves. We can take everyone."

"How many can fit?" Brannic asked.

"All of them, if they move fast and don't panic."

"People always panic," Maren said. She had come out onto her step with her staff planted, watching the fires spread along the lower rows. The tin-masked priest was working up the terraces methodically, censer swinging, and where it swung, doors opened—not by force, but because the people inside walked out singing, faces slack, offering themselves up. Compulsion-work. The crown had learned tricks since the old days. "How long to open your gate?"

"A few minutes once I'm there."

"Then take the children down now, and I'll buy you the minutes."

I turned. "No."

"Yes."

"Maren, they'll kill you."

"They'll try." Her smile showed teeth gone yellow as old ivory. "Boy, I have been singing storms since before your father learned to walk. I have argued with hurricanes and won on points. Forty grey-cloaked men and a man in a tin face do not worry me overmuch." She looked past me, down the burning stair-streets, and her voice dropped into something older and sadder. "But someone holds the gate, Tamsin. Someone always holds the gate. That's the price of the rest of you getting out. I'm old. My song is nearly sung anyway. Let me spend the last verse on something that matters."

"There has to be another way."

"There's always another way," she agreed. "And it costs the same, just paid by someone younger." She shoved me toward the stairs with a strength that belied her size. "Go. Get them out. And salt man—if you run north and bury yourself again after this, I will haunt you. I know storm-ghosts. We're famously persistent."

* * *

I got the cellar gate open in ninety seconds, hands steadier than they had any right to be. The tunnels ran cool and dark beneath the quarter, salt-crusted brick and the smell of the sea bleeding through old cracks, and into them we poured the gifted of Marrowgate—maybe sixty souls, most of them children, herded by parents whose faces were lit orange from the grates above.

Pip went down first, drumming his fingers against the wall to give the little ones a rhythm to follow in the dark—a heartbeat in the blackness, calm and constant. The twins stationed themselves at a bend and sang, very softly, a round that turned fear into forward motion; I watched a sobbing woman straighten her shoulders and keep walking because the harmony wouldn't let her do anything else. Song-magic, small and humble and saving lives one step at a time.

Ulyana stood at the tunnel mouth, counting people through, and above us the night roared.

I climbed the chandlery steps once, just once, to the street door, and looked out through the gap in the shutters.

Maren stood at the head of the great stair where the terraces met, alone, staff planted, hair loose from its cables and streaming in a wind that had no business existing on a still night. The wardens had formed up below her, forty strong, torches held high, and the tin-masked priest raised the censer and spoke words I couldn't hear.

Maren answered by singing.

I have heard Ulyana wake ice. I have heard the twins bend candleflames. I thought I understood the scale of the gift.

I did not understand anything.

The storm-singer opened her mouth and the sky over Marrowgate *tore*. Clouds that weren't there an hour ago boiled up off the Greywash, black-bellied and lightning-veined, and the wind came down the terraces like the fist of something ancient, snatching torches from warden hands, bending the fire back on itself, driving rain in sheets hard enough to raise steam off the burning doors. Thunder cracked so close it felt personal. She wasn't summoning a storm. She *was* the storm, a small woman on a staircase conducting a hurricane with a driftwood baton, and for one glorious minute the entire crown advance simply ceased to exist as an organized thing.

Then the priest lifted the censer higher, and sang back.

I heard it even through the shutter—wrong notes, notes that moved like insects, a counter-melody designed to unravel hers. Wardens staggered upright. The storm shuddered. Maren's voice climbed, cracked, climbed again, and I saw blood on her lips and understood she was spending herself faster than any human reserve should allow, buying minutes with the last of her life because a coward and some children needed time to get down a stairwell.

*Tamsin.* Ulyana's hand on my arm. Her face streaked with soot, eyes wide. "Tamsin, we have to close the gate. We have to go *now*."

"She's still—"

"She's dying," Ulyana said, gentle and brutal, "and she chose it, and if you stand in that doorway she died for nothing. Come *down*."

The last thing I saw through the gap: Maren raising both arms, staff flung wide, pouring everything remaining into one final rising chord as the lightning came down around her like a pillar—

—and then Ulyana pulled me into the dark, and the cellar door boomed shut, and the storm became a memory vibrating in salt-crusted brick.

* * *

We came out of the sea caves near dawn, sixty-some survivors blinking in grey light, salt spray and woodsmoke mingling on the wind. Behind us, Marrowgate's eastern terraces smoldered. The gifted quarter was gone. The harbor master's list would be shorter by morning, and longer somewhere else.

Pip sat on a rock at the tideline, drumming softly on his knees, and the little ones huddled against him because his rhythm was the steadiest thing left in their world. The twins sang the walking-round, barely audible, keeping the fear at bay. Brannic stood watch on the point, jaw set, and I saw him looking at the smoke and understanding, finally and completely, what the badge he'd torn away had really been for.

Ulyana found me at the water's edge. I was doing something useless with a coil of rope.

"She held the gate," I said.

"She held the gate," Ulyana agreed.

"I spent nine years telling myself that staying silent kept people safe. That invisibility was a kind of virtue. That if I just made myself small enough, quiet enough, the fire would pass over me and everyone near me." The rope slipped through my hands. I let it fall. "And last night forty wardens burned a quarter full of strangers because of *me*, and an old woman died on a staircase buying me ninety seconds, and my silence didn't protect a single soul. It never did. It just made me easier to ignore."

Ulyana didn't offer comfort. That's one of the things I was beginning to love about her. She just waited, the way you wait at a graveside.

"I'm done," I said. The words came out of me like a splinter leaving flesh after nine years. "Done hiding. Done being neutral. Neutrality is just a slower way of choosing a side, and I chose theirs every day I kept my mouth shut." I looked east, toward the high passes, toward the capital beyond them, toward the thing wearing Aldric that fed on voices like mine. "If Hollowmoot wants songs, it can have mine. Lead line and all."

Ulyana smiled—small, fierce, grief-stricken. "Maren would say it's about time."

"Maren would say I'm a coward who negotiates."

"She'd also say," Ulyana added quietly, "that word always sings."

Far out on the Greywash, where the dawn was coming up bruised and gold, thunder rolled—long after the storm should have died, rolling inland from the open sea, three times, like a salute. Or a promise.

Brannic came down from the point at a jog, and the look on his face told me the world had gotten worse while we mourned. "Riders on the coast road," he said. "Warden colors. But they're not chasing us." He paused, and I watched him decide whether to say the rest. "They're flying a parley banner. And their captain is asking for a man named Zhou. By name. She says—" his mouth twisted, "—she says the god under the world is screaming louder every day, and she wants to know what we intend to do about it."
