## 8. The Thing Wearing the Crown

I had been a warden captain for eleven years, and in that time I'd learned that the worst truths don't announce themselves. They don't kick down your door at midnight with torches and a warrant. They sit quietly in the corner of a room you've walked past a hundred times, waiting for you to finally look.

The throne room of Verreholm was the corner I should have looked into six years ago.

We were camped in the hollow below Veyra's ruined chapel, which was either the safest place in Thornfell or the most cursed, depending on how much stock you put in dead gods. The fire was small. Tamsin Zhou sat across from it like a man sitting across from an executioner, arms around his knees, watching me the way you watch a snake you've decided not to kill yet but haven't ruled out. Ulyana was curled against Ysolde's foreleg—the dragon being, I will note for the record, the only member of this company who slept like a civilized creature, all ninety tons of her snoring in a register that loosened my teeth.

They wanted to know why I tore off my badge. I'd been dodging the question for two days with soldier's deflections—*bad orders, worse men, you know how it goes*—but Tamsin had that look. The look of a man who has already lost everything once and therefore cannot be threatened by anything you say. It's a terrible look to be on the wrong side of.

So I told them.

* * *

"You've stood guard at the palace," Tamsin said. Not a question. "You were there when they brought the gifted in."

"I was there when they brought everyone in." I fed a stick to the fire because my hands wanted something to do that wasn't shaking. "After the Edict of Ash went out, the dungeons under the palace filled up in four months. Four. We'd spent years telling ourselves the hunt was about order. Dangerous magic, unlicensed singing, all the tidy language on the warrants. And then the wagons started coming in every day, and I did the arithmetic every captain does and pretends he doesn't: nobody burns that many villages over *order*."

"Feeding," Ulyana said softly. She'd said it before, back on the road. She said it now like she was confirming something she'd hoped she'd imagined.

"Let me tell it in order," I said, "because out of order it sounds like madness, and I need it to sound like what it is, which is worse."

The fire popped. Somewhere above us, wind moved through the chapel's broken bell tower, and I swear the old bronze hummed—a low, mournful note, like the building itself remembering how to sing. Tamsin's head came up sharply at the sound. He didn't say anything. Neither did I. There are things a man learns not to comment on.

"My rotation put me on the undercroft stairs," I said. "Third landing. You can see the door to the deep cells from there—the ones that predate Aldric's grandfather, the ones the palace architects' records call *the Quiet Rooms*, which should have been my first warning, because nothing in that building is named honestly." I turned the stick in my fingers. "Twice a week, they take prisoners down. Twice a week, they come back up."

"And?" Tamsin said.

"They come back up empty."

Ulyana made a small sound. Not a gasp. Something more controlled than that, the sound of a girl who has been hunted long enough to ration her fear.

"Not dead-empty," I said quickly. "Breathing. Walking. That's the thing—I want to be clear about this, because I spent three weeks trying to convince myself I'd misread it. They walk. They eat their supper. They answer questions in flat little voices. But—" I stopped. How do you describe a silence? I'm a soldier; I describe things with edges and weights, and this had neither. "You know how a room feels after a choir's finished? Full. Even after everyone leaves, the air holds it a while. These people came up from those rooms and the air around them was *scraped*. Like a bowl licked clean. They'd open their mouths to speak and what came out was words without anything underneath. No weight. A voice is supposed to be a person's whole history coming out of their throat, Captain Zhou. You'd know that better than anyone here."

Tamsin didn't move. But his jaw did something small and terrible, and I understood I'd just described his nightmare to him in detail.

"The gifted came up hollowed," I went on, because stopping would have been mercy and I hadn't earned the right to hand out mercy, least of all to myself. "And the ones who weren't gifted—the smugglers, the debtors, the unlucky—they came up the same way. Whatever it is down there doesn't check papers. It eats what it finds."

"What's down there," Tamsin said. Flat. The question of a man bracing himself.

"That's where it gets worse, and I want you both to appreciate that I genuinely did not think it could."

* * *

Here is what I saw, and I have gone over it perhaps ten thousand times since, looking for the crack in it, the trick, the explanation that lets me sleep.

Six years ago—before the Edict, before the hunts, back when I still believed the crown was a crown and not whatever it actually is—I drew palace interior duty. Honor assignment, they called it. Night watch on the royal apartments, which mostly meant standing in a corridor listening to ministers lie to each other through oak doors.

King Aldric kept strange hours. Everyone knew that much. The man rose before the kitchen fires and was still awake when the night bells rang, and the physicians issued increasingly frantic bulletins about tonics and rest cures, and the court learned to schedule itself around a king who never seemed to stop moving. Ambitious men loved it. A king who never sleeps signs twice as many decrees.

What nobody said—and what I only pieced together later, from chambermaids and a physician's assistant who drank too much and talked too freely—was that Aldric *couldn't* sleep. Hadn't in years. Not since the winter the old queen died and something changed behind his eyes.

I saw it myself once. My post took me past the king's study at the hour when the candles are changed, and the door stood open, and Aldric was alone at his desk, and for maybe ten seconds he looked up and our eyes met.

There was nothing behind them.

I don't mean cruelty. I've seen cruel eyes; cruelty has appetite in it, interest, a kind of horrible vitality. This was different. This was the look of a house with the family gone out of it—furniture still arranged, hearth still warm, but no one home, and something else wearing the family's clothes and doing its best with the smile. For those ten seconds the King of Verreholm looked at me the way I imagine a fish looks at the hook it hasn't felt yet: with vast, patient, alien attention.

Then he blinked, and Aldric was back—or the shape of Aldric—and he said, in his normal voice, "Cold night, Captain," and I bowed and walked away and vomited into a hedge at the end of the corridor like a first-year recruit.

"Cold night, Captain," Ulyana repeated, very quietly. "That's what it said?"

"That's what *he* said. Or it used his mouth to say it. By then I couldn't tell you where one ended and the other began." I threw the burnt stick into the dark. "The physician's assistant told me the king screamed in his sleep, back when he still slept. Screamed and begged and called it by name. Then the screaming stopped, and the sleeping stopped, and the Edicts started. Draw your own line between those points. I drew mine."

"And the name," Tamsin said. His voice had gone very quiet, the way water goes quiet right before it freezes. "Did the assistant give you the name?"

"He never heard it clearly. Said it sounded like—" I frowned, reaching for it. "Like a lot of voices saying one word. A crowd packed into a single throat. The assistant called it *the Moot*. Court gossip called the king's moods the Hollowmoot, the way you'd nickname anything you're afraid to name properly. Hollowmoot." I shrugged, hating how small the word sounded for what it stood for. "A joke that stopped being funny."

Ysolde stirred. I felt it before I heard it—a pressure change, the way the air leans when something enormous pays attention. Her eye, the one nearest our fire, slid open. It was the color of a banked coal, and it fixed on me with an ancient, terrible patience.

"*Hollowmoot,*" the dragon said, and her voice came out like a landslide deciding to be polite about it. "*So it has crawled back up to wear a crown again.*"

The fire shrank. I swear to you, the actual flames leaned away from her.

"You know it," Tamsin said. He was on his feet now, and I watched nine years fall off him—not youth, exactly, but the softness age puts over wounds.

"I knew its grandmothers." Ysolde's nostrils flared, exhaling a thread of smoke that smelled of cold iron. "It is old, little warden. Older than crowns. Older than the gods it ate to learn how. Your Veyra sang the world's edges into shape, and things like the Hollowmoot sang *counterpoint*—hunger set to music. It does not hate. Hatred requires wanting you to suffer. It only wants you *emptied*, the way a man wants bread. The Edict of Ash is not policy. It is a shopping list."

Nobody spoke for a while. The wind found the bell tower again and made it moan, and this time even Ulyana flinched at the sound.

"So the trials are theater," I said, because someone had to say it and my mouth has always run ahead of my judgment. "The sentences are theater. Six years of burnings and brandings and I stood at attention through all of it, and it was never justice. It was a *larder*."

"Yes," Tamsin said simply.

He wasn't looking at me with hatred, which somehow was worse. He was looking at me with the weary recognition of one survivor meeting another—the man who ran from his gift, the man who enforced the hunt for it, both of us standing in the same firelight having failed the same people in different directions.

"Why tell us this now?" Ulyana asked me. "Why defect? You burned villages too. You said so."

"I did." I let that sit. It deserved to sit. "And then last spring I was ordered to escort a wagon of condemned to the Quiet Rooms instead of the pyre, and I asked why the change, and my commander looked at me with the same scraped-bowl emptiness I'd seen in the prisoners, and he smiled with muscles that moved half a beat late, and he said, 'His Majesty has developed a taste for them fresh.' And I understood that the thing I served wasn't cruel, because cruelty would at least have meant it was *human*. I tore off my badge in the barracks yard and walked out through the front gate, because I figured if the thing on the throne wanted me, it knew where to find me." I laughed, and it came out ugly. "Turns out it sent wardens instead. So much for dignity."

Ulyana reached over and put her hand on my arm. Fifteen years old, half-frozen a week ago, hunted since childhood, and she was comforting *me*. I've thought about that every day since. I suspect I'll think about it the rest of my life, however long the Hollowmoot leaves me.

"So we go to the capital," Tamsin said. "To the throne room. To the thing wearing the king."

"We?" I raised an eyebrow. "Captain, I've seen what's under that palace. You're talking about walking into a larder and asking the butcher for a duet."

"No," Tamsin said, and there was something in his face I recognized then and understand now—the first crack of light through nine years of shuttered windows. "I'm talking about finishing a song."

Above us, in the ruined tower, the broken bell hummed once more—a low, patient note, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Or waking up.

Ysolde opened her other eye, and both of them glowed now, coals breathing brighter, and the old dragon said:

"Then you had best hurry, little singers. It has been feeding a long time. And it is nearly full enough to come looking for what's left."
