## 9. Dragonfire Guttering

I had imagined meeting a dragon a hundred times before I actually met one, and in every imagining I was braver. In the imaginings I strode up to her with my chin high and my voice steady and said something wise that dragons would remember for centuries. In reality, I climbed the last mile of the Ashen Stair on my hands and knees, wheezing like a bellows with a hole in it, while Brannic walked behind me carrying Pip on his shoulders and pretending not to enjoy it.

"You know," Brannic said, "in the wardens we'd have called this terrain 'a light stroll.'"

"In Marrowgate we'd have called you a liar," I said.

Tamsin said nothing. He hadn't said much since the tunnels, since Old Maren's song came down the mountain behind us like a landslide made of music. He walked at the front of our ragged line the way he always did—half a step ahead, as if he could outpace his own grief if he just kept moving—but there was something new in his silence now. It used to be a wall. Now it was more like a held breath.

The twins, Sorrel and Sable, brought up the rear, humming low harmonies to keep Pip's feet moving. Even exhausted, even hunted, they couldn't help it. Voices like theirs didn't know how to stop. And above us all, the sky did that thing the sky does near old magic: it went quiet. Not silent—quiet. The difference between an empty room and a room where someone has just stopped speaking.

"Is she really here?" Pip asked, from his perch. His voice was still a novelty to all of us, thin and rusted from disuse, spent so rarely that each word sounded like a coin paid out of a nearly empty purse. "A real dragon?"

"She's real," Tamsin said. "Whether there's much left of her is another question."

That was the thing nobody wanted to say plainly: Ysolde was dying. Everyone in every smuggler's tale agreed on that. The last dragon of Thornfell, chained by bargain to the crown since before Aldric's grandfather's grandfather wore it, dimming year by year like a lamp running out of oil. Some tales said she slept. Some said she was already dead and her bones were warm because mountains are warm. Brannic, who had been a warden captain and knew what the crown knew, only said: "She's on the lists. Maintenance lists. There's a warden detail that brings her... provisions." He wouldn't say what the provisions were. I think he didn't fully know himself, and what he suspected he couldn't bear to say aloud.

We crested the final ridge as the sun went down, and there she was.

* * *

Words fail me, which for me is saying something.

Ysolde lay coiled in a caldera like a ship run aground in its own harbor, vast beyond sense, her scales the grey of old ash with veins of ember-light running through them the way lightning lives inside a stormcloud. Chains crossed her—not iron, not quite; they looked like cooled lava, like promises made solid—and ran from her limbs and her neck and, horribly, her throat, down into the rock, down into whatever deep vault the crown had built to hold the terms of an ancient bargain. She was so large that my eyes kept trying to shrink her, kept insisting she was a hill, a ruin, a trick of the dusk. Her breathing moved the whole caldera. When she exhaled, the wind changed direction.

And she was dim. That was the worst part. The embers in her scales guttered like candles in a draft, flaring weakly, sinking, flaring again. A dragon should burn like a forge. Ysolde burned like the last coal in a grate on a cold morning, and everyone looking at her understood without being told that when that coal went out, nothing would ever relight it.

"Oh," Pip said softly, from Brannic's shoulders. "Oh, she's *sad*."

Dragons don't cry, I learned later. But they grieve in fire, or they would if they had any left.

We picked our way down into the caldera, and I felt her awareness settle over us before we were halfway there—a pressure like standing under a waterfall of attention. The twins stopped humming. Even the wind seemed to hold its place.

"A warden," Ysolde said.

Her voice didn't come through the air so much as through the ground, through our boots, through the bones of us. It was a voice like a landslide deciding to be patient. Brannic went very still.

"Former," he managed.

"There is no former. Not for the ones who wear the crown's leash. Only deferred." One vast eye opened, amber shot through with grey, and regarded him with the weary contempt of someone who has watched ten thousand small betrayals and expects nothing better. "You smell of the Edict. You smell of ash."

"I tore off my badge rather than burn children," Brannic said. His voice shook, but he didn't look away. "You can check. The crown keeps records of everything it hates."

The eye held him a long moment. Then it slid to me, and I felt myself weighed the way you weigh a coin by biting it.

"And you. The little singer who wakes sleepers." Something moved through her scales then, a ripple of ember-light, faint but real. "They told stories about your kind in the old days. Before the crown got hungry. Do you know why your gift frightens kings, child? Because waking is the one thing power cannot do to itself. Power can command, can punish, can devour. It cannot wake. Waking belongs to singers."

"I'm not much of a singer," I said. "I'm mostly a fugitive who hums."

"All great voices begin as fugitives from themselves." The eye closed again. "Why have you come? To see the last dragon before the crown finishes its slow meal? Come closer, then. Look at what a bargain costs. Let it be a lesson."

"We've come to ask for your fire," Tamsin said.

He said it simply, the way he says everything that matters, and the whole caldera seemed to lean in. Ysolde's breathing paused—one breath, two—and the embers along her spine flared and sank.

"My fire," she said. "Yes. They ask for my fire. Do you know where it goes, little ones? Every year, the warden detail comes with their carts and their chains of office, and they stand at the edge of my chain-length, and the crown reaches through the bargain—" her whole body shuddered, a tectonic event, "—and it *drinks*. It drinks and drinks and calls it maintenance. Sixty years I have fed that thing on the throne, and it grows no smaller and I grow no brighter. Do you understand what I am telling you? I am not dying of age. Dragons do not die of age. I am being *eaten*, slowly, politely, with paperwork."

Pip slid off Brannic's shoulders and stood there staring up at her, and I saw the drummer boy do the thing he does, the thing that made me love him: he saw a creature in pain and forgot to be afraid.

"What eats you?" he asked.

And Ysolde laughed. It was a terrible sound, ash and gravel and something underneath it that might once have been music, and the laugh guttered out almost as soon as it began.

"The crown," she said. "But the crown is only its glove. Its name—the name it wore before it learned to wear kings—is Hollowmoot."

The word landed in me like a stone dropped down a well. I heard it fall and fall and never heard it land.

"It was a god once," Ysolde went on, quieter now, the way you speak over graves. "In the elder dark, before your Veyra, before the singing gods, there were things that hungered instead of sang. Hollowmoot ate gods, one by one, drank their voices down to silence. Your Veyra fought it. Your Veyra lost—or did not lose, but did not win, which is often worse. And when the fight was done, Hollowmoot was wounded and starving and clever, and it did the cleverest thing a starving god can do. It found a throne and crawled inside it. It has worn your kings ever since. Aldric is only the latest coat it wears. The Edict of Ash is not cruelty, children. Cruelty wastes the meat. It is a *larder*. Every gifted voice it burns, it keeps. Every singer it silences, it swallows. It is building itself back up, voice by voice, god by stolen god, and when it is strong enough it will not need the crown anymore. It will need a world."

The wind came down into the caldera and died. Nobody spoke. Somewhere above us, a star came out and seemed embarrassed about it.

Tamsin stood very still. I watched his hands—they were shaking, just slightly, the hands of a man hearing his own nightmare read back to him in a stranger's voice. Nine years he had believed Veyra abandoned them. Fell silent mid-prayer, mid-hymn, mid-breath, and left his choir to die around him. Nine years he had carried that abandonment like a stone in his chest.

"Fell silent," Tamsin said at last, and his voice cracked on the second word. "It didn't fall silent. It was *taken*."

"Now you begin to understand," Ysolde said. "Your god screams beneath the world, little singer. I hear it sometimes, through the chains, through the bargain, when the crown drinks deep. A hymn with no ending. It has been unfinished for nine years, and an unfinished prayer is a wound that cannot close—for the god, and for the one who was meant to finish it." The amber eye turned to Tamsin, and there was something in it that was almost gentle, in the way a glacier is gentle to the valley it carved. "Do you want to know the cruelest part? Hollowmoot fears one thing. Not armies. Not wardens. It fears a finished prayer. It silenced your god mid-word because a completed hymn is a door it cannot lock."

* * *

We made camp that night in the caldera's lee, close enough to feel Ysolde's warmth radiating off her like a hearth the size of a cathedral. Nobody suggested leaving. I think we all understood, without discussing it, that we had come seeking a weapon and found a witness instead, and that the witness was more important.

Brannic sat apart, sharpening a knife that didn't need sharpening, wearing the face of a man doing arithmetic he doesn't like. I went and sat beside him.

"Say it," I said.

"Hollowmoot." He tested the blade against his thumb. "Six years I served the crown. Six years I told myself the Edict was fear, was politics, was Aldric being cruel the way kings are cruel. And the whole time I was guarding a larder." He looked up at the dragon's dim bulk against the stars. "I burned villages, Ulyana. I told myself it was mercy, quick fires, that the gifted died clean. And now I find out they didn't die at all. They're in there. Behind his eyes. Singing."

"Then we get them out."

"With what? We're seven. Seven, and half of us children, and our grand weapon is a dragon who hasn't enough fire left to roast a goose." He sheathed the knife harder than necessary. "Forgive me if hope needs a moment to find its boots."

"It'll find them," I said. "Hope's like you. Grumbles first, shows up anyway."

That startled a laugh out of him—short, rusty, real. From across the camp, Tamsin watched us, and something in his face had shifted. Not softened exactly. Recalibrated. He had spent nine years believing his god had left him, and tonight he had learned his god had been screaming for him the entire time, and I could see both halves of that knowledge fighting for possession of his face: the fury at nine wasted years, and the terrible, terrifying hope that the prayer might still be finished.

Later, when the others slept, I couldn't. I kept hearing it: *an unfinished prayer is a wound that cannot close.* I got up and walked toward the great dim shape of her, because grief draws the grieving, and I thought maybe she'd let me sit nearby the way you sit near a dying fire.

Her eye opened before I was within twenty paces. Of course it did.

"Couldn't sleep either?" I asked.

"I have not truly slept in sixty years. The bargain holds me at the edge of it, forever falling, never landing." The ember-veins pulsed slowly, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. "You should be sleeping, waker. Tomorrow you will need your voice."

"That's why I'm here." I wrapped my arms around my knees. "Ysolde—I heard what you said. About the crown drinking your fire. About how you're almost out." I made myself look at her, at the ash-grey and the guttering light. "How much do you have left? Truly."

The eye regarded me for a long time. Dragons, I was learning, do not lie, but they choose their truths the way misers choose coins.

"One ember," she said finally. "One true flame. I have hoarded it sixty years against a need worth the spending. I had begun to think there would be no such need—that I would gutter out with my one flame unspent, and that would be my final shame." A plume of breath moved the hair back from my face; it smelled of cold stone and old smoke. "Why do you ask, little singer? What need could seven fugitives possibly bring to the last dragon of Thornfell?"

And I told her. All of it—the shrine, the snow, the waystation, the chapel ruins, Marrowgate burning, Maren holding the gate with a storm in her throat, the dungeons we hadn't seen yet but already knew were full. I told her about the shard of god-bone and the prayer with no ending, though I didn't yet know how I knew these things would matter; I only knew that songs tell you things ahead of the words, the way weather tells you things ahead of the rain. And when I finished, the great eye had not blinked once, and the embers along her flank had risen from guttering to a low, steady glow, the first brightness I'd seen in her.

"A finished prayer," she rumbled. "A door it cannot lock." The ground trembled faintly with something that was not quite laughter. "Nine years I have felt that hymn snagged in the world's throat like a bone. And the answer has been walking around in a smuggler's coat refusing to sing."

"He's afraid," I said. "Not of dying. Of leading. The last time he led, everyone he loved stopped mid-note."

"Yes," Ysolde said. "And you are afraid of nothing, I suppose?"

"I'm afraid of everything. I just sing anyway. It's the family curse."

Something moved through her then—a sound too big to be a chuckle and too tired to be a roar. Then she lowered her head, the vast skull coming down and down until one amber eye hung level with me, close enough that I could see my own reflection in it, small and stubborn.

"Little singer," she said. "Come closer. There is a thing I have not done in sixty years, and I find, tonight, with your ridiculous hope sitting there warming itself at my fire, that I wish to try."

"What thing?"

"Sing."

* * *

I don't know how to describe what happened next without lying, so I'll tell it plain and let plainness do its work.

I started to hum. Just a hum, the way I'd thawed the ice in the smuggler's cave, the way I'd rocked Pip out of nightmares—nothing shaped, nothing meant, just a thread of sound offered to a creature who hadn't heard a willing song in sixty years. And Ysolde listened. The whole mountain listened. And then, deeper in her chest than any bell, something answered.

It was not loud. That was the miracle of it. It was the smallest sound I have ever heard come out of anything that size—a single note, low and cracked and ancient, like the first syllable of a word forgotten by everyone who spoke it. And in her throat, where the chains pressed tightest, where the bargain bit deepest, a light kindled.

One ember. Red-gold, no bigger than a candleflame, rising in the dark cavern of her mouth like a star being born in a burnt-out sky. It lit her scales from within, and for one moment the whole caldera glowed, and I saw her as she must have been six hundred years ago: not a dying prisoner but a pillar of morning, a creature the size of a season, magnificent past bearing.

Then the chains pulled tight.

I felt it happen—the bargain waking, the crown reaching, that cold grasping attention sliding down from the north like a hand into a purse. The ember flickered. Ysolde's jaw began, slowly, agonizingly, to close, the old terms dragging her obedience shut like a door.

And I did the stupid, wonderful, fatal thing that I am apparently put on this earth to do.

I sang *louder*.

I threw everything at it—the cave-song, the storm-song, the lullaby I'd hummed to Pip, Maren's last rising phrase that I'd carried out of Marrowgate burning in my chest like a coal I refused to set down. I sang at the chains, at the bargain, at the cold hand reaching down from Verreholm, and I sang *wake up* without using the word, because waking is the one thing power cannot do to itself, and I am a singer, and waking belongs to us.

The ember rose.

It cleared her teeth. It cleared the closing dark. It hung in the night air above the caldera, a single drop of dragonfire, sixty years hoarded, and Ysolde's eye met mine and I understood without words: *this is the one. This is the need worth the spending. Take it where it must go.*

The ember drifted to me like a leaf deciding. It settled against my sternum, sank through skin and rib, and lodged somewhere behind my heart, where it burned—not painfully, but permanently, the way truth burns, the way a name burns when it's yours. Warmth spread through my chest, and I gasped, and the night went ordinary again, and Ysolde's head sank back onto the stone, dimmer than before, but her eye—her eye was bright.

"Well," she rumbled, drowsily, terribly satisfied. "Now it begins."

"What begins?" I whispered. My voice came out scorched at the edges.

"The end of the long meal." The eye was closing. "You carry my last fire, waker. And I heard, in your song, in the shape of it beneath the words—the unfinished prayer. You are going to Verreholm. You are going to finish it." The ember-light in her scales gathered once, faintly, around her throat, like a hand pressed to a wound. "Listen well, little singer, for I will say this once and then I must rest sixty years of resting: Hollowmoot devours what it hears. Every voice raised against it becomes its food. That is its nature and its strength and its law. But a finished prayer is not food. A finished prayer is a *verdict*. When you stand before the thing on the throne and sing Veyra's hymn to its ending, you will not be feeding it. You will be sentencing it. And it knows this, and it is afraid, and a starving god that is afraid is most dangerous in the moment before the door closes."

"Then we'll close the door."

"Mm." The eye slitted, gleaming. "One more thing. The prayer you must finish—it cannot be sung alone. A hymn of the singing gods wants a choir. It wants many throats, one breath. Remember that when the time comes, and remember this also: the thing will offer you bargains, because bargains are how it was born and bargains are how it binds. Refuse every bargain. Finish the song. No matter what it holds out to you—no matter whose voice it wears, no matter what it promises to give back—"

She stopped. Her breathing slowed. For a horrible moment I thought she had gone out entirely, and I was on my feet with my hand pressed flat against her warm grey flank before the ember-veins pulsed once, twice, faint and stubborn.

"Go to sleep, little singer," she murmured. "You have a war to sing. And when you stand in that hall, and the thing looks at you with the king's stolen eyes—sing louder."

I walked back to camp under the stars with a dragon's last flame beating behind my breastbone like a second heart, and everyone was asleep except Tamsin, who sat up watching me come, and whose face in the firelight had gone strange and still.

"Ulyana," he said quietly. "What did she give you?"

I sat down beside him. Behind my ribs, the ember pulsed. Northward, past the ashlands, past the drowned villages and the caged voices, the capital waited with its hungry crown, and somewhere beneath the world, a god screamed the same unfinished note it had screamed for nine years.

"Fire," I said. "And a name. And instructions." I looked at him, at the man who had sworn never to lead again, and I saw the fear in him and the hope underneath it, wrestling for the same ground. "Tamsin—it has a name. Hollowmoot. The god that ate gods. And Veyra wasn't silent. Veyra was taken. Mid-prayer. And the prayer can still be finished—but not by me. Not alone." I took a breath, and the ember warmed my ribs like a hand. "It needs a choir. It needs a lead."

The fire crackled. Somewhere far north, I swear the wind changed direction.

Tamsin stared into the flames for a long, long time. And when he finally spoke, his voice was the quietest I had ever heard it, and the most terrified, and—if you knew how to listen, and I did—the closest to yes.

"Tell me everything," he said.
