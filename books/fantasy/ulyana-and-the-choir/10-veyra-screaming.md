## 10. Veyra Screaming

The hole in the world was exactly where Ulyana said it would be, which annoyed me, because I'd spent three days insisting it wasn't.

"It's behind the waterfall," she'd said, with the serene confidence of a nineteen-year-old who had never once been wrong in front of me and therefore saw no reason to start.

"Nothing is behind the waterfall," I'd said. "Waterfalls are nature's way of saying *nothing back here but wet rocks.*"

It was, of course, behind the waterfall. A seam in the cliff face, narrow as a coffin lid stood on end, breathing cold air that smelled of stone and old iron and—beneath all that, faint as a memory of a memory—of incense. Chapel incense. The kind Veyra's priests used to burn by the pound until the whole mountain smelled like a funeral for something sweet.

I stood at the lip of that seam for a long time while the waterfall roared past my boots and soaked through my coat, because some part of me understood that once I went in, I would come out different or not at all.

"You're doing the thing," Ulyana said.

"What thing."

"The thing where you stand very still and make your face into a locked door."

"I don't have a face like that."

"You absolutely do. Brannic says you've been doing it since Marrowgate. He says it's like watching a man nail his own shutters closed before a storm." She ducked under my elbow and slipped into the dark without waiting, which was her way of winning arguments: simply refusing to hold them in a location where I could respond.

I followed her. Of course I followed her. Nine years of careful, cowardly silence, and this girl had undone it in a snowdrift with four bars of humming. There was probably a lesson in that. I declined to learn it.

* * *

The passage went down the way grief goes down—not steeply, not all at once, but steadily, so that you only noticed the depth when you looked back and couldn't see where you'd started. The walls changed as we descended. First the honest grey granite of Thornfell, veined with quartz that caught our lantern light like frost. Then older stone, blacker, shot through with seams of something pale and dense that made my teeth ache when the light touched it.

"God-bone," Ulyana said, dragging her fingertips along one of the pale seams. "Ysolde says the mountain grew around him after he fell. Like a scab. Like the world trying to heal over a wound that wouldn't close."

"Ysolde says a lot of things for someone who's been asleep since before my grandmother's grandmother learned to walk."

"She's earned it."

She had, I conceded privately. You earn a certain authority by outliving empires. It didn't mean I had to enjoy being lectured by a lizard the size of a cathedral whose idea of encouragement was telling me I smelled like fear and salt.

The tunnel opened without warning into a cavern so large our lantern became a joke—a single candle insult offered to a cathedral of dark. The light found walls far off to either side, found a floor that sloped down and down toward something that glowed.

Not firelight. Not moonlight. A dull, bruised red-gold, pulsing slow, like the ember of a banked forge the size of a city. And threaded through the glow, rising from it, were shapes—pale columns, ribs, vertebrae the size of oaks, a skeleton of god-bone half-melted into the rock, arranged wrong, arranged *cruelly*, as though something had taken Veyra's remains and built them into a cage around itself.

Or a nest.

"Oh," Ulyana whispered beside me. Her hand found mine in the dark, small and cold and gripping hard. "Oh, Tamsin. It's bigger than Ysolde said."

Everything was bigger than Ysolde said. That was rather the theme of the last month of my life.

We picked our way down the slope, boots crunching on gravel that crunched wrong—too light, too brittle—and it took me an embarrassingly long time to understand what we were walking on. When I finally held the lantern low and saw, I wished I hadn't.

Bones. Small ones. Human ones, mostly, sorted into drifts like tidewrack. Ribs and finger bones and the delicate architecture of inner ears. And scattered among them, catching the ember-light, things that were not bone at all: rings, buttons, a warden's badge gone green with age, a child's wooden whistle.

Voices. The drifts were made of voices. Everything Hollowmoot had ever eaten, excreted here when it was done, the way an owl coughs up what it cannot digest.

Ulyana made a sound low in her throat and turned away. I didn't have the luxury. Someone had to look. Someone always has to look; that's the job nobody applies for.

* * *

The shard was Ulyana's idea, or rather Ysolde's idea delivered through Ulyana, who had become annoyingly fluent in dragon over the past week. Near the base of the great rib-cage, she said. Where the bone met the stone. A piece that had broken free when the mountain scabbed over. *Take it,* Ysolde had said, in that voice like continents shifting. *Let the man hear what he buried.*

I found it wedged in a crevice at the foot of the largest rib: a sliver of god-bone the length of my forearm, pale as milk glass, warm. Warm, in a cavern cold enough to see our breath. It hummed against my palms—not sound, exactly, but the *place* where sound would go, the negative space of a note, like pressing your ear to a door and hearing nothing but knowing someone is on the other side.

I knew that feeling intimately. I had been living inside it for nine years.

"Don't," I said, to myself, to Ulyana, to the god in the bone. My hands were already lifting it. Traitors, both of them.

"Tamsin—"

"If it takes my voice, I want you to know I told you so, and I want it noted in whatever record Brannic is keeping."

"He's not keeping a record."

"Then carve it somewhere. *He told you so.*"

And I pressed the shard against my sternum, over my heart, the way a choir leader holds the tuning bell, and I listened.

* * *

You need to understand what I expected. I expected nothing. That had been the arrangement for nine years—me and the silence, a marriage of mutual convenience. Veyra fell silent mid-prayer, mid-*hymn*, with forty-two voices of mine hanging on his last sustained note like laundry on a line, and then the note ended and the god ended and my choir ended, all in the space between one heartbeat and the next, and what was left was me, standing in the ruins of the chapel with smoke in my lungs and a promise in my mouth: *never again.* Never sing. Never lead. Never reach for that current again, because the last time I did, it carried everyone I loved over a falls and left me standing dry on the bank.

So I expected nothing.

What I got was screaming.

It came up through the shard and through my chest and through my teeth, and I went to my knees on the drifts of the dead, and I would have dropped the shard except my hands had locked around it like a drowning man's. The scream wasn't sound—it was the *memory* of sound, the shape of a voice pressed flat and folded small and stuffed into a space too tight for it, and it had been screaming for six hundred years, and no one had heard it, because gods scream below the range of mortal ears unless you hold a piece of their body against your heart like an idiot kneeling in a grave.

And beneath the screaming—threaded through it, wrapped around it, the way a vine grows through a fence—I heard the prayer.

His prayer. *Our* prayer. The hymn we'd been singing when he fell. I knew every note of it; I'd written the arrangement myself, back when I was twenty-four and arrogant and believed that leading a god's choir meant something would always catch us if we fell. It came through the shard broken, truncated, amputated at the exact bar where everything had ended—the final verse, the binding verse, the words that were supposed to be sung *together*, call and answer, god and choir, and it just stopped. Hung there. An unfinished sentence six centuries long.

*Hollowmoot didn't kill him,* I thought, dazed, the scream pouring through me. *It caught him mid-word.*

That was the horror of it. That was the thing I'd never understood, standing in the chapel ruins, blaming myself, blaming Veyra, blaming the universe's general policy of cruelty. A god doesn't die mid-prayer. A god can't. But a god can be *interrupted*—and if the prayer is never finished, if the last note never lands, then the god isn't dead. He's *held*. Suspended in the gap between the question and the answer, the way a sword hangs in the air when the arm swinging it is seized from behind.

Veyra wasn't gone. Veyra was *choking*.

And the thing wearing Aldric's crown had been sitting on top of him for six hundred years, feeding on the voices of everyone gifted enough to hear the difference, growing fat on stolen song, keeping the prayer unfinished because a finished prayer would—

Would what?

The shard pulsed against my chest, impatient, and the answer came not as words but as certainty, the way you know fire burns without anyone explaining combustion. A finished prayer is a door closing. A finished prayer is a name spoken all the way through. Hollowmoot was a parasite that lived in the unfinished—in gaps, in silences, in the space between a question and its answer—and it could not abide completion. It devoured what it heard, yes, but more than that: it *needed* the hymn to stop. Every voice it ate was another throat that might have finished the verse. The Edict of Ash wasn't hunger alone. It was *fear*. Six hundred years of a god-thing flinching away from the end of a song.

"Tamsin." Ulyana's hands were on my face, wiping tears I hadn't noticed. My nose was bleeding; I could feel it cooling on my lip. "Tamsin, come back. What did you hear? What did you—"

"He's alive," I said. My voice came out wrecked, scraped raw, and I laughed, because of course it did, of course the first full sentences I'd spoken in years would be delivered kneeling in a pit of bones with a nosebleed. "Ulyana. He's *alive*. He's been alive this whole time. We've been mourning a man who's drowning."

Her face did something complicated—joy and horror fighting for the same real estate. "The screaming. That's him?"

"That's him." I looked up at the vast dim ember-glow, at the cage of ribs built around it, and for the first time in nine years I let myself look directly, let myself *aim* my attention at it instead of flinching away. "Six hundred years, mid-prayer. Can you imagine? It's like—" I groped for it. "You know when you sneeze and it doesn't come? Now imagine that forever. Imagine the whole world holding its breath and calling it death."

"And the prayer—if it finished—"

"It kills the parasite. It has to. That's why it's kept unfinished. That's the whole architecture of the thing." I got to my feet, legs shaking, shard still clutched to my chest like a compass needle that had finally found north. "Hollowmoot lives in the gap. So we close the gap."

Ulyana stared at me. Then at the ember. Then at me again, and I watched the arithmetic run behind her eyes, quick and merciless the way her mind was. "One voice," she said slowly. "Could one voice finish it? Mine? Yours?"

I almost said yes. I wanted to say yes—wanted it so badly my mouth was already shaping the word, because a solo was simple, a solo was controllable, a solo meant I could walk into the throne hall alone and spare everyone, spare *her*, do the noble stupid thing I'd been rehearsing since Marrowgate burned.

But the shard was still against my chest, and the prayer was still in my skull, and the prayer itself answered for me. Call and answer. That was its structure. That was its *nature*. The final verse wasn't a melody; it was a *weave*—four lines interlocking, each one incomplete without the others, designed for a choir, for many throats, for harmony so true that the separate voices stopped being separate. Veyra had built the hymn that way deliberately. Gods build doors the way they need them built.

"No," I said, and the word cost me. "No. It needs a choir. Four lines, interlocked—no single voice can carry them all, and if you tried, Hollowmoot would eat you before the second bar. It eats what it hears, remember? One singer is a meal." I looked down at the drifts of small bones, and then, deliberately, away. "A choir is a *weapon*."

"A choir," Ulyana repeated. And then, with the terrible gentleness she sometimes deployed on me, like a blade wrapped in wool: "Tamsin. You haven't led a choir since—"

"I know when I led a choir."

"—and the strays. Pip can't sing at all, he drums. The twins fight like cats. Old Sera's voice cracks above the staff now. And Ysolde hasn't sung since before the crown existed, and half her fire's gone out, and—"

"Yes."

"—and you'd be singing lead. Again. Into the mouth of the thing that took the last choir you led."

"*Yes.*"

She stopped. Studied me. Outside of battle, I don't think I'd ever raised my voice at her before, and she was recalibrating, the way she did, fast and thorough.

"You understand what you're saying," she said quietly.

"I'm saying I knelt in a pit of the dead today and listened to my god scream for six hundred years, and the entire time I thought he'd abandoned *us*." My hands had stopped shaking. That should have frightened me more than it did. "I spent nine years being quiet because I thought silence was safety. It turns out the silence was the cage. All of it. Him, the king, the gifted, every burned village on the Edict's list—all of it held shut by one unfinished song." I lifted the shard, and its glow reflected in her wide eyes. "So we finish it. Whatever it costs. Because I have finally, *finally* worked out that the price of staying silent was never zero. I was just paying it in installments, and other people were making the payments."

For a long moment Ulyana said nothing. The ember-glow breathed against the far wall, slow and monstrous and, I could hear it now, terrified. Six hundred years of terror. Even monsters, it turns out, know what's coming for them eventually.

Then she smiled—that crooked, reckless smile that had gotten us thrown out of two taverns and one temple—and stuck out her hand.

"Choir of strays," she said. "Led by the most reluctant choirmaster in the history of music. Against a god-eating god. On the steps of the throne."

"When you say it like that it sounds insane."

"How would you say it?"

I looked at the shard. Somewhere inside it, beneath the screaming, the unfinished verse hung waiting, patient as a held breath, and for the first time I heard it not as a wound but as an invitation. *Call,* it said. *Waiting on the answer.*

"Rehearsal's at dawn," I said, and shook her hand.
