## 11. The Unbinding Rite

We learned what the rite would cost us on a Tuesday, which felt rude. Catastrophes, in my limited experience, should at least have the decency to arrive at dramatic hours—midnight, say, or during a thunderstorm—not while you're chewing on hardtack and complaining about it.

Ysolde told us. Or rather, Ysolde breathed a single curl of smoke that shaped itself into words above her snout, because the last dragon in Thornfell considered actual speech an extravagance she'd given up sometime before my grandmother's grandmother was a rumor.

*The unbinding is not sung alone,* the smoke said. *It is sung in harmony. Many voices. True voices. And the thing on the throne hears everything it devours.*

"Meaning what, exactly?" Brannic asked, arms crossed, wearing his deserter's face—the one that pretended he hadn't spent twenty years being exactly what he'd deserted.

Tamsin translated without looking up from the fire. He'd gotten good at reading smoke. Nine years of silence will teach a man to listen with his whole body. "Meaning if we sing at Hollowmoot, it hears us. All of us. Every voice in the choir becomes a door it can walk through."

*A door it will walk through,* Ysolde's smoke corrected. *It has never met a voice it did not try to swallow.*

I remember setting down my hardtack very carefully, as if it might notice my hands shaking and take advantage.

"So we're not singing *to* it," I said. "We're singing *at* something that eats songs."

"Yes," Tamsin said.

"And it eats what it hears."

"Yes."

"And we're going to sing anyway."

He looked up then, and there was something terrible and gentle in his face, the way there always was when he talked about Veyra. "The prayer has to be finished. That's the only way to unbind him—to unbind all of them. And it can't be finished by one voice. Believe me." His mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "I've had nine years to try."

Nobody said anything for a while after that. The fire crackled. Somewhere above us, Ysolde's breathing moved the cave air like slow tides.

Then Pip, our mute drummer boy, picked up two sticks and tapped out a rhythm against his knee. Four beats. Steady. The rhythm meant *yes* in the private language we'd been building since Marrowgate burned, since old Maren stayed behind at the gate and sang the storm down on the wardens' heads until they brought her down too.

Four beats. Yes. We're singing anyway.

Brannic swore under his breath, which for him was a form of voting. The twins—Sela and Sera, who harmonized so closely you couldn't tell where one voice ended—looked at each other, had one of their silent twin conversations, and nodded as one person. Which left me, and I said, "Obviously yes, but I want it noted that this plan has the structural integrity of wet paper," because someone has to keep the universe honest.

That's how we became a choir again. That's how Tamsin became a choirmaster again, though it took us another week of rehearsal to drag that particular truth out of him.

* * *

The first real rehearsal happened three nights later, in the deep hollow beneath Ysolde's ridge where the dragon's warmth pooled like bathwater and no warden's ear could reach us. She'd insisted—or her smoke had insisted, curling into shapes that read: *If you must sing the thing that eats songs, sing it where I can burn whatever comes for you.* Dragon hospitality. It grows on you.

Tamsin stood in front of us holding a shard of god-bone wrapped in leather, and he looked like a man holding his own heart outside his chest.

"The prayer has four movements," he said. His voice was rough. He hadn't spoken much in nine years, and now he was speaking constantly, teaching us, correcting us, and every word seemed to cost him something and give him something back at once. "Supplication. Naming. Lamentation. Release. Hollowmoot can devour any one of them alone. Any two. Even three. But when the fourth lands true—if the harmony holds through the release—it can't hold all of us. A god's prayer isn't food. It's a door opening outward."

"And if the harmony doesn't hold?" Sera asked.

"Then it eats us," Sela said, "in order of loudness." The twins had a cheerful way about horror that I found either admirable or alarming depending on the hour.

"We'll hold," Tamsin said, and there was iron under the roughness. "I held a choir once. I'll hold this one."

And I watched him flinch at his own words, because we both knew how the last sentence of that story went.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about rehearsing a prayer that can kill you: it heals you first. That's the trap of it. The music gets into the broken places before you understand what it's doing there.

I'd been singing since before I could talk—humming dragons awake out of their hundred-year sleep, which is a fine trick until the crown decides your voice is a national resource to be harvested. I'd sung running. I'd sung terrified. I'd sung alone in the dark of smuggler caves with ice in my hair.

I had never sung *with*. Not like this.

The supplication movement came together first. Tamsin gave me the lead thread—my voice carrying the melody while the others wove underneath—and the first time we ran it clean, start to finish, I felt something in my sternum unlock that I hadn't known was locked. Sela and Sera's harmony wrapped around me like hands. Brannic, whose voice turned out to be a low gravel-bass that surprised everyone including him, held the floor of it. Pip couldn't sing at all, but he drummed the pulse of it on an old barrelhead, and somehow the drumming wasn't outside the song; it was the ground the song stood on.

Even Ysolde hummed. From deep in her chest, so low I felt it in my teeth rather than heard it—a sound older than kingdoms, older than the parasite on the throne, maybe older than hunger itself.

When we stopped, nobody spoke. Pip was crying without noise, the way he did everything, and Sera put her arm around him, and Brannic studied the ceiling like it owed him money.

Tamsin stood apart, facing the wall, shoulders shaking once, twice.

"Tamsin?" I said.

"Don't," he said. Then, quieter: "Give me a moment. I'm—" He laughed, wet and awful. "I forgot what it sounded like. Having a choir. I made myself forget, and now it's just *there*, and it sounds like—"

He didn't finish. He didn't have to. We'd all lost things; that was the admission price for this particular family. But watching him stand there with nine years of self-imposed silence cracking down his back like river ice in spring—I understood, suddenly, that the rite was going to save him whether or not it saved Veyra. Whether or not it saved any of us.

The music had already begun its work.

* * *

But the naming movement nearly broke us.

It required precision—each singer naming a truth aloud in harmony, and here's the thing about truths: they bite. Tamsin taught us the notes, but the notes were nothing without the weight behind them. You had to mean it. A prayer sung falsely was worse than no prayer at all; Ysolde's smoke had been emphatic on that point, curling into a shape that looked unpleasantly like a snare trap.

So on the fifth night, Tamsin asked us to name our truths, one at a time, into the circle.

Brannic went first, because of course he did, deserters lead with their scars. "I burned the Callowmere orphanage," he said. "I followed the order. I tore off my badge after, but the badge didn't hold the torch." His bass note, when he added it to the chord, was black and heavy and absolutely steady.

Sera named the sister she'd left behind in Verreholm. Sela named the same sister, and their harmonies split—one grieving, one furious—and somehow the split was the truth, and the chord held both.

Pip couldn't speak, so he named his truth on the drum: a stuttering, broken rhythm that resolved, after long minutes, into the shape of a name. His mother's, I think. We all felt it land.

My turn. I could have named the easy thing—I hear Veyra screaming, I'm scared, I miss my mother. Instead I heard myself say: "I want to be the hero of this story, and I'm afraid I'm actually just a girl who runs fast." The chord wobbled. Then it steadied, because apparently cowardice honestly confessed is still load-bearing.

Then Tamsin's turn, and Tamsin stood in the circle and could not do it.

"I silenced myself," he finally managed. "Veyra fell mid-prayer and my whole choir died around me and I made a vow—I cut the gift out of my own throat, in every way but the literal one—and I have kept that vow for nine years, and I don't know how to unsay it. I don't know if the part of me that led them is even alive anymore. That's my truth. I'm leading you toward a thing I'm not sure I survive becoming again."

The silence afterward was enormous.

Then Ysolde exhaled, and her smoke wrote above us in slow letters: *You led them well. They died well. These are not the same failure you think they are.*

And Tamsin—wept. Quietly, standing straight, letting us see it, which I suspect was harder than the weeping. And when he lifted his voice to add his truth-note to the chord, it came out ragged and cracked and more beautiful than anything I'd ever heard, because it wasn't polished. It was *true*.

The naming movement locked into place that night. After that, only the lamentation remained, and then the release—the fourth movement none of us had even attempted, the one that had to be sung in Verreholm itself, at the throne, in the thing's own hearing.

* * *

On the eighth night, I found Tamsin alone at the cave mouth, watching snow come down over Thornfell.

"They're ready," he said, before I'd fully arrived. "The twins are better than my first choir ever was, and that's not grief talking. Brannic's pitch is criminal for a man who spent his life burning things. Pip keeps time like a heartbeat. And you—" he shook his head. "You sing like the world owes you money and you intend to collect."

"High praise from the ghost of choirmasters past."

"I'm serious, Ulyana." He turned to look at me, and the firelight caught the grey in his stubble, the lines around his eyes that hadn't been there in the chapel ruins when I first dragged him into my disaster. "Whatever happens in Verreholm—whatever that thing does when it hears us—you have to promise me you'll keep singing. Not for Veyra. For you. Your voice wakes dragons. Do you understand what that means? Voices like yours end famines. They end wars. Whatever happens to mine—"

"Stop." I said it sharper than I meant to. "You sound like a man writing his own eulogy and asking me to proofread."

"Somebody should. My penmanship is dreadful."

"Tamsin." I took his hand, the one holding the god-bone shard, and pressed it between both of mine. "Your first choir died because a god went silent, not because you failed them. And this choir doesn't follow you because you're perfect. We follow you because you came back. Nine years of silence, and when a half-frozen girl said Veyra's name, you came back. That's the whole job. Showing up. You keep doing it."

Below us in the hollow, faintly, Pip began drumming. Just warming up, keeping his hands busy. But the rhythm carried up through the stone, four steady beats, and Sera's voice joined it, humming, and then Sela, and then Brannic's grave-floor bass, and before any of us decided anything, the whole choir was singing the supplication in the dark of the mountain, unbidden, unrehearsed-in-that-moment, just because the song wanted to exist and we were the people it lived in.

Tamsin listened with his eyes closed.

"Well," he said, when it ended. His voice was wrecked. "I suppose I'm leading again."

"You suppose correctly, choirmaster."

He almost smiled. It was a near thing, crooked and tired, but real. "Three days to Verreholm," he said. "One rehearsal left. Tomorrow we attempt the release movement for the first time—the part that has to be perfect, the part Hollowmoot will hear coming."

"You'll get us there."

"I'll get us *there*," he agreed. "Getting us *back* is going to require significantly more divine intervention."

Above us, Ysolde shifted her vast weight, and her smoke curled up against the falling snow, forming words that hung in the dark like an omen dressed as a blessing:

*Rest tonight, small singers. Tomorrow we practice dying loudly.*

None of us slept much after that. But in the morning, we sang.
