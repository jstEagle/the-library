## 12. Bargain at the Border

I'd spent eleven years teaching recruits that a warden's word was iron, and now I stood on the border of the Ashlow with my badge in my pocket and my word about to be tested against the only men I'd ever called brothers.

The crossing at Redford Bridge was the last chokepoint between Thornfell and the capital. Beyond it, three days of open road to Verreholm, and every mile of it patrolled. We couldn't smuggle our way through—not with two children, an old woman who moved like wet paper, and a dragon the size of a cathedral sleeping in the hills behind us. Ysolde couldn't fly her distance anymore; we'd all seen how the ember of her guttered when she tried. So the road it was, and the road meant wardens.

Forty of them, by Brannic's count from the ridge. He counted twice, lips moving, and came down looking like a man who'd swallowed a stone.

"Redford garrison," he said. "Serl's command." He said the name the way you'd spit out a bad tooth. "He was my second, once."

"You trust him?" Tamsin asked.

"No," I said. "But I knew him when he still asked questions. That's worth something. It has to be."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment. He had that hollow-eyed patience he wore like armor, the look of a man who'd already buried one family and was bracing to bury another. "And if it isn't?"

"Then we fight forty wardens on a bridge," I said, "and lose, and the thing on the throne eats five more voices and grows a little fatter. Or we don't cross, and Hollowmoot wins by default. I've run out of options where nobody bleeds, Tamsin. All that's left is choosing whose blood buys what."

Ulyana tugged my sleeve. She'd been quiet all morning, which frightened me more than screaming would have. "They'll listen," she said. "Some of them. I can hear it when they pray at night, the ones on watch. They don't pray like men who believe in what they're doing. They pray like men asking to be forgiven in advance."

That was the girl's gift talking, or maybe just the girl. Either way, I chose to believe her, because the alternative was a bridge running red.

* * *

I went down alone at dusk. That was part of the bargain I was making with myself—my life against their passage, offered openly, no ambush behind it. If Serl wanted my head, he could have it clean, without his archers spending arrows on children.

The sentries stopped me fifty paces out. Crossbows leveled, torchlight jumping on oiled steel. I raised my hands and let them see my face.

"It's Vane," someone whispered, and the whisper ran down the line like fire through dry grass. *Vane. Captain Vane. The one who tore off his own badge.*

Good. Let them whisper. A reputation is a weapon too, and mine had been sharpening itself since the day I walked out of the burning quarter at Marrowgate with soot in my lungs and a child's shoe in my hand and nothing else.

"Captain Vane," Serl said, pushing through his men. He'd gone grey at the temples since I'd seen him last. There was a new scar through his eyebrow. His hand rested on his sword, but loosely—the way a man rests his hand on something he hopes he won't need. "You have a lot of nerve walking up to forty drawn blades."

"I have a lot of nerve generally," I said. "It's why they made me captain."

Someone laughed before he could stop himself. Serl shot him a look that could have curdled milk.

"You're under Edict," Serl said. "Your name's on the list. Third page. Under the harmonists and above the storm-witch."

"I know where my name is. I wrote half those lists once." I kept my voice level, almost bored. Boredom disarms better than steel; I'd learned that breaking up tavern fights in Verreholm's low town. "I'm not here to surrender, Serl. I'm here to trade."

"Trade."

"Witnesses for passage." I let that sit. Around us the torches crackled, and somewhere behind me an owl complained about the state of the world. "Forty of you are going to spend the rest of your lives wondering what you were part of. I can tell you. I was inside the palace, Serl. I saw the king's face at three in the morning, six years into a sleeplessness no man survives. I saw what comes out of the dungeons when they carry the gifted down. I heard what the crown does with the voices it takes."

"You're lying," Serl said, but his hand had tightened on the sword hilt, and not in the way tightening means *draw*. Tightening the way it means *hold on*.

"I'm offering testimony. Sworn, witnessed, before any tribunal the realm can scrape together after this is done. Every name, every order, every order-giver. Mine included—I burned Marrowgate's quarter, or I carried the torch to the men who did, and I'll hang for it if hanging's what justice costs. But I'll testify for the living first." I turned, slowly, so the whole line could see me do it. "You've all heard them sing. Don't tell me you haven't. You've stood outside a cell and heard some grandmother hum a storm to sleep, and something in your gut told you that wasn't wicked. Your gut was right. The thing wearing Aldric's face isn't the crown. It's a hunger that's been eating us from the inside for six years, and the Edict is its table setting."

Silence. The kind of silence that has weight, that presses on a man's shoulders. I'd heard it before—on the night I tore off my badge, when I asked my squad who among them would burn a child, and not one hand moved, and not one voice said stop either.

"Passage," Serl said finally. "You want us to just step aside. For traitors."

"For witnesses," I said. "There's a difference, and in a year you'll either understand it or you won't, and that's not mine to fix tonight."

* * *

It nearly went wrong twice.

The first time was a young warden—Dann, I learned later, Dann Merrow, nineteen years old and holding his crossbow like it might bite him—who stepped out of line and said, "My sister's name is on the list."

Everyone froze. Serl's jaw worked. Dann's eyes were wet and furious and terrified, all three at once, the exact combination I remembered from my own mirror nine years ago.

"She sings," Dann said, to no one, to everyone. "She sings while she washes. She doesn't even know she does it. Somebody reported her from our own village." He looked at me. "Is it true? What you saw in the palace?"

"It's true," I said. "And there's a girl behind that ridge right now who hears your sister's voice if it's been taken. Ask her. She'll tell you whether Mera Merrow still sings."

I hadn't known the sister's name until it came out of my mouth. Ulyana had told me on the ridge—she'd heard the young sentry praying, and praying about a sister, and she'd listened the way she listens, which is to say completely. When Dann stumbled past the picket an hour later to speak with her, I didn't follow. Some conversations aren't for captains.

The second time it nearly went wrong, it went wrong properly.

A lieutenant named Corvas—hard man, true believer, the kind the Edict grew like mold in damp corners—decided that testimony was treason and drew steel in front of thirty witnesses. "We're not negotiating with traitors," he said, and his sword came out, and for one horrible heartbeat I thought the whole bridge would go red after all.

What saved us was Serl.

He didn't draw. He did something worse, for a man like Corvas: he stepped between us, unhurried, and said, "Put it up, Lieutenant."

"The Edict says—"

"The Edict says a lot of things." Serl's voice was flat as slate. "It said to burn the gifted quarter at Marrowgate, and Vane here refused, and every man in this company knows why he refused, because every man here has stood outside one of those cells and heard what comes out of them. If you want to call it treason, Lieutenant, call it loud enough for the men to hear, and see how many of them step forward to help you."

Corvas looked down the line. Nobody stepped forward. Two men actually took a half-step back, which in soldier language is a paragraph.

He sheathed the sword. He didn't like it—you could see the hate sitting in him like a swallowed coal—but he sheathed it, and later that night he rode east alone toward Verreholm, presumably to report us, which meant we had maybe two days' head start instead of four. Nothing is free. Not even mercy. Especially not mercy.

* * *

Eleven of them crossed over before dawn.

Not forty. Eleven. I'd have taken one. Serl came to me in the grey hour with his badge in his fist—the same gesture I'd made at Marrowgate, tearing it free, and seeing it done by another man's hands made my chest hurt in a place I thought had scarred over.

"I'm not doing this for you," Serl said. "I'm doing it because my daughter hums when she thinks no one's listening, and I lie awake wondering which neighbor is writing her name on a list. If the crown falls, it falls. If it doesn't, I'd rather die having crossed the bridge than having held it."

"That's the finest speech of treason I've ever heard," I said. "I'd applaud, but my hands are full of guilt."

He almost smiled. Almost. "The others stay. Corvas took eight loyalists east. The rest—" he shrugged, a soldier's shrug, the shrug that means *the world is bigger than my orders* —"the rest will hold the bridge and see nothing, hear nothing, and spend the rest of their lives telling themselves stories about the night nothing happened at Redford."

"Those are the best stories," I said. "Nobody gets hanged in them."

We crossed at first light. Forty became twenty-nine became eleven became a column of hunted singers, a mute drummer boy riding double with a defected warden, an old woman wrapped in three cloaks, twins who wouldn't stop humming harmony even now, a god-touched girl leading a limping dragon through the hills like a shepherd leading the world's largest, saddest sheep—and me, badgeless, lawless, carrying testimony in my skull that might hang half the peerage if any of us lived to give it.

Tamsin fell into step beside me on the far side of the bridge. He didn't look back at the wardens on the wall. I admired that. I looked back for both of us.

"Eleven," he said quietly. "Out of forty."

"Eleven is a choir," I said. "I'm told choirs start smaller than that."

He glanced at me sideways, and for a moment—the first moment since I'd met him—he looked less like a man guarding a grave and more like a man remembering music. "Where did you hear that?"

"A girl who wakes dragons," I said. "She talks in her sleep. You should listen sometime."

Ahead, the road bent east toward Verreholm, toward the ash-choked lowlands and the thing on the throne, and behind us Redford Bridge stood empty in the morning fog except for the men who'd chosen to forget us, which is its own kind of courage, though history will never thank them for it.

Two days, maybe, before Corvas's report reached the palace. Maybe less.

I lengthened my stride, and the choir of strays followed me east.
