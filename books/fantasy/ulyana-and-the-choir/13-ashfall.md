## 13. Ashfall

The lowlands began where the green things stopped.

There was no border to it, no line drawn in the dirt—just a slow surrender of color, the heather going grey at the edges like old cloth, then the birches standing bare and black as burnt matches even though they hadn't burned. The ash did that. It didn't need fire to kill a tree; it just settled on everything, a fine grey snow that fell without sky, sifting down out of a haze so thick we couldn't tell whether it was morning or evening or simply the end of weather altogether.

"Charming country," Brannic said, pulling his scarf up over his nose. "You bring all your choirs here?"

"Only the doomed ones," I said.

He had the grace not to laugh. Ulyana laughed enough for both of them, which I was learning was her way of handling fear: swallow it whole and let it come back out as noise. She walked at the front of our straggling line with her hood down, letting the ash settle in her dark hair like she was collecting it for some later spell, and behind her came the twins hand in hand, and behind them old Maren with her storm-singer's stoop, and behind Maren came Pip.

Pip carried his drum strapped to his back like a shield worn backwards, which I suppose it was. He hadn't spoken since Marrowgate—not that anyone expected him to. Brannic said the boy hadn't spoken in three years, not since wardens burned the drum-circle school in Callowmere and left him the only one who walked out. He spoke in rhythm instead. Tap-tap on a fencepost. Tap on a saddlebow. Tap-tap-tap on the top of my pack when he wanted me to look at something, which was how I'd learned he had opinions about my route-planning, most of them unfavorable.

The ash got into everything. Into our water, our bread, the creases of our hands. By the second day we were all the same color, grey ghosts walking through a grey country, and the only clean thing among us was Ysolde—or rather, the thought of Ysolde, who'd turned back at the high pass because a dragon the size of a chapel cannot cross farmland without announcing herself to every warden between here and the sea. She'd pressed her great dim head down to Ulyana one last time, exhaled a breath hot enough to peel paint, and told us—in that voice like a landslide deciding to be patient—that she would come when called.

*If* there was anything left to call her for.

* * *

We passed the first village at midday, if it was midday. You couldn't say. The sun was a pale coin behind the haze, a coin rubbed smooth by someone's anxious thumb.

The village had been called Hallowfen. We knew this because the signpost still stood, though the sign itself had been pried off—wardens did that now, Brannic told us, took the names down before they burned the places, so that afterward there would be nothing to mourn by name. A village without a name is just a stain. Easier on the conscience. Easier on the paperwork.

What remained was chimneys. Chimneys and foundation stones and the twisted iron skeleton of a well-frame, all of it furred with ash, all of it soft-edged, as though the fire had happened a hundred years ago instead of—by Brannic's reckoning—eleven weeks.

"They were storm-gifted, mostly," Maren said quietly. She stood at the edge of what had been a garden, looking at a fence row that led nowhere. "Fen folk usually are. All that weather coming off the marshes, it gets into the blood. My grandmother was from country like this." She bent and picked up something from the ash—a child's clay whistle, cracked but whole—and held it in her palm for a long moment before setting it gently back down, the way you'd return something to a grave.

I looked at the chimneys and I did the arithmetic I always did, the arithmetic I hated. Nine years ago I'd have sung for these people. Nine years ago I'd have stood in the square with my choir at my back and raised a working-song strong enough to pull the fire out of the thatch and send it up harmless into the clouds. That was what Veyra's gift was *for*. Not thunderbolts, not wonders. Just this: a hundred small salvations, sung daily, taken for granted until the day the god went quiet mid-prayer and the fire came and stayed.

And nine years ago I'd have died with them, probably. Which was the bargain I'd actually made, wasn't it? Invisibility for survival. Silence for safety. I'd paid it gladly for nine years and told myself it was wisdom, and now I walked through the receipts of other people's refusal to make the same deal, and every chimney was an accusation.

"You're doing the face," Ulyana said beside me.

"What face?"

"The one where you're counting." She nudged my elbow with hers. "Stop counting. It doesn't help them and it makes your forehead do a thing."

"My forehead does not do a thing."

"It absolutely does a thing."

Brannic, ahead of us, held up a fist and we stopped. He crouched over the road, reading it the way Pip read rhythms. "Cart tracks. Recent. Heavy load, heading east." He glanced back at me. "Refugees go west. East is toward Verreholm."

"Wardens, then. Or supply wagons for the garrisons."

"Either way, we're off the road." He pointed to a sunken lane running parallel, half-swallowed by ash-drift. "Single file. Nobody sings. Nobody hums. Nobody—" he looked meaningfully at the twins, who had been caught harmonizing over breakfast and had melted two spoons, "—*harmonizes.*"

The twins looked wounded. Everything about the twins looked wounded; they were fifteen and identical and treated every inconvenience as a personal tragedy staged for their benefit. But they went silent, and we dropped into the lane, and the ash fell on us like a shroud being fitted.

* * *

That night we sheltered in the cellar of a tithe-barn that had somehow survived when its village hadn't. Brannic took first watch above; Maren slept like the dead, which at her age was less a figure of speech than a rehearsal; the twins curled together like quotation marks. Ulyana sat against the wall turning her mother's ring around and around on her finger, and I sat across from her pretending not to notice, which was becoming my principal occupation.

Pip sat apart, as he always did, drum across his knees. In the dark he wasn't drumming. His fingers just rested on the skin of it, light as flies, and I realized after a while that he was tracing patterns without sounding them. Silent music. A boy who'd lost his words keeping the last ones he had locked inside a drum.

I knew about locked things.

"Pip," I said softly.

His head came up. In the faint grey light through the grate his eyes caught it and held it, owl-bright.

"I'm going to check the watch rotation," I lied, and got up, and crossed to him, and sat down against the wall beside him because sitting opposite felt like an interrogation and this wasn't that. Or wasn't supposed to be. "I wanted to say—I'm sorry. About the pace today. About the lowlands generally. This is ugly country to walk through when it's yours."

He looked at me. He didn't tap anything. That was answer enough: *it's mine.*

"My choir was from country like this too," I heard myself say. God help me, my mouth just kept going. Nine years of silence and apparently all it took was one mute child and a cellar full of ash. "River valley south of here. Veyra's chapel overlooked it. There were forty-one of us. Singers, I mean. Cooks and muleteers besides. Forty-one voices, and I was lead, and on the morning it ended we were singing the dawn office, and Veyra—" I stopped. Started again. Stopped again.

Pip waited. He was very good at waiting. Three years of practice, probably.

"He stopped," I finally said. "Mid-word. Mid-*syllable.* And everything I'd ever sung stopped with him, like a rope cut, like—like forty-one people suddenly holding instruments with no strings. And the fire that came through that valley a month later, we couldn't stop it. We couldn't stop *anything.* And I walked away. I was the only one who walked away, because I wasn't there when it mattered, because I'd gone down to the village to argue about grain prices, of all the stupid—" My voice cracked and I hated it and kept going anyway, because apparently tonight was the night for it. "Forty-one names, Pip. I can still recite them. I recite them every year on the anniversary, alone, in my head, because saying them out loud would mean using the gift and I swore I wouldn't, and sometimes I think that vow is the only thing holding me together and sometimes I think it's the thing that killed everyone twice."

The cellar was very quiet. Even the ash seemed to fall more softly.

Then Pip reached over and put his hand flat on my chest, over the sternum, and tapped. Once. Gently. Like a heartbeat asking a question.

"I know," I said. "I know it's still in there. That's the problem."

He shook his head. Slowly. Then he tapped my chest again, once, and then pointed—at me, firmly, both hands, the way you'd point at a thing that was refusing to see itself—and then he pointed at the wall, at nothing, at the empty air where a ghost might stand if ghosts stood in cellars, and shook his head again with a violence that made his whole thin body shake with it.

And then he opened his mouth, and what came out was rusty and small and cracked down the middle, a voice that hadn't been oiled in three years, a voice like a gate swinging on a hinge that had seized—

"You're *not* them."

I stopped breathing.

"You keep looking at us like we're them." The words came slowly, dragged up from somewhere deep, each one costing him, and he spent them anyway, spendthrift, generous, furious. "The twins. Maren. Me. You look at us like we're already dead and you're just—just waiting for the part where you survive it again." His breath hitched. "But we're not them. I'm not your ghost. None of us are. We're *here.*"

"Pip—"

"We're *here,*" he said again, louder, and the drum under his hands thrummed in sympathy, a low note rolling through the cellar like distant thunder, and Maren stirred in her sleep and the twins' breathing changed, and Ulyana, across the room, had gone very still, watching us with her ring forgotten on her finger. "And you can bury forty-one names, Tamsin, you can carry them however you want, but if you get us all killed because you've already dug our graves in your head—if you hold back one note because you think we're already lost—then *that's* the thing that kills everyone twice. Not the vow. *You.*"

The word landed like a hammer on an anvil. Like a drumbeat. Of course it did. He was a drummer; he knew exactly where the beat fell.

I sat there with my chest open and my mouth shut, and the terrible thing—the truly terrible thing—was that it didn't feel like cruelty. It felt like grace. Blunt, graceless, grace. A twelve-year-old boy who hadn't wasted a single word in three years spending eleven of them on me, and every one of them true.

"I don't know how to do it differently," I said at last. My whisper sounded enormous in the dark. "Every time I start to care about someone, I hear the dawn office. I hear the rope cut."

Pip considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then he picked up my hand—his fingers were ash-grey and drum-calloused—and pressed my palm flat against the skin of his drum, and held it there.

Beneath my hand, faint as a moth's wing, I felt his pulse. And beneath that, fainter, the drum's own memory, the wood remembering every rhythm it had ever been given. Two heartbeats in one small instrument. Alive, alive, alive.

"That's how," he said. "You just keep touching the thing that's real."

Then he closed his eyes, laid his cheek against the drum, and was asleep within a minute, the way children sleep, instantly and completely, as if courage were a chore on a list and he'd finished it and could move on to the next thing.

I stayed where I was a long time, hand on the drumskin, feeling the small stubborn pulse of him.

Across the cellar, Ulyana met my eyes. She didn't say anything. She just smiled—small, tired, knowing—and turned her face to the wall, and I understood that she'd heard every word and would never mention one of them, and that this too was a kind of choir: people holding their parts silent so yours could be heard.

* * *

We crossed the worst of it on the fourth day.

The company that had burned Hallowfen had moved on to its neighbors, and their neighbors' neighbors, and the lowlands became a ledger of loss written in chimney smoke. We passed a shrine to Veyra—still standing, miracle of miracles, though its bell had been cut down and its god-face defaced with tar—and I stopped, and the company stopped with me, and I did something I hadn't done in nine years.

I sang.

One note. Barely a note—a thread of sound, a whisper with ambition, pitched low so it wouldn't carry past the shrine's broken walls. But it was Veyra's key, the dawn-office key, the first note of the prayer I'd abandoned, and as it left me the ash in the air around the shrine seemed to *hesitate*, hanging suspended for one impossible moment like a held breath, before falling on.

Ulyana's hand found mine. Her eyes were wet. "It heard you," she whispered.

"No," I said. But my voice shook, because the warmth in the old stone under my palm said otherwise, faint and far away, like a heartbeat through a wall. Like something sleeping, dreaming of an ending it had never been allowed to finish.

Pip stood beside me, and when I ran out of breath he tapped the rhythm of the note on the shrine stone—once, gently, a heartbeat asking a question—and we walked on.

East. Toward Verreholm, toward the dungeons full of caged voices, toward the thing that ate prayers. Behind us the ash fell on forty-one names and a thousand more, and ahead of us, far off through the haze, we could see the capital's towers rising like needles, and threaded through the wind, so faint we might have imagined it, came a sound none of us could mistake.

Singing. Hundreds of voices, drained and murmuring, crying out from beneath the palace stones.

Pip heard it too. He looked up at me, and set his jaw, and tapped the rhythm of it against his thigh—*there, there, there*—marking the place where our song would have to end theirs.
