## 14. The Gates of Verreholm

The smuggler roads ended the way all good things end: abruptly, in the dark, with a smell.

"That," said Brannic, lifting the lantern, "is the Gates of Verreholm."

He was joking. I think he was joking. The tunnel mouth before us was a rusted grate set into wet stone, and beyond it the sewer ran wide as a road, because in Verreholm it *was* a road—the old kings had built their drains to move armies' worth of filth, on the theory that a city's greatness could be measured by what it flushed away. By that measure we were about to enter the greatest city in the world.

"Charming," said Tamsin. He hadn't said much since the ashlands. Since Pip spoke. Something in him had come loose and not yet settled back into place, like a tooth you keep touching with your tongue. "Nine years of smuggling salt through Thornfell and this is my first time taking the scenic route."

"You'll want to breathe through your mouth," Brannic said.

"I've been breathing through my mouth since Marrowgate."

That shut everyone up for a moment. We stood there in the last dry stretch of smuggler tunnel—Brannic with his lantern and his warden's map drawn from memory, Tamsin with his hands jammed in his coat, the twins pressed shoulder to shoulder, Ysolde's ember-glow pulsing faintly through the scales wrapped around my wrist where she'd shed them for me, *carry these, little spark, so I can find you when it matters*. Pip drummed soft fingers against his thigh, his only music now, his voice still spent from that one sentence in the ashes.

*I'm not your ghost.*

I kept hearing it. I kept watching Tamsin hear it too.

"All right." Brannic crouched at the grate and worked the lock with two picks and a warden's patience. "Once we're in, no singing. None. Not humming, not thinking too loudly in rhythm. The dungeons sit under the palace, and the palace sits over everything. Sound travels down here like water finds a drain."

"And if something hears us anyway?" one of the twins asked—I never could tell them apart in the dark, which felt rude after everything.

"Then we run," Brannic said, "and we're very sorry."

* * *

The sewers of Verreholm were older than the Edict, older than Aldric, older maybe than the crown itself, and they were full of echoes that didn't belong to us.

That was the first wrong thing. A sewer should echo with drips and rats and the distant groan of a city digesting. This one echoed with *voices*. Faint ones. Thread-thin. Coming up through the stone from somewhere below and ahead, murmuring in a way that made the hair on my arms stand up, because murmuring is what voices do when they've forgotten anyone is listening.

We walked for hours by Brannic's count, though time went strange down there. The tunnels branched and doubled; Brannic navigated by memory and chalk marks he'd left himself years ago, back when he'd been a different man with a badge. Twice we passed side-chambers where smugglers had cached goods—salt, brandy, one crate of what Tamsin identified, with a connoisseur's sniff, as extremely illegal prayer-candles. He took three. Nobody argued.

It was on the third rest stop that I made my mistake.

We were sitting in an alcove, sharing hardtack, when I heard it properly for the first time. Not the murmur as a whole—one voice inside it. A woman's voice, low and frayed, singing half of a lullaby. Just half. It would get to the turn where the melody folds back on itself and then it would stop, and start again, and stop again, like a person trying to remember a room they'd been locked out of.

I knew the song. My mother sang it. Every mother in the river valleys sang it.

And I answered her.

I didn't decide to. That's the thing I need you to understand, whoever ends up reading this or remembering it or being told about the girl who nearly drowned herself in other people's stolen voices. I did not *decide*. A singer hears a broken phrase and the whole body leans toward mending it, the way a hand reaches for a falling cup. I was humming the fold-back line before I knew my throat was open, and the moment I did it, every voice in the dark turned toward me.

Every voice.

The murmur stopped. And then, from everywhere—from the walls, from the water, from under the floor and above our heads—came a sound like a thousand people inhaling at once.

"*There*," said the lullaby woman, right against my ear, though there was nothing near me but dripping stone. "*Oh, there you are. You can hear us. You can hear us, can't you.*"

"Ulyana." Tamsin's hand closed on my shoulder, hard. "Stop. Close your throat. Now."

But they were all talking then, all of them, a flood of them, and each one was a hook:

"*It's warm where you are—is it warm? I can't remember warm—*"

"*My name was Sera. Tell someone my name was Sera—*"

"*Don't stop, don't stop, if you stop it goes dark again—*"

"*Little singer, little singer, he eats us slow, he eats us slow—*"

They weren't ghosts. That was the horror of it, the thing I couldn't make my mind hold. They were alive. Somewhere below us, in cages, were living throats attached to living people, and what I was hearing was their voices—ripped out, stored, *kept*—drifting up through the stone like heat through a floor. Hollowmoot wasn't eating them the way a wolf eats. It was eating them the way a miser counts coins. Over and over. Keeping them shiny.

And they were so *lonely*.

I opened my mouth to answer Sera. I wanted to more than I have ever wanted anything—to give her the rest of the lullaby, to say *your name, I'll carry your name*, to be warm for all of them, to pour myself out into that dark until the dark wasn't hungry anymore—

Something struck me across the face.

Not a fist. A hand, flat, the old remedy, and it was Tamsin who'd done it, kneeling in front of me, gripping my jaw with both hands, his eyes furious and terrified an inch from mine.

"You do not answer them," he said, in the voice he must have used on green recruits once, on frightened choir-singers, on himself. "You hear me? Whatever they ask, whatever they promise—you do not answer. Answering is how it feeds. Look at me. *Look at me.*"

I looked at him. His face was wet. I think some of that was mine.

"They're people," I whispered.

"I know." And his voice cracked straight down the middle, because he knew better than any of us—he'd heard a choir die mid-hymn, he knew exactly what those voices were and what it meant that they still sang. "I know they're people. That's why we finish this. But you go down there answering them one at a time and there won't be enough of you left to sing the rite. Do you understand? They will drink you dry out of sheer gratitude."

The twins had hold of my arms. When had that happened. Pip was pressed against my side, drumming his heartbeat against my ribs, steady, steady, steady—an anchor in the only language he had left. Brannic stood at the mouth of the alcove with his sword drawn, facing the dark, because even without the gift he could feel it: the attention of the deep places, disappointed, receding.

Slowly, grudgingly, the murmur resumed. The lullaby came back to its broken turn and stopped, and started, and stopped.

Sera's voice, smaller now, almost polite: "*...oh. Oh, you're gone. You're always gone.*"

I put my fist against my own mouth and held it there until the shaking stopped.

* * *

We didn't speak again until we reached the place Brannic called the Undercroft—a junction where five tunnels met beneath a vaulted ceiling carved with old royal faces, worn smooth, their mouths deliberately chiseled away by some pious mason centuries ago. Even the stones, it seemed, had once worried about what the palace might hear.

From here, Brannic said, a service stair climbed toward the palace kitchens, and beyond that the choir loft, and beyond that the throne hall. And below us—below, past a door sealed with lead and crowned with seven locks—sat the dungeons.

We should have gone up. That was the plan. Up, quietly, toward the loft, and wait for Ysolde's ember to call us when the hour was right.

But I heard them again. Closer now. And this time I recognized something in the murmur that stopped my breath: harmony. Fragments of it. Voices reaching for each other across their separate cages, finding thirds and fifths in the dark, building the skeleton of a chord no one would ever let them finish.

They were singing together. Down there, drained and caged and slowly eaten, they were still doing the one thing the crown could never quite take: they were making what they had into music.

Tamsin heard it too. I watched it hit him—watched his whole body flinch like a man hearing his own name shouted from a burning house.

"The rite needs many voices in true harmony," I said quietly. "You said it yourself. Every singer risking everything."

"No." He said it fast, flat, final. "No. Those aren't singers anymore, Ulyana, they're *remnants*. Half-drained. If we open those cages we don't free reinforcements, we free—"

"We free the chorus," I said. "Tamsin. Listen to them. They've been holding the harmony for years with nobody to resolve it. That's not remnants. That's a choir waiting for a conductor."

For a long moment he just looked at me. In the lantern light his face did something complicated—grief and terror and, underneath both, buried so deep he probably didn't know it was there, hope. The same look he'd had in the snow, the day I said Veyra's name and nine years of silence cracked.

"The seventh lock," Brannic said behind us, "is a prayer-lock. Old law. It opens to a finished hymn—first verse and last, sung true." He paused. "I never could carry a tune. Always thought that was funny, in a grim way. Wardens guarding a door that only opens for the thing we burn people for."

Tamsin closed his eyes. I saw his throat work once, twice.

"If we open that door," he said finally, "everything below hears us. Everything above hears us. There is no quiet after that. No going back up the stair and waiting for a better hour."

"There was never going to be a better hour," I said. "There's only the one we're standing in."

He looked at the sealed door a long time. Then at me. Then—at last, and this is the part I'll keep forever—at Pip, who had climbed up onto a fallen block of stone, and was looking at the door with his chin lifted, fingers poised over his thighs, ready.

Pip nodded at him. *Lead us.*

Tamsin exhaled, and opened his mouth, and began.

His voice was a whisper of what it must have been—I'd never heard him sing full-voiced, but even spent and careful, even pitched barely above breath, it was Veyra's own hymn, first verse, the one every shrine in Thornfell still bore scorch-marks for. The twins slid in beneath him, finding the low harmony like water finding its level. I came in on the third. Brannic, bless his tone-deaf heart, hummed the drone, flat and stubborn as a wall.

And Pip—Pip drummed the rhythm on the stone, soft as rain on a roof, the heartbeat under everything.

The prayer-lock drank the song. I felt it drinking, felt the door listening the way the voices below had listened, but this was different—this was a door built by people who had believed a finished prayer was the most powerful thing in the world, and it opened the way belief opens, reluctantly and then all at once. Seven locks turning in sequence, deep and heavy, like a heart deciding to beat.

The door swung inward on darkness and cold.

And the murmur rose to meet us—not a thousand voices anymore, but close, hundreds, all of them turning upward at once, all of them straining toward the light spilling in around our shoulders, and I heard Sera among them, and I heard children, and I heard old men, and I heard the lullaby reach its broken turn one more time and hang there, unfinished, waiting.

Tamsin stepped forward to the threshold and stopped. Below us, in the black, something vast shifted its weight—a hunger the size of a cathedral, turning over in its sleep, beginning to notice.

Above us, faint through miles of stone, came a sound none of us expected: a bell. One strike. Then another. Then a peal, wild and urgent, ringing from every tower in Verreholm at once.

Brannic went white. "That's the Ash Bell," he said. "They ring it when the crown knows. When the hunt has found something it wants."

The bell rang on, and on, and far above, through the dark, I swear I felt it—felt *him*—turning his attention downward, toward the door, toward the song, toward us.

Tamsin took one step back from the threshold, and his whisper came out steady as steel.

"Then we'd better teach the chorus the ending before he gets here."
