## 15. The King Who Remembers

The sewers of Verreholm smell like a god's regret.

I want it noted that I said this out loud, quietly, to no one in particular, and that Ulyana elbowed me for it. Nine years of smuggling salt through Thornfell's back passages teaches you a taxonomy of stink—there is rot-stink, there is tannery-stink, there is the particular sweetness of a pig-farm upstream—and this was none of them. This was old. Old like a chapel left shut for a century, incense gone to wax and dust, except underneath it ran something sweeter and wrong, like honey poured over a wound.

"That's him," Ulyana whispered. "That's what I've been hearing. Under everything. That smell."

"You can *smell* a parasite?"

"I can hear it. The smell just comes with." She wiped her nose on her sleeve and kept walking, one hand trailing the slime-slick wall. Behind us came the others: Brannic moving like a man who knew every patrol route of this city by heart and hated each one personally; the twins, Sefa and Maren, pressed shoulder to shoulder; old Ysolde folded down into a shape barely small enough for the tunnel, her scales dull as river stones, her breath a faint heat against my neck like an oven door left open in winter. And Pip, mute as ever, drumming two fingers against his thigh in a rhythm I'd come to understand as his way of holding someone's hand when he couldn't reach.

We had come down through the smuggler roads until the smuggler roads ended, which is the thing about smuggler roads nobody tells you: they always end somewhere you don't want to be. Ours ended here, beneath the palace hill, in a junction where five tunnels met and the ceiling rose into vaulted stone that no sewer engineer had any business building. Carved vaults. Ribbed like the inside of a throat.

"Old work," Brannic murmured. "Pre-Aldric. Maybe pre-dynasty."

"Everything under this city is pre-something," I said. "That's how cities work. They build on top of their own graves and call it progress."

I was trying for wry. It landed somewhere near bitter, which, honestly, has been my whole register since Marrowgate burned. Since Maerlin stayed at the gate and sang the storm down on the wardens until they burned her for it. I have been carrying her last note around in my chest like a swallowed coal, and every joke I make now has scorch marks on it.

Ulyana stopped so suddenly I walked into her.

"What—"

"Shh." Her head tilted, the way it did when she listened to things the rest of us needed instruments to hear. "Do you hear that?"

I listened. Dripping water. Ysolde's slow furnace-breath. My own pulse doing its usual coward's drumroll. "Hear what?"

"Singing," she breathed. "Far away. Above us. A hymn. Old one—the Vespers for the Quiet Hours." She turned to me, and in the dark her eyes were enormous. "Tamsin, that's *Veyra's* liturgy. Nobody sings that anymore. Nobody alive even knows it except—"

"Except people who were in my choir," I finished, and felt the floor tilt under a sentence.

Because there was exactly one person left who might sing Veyra's Vespers, and he wasn't supposed to be singing anything. He was supposed to be a hollowed-out husk wearing a crown, six years without sleep, a mouth full of somebody else's hunger.

"Brannic," I said. "How do we get up from here?"

* * *

The answer was a stair.

Of course it was a stair. Every forgotten corridor in every palace in the world eventually becomes a stair, because palaces are built by people who cannot bear the idea of a space without purpose, and then abandoned by people who forget purposes faster than they build stairs. This one climbed through the dark in tight spirals, its steps worn concave by feet long dead, and halfway up, Ulyana grabbed my arm hard enough to bruise.

"The singing stopped," she whispered.

"Maybe he stopped."

"Maybe *it* stopped him."

We went up slower after that. Brannic took point with his stolen warden's blade drawn low, and I hated that too—hated that we were creeping toward a king we meant to save with knives out, but the alternative was arriving empty-handed in a place where the walls themselves might be listening. Hollowmoot ate voices. I did not intend to offer mine up as an appetizer before the main course.

The stair let out through a broken grate into a corridor that time had clearly filed under *lost*. No torch brackets. No guards. Dust lay on the flagstones in a smooth grey sheet, unbroken except by a single line of footprints—bare feet, human-sized, going one direction only.

No return prints.

"Six years," Brannic said quietly. "He hasn't slept in six years. You know what I heard from the palace servants, before I tore off my badge? That the king walks at night. That guards are ordered not to look at him. That the ones who look—" He didn't finish. He didn't need to. We all knew what happened to people who looked at things behind other people's eyes.

The bare footprints led us maybe two hundred paces down that dead corridor, past doorways bricked over so long ago the mortar had gone the color of bone, and then the corridor opened—abruptly, like a held breath released—into a small round chamber under a dome of grey glass, where moonlight fell through in a single pale column, and a man stood in the middle of it with his hands clasped behind his back, singing.

Singing Veyra's Vespers for the Quiet Hours.

His voice was wrecked. That was the first thing I understood—not the melody, though the melody hit me like a fist under the ribs, because I knew it, I had sung its lead line while forty voices harmonized beneath me and a god leaned down close enough to warm our faces. His voice was ruined the way a bell is ruined when it's cracked: the shape of the sound still there, the beauty leaking out through the fracture. He got four bars further and his voice simply gave out, and he stood there in the moonlight with his mouth still open, shaping words that wouldn't come, and I recognized that too.

I knew exactly what it was to stand with a song dying in your throat.

"Aldric," Brannic said, and went to one knee, blade flat across both palms—an old gesture, surrender or fealty, and I genuinely could not tell which he meant.

The king turned.

Here is what I expected: the thing from Brannic's stories. Something behind the eyes. A hunger looking out through a face like through a window. I had spent three weeks imagining it, the way you imagine a wound before you see one, making it worse than flesh can manage.

What I saw was a tired man in his fifties wearing a nightshirt, barefoot on cold stone, with a face like a map of every bad night he'd ever survived. Grey at the temples. Cheeks fallen in. Hands that trembled slightly, the way hands do when they haven't rested in—well. In six years, apparently. And his eyes—

His eyes were wet. Red-rimmed. *Human.*

And behind them, far back, deep in, the way you can see a fish far down in dark water—something moved. Something that noticed us noticing it. For half a heartbeat the whole quality of his gaze changed, went flat and vast and interested, the way a well goes from reflecting sky to showing you depth, and my skin crawled from scalp to sole, and Ysolde made a sound low in her chest like a landslide deciding whether to happen.

Then it sank again. And the man surfaced, gasping, like a drowning swimmer coming up for one clean breath.

"You're real," Aldric said. His voice scraped. "You're—you're actually real. Not another of its—oh, thank every quiet god, you're *real*." He took a step toward us and his legs nearly went out from under him, and Brannic caught his elbow, and the king gripped the former warden's arm like a man gripping a rope over a drop. "It shows me things. It shows me my sons grown. It shows me my wife alive. It puts people in these corridors and lets me talk to them and then it laughs while they turn to smoke. Six years. Six years of—"

"Your Majesty," Brannic started, formal as a parade ground.

"Don't." Aldric's laugh was terrible, dry as ash. "Don't kneel, don't title me. I know what sits on the throne wearing my face. I watch it eat children's voices through my own eyes. Do you understand? I am *in here*. I have been in here the whole time, watching, screaming into a body that won't carry it—" His hand flew to his own throat, pressed there. "Every voice it takes, I feel it go down. Like swallowing hot coals. Every single one. There was a girl three days ago. Fourteen. They brought her in singing some harvest song her grandmother taught her, and it made me open my mouth and *drink*, and I felt her get smaller and smaller until there was nothing left but the tune, and now the tune lives in me and I hum it at night because it's all that's left of her and I *hate* that it's all that's left of her—"

He was crying now. Kings aren't supposed to cry, I suppose, but kings also aren't supposed to be puppets, and the world had already broken that rule thoroughly enough that the second one hardly mattered.

"Stop," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I hadn't planned to speak. The word had just come out, rough and low, the same tone I'd have used on any grieving man in any smuggler's tavern at closing time.

"Stop talking about it in front of it," I said. "Whatever's listening through you—it eats what it hears. You're feeding it your grief."

Aldric stared at me. And then, horribly, wonderfully, he laughed—a real laugh, cracked and small, but real. "Oh, you're one of them. One of the gifted. I can tell. It flinched when you spoke. Did you know that? It *flinched*." He leaned closer, urgent, gripping my sleeve with fingers that shook. "It always flinches at the gifted. It pretends not to. It wears my face and does its hunger like a performance, but when a true voice speaks near it, something behind my eyes pulls back like a hand from a stove."

"It fears us," Ulyana said softly.

"It fears *finished songs*," Aldric said, and the words dropped into the room like a stone into a well, and I heard the splash all the way down.

* * *

He told us sitting down, because his legs were done pretending. Brannic gave him his cloak and the king wrapped it around himself like any old man in any cold room, and if that image doesn't tell you everything about what six years of possession does to majesty, nothing will.

"It began before the Edict," he said. "Before anyone knew. There was a night—I don't remember most of it. I remember waking at my desk and my candle had burned to nothing and there was a taste in my mouth like old coins, and a thought in my head that wasn't shaped like my thoughts. Smooth. Hungry. Patient." His hands worked in his lap. "It grew. Slowly at first. It learned to be me better than I was me—it signed my decrees, it charmed my council, it never slept so it studied while I dreamed, and then one day I realized I hadn't dreamed in a year, because it had taken the sleeping too. It keeps me awake so it can use the hours. Do you know what that is? Watching your own kingdom through a window you can't open?"

"Why the gifted?" I asked. Though I knew. I had known since Brannic first described the feeding. But knowing a thing and hearing its architect say it are different animals.

"Voices." He said it simply, terribly. "It eats voices. That's all it's ever eaten—that's all it *is*, I think, an appetite that learned to wear shapes. Every gifted singer it takes makes it stronger, brighter, harder to dislodge. It started with beggars and hedge-witches, gifts too small to miss. Then the Edict. Then the hunt. Then—" his face crumpled and reassembled itself, a man practicing being a king with no kingdom left inside him, "—then the pyres, and the cages, and my dungeons full of people who hum in their sleep because it's the only part of them left."

Ysolde shifted, and the dome above us shivered with condensation. When she spoke, her voice was the sound mountains would make if mountains grieved: vast, slow, and old. "Little king. What is its name?"

Aldric looked up at her—at a dragon, in a forgotten corridor, asking him questions—and I watched him decide, visibly, that he had passed beyond surprise into some country where nothing could astonish him anymore. "It doesn't give one. But I've heard it think, in the early hours, when it's sated and careless. Two syllables. Old ones." He pronounced them carefully, like handling broken glass: "*Hollow-moot.*"

Beside me, Ulyana made a small sound. Ysolde closed her eyes. "So," the dragon said. "It lives. We hoped otherwise, in the long dim centuries. A god that ate gods should not be able to starve, I suppose. Only hide."

"And it hides in my crown," Aldric said bitterly. "In my *head*. And here is the joke of it, singers, the grand joke: I know its weakness. Have known for years. Haven't been able to use it, because using it requires a voice, and it has taken every voice in this palace, and the ones it couldn't take it hunted across the whole cursed realm." He turned to me, and his red-rimmed eyes fixed on mine with a ferocity that had nothing of the puppet in it. "It cannot abide a finished prayer. Do you understand me? Hymns, yes—it drinks hymns, drains them mid-phrase, that's why it silences choirs *during* the singing, why your god's choir died mid-hymn—"

The floor tilted. Ulyana's hand found mine without either of us deciding it.

"—but a prayer *completed*," Aldric went on, relentless, leaning forward, "a song carried through its final cadence, resolved, *ended*—that is the one thing it cannot swallow. It tried once, early on. There was an old priest in the lower cells who wouldn't stop chanting a burial rite. It sent guards, and the guards' swords couldn't stop the chant fast enough, and the priest reached the final amen, and I felt it *scream* through my own skull. One amen. It was sick for a week. Weak. Visible. I could almost move my own hands." His grip tightened on my sleeve. "One finished prayer, from one dying old man, and the thing that eats gods was sick for a *week*."

"So we finish one," I said. My voice sounded strange to me, far away. "A big one."

"Not big." He shook his head sharply. "Complete. Size means nothing to it—it has swallowed cathedrals of sound. What it cannot survive is *resolution*. An ending it didn't choose. A prayer that reaches its last word." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen, because it was hopeful. "And I know the prayer it fears most. It thinks about it constantly. It circles it like a tongue circling a broken tooth. The prayer of the god it silenced. The one that was never finished."

Veyra's unfinished prayer. The hymn cut off mid-phrase nine years ago while forty of my people fell down dead around me and a god's voice tore out of the world like a nail from wood.

"It dreams about that prayer," Aldric whispered. "When I'm allowed to see its dreams. It is *afraid* of the ending it never heard."

* * *

There was more, after that. Practical things—corridor routes, guard rotations, the location of the dungeon cages, the fact that the throne hall's acoustics were built by the same pre-dynasty masons who built this dome ("they built rooms to *carry* song, all of them, the whole palace is an instrument, it never realized"). Brannic memorized routes. Ulyana asked questions in a low fierce voice and wrote nothing down because none of us wanted ink near this. Pip sat against Ysolde's flank and drummed soft rhythms on his knees, and twice the king watched him with an expression I couldn't name until I could: it was envy. The envy of a man watching someone keep a silence freely, when his own had been imposed.

But it was at the end, when we were gathering ourselves to go, that Aldric caught my arm one last time.

"You," he said. "The one it flinched at. You'll sing it. The lead."

"I buried that voice," I told him. "Nine years ago. I swore an oath over forty graves."

"I know what it is to swear oaths to the dead." His voice was very quiet. "I swore I'd protect my realm. Look how that's going." He looked down at his shaking hands. "Listen to me. Whatever it costs you—and it will cost, it always costs—finish it. Even if finishing kills me. *Especially* if it kills me." He met my eyes and there was nothing behind them now but a man, exhausted and clear. "Six years I have been a window it looks out of. If the price of breaking the window is the glass, pay it. I am so tired. I want to sleep. I want to dream about my wife the true way, not the way it feeds her to me. Promise me you won't stop the rite to save me. Promise me you'll sing through."

"I can't promise that," I said.

"No," he agreed, and something in his face eased, as if honesty were a kind of rest. "No, I suppose you can't. None of them ever can. That's rather the point of you." He released my sleeve and stepped back into the column of moonlight, barefoot on the cold stone, a king in a nightshirt in a room the world forgot. "Go, then. Quickly. It will notice soon—it always notices when I'm lucid this long. And when you stand in that hall, when it wears my face and begs you, remember: whatever it says in my voice—"

"It will lie," Ulyana said.

"Worse." Aldric smiled that terrible hopeful smile one last time. "It will *tell the truth*. Perfectly. In my voice, with my grief, about my sons. That's how it holds a room. So hold onto the one thing it can't counterfeit." He laid his trembling hand flat against his own chest. "An unfinished prayer. It has never once, in six years, been able to finish a sentence of Veyra's. It gets to the threshold of the ending and it *stops*, every time, like a man afraid of a door."

Above us, far off through the dome, a bell began to toll the third hour, and the king's head snapped up, and I watched it happen—watched the depth rise in his eyes like floodwater, watched the man recede behind his own face, watched his shoulders square and his chin lift and the puppet settle back onto its strings with a smoothness that made my stomach turn.

"Guards," it said, in his voice, pleasantly, to no one. Then, softer, almost to itself, almost curious—a thing tasting the air:

"*Someone brought a dragon into my house.*"

We ran.
