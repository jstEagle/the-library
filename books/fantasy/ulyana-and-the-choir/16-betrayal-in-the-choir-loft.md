## 16. Betrayal in the Choir Loft

I want it on the record, wherever records go when no one keeps them, that I never trusted Warden Corvel as far as I could throw his badge. And I could not have thrown his badge far at all, because he'd left it in my hand on the night he defected, and I'd been carrying the damn thing ever since like a stone I couldn't decide whether to skip across a river or swallow.

He was one of mine once—third rank, Verreholm garrison, a tidy man with tidy boots and a way of standing that suggested he'd practiced standing in front of a mirror. When the Edict of Ash went up and I tore my own badge off rather than march children to the palace dungeons, Corvel was one of the four who followed me out. I'd thought, in the soft-headed way of a man who wants to believe his own choices were contagious, that they followed because they believed what I believed.

Three of them did.

The fourth had been counting from the start.

* * *

We holed up in the choir loft above the old Cathedral of Nine Voices, which is exactly the kind of irony Hollowmoot would appreciate if it had a sense of humor instead of an appetite. The loft had been sealed since the cathedral burned forty years back—char-black rafters, pigeons nesting in the organ pipes, dust thick enough to write your sins in. But it was high, and it was forgotten, and from its broken rose window you could see the whole sweep of the palace district: the throne hall's gilded dome, the warden barracks, the smoke still rising off the low quarter where the crown's bonfires of confiscated hymnals had burned for three days straight.

Tamsin hated it immediately.

"Too much echo," he said, whisper-voiced as always now, standing in the middle of the loft with his head tipped back toward the holes in the roof. "Every sound we make up here comes down twice."

"Then we'll be quiet twice," Ulyana said, and spread her arms under the rose window like she was greeting an audience of ghosts. "It's perfect. It's *made* for singing. Can't you feel it? The walls remember."

Tamsin looked at her the way he always looked at her—that exhausted, defeated tenderness of a man watching someone walk cheerfully toward the cliff he fell off nine years ago—and said nothing, because Tamsin's silences are louder than most people's arguments.

The twins, Sorrel and Sennet, were already up in the rafters running harmonies through the broken organ pipes, their two voices braiding into something that made the pigeons evacuate in a body. Pip sat cross-legged beneath them with his drum silent in his lap, tapping rhythms against his knees with his fingertips, mute as ever, patient as stone. Old Maren had gone below with Ysolde's ember-scale wrapped in oilcloth, keeping it warm against her chest like a hen on one impossible egg.

And Corvel stood by the door with his tidy boots and his tidy posture, asking questions.

That should have been the tell. Wardens don't ask questions when they've committed. A man who has burned his bridges doesn't ask where the next bridge is; he builds one or he drowns. But Corvel asked them all week—where the tunnels ran, how many entrances, who else knew the routes, when we meant to move on the palace, what signal would bring Ysolde. And I answered him, God help me, because I was tired, and because he'd stood beside me at Marrowgate's edge with soot on his face and looked like a man who had finally seen clearly.

Looks deceive. That is the first thing they teach you in the warden academy, right before they teach you how to hold a torch.

* * *

It came apart on the sixth night, an hour past midnight, when the cathedral bells rang wrong.

Not the third hour. Not any hour. They rang in the pattern wardens use for a found target—three quick, three slow, three quick—and every hair on my body stood up before my mind caught up to my skin. I'd heard that pattern a hundred times across a hundred burning villages. You never forget the sound of your own profession turned hunting-horn.

I came up the ladder fast and found the loft already awake. Tamsin had Ulyana by the wrist, pulling her back from the window. The twins were frozen mid-note on the rafter beam. Pip had his drum hugged to his chest like a shield.

And Corvel was gone.

His bedroll was still warm. His boots—he owned two pairs, I knew this about him, I knew *everything* pointless about him—both pairs were gone. On the floor where he'd slept, folded neat as a napkin, lay a piece of paper I didn't need to read but read anyway.

*Captain Brannic surrenders himself at the palace gate by dawn, alone, or the crown takes the singers it already knows it can have. The girl first. Signed with the badge you gave me, captain. It opens doors.*

Below us, through the black lattice of the city, lanterns were moving. Not scattered like patrols—converging, deliberate, closing on the cathedral square from six streets at once like fingers making a fist.

"They know," Ulyana said. Her voice didn't shake, which frightened me more than shaking would have. "Brannic, they *know*. How do they know the loft, the numbers, the—"

"Because I told him." My voice came out flat as a coffin lid. "Routes, signals, timing. All of it. He asked and I answered."

Tamsin turned from the window. In the dark his face was all shadow except his eyes, and his eyes had that look—the one from Veyra's chapel, the one that said he'd spent nine years learning exactly how much trust costs. He didn't say "you fool." He didn't say anything. Somehow that was worse.

"The sewers," I said, because self-pity is a luxury for men who aren't captains anymore. "West stair, down through the crypt, into the smuggler runs. Tamsin knows them better than I do. Get everyone moving. Now."

"And you?" Ulyana said.

"I'm going to the gate."

"No," said Tamsin, whisper though it was, and it landed like a shout. "You're not trading yourself for—"

"I'm not trading myself." I was already buckling on my sword, my old warden sword, the one weapon I'd kept clean of village ash. "I'm buying time. There's a difference, and the difference is that you're all going to be underground and moving while every lantern in this district converges on one man walking slowly to the palace gate. They'll take me. Fine. I'm worth more to Hollowmoot alive than dead—a deserter captain, a name on the Edict, a trophy. While they're busy with their trophy, you sing your rite."

"You don't know what it does to voices," Ulyana said quietly. "In those cages. We *heard* them, Brannic. Drained people. Murmuring. It doesn't kill quick."

"I spent eleven years burning villages for that thing," I said. "Whatever it does to me, I've got eleven years of debt to pay it in. Consider this the first installment."

Pip moved then. The boy crossed the loft without a sound—he was always silent even in his silence, moving like a held breath—and put his small hand flat against my breastbone, over the heart, and looked up at me. He didn't speak. He hasn't spoken since Ashfall, since that one word to Tamsin on the road. But his hand said everything his mouth wouldn't: *don't*.

I knelt down to his level, which is a thing captains are not supposed to do, and which I recommend anyway.

"Pip," I said. "When I was a warden, there was a night in Thornfell I let a family run because I couldn't make myself light the torch. One act. One. And it wasn't enough—it was never enough—but it was the only coin I had. This—" I touched his hand where it pressed my chest. "This is the rest of the purse. Go with Tamsin. Sing loud enough for both of us."

He stared at me a long moment with those old, old eyes children get when the world has been too honest with them too young. Then he took his hand back, and he did something he hadn't done since I'd known him.

He tapped his drum. Once. Soft. Like a heartbeat answering.

Then he turned and went to stand beside Tamsin, and I understood I'd just been given a blessing by a child who barely speaks, and that I would carry it wherever they took me.

* * *

The twins nearly broke it all.

Sorrel came down from the rafters swinging, actually swinging, little fists and a voice building in her throat toward whatever harmonic demolition she and her brother could manage, and Sennet had to wrap his arms around her and hum her down—literally hum her down, a low steady note that drained the fury out of her muscles note by note until she stood limp and crying in his arms.

"They'll cage our voices," she whispered over her brother's shoulder. "They'll cage us like the ones in the dungeon. Brannic, don't—"

"Sorrel." I crouched, took her wet face in my hands. "Listen to me. In true harmony, one voice carries further than it should. That's the whole doctrine of the choir, isn't it? So I'm going to be carried. Whatever happens in that palace, part of me goes with every note you sing tomorrow. Make sure I'm somewhere worth being."

She laughed through her tears, one broken huff of a laugh, and hit me on the shoulder hard enough to mean it, and then she let Sennet steer her toward the crypt stairs.

Ulyana stopped at the top of the ladder last, after Tamsin and Pip and the twins had gone down into the dark. She looked at me with those dragon-waking eyes, and for a moment I thought she'd argue, and I braced for it.

Instead she reached up and took the badge from my belt—the badge I'd torn off my own chest the night I deserted, the one I'd carried like a wound ever since—and she pressed it back into my hand.

"You'll want something to surrender *with*," she said. "They respect the uniform. It's the only language the thing on the throne speaks."

Then she kissed my cheek, swift as a bird, and was gone down the ladder, and I stood alone in the burnt cathedral loft with a badge in my fist and the sound of my whole ragged family descending into the earth beneath me.

I listened until the last footfall faded. Then I squared my shoulders the way Corvel used to square his, in front of his mirror, and I went down the other stairs, out into the ringing night, and started walking toward the palace gate—slowly, deliberately, a man delivering himself.

Behind me, faint through the stones, I heard the bells change pattern. Three slow, three slow, three slow.

Target acquired. Come and see.

* * *

They met me two streets short of the gate, which flattered me—I'd expected them to let me walk the whole way. Twenty wardens in full kit, torches banked red, and at their center, of course, Corvel. New badge polished bright on a new coat. Someone had promoted him already. Eleven years of service and I'd made captain; one night of betrayal and he'd skipped a grade entirely. Hollowmoot rewards appetite. It always has.

"Captain Brannic," he said, formal as a parade ground. "Surrender your blade."

I drew it—slow, so the torchlight ran along the steel—and for one long moment twenty hands moved to hilts, because they remembered what I was. Then I reversed it and offered it grip-first, and the weight of it leaving my hand was the strangest relief of my life. Eleven years of carrying that sword, and it turns out the whole time it weighed nothing compared to what I'd done with it.

"Where are the others?" Corvel asked.

"Gone where you can't follow. I came alone. Your paper said alone."

"My paper said alone or we take the girl." He smiled, tidy as his boots. "You came alone. The terms were met either way. You understand I'd have preferred—"

"I understand exactly what you'd have preferred." I stepped closer, close enough that only he could hear, and watched his smile flicker. "Here's what you don't understand yet, Corvel. You think you sold information. You sold *us*—a drummer boy who can't defend himself, two children, a girl. And the thing you sold us to will eat every one of those voices and grow fatter, and one day it will be hungry again, and it will look around at its servants, and it will find yours. It ate a king, Corvel. What makes you a better meal than Aldric?"

Something moved behind his eyes—not doubt, not yet, but the seed of it, the first cold drop before the storm. I've seen that look on recruits the first time they burn a shrine and realize the fire doesn't feel like justice. It passes, usually. Sometimes it doesn't.

"Bind him," Corvel said, louder than necessary, and turned away first. That was answer enough.

They marched me through the gate, through courts I'd patrolled a hundred times, down stairs I'd descended a hundred times, and the whole way I kept my eyes forward and counted my heartbeats like a rosary. In the dungeon antechamber they stopped me, and a clerk with ink-stained fingers consulted a list, and read my name aloud in a bored voice—my name among hundreds, a ledger of the stolen—and two handlers took my arms.

And here is the thing I will tell whoever finds this account, if anyone does: I was afraid. Properly, thoroughly afraid, the fear sitting in my stomach like swallowed ice. But underneath the fear, underneath everything, there was a note holding. Somewhere beneath the palace, in the dark of the smuggler runs, a mute boy's single drumbeat and a girl with dragon-fire in her throat and a hollow man remembering how to sing. True harmony carries further than it should.

I'd bought them hours. Maybe a night.

"Hall first," one handler said to the other. "The throne wants to look at this one itself."

The throne wants to look. My blood went cold, and colder still when I understood why that pleased some grim corner of me: every minute that thing spent looking at me was a minute it wasn't listening for the others.

They opened the great doors, and the light that came out was wrong—too gold, too warm, warm like a mouth—and I walked into it with my chin up and my empty hands open, a man with nothing left to give except exactly what he'd promised.

Behind me, far off under the city, so faint I might have dreamed it, I heard singing begin.
