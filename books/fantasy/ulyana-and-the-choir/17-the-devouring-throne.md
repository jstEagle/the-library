## 17. The Devouring Throne

They took my boots.

I want that on the record, somewhere, in some ledger of the world's small cruelties: they dragged me through the palace of Verreholm barefoot, over marble so cold it felt like walking on the bones of winter itself, and someone—a warden with a bored face and a fresh scar across his jaw—decided my boots were a threat to national security.

"Superstitious," he'd said, when I asked why. "Gifted hide things in their boots."

I had a pebble in my left one and half a biscuit in my right, and now both belonged to the crown. I hope the king chokes on the biscuit. I hope it's stale.

The corridor they marched me down was long enough to have opinions about itself. Every twenty paces, a niche held a statue of some dead monarch, and every statue had been defaced in the same peculiar way—not broken, not toppled, but *smoothed*, their faces worn to blank ovals, as if something had licked them clean over centuries. I tried not to think about what else might have been licked clean in this place. I failed. Thinking about it was better than thinking about Brannic, walking away down a different corridor with his hands bound and his spine straight, buying our lives with his own because that was simply the kind of man he'd decided to be somewhere between burning children and refusing to.

*Don't sing until you can't help it,* Tamsin had told me. *And then don't stop.*

Good advice. Terrible advice. The kind of advice you give when you love someone and want them to live and know those two things no longer fit together neatly.

The doors at the end of the corridor were black iron, taller than Ysolde, carved with a scene I didn't understand until much later and understood too well after: a circle of figures with open mouths, and above them a shape like a crown, or a mouth, or both.

Then the doors opened, and I stopped having room in my head for anything but what was inside.

* * *

The throne hall of Verreholm was not a hall. That's the only way I can describe it. It had walls—white stone veined with gold, pillars thick as old trees, windows of colored glass throwing red and violet light across the floor—but none of it added up to a *room*. A room holds things. This space *held nothing*, because everything in it had been taken. No dust. No echo. My footsteps made no sound at all, which is a sentence I need you to sit with for a moment. I walked, and the world declined to confirm it.

And on the throne, King Aldric sat, and did not sit.

He looked wrong in the way a puppet looks wrong when you notice the strings. His body was there—broad shoulders, a king's bearing, hands resting on the arms of the throne with terrible patience—but his eyes were the problem. They were open. They were looking at me. And behind them, far back, like a man drowning under river ice, pressing his palms up against the underside of the surface, was someone else. Someone screaming without a mouth.

*Aldric,* I thought. *That's Aldric. He's still in there.*

And sitting on him, wearing him, growing out of him like ivy grown into the mortar of a wall until you can't pull it free without pulling the wall apart—

Hollowmoot.

I had expected a monster. Scales, or shadow, or teeth. Something you could point at. What I saw instead was worse: I saw *light*. Hollowmoot glowed. It sat in and around the king like honey poured through a honeycomb, golden and warm and beautiful, radiating an aura of such perfect contentment that my knees nearly buckled with the urge to kneel. Every instinct I had—the ones that had kept me alive since the wardens burned my village—screamed that this thing was holy.

That's how it hunts, I realized. That's how it's always hunted. Not with claws. With *worship*.

"You brought me a singer," it said.

Its voice came from everywhere. From the pillars, from the glass, from inside my own skull where my thoughts should have been alone. It sounded like a choir humming one note in perfect unison, and if you've never heard that, let me tell you: it is the most beautiful sound in the world, and it is also the sound of every individual voice being erased.

"The girl who wakes dragons." The light pulsed, pleased. "Ysolde's little ember-stirrer. Do you know how long it has been since anything sang to me uninvited?"

"I'm guessing six years," I said. "Since you started wearing the king like a coat."

The warmth flickered. Just for a heartbeat, the hall went cold, and I saw—glimpsed—the truth underneath the gold: something vast and gray and *hungry*, folded impossibly into the small space of a human king, all appetite and no shape. Then the warmth came back, smoother than before, and I understood I'd been shown the flicker on purpose. A warning dressed as honesty.

"You may watch," it said. "Before your turn. I find it goes easier, afterward, when they've seen."

*Seen what?*

I found out immediately.

* * *

The cages lined the wall behind the throne.

I'd heard about the dungeons from Tamsin—caged voices, drained and murmuring—but hearing about a thing and standing ten feet from it are different animals entirely. There must have been forty of them: iron cages, each barely large enough for a person to sit curled, each holding a prisoner who was not quite a prisoner anymore. Their bodies hung slack. Their chests rose and fell. But their mouths moved, endlessly, silently, shaping words and songs and names with no breath behind them, because the breath had been taken. Because the *voice* had been taken.

And above each cage, faint as heat-shimmer, hung the voice itself. Visible. Golden threads of light, drifting up from the prisoners' lips like smoke from candles, rising, rising—and pouring into the radiant mass around the throne.

Hollowmoot was eating breakfast.

It ate slowly, luxuriously, the way I imagine emperors eat. Each thread of stolen song drifted into its glow and dissolved, and with each one the light grew brighter, fatter, more *certain*. I could hear fragments as they went in—a lullaby, a work song, someone's grandmother singing a harvest hymn, a child practicing scales—all of it stripped of its singer, all of it becoming just more fuel for the beautiful, horrible warmth.

One thread, near the top, still carried words. An old woman's voice, thin and stubborn:

*"...and though the road be long and cold, the fire we carry—"*

It dissolved before she could finish. Hollowmoot sighed with the satisfaction of a full belly, and the woman in the cage below the fading thread slumped a little further into herself, her mouth still moving, still singing, with nothing left to sing *with*.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very hard.

Here's what nobody tells you about terror: past a certain point, it stops feeling like fear and starts feeling like clarity. The world narrows. The noise falls away. You see exactly what is in front of you, exactly what you are, exactly what you have left.

What I had left was a voice. One voice. Mine.

Tamsin would have told me to wait. Wait for the others, wait for the plan, wait for Ysolde's fire and the freed captives and the rite and the harmony—wait, wait, wait, because waiting is what survivors do, waiting is how you stay alive. And Tamsin would be right. Tamsin is usually right, in the exhausting way that people are right when they've already lost everything once and built their whole personality around never losing it again.

But the old woman's harvest hymn was still dissolving in the air, unfinished, and I have never in my life finished a song the way it deserved and I was not going to start by letting hers die incomplete.

So I did the stupidest, most necessary thing I have ever done.

I planted my bare feet on the silent marble, filled my lungs with air that tasted of gold and hunger, and I sang.

* * *

I didn't start with power. I'm not an idiot. Well—I'm *some* kind of idiot, but not that kind. I started soft, a single low note, the note Ulyana-the-smuggler used to hum to calm horses, the note that thaws ice. I put the old woman's hymn under it, picked up her melody where Hollowmoot had swallowed it, and carried it forward.

*"—the fire we carry shall not go out."*

The effect was immediate and catastrophic.

Every cage rattled. Forty drained prisoners lifted their heads—*lifted their heads*, the first movement most of them had made in who knew how long—and their silent mouths turned toward me like flowers toward a sun they'd forgotten existed. And in the air around them, the golden threads *trembled*. Stopped drifting toward the throne. Quivered in place, like compass needles finding north.

Because here's the thing about voices, even stolen ones: they remember who they belong to. And mine was telling them, in the oldest language there is, *not yet. Not this. Hold on.*

"Stop," said Hollowmoot.

I did not stop.

I opened up. All of it—the dragon-waking register, the one that had gotten Ysolde's ember burning, the one the wardens had chased me across three provinces for. The note came out of me like a struck bell, and the silent hall *shattered* into sound. For the first time since I'd entered, the throne hall echoed. My voice bounced off the white stone and came back multiplied, and the caged voices rose to meet it—thin, ragged, barely there, but *there*, forty ghosts humming along, and even that much harmony was more than this place had held in years.

Hollowmoot screamed.

Not with its choir-voice. With something rawer, older, uglier—the gray thing beneath the gold, the hunger with its mask knocked askew. The light around the king convulsed. The warmth curdled into pressure, a fist closing around my throat from thirty feet away, and pain went through my skull like a hot wire drawn tight from ear to ear.

I kept singing.

Blood, I discovered, has a taste. Copper and salt. It ran warm over my lip while I held the note, and every second I held it, the threads above the cages pulled harder against the thing feeding on them, and somewhere behind the king's drowning eyes, I swear I felt Aldric press his palms against the ice again—*harder*—because he could hear it too. Because someone was finally making noise in the house where he'd been buried alive.

"You are *nothing*," Hollowmoot roared, and the fist around my throat became a hand, became a jaw, became the whole gray weight of it turning its attention on me, and I learned what it meant to be *heard* by a god that eats songs. It reached into my voice the way you'd reach into a birdcage. It began to pull.

Seconds. I was buying seconds. That was always the plan, wasn't it—Brannic's surrender bought us hours, my singing bought us seconds, and seconds were going to have to be enough because somewhere in the dark under this palace, Tamsin Zhou was moving toward me with a prayer nine years unfinished and a company of strays at his back, and every second I stayed upright, every second the parasite's attention was on *me* instead of the doors, was a second closer to the sound I'd crossed a kingdom hoping to hear.

My knees hit the marble. The note bent. Blood filled my mouth properly now, and the golden threads above the cages strained toward me like hands reaching through water, and I thought—with the last corner of my mind still mine—*hold on, hold on, hold on, I am coming to finish what Veyra started, I am coming with everyone I love, just keep breathing, all of you, just keep—*

The great iron doors boomed.

Once. Twice. A third time, deeper, hotter, and through the black iron seams I saw light—not gold, not the fat warm glow of the thing on the throne, but *fire*. Guttering, ancient, furious fire, the color of the last ember in a dying hearth refusing, absolutely refusing, to go out.

Hollowmoot's grip on my throat loosened—one instant of distraction, one instant of a god looking over its shoulder—and I dragged air into my ruined chest and did the only reasonable thing left.

I sang louder.

The doors burst inward in a rain of molten iron, and standing in the smoke, backlit by dragonfire, was Tamsin Zhou—pale, terrified, absolutely certain—and behind him, limping and blazing and magnificent, came Ysolde, and behind *her*, filling the doorway like a tide coming in, came every voice we had left.

The last thing I saw before the world became music was the king's face. Aldric's face. For one clear moment, the drowning man broke the surface of his own eyes, looked straight at me, and mouthed two words:

*Finish it.*
