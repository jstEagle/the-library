## 18. Every Voice We Have

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in a palace built on stolen voices. I'd heard it once before, nine years ago, on a mountain, in the half-second after Veyra stopped mid-prayer and forty singers fell down dead with their mouths still open. It's not an absence of sound. It's an absence of *permission* for sound. A silence that watches you.

The throne hall of Verreholm was full of it.

We came up out of the dark the way smugglers do—badly lit, badly armed, and ahead of schedule. Brannic had bought us the corridor with his surrender; Ulyana had broken the dungeon locks with three notes pitched so low I felt them in my back teeth rather than heard them. The freed captives shuffled behind us like people waking from a fever, hollow-eyed, mouthing words that had no weight left in them. Their voices were gone. Eaten. But they could still walk, and they could still follow, and when Ysolde's shadow passed over the sewer grates above us—vast and guttering and older than the kingdom—they lifted their faces toward it the way you lift your face toward sun you'd stopped believing in.

"Left at the cistern," I whispered, because old habits die hard and new ones hadn't been born yet. "Then straight up the service stair until someone tries to kill us."

"That's most stairs," said Pip.

I nearly dropped the lamp. The mute drummer boy—the boy who hadn't spoken since Marrowgate, who communicated in taps and scowls and one devastating raised eyebrow—had spoken twice now in as many days, and both times it had landed somewhere under my ribs and stayed there.

"Most stairs," I agreed, and we went up.

* * *

The doors to the throne hall were gold, because of course they were. Everything about Hollowmoot was gaudy in the way of things that have never once worried about being hungry again. Twin leaves of beaten gold taller than Ysolde's shoulder, worked with scenes of King Aldric's coronation, except the artist had given the king too many teeth and no one had ever corrected it.

Ulyana put her hand flat against the metal. "It's humming," she breathed. "Tamsin, the whole door is humming."

She was right. Beneath the stone and gold ran a vibration, slow and rhythmic, like something enormous digesting. Every voice it had taken in six years, murmuring together inside that thing's chest. Forty thousand swallowed songs, keeping the parasite's heart beating.

Ysolde reared up behind us in the stairwell, her scales dull as old iron, the fire in her throat barely an ember now—she had spent most of what remained breaking the dungeon cages open with a single cough of flame, and she was running on fumes and fury and nine hundred years of stubbornness.

*"Little thief,"* she rumbled, and her voice came through the floor more than the air. *"When I burn, stand behind me. When I stop burning—"*

"When you stop burning," I said, "we sing."

*"Good. Because I am very nearly stopped."*

Brannic would have wanted to say something tactical. Brannic wasn't here—he was two levels down in a warden cell, trading his freedom for ours, and every step I took away from him felt like leaving a debt unpaid. Later. If there was a later, I'd pay it. That was the whole arithmetic of survival: do the thing in front of you, and let the debts pile up behind you like snow.

I set my palm against the gold doors. And for the first time in nine years, I didn't flinch from what answered.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about burying a gift: it doesn't die. It just gets patient.

For nine years my song-magic had lain in me like a knife wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of a well. I'd told myself it was dead. I'd sworn it dead, standing over the bodies of my choir with Veyra's unfinished prayer still ringing in my skull like a snapped bell-wire. *Never again.* I'd paid roadside shrines in silence. I'd watched wardens burn a village shrine and done nothing, because doing nothing was the price of being no one, and being no one was the price of being alive.

But a knife in oilcloth is still a knife.

When I touched the door, the hum of all those eaten voices reached into the well and found the handle. And my gift rose toward it—not roaring, not dramatic, just rising, the way water rises in a dry riverbed when the mountain snow finally gives. Warm. Familiar. Horrible.

*Tamsin,* it seemed to say, in a voice that was mine and also, faintly, underneath, someone else's—someone with a voice like morning over the high passes. *You kept it. All this time, you kept it.*

"I kept nothing," I whispered. "I hid."

Same thing, said the warmth. Same thing.

"Tamsin?" Ulyana's hand on my arm. "What do you feel?"

"An old friend," I said, "who owes me an apology." I looked at her—at this girl who'd walked into my smuggling route half-frozen and humming, who claimed she heard Veyra screaming under the world, who had turned my careful gray life into a war in the space of a season. "Are the others ready?"

Behind us, the strays had arranged themselves without anyone arranging them. The twin harmonists, Sef and Sorrel, shoulder to shoulder, breathing already matched. Old Maren, the storm-singer's protégée, hands steady on her walking stick. Pip with his drum slung across his back, because a boy who couldn't speak had decided long ago that rhythm was his voice, and tonight his voice would matter as much as any. And the freed captives, dozens of them, voiceless, leaning against the walls of the stairwell with their ruined throats and their unbroken eyes.

"They've been ready since the dungeons," Ulyana said. "They're just waiting for you."

That was the sentence I'd been dodging for nine years, delivered by a nineteen-year-old in a stolen coat. Waiting for *you*. Not for a god. Not for a miracle. For the man who used to conduct, whose lead line used to hold forty voices together through hail and horror and the long cold nights on campaign, before the night everything stopped.

"I'm not a leader," I said. "I'm a salt smuggler with a bad knee."

"You're a choir master," she said, "with a bad knee. There's a difference, and it's about four hundred miles north of here, and we're going to sing it home."

I laughed. Actually laughed, there in the dark before the golden doors, which tells you either how terrified I was or how far I'd come. Possibly both are the same thing.

"All right," I said. "All right. Here's how it goes."

* * *

Ysolde burned the doors.

Not opened them—*burned* them, melted them into two golden rivers that ran smoking across the marble, because the last dragon in the world has opinions about entrances, and hers is that if a door cannot survive meeting you, it did not deserve to be a door.

The light hit the throne hall like dawn hitting a slaughterhouse.

Hollowmoot sat on the throne wearing Aldric the way a hermit crab wears a borrowed shell—but there was nothing borrowed about how well it fit. It had grown into the shape of the king, filled him out, gleamed through his skin. It was radiant. That was the worst part. Six years of feeding had made it beautiful, tall and luminous, crowned in actual living light, and around its feet the courtiers knelt with expressions of perfect bliss, because something that eats voices can also exhale a little counterfeit peace, and peace is the easiest lie in the world to swallow.

It turned its head—all the way around, past where a neck should stop—and looked at us.

And it smiled with Aldric's mouth, and said, in a voice made of forty thousand stolen singers, in a voice I recognized down to its bones:

***"Veyra's little tithe-payer. Come to finish the prayer at last? Come closer. I have been keeping it warm for you."***

Every hair on my body stood up. Because it was right—that was Veyra's cadence, Veyra's phrasing, worn like a coat, and hearing my god's voice come out of the thing that ate him was a violence I don't have clean words for.

"You don't get to use his voice," I said. My own came out low and even, smuggler-calm, and I was grateful for it. "You've had six years of everyone else's. You don't get his."

***"I have had everything,"*** it said, rising from the throne, light pouring off it like heat off a forge. ***"I ate gods before your god had a name. I will eat you last, little thief, and I will keep your grief—it has such good bones."***

"Yeah," said Pip, from behind me, and beat his drum once. "Come and try."

Gods bless the terrible brave children of the world.

* * *

Maren went first, because Maren had learned from the best and the best had died holding a gate, and some lessons pass down like blood.

Her voice came out of her small body like weather—a low rolling note that bent the torch flames sideways and made the kneeling courtiers clutch their ears. Hollowmoot flinched. Actually flinched, its radiance guttering, and I understood in that instant what the old woman had known: the parasite could eat a voice, yes, but eating takes *time*, and a voice aimed true hurts on the way down.

Sef and Sorrel came in over her line, harmonies crossing like blades, and the air itself began to shiver. Ulyana took the high descant, that impossible clear thread that had thawed a frozen cave and woken a dying dragon, and beneath it all Pip laid down the pulse—boom, boom-boom, boom—the heartbeat of a choir assembling itself out of strays and ruins and sheer refusal.

Hollowmoot screamed. The courtiers scattered. Warden guards poured in from the side galleries and Brannic's testimony must have traveled faster than we thought, because half of them threw down their weapons rather than raise them against children and grandmothers, and the other half hesitated exactly long enough—

Exactly long enough for Ysolde.

The last dragon of Thornfell dragged herself through the melted doorway, ancient and dimming, ember-eyed, and opened her mouth over the throne hall, and gave us everything she had left. Not the fire of legend. Not the fire that had burned the dungeon cages open. This was the dregs, the last coal in a nine-hundred-year stove—but a dragon's dregs could still turn marble to glass, and the wave of heat rolled over the hall and broke against Hollowmoot's light like surf on rock, and in the steam and smoke and chaos, the drained captives began to whisper.

That was the moment. I want to be exact about it, because I have thought about it every day since.

They had no voices. The thing on the throne had seen to that. But a whisper is not a voice, not exactly—a whisper is what's left when everything else is taken, the shape of a song remembered by a body the song has already been evicted from. They couldn't carry a melody. They couldn't hold a harmony. But they could breathe in rhythm, dozens of them, hundreds, a rustling tide of breath like wind through winter wheat, and when they breathed *together*—

The choir found its floor.

And I stepped forward into the space where a lead line goes, the space I had left empty for nine years, and I opened my mouth, and I sang.

* * *

I won't tell you it was beautiful. It was necessary, which is different, and harder, and lasts longer.

The prayer of Veyra is not a hymn. I need you to understand that. Hymns are things people write *about* gods. The prayer was the god's own voice, the one he was singing when he fell silent mid-phrase, the one that had been hanging unfinished in the world for nine years like a door left open in winter. I had learned it at his knee, or whatever counts as a knee for a god of the high passes. I had sung it every dawn with my choir until the morning I swore never to sing anything again.

It begins low. Almost speech. *Light before the road, road before the door, door before the hearth—* it's a wayfinding prayer, a traveler's prayer, a prayer for getting home. Veyra was the god of the long road and the safe arrival, and his great work, the one he never finished, was a song that would call every lost thing home. Every exile. Every refugee. Every voice locked in a cage or buried in a grave or eaten by a hungry god.

*Home,* the prayer says, over and over, in forty languages, *home, home, come home.*

My voice cracked on the second phrase. Nine years of rust, and terror, and the memory of forty bodies falling. I cracked, and the crack went out into the hall, and instead of swallowing me, the choir caught it—Ulyana's descant slid under my break and held it up, the twins braided around it, Maren's weather grounded it, Pip's drum marked time for it, and the whispering captives breathed the rhythm of it, and my broken note became a *shared* note, and that is the entire secret of singing that no soloist ever believes until it happens to them.

A voice alone can be devoured. A voice among voices is a door, and doors open both ways.

Hollowmoot came off the throne in a rush of stolen light, and it was screaming in forty thousand voices at once—screaming in my dead choir's voices, in the twins' mother's voice, in voices I knew and voices I never would—and it reached for me with hands made of everything it had ever eaten. I felt it take hold of my song. Felt the pull, the awful suction, the same feeling from nine years ago on the mountain, the feeling of being *unmade mid-note*—

And I sang louder.

Because here is what I understood in that moment, the thing that took me nine years and a girl in a frozen cave and a drummer boy's two sentences and a dragon's last ember to understand: I had spent those nine years thinking silence was safety. Thinking that what happened to my choir was the price of singing, and that never singing again meant it could never happen again. But my choir hadn't died because they sang. They died because they sang *alone*, unanswered, their god struck dumb above them, their song going out into a darkness with nothing on the other end.

Tonight there was something on the other end.

Tonight the whole hall was on the other end.

*Home,* we sang, all of us, the cracked and the whole and the voiceless breathing in time, *home, home, come home*—and the prayer climbed toward the phrase where Veyra had fallen silent, the unfinished place, the wound in the world, and I felt the god-bone shard warm against my chest where Ulyana had wired it under my shirt like a smuggled jewel, and through it, faint as a pulse, I heard it:

Veyra. Not screaming anymore.

*Waiting.*

Waiting for the last line. Waiting nine years for someone to finish the sentence.

I got to the edge of the silence. I stood on the rim of the place where my god's voice had been cut off, with the parasite's claws in my throat and my choir's ghosts in my lungs and a girl's descant holding up the sky, and I took the breath that was supposed to carry the final phrase of a god's prayer home—

And Hollowmoot *took my voice.*

Not my breath. Not my life. My *voice*—ripped out of me whole, the way it had ripped the prayer from Veyra, the way it had emptied the dungeons, a theft so fast and total that my mouth kept moving and produced nothing but air. The final line died in my throat. The choir faltered. The parasite reared back with my song shining in its fist like a pulled tooth, radiant, triumphant, Aldric's face stretched around a grin of pure hunger—

And Ulyana stepped into the space where my voice had been.

She looked at me—just a glance, half a second, a whole conversation in it—and then she took the lead line, the prayer's final phrase, the words I'd carried for nine years and been robbed of at the threshold, and she sang them.

I stood voiceless in the burning hall of Verreholm, mouth open, hands half-raised in the shape of a conductor, and listened to a nineteen-year-old finish what my god started.

The last line of Veyra's prayer, the one that had hung unfinished over the world for nine years, rang out at last:

*"—and when the road ends, let the road remember me."*

The shard of god-bone against my chest went white-hot.

And the roof of the world opened.
