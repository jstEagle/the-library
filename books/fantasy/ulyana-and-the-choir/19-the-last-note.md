## 19. The Last Note

I have sung in front of dying men before. I have held the last verse of a funeral hymn while the light went out of a friend's eyes, because someone had to, and I was the one whose voice could carry it.

None of that prepared me for singing at the thing that eats voices.

The throne hall of Verreholm was built to make you feel small. Vaulted ceilings like the inside of a throat, banners of every conquered province hanging dead and heavy along the walls, and at the far end, the throne itself—black stone shot through with veins that pulsed faintly gold, like something alive had been poured into the masonry and never quite set. Hollowmoot's light. It had grown fat on nine years of stolen song, and it wore its fullness now the way a tick wears blood.

Aldric sat beneath it, a man-shaped absence. His eyes were open. Nothing behind them was home.

Ulyana stood before the throne, alone where she'd been dragged, her wrists bound in warden-iron that was supposed to smother her gift—and wasn't, because gifts don't live in the flesh they're bound to, they live deeper, and whoever forged those manacles had forgotten that or never known. She was singing. I heard her from the shadowed colonnade where we'd come through the broken dungeon cages, and my chest hurt hearing it, because she was buying seconds with pain. Every note cost her. The parasite was leaning into her voice like a diner leaning into soup, drinking as fast as she poured, and still she sang, defiant, furious, *loud*, holding its attention the way you hold a wolf's eyes while your family crawls toward the door.

Ysolde moved beside me, vast and slow, her scales dull as old iron, the ember of her fire barely a coal. Even guttering, a dragon is an argument the architecture loses. Her shoulder took out two columns as she entered, and the whole hall rang like a struck bell.

Hollowmoot turned.

It didn't turn Aldric's head so much as pour his attention around, the way water finds a new level. And when its gaze found us—the dragon, the strays, the freed captives stumbling in our wake, whisper-throated and hollow-eyed but *here*, by every god living or dead, *here*—it made a sound.

Not with Aldric's mouth. With everything. The banners shivered. The gold veins in the throne flared. And inside my skull, a voice like a hundred stolen choirs speaking in unison said:

*I know that voice.*

It was looking at me.

"Sing," Ulyana screamed, and her voice cracked down the middle. "Tamsin, *sing*!"

So I did.

Nine years I'd kept it caged. Nine years of wordless tithes and salt-smuggling and pretending the music in me was a scar instead of a wound that still bled. And now I opened the cage and let Veyra's unfinished prayer come out of me raw, no polish, no craft, just the thing itself—the hymn my choir died singing, the prayer that stops mid-line, the one that asks a god to unbind what is bound and bind what is loose. My voice came out strange after so long, cracked at the bottom, thin at the top, and none of it mattered, because the rite doesn't want beauty. It wants truth.

Pip found the rhythm first. He always does. His sticks came down on the drumhead he'd carried three hundred miles, and the beat went through the floor like a pulse remembering itself. The twins came in next, harmonizing a third apart the way only they can, close enough that their voices braided into something neither could make alone. Old Marta—no, Marta was ash at Marrowgate, holding the gate; the thought arrived and I let it pass through me like weather—Brannic came in then, and I didn't know the warden captain could sing, low and steady, a bass line like bedrock. The freed captives lifted their ruined whispers, threadbare scraps of voice, and the rite gathered them too, because the rite doesn't ask if you're strong enough. It asks if you're willing.

And Ulyana—Ulyana stopped fighting alone and turned, chains rattling, and gave her voice to the harmony, and the sound we made in that murdered hall was the first true thing Verreholm had heard in six years.

Hollowmoot screamed.

The gold veins went white. Aldric's body convulsed on the throne, hands clawing the armrests, and for one lucid instant his eyes were his own—terrified, human, *begging*—and then the parasite dug in and the pulling started.

I felt it take the others first. The twins' harmony thinned, frayed, and I watched their mouths keep moving while their sound drained away into that hungry light. Pip's rhythm stuttered. Brannic's bass dropped out note by note like men falling off a wall. It ate them the way it had eaten my choir—mid-hymn, mid-breath, mid-offering—and nine years fell away and I was twenty-three again in a mountain chapel watching eleven people go silent one after another while I stood there holding a note nobody answered.

No.

*No.*

I pushed harder. I threw everything at it—the grief, the guilt, the salt roads and the shrines I'd tithe in silence, all of it packed into the lead line, and for a moment it worked. The prayer surged. The veins flickered. Aldric's face slackened, the grip loosening—

Then Hollowmoot did what cornered things do.

It stopped eating everyone else and took hold of *me*.

* * *

You would think losing your voice would be quiet.

It isn't. It is the loudest silence in the world. It is being pulled inside-out through your own throat. It is every note you have ever sung being called home at once, demanded back, repossessed, and I could feel them leaving me—my mother's lullabies, the work songs of the caravans, the hymn I'd sung over Brannic's badge when he tore it off, the tithe I'd whispered wordless for nine years suddenly finding its words at the worst possible moment—and Hollowmoot hauled all of it up through me like a fisherman hauling net, greedy, triumphant, certain.

My mouth was still open. Nothing came out.

The choir faltered around me. I saw Ulyana look at me—saw her understand in one terrible heartbeat what had happened, saw her see her own future in it—and I tried to tell her with my face: *keep going*. I tried to say it with my hands, the conducting hands, the ones I'd taught myself to trust when my voice became a liability. *Finish it. Finish the prayer.*

She didn't finish it.

She *changed* it. She stepped into the lead line—seventeen years old, half-starved, wrists bleeding against warden-iron—and she took the melody I could no longer carry and carried it herself, and the strays rallied to her, and here is the thing about the unbinding rite that I understood only then, kneeling voiceless on the stones of my enemy's hall:

The prayer doesn't need my voice. It never did. Veyra wrote it to be finished by *anyone* who means it. That was the whole design. A god who wanted his song to survive him built it to be picked up by strangers.

Hollowmoot hadn't understood that. It had eaten lead singers for centuries, believing the song lived in the throat it stole. It had devoured my choir thinking it had killed the hymn. It was devouring me now, drinking nine years of hoarded voice, and it thought it was winning.

Meanwhile a girl who hears screaming gods sang the line after mine, and a mute boy beat time, and two sisters harmonized, and a traitor-captain held the bass, and forty hollowed captives whispered the descant, and Ysolde opened her mouth.

* * *

I have seen dragonfire. Everyone has, in stories. The stories are wrong.

What came out of Ysolde was not fire. It was the last ember of the oldest living thing in Thornfell, given freely, spent completely—one breath of flame that should have ended her, and maybe it will, and she spent it anyway, because that is what dragons understand about gifts that people like me take nine years to learn: a gift you keep is a possession. A gift you give is a prayer.

Her fire rose through the center of the hall and met the parasite's light, and where they touched, Veyra's voice came through.

I heard it with what was left of me, with the part that isn't throat or tongue or any organ a parasite can grip. I heard my god—not returned, not resurrected, nothing so kind—but *present*, the way warmth is present in a stone long after the sun has moved on. The final prayer rising on dragonfire, sung by a girl and a choir of strays and the ghost-warmth of a silent god, completing the line that had hung unfinished for six thousand years:

*Unbind what is bound. Bind what is loose. Let the borrowed be returned.*

Hollowmoot shrieked with every voice it had ever stolen—a thousand stolen throats crying out at once, and some of them, gods forgive me, some of them sounded *relieved*—and the gold veins in the throne went dark vein by vein like a city losing power district by district, and the thing wearing Aldric tore.

I don't have words for what tearing a god out of a king looks like. Smoke and light and a shape like a mouth, a shape like hunger given geometry, thrashing against the rite, reaching for anything left to eat—and finding, in me, nothing. It had taken it all. Every note. I knelt in the wreckage of my own voice, empty as a rung bell, and the parasite scrabbled at my silence and got nothing, because I had finally learned the lesson nine years late: what you give away, it cannot take.

Veyra's warmth closed over it like a hand closing over a moth.

And the last thing I felt, before the light went out of the throne forever, was not victory. It was the stolen voices going home—forty captives in the dungeons breathing deep, the twins' harmony returning to them mid-note, Pip's rhythm landing solid, Brannic's bass rolling back like a tide coming in—and one voice, one small bright thread of mine, coming back too.

Part of it. A whisper's worth. The rest stayed in the fire, spent, and I found, kneeling there deaf to my own singing, that I could not bring myself to regret it.

Aldric collapsed forward off the throne like a puppet with its strings cut, and Brannic caught him before he hit the ground—an old man, a *small* man, sixty years of stolen sleep crashing down on him at once, weeping without sound.

Ulyana lowered her arms. The hymn ended on the note it had waited six thousand years to end on.

And in the ringing silence after, I opened my mouth to say something—anything, a thank-you, a prayer, her name—and what came out was a whisper, thin as smoke, barely mine.

It was enough.

It was, I realized, sitting down hard on the cold stones of a hall that smelled of ash and ozone and old gold, entirely, exactly enough.

Ulyana knelt in front of me, soot-streaked and shaking, and looked at my mouth as I shaped the words. She read them off my lips the way she reads everything—directly, impatiently, completely.

"What did you say?" she asked.

I whispered it again, and this time she laughed, wet and disbelieving, and the dragon behind us exhaled a thread of smoke that might have been a sigh, and somewhere above us dawn was coming to a city that hadn't earned it yet but was going to get it anyway.

I said: *Now we bury our dead, and then we rebuild the chapel.*

Because a prayer that finishes doesn't end.
