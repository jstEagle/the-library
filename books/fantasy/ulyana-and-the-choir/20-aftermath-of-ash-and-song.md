## 20. Aftermath of Ash and Song

Here is what nobody tells you about killing a god: it is mostly quiet afterward.

I had expected—what? Thunder. The world cracking open like an egg. Some cosmic groan as Hollowmoot was pried off the throne of Verreholm like a leech lifted from skin. Instead there was a long exhale that moved through the whole palace, through the whole city, through me, and then nothing but the sound of ash drifting down through the shattered skylights of the throne hall, settling on the dead and the living alike without any particular preference for either.

Ash, I should say, from Ysolde's fire. Ash from the burned villages we'd crossed to get here. Ash from six years of Edict pyres, all of it coming home at once, sifting over the marble like the realm itself was trying to bury its own mess before company arrived.

I was sitting on the steps below the dais because my legs had stopped being interested in standing. Tamsin was crumpled at the foot of the throne, breathing in little ragged pulls, one hand pressed flat against his own throat as if he could hold his voice in by force. He couldn't. We all knew he couldn't. Hollowmoot had gone out of the world with its fingers hooked in him, and when it tore free it took most of nine years' worth of buried song along for the road.

"Say something," Pip whispered beside me. He'd spoken more words in the last hour than in the last three years, and every one of them still came out sounding like a miracle someone had dropped.

Tamsin's mouth moved. What came out was barely a breath. *It's done.* Two words, and they cost him like a whole hymn.

"Yeah," I said, because somebody had to be loud enough for both of us, and apparently that job was mine now. "Yeah, it's done."

* * *

The king woke up screaming.

Not the way Aldric had screamed before—the hollow, borrowed sound the thing wearing him used when it was pleased, which was worse than screaming really—but actual screaming, human and cracked and terrified, the sound of a man surfacing from six years underwater into lungs full of light.

Brannic reached him first. Brannic, who had walked back into the hall after giving himself up for us, who should by every law of stories be dead, and who instead had spent Hollowmoot's final moments flat on his belly behind an overturned brazier, watching everything, cataloguing everything, because that was simply who Brannic was: a man who could not stop being a warden even after he'd torn the badge off his own chest and thrown it in a river.

"It's all right," Brannic was saying, low and steady, the voice you use on spooked horses and children pulled from fires. "Your Majesty. It's all right. It's gone. You're—" He hesitated. You could see him deciding how much truth a man newly returned to his own skull could carry. "You're yourself."

Aldric stopped screaming. He looked at his own hands like they belonged to a stranger. They did, in a way; they were older than they'd been six years ago, and thinner, and shaking. Then he looked at the throne—the actual chair, scorched black down one side where Ysolde's guttering flame had licked across it—and made a sound I will never manage to describe properly. Not a scream. Not a sob. Something between a laugh and a wound.

"Six years," he said. His voice was rusted. "Has it been six years?"

"Six years, Your Majesty." Brannic hadn't moved to help him up. Smart. Some men need to stand on their own or not at all.

Aldric stood. It took him three tries. When he finally got upright he swayed there at the top of the dais, an old man in a scorched coat, looking down at us—at the drained captives huddled among their broken cages, at the twins holding each other, at Pip's drum strapped to his back like armor, at Ysolde's vast dim shape coiled through the ruined far wall with her ember-heart flickering like a lamp running out of oil—and at last at Tamsin, who was trying and failing to get his feet under him.

"You're the one." Aldric's eyes were wet and terribly clear. "You sang it. You finished the prayer." He started down the steps, one hand gripping the rail, and I braced myself for gratitude, which is a heavy thing to have handed to you when your throat has just been half-emptied for the sake of the realm.

But Aldric didn't thank him. He knelt. The King of Verreholm went down on both knees on the ash-covered marble in front of a smuggler who couldn't speak above a whisper, and bowed his head.

"I heard everything," the king said. "All of it. Every voice it took. I was in the front row, do you understand? Six years, front row, unable to close my eyes or my ears or my mouth while it ate them one by one and smiled my smile and signed my name." His shoulders shook. "I know their names. All of them. I kept count. Four hundred and eleven. I will remember four hundred and eleven names until I die, and I want you to know that I intend for that to be soon, if the realm can spare me, because I don't—"

"Stop," Tamsin whispered.

The king stopped.

"You're not done yet." Tamsin's whisper was wrecked, gravel and thread, but it carried in the silence the way only true quiet lets small sounds carry. "Four hundred and eleven. That's not a count. That's a list of debts. And debts get paid by the living." He looked at the man kneeling in front of him, and something passed between them that I wasn't part of—survivor to survivor, hostage to hostage, the two people in that hall who knew best what Hollowmoot had been from the inside. "Get up. Get up and pay them."

Aldric got up.

And then, in a voice like a door dragged over stone, the King of Verreholm said words I genuinely never thought I'd hear: "Bring me the Edict of Ash."

* * *

He abolished it that same day, before the sun fully cleared the smoke-haze, because he refused to let the thing sleep even one night in the lawbooks.

We watched from the terrace above the palace square, all of us strays lined up like crows on a rail, too tired to be properly awed and too stubborn to leave. Below, the square was filling—not with soldiers, at first, but with ordinary people, drawn out of their cellars by the sudden absence of dread. Word travels fast in a city that has been holding its breath for six years. By the time Aldric appeared on the balcony, unarmored, grey-faced, leaning visibly on Brannic's arm, half of Verreholm was packed shoulder to shoulder beneath him, staring up, waiting to learn what kind of morning this was going to be.

He didn't make a speech, exactly. He tried, and his voice broke twice, and the third time he gave up on grandeur altogether and just told them the truth: that a hunger had worn his crown and fed on their neighbors, that the Edict was its feeding trough, that it was dead now, and that every name on the lists would be struck through in his own hand.

Then he said the thing that mattered. He said it plainly, no music in it at all, which somehow made it louder:

"There will be trials. Not pyres. No more burning. Anyone who served the Edict willingly—who hunted, who chained, who lit the fires—they'll answer for it in open court, in daylight, before judges named publicly so no one can buy them in the dark. And the gifted—" and here his voice did catch, and he let it, "—the gifted are subjects of this realm. Not stock. Not fuel. Subjects."

The square was silent for a long moment. Long enough that I felt my stomach drop, thinking: *they won't take it, six years of fear doesn't lift in a sentence, they'll want someone to burn anyway, crowds always want someone—*

And then an old woman near the fountain started to sing.

Just a snatch of some harvest song, cracked and off-key, the kind of thing every village in Thornfell knows. She sang two lines and stopped, looking frightened at herself, waiting for the wardens.

No wardens came.

Someone else picked it up. Then a dozen voices. Then a hundred. The song rolled across the square, ragged and imperfect and alive, and I stood on that terrace with ash in my hair and cried like a child, and I wasn't even embarrassed about it, because half the terrace was crying too, including, I'm fairly certain, the dragon.

Ysolde's ember had brightened. Just slightly. Just enough.

* * *

Afterward—the same endless day, evening now—we ended up in a guard barracks that Brannic commandeered with the sheer moral authority of a man who looked like he'd been awake for a month, which he had. Someone produced food. Actual hot food. I ate three bowls of barley stew without tasting any of them and then sat against the wall watching the room the way you watch a fire you're not sure is safe yet.

Tamsin sat apart, as usual. But not far apart. That was new.

He'd gotten hold of charcoal and paper somewhere—I suspected the twins, who had appointed themselves his quartermasters within an hour of the battle—and he was writing, slow and careful, his hand steadier than it had been that morning. When I drifted over, he tilted the page toward me without being asked.

*I keep reaching for it,* the note said. *Like a tongue finding the gap where a tooth was.*

"Do you miss it?" I asked. Then, because wry deflection is my native tongue even in the ruins of everything: "The big voice. Not the tooth."

He considered that longer than I expected. Charcoal scratching. Then:

*I spent nine years missing something I thought was gone forever. Turns out I had it the whole time and was afraid of it. Now I've used it the way it was meant to be used, and lost most of it honestly. It's a different grief. Cleaner. Like the difference between a lie and a scar.*

"That's very wise," I said, "and also completely depressing."

His mouth did the thing that was almost a smile, the one I'd spent a whole winter learning to read. More charcoal:

*What about you?*

"What about me?"

*You heard them. In the dungeons. The drained ones. You nearly answered. Ulyana—you went somewhere I couldn't follow and came back wrong.*

I looked away. He waited. Tamsin was very good at waiting; nine years of practice.

"They were still murmuring," I said finally. "Even after it died. The voices it drained—they don't just come back, do they? The people in those cages. Some of them are going to wake up with gaps where their gift used to be, or worse, with nothing at all. And I heard them calling before the fight, and part of me—" My hands had closed into fists without consulting me. "Part of me wanted to answer. Not to help them. To *be* them. To go into that murmur and hide there where nothing hungry could ever find me again."

Charcoal. Scratch, scratch.

*But you didn't.*

"No."

*Why not?*

And that was the question, wasn't it. I thought about lying, or making a joke, but Tamsin had sung lead today with his whole soul laid bare and it seemed obscene to answer honesty with anything cheaper.

"Because you were singing," I said. "And I realized halfway down into that dark warm nothing that if I went under, nobody would ever finish teaching me the harmony line. And I was furious about that. Furious enough to climb back out." I shrugged, aiming for casual and landing somewhere near honest. "You've ruined me, Tamsin Zhou. I have ambitions now. Choir ambitions. Do you understand what you've done?"

This time the almost-smile went all the way. He wrote:

*Good. Because I can't sing anymore. Somebody has to take the lead.*

"I know," I said. And then, quieter, because the room was noisy but that sentence deserved its own silence: "I know. I heard."

* * *

They brought us the news at dawn, because news always comes at dawn, riding in on whichever survivor is fastest and least injured.

Marrowgate had held. Barely. The wardens garrisoned there had received no orders since the throne fell—orders travel slowly when the throne in question spent the previous day being unmade—and for one terrible night it seemed the town might burn on momentum alone. But Brannic's defected brothers had marched through the night, and when they arrived flying the king's own banner with a written pardon sealed in fresh wax, the garrison stood down without a fight. Mostly. There was blood. There is always blood. But Marrowgate stood, and its gifted quarter—our quarter, the quarter where old Maren had died singing at the gate—still had walls around it.

Maren. I hadn't let myself think her name straight through in days. Now, in the grey light of an exhausted dawn, with the adrenaline long burnt out of me and the steward's stew sitting in my stomach like ballast, her name arrived and sat down in my chest and refused to leave.

She'd stayed at the gate so we could run. She'd sung the storm down onto the wardens' heads until her heart gave out mid-thunderclap. And now the realm was going to have trials instead of pyres, and she wasn't going to see a single one of them, because justice moves slower than sacrifice and always has.

Tamsin found me on the barracks roof. Of course he did; he had a smuggler's instinct for wherever people went to be privately miserable.

We stood together at the parapet and watched the sun come up over a city that smelled like smoke and rain. Down in the square, workers were already clearing rubble. Somewhere a hammer rang against stone—rebuilding starting before the mourning was even finished, which was either obscene or exactly right, and I hadn't decided.

He wrote, after a while:

*Trials won't feel like enough. For her. For any of them.*

"No," I agreed. "Nothing will. That's the trick of it, isn't it? Hollowmoot took four hundred and eleven voices and we killed it and the math still doesn't work. It never works. You don't get refunds for the dead."

Scratch, scratch.

*No. But you get what comes next. That's the payment. Not to them—to forward.*

I read that three times. *To forward.* His handwriting had gone a bit unsteady at the end, and I chose to believe it was the cold.

"So what does next look like?" I asked. "Practically. You're the one who's led a choir before. Well." I winced. "Led a choir before. Bad phrasing. You know what I mean."

He looked out at the sunrise for a long moment. Then he turned the page and wrote, in letters bigger than anything he'd written yet, as if he needed the charcoal to shout for him:

*Ysolde flies home to Veyra's mountain when she's strong enough. She asked—asked me—if anyone would come. To rebuild the chapel. To sing the mountain awake again. Not a god this time. Just a place where the song lives.* A pause. *She said I could bring the choir. She said—her word—strays make the best foundation stones. They're already used to bearing weight nobody thanks them for.*

My throat did something embarrassing. I covered it by leaning on the parapet and squinting heroically into the sun.

"When do we leave?" I said.

Charcoal. Scratch. And then he held up the page, and the last line of the longest thing he'd written all day was just two words, in a whisperer's handwriting, steady as bedrock:

*Tomorrow. Together.*

Below us, the hammer rang on. Above us, far to the north and east, past the smoke, I could almost imagine a single ember brightening on the horizon—a dragon's heart, a god at rest, and a mountain waiting to learn how to sing again without needing anyone to die for it first.

Almost. Not quite. But almost was more than we'd had yesterday, and tomorrow, we'd bring the choir.
