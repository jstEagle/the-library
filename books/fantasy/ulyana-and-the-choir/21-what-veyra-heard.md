## 21. What Veyra Heard

The mountain had opinions about my return, and most of them were expressed in weather.

Nine years ago I'd come down this slope with ash in my lungs and a dead god's silence in my ears, and I had sworn — loudly, dramatically, the way young men swear at funerals — that I would never climb it again. Mountains, it turns out, are patient. They know that everyone who says *never* is just scheduling an appointment they haven't admitted to yet.

So here I was, halfway up Veyra's Peak in the first thaw of spring, with a satchel of carved names against my back and a whisper for a voice, while sleet came at me sideways like it held a grudge. Which, given what had happened here, it might have.

"You could have waited for summer," Ulyana had said, back in Marrowgate, when I told her my plans.

"The mountain doesn't have a summer," I'd told her. "It has a brief truce with the sky, and then it goes back to war."

She'd laughed and kissed my cheek and said I was the gloomiest man she'd ever loved, and I hadn't argued, because what was I going to do — sing about it?

I climbed. My legs complained. The sleet turned to snow, then to that thin gray rain that can't decide what it wants to be when it grows up. And the mountain, beneath all of it, was green.

That was the thing nobody had warned me about. I'd remembered Veyra's Peak as a place of cinders. In my memory the chapel was a black tooth, the terraces were scorched bone, the whole mountain was a wound that had never been allowed to close. But three years of rain and wind and the stubborn arithmetic of seeds had done their work. Birch saplings stood in the ruins of the choir colonnade like a congregation that had arrived late and decided to stay. Moss upholstered the altar steps. Somewhere above me, in the fog, a bird was making a sound I can only describe as *insulted enthusiasm*, and it went on making it for the better part of an hour.

The world, I reflected, is very good at moving on. It's people who insist on carrying things.

I carried mine up to the chapel.

* * *

The chapel was half a ruin and half a rebuilding, because I'd paid for the rebuilding — quietly, through smuggler channels, the way I did everything for the first two years after the war. A roof over the nave. The east wall standing. The great window empty, because nobody had figured out how to replace glass that had been made by a god's breath, and honestly I'd told the masons to leave it. An empty window frame is its own kind of honesty. You can see the sky through it, and the sky doesn't pretend to be anything it isn't.

The prayer-stone stood where Veyra had fallen silent. That was the masons' doing, and mine. A single block of the mountain's own granite, hauled up and set on end where the altar had been, and cut into its face — by my hand, with a chisel, over one long winter — the prayer.

*Keep them. Keep them all. Every voice I have gathered, keep them—*

It stopped there, of course. That was where Veyra had stopped. Mid-word, mid-breath, mid-*thought*, nine years ago, when the thing we would later learn to call Hollowmoot had reached up out of the deep places and torn the god's voice out by the roots, and my choir — forty-one singers standing in this room with their mouths open on the next syllable — had died in the silence that followed.

I had spent nine years not finishing that prayer. First because I'd sworn my voice away. Then because I was afraid of it. Then, after the unbinding, because I literally couldn't — the rite had taken most of what was left of me, and what remained was a whisper that made shopkeepers lean in and children ask their mothers why that man talks like the wind.

But a prayer doesn't need volume. I'd learned that. A prayer needs finishing, and finishing is a matter of the heart, not the throat.

I set down the satchel at the base of the stone and stood there getting my breath back, because I was forty-one years old and the mountain had not gotten any shorter out of respect for my suffering.

"Hello," I said to the empty chapel. My voice came out like paper turning over. "You look terrible. You look wonderful. You look like a ruin that's been adopted by birch trees, which is more than most of us get."

No answer. I hadn't expected one. I'd stopped expecting answers a long time ago, and then, for one impossible hour in the throne hall of Verreholm, I'd gotten the biggest answer in the history of answers — Veyra's voice rising through dragonfire, vast and grieving and *finished at last* — and then it had gone again, spent, carried home, whatever you want to call it. The god had used the last of what Hollowmoot left of him to burn the parasite out of the world, and what remained afterward was not a god. It was not even a ghost.

It was, I had come to believe, a warmth.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. First, the names.

* * *

I'd carved them over the winter in Marrowgate, in the workshop behind the sanctuary hall, with Ulyana's twins — not twins, the harmonists, Sorrel and Sable, but everyone called them the twins and they'd stopped correcting people — coming in to argue about letterforms. Forty-one names. I knew them all the way you know a scar: Lira Voss, who sang descant and cheated at cards. Odo Merrin, bass, who could hold a low note so long the candles guttered. The Ansel twins, who were actual twins and found the other kind tiresome. Little Wren, sixteen, who'd joined the choir three weeks before the end because her mother said she had a voice like water and I'd agreed and taken her on, and I had carried that particular stone for nine years and would carry it for ninety more and knew it.

Forty-one names, each carved on a small stone, the way you'd mark a grave, because there had never been graves. There had never been anything to bury. Hollowmoot's silence had taken them whole, and the crown's wardens had taken the chapel, and by the time anyone was allowed back up the mountain there was nothing left to lay in the ground but ash and the god's abandoned name.

So I was going to bury the names. It wasn't much. It was what there was.

I dug at the base of the prayer-stone, in the thin mountain soil, and it went easier than I expected, because the ground here was *alive* — full of roots and worms and the busy dark machinery of the world getting on with itself. I thought, not for the first time, about Pip. *You aren't them,* he'd told me in the ashlands, in his first words in six years, blunt as a hammer. *They're done. You're not.* And then he'd gone back to not talking, having said his piece, because Pip believed in spending words like a miser spends gold and he'd apparently decided I was worth the outlay.

I laid the stones in the earth in the order they'd stood in the choir — Lira at the descant end, Odo at the bass, Wren in the middle where I could see her — and I covered them, and I pressed the ground down with my palms, and I said each name aloud in my paper whisper, all forty-one, and it took a while, and I did not hurry.

The bird above the chapel had stopped its insulted enthusiasm. The fog had come down close. And the chapel was very quiet, the way chapels are, the way this one had always been quiet even when it was full of song — a quiet that felt less like absence and more like *listening*.

I know how that sounds. I was a smuggler for nine years; I'm not a mystic. But there are two kinds of quiet, and anyone who has stood in a room where something loved has died knows the difference.

I finished the last name — Wren — and patted the earth flat, and sat back on my heels, and my knees made a sound like the bird had been making, and I laughed, which turned into something that wasn't laughing, and I let that happen too. Nine years of practice had taught me that grief is not a door you close. It's a room you furnish. You learn to live in it. And some days you sit down in the middle of the floor and let it be a room.

When I was done, I wiped my face with my sleeve, which was already wet from the fog, so it made no difference, and I stood, and I looked at the prayer-stone.

The prayer stopped where Veyra had stopped. *Keep them. Keep them all. Every voice I have gathered, keep them—*

And beneath it, in smaller letters, cut by the same hand in the same winter, I had added the ending. Not pretending it was Veyra's. Marking it as mine. A small line beneath, and then:

*—because I could not. So I am asking you to do what I cannot, which is everything. Amen.*

It had taken me a whole winter to find those words, and I still wasn't sure they were right, but prayers are like that. You don't write them. You just stop arguing with them.

"Here's the thing," I said to the stone, to the chapel, to the fog, to whatever was or wasn't listening. "I spent nine years thinking you'd abandoned us. That the silence was a door closing. And then I learned it was a *theft*, which is worse and also better, because a door closing is a choice and a theft is a crime, and I can hate a crime. I know how to hate a crime. I'm a smuggler."

The fog pressed at the empty window. The birches stood in their colonnade, patient as choristers.

"But here's what I actually came to say." My whisper caught. I stopped, breathed, started again. "I came to say thank you. Not for the silence — I'm not that far along, and I might never be, and I've decided that's allowed. Thank you for the forty-one years before it. Thank you for the music. Thank you for letting a gutter-born salt-runner with a decent voice stand in your choir and feel, for four years, like the world had a shape. You gave me that. Hollowmoot took the rest, and I took the rest after that, by hiding, by running, by choosing my own skin over other people's — and I'm not going to pretend that weight belongs to anyone but me. But the music was yours, and it was good, and it was real, and nothing that happened after can make it not have happened."

I put my hand on the stone. The granite was cold, because granite is granite.

"I don't need you to come back," I said. "I spent nine years needing that, and it nearly killed me, and then the needing itself nearly killed everyone I love, because need is how that thing on the throne got fat. So I'm done needing. I'm just — grateful. Grateful and tired and standing on your mountain with dirt under my nails. That's the whole prayer. That's the finish. Amen, you enormous silent bastard. Rest well."

* * *

And the shrine answered.

Not with a voice. I want to be clear about that, because I know what people want to hear, and Ulyana will ask me, and the pilgrims who've started coming up the mountain — a trickle, three or four a week, the gifted and the grieving — will ask me, and I will tell them the truth, which is this:

The stone grew warm under my hand.

Not hot. Not burning. Not the fire of the unbinding or the fire of Ysolde's ember on the horizon. Warm, the way a hearthstone is warm an hour after the fire's gone out. Warm the way a hand is warm when it's been holding yours. The warmth spread from under my palm down through the granite into the earth, into the buried names, into the thin soil and the roots and the worms and the whole busy dark machinery of the world, and it stayed.

I stood there with my hand on it for a long time. The fog thinned. The bird started up again, somewhere above, insulted and enthusiastic, and I started laughing, quietly, in my paper whisper, because it was so *small*, the answer. After everything — after a god devoured and a god avenged and a parasite-king unmade and a dragon's last fire spent — after all that, the miracle I got was a warm stone and the feeling, absolute and unarguable, of having been *heard*.

Not answered. Heard.

There's a difference, and the difference is everything. An answer would have been Veyra coming back, and I'd have spent the rest of my life being needed again, and need is the door Hollowmoot walks through. But this — this was a god at rest, the way a fire is at rest in its embers, the way a song is at rest when the last note has properly finished, which is not the same as silence, because a finished note is still ringing somewhere, in the walls, in the ear, in the shape it left in the air.

Veyra was not gone. Veyra was *done*. And there is all the difference in the world between a thing that has left you and a thing that has finished its work and is sleeping well.

"Rest well," I said again, and meant it, and took my hand off the stone, and the warmth stayed in my palm all the way down the mountain.

* * *

Ulyana was waiting at the foot of the trail, because of course she was. She'd sworn she'd give me the climb alone, and she had, and then she'd walked up from the village to meet me anyway, wrapped in her gray cloak, with snow in her dark hair and that look on her face that she thinks is neutral and has never once been neutral.

"Well?" she said.

"Well what?"

"Tamsin."

"It's a warm stone," I said. "It's a warm stone and forty-one buried names and a bird that sounds personally offended by the weather, and it's enough. It's more than enough." I looked back up at the mountain, at the fog and the birches and the empty window catching the last gray light. "He heard me. That's all I wanted. That's all I'll ever want now, I think. To be heard. Everything else was always someone else's hunger."

Ulyana took my arm, and we started down toward the village, where there was a fire and a meal and a bed, in that order, and she was quiet for a while, which for Ulyana is a heroic act of discipline.

Then: "The pilgrims are asking about the shrine. Whether it answers."

"It answers," I said.

"And what do I tell them it says?"

I thought about it. The warmth was still in my palm. Somewhere behind us, the bird was still going, and the mountain was letting it.

"Tell them it doesn't say anything," I said. "Tell them it listens. That's the whole religion now, as far as I'm concerned. We listen to each other. That's it. That's the liturgy."

She laughed, and squeezed my arm, and then she said the thing she'd clearly climbed down the mountain to say, because Ulyana has never once in her life delivered news in an order that made sense:

"Also, a rider came through from Verreholm this morning. Aldric's calling the first of the trials — the warden trials, Brannic's testimony, the whole rotten archive. They want singers at the opening. Witnesses. And—" she hesitated, and I knew what was coming, and I let her say it, because being heard starts with letting people finish. "—they want you to conduct the mourning hymn. The real one. Yours. The one your choir never got to finish."

I stopped on the trail. The snow was starting again, soft this time, almost polite. Somewhere in my chest, the old fear stirred and turned over and lay back down, because it was tired too, and because I had spent a whole winter carving forty-one names and a whole morning burying them, and fear is no match for a man with dirt under his nails.

"Tell them yes," I whispered.
