## 22. The Choir That Remains

The first thing we rebuilt in Marrowgate was not a wall or a roof or even the well, though gods know the well was a close third. The first thing we rebuilt was the gate.

Not because we needed a gate. Nobody was coming to burn us anymore — or nobody official, at least; smugglers still came through the tunnels, and Brannic still pretended to charge them toll out of some deep warden instinct that would probably outlive us all. We rebuilt the gate because Maren Storm-voice's ashes were in it. Because she had held that scorched arch with nothing but her throat and her fury while the rest of us ran, and when Tamsin said — whispered, rather, his voice a frayed thread of itself now — "we start here," none of us argued.

You don't argue with a man who has dirt under his nails and forty-one names behind him. You hand him a stone.

That was two years ago this autumn, if you count by the festival we throw, which I do, because counting by years felt too much like something a tax collector would do. Two autumns since the unbinding, since Hollowmoot came apart mid-scream inside dragonfire, since Aldric woke on his own throne an old man of fifty-three who looked ninety and wept for six hours without stopping. Two autumns since the Edict of Ash became paper, and then became kindling, which is the only good use I've ever seen made of royal decrees.

I sing lead now.

I want to tell you that I earned it, but the truth is more embarrassing: Tamsin gave it to me the way he gives away everything, quietly and before anyone thinks to ask. He can't carry a line anymore. The parasite took its tithe from him on its way out the door — grabbed his voice like a thief snatching the silverware — and what's left is a whisper, soft as snowfall, that doctors and hedge-witches and one very opinionated dragon all agree will never be more. He pretends this doesn't grieve him. He is terrible at pretending. But he found something better than the voice he lost, and I watch him use it every single morning.

He conducts with his hands.

You have never seen anything like it, so let me describe it properly. He stands on the low stone dais in our meeting-hall — we call it the choir hall, though it used to be a granary and before that, according to Pip, who knows everyone's business, a smugglers' dice den — and he says nothing at all. He just lifts his hands, and forty voices rise. His fingers curl to bring the harmony in. A flick of the wrist pulls the twins back before they run ahead of the tempo, which they always do, because they're twins and running ahead is their entire personality. When someone falters — and someone always falters, because half our singers learned their gift in hiding and flinch at their own volume — Tamsin doesn't correct them. He just looks at them. And there's something in how he looks, something patient and unhurried and absolutely certain you'll find the note, that makes finding the note the only possible outcome.

Brannic calls it sorcery. Brannic calls everything sorcery, including bread, so we ignore him.

* * *

The town has grown the way gardens grow — messily, unevenly, full of things nobody planted on purpose. There are maybe three hundred of us now. Gifted, mostly, and their families, and a scattering of folk who simply wanted to live somewhere the word *gifted* wasn't a death sentence or a tax bracket. We have a school where children learn scales before letters, on the grounds that scales are harder and more useful. We have a shrine to Veyra on the hill, small and warm, tended by no one and everyone. We have a rule, carved over the gate in Maren's memory: *No voice shall be taken here.* It applies to arguments, weddings, and one memorable dispute about fence lines that Pip refereed by drumming both parties into exhausted silence.

Pip speaks now. Not much — he hoards words like a dragon hoards gold, spending them only when the purchase matters — but he speaks. Last month he stood up in front of the whole school and said, "My name is Pip Zhou." He took Tamsin's surname two winters back, formally, in front of witnesses, and Tamsin cried about it for a week and denied every tear individually. The boy drums the rhythm section of every hymn we sing. When Tamsin raises his left hand flat — hold — Pip's sticks stop midair like trained birds. I have seen grown wardens weep watching it. Well. Former wardens. Brannic insists he's retired, then trains our town guard four mornings a week, then retires again over dinner.

The twins, Sera and Solenne, teach the harmony classes and finish each other's insults. The old storm-cellar women who survived Marrowgate's burning came out of the hills last spring and now teach weather-singing to anyone brave enough to try, which means our harvest festivals have a documented seventy percent chance of dramatic cloud effects. Nobody complains. Dramatic clouds are free entertainment, and after what this town paid for its freedom, we've earned spectacle.

And on clear evenings, when the light goes long and amber over the ruined-and-mending rooftops, we gather in the choir hall and sing.

* * *

I should tell you about Ysolde, because everyone always asks about Ysolde, and because she'd eat me if I didn't — not literally, probably, but she keeps the ambiguity alive on purpose, the way old dragons keep everything alive: out of spite and tradition.

She stayed in Thornfell at first, coiled around the mountain where Veyra's chapel had been, sulking in the dignified way that only creatures older than kingdoms can manage. Her ember was guttering when we found her — dim, blue at the edges, a candle in a draft. Ulyana's song woke one flame out of her, one, and that one flame burned a god. But fire spends, and hers had been spent for centuries, chained by bargain and grief to a crown that never deserved it.

Here is what I did not expect: she got better.

Tamsin swears it's the singing. Every season, when the roads open, our choir makes pilgrimage to Thornfell — three days' walk, the little ones riding supply sleds and complaining magnificently — and we stand in the chapel ruins, which Tamsin rebuilt stone by stone with his own hands, whispering instructions and pointing a lot, and we sing the finished prayer. Veyra's prayer. The one that ended mid-word nine years ago with a dead choir and a broken man, and that we finished in a burning throne hall with drained captives and a dying dragon and a conductor whose voice gave out on the final line.

When we sing it, Ysolde's ember brightens.

It's not dramatic, exactly. It's not the inferno of legend. But the blue fades from her fire and the gold comes back, degree by degree, year by year, the way a fever breaks slowly and then all at once. Last winter, Brannic — who had climbed the mountain ostensibly to inspect the trail and actually because he missed her, though he'd sooner swallow his badge again than admit it — swore he saw her flame reach the height of a man. She called him a sentimental lizard-worshipper. They're very fond of each other.

This morning, standing on Marrowgate's new wall with a cup of cider going cold in my hands, I saw the horizon over Thornfell glow faintly gold at dawn. Not sunrise. Sunrise comes from the east, and Thornfell is north. Just a soft, steady warmth on the edge of the sky, like a hearth seen from far down a long road.

Ysolde's ember, brightening. Still brightening.

Tamsin stood next to me, following my gaze. He didn't say anything — he rarely says anything, and when he does it costs him, and he rations words like a miser — but he smiled the way he smiles, which is to say reluctantly, as if caught at it, and touched two fingers briefly to his heart and then pointed north. *Her,* that gesture said. *Good.*

Then he turned toward the choir hall, because it was nearly time for morning practice, and morning practice waits for no dragon, no god, and certainly no epilogue.

* * *

Here is the thing I understand now, that I did not understand half-frozen in a smuggler's cave with a god's name in my mouth and ice in my blood: the song was never mine to keep. None of ours were. Voices aren't possessions. They're seeds. You plant them in other throats or you bury them, and buried things either rot or wait, and I spent nine years learning the difference between those two options from a man who chose rot and then, impossibly, chose waiting instead.

Tamsin buried his voice for nine years. Then a girl hummed in the snow, and it waited, and it grew, and it died gloriously killing a god, and what sprouted in its place was this: a hundred hands raised in a granary-turned-hall, teaching children who will never know what an Edict was. This: a mute boy with a surname and a pair of sticks. This: a retired warden who bakes terrible bread and inspects trails he doesn't need to inspect. This: a golden glow on the northern horizon that gets a little brighter every year, like a debt being repaid at interest.

We rehearse the great hymn every morning. Not because we must. Because it's ours now — finished, owned, sung by forty throats instead of stolen by one hunger. Some mornings Tamsin's hands shake when he lifts them. Some mornings mine do too, when I take the lead line and feel the old fear stir in my chest, the cave-dark fear, the *what if it hears me* fear.

But it doesn't hear. It's gone. What listens now is only us — and the hilltop shrine, warm to the touch, and whatever rests beneath it, resting at last.

This morning, as I took my place before the choir and Tamsin raised his hands and the room went quiet as held breath, Pip leaned over from his drum and whispered — one of his rare, rationed words, spent like gold:

"Ready?"

I looked at our conductor, standing silent in the morning light with forty voices waiting on his fingers, at the scarred gate with its carved law, at the north window and the gold glow beyond it.

"Always," I said.

Tamsin's hands began to rise.
