## 1. Charms for Copper

The rain began at dawn, as it always did in Veymark, and Silas Fairweather was grateful for it.

Not because he liked the wet — ten years in this city had cured him of any romance about damp — but because rain meant the harbor fog stayed thick, and thick fog meant the harbor guard's morning patrol moved slow and half-blind, which meant Silas could get his stall set up before anyone with a badge or a lantern came asking what a lean, gray-templed man was doing arranging knotted cord on a cloth like they were jewels.

They weren't jewels. That was rather the point.

He laid out his wares with the care of a man setting a table for royalty who would never arrive: luck-knots braided from tarred hemp, three coppers apiece; fever-charms that were, strictly speaking, dried willow bark and mint sewn into red felt; salt-bags against nightmares, which worked because salt did, actually, work against nightmares, if you believed it did, and belief was the only coin Silas had never run short of.

And underneath the folded blanket at the back of the stall — beneath the spare felt, beneath the false bottom Bren's carpenter had made him years ago — there were eleven charms that worked for reasons no one in Veymark was permitted to say out loud anymore.

Magic was outlawed here. Had been since before Silas came, since the old king put down the storm-line and declared every gift thereafter to be treason wearing a friendly face. The edicts said *registered or ruined*, and registration led to ruined soon enough. So Veymark's gifted had learned to be small. To sell luck instead of making it. To charge copper for things that cost them nothing but the careful hiding of what they were.

Silas tied off the last knot, blew on it out of habit — an old habit, an old blessing, *fly straight and land soft* — and told himself, as he told himself every morning, that the knot was only a knot now. Just string. Just commerce.

The knot disagreed, faintly, warmly, the way a cat disagrees by purring.

"Morning, Fairweather," called Old Sefa from the pie stall two down, already elbow-deep in eel pastry. "You look like something the tide refused."

"Good morning to you too, Sefa."

"It's not a good morning. It's a Tuesday." She said Tuesdays the way other people said *plague*. "Tuesdays are for customs men and bad pies. I'm making bad pies on purpose today. Defense."

"Against customs men?"

"Against everything. Buy one later and find out."

Silas smiled despite himself, arranged his little brass bell where customers could ring it without touching anything that mattered, and settled onto his stool to begin the long daily performance of being nobody in particular.

* * *

The Nightmarket woke around him the way Veymark did everything — reluctantly, complaining, but thoroughly. Lanterns bloomed along the awnings, orange and greasy in the drizzle. Fishwives argued prices with the solemnity of diplomats. Somewhere a fiddle started up and was immediately told to stop, and started up again anyway three stalls away, because in the Nightmarket even music was contraband by degrees.

His first customer came at mid-morning: a cooper's widow named Annet, who bought a fever-charm every week whether anyone in her house had a fever or not.

"For your granddaughter?" Silas asked, wrapping it in paper.

"The little one has a cough." Annet lowered her voice. "A real one. In the chest."

Silas held the packet a moment longer than paper required. Under the counter, hidden by the fold of his sleeve, his thumb pressed the felt and found the small warm hum he'd stitched into it — nothing dramatic, nothing any witch-hunter could name, just a nudge, a suggestion, a whisper to the body that it might consider healing itself a little faster than nature intended.

Willow bark and mint, he'd have sworn to Crane's own face. Willow bark and mint and a mother's stubbornness.

"She'll be better by Sunday," he said. "Make her drink it steeped, not chewed. And keep her feet dry."

"You're a fraud, Fairweather," Annet said fondly, paying him four coppers for a three-copper charm.

"Completely. Everyone knows it."

That was how it went all day. A fisherman wanting a luck-knot for the herring season — Silas gave him the one with the double weave, the one that would hold a little steadier than string had any right to, and charged him extra for the "premium braid." Two apprentice girls splitting a nightmare-salt bag between them because neither could afford a whole one, giggling, shoving each other. A dockhand with a burn who didn't want a charm at all but wanted to stand near the stall a while, near whatever it was about Silas that made hurts feel further away, and Silas let him stand, and charged him nothing, and pretended not to notice when the man left flexing his bandaged hand like it had surprised him.

This was the fragile economy of hidden magic: you sold people the story, and you gave them the truth for free, and you prayed the difference never got anyone killed.

By late afternoon the drizzle had committed fully to rain. Business slowed. Silas counted his take — a modest hill of coppers, two silver bits, and one button someone had paid him with either by accident or as commentary — and set aside the portion he owed.

Then he pulled his hood up and went to see about the harbor guard.

* * *

Corporal Denny Hask walked his route along the quay with the enthusiasm of a man following a mule he didn't trust, and indeed Denny did not trust his mule, nor his boots, nor the sea, nor most of his fellow guardsmen. What Denny trusted was routine, and Silas Fairweather had been part of his routine for six years.

"Evening, Corporal."

"Fairweather." Denny didn't look at him. Looking at things was against his policy. "Weather's foul."

"It usually is."

"Fouler than usual."

"That'll be the season."

They walked together, unhurried, past the fish-crates and the sleeping gulls and the customs shed with its lamp burning official and yellow. At the corner where the route bent away from the market and toward the guardhouse, Silas slipped a small purse into the space between them, into the fold of Denny's oilskin, smooth as passing salt.

"Contribution to the watchmen's comfort fund," Silas said.

"There's no such fund."

"No. There isn't."

Denny weighed it through the cloth with one practiced hand. "It's light this month."

"Business is light this month."

"Edict talk's got people nervous. Nervous people don't buy knots." Denny finally glanced at him, and for a moment the dull routine in his face cracked and something almost human looked out — something tired and sorry. "There's criers coming tomorrow, they say. From the palace. Official word." He paused. "You'd do well to hear it early, is all I'm saying. And to be somewhere else while you do."

"Denny—"

"I say nothing to anyone. That's the fund. But I've got daughters, Fairweather, and my eldest buys her charms from you, and I'd rather she bought her charms from a live man." He tugged his collar up against the rain. "Hear it early. Be somewhere else."

He trudged off into the gray, and Silas stood a moment on the emptying quay with the rain ticking on his hood, feeling the words settle over him like weather. Which was foolish. There was always edict talk. There had been edict talk every year for ten years — new registrations, new oaths, new lists — and every year the talk swelled and then broke against the simple fact that Veymark ran on small magics the way it ran on herring, and no city willingly guts its own belly.

Still. His hands were cold, and not entirely from the rain.

He bought a loaf from the baker on Tanner's Row — the good loaf, the one with the dark crust, though he complained about the price with theatrical vigor until the baker's wife threatened him with a rolling pin, which was their version of friendship — and tucked it inside his coat, against his chest, where the warmth would keep.

Because there was one more customer. The one who never paid.

* * *

She was waiting in the alcove behind the chandlery, sitting on an upturned crate with her knees drawn up, trying to look as though she hadn't been waiting, and failing at it with the whole desperate transparency of nine years old.

"Took you long enough," said Ulyana.

"I run a business, not a charity kitchen."

"You're late."

"I'm punctual. The world is early." Silas sat down on the opposite crate, joints announcing their opinions, and produced the loaf. She took it the way she took everything — fast, sharp-eyed, checking first that his hands were empty of anything else, checking the street, checking the shadows, a whole audit conducted in the space between heartbeats. Then she tore off the heel and ate it like a person who remembered being hungry enough to eat bread whole.

Silas watched the rain drip off the chandlery's eave between them and asked, in the tone of a man discussing freight rates, "Where are you sleeping these days?"

"Places."

"Warm places?"

"Warm-ish."

"Dry places?"

"Define dry."

"Ulyana."

"Define *Ulyana*," she said, mouth full, eyes daring him, and Silas had to turn a laugh into a cough so it wouldn't count against him. Ten years of exile had taught him to be careful with attachments. Attachments got people questioned. Attachments got *him* questioned. And yet here he was, every evening, feeding a stray child whose entire strategy for survival was to be more trouble than she was worth to catch.

There was something else about her, too — something he'd noticed years ago and told himself firmly not to notice. On stormy nights, when she slept under his stair (she believed he didn't know; he had never had the heart to tell her the stair was the warmest spot in the building and he'd chosen his rooms because of it), the air around her prickled. Static in her hair. Sparks on the iron latch. Once, waking from a nightmare, she'd made the candle flames gutter in a room with no draft, and Silas had lain awake until dawn doing arithmetic he didn't like: the age of her, the year of the purges, the storm-line stamped out root and branch — supposedly.

Root and branch. Except roots are patient things.

"You're staring," Ulyana accused.

"I'm thinking about rent you owe me."

"For what? You give me bread. Bread isn't rent."

"It accumulates."

"In what currency?"

"Gratitude."

She made a face like he'd offered her a dead rat. "I don't deal in that. Bad investment. Nobody honors it." But she shifted on her crate — closer, by exactly the width a proud girl could pretend was accidental — and shoulder-checked him lightly, once, which from Ulyana was a sonnet.

Silas looked out at the rain coming down silver through the lantern-light, at the market packing itself away stall by stall, at the city he'd spent ten years learning to survive in and had somehow, without meaning to, learned to love. He thought about Denny's warning. Criers tomorrow. Official word. Be somewhere else.

Tomorrow, probably, it would be nothing. Another oath, another list, another year of knots and copper and stolen evenings behind the chandlery.

Probably.

"Eat the rest," he said, standing, knees cracking. "And Ulyana — if tomorrow you hear bells, a lot of bells, you come find me. Not the Remnant folk, not the smugglers, not whoever's taken you in this week. Me. Straight to me."

"Why? What's tomorrow?"

"Nothing, likely. Probably nothing." He pulled his hood up. "But if it's not nothing, I want you where I can see you."

She squinted up at him, reading his face with those quick gray eyes that missed nothing and forgave less, and whatever she saw there made her stop joking.

"All right," she said quietly. "Straight to you."

He walked home through the wet streets with the taste of dread like copper at the back of his throat, telling himself it was only weather.

Across the rooftops of Tanner's Row, unseen, the palace riders were already entering the city gates, carrying proclamations sealed in black wax.
