## 2. The Proclamation

Silas Fairweather had three rules for surviving Veymark, and he recited them every morning while knotting the day's stock of luck-charms: *Never sell a charm that works too well. Never look a guard in the eye longer than politeness requires. Never, under any circumstances, hum while braiding storm-silk.*

The humming rule was new. It was also the only one he'd ever broken, and he'd broken it precisely once, eleven years ago, on a dock in Greyford, with lightning still cooling in his fingertips and half a fleet burning behind him. A harbor-witch had heard him. She'd said nothing. She'd only looked at him the way you look at a man walking on thin ice, and Silas had learned that day that his magic had a voice, and that voice was music, and music carried.

So: no humming. He knotted cord instead, fingers moving through patterns so worn they'd gone smooth as river stones — over, under, tuck, whisper, seal. The whisper was not a spell. It was habit. It was the shape of a word he refused to say, pressed flat until it was just breath.

"Morning, Master Fairweather."

Silas looked up from his stall and arranged his face into the expression that paid his rent: mildly put-upon, fundamentally honest, dangerous to nobody.

"Mistress Odell." He nodded at the fishmonger, who was already elbow-deep in her morning argument with a crate of eels. "They giving you trouble again?"

"They're eels. Trouble is their entire profession." She slapped one back into the barrel and leaned across his table, lowering her voice out of pure theater, since everyone within ten feet could hear her perfectly. "I need another luck-knot. The big kind. My Tam's got the trials for the harbor apprenticeship Thursday."

"The big kind costs double."

"The big kind *always* costs double. That's how I know you're honest. Cheap luck's a swindle; expensive luck might be real." She counted coppers onto the boards with the grim satisfaction of a woman paying a ransom she considered fair.

Silas took the coins, slid a knot across the table, and did not tell her that the knot contained nothing but cord, patience, and a small prayer-shaped thought he offered up with every one he made — not to any god in particular, more to the general concept of things going gently. He didn't know if the prayers worked either. But Odell's son would stand a little taller wearing it, and standing taller won trials as often as anything did.

That was the whole secret economy of hidden magic in Veymark: after the Puricts came through forty years ago and the crown outlawed the gift outright, what survived wasn't power. It was comfort. Luck-knots and fever-charms, salt-blessings for boats and sweetening-spells for sour milk — all of it technically nothing, all of it priced like something, because people needed to believe their small lives were being tended by someone. Silas sold them the tending. He kept the actual magic coiled up small and dark inside himself, fed on almost nothing, dreaming of storms.

Most days, that was enough.

* * *

By midmorning the Nightmarket had filled to its usual simmer. The Nightmarket earned its name honestly — it never fully closed, just dimmed at dawn like an old man dozing in a chair — but mornings were when it woke properly: the spice-row breathing cinnamon and pepper into the fog off the harbor, the tinker stalls ringing, the pie-cart lady, Bess, shouting her eternal and entirely sincere claim that her pies contained *no rat whatsoever*.

"No rat whatsoever!" she bellowed at a skeptical sailor. "You insult me, sir. Rat costs money."

Silas grinned down at his cords. Ten years in this market. He knew which awnings leaked, which guards took their bribes in ale rather than coin, which corners flooded first when the tide came up wrong. He knew the sound the cobblestones made before rain — a particular hollowness, like a drum remembering — and this morning they were singing it softly, a gray drizzle gathering somewhere out over the water and thinking about committing.

He was reaching for his kettle when the bells started.

Not the tide-bells. Not the fire-bells. The deep ones, the palace bells, the ones that only rang for births, deaths, and edicts, and Veymark hadn't heard them ring in nine years.

The market went quiet in stages, like a wave pulling back. First the haggling stopped. Then the hammering. Then even the gulls seemed to take notice, wheeling up off the fish-rows in a single gray sheet.

Down the Spindleway, between the chandler's and the shuttered playhouse, a crier climbed the old proclamation steps — a boy of maybe sixteen in palace livery two sizes too big, flanked by two soldiers who did not look bored anymore. They looked like men who had rehearsed this.

The boy unrolled a scroll. His voice cracked only slightly on the first word.

*"By decree of His Majesty, King Aldous the Second, and by authority of the Office of Inquisition—"*

Silas's hands went still around a half-finished knot.

*"—be it known to all subjects of the Crown that the scourge of unsanctioned sorcery has again taken root among the faithful. Let it therefore be proclaimed, throughout the realm and its cities: that all persons possessed of the forbidden gift shall present themselves for registration at their district temple within thirty days' time. Registered practitioners shall be marked, licensed, and bound to lawful service."*

A pause. The soldier beside the boy cleared his throat and prompted him with a glance.

*"And be it further proclaimed,"* the boy read, quieter now, *"that any gifted person failing to register within said term, or found concealing the gift or abetting those who conceal it, shall be taken as an enemy of the crown."* Another prompt. Another swallow. *"Harboring is as damning as casting. There is no middle mercy. Registration — or the pyre."*

The last word fell into the market and just lay there.

Nobody screamed. Nobody ran. That was the horror of Veymark, Silas thought distantly; it was a city that had practiced being afraid for so long it had gotten good at it, quiet and orderly about it, folding fear away like laundry. Stalls stayed open. Hands kept working. Eyes simply moved — sideways, downward, everywhere except toward other eyes — and in that sudden network of avoided glances, everyone became both suspect and informer.

The criers moved on to the next square. The bells kept tolling, patient as tide.

"Well," said Bess, at last, to nobody, in a voice pitched carefully light. "No rat whatsoever in my pies, and now this. It's a strange morning."

Nobody laughed. She laughed anyway, alone, and the laugh died fast, and that was worse than silence.

* * *

Silas told himself, as the morning crawled on, that it meant nothing. Edicts came and went. There'd been a registration push seven years ago — a bad season, sure, old Tansy the hedge-healer had been taken, but the paperwork had drowned in its own ink and half the registered names had turned out to belong to dead men and one very confused goat. The crown liked proclamations the way Bess liked shouting: it was noise that felt like action.

But the soldiers weren't bored this time. That was the difference. Seven years ago they'd leaned on their pikes and picked their teeth. Today they stood like fence posts, and there was a third man now, a clerk, actually writing names in a ledger as shopkeepers drifted past the proclamation steps — some curious, a few already queuing, one or two with the eager faces of people who had been waiting years for permission to point.

Silas watched the queue form and felt the dread settle over the market the way weather settles — not arriving, exactly, but assembling itself out of pressure he'd been ignoring for weeks. The king's health failing. The witch-trials restarted in the southern provinces last spring. Bren's smugglers grumbling that crown patrols had doubled on the estuary. All of it separate weather-signs, and here was the storm front finally making landfall.

*Thirty days,* he thought. *Thirty days, then harboring is as damning as casting.*

His mind went where it always went, unbidden — to a sharp-eyed orphan who slept most nights in the loft above the ropewalk, who had eaten his bread for six years, whose name existed in no ledger anywhere because Silas had made certain of it. Ulyana. Nine years old when she'd appeared on his step like something the sea rejected. Fifteen now, all elbows and suspicion and a hunger for stories she pretended not to have.

*Harboring.* The word fit him like a tailored coat.

"Master Fairweather." Old Odell again, back at his stall, and her face had changed since morning. The theater was gone. "You've heard."

"I've heard."

"You'll register, then?" Her eyes flicked up, quick and searching, and Silas understood suddenly that the question was not about him. It was about her. About whether buying charms for a decade made her a fool, an accomplice, or neither.

"I sell knots and blessings," Silas said, mild as milk. "Cord and kind words. There's nothing in that to register."

"No," Odell agreed. "Nothing at all." And then, lower, with the terrible casualness of a woman testing ice: "Though they say old Wrenlow's been seen lighting his pipe without a match."

Silas's stomach dropped, slow and cold.

Wrenlow. The lamplighter. Seventy if he was a day, arthritic hands, a man who'd spent forty years carrying flame up ladders until his shoulders gave out — and yes, Silas knew, everyone on this row knew, that Wrenlow had a trickle of the gift in him, no more than a candle's worth, enough to warm a wick with a touch. Enough to keep working after his arms failed. Half the street pretended not to know because Wrenlow lit the lamps beautifully and complained about the weather like a professional.

"He does," Silas said carefully, "have very steady hands."

"Mm." Odell looked at the ledger-man by the proclamation steps. Looked back. And then Silas watched, with a sensation like watching a cart go over slowly, as she straightened her shawl, raised her voice just enough, and said, clearly, for anyone's ears:

"Well. Someone ought to tell the authorities, shouldn't they? If it's true. Rules are rules, and I've a license to think of, and a son." She was already turning, already moving toward the steps with the purposeful stride of the righteous. "It's a kindness, really. Better he registers now than burns later. I'm doing him a kindness."

She believed it. That was the worst part. Silas could see it in her back, in the set of her shoulders — she had already built the story where denunciation was mercy, where the queue was salvation, where informing was the same as caring. People were wonderful at that. People could build cathedrals out of that.

He watched Odell reach the clerk, watched her lean in, watch the clerk's pen start scratching. Watched, a few minutes later, two soldiers detach themselves and walk unhurriedly up Lamp Row toward a stooped old man on a ladder, and Silas stood behind his little table of harmless knots and did nothing, because there was nothing to do, because intervening was death and non-intervention was only shame, and shame, at least, kept a man alive.

Wrenlow saw them coming. From the top of his ladder, the old lamplighter looked down at the soldiers, and then — Silas would remember this forever — he laughed. A short, wheezing, genuinely amused laugh, as though the universe had told him a decent joke at last. He touched the lamp beside him. It bloomed warm and gold without any spark struck. And he climbed down the ladder on his own two feet and held out his wrists like a gentleman offering to dance.

The soldiers walked him past the market. Past the stalls. Past Bess, who had suddenly developed a passionate interest in the interior of her pie oven. Past Silas, whose eyes Wrenlow found and held for one long second — and in that second the old man winked.

*I'm not worth your storm, boy,* the wink said. *Keep it sheathed.*

Then the corner took him, and the lamps along Lamp Row burned unattended, and nobody relit the one at the far end for three days.

* * *

By late afternoon, the market had thinned to ghosts. The drizzle had arrived at last, fine and needling, beading on the awnings. Silas packed his cords with hands that wanted to shake and refused to. Around him, vendors closed early, spoke in murmurs, counted their children. Somewhere down the row a family was loading a handcart at speed, and no one remarked on it, because remarking was pointing and pointing was the day's new currency.

Thirty days, Silas thought again. Or less. Proclamations were the polite version.

He should run. The thought arrived with the clarity of cold water. Take what coin he had, go to Bren tonight, buy passage on whatever leaky hull the Saltwrights had leaving the estuary, and be a hundred miles away before the temples opened their registration books. It was what a sensible man did. It was what he'd have counseled anyone else to do, in fact, with great warmth and sincerity, while privately noting that sensible men left and the ones who stayed were the reason the word *harboring* existed.

And Ulyana would come back from wherever she roamed tomorrow, or the day after, and find an empty stall and an empty loft and no explanation, because he couldn't leave one, because explanations were evidence.

Silas stood in the rain behind his shuttered stall and let the arithmetic finish itself, ugly and inevitable. He could not leave. Which meant he needed papers. Which meant Bren. Which meant the Saltwrights, and the Saltwrights' prices, and everything that came after.

Thunder muttered somewhere out past the breakwater — distant, idle, a dog dreaming. Silas felt the thing coiled in his chest turn over in its sleep and press against the walls he'd built around it, testing them the way it did on nights like this, and he breathed slow until it settled.

"Not yet," he told it, under the rain, under the bells, under the sound of a city quietly teaching itself new fears. "Whatever's coming — not yet."

Behind him, unnoticed until now, a small figure in an oversized coat was crouched under the awning's drip-line, watching him with sharp eyes that had counted the soldiers, the ledger, the emptying stalls, and drawn conclusions Silas didn't want to guess at.

"You're still here," Ulyana said. It wasn't relief in her voice. It was accusation.

"For now," Silas said.

She frowned at him — at the shuttered stall, the empty row, the whole rearranged world — and Silas felt the next chapter of his life open beneath him like floodwater.

"We need to talk," he said. "Somewhere dry. Somewhere *nobody* goes."
