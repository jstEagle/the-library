## 3. What Ulyana Carries

The cellar under the tanner's row flooded every time it rained, which in Veymark was most of the time, and so Ulyana had decided years ago that if she was going to have a secret place, it might as well be a secret place with a view of her own reflection.

She sat on the upturned crate that Silas had hauled down here two winters back — "so you'd stop sitting on wet bricks and then complaining to me about your wet trousers" — and watched the water shiver. It always shivered when she was nervous. That should have been a clue. She had been very good at not taking clues.

Silas came down the ladder with their coat collar up and their expression already halfway to annoyed, which for Silas was practically a warm greeting.

"You sent a boy," they said, wringing out one sleeve. "A boy of perhaps eight years, who told me — and I quote — *'the charm lady says come quick, she's got something drowning.'* Ulyana, I thought you were drowning. I ran."

"I didn't drown."

"I can see that. I can also see that I owe Tam three coppers for running, because he demanded payment before he'd tell me where you were. You've taught him extortion. He's eight."

"He's enterprising." Ulyana pulled her knees up on the crate. The joke had been easy to carry all the way from the Nightmarket; it was getting heavy now that she'd arrived. "Sit down, Silas."

Silas looked at her. They had a way of looking at people like they were reading small print, and Ulyana had spent four years learning what that look meant: *I will help you, but I will pretend this is an inconvenience.* She had never once seen them actually refuse.

They sat on the second crate, the wobbly one, and immediately regretted it, which gave her another few breaths of delay while they shifted their weight and muttered about rotting wood and people who summoned them through child intermediaries.

The rain came harder overhead. Through the grate at street level, boots splashed past, and someone swore cheerfully about the weather, and the lamplight swung green-gold across the black water. Down here it smelled of stone and old salt and, faintly, of the tannery vats upstream, which nobody ever managed to get used to.

"You're frightened," Silas said.

"I'm not."

"You've chewed the string off your left cuff. You only do that when you're frightened or hungry, and you ate half my lunch not two hours ago, so."

Ulyana looked down at her cuff. Frayed to threads. Traitor cuff.

"I need to show you something," she said, "and then you're probably going to leave the city forever, and I want you to know before you do that I'm sorry, and also that you taught me the trick with the lockpicks, so some of this is legally your fault."

Something flickered across Silas's face — the particular stillness they got when a joke landed on something tender. "Try me," they said quietly.

So she stood up. Her legs felt like borrowed legs, someone else's, badly fitted. The cellar water lay flat and dark around the crates, deep enough by now that it lapped the bottom step, and she could see the lamp flame doubled in it, wavering.

"When I was little," she said, "before you found me, there was a woman who kept me for a while. Not kindly. She said I was cursed, because — " Her throat did a thing. She swallowed past it. "Because of this."

"Don't," Silas said softly.

But she was already singing.

It was the song from before — the half-remembered one, the one the woman used to slap her for humming, a lullaby in a language she didn't know, all falling vowels and long held notes like wind finding a gap under a door. She sang it low, the way she'd practiced alone in alleys and on rooftops where no one listened, and her voice shook, and that was fine, because it had never once mattered how well she sang.

It mattered that she sang at all.

The water moved first. A ring of pale light spread out from nowhere — from everywhere — skimming across the flooded floor like someone had dropped a moon into it. Then the air above the water began to tick, small blue sparks popping along the surface, crawling up the brickwork, threading through the rungs of the ladder. Her hair lifted off her neck. Static crawled along her arms in slow, bright filigree, and the lamp guttered, and somewhere above them thunder answered — a single low roll, though the storm overhead had been all grumble and no teeth until now.

She stopped singing. The light died reluctantly, sparks winking out one by one, and the last of them hovered near Silas's face like a curious moth before going dark.

The silence afterward rang.

Ulyana realized she was gripping the edge of the crate hard enough to hurt her fingers. She made herself let go.

"That's what I am," she said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved. "That's what I've been hiding since I was six years old. When I sing, it answers. And I don't know what *it* is, Silas, except that it's getting bigger every year, and last month I sang in the loft over the chandlery and it blew out every candle on the street and the watch came looking, and I heard them say the word *gifted*, and I heard them say the other word too." *Burned.* Neither of them said it now. It hung in the dripping dark anyway. "And you're the only person in this whole city who knows anything about magic that doesn't come out of a lie, so either you tell me what I'm supposed to do, or you tell me to run, and either way I wanted — "

She stopped, because Silas had gone white.

Not shocked-white, exactly. Something older than shock. They were staring at the spot where the sparks had been as though the air still held writing only they could read, and their hands — those clever, charm-knotting, bread-sneaking hands — had curled into fists in their lap.

"Sing it again," Silas said. Barely a whisper. "No. Don't. Don't sing it again. Sit down. Sit — yes. All right."

They pressed both palms flat against their knees, and Ulyana watched them take three slow breaths, the way they did when a customer tried to haggle below cost, gathering patience like coins off a table.

"Where did you learn that song?"

"I didn't learn it. I've always known it. Before I could talk, the woman said. She found me after — after Greyford." The word came out of her strangely, a name she knew without knowing why she knew it. "There was a fire. A big one. Everyone always went quiet when I asked about it, so I stopped asking. She said I was dragged out of it. Out of the ash. She said anyone who survived that fire was cursed, and she charged people money to look at me, sometimes, like a freak at a fair." She laughed, and hated how thin it sounded. "Maybe she was right about the curse part."

"She wasn't." Silas's voice had gone rough. "Ulyana. Look at me. That song — do you know what it is?"

"No."

"It's a calling-song. Storm-craft. There were — " They stopped. Started again, slower, like a man walking out onto ice and testing each step. "Once, there were families who carried the storms in their blood. Not charms, not knots, not the little borrowed things I sell. The real thing. Lightning that answers. Rain that follows. They were called the storm-line, and they — " Their jaw tightened. "The crown killed them. Twenty-three years ago. Every soul they could find, in one night, in a fire, and the story everyone tells is that they were monsters plotting to drown the kingdom. I grew up on that story. Half the world did."

The water lapped against the crates. Somewhere far off, thunder grumbled again, like a dog turning over in its sleep.

"And me?" Ulyana asked. It came out very small. "What's the story about me?"

Silas looked at her for a long moment. In the lamplight their eyes were wet, and they didn't seem to know it, or didn't care.

"You," they said, "are the story the crown missed."

* * *

Afterward — she would never be sure how much later, ten minutes or an hour — they sat side by side on the two crates with their feet nearly touching the black water, and Silas talked the way they only talked when they'd forgotten to be guarded: low and fast and with their eyes fixed on the middle distance.

"The storm-line wasn't just one family. It was bloodlines braided together, generations deep. Kings married into it for the blessing of it — a harvest shielded from hail, a fleet brought home safe. The old palace had a storm-crown, did you know that? An actual crown. It chose its wearers. Or so the histories say." They snorted softly. "I read a great many histories in a former life."

"You were there," Ulyana said. Not a question. "At Greyford. You were one of them."

Silas was quiet long enough that she thought they wouldn't answer.

"I was a storm-caller," they said finally. "Lower line. Distant blood. Enough to call real weather if I was desperate, not enough to matter to anyone important. I fought at Greyford because everyone I loved told me it was duty." Their voice flattened. "We lost. The ones who surrendered were given a choice between the pyre and exile with their gift burned out of them. I chose neither. I ran. And I have spent ten years selling fever-charms that work because people believe in them, and paying off harbor guards, and keeping my head so far down I could count the cobblestones with my nose — all so that no one would ever, *ever* hear thunder answer a human voice in this city again."

"Why?" she asked. "Why hide? Why not fight, or flee overseas, or — "

"Because I saw what happens when we don't hide." For the first time, Silas turned and looked at her straight on, and there was no softness in it at all. "Greyford burned for three days, Ulyana. Not from our lightning — from theirs. And the people who died in it weren't soldiers. They were bakers' families. They were children in cellars exactly like this one." A breath. "I swore an oath. No storms. Never again. Not for any cause, not for any price. Because every time one of us raises a hand, the crown burns a neighborhood to prove a point, and I promised myself I would not hand them the match."

The words settled onto Ulyana like wet wool. She had imagined — without letting herself imagine it, exactly — that showing Silas her secret would end with answers. With a plan. With *here's how we fix you.*

Instead Silas reached over and took her frayed cuff gently between two fingers, examining it like a charm gone wrong.

"How long," they asked, "have you known it was getting stronger?"

"A year. Maybe more."

"And how often do you sing?"

"Not often." She picked at her sleeve. "Almost never. Only — " She hesitated, and then, because apparently today was the day she emptied herself out entirely: "Only when I miss people. It helps. The storm feels like — company."

Silas closed their eyes briefly. When they opened them, they were doing the thing Ulyana thought of as their market-face, the composed one, but it kept slipping, like a shutter in bad wind.

"All right," they said. "Listen to me. From tonight, you don't sing. Not alone, not sad, not ever, unless I'm standing next to you and I say it's safe. Can you do that?"

"What are you going to do?"

"I'm going to think." They rubbed their face with both hands. "I'm going to think very hard, and possibly drink something, and meanwhile you're going to go home through the back ways, and tomorrow you'll come to the stall at the usual hour and buy a luck-knot with the usual insolence, and everything will look exactly normal. Agreed?"

"Agreed." She paused. "You called it *home*."

"I called it the place where your things are."

"I own a blanket and a stolen teapot."

"A very nice teapot."

Despite everything — the terror still fizzing in her fingertips, the word *burned* still lodged under her ribs — Ulyana grinned. And then, before she could think better of it, before fear could catch up with her, she leaned over and hugged Silas Fairweather, exiled storm-caller, the stiffest, flinchiest person in all of Veymark.

They froze. They always froze. But after a moment, one hand came up and rested awkwardly on her shoulder, patting twice, like a man defusing something.

"There there," they said. "Please stop crying on my coat. It's expensive."

"It's patched in four places."

"It's *sentimentally* expensive."

* * *

She climbed the ladder first, per agreement — Silas wanted to sit alone in the dark for a while, "composing myself into a convincing liar," they said. At the top, she paused with her hands on the cold grate and looked back down.

Silas hadn't moved. They sat on the wobbling crate with the lamp between their feet, and even from above she could see their lips moving, silent, counting or praying or rehearsing. The water around them had begun, very faintly, to glow — a thread of pale light rising off their skin like heat-shimmer, coiling upward toward the ceiling and dissolving.

Ulyana had seen Silas's magic plenty of times: the little warmth in a fever-charm, the faint hum of a luck-knot. Nothing like this. This was the same thing she'd done, the same light, the same answering power — older and sadder and worn down to almost nothing, like a song sung so long in whispers it had forgotten its own volume.

*Storm-craft,* she thought. *He's been carrying it all this time. Ten years of whispers.*

And underneath the thought, quieter, colder, came the other thing — the thing she'd been circling all evening and refusing to land on:

*If they find me, they'll burn me. If they find him protecting me, they'll burn him too. And the criers say new edicts are coming. Everyone can feel it coming, like weather.*

She slipped out through the back ways as instructed, past the chandlery and the cooper's yard, and it wasn't until she reached the loft and her blanket and her very nice teapot that she let herself shake.

Outside, the rain thickened. Thunder rolled in off the sea, closer than the storm had any business being, and it sounded — she pressed her face to the window and listened, heart hammering — it sounded less like weather and more like a voice practicing her lullaby, very far away, patient as tide.

*Come find me,* she thought at it, absurdly. *Tell me what I am.*

The thunder rolled again, nearer. And in the morning, when she came down to the Nightmarket with her coppers clutched in her fist and her insolence freshly polished, the criers were already in the square — and the first word booming off the walls was *edict*.
