## 4. Ten Years of Rain

The rain came down on Veymark the way it always did in autumn—sideways, apologetic, and smelling faintly of fish.

Silas Fairweather stood under the awning of their stall in the Nightmarket and watched it, and hated how much they missed it.

That was the dirty secret of exile nobody warned you about: you could spend ten years swearing off a thing, and your body would still lean toward it like a plant toward a window. The rain rolled off the canvas above them and pooled in the gutters, and every drop that struck stone sent up a little whisper only Silas could hear. *Hello,* said the rain. *Hello. Hello.* Ten thousand small voices, all of them asking the same question they'd asked for a decade.

*Are you ready to come home?*

"No," Silas told the gutter, quietly, so the woman buying fever-charms wouldn't think her charm-seller had gone strange. "Absolutely not."

"Sorry?" said the woman, counting coppers.

"Nothing, Mistress Hobb. Three for the charms, one extra because you'll lose one within the week."

"I never lose them."

"You lost two last month."

Mistress Hobb sniffed, paid four coppers, and tucked the knots of braided red thread into her shawl with the tenderness of a woman handling sleeping birds. The charms did nothing, of course. That was the point. A knot of thread could not break a fever. But a knot tied by someone who looked at you kindly while you described your daughter's cough—that could do a surprising amount. Silas had learned long ago that most of magic was just attention, and attention was the one gift they were still allowed to give.

The rain whispered. *Hello.*

"Shut up," Silas murmured, and went back to tying knots.

* * *

The Battle of Greyford had lasted eleven minutes.

Silas counted them still, some nights, lying awake in their rented room above the tallow-chandler's. Eleven minutes from the first horn to the last scream. They had been twenty-two years old and the youngest storm-caller ever sworn to the Greyford garrison, and they had stood on the ridge with forty other callers and raised their hands when the command came down the line like a wave passing through water.

*Now.*

And the sky had come down.

Silas remembered it in pieces, the way you remember anything too big to hold whole. The smell of ozone and turned earth. The enemy line—a rebel host out of the eastern valleys, farmers mostly, men with pikes sharpened on cart-wheels—coming apart like wet paper. The lightning walking across the field on legs of white fire, each strike a fist through the world. And underneath it all, threaded through everything, the *singing*. Storm-calling felt like singing, if singing could crack stone. Every caller heard it differently. Silas's mother had said hers sounded like her own mother's voice. Silas's had sounded, even then, like rain on a roof at night. Safe. Cozy. While it killed three hundred people below.

Eleven minutes.

Then the order came again—*hold, hold, they're breaking, drive them into the river*—and the river rose, and after that Silas stopped counting bodies because the river was doing the arithmetic for everyone.

They told the story differently afterward, in the official version. The Crown histories called it the Reclamation of Greyford, a great victory over the Coven's eastern sympathizers. What the histories did not mention—and what Silas learned three days later, standing in the mud helping pull the dead onto carts—was that half the "rebel host" hadn't been rebels at all. They'd been a village caught marching between armies. There had been children in that column. Silas knew because they had pulled one out of the shallows themselves, a boy no older than eight, his small hand still closed around a wooden horse with one leg chewed off.

The boy's name, Silas learned later, from a list nobody wanted to keep, had been Tam.

Silas did not cry at the burial detail. Something in them had gone past crying into a flat, ringing silence, like the inside of a bell. But that night, packing their kit in the officers' tent, they had felt the storm gathering over the camp—the ordinary, innocent evening rain coming in off the hills—and every drop of it had sung *hello*, and Silas had understood, with the terrible clarity that arrives only once or twice in a life, exactly what they were.

Not a soldier. Not a weapon, even. Weapons don't enjoy it.

They had walked out of the camp before dawn without leave, and the desertion would have hanged them if the war hadn't ended six weeks later in an armistice nobody trusted. Instead there were papers. Papers with seals. An inquiry board that sat Silas in a cold room and asked, gently, whether they had followed orders at Greyford, and Silas—who had learned something in eleven minutes about what happened to people who followed orders—said yes. Yes, I followed orders. Yes, I raised my hands. Yes, the sky came down.

The board gave them a choice, because the war was over and hanging heroes was bad for morale: the block, or exile. Banishment from the crown's service, oath revoked, gift formally renounced, one way passage to whatever city would have them.

Silas chose Veymark because it was far away, because it rained constantly, and—this was the part they never admitted to anyone, not even later, not even to Ulyana—because it hurt. A city built on a drowned harbor, where the sea came up through the streets twice a day and the air tasted of salt and rot, was the cruelest place they could imagine for someone who loved storms. It was self-punishment dressed as practicality. Guilt makes its own geography.

They signed the renunciation with a shaking hand. The clerk stamped it. *Gift formally surrendered,* the seal said, which was a lie, because you can't surrender weather. You can only promise not to answer when it calls.

Ten years now, and Silas had kept the promise. Through fires and floods and one memorable incident involving a runaway grain barge, they had kept their hands at their sides and their mouth shut while the rain sang *hello* against every window in Veymark.

It was, they reflected, tying another luck-knot as the market lamps flickered on, the longest anyone had ever gone without answering their mother's voice.

Because that was the thing about the singing. In the last years of her life, when the calling had eaten her thin as winter grass, Silas's mother had told them what the storm really was. Not power. Not a weapon. *It's the dead, love,* she'd said, her hands around a cup of tea she was too shaky to drink. *Every storm is full of everyone the storms took. That's why it sings. It's not calling you to kill. It's calling you to listen.*

Silas had thought that was the fever talking.

Then Tam's hand, and the wooden horse, and ten years of rain saying *hello* in a voice that sometimes, on certain nights, sounded eight years old.

* * *

The foundling arrived on the third of Frostmonth, in a basket that was frankly an insult to basketry.

Silas opened their door that morning to find it sitting on the step between the milk churn and a delivery of candle wax, woven from reeds that were already unraveling, and inside it—not a bundle of blankets, as the stories promised, but a girl of perhaps five or six, sitting bolt upright, wide awake, watching Silas with eyes the color of a sky deciding whether to misbehave.

She did not cry. She looked Silas up and down, taking inventory, the way sailors assess a ship, and then she said:

"You've got bread on your face."

Silas touched their cheek. She was right. There was bread on their face. It had been that kind of week.

"There's more inside," Silas said, because it was true and because they could think of nothing else.

"I know," said the girl, and climbed out of the basket herself, since apparently the basket had been a formality.

Her name, she said—or rather, the name she answered to, having clearly decided that whoever had left her in the reeds by the tidal stairs had forfeited naming rights—was Ulyana. She didn't know where she'd come from before the basket. She knew the docks, every alley off Fishgutter Lane, which shopkeepers chased you and which pretended not to see you, and exactly how long a stolen roll stayed warm. She did not know why, she said, examining Silas's cluttered workbench with frank curiosity, "the lamps go funny near me sometimes."

Silas went very still.

"Funny how?"

"Just funny." She shrugged, five-going-sixty years old. "Flickery. Mam used to say—" and here she stopped, because *Mam* was evidently a door she kept locked, and even at six she knew better than to open doors in strangers' houses.

But Silas had seen it already, hadn't they? Looking back on that morning from ten years' distance, they could admit it. The way the candle flame had leaned toward the girl like a flower toward sun. The static in the wool blanket she'd wrapped herself in, crackling softly when she moved. And underneath it, faint as a heartbeat under floorboards, something in the *rain* outside shifting its song—turning from the usual chorus of *hello, hello* toward something newer, softer, almost wondering.

*Oh,* the rain had seemed to say. *Oh, look who's here.*

Silas should have turned her over to the parish wardens. That was the sensible thing; that was the law-adjacent thing; Veymark had institutions for exactly this. But Silas had spent eleven minutes following orders once and had spent every day since paying for it, and besides, the girl was eating the rest of the loaf with the focused devotion of someone who knew meals were weather—sometimes they came, sometimes they didn't—and it is very difficult to hand a child to the wardens while she is still chewing.

"You can stay till the rain stops," Silas said.

It was Veymark. The rain did not stop for four months.

By then she slept on the pallet by the stove, and had opinions about Silas's knotwork ("that one's crooked"), and had learned that the man with bread on his face flinched, very slightly, whenever thunder rolled—and she, being Ulyana, noticed this and filed it away with everything else, behind those storm-colored eyes, in the great quiet ledger she kept of the world.

* * *

Presently, the market bell rang the hour, and Silas came back to themselves at the stall with the memory of small hands and unraveling baskets still warm behind their ribs.

Ulyana appeared at the edge of the awning exactly on schedule, fourteen now and tall as a whip stalk, hood up against the rain, carrying the look of a person who had information and was enjoying the suspense.

"You," she said, "are being watched."

"I'm always being watched. I'm devastatingly handsome."

"You've got flour on your face." She reached up and brushed Silas's cheek with two fingers, matter-of-fact, the way she'd done since she was six, and Silas let her, because some habits are load-bearing. "No—watched. Properly. There's a woman at the corner table of the Gilded Eel. Fine cloak. Boots that cost more than this whole stall. She's been writing in a little book every time someone talks to you."

Silas's hands went still on the knotwork.

"How long?"

"Since midday. I bought nothing, sat with a cup of the Eel's terrible cider, and watched her watch you. She tips well, by the way. Everyone likes her. Old Pemmy from the chandlery came out looking flattered, and Pemmy has never once in recorded history looked flattered."

A fine-cloaked stranger, charming the Nightmarket, taking notes on a charm-seller. In the season of the Edict, when criers were reading proclamations off the steps of every customs house and the word *register* had started appearing in sentences next to the word *or else*.

Silas looked past Ulyana's shoulder, down the wet lane toward the tavern, and saw nothing but lamplight and rain—which meant, of course, that the watcher had either gone or was very good indeed.

"Ulyana," Silas said carefully. "Go home the long way tonight. By the chandler's. Not the lane."

"It's raining."

"It's Veymark."

She held their gaze a moment, that old ledger of hers visibly opening, pages turning. Then, mercifully: "Fine. But you owe me a story. The real one, about the thunder-flinch. I've waited eight years, Silas. My patience is legendary but not infinite."

"Tomorrow," Silas said. "I'll tell you tomorrow."

She went, hood up, quick and light along the slick stones, and the rain sang after her—*oh, oh, look who's here*—and Silas stood alone under the awning with flour on their face and dread settling over the market like weather, thinking that tomorrow might be a promise the world would not let them keep.
