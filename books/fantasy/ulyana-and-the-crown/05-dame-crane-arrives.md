## 5. Dame Crane Arrives

The rain had followed Rosalind Crane from the capital for three days, as if it disapproved of her mission and intended to make its opinion known at every opportunity. She did not hold it against the weather. Weather, unlike people, was honest about its hostility.

She arrived in Veymark on the morning coach, dressed not in the grey-and-scarlet of the Inquisition but in a traveling gown of unremarkable brown wool, her badge of office wrapped in oilcloth at the bottom of her second trunk. The trunk itself was full of books. This was not a disguise so much as a preference. Inquisitors who announced themselves got lies; inquisitors who arrived as widowed schoolmistresses got gossip, and gossip was simply truth wearing its nightclothes.

"Madam Crane," said the driver, hauling down her bags. "You'll want the Gilded Eel, up by the customs house. Clean beds."

"I'm sure I would," she agreed pleasantly, "but I've taken rooms above the Nightmarket. The Lamplighter's Rest."

The driver's face performed a small, involuntary tragedy. "Above the *market*? Madam, that's — it's noisy, is what it is. Fishwives from four in the morning. And the folk down there—" he lowered his voice as though the cobblestones might be informants, "—they're not the sort for a lady alone."

"No," Crane said, smiling. "I imagine they're not. That's rather the point."

She paid him, tipped him too much, and watched his face decide between suspicion and gratitude. Gratitude won, as it usually did. People wanted so badly to be liked that they mistook generosity for innocence. It was, in her long experience, the single most reliable flaw in the human character, and she had built a career on it.

The rooms above the Nightmarket were exactly as advertised: small, damp, and overlooking everything. From her window she could see the market square laid out like a hand of cards — the fishmongers' row steaming in the drizzle, the charm-stalls with their ribbons and knotted cords, the pawnbroker's shuttered face, the alley mouths where children moved like minnows. She unpacked her books first, then her badge, then a small lacquered box she set on the sill without opening.

Then she made tea, sat down, and began to listen.

Not to anything in particular. That was the mistake young inquisitors made — they listened *for* something, and so heard only what they expected. Crane listened the way one listens to an orchestra tuning: no melody yet, only the shape of what was about to play. Veymark tuned itself around her all afternoon. A cart axle squealing. A woman scolding a boy for stealing plums — too loudly, Crane noted; the scold was for an audience. Somewhere below, a vendor laughing at a customer's joke with the particular warmth of someone who needed the customer to come back tomorrow.

And underneath it all, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, something else. A hum. Not sound, exactly. More like the memory of sound, the way a bell keeps humming after the ear gives up on it.

Crane sipped her tea and wrote nothing down. Writing came later, when listening had done its work.

* * *

She opened her shop interviews the next morning with the pawnbroker, because pawnbrokers knew everyone and trusted no one, which made them ideal first conversations. You learned the shape of a town's denials from the man whose whole trade was other people's secrets.

"Herrin Voss?" The pawnbroker, a narrow man named Quill with ink permanently stained into the creases of his fingers, looked at her letters of introduction — schoolmistress, gathering material for a history of coastal trade — and relaxed by perhaps four percent. "Voss does honest work. Luck-knots, fever-strings, that sort of thing. Superstition, mostly. Harmless."

"Harmless," Crane repeated, as though tasting the word. "And do you believe in luck-knots, Master Quill?"

"I believe in copper," said Quill, "and copper believes in whatever you tell it."

She laughed — genuinely, which surprised her — and bought a chipped teacup she did not want. By the time she left, she knew three things: that Quill feared the new edict more than he feared her; that he had once sold a silver locket to a harbor guard who could not possibly have afforded it honestly; and that when she mentioned charm-sellers, his eyes had flicked — briefly, involuntarily — toward the far end of the fishmongers' row.

Eyes always flicked toward the thing they were protecting. It was practically a confession with better manners.

She worked through the row methodically over the following days. The fishwife Marta Hollis, broad-handed and blunt, who fed stray cats and lied about nothing except her age. The ribbon-seller who talked for forty minutes without saying anything, a feat Crane privately admired. The oyster-man who wept twice, once about his knees and once about the edict, and meant both.

"The Cleansing," the oyster-man said, wiping his eyes with a sleeve that had seen decades of similar service. "They say gifted folk have to register now. Register! Like criminals. My grandmother read tea leaves, God rest her. Am I to report my own gran? She's dead, but still. Principle of the thing."

"And if she weren't dead?"

He looked at her with the naked misery of a good man doing arithmetic he hated. "I'd bring her tea," he said finally. "Every day. And I'd pray nobody asked me direct."

"There it is," Crane said gently. "That's the whole edict in one sentence, isn't it. Nobody wants to ask, and everybody's afraid of being asked."

He stared at her. "You're not like most of these crown folk that come through."

"I'm a schoolmistress," she said, and smiled, and let him believe it, because the alternative would have required her to arrest him for obstruction, and she found she liked his answer too much for that.

It was on the fifth day, in the gray hour before the lamps were lit, that she reached the stall at the end of the row.

* * *

Silas Fairweather's stall was unremarkable in every particular, which was itself remarkable. Every other seller in the Nightmarket arranged their goods to catch the eye; this one arranged them to slide past it. Luck-knots in undyed cord. Fever-charms of plain wood. A basket of river stones polished smooth, labeled, in a careful hand, FOR WORRYING (HOLD ONE). Prices chalked low enough to be forgettable.

The man behind the stall was lean and gray-eyed, somewhere past forty, with the weathered look of someone who had spent years standing very still outdoors. He was mending a knot of cord with the absorbed patience of a monk, and when Crane stopped at his stall he looked up with the expression of a man greeting a customer and nothing more.

"Good evening," she said. "You're the charm-seller."

"Luck-knots, fever-strings, worry-stones." He gestured at the basket. "No cures guaranteed, no futures told. If your luck improves, the knot gets the credit. If it doesn't, you've lost two coppers and gained a stone."

"That's very nearly honest commerce."

"It's the closest thing to it I sell." His voice was dry, unhurried. Something in it snagged at her — an accent worn smooth, like a coin rubbed until the face faded. Coastal, but not Veymark coastal. Northern, maybe. Greyford way. She filed this away without letting it reach her face.

"I'll take a fever-charm," she said. "For a friend's daughter. She's prone to winter chills."

"Third one on the left has ash wood — traditional for fevers. Second one's oak, if you prefer stubbornness to tradition." He held out three charms on his open palm, and there was nothing in the gesture at all, no flourish, no theater. Just a man selling carved wood.

Crane selected the ash one and paid him. And then, because courtesy cost nothing and revealed everything, she let her fingers rest on his wrist as she took the charm — just a moment, just the natural brush of a transaction prolonged by a smile.

His pulse did not quicken.

That was the interesting part. Guilty men flinched. Innocent men flushed or stammered or laughed. But Silas Fairweather went perfectly, deliberately still, the way a hare goes still under a hawk's shadow — not because stillness saves it, but because movement certainly doesn't. For one heartbeat, two, three, his wrist lay in her grip like something already surrendered, and behind his gray eyes she watched a door close with great care and quiet, the way one closes a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

Then he smiled — a small, tired, entirely charming smile — and said, "Careful, madam. Hold it any longer and I'll have to charge you for the charm *and* the wrist."

"Forgive me." She released him. "Your pulse distracted me. It's remarkably calm, for a man whose king has just declared his trade a hanging matter."

"My trade is cord and carpentry." He began wrapping her purchase in paper, unhurried, each fold precise. "The king can hang carpenters if he likes. There'll be no houses left, but that's government for you."

"You're not afraid of the edict."

"I'm not fond of it." He handed her the parcel. "Fondness and fear are different animals. Fear runs. Fondness stays and complains about the weather." He glanced up at the sky, which was indeed beginning to spit again. "Speaking of which. You'll want to be indoors. Rain's coming in earnest tonight — I can feel it in the knots."

"In the knots," Crane repeated.

"Cord tightens before wet weather. Everyone knows that." He said it lightly, easily, the way a man says something he has said ten thousand times, and that was precisely what made her certain he was lying. Not about the cord. About the ease.

"Thank you for the charm," she said. "I hope your friend's daughter — I mean, my friend's daughter — finds it useful."

"I hope nobody needs it at all," he said, and turned to his next customer, a small sharp-eyed girl who had appeared at the stall's edge with the silent competence of a cat, and who took a bread roll from beneath the counter with the unremarkable smoothness of a transaction performed daily for years.

Crane walked back through the darkening market, the charm warm in her pocket, and permitted herself, at last, the small pleasure of certainty.

There it was. Ten years of harmless charms, of luck-knots and worry-stones, of a man so carefully unremarkable that his unremarkableness rang like a struck bell once you stood close enough. Bread slipped to orphans. Charms priced for the poor. A pulse that didn't race because it had spent a decade learning not to.

She climbed the stairs to her rooms, lit her lamp, and opened the lacquered box on the sill. Inside, on a bed of velvet, lay a single dried sprig of rowan — mountain ash, the old warding wood — taken from the pyre of the last storm-caller burned at Greyford, ten years ago. It had never failed her yet. Held near a true gift, it warmed.

She carried it to the window and held it out over the Nightmarket, over the stalls packing up, the lamps coming on one by one, the small sharp-eyed girl vanishing down an alley with her stolen bread.

The sprig stayed cool as river stone.

Crane frowned at it for a long moment. Then she laughed softly at herself, set it back in its box, and sat down to write her first report.

*The subject S.F. presents as common tradesman. No evidence of active gift. Recommend continued observation.*

All of it true. None of it complete. The sprig was cold, yes — but storms, she thought, remembering some half-forgotten lesson from her own schooldays, storms were strange things. They built far off, in silence, where no instrument thought to look. And the man at the end of the fishmongers' row had felt the rain coming in his knots.

Below her window, the first true drops began to fall, and somewhere in the city a bell that had not been rung in ten years seemed, very faintly, to begin humming.
