## 6. The Smugglers' Price

The Saltwrights kept their office above a fish-pickle warehouse in Brinewalk Row, which meant that anyone who came to do business with them arrived smelling like a hero's funeral. Silas climbed the stairs slowly, both because the stairs were rotten and because he wanted the extra time to rehearse.

*Good morning, Bren. Terrible weather we're having, what with me not being responsible for any of it.*

No. Too defensive.

*Bren! Old friend!*

Worse. Nobody had ever called Bren a friend without immediately regretting it.

He settled on knocking twice, pausing, then once — the old signal from ten years ago, when the Saltwrights had still been his route out of Veymark instead of just another thing he owed money to. The door opened on a chain, and one of Bren's nephews squinted at him through the gap like Silas was a bill collector.

"Tell him it's Fairweather."

The nephew's face did something complicated — half hunger, half pity — and shut the door. Silas heard boots inside, low voices, the scrape of a chair. Then the chain rattled off and he was waved into a room that smelled of tallow, tobacco, and pickling brine, furnished entirely with things other people had probably been looking for when they disappeared.

Bren Saltwright sat behind a desk that had once been a church pew, and he had not aged so much as *cured*. His face was all weathered leather and salt-cracked seams now, his beard going the color of old rope, but his eyes were the same as they'd always been: small, quick, and doing arithmetic at all times. Even now, watching Silas cross the room, Silas could practically hear the abacus clicking.

"Silas Fairweather," Bren said, spreading his arms wide enough to be friendly and not wide enough to invite an embrace. "Ten years, and you finally come up my stairs. I'd be flattered if I didn't already know it means trouble."

"When has it ever meant anything else?"

"True." Bren gestured to a stool. "Sit. Drink?"

"If it's the good bottle."

"It's never the good bottle."

"Then no."

Bren laughed — a real laugh, rusty from disuse — and poured two cups of the bad bottle anyway, pushing one across the desk. It tasted like a shipwreck felt. Silas drank it and pretended otherwise, because manners cost nothing and Bren was currently the only man in Veymark who could make papers appear out of thin air without magic.

"So," Bren said. "Trouble."

"You've heard the criers."

"I've heard the criers." The humor drained out of Bren's face the way water drains out of a tidepool, leaving everything exposed and blinking. "Edict of Cleansing. Register or burn. Very tidy of them. My niece registered last week." He said it flatly, the way a man says *my knee aches in winter*, a fact worn smooth by handling. "She came home with a lead seal on a cord around her neck and she hasn't stopped washing her hands since."

Silas set down the cup. "I need passage out. For two."

Bren's eyebrows went up, then came back down very carefully, like a man lowering something fragile. "Two."

"Me and a girl. Thirteen years old. She's—"

"A relative?"

"No."

"A ward?"

"Not exactly."

"Silas." Bren leaned back until the pew creaked. "In thirty years of this trade, every passenger who wasn't a relative or a ward was either cargo or catastrophe. Which is she?"

Neither, Silas thought. She's the last living heir of the storm-line, and if the crown learns her name they'll burn her on the palace steps, and if the covens learn her name they'll put a crown on her head and a war in her hands, and she is thirteen years old and likes her bread torn rather than cut and laughs at her own jokes before she finishes them.

"She's mine to look after," is what he said. "That's all anyone needs to know. Including you."

"Mm." Bren turned his cup in circles on the desk, around and around, a habit Silas remembered from a decade ago, from the night they'd smuggled him past the harbor chain in a barrel of salt herring. Around and around. "Passage for two costs double. Papers for two cost triple. Papers for a man whose face is about to be on every wall in Veymark—" He stopped. Looked up. "Ah. That's coming, isn't it. That's why you're here *now*, before the walls get your face on them."

"Bren—"

"Don't. Don't 'Bren' me yet." Around went the cup. "Let me tell you what I see, because you always did appreciate honesty more than you appreciated me. I see a charm-seller from the Nightmarket who pays my harbor boys too promptly and asks too few questions. I see a man with storm-callers' hands who's spent ten years pretending to have a fishmonger's soul. And now I see him needing out of the city fast, with a child nobody will describe properly, right when the crown starts paying gold for gifted persons and *double* gold for persons the Inquisition marks as interesting." The cup stopped turning. "So before we talk prices, Silas. What am I actually selling passage *from*?"

Silas held his gaze. Ten years of practice, holding things behind his eyes. "A bad law. That's all. We're running from a bad law."

"And the girl? Is she running from the same bad law, or is she running *toward* something?"

"The same law."

Bren studied him for a long moment. Outside, gulls argued over the warehouse roof, and somewhere below, the pickle vats burped their eternal sour breath into the street. Then Bren smiled, and it was the smile Silas remembered from the barrel night, warm and crooked and absolutely worthless as a guarantee.

"All right," Bren said. "All right. For old times. Papers for two, passage on the *Kindly Fortune*, three nights from now, out with the ebb tide. Cost you forty crowns."

Silas blinked. Forty crowns was roughly everything he owned plus a debt of gratitude, but it was also — he ran the numbers again — suspiciously reasonable. Suspiciously *low*. "Forty."

"Forty. Old friend rate." Bren spread his hands. "I'm getting sentimental in my old age. My physician says it's the pickling fumes."

Every instinct Silas had developed in ten years of hidden living stood up at once and started shouting. He made himself sit still. He made himself say, "Done. Half now?"

"Half now, half at the boat." Bren counted the coins Silas laid out without actually looking at them, which somehow made it worse. Then he stood, extended his hand, and gripped Silas's forearm in the old smuggler's fashion, skin to skin, the grip that meant *we are bound by this handshake until one of us unwinds it.* His hand was dry and strong and did not let go quite as quickly as it should have.

"Three nights," Bren said. "Brinewalk stairs, third pier, the *Fortune* flies a green lamp at her stern when she's ready. Bring the girl after dark. And Silas—" the small eyes glittered "—bring her calm. Nervous passengers make nervous crews, and nervous crews talk."

* * *

Silas walked home the long way, which was to say he walked in circles for an hour, which was to say he was afraid and dressing it up as caution.

Forty crowns. Bren had once charged a widow sixty to move two crates of untaxed wool. Bren had once told Silas, laughing over the herring barrels, that sentiment was the most expensive cargo a man could carry and he'd sunk every shipment of it he'd ever loaded. Forty crowns for forged papers and quiet passage out of a city under cleansing edict was not a price. It was a hook, baited and gleaming, and Silas had bitten it anyway because there was nowhere else to bite.

He bought bread on the way home — the dark rye Ulyana liked, torn not cut — and climbed the crooked stairs to the room above the chandler's where the two of them had lived for six years under the fiction that he was her uncle and she was his late sister's daughter and neither of them was anything remarkable at all.

She was sitting on the windowsill doing sums on a slate, because Mother Tabb in the market had decided the girl had a head for figures and Ulyana had decided that being praised was worth enduring arithmetic. She looked up when he came in, and her face did the thing it always did — the quick flicker toward his hands, checking they were empty, the quick flicker toward the street below, checking it was empty too — before settling into the ordinary brightness she wore like armor.

"You're late," she said. "You're always late. You walk like the streets owe you money."

"The streets do owe me money." He handed over the bread. Her whole face lit, and something in his chest that had been clenched since Brinewalk Row loosened by one grudging degree. "Ulyana. We need to talk."

Her slate stopped scratching. Thirteen years old, and she could read a tone of voice better than Mother Tabb's ledger. "Bad talk or good talk?"

"Complicated talk."

"That's the worst kind."

"It usually is." He sat down across from her, on the crate that served as a second chair, and made himself meet her eyes. Grey eyes, storm-grey, though only he knew to look for the color of them. "In three nights, we leave Veymark. There's a boat. A smuggler named Bren is making us papers, and he's doing it cheap, and I don't trust cheap, but I don't see another road. So in three nights we go, and we don't come back, and you'll need to be brave, and you'll need to be quiet, and you'll need to be—"

"Calm," Ulyana said.

He stared at her.

"You've got your telling-people-to-be-calm face on." She hugged the bread to her chest. "You get it when you're scared. Your left eyebrow does a thing."

"My eyebrow does *not* do a—"

"It does a thing." But she was smiling, and then she wasn't, and she asked the question he'd been dreading in the smallest voice he'd ever heard her use. "It's because of the edict. Isn't it. Because of what I—" She glanced at her own hands, curled around the loaf, as if lightning might be sleeping in her knuckles. Because it was. "Because of what I can do."

"Yes," Silas said, because lying to her had stopped being possible somewhere around the flooded cellar, somewhere around the first time she'd sung and the sky had answered. "Because of what you can do. But mostly because of who you are, which is a person who deserves to grow up someplace where nobody checks her neck for a lead seal."

Ulyana looked down at the bread. When she spoke again, the smallness was gone, filed away somewhere, and what remained was the sharp-eyed orphan of the Nightmarket, the girl who could count a crowd and find the exit in four seconds flat.

"Three nights," she said. "What do I pack?"

* * *

The next day, Silas sold charms.

It felt obscene, almost, standing at his stall in the Nightmarket with his luck-knots hung in rows and his fever-charms in their little drawstring pouches, while the whole city hummed with fear like a struck bell. But coin was coin, and he needed twenty more crowns by tomorrow, and besides — the routine was its own kind of prayer. Measure the cord. Tie the knot. Say the harmless words that meant nothing and did nothing and comforted everybody anyway. The great secret of Silas Fairweather's decade of exile was that ninety percent of his merchandise had never held a drop of real magic, and the customers had never cared, because what they'd been buying was the feeling that someone had tied a knot on their behalf, and that feeling worked whether or not the storm-calling did.

Old Widow Petch bought a luck-knot for her goat. The cooper's boy bought a fever-charm for his mother and tried to pay with buttons and got talked down to buttons-plus-two-coppers. Life, stubbornly, went on being life.

It was near dusk, packing up the stall, when Tam Wicker found him.

Tam was a Saltwright nephew — the one who'd squinted through the chained door — and he came sidling through the thinning market with his cap in his hands and his eyes doing the arithmetic thing his uncle's did, only badly, like a boy counting on his fingers while everyone watched.

"Master Fairweather." A glance left. A glance right. "Uncle Bren sent me. Says there's been a... an adjustment. To the arrangement."

Silas's hands went still over the charm-boxes. "An adjustment."

"He says come by tomorrow morning. Early. Says it's about the second passenger." Tam wet his lips. "Says it's about what the second passenger's *worth*."

The Nightmarket noise seemed to fall away, the way sound falls away underwater. Somewhere behind Silas's ribs, an old muscle he hadn't used in ten years flexed once, testing itself, and the wind off the harbor shifted direction without asking anyone's permission.

"Tell your uncle," Silas said, very evenly, "that the price was agreed."

Tam's face did the complicated thing again — hunger and pity, pity and hunger — and he backed away without answering, which was itself an answer.

Silas walked home the long way again. This time he wasn't circling. This time he was memorizing routes: the alley behind the chandler's, the drain that opened onto Brine Lane, the gap in the seawall fence that a slim thirteen-year-old could slip through in four seconds if she had to. He counted coins. Twenty crowns short of the second half, and even if he had it, even if he laid it in Bren's palm tomorrow, he knew — the way you know rain's coming in your knees — that the price had already changed. That somewhere above a pickle warehouse, a cup was turning in slow circles on a church-pew desk, and a small quick man was doing arithmetic, and the sum kept coming out the same:

One storm-caller: worth his weight in trouble.
One heir of the storm-line, delivered breathing to the Inquisitor's door: worth more than thirty years of smuggling.

And Bren had shaken his hand. Skin to skin. *Bound by this handshake until one of us unwinds it.*

Silas stood in his doorway in the dark, listening to Ulyana humming inside — some tuneless little song, harmless, he prayed, harmless — and understood that tomorrow he would climb those rotten stairs again, and that whatever happened there, one of two people was going to be betrayed before the week was out. He only had to decide who would do the betraying, and how much of his soul it would cost to live with the arithmetic afterward.

Inside, the humming stopped. "Silas?" Ulyana's voice, muffled through the door. "Is that you? Why are you standing out there?"

He put his hand on the latch and, for the first time in ten years, wished he still knew how to call the rain — if only so the whole city would be too wet and miserable tonight for anyone to come looking.

"Coming," he said.
