## 7. Running the Rooftops

Ulyana had learned early that the best place to hear secrets in Veymark was the loft above Bren Saltwright's counting room, because nobody ever looked up. Grown-ups, she had decided sometime around her ninth year, were a species that believed the world ended at eye level. Anything above a man's shoulder—rafters, roofbeams, the dusty gap between the ceiling and the sky—might as well have been the moon.

So she was in the loft, curled on a flour sack with a stolen heel of bread, when Bren said her name.

"—the girl herself. That's my price."

The bread went still in her hand.

She should have left then. Any sensible person would have left then. But Ulyana had spent ten years learning the city the way other children learned prayers, and one of the things Veymark taught you was that the first sentence of a secret was never the worst part. The worst part was always coming. You stayed to hear it, the way you kept your hand on a hot stove to confirm it was really happening.

Below her, through the gap between the floorboards, she could see the edge of the table, a ring of wet tankards, and Silas's hands.

She knew Silas's hands better than her own. Long-fingered, ink-stained at the knuckles, always half-curled as though they were holding something fragile even when they weren't. Right now they were pressed flat against the table, very still, the way they went still when Silas was frightened and didn't want anyone to know it.

"You're not funny, Bren," Silas said. His voice had the careful flatness he used in the market when a customer tried to haggle him below cost. "Ask for coin. Ask for the boat itself. I'll row."

"I'm asking for the boat's worth," Bren said. "The crown's paying fifty crowns for every gifted soul turned in, and I'd wager my other eye it's paying ten times that for anything with royal blood in it. You've been feeding that little stray for years, Fairweather. Don't insult us both by pretending she's nothing."

"She's a child."

"She's an *asset*." A chair scraped. Ulyana saw the shadow of Bren's bulk shift across the boards. "You want forged papers, a boat, a crew that keeps its mouth shut? That costs. The girl costs. It's arithmetic, Silas. I'm not a monster. I'll even make it quick and clean—hand her to the harbor patrol myself, no soldiers, no screaming. She'll never know you sold her."

"She'll know," Silas said quietly. "That's the thing about children, Bren. They always know."

Ulyana's heart was doing something strange—too slow and too fast at once, like a drum being beaten underwater. She should have been crying. She was aware, distantly, that crying was the appropriate response, the way it was appropriate to scream when you fell. But what she felt instead was a cold, bright clarity, the same clarity that came when she picked a lock or lifted a purse: the whole world simplified into a single problem with a single solution.

*Get down. Get out. Get gone.*

She began to move backward across the flour sacks, on her hands and knees, the way she'd climbed up. Slowly. Silently. The loft was full of things that wanted to creak—old rope, dry boards, a stack of oars Bren was too cheap to store properly—and Ulyana knew each one by heart. She'd mapped this loft the way she'd mapped every rooftop, cellar, and gutter in the Saltwright district, because a girl with no parents and no name needed a map of everything.

She was three feet from the loft ladder when her knee found the one board she'd never tested.

It sang.

Not loudly. A small wooden groan, the kind of sound a house made in its sleep. Below her, the voices stopped.

"Rats," Bren said.

"We don't get rats up there," said someone else—one of Bren's men, the one called Fitch who smelled of tar. "Bren had the loft salted against them."

The silence below was the worst sound Ulyana had ever heard. It had weight. It had *intent*.

She didn't wait for the ladder. She went through the loft's back window—the small one, the one that opened onto the gap between the Saltwright warehouse and the dyer's shop next door—shoulders first, the way she'd gone through a hundred windows before, dropping into the narrow dark between the buildings and catching the dyer's drainpipe on the way down. Rain met her there. It had been building all evening, that heavy gray pressure off the sea that Veymark got in the wet months, and now it came down in ropes, cold and immediate, soaking her to the skin in three heartbeats.

Good, she thought. Rain was cover. Rain was noise. Rain meant no lanterns, no faces at windows, no eyes.

Rain, she would remember later, also meant *her*.

* * *

The rooftops of the Saltwright district were Ulyana's true country. She'd been running them since she was six, first to steal, then to hide, then simply because up there the city made sense. Down in the streets, Veymark was a maze of rules that didn't apply to her—whose door you couldn't sleep against, whose stall you couldn't touch, whose eyes to avoid. Up on the ridgelines, it was simple: the world was a series of gaps, and every gap had a jump, and every jump had a landing, and if you were fast and light and knew your map, you were free.

She ran now the way she'd never run before—not for practice, not for pride, but with the whole animal engine of her turned to flight. Rain hammered the slate. Her bare feet found the wet ridges and knew them, old friends in the dark: the dyer's roof with its treacherous slick of dye-runoff, the cooperage with the long flat expanse where you could really open your legs, the church of the Tidewife with its squat bell tower that marked the halfway point to the harbor wall.

*She'll never know you sold her.*

She was over the cooperage when the thought finally caught her, mid-jump, and it hit harder than any fall could have. Silas. *Silas* had been sitting at that table, and Silas hadn't walked out. Silas, who slipped her bread and lied that it was going stale anyway. Silas, who had taught her letters on a slate in the back of the charm stall on slow afternoons, drawing the shapes and saying, *this one's an S, like a snake, like a storm.* Silas, who was the only person in ten years who had ever—

No. No, that wasn't right either, and the not-rightness of it snagged in her chest like a fishhook. Because she'd heard him. She'd heard him say *she's a child* in that voice, the flat one, and flat voices could mean anything. Flat voices could mean a man buying time. Flat voices could mean a man who'd already decided and was only haggling over the manner of it.

Ten years. He'd known her ten years, since the night she'd shown up half-frozen at the back of his stall with her hands bleeding from a lock she'd failed to pick, and he'd bandaged them without asking her name, and she'd decided, in the way of small children deciding enormous things, that this one was hers.

And he'd sat at Bren's table and haggled.

The sky flickered.

It was a small thing, at first. A pale stutter behind the clouds, off to the west, like someone testing a lamp. Ulyana noticed it the way you notice your own heartbeat—peripherally, without meaning to—and then noticed something stranger: it had flickered *with her*. In time. The flash had come exactly when the fishhook of the thought had sunk deepest.

That happened sometimes. She'd never told anyone, not even Silas. When her feelings got big—too big for her body, too big for her skin—the air got strange. Static in her hair. Sparks off iron railings. Once, in a bad winter, she'd hugged a doorpost and the frost on it had bloomed outward in ferns. She'd told herself it was nothing. She'd told herself it was the kind of nothing you kept hidden, because in Veymark, since the edicts, *nothing* was the only safe thing to be.

The edict. The criers, three days ago, their bells and their flat proclamations: *all persons of the gift to present themselves for registry, upon pain of—* she hadn't stayed to hear the pain part. She hadn't needed to. The whole market had emptied like a lung.

*The crown's paying ten times fifty crowns for anything with royal blood in it.*

Bren had said it like it was obvious. Like it was *known*. Which meant—

Which meant Bren knew what she was. Which meant Silas knew what she was, or at least suspected, which was impossible, which was—

The sky flickered again, longer this time, and this time she felt it go *through* her, a warm wire pulled tight from the soles of her feet to the roots of her hair, and somewhere over the harbor a rumble rolled, lazy and low, like a big dog waking up.

"Stop it," she whispered to herself, to the rain, to whatever was listening. "Stop it, stop it, stop—"

Because here was the arithmetic she couldn't escape: if the storm answered her, then she was gifted. And if she was gifted, then the criers meant her. And if the criers meant her, then every flash she pulled out of the sky was a signal fire with her name on it.

She ran faster.

* * *

The jump to the chandlery was the one she'd done a hundred times—a gap of maybe eight feet, an easy arc, land on the low roof, roll, up and away. She took it without thinking, and it was in the middle of the arc, hanging in the rain-dark with nothing under her but the drop, that the fear crested.

It didn't feel like fear. That was the thing she could never explain afterward, to Crane, to the council, to anyone who asked. It didn't feel like the cold-clawed animal terror of a near fall. It felt like *plenty*. Like her chest was a harbor at high tide and something enormous was coming in on the water, and there was no room for it and it was coming anyway.

The world went white.

Not a flash. A *flood*. Lightning came down somewhere behind her—close, terribly close, close enough that the thunder arrived with the light instead of after it, a crack like the sky splitting along a seam—and the chandlery roof lit up like the inside of a lamp. For one instant Ulyana saw the whole of the Saltwright district printed in silver: the rooftops, the rain, the harbor, and, at the edge of the light, three small figures pouring out of Bren's warehouse door, one of them huge and rolling like a barrel, all of them pointing.

*Up. They're pointing up.*

At her.

"No," she said, and her voice came out strange, doubled, as if the thunder had learned to speak through her. "No no no—"

She landed wrong. The roll saved her from the worst of it—Silas had taught her that too, *tuck your chin, little storm, the ground forgives the tucked*—but her shoulder cracked against slate and she slid, scrabbled, caught the chimney, hauled herself up. Below and behind her, boots on the warehouse stairs. Bren's men. Fitch with the tar-smell. Maybe Bren himself, though Bren didn't run, Bren *sent*.

Another flicker. She felt it building this time, felt it in her teeth, in the fillings of the air, a pressure like the moment before a sneeze but a hundred times vaster. The storm wasn't something outside her. She understood that now, with the terrible clarity of a girl hanging off a chimney in the rain. It was something *in* her, and it had been waiting ten years behind a door she'd kept shut by never looking at it, and fear had just kicked the door off its hinges.

*Don't*, she told it. *They'll see. They'll find me. Don't.*

But it was like telling her own heart to stop beating. The lightning came down again—two strikes this time, out over the water and then, horribly, one close, close enough to turn the rain to brief silver needles—and she heard shouting now, not just behind her but below, in the streets, doors opening, voices: *did you see, there, again, up on the roofs—*

She ran.

The bell tower of the Tidewife's church rose ahead of her, black against the gray. If she could get over it, down the far side, into the tanner's lanes where the roofs were low and the rain ran in channels and nobody looked up because everybody down there was busy with worse smells—

She jumped the gap to the tower's shoulder, caught the wet stone, and clung, and in the moment she clung, gasping, rain in her eyes, the fear crested again—because below her, spilling out of the alley at the tower's base, came Fitch and two others, lanterns swinging, faces turned up.

And the sky answered her.

She didn't call it. That was the truth she would carry for the rest of her life, the truth that would make an old herbwife weep and an inquisitor go quiet: she did not call it. It rose out of her like a shout out of a nightmare, a full-body *no* flung at the lanterns, at the hands reaching up, at the whole arrangement of a world in which she was something to be sold—and the sky took it and made it real. Lightning walked down the tower's iron weathervane and burst across the wet slate at her feet in a corona of blue-white fire, and the men below screamed and fell back, and every bell in the Tidewife's church rang at once though no hand touched them, and the sound went rolling out over Veymark like an alarm.

Ulyana stood on the tower's shoulder in the ringing dark, her hair standing off her head in a halo, her whole body trembling with borrowed thunder, and she thought, with a strange small calm in the eye of it all:

*Oh. So that's what I am.*

Then the calm broke, because the city was waking. Lights in windows, all along the streets, all pointing toward her. Voices. Whistles—the harbor patrol's whistles, three notes, the signal for *gifted sighted*. Somewhere far off, a deeper sound, boots, many, in rhythm. Soldiers.

They'd seen. Everyone had seen. She had spent ten years being nothing, being careful, being the girl nobody looked at twice, and she had just lit herself up like a festival lantern over the whole of the Saltwright district.

*Get down. Get out. Get gone.*

She went over the far side of the tower, down the drain-spout, into the tanner's lanes, and ran.

* * *

She ran until the lanes ran out and the rooftops ran low and the harbor wall rose gray and endless ahead of her, and there she stopped, at last, at the very edge of her country, where the map ended and the sea began. The rain was easing. The storm was moving off eastward, grumbling, like a dog that had done as it was told and couldn't understand why everyone was angry.

She stood on the seawall with her chest heaving, soaked through, shoulder bruised, hair still lifting faintly with static every time her heart stumbled, and she made herself think, because thinking was the one thing she had left that nobody could sell.

Bren would sell her. That was certain now. Silas had—Silas had *not walked out*, and she turned that fact over and over like a coin she couldn't read, because one side said *traitor* and the other side said *she's a child* in that flat, careful voice, and she didn't know which side was true, and not knowing was worse than either.

The patrol whistles were still going, blocks away now, but going. Soldiers' boots, somewhere. And behind all of it, faint but getting less faint, the sound of the city organizing itself into a hunt.

There was one place in Veymark where nobody would look for her tonight, because even she had never dared go there: the flooded cellar under the ruined customhouse, the one with the old storm-mark scratched on the door. Silas had told her once, in the early days, *never go in there, little storm, that place is bad luck*—and Silas lied about bread going stale and about not being lonely, but Silas had never once lied about a place being dangerous.

Which meant, she reasoned, with the fierce crooked logic of a girl with nothing left to lose, that it was the one place in Veymark nobody would ever think to find her.

She wiped the rain out of her eyes, turned her back on the sea, and ran—down off the wall, through the sleeping lanes, toward the ruined customhouse and the dark water underneath it, while behind her, over the harbor, the clouds began, very slowly, to gather again.

She didn't know, then, that the gathering was not hers.

She didn't know that across the city, in a charm-seller's shuttered stall, a man with ink-stained hands had just looked at the sky, and the sky had looked back, and he had said one word—*no*—in a voice like the bottom of the sea.

The storm that was coming for Veymark that night was Silas's. And it was coming to save her.
