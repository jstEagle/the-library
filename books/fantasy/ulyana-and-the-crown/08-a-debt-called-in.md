## 8. A Debt Called In

The word *no* was still hanging in the air of the shuttered stall when Silas realized what he had done.

He had spoken to the sky. Out loud. In a city where a single whispered syllable of magic could cost you your tongue, he had stood in his own shop with the lamp unlit and answered the weather like an old acquaintance, and the weather had answered back.

Ten years. Ten years of luck-knots and fever-charms and harmless little lies woven from nothing but string and suggestion, and all it had taken was one girl on a rooftop with lightning in her hair to make him open his mouth and *talk*.

Silas pressed both ink-stained hands flat against the counter and breathed.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could feel it: the Saltwrights moving through the wet lanes like eels, six of them at least, Bren's voice carrying up between the raindrops with that easy harbor drawl that had once bought Silas forged papers and now bought a girl's life instead. *"She went toward the customhouse. Get her before the watch does — Crane's people are already sniffing the waterfront."*

And Ulyana — small, sharp-eyed Ulyana, who stole bread with more grace than most nobles walked — running alone through the dark with her fear sparking off her like flint, lighting up the sky behind her in great stuttering banners that said *here, here, here* to anyone who knew how to read them.

To anyone like Silas.

To anyone like Dame Rosalind Crane, who was almost certainly sitting somewhere dry with her boots off and her ledger open, reading those banners like a woman reading her morning correspondence.

Silas looked at his hands. Ordinary hands. A charm-seller's hands, soft from ten years of not using them for anything but knots.

"There's a debt," he said quietly, to no one, to himself, to the storm gathering over the harbor like a held breath. "I owe a debt."

The sky, very politely, did not point out that he was the one who owed *it*.

* * *

He had rules. He'd made them the night he crawled out of Greyford with his hair half burned away and his name struck from every roll in the kingdom, and he had kept them the way other men kept religion.

Rule one: never call. Not wind, not rain, not so much as a breeze to cool your soup. The gift was a beacon; every storm-caller who had ever lived had died because someone saw the clouds move wrong and asked why.

Rule two: never feel it. The gift lived in feeling — grief, joy, rage, the whole loud orchestra of the heart — and so Silas had spent a decade teaching himself to be a quiet man. He sold charms. He haggled. He smiled at customers he didn't like. He had gotten so good at being small that some mornings he woke up genuinely bored, and boredom, he had decided, was the safest emotion ever invented.

Rule three: never, under any circumstances, love anything the sky could see.

He had broken rule three about nine years ago, the night he'd found a scrap of a child asleep in his doorway with frost on her eyelashes and a stolen apple clutched in one fist like a scepter. He'd told himself it was charity. Charity was safe. Charity was just fever-charms and day-old bread slipped across a counter with a scowl, tenderness disguised as indifference, the oldest trick in the exile's book.

But tonight a girl was running through the rain with a bounty on her head and a crown's worth of lightning in her blood, and Silas Fairweather stood in his shuttered stall and admitted, with the calm of a man stepping off a cliff, that he had loved her since the first time she'd called him *old man* and stolen two rolls instead of one.

Rule three was broken. Which meant rules one and two were just vanity.

He rolled up his sleeves.

* * *

Calling a storm, for those who have never done it, is less like commanding and more like arguing with something enormous that has been waiting a very long time for you to change your mind.

Silas climbed to the flat roof above his stall, where the rain hit him full in the face, cold and honest. Below, Veymark sprawled in its usual nighttime sulk — ten thousand chimneys, ten thousand secrets, the Nightmarket's gutted lanterns swinging in the rising wind. Somewhere out there, Dame Crane was sleeping or pretending to sleep. Somewhere out there, six smugglers were closing on a twelve-year-old girl. Somewhere beyond the harbor wall, the sea was doing what the sea always did, which was waiting.

"Hello," Silas said to the clouds.

They rumbled. It sounded, if he was being honest, smug.

"I know. I know what I said." He pushed his soaked hair out of his eyes. "I said I was finished. I said I'd rather sell string than summon weather. I said a great many things, most of which were cowardice wearing a sensible coat." He spread his arms, palms up, the old gesture, the one his mother had taught him before Greyford, before the exile, before everything. "But there's a girl. And she is mine to look after. And I am calling in the debt."

The sky said: *which debt?*

"The one from Greyford." His voice cracked on the name, and he let it. Feeling was fuel; rule two, inverted. "You took my mother. You took my name. You let me live, which was either mercy or cruelty, and I've spent ten years deciding which. Tonight you can pay me back. One storm. Small. Local. *Wet.*"

The clouds gathered themselves like a cat deciding whether to knock something off a table.

"And if I refuse?" the thunder asked, in the way thunder asks things.

"Then I'll stand here in the rain feeling things," Silas said, "and you know perfectly well what happens when I do that."

The sky laughed. It was not a kind sound. It was, however, an agreement.

Silas closed his eyes and opened everything else.

* * *

It came up through him like a tide returning to a shore that had forgotten it.

Ten years of dammed feeling broke all at once — the grief for Greyford, the guilt for every child he hadn't fed, the terror of the last hour, and underneath it all, vast and embarrassing and absolutely load-bearing, the love. For a sharp-eyed orphan. For a shabby city. For the whole ridiculous, stubborn, rain-soaked enterprise of staying alive and being decent anyway.

The storm drank it all and gave it back as light.

The first bolt hit the harbor itself, a white pillar from cloud to water, and the boom of it rolled through Veymark like the city clearing its throat after a decade of silence. Silas felt it leave him and thought, distantly, *oh, I remember this*, and then thought nothing coherent at all, because calling a true storm is what flying must feel like, if flying came with a conscience.

He steered it the way you steer anything enormous: barely, and with respect. He pulled the rain down in curtains over the Saltwrights' lane — walls of water, blinding, deafening — and drove a gust through the customhouse square that scattered their lanterns like startled birds. He heard Bren shouting, felt the smugglers' pursuit dissolve into confusion, boots slipping, curses swallowed by the downpour. He raised the level of the flooded streets by a hand's width, then another, until the low lanes ran like rivers and every boat, cart, and pursuer in the district was suddenly, thoroughly, defeated by geography.

Not drowned. He wasn't Greyford. He wasn't the crown. He simply made the city itself say *no* on Ulyana's behalf, and the city, being Veymark, said it with relish.

Exhilaration went through him like wine. Ten years of selling luck-knots, and *this* was what his hands were for — this vast, singing, terrible conversation with the sky, every nerve alight, every year of smallness redeemed in a single crackling breath. He laughed out loud, alone on his rooftop in the deluge, and the thunder laughed with him, and for one shining moment Silas Fairweather was the happiest man in Veymark.

Then he counted the windows.

* * *

Because that was the thing about storms. Everyone looks up.

In a hundred houses across the Nightmarket, faces appeared at glass. On the seawall, the watch paused mid-patrol. In a rented room above a chandler's shop, a woman with patient gray eyes sat up in bed, fully dressed despite the hour, and watched lightning strike the same patch of harbor three times in patterns too deliberate to be natural, and reached for her ledger.

Every bolt was a signature. Every gust was a confession. There had been exactly one person in Veymark capable of this, ten years ago, and the crown had burned his name into its records with a special annotation: *survived.*

"Oh," Silas said, the exhilaration draining out of him like water through sand. "Oh, you idiot."

The storm, still singing around him, did not care. Storms never understood consequences; that had always been the trouble with them, and with the people who loved them. It wanted to keep going. It wanted the whole horizon. It could feel his joy and mistook it for permission.

"That's enough," Silas said. "Thank you. That's — thank you. We're done."

The sky sulked, but it obeyed, the way it always obeyed his mother, the way it apparently still obeyed her son. The rain gentled. The lightning withdrew into grumbling distance. And Silas stood on his rooftop in the sudden ordinary drizzle, shaking, hollowed out, lit up from inside like a paper lantern, knowing exactly what he had just done.

He had saved her. And he had written his name across the entire sky while doing it.

By dawn, every spy, informer, and ambitious neighbor in Veymark would have reported the same thing: the storm-caller lives. By noon, Dame Rosalind Crane would have a description, a district, and a decade of charm-sales to work backward through. By tomorrow night, his face would be on the walls next to hers.

Silas sat down on the wet roof tiles and laughed — a small, unhinged, exhausted sound.

"Worth it," he told the city below him. "You hear me? Worth it."

The city, being Veymark, declined to comment.

* * *

He found her an hour later, or rather she found him — because of course she did, because the girl had the survival instincts of a harbor rat and twice the cunning. She came out of the shadows of the customhouse ruins with her hair plastered to her skull and her eyes enormous, and she looked at him standing there dripping on the seawall steps, and she said:

"That was you."

It wasn't a question. Lightning answers lightning; she had felt his storm the way you feel someone call your name across a crowded room.

"That was me," Silas admitted.

"You said—" Her voice wobbled, and she hated it, he could see her hating it. "You told me you couldn't anymore. You told me it was gone."

"I lied," Silas said. "I'm a charm-seller. Lying is most of the trade."

She stared at him. Rain ran off her chin. Behind her, the harbor heaved and settled, and the last of his storm muttered away over the open sea like an old man grumbling about the young.

"Why?" she said finally. "Why break your rules? After ten years?"

Silas considered several answers — the practical one, the noble one, the funny one — and discarded them all, because she'd earned better than his best material.

"Because you were running," he said, "and I was tired of watching you run alone."

Ulyana's face did something complicated. Then she crossed the space between them and hugged him, quick and fierce and soaking wet, the way you hug someone when you're afraid the hug might be the last free thing you get to do.

"They'll know now," she said into his coat. "Whoever made that storm. They'll come looking."

"Yes," Silas said.

"And they'll find you."

"Almost certainly."

She pulled back and glared at him with an expression he recognized intimately, because he'd been wearing it in mirrors for a decade: the fury of someone who has just been sacrificed for and cannot bear it.

"You're an idiot," she informed him.

"Frequently," he agreed. "Come on. We can't go back to the stall, and we can't stay by the water. I know a place."

"Whose place?"

"An herbwife named Mother Ives. She owes me for forty years of fever-charms and pretends she doesn't." He started up the seawall steps, and she fell in beside him, matching his stride the way she always did, as if they'd been walking together all their lives instead of nine accidental years.

Above them, far out over the dark water, the clouds were already rebuilding themselves — patient, inevitable, the way debts always were.

And somewhere behind them, in a warm room above the chandler's shop, a candle had just been lit, and a ledger opened, and a pen dipped in ink.

Dame Rosalind Crane wrote a single line, underlined it twice, and smiled.

*Storm-caller confirmed. Begin at the Nightmarket.*
