## 9. The Coven Below

The herbwife's shop smelled like every argument between a garden and a kitchen at once.

Ulyana sat on a stool too short for her legs, wrapped in a blanket that smelled of camphor and lavender and something sharper underneath—feverfew, maybe, or the memory of fevers. Her boots were drying by the stove, steaming gently, and her toes had only just stopped feeling like ten small betrayals.

"You'll take the tea," said Mother Ives, "or you'll take the fever. Those are your options, girl, and I've already buried three people this winter who thought they knew better."

"I'm not sick."

"You ran across half of Veymark in the rain with lightning coming out of your skin. That's not health, that's a dare." Ives pressed a clay cup into her hands. It was hot enough to hurt, which Ulyana suspected was the point. "Drink."

Ulyana drank. It tasted like boiled earth and honey and something that made her tongue go briefly numb. She decided not to ask what was in it. There were questions you asked an old woman who'd hidden you from soldiers, and there were questions you kept behind your teeth like coins.

The shop above was narrow and cluttered and honest-looking: bundles of dried things hanging from the rafters, jars labeled in a crabbed hand, a cat asleep in a basket of chamomile as if it were being paid to do so. But below—

Below was another matter entirely.

Ives had led her down through a trapdoor behind the drying racks, down a stair so steep it was nearly a ladder, into a cellar that was not a cellar. The walls were old stone, older than Veymark's walls, older than anything the crown claimed to have built. And the room itself was full of people.

Not many. A dozen, maybe fifteen, sitting on crates and upturned barrels around a single lamp. A woman with burn-scarred hands who moved them constantly, as if playing an invisible instrument. A boy not much older than Ulyana whose shadow, she noticed after a long moment, did not quite match his body—it leaned when he stood straight. An enormous man knitting something green and furious, needles clicking like insects.

They had all gone quiet when she came down the stairs. Not hostile-quiet. Hungry-quiet. Like cats watching a door someone might open.

"Sit," Ives had said, and now Ulyana sat, upstairs again, tea in hand, while the herbwife pretended to sort valerian and actually watched her the way a fisherman watches a line.

"So," Ulyana said at last. "You're going to tell me what this is. Or I'm going to start guessing, and my guesses are usually worse than the truth."

Ives snorted. "Usually?"

"Once I guessed a smuggler was hiding treasure in his boat and it turned out to be his mother-in-law."

"And what did you do?"

"Charged him for my silence. He paid in dried fish." Ulyana wrapped both hands around the cup. "What is this, Mother Ives? Who were those people?"

The old woman set down her herbs. She took her time about it, aligning each bundle just so, and Ulyana recognized the performance for what it was—a person giving herself a moment to decide how much truth she could afford.

"They call themselves the Remnant," Ives said. "Which is a fancy word for *leftovers*. What's left of the gifted folk of Veymark, and the coast, and the Grey marches, after the crown spent thirty years burning us out of existence. Weavers. Storm-callers. Hedge-witches. A bone-reader or two, though they're more trouble than they're worth." She glanced up. "And one very stubborn herbwife who never claimed to be anything but good with plants, which is technically true and legally useless."

"The crown says magic is outlawed because it's dangerous."

"The crown says a great many things." Ives lowered herself onto the stool opposite, joints cracking like green wood. "The crown said the storm-line was a threat to the realm. The storm-line were farmers and sailors and midwives, mostly. People who could call rain to a dry field and calm a squall before it ate a fishing boat. Dangerous the way bread is dangerous, if you've decided everyone should starve."

Ulyana's hands tightened on the cup. "You know about the storm-line."

"Girl." Ives's voice went gentle, which was somehow worse than sharp. "When lightning answers a child's singing, everyone old enough to remember learns the words for it within the week. You don't think the Remnant heard?"

Ulyana looked down into the tea. Her reflection wobbled there, small and drowned-looking. "Then those people downstairs. They know what I am."

"They know what you *might* be. Which, in their arithmetic, amounts to the same thing."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," said a voice from the stairs, "that we've been waiting eleven years for a miracle, and miracles are terrible at keeping appointments."

Ulyana turned. The man descending the ladder-stair was tall and thin and gray all over—gray hair, gray coat, gray eyes that caught the lamplight like coins at the bottom of a well. He moved carefully, precisely, the way a chess player moves pieces he's already decided are lost. Behind him came two others: the woman with the scarred hands, and the boy with the wrong shadow.

"Mother Ives," the gray man said, inclining his head with a courtesy so smooth it felt rehearsed. "Thank you for sending word."

"I sent word that the girl needed rest, Corvess. Not an audience."

"And she shall have rest. After." Corvess turned to Ulyana and smiled, and it was a good smile, warm at the edges, practiced at the center. "You must be exhausted. Frightened. Hunted by Dame Rosalind Crane herself, if the whispers are true—and they usually are; Crane has a gift for making whispers. You've lost everything in three days. Your home, your safety, the man who raised you—" he paused delicately, "—or sheltered you, at least. And now strangers pull you into cellars and make you drink things." He gestured at her cup. "Is it valerian and willow bark? Ives, honestly, you could sedate a horse."

"It's my recipe," Ives said flatly, "and it's none of your business."

"You see?" Corvess spread his hands toward Ulyana, as if presenting evidence. "Everyone here argues. Everyone here disagrees loudly and often. We are not an army, whatever the crown thinks. We are survivors who happen to share a cellar." He pulled a crate over and sat, unhurried, folding his long frame onto it like a heron settling. "May I ask you a question, Ulyana?"

"You already know my name."

"I know a great deal about you. That's different from knowing *you*. May I ask?"

Ulyana shrugged, which she had learned young was cheaper than yes.

"When the lightning comes," Corvess said, "what does it feel like?"

She hadn't expected that. She'd expected demands, oaths, the whole weight of a secret bloodline landing on her shoulders like wet wool. Instead—a question about the inside of her own head, which almost nobody had ever asked.

"Warm," she said slowly. "No. Not warm. Like—the moment before you sneeze. Everything pulls tight and then lets go all at once. And it feels like it knows where it wants to go. Like it's been waiting outside a door for years and finally someone opens it."

Something flickered across the scarred woman's face. Recognition, Ulyana thought. Or grief wearing recognition's coat.

"That," Corvess said softly, "is exactly how the last storm-caller described it. Before Greyford. Before the purges." He leaned forward. "Child, do you know what you carry?"

"A curse, according to some. A death sentence, according to the criers."

"A *crown*." The word dropped into the room like a stone into still water. "The storm-line didn't rule by conquest, whatever the histories say. They ruled because the old crown chose them—chose its bearer, generation after generation, and no army could take it from them, and no pretender could wear it. It answered to their blood and their worthiness and nothing else. When the last of them died at Greyford, the crown went silent. Dormant. Waiting." His gray eyes held hers, patient as tide. "Waiting, we believe, for you."

The tea had gone cold in Ulyana's hands. She set it down on the floor very carefully, because her fingers had started doing the tight-sneeze-pull thing, and the last thing anyone needed was her boiling it over or shattering it or lighting the lamp without meaning to.

"That's a story," she said. "A nice story. Stories get orphans killed."

"Yes," Corvess agreed pleasantly. "So does being an orphan with nowhere to hide. Consider your options honestly. The crown wants you registered or burned. The Saltwrights want to sell you. Dame Crane is patient and thorough and kind, which makes her the most dangerous creature in this city. And Silas Fairweather—" he watched her flinch and noted it, filed it away, she could see him do it, "—Silas Fairweather called a storm two nights ago that announced your existence to every gifted ear and every listening spy from here to the capital. Whatever his intentions, and I grant they may be tender ones, he has painted a target on you the size of the sky."

"He saved my life."

"He endangered it magnificently. Both things can be true." Corvess folded his hands. "We offer you what nobody else can. Shelter. Training. People who understand what's happening inside you instead of flinching from it. The boy behind me set fire to his family's house at age six because no one taught him his shadow wasn't supposed to move. Maren here—" the scarred woman nodded, "—can mend broken bone with her hands, and spent nine years pretending to be a midwife because it was the only healing anyone would permit her. We are your people, Ulyana. Not by law. By *kind*."

It landed somewhere soft and unguarded, and Ulyana hated how well it landed. Three days of running, of wet stone and colder alleys, of hearing boots behind her that were sometimes real and sometimes just her own heartbeat. And here was a warm cellar and a cat upstairs and a dozen people who looked at her like she was a sunrise instead of a wildfire.

Belonging, offered on a plate.

She was twelve when she learned that plates could be pulled away. She was thirteen when she learned some plates came with bills hidden underneath.

"What's the price?" she asked.

Corvess blinked. "Price?"

"There's always a price. Bread costs copper. Silence costs fish. Kindness costs—" she waved a hand, "—something. Nobody keeps a cellar full of fugitives out of the goodness of their hearts. So. What do you want from me?"

For a moment the only sound was the cat upstairs turning over in its chamomile, a faint thump through the floorboards. Ives was looking at Corvess with an expression that said *she's sharper than you expected, isn't she*, and enjoying it immensely.

"Honest girl," Corvess said, and he sounded genuinely pleased, which unsettled her more than anger would have. "Very well. Honestly, then. We want what any remnant wants. To survive. And more than survive—to end the thing that's killing us. The Edict of Cleansing won't stop at Veymark. It will roll across the whole coast until there isn't a gifted soul left unregistered or unburned. We intend to stop it. And to stop it, we need legitimacy. A cause the common folk can rally to. A symbol the old stories still speak of." He smiled again, gentle as snowfall. "We need the storm-line, Ulyana. We need *you*."

"As what? A banner?"

"As a queen."

The word hung there. Ulyana found, to her own surprise, that she wanted to laugh. "Me. I sleep under bridges. I once fought a gull for a sausage and lost."

"Gulls are vicious," the boy with the wrong shadow said solemnly. "Don't be ashamed."

"Tam." Maren elbowed him.

"I'm serious," Ulyana said, standing, because sitting made her feel small and standing only slightly less so. "I don't want a throne. I don't want a war. I want—" and here her voice betrayed her, cracking on the plainness of it, "—I want to not be hunted. I want Silas. I want to eat something that isn't tea. That's the whole list. That's everything."

"And you may yet have all of it," Corvess said. "Thrones are strange instruments. Sometimes they exist to be broken." He rose, unhurried, and for a moment his courtesy slipped and she saw the thing underneath—not cruelty, nothing so simple, but a certainty so complete it had worn grooves into him, like water wearing stone. "You needn't answer tonight. Rest. Eat. Learn our names. All we ask—all any of us asks—is that you stay. Under our roof. Among your own kind. Where no one can reach you."

*Where no one can reach you,* Ulyana thought. *Including me.*

But the cellar was warm, and the blanket smelled of lavender, and the boy was offering her bread with an earnestness that suggested he'd fought gulls himself and understood. And she was so tired. Tired down in the marrow, tired in a way sleep didn't fix anymore.

"Stay," Ives said quietly, when the others had gone back down the ladder-stair, their voices fading into the old stone. "Not for them. For you. You can stay under my roof and think your own thoughts, girl, whatever Corvess believes. Old women keep their own cellars." A dry pause. "Mostly."

Ulyana looked at the trapdoor, at the lamplight leaking up through its seams, at the warmth rising from below like an invitation and a debt in equal measure.

"One condition," she said.

Ives raised an eyebrow.

"Nobody tells me what my lightning is *for*." She hugged the blanket tighter. "It's mine. Whatever else it is—crown, curse, symbol—it came to *me*, before any of you knew I existed. So I'll learn. I'll even fight, if it comes to that. But the day somebody tries to aim me like an arrow—" she lifted her chin, and somewhere far overhead, thunder grumbled agreement, low and casual, like a large dog clearing its throat, "—they'd better stand well back."

Ives studied her for a long moment. Then the old woman laughed, a real laugh, rusty and delighted, slapping her knee hard enough to raise dust from her apron.

"Oh, the storm-line always were contrary," she said. "Welcome home, child. Mind the third stair; it bites."

* * *

That night, in the narrow cot behind the drying racks, Ulyana slept deeper than she had in three days, and dreamed of a crown of storm-light resting on empty air, waiting, patient as rain.

She woke near dawn to voices below—low, urgent, filtered through old stone. She should have stayed in bed. Curiosity had gotten her into every bad situation of her life, and it got her out of the cot now, barefoot on cold boards, easing the trapdoor open a finger's width.

Corvess's voice, taut as wire: "—Crane has taken rooms above the Nightmarket. She's interviewing shopkeepers. And her first appointment yesterday—" a pause, papers rustling, "—was a charm-seller. Gray-haired. One wrist scarred from an old binding."

Ulyana's heart stopped, stumbled, restarted.

"She bought a luck-knot," Corvess continued, "and held his hand a moment too long. She knows him, Ives. She's *close* to him. And through him, she may already be close to us."

Through the gap in the floorboards, lamplight swayed. Somewhere above the Nightmarket, in a rented room, a patient woman was writing names in a ledger.

And Silas's name, Ulyana understood with cold certainty, was already in it.
