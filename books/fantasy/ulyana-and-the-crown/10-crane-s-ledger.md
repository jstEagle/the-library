## 10. Crane's Ledger

The Saltwright they'd fished out of the harbor was called Petch, and he cried before Dame Rosalind Crane had even asked him a question.

She noted this in her ledger. *Prisoner weeps readily. May be genuine fear; may be performance. Test with silence.*

Silence was her favorite instrument. She had learned it from her mother, who had never once raised her voice and had never once needed to. Rosalind had inherited the stillness along with the hazel eyes and the unfortunate chin, and she had spent thirty years sharpening all three into tools.

So she sat across the table from Petch and said nothing while he wept, and said nothing while he stopped weeping, and said nothing while he began, in the manner of all men left alone with their own noise, to talk.

"—didn't do nothing, madam, that's the truth of it, I run cargo, honest cargo, rope and salt fish and—"

"Rope," Crane said mildly, "is not usually smuggled."

Petch blinked at her as though she'd spoken in tongues.

"Unless," she continued, turning a page, "one is smuggling it *out* of somewhere it is needed. Or *in* to somewhere it is forbidden. Which is it?"

"It's just rope."

"Mm." She wrote something. It didn't matter what. The writing itself did the work; prisoners watched the pen the way birds watch a cat's tail, and their confessions moved wherever the pen seemed to point. She had broken harder men than Petch with less than a raised eyebrow. She had once extracted a full confession from a bishop using only a teacup and the phrase *"go on."*

But Petch was not why she had requested this particular prisoner. Petch was a thread, and Crane had spent two weeks in Veymark learning to pull threads, and this one led somewhere interesting.

"Tell me about the charm-seller," she said.

* * *

The Nightmarket kept its own hours and its own laws, or so its vendors believed. Crane had taken rooms above a pie shop on the third day of her arrival — chosen deliberately, because the widow who owned it saw everything and repeated most of it — and she had spent her evenings below among the stalls, buying things she did not want, listening to people who did not know they were speaking.

Veymark was a city that had outlawed magic and then, in the way of cities, had quietly renegotiated the terms. Magic for war: burned. Magic for profit: taxed. Magic for luck-knots and fever-charms sold at a copper apiece from a folding table: tolerated by everyone except, apparently, the Crown, whose Edict of Cleansing had arrived that week like weather nobody wanted but everybody had expected.

Crane had heard the criers. She had watched the market empty. And she had noticed one stall that emptied faster than the rest — as if its owner had known.

The charm-seller's name, according to the pie-shop widow, was Fairweather. Silas Fairweather. Ten years in Veymark. Kept to himself. Paid his harbor bribes on time. Fed a stray dog, fed a stray child, and — here the widow had lowered her voice with the relish of a woman who had waited years for someone official to tell — *never aged.*

"He's got a face like wet paper now," the widow admitted, "but when he first come, oh, ten year back, he looked thirty. Looks fifty now. That's aging, I suppose. But wrongways. Like a candle burning down too fast."

Crane had thanked her and bought a pie she did not eat.

Now she sat across from Petch, who had sold rope and other things for the Saltwright crew, and she opened her ledger to a fresh page, and she began, with the patience of a woman building a cathedral out of matchsticks, to reconstruct a decade.

* * *

"Fairweather," Petch said. "Yeah. Everyone knew him. Bought passage off him once—" he caught himself. "I mean, he *bought passage*. Off us. To nowhere. Never went."

"Why pay for passage and not take it?"

Petch's mouth worked. "In case. You buy the door before you need the door. Captain Bren always said Fairweather paid like a man keeping a horse saddled."

Crane wrote: *Maintains escape option. Has maintained it for years. A man planning to flee does not feed orphans.*

"When did you last see him?"

"Night the edict got read out. He came down to the Saltwright warehouse after dark. Soaked through. Wanted papers. Forged ones. Clean ones. For himself and—" Petch stopped.

"For himself and?"

Another pause. This one had weight. Crane set down her pen and folded her hands, which was worse than any pen.

"Petch," she said gently, "you have already told my sergeant everything you know. He has it written down. What happens in this room is not whether you tell me, but whether you tell me *usefully*, and useful men eat hot meals and sleep in dry bunks, and useless men go on the transport south. I would prefer you useful. I suspect you would prefer it more."

"There was a girl," Petch said miserably. "He wanted papers for a girl."

"What girl?"

"Dunno her name. Street kid. From the Nightmarket. He's been slipping her bread for years — everyone knew that too, thought he was soft in the head, feeding strays. But that night he wanted two sets of papers and he wanted them fast, and Bren—" Petch flinched. "Bren named a price."

"Which was?"

"The girl."

Crane waited.

"Bren reckoned the crown's paying for gifted folk now. Registration's one price, but a *live capture*, an unregistered gift-bearer brought in voluntary-like — there's bounty above that. And if the girl's worth more than papers, then whatever Fairweather's protecting, it's worth more than he is. Bren figured the girl was the treasure. Sell the treasure, keep the friend, everyone eats." Petch shrugged the shrug of a man describing arithmetic rather than sin. "It were just business."

"I see," said Crane, and she did see, and what she saw made her write in small, neat letters: *Captain Bren. Smuggler. Will sell anything, including allies. Note for later: men like Bren are useful exactly once.*

"And Fairweather's answer?"

"He said no. Said he'd pay double for the papers alone. Bren said the price weren't coin." Petch rubbed his face. "Then Fairweather went quiet, and the lamps flickered, and the rain started coming sideways, and I ain't never seen rain come sideways off a flat sea, madam, not without—" He stopped, because the sentence he was building toward was a confession, and even Petch could hear the shape of it.

"Without what?" Crane asked softly.

"Without somebody *calling* it," Petch whispered. "Storm-calling's death-penalty magic. Old war magic. They hanged the last storm-callers at Greyford, my da watched it done. There ain't none left. There *can't* be none left."

"No," Crane agreed. "There can't."

She turned the page.

* * *

That night, in her room above the pie shop, Crane laid out her ledger and read it end to end, the way she read everything end to end, twice, because the second reading was where lies revealed themselves — not by confessing, but by growing tired.

The entries, in order:

*Aged ten years in appearance over ten years in residence. Consistent with sustained magical expenditure.* She underlined *sustained*. Small magics, coin-charms and luck-knots, cost nothing. Something had been draining this man steadily for a decade, and it wasn't his trade.

*Feeds an orphan. Regularly. Discreetly. Disguised indifference — the widow reports he pretends to shoo the girl off while leaving bread where she'll find it.* Crane had interviewed enough parents to know the gesture. It was the gesture of a man who had decided, at some point, that a specific child was his problem, and had then discovered that problems of that kind are not solvable, only carried.

*Bought passage annually. Never used it.* Not indecision. Insurance. A man does not saddle a horse for ten years unless he expects, someday, to ride hard.

*Sought forged papers for two, urgently, the night the edict was read.* He had known before the criers. Of course he had known; the market had emptied around him like water finding a drain. But knowing was common that night. Urgency of *this* shape — two papers, one adult, one child, no negotiation on the child — meant the child was not an afterthought. The child was the cargo.

*Refused to surrender the child at any price. Then, allegedly, called a storm.* Unverified. Storms happened. But sideways rain off a flat sea, precisely at the moment of refusal, precisely over a smugglers' warehouse... Crane did not believe in coincidence. Coincidence was what lazy investigators wrote in their ledgers instead of work.

She sat back and looked at the pattern the way a cartographer looks at coastlines, and the pattern resolved, and it was simple, and simplicity in her experience was almost always the truth wearing plain clothes.

Silas Fairweather had been protecting one specific person for years. Not the city — a man who called storms to drown his own creditors was not sentimental about cities. Not the Remnant, if the Remnant even existed outside rumor; a network would have spread the risk, and Fairweather hoarded risk like a dragon hoards gold. One person. One child. And every kindness, every charm, every copper, every saddled horse, every sideways rain — all of it, the whole decade, bent around keeping that one child alive and unnoticed.

Which raised the only question that mattered, and Crane wrote it at the top of a fresh page in letters larger than her usual hand:

*Why her?*

There were thousands of orphans in Veymark. Charm-sellers did not buy annual passage for thousands of orphans. Men did not burn a decade of their own life — because that was what the drained face meant, she was certain of it now, the man was spending *himself* — for a random stray. Either Fairweather loved the girl, which was possible and investigable, or the girl was *worth* something, which was likelier and far more dangerous.

And there was a third possibility, the one that made Crane trim her lamp low and check the lock on her door, though she'd have denied checking it if asked.

The third possibility was that the girl was worth something *to history.*

* * *

In the morning she returned to Petch, who had slept, eaten, and recovered enough of his courage to attempt bargaining.

"If I give you summat good, madam, real good — the transport south, that's off? Because south's swamp fever country, and I've got a bad knee."

"Petch," Crane said, pouring him tea she had brought herself, because the prison tea was an abomination and she had standards, "the transport south is for men who waste my time. You are not wasting my time. Therefore you are not going south. Tell me something good and you'll have bread with the tea."

"That *is* good, that is," Petch said, visibly recalculating his entire life against the price of bread. He drank. He wiped his mouth. "The girl," he said. "The one Fairweather wanted papers for. I seen her. Before, I mean. At the market."

"Describe her."

"Young. Twelve, thirteen maybe. Dark hair, always half-soaked, Veymark being Veymark. Quick hands. Fairweather'd give her bread and she'd give him cheek, and he'd take it, which nobody else coulda got away with." Petch frowned, dredging. "One thing, though. One thing I remember clear, 'cause it scared me and I don't scare easy, I've been knifed twice."

Crane leaned forward slightly. "Yes?"

"Thunderstorm, maybe three summers back. Whole market running for cover, and the girl just — stood there. In it. Arms out. Face up. Laughing." Petch's voice dropped. "And the lightning come down close, madam. Close. Close as a hangman's rope to a tree. And it didn't touch her. Curved round her like she was a stone in a river. And she laughed *harder*."

Crane sat very still.

Lightning avoided her. Lightning, which did not avoid anything, which struck church spires and sleeping cattle and the tallest man in a field with the impartial appetite of a natural law — lightning had curved around a street child like water around a stone.

She wrote it down. Her pen pressed hard enough to emboss the next three pages.

*Subject displays passive affinity: atmospheric phenomena decline to strike her. Suggests inheritance, not training. Trained mages command; blood remembers.* And beneath it, because she was honest with herself even when honesty was inconvenient:

*The storm-line was exterminated at Greyford twenty-two years ago. Officially: completely.*

Officially complete exterminations had a poor survival rate as historical claims. Crane had read the Greyford records during her ascent through the Inquisition — she read everything; it was considered eccentric and had slowed her promotion twice, and repaid her a hundred times since. She remembered a discrepancy. A small one. A list of the executed that numbered forty-one souls, and a burial registry from Greyford parish that recorded forty graves dug.

One grave short. One body unaccounted. Filed, in those days, under *clerical error*, and forgotten by everyone except a junior inquisitor with a habit of finishing other people's sums.

She had been that junior. She had never forgotten.

* * *

"Madam?" Petch was watching her. "You've gone all still. Like a heron."

"I'm thinking, Petch. It looks like stillness. Drink your tea."

Because here was the shape of it now, whole, terrible, elegant: a storm-caller exiled from a war he'd survived, hiding in the city that had outlawed his kind, spending ten years feeding and shielding and *insuring* a child whom lightning refused to touch. A child of the right age. A child of the right blood. The grave that was never dug, walking around Veymarket with rain in her hair, eating bread bought with a hunted man's last coins.

And the Crown had just declared, in ink and iron, that every gifted soul must register or burn — which meant that somewhere in the machinery of state, someone either knew what the girl was, or was about to find out, and neither possibility ended quietly.

Crane closed the ledger.

Her orders were clear. Find the unregistered. Register or burn. She had served the Crown for thirty years and had never once found the orders unclear, and she distrusted, professionally, how unclear these felt.

"Thank you, Petch," she said, standing. "You've been useful. Bread and tea daily. And Petch — if Captain Bren visits your dreams, report it. Men like Bren sell everything eventually. I'd rather buy first."

"Aye, madam. And — and the charm-seller, will you catch him?"

"Oh," Crane said, with the mild certainty of a woman who had caught bishops and smugglers and one memorable serial poisoner who had disguised himself as his own victims' grief counselor, "I intend to. But not for the reason you think."

She gathered her ledger, nodded to the sergeant at the door, and paused in the corridor, where the light through the barred window fell in gray stripes across the stone.

Ten years of charms. Ten years of bread. Ten years of a saddled horse never ridden, until the night it had to be. Every entry in her ledger pointed the same direction now, and the direction was a flooded cellar, a laughing child in a thunderstorm, and a question the Crown had buried in a clerical error twenty-two years ago.

*Why her?* She had her answer. What she did not yet have — what kept her standing in that striped light, tapping the ledger's edge against her knuckles — was whether the answer would save the girl or condemn her. That depended on what the Remnant knew, and where the herbwife Mother Ives had hidden her, and whether Crane could reach either before the other side of her own conscience woke up.

Somewhere in Veymark, an old woman grew herbs over a cellar door. Crane had a name, a district, and a growing suspicion that the most dangerous thing in this city was not the storm-caller, nor the child, nor even the edict.

It was whoever got there first.

She buttoned her coat against the drizzle and went out into the street, humming — her mother's habit, adopted wholesale, along with the stillness — and the tune was an old one, and the old women at the market who recognized it said it was a lullaby from before the war.

From before Greyford.

From the storm-line's own country.

Crane did not hum it on purpose. But she noticed, because she noticed everything, that she had been humming it ever since the day she'd read the name *Fairweather* on a market license.

And she wondered, for the first time in thirty years of clean, orderly service, whether some part of her had known the answer before her ledger did.
