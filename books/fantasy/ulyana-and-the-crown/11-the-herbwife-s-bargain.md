## 11. The Herbwife's Bargain

The herbwife's shop smelled of thyme and old secrets, which was to say it smelled like every other shop in Veymark, except that the secrets were better organized.

Silas stood in the doorway for a long moment, letting the bell above the frame finish its small silver complaint. Rain dripped off his coat and pooled on the flagstones. Somewhere behind the hanging bundles of sage and feverfew, a kettle was singing.

"You'll catch your death standing there," said Mother Ives, without turning around. "And then who'd buy my comfrey? Dead men have no coin."

"I'm told dead men have excellent credit," Silas said. "They never argue about the price."

She came around the counter at last. She was smaller than her reputation — everyone in Veymark was smaller than their reputation; the city ran on exaggeration the way other cities ran on grain — but her eyes were not small at all. They were the eyes of someone who had spent sixty years deciding what people needed before they knew it themselves, and had been right often enough to become insufferable about it.

"So," she said. "The storm-caller comes to my door at last."

"Not the storm-caller." He wiped his boots on the mat, an old habit, politeness as camouflage. "Just a charm-seller with a problem."

"Charm-sellers don't get followed by three different sets of watchers between the Nightmarket and here." She poured tea without asking whether he wanted any, into a cup that had been waiting, he noticed, on the second saucer. "I counted four, actually, but one of them was mine, so we won't count him against you."

Silas sat down. The chair was hard, wooden, deliberate. Everything in this room was deliberate. The dried herbs hung in careful rows, and he understood suddenly that they weren't decoration — they were inventory, and some of them were not herbs at all, or not only herbs, and the arrangement of them was a language he only half remembered how to read.

"You know why I've come," he said.

"I know why you think you've come." She pushed the cup toward him. "Drink. You look like ten miles of bad weather."

"That's generous. I feel like twenty."

He drank. The tea was bitter and warming and almost certainly good for him, which annoyed him on principle.

"The girl," Ives said, when he'd set the cup down. "Ulyana."

The name landed in his chest like a dropped stone. "You know about her."

"Child, I've known about her since she started buying bread from you with coins she found in gutters." Ives settled into the chair across from him, folding her hands. "You thought you were being subtle. A grown man slipping half-loaves to an orphan every market day for six years, and pretending each time it was an accident of arithmetic? Subtle as a landslide, that was. The whole street knew. The whole street said nothing, because the whole street liked you, and because the whole street understood that some kindnesses die if you look at them straight on."

"She's just a girl," Silas said.

"She is the last living heir of the storm-line," Ives said, gently, the way one might correct a child's arithmetic, "and she sings lightning, and the crown has posted a bounty on her existence that would buy this entire quarter twice over. But yes. Also just a girl."

The rain thickened against the window. Silas looked at his own hands — charm-maker's hands, scarred and ordinary, hands that had spent ten years pretending to be exactly what they appeared to be — and felt the familiar guilt settle over his shoulders like a wet coat.

"I need protection for her," he said. "Real protection. Not luck-knots. The Saltwrights sold me out, Crane is two doors from finding her, and the Remnant—" he hesitated.

"The Remnant is what you came for," Ives finished. "Say it. It costs nothing to say."

"The Remnant can hide her. The catacombs, the network, the safehouses. No one else in this city can."

"No one else can," she agreed. "And what are you offering?"

Here it was, then. Silas had rehearsed this on the walk over, in the space between raindrops, and every rehearsal had ended the same way: with him saying the thing he'd sworn on Greyford's mud never to say again.

"My service."

Ives raised an eyebrow. It was a magnificent eyebrow. It had probably ended negotiations before.

"Elaborate."

"I was a storm-caller before I was anything else. A real one, trained, battle-tested. What I did on the rooftops three nights ago — that wasn't even a tenth of what I can do when I stop being careful." He turned his cup slowly on its saucer. "You're planning something. Don't insult us both by denying it; I saw Corvess's people moving supplies through the tannery tunnels last month, and I've seen how the safehouse traffic has changed. You're going to hit back. And when you do, you'll want someone who can pull a storm out of a clear sky."

"And in exchange?"

"In exchange, Ulyana disappears. Completely. New name, new face if it comes to it, tucked somewhere even your own people can't find her without my say-so. She wants nothing to do with crowns or covens or wars. She's twelve years old, Mother Ives. Twelve. Somebody in this arrangement has to remember that."

For a moment the herbwife said nothing. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, someone's cart rattled past through the puddles, and the whole room seemed to lean toward the sound and then away.

"You love her," Ives said at last. It wasn't a question, and it wasn't quite an accusation either.

"She eats too much bread and lies about practicing her letters. There's very little to love."

"Mm." Ives rose and went to the window, peering out at the rain with the expression of a woman checking on a crop. "Let me tell you what you're actually offering, Fairweather, because I don't think you know. You're offering to be a weapon. Weapons don't choose their battles. When Corvess says burn, you burn. When he says the depot tonight, the depot tonight — no vetting, no second thoughts, no standing in the rain deciding who deserves to live. That's the bargain. That's what serving the Remnant means."

"I understand the terms."

"Do you?" She turned. "Because I've watched you for ten years, storm-caller. You're the most indecisive merciful man I have ever met. You weigh every kindness like a jeweler weighs gold. And now you want to hand yourself to a war committee and pretend it's strategy?" She shook her head. "It isn't strategy. It's sacrifice wearing strategy's coat. And those fit differently, whatever the tailors tell you."

Silas felt the words go in deeper than he wanted them to. "If it keeps her safe—"

"If it keeps her safe," Ives agreed, "it's worth doing anyway. That's the cruelty of loving children. Every price becomes reasonable." She came back to the table and sat, and her voice changed — became businesslike, brisk, the voice of a woman who had bargained with smugglers and inquisitors and worse and buried most of them. "So. Terms."

"Terms."

"One: you serve the Remnant as a storm-weapon until such time as the council releases you. Not 'until Ulyana is safe' — until *released*. We decide when that is."

Silas's stomach tightened, but he nodded. "Two?"

"Two: you take no independent action. No private storms, no freelance rescues, no heroic nonsense. If you see an opportunity, you report it and wait for orders."

"That one I'll hate."

"Yes," Ives said placidly. "Three: the girl stays under our protection, but under *your* teaching. Corvess will object — he wants her shaped by the coven, wholly, from the start. You'll be her tutor in gift-work. Which means you'll be the voice in her ear, storm-caller, and you'd best make certain the things you whisper to her are worth hearing."

Something loosened slightly in his chest, and he distrusted the loosening immediately. It was, he realized, exactly the sort of concession that made a bad bargain feel like a good one.

"And in return," Ives continued, "she gets the catacombs. The full network. Papers, passage, sanctuary — everything the Remnant has, we give her. And when the fighting starts, if it starts, we spend our lives before we spend hers. On my word, and I am old enough that my word is nearly all I have left of value."

"On your word," Silas repeated.

"On my word." She extended her hand across the table. "Do we have a bargain, Silas Fairweather? Last chance to run. You're good at running. Ten years of practice."

He looked at her hand — small, spotted, steady — and thought of Ulyana's face when she'd shown him the lightning in the flooded cellar, terrified and luminous, trusting him absolutely. Thought of the proclamation nailed to every door. Thought of Dame Rosalind Crane's patient, courteous smile, and the ledger she was surely filling somewhere with the shape of his decade.

Strategy, he told himself. This is strategy. This is the move that saves her.

But Ives's eyes held his, and there was no deception possible between them, and both of them knew what this actually was. A man walking into a cage and calling the bars a fence.

"We have a bargain," Silas said, and took her hand.

Her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "Good," said Mother Ives. "Then welcome to the Remnant, weapon. Corvess will want to meet you tonight — he's been asking after you for weeks, and he does hate to be kept waiting." She released his hand and began clearing the cups, brisk again, already moving on. "Oh — and one more thing."

"What's that?"

"He'll ask you to swear the oath tonight. All of us swear it. Blood and salt and the names of the dead." She glanced back at him over her shoulder, and for just a moment the briskness fell away and he saw the woman underneath, tired and old and carrying more graves than she let show. "Swear it carefully, Fairweather. Oaths to desperate people bind tighter than oaths to kings. Kings forget. We don't."

* * *

He left the shop as the light was failing, and the rain had stopped, and the streets of Veymark shone like hammered tin.

Silas walked without direction for a while, the way a man does when his legs haven't yet agreed to what his mouth has signed. Past the shuttered chandlery. Past the fountain where Ulyana had once dared him to toss a copper and wish, and he'd wished, and she'd never known what for.

*That it would always be this simple,* he'd wished. *Bread and banter and her laughing at my terrible jokes.*

Ten years ago, after Greyford, he had made a different oath — a private one, sworn to nobody, in the smoking ruin of everything he'd been: *never again*. Never again would he be the thing that armies pointed at cities. Never again the weapon.

And here he stood, having signed himself over to be precisely that, and called it love, and called it sense.

*It IS strategy,* he told himself, walking. *The Remnant protects her. I protect the Remnant's interests. Everyone's incentives align. It's clean.*

It wasn't clean. He knew it wasn't clean the way you know a counterfeit coin — by the weight of it, slightly wrong, by the sound it makes when you drop it. Sacrifice dressed as strategy. Ives had named it, and naming a thing doesn't kill it, only makes it harder to ignore.

But there was bread to think of. A girl who ate too much of it. A bounty on her head and an inquisitor closing like a weather front.

There was no clean way to save anyone in Veymark anymore. There was only choosing which dirt to kneel in.

By the time he reached the herbhouse — Ives's real one, the refuge behind the tannery row, where Ulyana was waiting under the protection he'd just purchased — the first stars were out, thin and cold between the clouds.

Mother Ives opened the door before he knocked.

"He's here early," came Ulyana's voice from inside, bright and wary. "Is that bad? Silas, is that bad?"

And behind her, rising from the depths of the house like smoke given form, came the elder Corvess — tall, grey-robed, eyes like banked coals — and he was smiling.

"Storm-caller," Corvess said. "We have so much work to do. And so little time. Come in — the council convenes at moonrise, and there is a matter of a registration depot we must discuss."

Silas stepped over the threshold, and the door closed behind him like a verdict.
