## 12. Lessons in Lightning

The catacombs beneath Mother Ives's herbhouse smelled of wet stone, old candle smoke, and something faintly green—like the memory of a garden that had given up on gardens. Ulyana had lived in the Remnant's tunnels for nine days now, and she had learned three things: the dark down here was older than any darkness above; the bread was terrible; and Corvess never blinked as much as a normal person should.

She was thinking about the bread when the lightning came.

"Again," Corvess said.

Ulyana sighed and held out her hand. She thought about the storm the way he'd taught her—not as a thing she *did* but as a thing she *was*, a wire waiting for the sky to notice it. The spark crawled up her arm, gathered in her palm, and leapt—a bright, stuttering arc across the chamber to the iron ring he'd hung from the ceiling on a rusted chain.

It hit the ring. Mostly. A stray tendril licked sideways and set fire to a stack of very old hymnals, which Corvess extinguished with the weary efficiency of a man who had been setting out small fires for forty years.

"Better," he said.

"It hit the wrong thing."

"It hit." He turned from the smoking books, and his face did the thing it always did—the softening, the warmth, the look of a grandfather who had never once in his life been disappointed in anyone. "When you were pulled off the streets nine days ago, could you do that?"

"No. I just made things buzz."

"And now?" He gestured at the scorched ring, the smoking hymnals, the scorch-mark shaped vaguely like a rabbit that Ulyana refused to explain to anyone.

"Now I make things catch fire," she said.

"*Now* you make things obey," Corvess corrected gently. "There is a difference, and the difference is everything."

Ulyana looked down at her palm. The sparks had left little white traceries across her skin, like frost that burned instead of bit. Ten years of hiding this. Ten years of pressing her hands under blankets when storms rolled over Veymark so nobody would see them glow. And now there were people who *wanted* to see it. Who watched her practice with something like hunger and called it reverence.

She wasn't sure yet which one it actually was.

* * *

Mornings began with Mother Ives, who was a different kind of teacher entirely.

Ives didn't care about lightning. Ives cared about whether Ulyana had eaten, whether she'd slept, whether she knew which tunnel flooded first when the tide came up, and whether she'd apologized to Tamsin, the herbwife's other ward, whom Ulyana had accidentally static-charged so thoroughly that his hair stood on end for two days.

"He looked like a frightened dandelion," Ulyana had said, not sorry.

"You'll apologize anyway," Ives said, "because being powerful is no excuse for being careless with people smaller than you. That lesson comes free. Most of my others cost extra."

So mornings were bread (terrible), chores, lessons in herbs and tunnels and which guards on the night watch could be bribed with poppy wine and which ones couldn't be bribed with anything, which Ulyana found deeply suspicious. And afternoons—

Afternoons were Corvess.

He trained her in a wide chamber deep beneath the herbhouse, a vaulted room whose walls were carved with figures: men and women standing in storms, arms raised, crowns of lightning above their heads. The first time she'd seen them, she'd felt something twist in her chest—recognition, like finding your own face in an old family portrait.

"The storm-line," Corvess had said, watching her look. "Your ancestors. They ruled Veymark before the crown existed. Before the crown *betrayed* them." His voice had gone quiet in the way that made you lean in. "These carvings are four hundred years old, child. They've waited four hundred years for someone to stand beneath them again."

Now, on this particular afternoon, Ulyana stood beneath them trying to make lightning hold a shape.

"A ball," Corvess said. "Not a bolt. A bolt is a weapon you throw away. A ball is a weapon you *keep*. Control is the difference between a storm-caller and a girl who frightens cats."

"I like frightening cats," Ulyana said. "It's the only power I had before."

"You had more than you knew." He circled her slowly, hands clasped behind his back. "Hold it. Let it turn. Don't chase the spark—invite it."

Ulyana gritted her teeth. Inviting was harder than chasing. Chasing was instinct: the spark ran, she followed, they both ended up somewhere dramatic. Inviting meant standing still and trusting the storm to come to her, which felt about as sensible as standing still in front of a runaway cart.

But she tried. She opened her hand. She thought about the feeling from the cellar—that first night, singing to herself in the flooded dark because singing kept the fear quiet, and the lightning coming up through the water to answer like it had *missed* her.

The spark rose. It sat in her palm. It turned, slowly, a small furious star, and for three whole seconds it held its shape—and then it collapsed into a bolt that took out another hymnal.

Corvess clapped once, softly. "Three seconds. Last week you managed half of one."

"Last week I also set fire to Tamsin."

"Tamsin has recovered. His pride may take longer." But he was smiling, and the smile reached all the way up, and Ulyana felt the warm bloom in her chest that his smiles always produced. Pride. Real pride, hers, earned. Three seconds. She could feel how big three seconds could become.

"Again," he said.

* * *

She got to five seconds before supper, and she floated down to the kitchen on a cloud of her own accomplishment—which, in the catacombs, meant she walked into the middle of an argument.

"—can't keep training her blindfolded," Tamsin was saying. He was sixteen and had the permanent air of a boy arguing with the world and losing gracefully. "She doesn't know what we're *for*, Ives. You can't aim a weapon if it doesn't know the target."

"She's not a weapon," Ives said, without looking up from her mortar. She ground dried feverfew with the patience of geological process. "She's a twelve-year-old girl who likes frightening cats."

"She's the last storm-line in the world."

"She's a child who sleeps with a rag doll shaped like a fish."

"That's—" Tamsin threw up his hands. "That's exactly my point! Corvess is filling her head with crowns and ancestors and destiny, and nobody's told her what happens next. She deserves to know what she's being sharpened for."

Ulyana stood in the doorway, and the warm cloud inside her went suddenly cold.

"What happens next?" she asked.

Both of them turned. Ives's face did something complicated and settled into gentleness, which was worse than alarm. "Child. How long have you been standing there?"

"Long enough." Ulyana crossed her arms. "What am I being sharpened *for*, Tamsin?"

Tamsin opened his mouth, glanced at Ives, and visibly lost his nerve. "Ask Corvess," he muttered, and fled toward the storeroom with the dignity of a retreating army.

Ives put down her mortar. "Sit."

"I'm not hungry."

"You're always hungry. Sit anyway." Ulyana sat. Ives studied her for a long moment with those faded eyes that saw too much. "Corvess believes the crown must fall. He's believed it since Greyford, since they burned everyone he loved and called it cleansing. He is not lying to you, Ulyana. Remember that. He simply believes some truths can wait until after they're needed."

"That's not how truths work."

"No," Ives said. "It isn't." She pushed a heel of bread across the table—the good dark bread she hid from everyone else, which meant Ulyana was in trouble or in favor, possibly both. "Eat. And when he tells you what you're for, you come back and tell me exactly what words he used. Word for word. Corvess is a careful man, and careful men choose their words like weapons."

Ulyana ate the bread and thought about that.

* * *

Corvess chose his words that evening like a man laying out knives on velvet.

They sat in the chamber of carvings, by candlelight, on a stone bench worn smooth by centuries of sitting. He had brought tea, which he never did, and poured it himself, which he really never did, and Ulyana felt the ceremony of it settle over her like a cloak.

"Do you know what happened at Greyford?" he asked.

"Some battle. Before I was born."

"Not a battle." His voice was soft. "A massacre. Your grandmother's grandmother stood with us—the covens, the gifted, every soul in Veymark who carried a spark. We asked for nothing but the right to exist. The king answered with fire and edicts. Four thousand dead in a single night. They sang while they did it, Ulyana. Hymns. In the name of order." He let the silence sit. "Every person in these tunnels lost someone. I lost a wife and two daughters. Mother Ives lost a sister. Tamsin's mother died in the registration lines, waiting patiently, doing everything right, because she believed the law would protect her."

Ulyana's throat had gone tight. "I didn't know."

"Nobody tells children horrors. It's a kindness, usually." He turned to look at her, and the candlelight caught the deep seams of his face. "But you're not only a child anymore, are you? You stood in a flooded cellar and showed Silas Fairweather what lives in your blood. You've made lightning sit in your hand and *turn* when you ask it to. So I'll tell you what Tamsin was too frightened to say."

He leaned forward.

"The Remnant has spent twenty years surviving. Hiding. Burying our dead and keeping our heads down. And it has bought us nothing—the edicts tighten every year, the burnings continue, and now Crane sits above the Nightmarket writing names in a ledger, and every name is a promise of ash. Survival has failed, child. What remains is *reckoning*."

"The registration depots," Ulyana said quietly. She'd heard the whispers in the tunnels for days, plans moving through the dark like water. "You're going to strike one."

"We are going to strike the depot in the Tannery Quarter, where they keep the lists. Names, addresses, families marked for the pyre. Burn the lists, and hundreds live." His voice didn't rise at all; that was the terrible thing about Corvess, he never needed volume. "And you—you will stand with us when we do. Not fighting. Not yet. But *there*. Because when the last heir of the storm-line shows her face beside the Remnant, every gifted soul in this city will know the hiding is over. You won't be a weapon, Ulyana. You'll be a *banner*."

"A banner," Ulyana repeated.

Something in her chest swelled and hurt at the same time. She thought of ten years of ducking her glowing hands under blankets. She thought of the carvings above her—her ancestors, crowned in lightning, standing tall in their storms. She thought of Tamsin's mother dying patiently in a line, believing in rules that wanted her dead.

"They killed your family," Corvess said, gentle as snowfall. "They tried to kill you before you could even walk. Every spark you've ever hidden, every night you spent afraid of your own hands—that was theirs. The crown built that fear, brick by brick. Isn't it time it learned what it built?"

The lightning stirred under her skin without being invited. For once she didn't press it down.

"Yes," she said.

Corvess smiled, and it was the warmest smile she'd ever seen him wear, and she thought: *this is what having a grandfather must feel like.* She had never had one. She had never had anything except Silas's gruff, disguised tenderness and the taste of stolen bread.

"Five seconds today," he said, rising, gathering the teacups. "Tomorrow we'll try seven. And soon, child—soon you won't need to count at all."

He left her alone with the carvings and the candles, and Ulyana sat beneath her ancestors' stone faces and felt enormous. Chosen. Crowned already, almost, in everything but metal.

She didn't notice, wouldn't notice until much later, that Corvess had answered none of her questions—only replaced them with bigger, brighter ones. That *banner* was a word for a flag, and flags exist to be planted in things, and no one asks a flag where it wants to stand.

She climbed to her bunk humming. The rag doll fish watched her with its button eyes.

"Seven seconds tomorrow," she told it.

The fish said nothing. Fish were wise that way.

* * *

Two days later, Corvess brought her a cloak.

It was grey wool, hooded, plain—except along the hem, where someone had embroidered a pattern in silver thread: small branching lines, like lightning frozen mid-fall. Like roots. Like a family tree drawn in stormlight.

"For the Tannery Quarter," he said. "Wear it with the hood back. Let them see your face."

"When?" Ulyana asked. Her heart was hammering, half terror, half that swelling, hurting pride.

"Soon," Corvess said. "Very soon. There's one more lesson first." And he smiled his warmest smile, and somewhere far above them, muffled by fathoms of stone, thunder rolled over Veymark—and Ulyana, who had listened to storms her whole life, thought it sounded less like weather than like a warning.

She was learning to shape lightning. Nobody had yet taught her to shape the people holding the matches.
