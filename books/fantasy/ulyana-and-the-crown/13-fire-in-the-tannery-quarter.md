## 13. Fire in the Tannery Quarter

The night before the fire, Corvess fed them all stew.

It was good stew, too—barley and salt pork and turnips, the kind of thing that stuck to your ribs—and Ulyana thought later that this was the part she could never explain to anyone who hadn't been there. That the man who ordered the burning ladled out dinner like a grandmother, going down the line of the catacombs with the pot balanced on one arm, saying *eat, eat, you're skin and bone*, and pressing extra into the bowls of the youngest ones.

"You'll want a full belly tomorrow," he said, when he reached her corner. He set the bowl in her hands and did not move on right away. His eyes were pale as river ice, and they held hers with a gentleness that frightened her more than Dame Crane's ever had. "You'll want to remember, afterward, that we are people who feed each other."

"I don't understand what's happening tomorrow," Ulyana said.

"No," Corvess agreed pleasantly. "And it's better you don't ask. Asking is how the crown got its hooks into us the first time. Everyone wanted to *discuss*. To *negotiate*. And while they discussed, the pyres were already built." He tapped the rim of her bowl with one knuckle, a small bell-like sound. "Eat."

He moved on down the line. Behind him, Mother Ives caught Ulyana's eye across the candlelight and gave her a look that said, plainly: *you may not like where this is going either, little bird, but eat anyway.*

Ulyana ate.

* * *

The registration depot had been a granary once, back before the crown decided that counting people was easier than feeding them. It sat at the edge of the Tannery Quarter like a squat grey tooth, ringed with new timber palisades, and every morning a line of the gifted wound around its walls to be measured, marked, and entered into a ledger that would decide whether they lived quietly or burned loudly.

Corvess explained it to them in the assembly room, pacing before a chalk map of the quarter, his voice rising the way it always did until it filled the stone like water filling a cellar.

"Tomorrow night, the depot burns. The ledgers burn with it. Every name in those books becomes a name the crown cannot find." He struck the map with his pointer. "No ledgers, no lists. No lists, no pyres. This is not cruelty, friends. This is *amputation*. You do not negotiate with a rotting limb."

"What about the guards?" someone asked—a tanner's widow named Sef, whose hands were permanently stained brown to the wrist.

"Guards can run," Corvess said. "Paper cannot."

Later, in the narrow bunk-nook she shared with two other girls, Ulyana turned the plan over in her mind the way she'd once turned stolen buttons over, looking for the flaw, the crack, the thing that would make it worthless.

She couldn't find one. That was the trouble. It was a good plan. The depot was a wound in the city's side; burning it was almost mercy. She told herself so three times, and fell asleep telling herself so, and dreamed of nothing at all, which was rare enough that she woke feeling rested and slightly guilty about it.

* * *

Silas heard about the depot from a fishwife who'd seen the smoke and come running to the Nightmarket shouting that the world was ending, which in Veymark was a phrase with roughly the same informational value as *good morning*.

But then he saw the color of the smoke.

Charm-smoke, ordinary smoke, cookfire smoke—all of it grey or white or the lazy brown of wet wood. This smoke was black as spilled ink and it climbed the sky in a column so straight and furious it looked drawn with a ruler. Silas stood in the mouth of his alley with a half-packed tray of luck-knots forgotten under his arm and felt his stomach drop through the cobbles.

"That's the Tannery Quarter," said the fishwife, who had followed him out.

"I can see that."

"That's the depot side of it."

"I can see *that* too, Marta, thank you."

"The Remnant, then." She said it without judgment, which was its own kind of terrible. In Veymark, everyone had someone in the Remnant, or had been someone in the Remnant, or had sold bread to someone in the Remnant. "About time somebody hit back."

Silas didn't answer. He was watching the wind.

Ten years of hiding had not cured him of reading weather the way other men read faces, and the wind off the harbor was coming in low and steady from the east, straight toward the residential rows behind the depot. The rows where the tanners lived because nobody else wanted to live downwind of the tanning vats. The rows where the rent was cheap and the houses leaned on each other like drunks and the roofs were tarred pitch because pitch was cheap too.

"Those houses will go up like candles," he said.

"So will the depot."

"The depot is stone, Marta. Those houses are kindling with families inside." He set down the tray. "Watch my stall."

"Where are you—"

But he was already running, and Marta sighed the sigh of a woman who had lived sixty years in Veymark and knew better than to finish questions the answers to which would only upset her.

* * *

From the inside, the burning of the depot was almost disappointingly easy.

Ulyana crouched on a warehouse roof across the yard with three others—Sef, a wiry ex-soldier called Doss, and a boy of nineteen named Pim who kept giggling in a high, terrified way—and watched the firebloom spread through the granary's windows. Corvess's plan had been simple: Doss and Pim took out the gate watch with sleep-herb darts, Sef fired the eastern stores, and Ulyana—

Ulyana's job was to be the storm.

"If anything goes wrong," Corvess had told her, gripping her shoulders, "you call the lightning and you scatter them. Not to kill. To terrify. Fear does more work than fire, little bird. Remember that."

Nothing went wrong. That was the horrible part. The herb-darts whispered, the guards folded up like laundry, the oil went down into the ledger-room vents, and Sef's fire caught with a sound like a great indrawn breath. Within minutes the whole depot was roaring, and the names of four hundred registered mages were curling into ash, and Ulyana felt—

She felt powerful. That was the shame of it. She stood on the roof with the heat washing over her face and static crawling up her arms, and some deep animal part of her sang *yes, yes, this is what you're for*, and she hated that part and loved it and couldn't tell them apart.

"We're done," Sef said. "Move, move, before the garrison—"

That was when the wind changed.

Or rather, that was when Ulyana noticed the wind had been wrong for several minutes, because she had been too busy feeling powerful to do the one thing Silas had drilled into her across a hundred cold mornings on the seawall. *Read the air before you trust it,* he used to say, stealing her bread if she forgot. *Fire is honest. Wind lies.*

The wind was lying. It had been blowing the fire west all along—not away from the depot but into the residential rows, and the tarred roofs were catching one after another like a row of wicks, and now there were screams coming from the houses, human screams, and bells beginning to clang in the distance, and people pouring into the street in their nightclothes with bundles clutched to their chests.

"Corvess said the quarter was cleared," Pim said. His giggling had stopped entirely. "He said—they moved everyone out—"

"He said," Sef repeated flatly. She was staring at the burning houses, and her stained hands had gone white-knuckled on the roof ledge. "Well. He's an elder. Elders know things."

None of them moved. That was the moment Ulyana would remember longest, she thought—not the fire, not the screams, but the four of them standing frozen on the roof like figures in a clock, ticking and doing nothing.

Then a figure came out of the smoke below them, running *toward* the houses, coat flapping, and even at that distance and through the murk Ulyana knew the walk. She'd have known it anywhere. Ten years of watching that walk bring bread and charms and gruff non-affection to a hungry orphan.

"*Silas?*" she breathed.

He couldn't hear her over the roar. He was dragging a door off its hinges with his bare hands, using—she saw it clearly, saw the blue-white flicker crawl up his arms—the gift he'd sworn off, the gift that would get him killed, prying the burning doorframe wide and reaching into the smoke.

* * *

The house was worse inside than out, which Silas had not believed possible until he was in it.

Heat pressed on him like a hand. The stairs were gone; the second floor hung open above him like the ribcage of something eaten. Somewhere in the black a child was screaming in the particular exhausted, hiccupping way that meant the screaming had been going on a long time, and Silas followed the sound with his eyes shut and his magic open, letting the storm-sense paint the room for him—there, behind the collapsed wardrobe, small heartbeat, fast, terrified, alive.

"Hello!" he shouted. "I'm a friend! I'm a horrible, disreputable friend, and I'm coming to get you!"

The screaming paused. Even terrified children, Silas had learned long ago, could be briefly distracted by an adult volunteering to be disreputable.

He found her wedged in the gap between the wardrobe and the wall, maybe six years old, hair singed at the ends, clutching a cat that was somehow both furious and unharmed. Cats, Silas reflected, hauling rubble aside with hands that were starting to blister, cats would outlive the sun.

"There you are," he said. "Is the cat yours?"

"Nnnn—" said the child.

"Excellent. Hold onto the cat. Cats are lucky. I sell luck professionally, I should know." He got his arms under her, felt the wardrobe shift above them with a groan of dying wood, and made a decision he'd spend the rest of his short remaining life defending: he let the storm in.

Just a thread of it. Just enough. Lightning is a coward's element in some ways—it only ever wants the quickest path—and Silas gave it one, a seam of ionized air from the broken window to the wardrobe, and the wardrobe exploded outward in a spray of splinters and blue light, opening their road to the street.

They came out through the front door into a chaos of bucket-lines and screaming and neighbors passing children hand over hand like cargo. Someone thrust a blanket at the girl. Someone else thrust a bucket at Silas, apparently on the principle that any able-bodied man near a fire ought to be carrying water, and Silas, dizzy and half-deaf, found himself actually working a bucket-line for a full minute before he remembered he was a wanted man and slipped away into the alley-dark.

Behind him the block burned, and burned, and burned.

* * *

Ulyana found him at dawn, or rather he found her, sitting on the seawall steps with her knees pulled up, watching the smoke smear the sunrise into something the color of old bruises.

Neither of them spoke for a while. The tide came in below, patient and indifferent.

"A girl died," Ulyana said finally. Her voice was scraped raw. "In the third house. They got her mother out but the roof came down first. I heard them say her name. Petra. She was nine." She wrapped her arms tighter around her knees. "I stood on a roof and I *felt good*, Silas. While that was happening, some part of me was singing."

Silas lowered himself onto the step beside her with the care of a man whose body had filed a formal complaint overnight. His hands were bandaged. There was soot in the lines of his face that would take days to wash out.

"When I was seventeen," he said, "at Greyford, I brought down a storm on an army and I felt exactly the same. Powerful. Righteous. Like the sky itself had finally noticed me and approved." He picked at the edge of a bandage. "Two hundred soldiers died. So did forty villagers who happened to be standing in the wrong field during the wrong argument. Nobody sang about them. I didn't even learn their village's name until years after."

"But that was war. This was supposed to be—" She gestured helplessly. "Corvess said no one would be hurt. He said the quarter was empty. He *knew*—"

"Ulyana." Silas looked at her, and his eyes were very tired and very kind. "I've known Corvess twenty years. He believes every word he says, including the words that aren't true. That's what makes him dangerous. A liar you can catch. A true believer just keeps feeding the fire and calling it warmth." He hesitated, then said, quieter: "Your lightning answered you tonight, didn't it. When the depot burned. It was glad."

Her breath caught. "How did you—"

"Because mine was glad at Greyford." He said it simply, without drama, the way you'd report the weather. "The gift doesn't know the difference between a cause and a bonfire, little bird. It just wants to burn. The knowing—that has to come from somewhere else. From somewhere slower." He nudged her shoulder with his, gently. "Somewhere that asks annoying questions."

Ulyana stared out at the water. The smoke column still rose behind them, thinner now, defeated by morning. Somewhere in the ash of the Tannery Quarter there was a ledger-room full of dead names and a house full of one dead girl, and both of them had burned in the same fire, and Corvess would stand up at evening assembly and call it amputation, and everyone would nod, because the stew was good and the cause was just and asking questions was how the crown got its hooks in.

"Petra," she said aloud, testing the name. Keeping it. Deciding that she would keep it, the way you keep a stone in your shoe—not to suffer, but to remember the ground was real.

Silas heard the change in her voice. He didn't smile, exactly, but something in his face eased, the way a knot eases.

"That's the first crack," he said softly. "It sounds like breaking. It isn't. It's the sound of a door unlocking."

Before she could answer, a Remnant runner came pelting down the seawall steps—young Pim, soot-streaked, wild-eyed, skidding to a stop in front of them.

"They're saying—" he panted, doubled over, hands on knees. "The garrison's saying a storm-caller broke the wardrobe open. Blue lightning, witnessed by six people. They're saying the exile Fairweather's been found. There's proclamations going up on every wall by noon, and—" he swallowed, looking at Ulyana with something like awe and something like pity— "and Corvess is asking for you. Both of you. He says the fire changes everything. He says it's time."
