## 14. The Interrogation

Dame Rosalind Crane had conducted one hundred and eleven interrogations in her career, and she had learned long ago that the room mattered more than the questions. Fear filled a stone cell like water fills a boot. Comfort, on the other hand—comfort made people talk. People who were comfortable forgot they were prisoners. They began to think of you as a host rather than a jailer, and hosts, as everyone knew, owed their guests honesty.

So the room she'd chosen for Mother Ives was not a cell at all. It was a clerk's office in the White Tower's lower wing, with a real window, a real fire, and two cups of real tea steaming on the desk. The window didn't open. The fire was the only source of heat, and Crane controlled it. But it looked, from any angle a frightened old woman might take, like a parlor where two ladies might discuss the weather.

Ives was brought in at the ninth bell, wrists bound with cord rather than iron—iron was for mages who fought, and this one hadn't. She walked in on her own feet, looked around the room with the appraising eye of a woman pricing vegetables, and sat down in the offered chair without being asked twice.

"They told me I'd be questioned by Dame Crane," the herbwife said. "The Terror of Greyford Marsh. The Witch-Widow of the Coast Road." She peered at Crane over her own cupped hands, which were free, because Crane had ordered the cords cut once they were both seated. A gamble. Most gambles Crane took were calculated ones; this one was simply curiosity. "You're smaller than the stories."

"Everyone is," Crane said. "The stories do the work so I don't have to." She pushed one teacup across the desk. "Chamomile and honey. Your shop on Tanner's Lane sells something similar. Or sold. We inventoried it this morning."

"Did you now." Ives took the cup. She drank. She did not look at the window, or the door, or the two guards by the wall. She looked at Crane the way Crane imagined a chess player looked at an opponent who had just made an interesting mistake. "And what did your inventory find, besides forty years of receipts and my late husband's boots?"

"Nightshade. Hemlock. Wolfsbane in quantities sufficient to fell a plow team." Crane opened the ledger on her desk—the case file, thick as a brick already. "Also feverfew, willow bark, chamomile, comfrey, and enough dried mint to sweeten the breath of the entire harbor watch. Which, incidentally, explains why half the harbor watch buys from you."

"I give a discount to men with bad breath and good hearing."

Crane almost smiled. She killed the smile before it landed. One hundred and eleven interrogations, and the ones that went wrong were always the ones where she started liking the prisoner.

* * *

The file on Mother Ives was, by the standards of the Edict of Cleansing, damning. Unregistered gift, first flagged nineteen years ago. Suspected member of the Remnant. Shelterer of fugitives. And as of three days ago, captured fleeing a safehouse in the Tannery Quarter with a satchel of burn salves and a list of names written in a cipher Crane's cryptographers were still chewing through.

But files were paper, and paper lied by omission. What the file did not contain was the woman herself: seventy-some years old, hands like tree roots, eyes that had seen more funerals than weddings and had stopped pretending otherwise sometime around the second war.

"You know how this goes," Crane said. "I'll be plain about it, because I find plainness saves us both an afternoon. You are guilty under the edict. There is no version of events in which you are not. The question is not whether you hang. The question is whether you hang alone."

"Cheerful little sermon," Ives said. "You practice that in a mirror?"

"Every morning. It helps the delivery." Crane turned a page. "Give me names. The Remnant's leadership. Where they're sheltering. Who their couriers are. In exchange, you die quickly and privately, and your body goes to your congregation for burial instead of to the gibbet for crows. That is the entire offer. There is no better one behind it. I've never understood inquisitors who pretend there is—it insults everyone involved."

Ives set down her tea with a small click, precise as punctuation.

"You want me to sell children," she said.

"I want you to name adults who have chosen to break the law."

"The Remnant is mostly children, Dame Crane. Children and grandmothers and a one-eyed fisherman who can make nets mend themselves. You burned the Tannery Quarter. Did you count the dead? I did. Eleven. Two of them registered charm-sellers with papers signed by your own office. Law-abiding little magicians who paid their fees and still died in the ash, because ash doesn't check its paperwork." Her voice never rose. That was the unsettling thing about it. It stayed level as a millpond, and things moved underneath. "So no. I won't be giving you names. Not theirs."

"And yet," Crane said, "you'll talk. I can see it. You came in here planning to talk. So let's skip to the part where we find out what you think you're accomplishing."

For the first time, the old woman grinned. It transformed her face entirely—from granite to mischief in the space of a heartbeat—and Crane felt, absurdly, like a schoolgirl caught passing notes.

"Clever girl," Ives said. "Yes. All right. Ask me anything. Anything at all. I'll answer true, on my husband's grave, which is the only oath I keep anymore. Except the names. Everything but the names."

"That's a strange bargain to offer your interrogator."

"It's not a bargain, dear. It's a courtesy. You're going to learn everything eventually—you're too good not to. I'd rather you learned it from someone who likes you slightly, so you hear it right."

* * *

They talked for four hours.

Crane asked about the nightshade, and got a lecture on dosage that was frankly useful. She asked about the cipher, and Ives laughed and said her granddaughter had invented it and even she couldn't read it without her spectacles, which were currently in Crane's evidence locker, and no, she wouldn't be telling anyone how to read it either, but wasn't it clever? She asked about the Remnant's structure and received, in return, a history of every persecution Veymark had ever visited on its gifted, delivered with dates, anecdotes, and two genuinely funny stories about a bishop and a haunted outhouse.

And slowly, deliberately, the way a cook adds salt a pinch at a time, Ives gave her Silas Fairweather.

Not all at once. Never the whole shape of him. But pieces. Pieces that fit.

"He came to me ten years ago," she said, somewhere past the third hour, staring into the fire. "Half-dead. Rain-soaked. Wouldn't use his gift to dry himself off even then—stood dripping on my floor like a proud idiot while I toweled his hair like he was my grandson. He'd sworn off storms, you see. Swore it on something at Greyford that I've never pried out of him, and I've pried at a great many people in my day."

"The Battle of Greyford," Crane said. "He called a storm that drowned two companies of crown soldiers."

"He called a storm that ended a battle," Ives corrected, mild as milk. "Whether it drowned them or the river did depends on whether you believe rivers have opinions. He was twenty-two years old and someone handed him a sky and told him to aim it. What would you have done at twenty-two, if someone handed you a sky?"

Crane did not answer. She wrote instead: *Subject expresses remorse consistent with coerced service.* Then she looked at what she'd written and found it sounded like something her superior, Lord Inquisitor Havelock, would say, and crossed it out.

"He sells charms," she said. "Luck-knots. Fever-charms. Harmless things. Ten years of harmless things. Why does a storm-caller spend a decade tying knots?"

"Why does a woman with your talents spend her days hunting herbwives?" Ives countered. "Because it's the only honest work left for what we are. His knots aren't magic, you know. Did you know that? I've examined them. There's nothing in them. No working, no spark, nothing. He ties them beautifully and charges a copper and they work because people believe they work, and that—" she tapped the desk with one root-knuckled finger, "—that is the most honest magic left in this city. Faith, freely given. No compulsion in it anywhere."

"Then why pay off the harbor guard?"

"Because a man with his face and his history can't afford to be noticed, believed or not." Ives leaned back. "You've been watching him. How long?"

"Six weeks."

"And what did you see?"

Crane hesitated. It was a small hesitation, less than a breath, but Ives caught it the way an old fisherman catches a change in current.

"You saw a tired man selling trinkets," the herbwife said gently. "And slipping bread to a street orphan when he thought no one saw. And paying bribes he couldn't afford so the guards would look elsewhere. Six weeks, Dame Crane, and the worst crime you caught him at was kindness performed badly."

"There is a girl," Crane said. "Ulyana. Approximately twelve years old. Unregistered. And there is reason to believe—"

"There is reason to believe nothing," Ives said, and for the first time the millpond voice went hard, went cold, went flat as ice over deep water. "There is a child who has been hungry and hunted her whole life, and the only person in this city who ever fed her is sitting in your tower because of it. Whatever you imagine she is—a weapon, a bloodline, a banner for some cause—she is a child first, and I will hang before I hand you a child."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, a bell rang the quarter hour. Crane became aware that her tea had gone cold some time ago and that she had, at some point, stopped taking notes.

"You love him," she said. "Fairweather. Like a son."

"I love a great many people. It's a habit I refuse to break. It gets expensive." Ives picked up her cold tea anyway and drank it, grimacing. "Now. You want to know why I'm talking at all, since I won't give you names. Shall I tell you?"

"Please."

"Because you're going to catch him regardless. You're too thorough, and he's too soft-hearted, and soft-hearted men leave trails a blindfolded bloodhound could follow. So I'd rather you knew him truly before you took him. Because there's a chance—small, mind, but I'm an old woman and I hoard chances like other folk hoard buttons—that if you understand what he actually is, you might hesitate. Half a heartbeat. That's all hesitation ever is. Half a heartbeat. But I've seen half a heartbeats save lives."

"And if I don't hesitate?"

"Then you'll hang him knowing exactly what you hanged," Ives said, "and you'll carry it. That's all I can do to you, dear. That's the whole of my power in this room. I can make sure you carry it."

* * *

Crane dismissed the guards at the seventh bell and sat alone with the ledger for a long while after Ives was taken back down to the cells.

She reviewed her notes. They were extensive. Dates, details, the topology of a decade: the widow Marta whose daughter had lived through scarlet fever because of a fever-charm that should, by all Crane's own analysis, have been inert. The harbor guards on a bribe schedule so regular it could have been entered into the civic accounts. The orphan, appearing in the margins of witness statements for years—always fed, always clothed just adequately, never once named aloud by the man who kept her alive.

One hundred and eleven interrogations, and Crane had a private taxonomy of liars. The terrified, who invented too much. The arrogant, who invented too little. The devout, who lied only about God and therefore were nearly useless. Mother Ives fit none of the categories. She had lied about exactly one thing—the names—and about everything else she had been so scrupulously truthful that Crane had twice checked her claims against independent sources and twice found them exact.

Which meant the truthfulness itself was the weapon. Every verifiable truth made the one refusal look less like defiance and more like conscience. And conscience, Crane was beginning to suspect, was contagious.

She thought about the Tannery Quarter. Eleven dead, two of them registered. She thought about Havelock's face when she'd raised the discrepancy—registered citizens among the dead—and the smooth way he'd said, *the edict is not responsible for the actions of arsonists, Dame Crane,* as though responsibility were a coat you could hang on someone else's hook.

She thought about a man tying luck-knots for copper, ten years of harmless knots, and a bread crust passed to a hungry girl in an alley.

*He is protecting something specific,* she wrote in the margin of the file, and then underlined it, and then sat looking at the underline for a long time. Not a network. Networks had structure, and Fairweather had none—he wasn't in the Remnant's chain of command, Ives's careful answers had made that clear, he stood outside all of it, connected to everything and enrolled in nothing. He was protecting a thing. A person. A secret with a name, probably a young one, probably—

The bell rang eight. Crane closed the ledger.

Orders had come down that morning, before dawn: the Fairweather matter was to be escalated. Warrants for arrest on sight. Description circulated to every gate, every garrison, every market watch in Veymark. By tomorrow morning his face would be on every wall in the city, rendered in woodcut by artists who had never seen him and would therefore give him the jaw of a stage villain and eyes like a butcher's.

By tomorrow morning, the soft-hearted man with the harmless knots would have nowhere in Veymark to buy bread.

Crane capped her ink, and paused, and did something she had never done in one hundred and eleven interrogations. She unlocked her desk drawer, took out the evidence tag marked *IVES — SPECTACLES*, and slipped it into her coat pocket instead of the transfer tray.

It was, she told herself, a clerical error.

She was not entirely sure she believed herself, and for the first time in her career, Rosalind Crane found that she did not much care.
