## 15. Wanted: The Storm-Caller

The paper went up on the Salt Gate the morning after the storm, and by noon it was on every wall in Veymark.

Silas saw it first outside the chandler's shop, pasted crooked over an old notice about quarantine regulations, and for a moment he thought it was a joke. Someone had drawn him. Someone had *drawn* him well — the sharp jaw, the grey threading through the black of his hair, the tired eyes that always looked like they were apologizing for something. Beneath the portrait, in letters tall enough to read from horseback:

**WANTED: THE STORM-CALLER.**
*Answering to the name Silas Fairweather. Last seen fleeing the harbor quarter during the tempest of the ninth. Registered gift or not, this person is declared an enemy of the Crown. Fifty crowns for information leading to capture. One hundred for the head.*

One hundred crowns. He'd sold luck-knots three for a copper, and here was his face priced like a prize hog at autumn fair. There was a grim sort of arithmetic in it that almost made him laugh — ten years of harmless charms, and the crown finally valued his work, just at the exact moment it intended to end it.

He didn't laugh. He stood in the shadow of the chandler's awning with his hood up and his collar high, and he read the notice four times, because the fourth time he noticed the small print at the bottom: *Any person found sheltering the above shall share his sentence.* Not a fine. Not imprisonment. His *sentence*. Which, under the Edict of Cleansing, meant fire.

So this was what it came to. Every door in Veymark was now a test. Every kindness had a price printed beneath it in official ink. He thought of Marta's kitchen, of Ulyana somewhere out there in the rain-soaked city believing the worst of him, of Mother Ives rotting in some White Tower cell because she'd refused to trade a child's name for her own life. He thought of all of it, and then he pulled his hood lower and walked into the crowd, because that was the only direction left.

* * *

The Nightmarket still opened at dusk. Of course it did. Fear closed churches and courts; it had never once managed to close a market. People still needed salt and lamp-oil and someone to complain to, and so the stalls went up between the burned-out tannery quarter and the harbor wall, thinner than before, quieter, the vendors keeping their voices low like mourners at a wake.

Silas walked through it with copper in his pocket and nothing to sell. That was the strange part — he could have kept selling. A fever-charm was just knotted thread and a spoken word, and half the city would swear the word meant nothing at all. But every knot he tied now was evidence, and every customer who bought one became a witness against themselves. So he walked the rows like a ghost at his own funeral, hands in pockets, listening.

What he heard turned his stomach.

At the fishmonger's stall, two women argued over the price of herring, and one of them said, "Well, if you're so worried about coin, there's a hundred crowns walking around wearing a hood." The other laughed. It wasn't a cruel laugh, exactly. It was the laugh of people who had stopped being able to afford cruelty as a luxury and had started treating it as income.

At the pie stand, a boy Silas had known since the boy was knee-high — a boy whose mother had bought a fever-charm every winter for six years running — looked straight at him, recognized him, and spat. Missed, thankfully. Spat again. Hit his boot.

"Storm-caller," the boy said, with the relish of someone reciting a lesson learned that afternoon. "My da says you drowned the Saltwrights. My da says the harbor ran red."

"The harbor ran *salt*, love," Silas said mildly, and the boy spat a third time, and Silas wiped his boot on the cobbles and kept walking, because arguing with a child parroting his father was a battle even he couldn't lose gracefully.

It stung more than he expected. He'd spent a decade telling himself the charms were nothing — thread and breath and belief — but the people buying them hadn't thought they were nothing. They'd come to him at midnight with dying daughters. They'd pressed their last coppers into his hand and gone home to wait, and some of them had watched those coppers *work*, or believed they had, which amounted to the same thing in a sickroom at three in the morning. And now the same faces that had wept with gratitude at his stall were spitting at his boots, because fear is a solvent and gratitude dries hard and cracks along the first fault line.

He understood it. Understanding didn't help. There is a particular loneliness in being hated correctly — in knowing that the people cursing you have excellent reasons, that you *did* drown men in the harbor, that some of them had wives, that one of them had a mother who was probably, right now, standing at her own window reading his name on a notice and whispering *there he is, there's the monster who took my boy*. Silas carried that mother with him everywhere he went now. She lived in his chest alongside everything else he'd failed to be.

By the third row of stalls, the market had noticed him whether he wanted it to or not. Word travels in a poor district the way water finds a crack. A chandler sidled up and asked, too politely, whether he was buying or selling tonight. A guard's informer — Silas knew his face, everyone did, a fat man named Petch who made his living knowing things — leaned against a post and watched him with the patient attention of a cat watching a mouse hole.

Silas bought a pie he didn't want, from the boy who'd spat at him, purely to move along before Petch got comfortable. Overpaid by triple. Left the pie on a windowsill for the stray dogs. It was the closest thing to magic left in his repertoire: small, pointless kindnesses, performed where no one could see them, worth exactly nothing, costing exactly what he couldn't spare.

Then he turned down Tanner's Lane, away from the lamps, toward the only address in Veymark he still trusted.

* * *

Marta Kell's house stood at the dead end of Tanner's Lane, leaning against its neighbors like a drunk leaning on friends, three stories of sagging timber with a blue door she repainted every spring whether it needed it or not. There was a pot of rosemary on the step. Rosemary, Silas knew, for remembrance — though whose remembrance, hers or the plant's, she'd never made clear, and he'd never asked, because some questions are just ways of being polite and some politeness is just ways of asking.

He knocked. Knocking felt dangerous these days. Knocking announced himself to anyone within earshot, and earshot included Petch, who had a habit of loitering near the houses of people who used to buy charms. But Marta had told him once, years ago, in a tone of absolute authority: *"If you ever need me, knock loud. I'm old. I miss soft knocks."*

So he knocked loud.

She answered on the second knock, which surprised him — he'd braced for the door to stay shut, for the woman who'd fed him stew through three bad winters to look at his wanted poster and do the arithmetic every sensible person in Veymark was doing. Instead the blue door swung open and there she was, small and round and silver-haired, wiping flour off her hands onto an apron that had been winning that fight for twenty years.

"Well," she said. "Look what the tide brought in."

"Marta—"

"Don't." She held up a floured finger. "Don't apologize, don't explain, don't tell me it isn't what the paper says. I can read. I chose the door before you finished knocking. Get inside before Petch sees you standing on my step like a lost dog."

Inside, the kitchen smelled of bread and onions and lamp-oil, the eternal smell of Marta's house, the smell Silas had privately classified years ago as *the opposite of drowning*. There was a fire in the hearth. There was a cat named Admiral asleep in the warm spot, a one-eyed tom with opinions about laps. There was a chair by the fire that was, by unspoken treaty, Silas's chair, and had been since the second winter, when Marta had started leaving it empty on nights he didn't come, "so the room remembers you."

He sat in it. The room remembered him. He cried a little, quietly, into the warmth, and Marta pretended not to notice, which was its own form of grace — the kind that doesn't demand confession, just offers a blanket.

"You know what that paper says," she said, pouring tea without asking whether he wanted any, because in Marta's house tea was not a question, it was weather. "*Any person found sheltering the above shall share his sentence.* You know I've got Lottie upstairs and the grandsons across the lane. You know what they'd do to us."

"I know," Silas said. "You should turn me out. I'll sleep in the tannery ruins. I've slept worse."

"You have not," Marta said flatly. "I've heard your sleeping arrangements described. Tannery ruins smell like a dead whale's opinion of itself." She set the teapot down and looked at him across the table, and her eyes were wet and furious and absolutely certain, all three at once, which was a trick Silas had never managed. "Ten years ago my daughter was burning up. Fever, the bad kind, the kind that takes children in this city every winter and calls it God's will. The physicians bled her twice and charged me for the leeches. And a man in the Nightmarket sold me a knot of thread for a copper and told me, *boil it, breathe the steam, sing to her — the charm's mostly an excuse to make you sit up with her all night, and sitting up is the real medicine.*"

"It was," Silas said. "Mostly."

"She lived." Marta's voice cracked clean down the middle. "Thirty-one winters I've buried neighbors' children. Thirty-one. And mine lived, and I never forgot who sat me down next to her bed and taught me the song. So no, I will not turn you out. I will not weigh your life against mine on the crown's little scales. The crown can send its men. Let them find an old woman, a cat, and a kettle."

Silas put his hands around the cup because if he didn't, they'd shake visibly, and he was spending everything he had on looking steady. "They'll burn the house, Marta."

"Then they'll burn the house," she said, and refilled his tea. "Lottie's grown. The grandsons are quick. And I am seventy-one years old and I have been preparing to die my whole life, same as everyone, only now I get to pick the reason, and I pick *this one*. Sit up straight. You look like a question mark."

He sat up straight. It helped less than she implied it would.

* * *

He stayed three days.

In that time he learned that hiding in Marta's house meant becoming useful in the ways available to a man with no magic and a price on his head. He fixed the loose shutter on the second floor. He re-caulked the window in Lottie's old room, which leaked rain directly onto the pillow, a flaw Marta had complained about for six years and defended for six years ("the leak tells me when it's raining without going outside"). He mended the Admiral's torn ear, which involved more blood loss on Silas's part than on the cat's. He chopped wood until his palms blistered, because Marta's woodpile had been aspirational rather than actual since midsummer.

And on the second night, Lottie came home.

She was nineteen now, tall and sharp-tongued, working the mending stalls near the fishmarket, and she came through the blue door, saw Silas at the table, and stopped with her hand still on the latch.

"You," she said.

"Me," Silas agreed.

"I spat at you Tuesday."

"You did. You missed twice and hit my boot. Your aim's improved since you were nine."

"That was—" She flushed dark. "That was before I knew it was you. Da said the storm-caller wore a hood and drowned the Saltwrights, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again, angrier at herself than at him. "I thought it was some stranger. Some *thing*. And then Petch described you at the market and I realized I'd been spitting at the man who—" Her voice wobbled. "My ma keeps the song. The one you taught her. She sings it when the fever comes through the lanes, to other people's children now. Did you know that? Three families on this street, she's sat up with their kids singing your stupid song over your stupid thread knots."

"It's a good song," Silas said. "I stole it from my mother."

"Well." Lottie hung up her cloak with great violence. "Da says you're a danger to the household. Da says a hundred crowns is a year of rent. Da has a point, and Da can say it to my face while I'm holding the poker." She paused. "Also Ma already hid you, so Da's opinions are ceremonial at this point."

"They are," Marta called from the kitchen, serenely. "Supper's ready. Both of you wash your hands, and Silas, you're peeling onions, because guilt is best worked out through root vegetables."

So he peeled onions. He peeled enough onions for a family of eight, weeping steadily the whole time, and nobody in the Kell household considered this remarkable, and somewhere in the middle of it the tightness in his chest loosened by perhaps a tenth, which in his current condition constituted a miracle.

On the third night, after Lottie had gone up and the fire had burned to orange ribs, Marta sat across from him with her sewing and said, without looking up: "You're leaving tomorrow."

"I am?"

"You are. You've been safe here long enough to forget you're not safe. That's how it works — three days of warm bread and a man starts thinking the walls will hold forever. They won't. Petch has been sniffing up and down this lane since Tuesday. I've seen him twice from the bedroom window, standing at the corner, doing nothing, which is what he does *before* he does something." She bit her thread. "There's a boat. My late husband's cousin runs oysters out of the north stairs, below the tide-stairs, the ones nobody uses since the new customs house went up. He owes me. He'll take a passenger who doesn't ask questions and pays in silence. You can be in Greyford waters by Friday."

Greyford. The name landed in Silas's chest like a stone dropped down a well. Greyford was the battle. Greyford was the field where he'd called his last true storm before this week, where the coven had fallen, where his exile had begun, where his mother's voice had gone silent mid-word. Going back to Greyford waters was going back to the scene of everything.

"Why there?" he asked.

"Because far is safe and familiar is found," Marta said, in the tone of a woman quoting somebody smarter than both of them. "And because whatever you're carrying around in that chest of yours, son, it isn't going to let you run to some foreign port and start selling luck-knots under a new name. I've watched you for ten years. You don't want escape. You want to finish something." She threaded her needle. "So go finish it. But eat breakfast first. Nobody finishes anything hungry."

* * *

He left at dawn, before the lanes woke, before Petch's corner acquired its daily occupant. Marta walked him to the door and pressed a parcel into his hands — bread, hard cheese, a jar of onion soup that weighed as much as a brick — and a folded paper besides, which he didn't open until he was three streets away.

It wasn't money. It was a recipe. Her onion soup, written out in a shaky hand, with annotations: *simmer longer than you think*, *the secret is patience, which cannot be bought*, and at the bottom, in different ink, added later: *for the girl, when you find her. Children need feeding before they need saving.*

*For the girl, when you find her.*

She'd known. Of course she'd known. Ten years of watching him slip bread to a sharp-eyed orphan at the edge of the market, ten years of pretending not to see, and Marta Kell had seen everything, the way powerless people always see everything — because seeing is the one power that can't be outlawed, taxed, or burned.

Silas stood in the grey dawn at the mouth of Tanner's Lane with a soup recipe in his pocket and tears he didn't bother hiding, because the lane was empty and there was no one left to perform steadiness for. Grace, he thought. That's what this was. Not the grand kind from stories — not saints and martyrs and burning hearts — but the small, ferocious, ordinary grace of people with nothing, giving anyway. A widow with a poker and a kettle. A daughter who'd spat at him Monday and defended him Wednesday. An onion soup annotated for a child she'd never met, because children need feeding before they need saving.

The powerful had given him edicts and posters and a hundred-crown bounty. The powerless had given him a chair by a fire that remembered him, and a song that outlived its author, and breakfast before the finishing of things.

He went down toward the tide-stairs with the parcel against his chest, and the gulls were screaming over the north harbor, and the water lay flat and pewter-colored and waiting.

That was when he heard the footsteps behind him. Two sets. Unhurried. Confident. The tread of men who had done this before, closing the distance the way hunters do — not rushing, not calling out, just *arriving*, letting the quarry hear them coming because the hearing was part of the trap.

Silas didn't turn. He counted the steps instead. Twelve paces back. Then ten. Then, at the corner ahead, a third figure stepped out of an alley to block the lane, arms folded, waiting, and Silas recognized the silhouette immediately, because he'd seen it loitering outside the Nightmarket for a decade and had always assumed it was harmless.

Petch. With friends. And behind Petch, at the far end of the lane, a wagon stood waiting with its canvas tied down — not a cargo wagon. The canvas was too new, the wheels too clean, and chained to the frame, visible even at this distance, glinted the thing Silas had hoped never to see again in his life: an iron cage, crown-stamped, sized for one.

They weren't here to arrest him quietly. Arrests happened at doors, with papers. This was a *collection* — the kind Dame Rosalind Crane authorized personally, the kind that ended with a cage on a wagon and a trial that lasted as long as it took to read a name aloud.

Twelve paces. Ten. Eight.

Silas looked at the sky. It was the color of wet slate, heavy and low, the pressure wrong, the gulls flying inland, the air tasting of iron — all the signs he'd spent ten years teaching himself to ignore, all the old language rising in his blood like a tide refusing to be reasoned with. There was no storm in it yet. But there was *material*. There was always material. The sky was a pantry and he had spent his whole life starving at its door out of politeness.

Six paces. Four.

He thought of Marta's kitchen. He thought of the song. He thought of a girl somewhere in this city who believed he'd sold her, who deserved to learn otherwise from someone other than a corpse on a wagon. He thought: *nobody finishes anything hungry,* and he ate the last of the bread, quickly, standing in the lane, because Marta had said to, and because a man about to do something unforgivable should at least follow the last instructions of the last person who was kind to him.

Two paces.

"Petch," Silas said pleasantly, turning at last, hands visible, voice level. "Lovely morning. Before you and your colleagues do whatever comes next — you might want to look up."

Petch looked up.

Everyone looks up, eventually. It's the oldest trick there is, and it works because the sky is the one wall in Veymark no one ever watches — except, as of this moment, the one man in the city forbidden to watch it, standing in a lane with a full stomach, a soup recipe, and absolutely nothing left to lose.

Above the rooftops, the slate-grey ceiling of the morning began, very quietly, to boil.
