## 16. Ulyana Alone

The lie arrived the way most lies do: quietly, in an ordinary voice, from someone who had no reason to lie.

"You didn't hear it from me," said Tansy, one of Mother Ives's older girls, catching Ulyana by the sleeve in the herbhouse corridor. Her face had that pinched look people got when they'd been carrying news around like a hot coal. "But Petch was talking. One of the Saltwrights, before they took him. He said Fairweather made a bargain. Said he gave up where you were hiding in exchange for being let walk."

The words went into Ulyana like a splinter — small, clean, and deep enough to touch bone.

"That's not true," she said.

"I'm only telling you what was said." Tansy wouldn't meet her eyes. That was the worst part. People could say anything while looking at you; it was the looking-away that told you what they believed.

Ulyana stood very still in the corridor that smelled of dried yarrow and lamp oil, and let the thought finish arriving. *He sold me.* Ten years of bread crusts and luck-knots and that gruff, sideways tenderness he wore like a disguise over his actual face — and it had all been the long game. Keep the orphan fed. Keep the orphan close. Keep the orphan alive until her blood was worth more than his freedom.

*And isn't that exactly what a clever man would do,* whispered the small cold voice that lived behind her ribs and had kept her breathing through six winters on the streets before Silas ever found her. *Isn't that exactly what you'd have done?*

She didn't scream. She didn't cry. She did what she had learned to do before she could write her own name.

She ran.

* * *

Getting out of the herbhouse was easy. Getting out of the Remnant's territory was harder, because the Remnant guarded their miracle the way misers guard coins — constantly, and with visible resentment of the expense. But Corvess's watchers watched doors and alleys, and Ulyana had spent her childhood learning that cities are mostly roof.

She went up through the cooper's yard, across the drying-sheds, along the spine of the tannery row where the tiles were slick with render-fat and nobody in their right mind followed. The rain helped. It always rained now, or it felt like it did; the whole city seemed to be holding its breath under low grey cloud, waiting for the storm-caller's next performance. She moved through the wet dark on hands and knees at first, then upright, then almost flying, the way she could when she stopped thinking about falling.

That was the thing about the rooftops. Up there, nothing belonged to anyone. Not the crown, not the coven, not the smugglers, not the dead kings whose faces were stamped on every coin down below. Up there a girl could be nobody, which after everything was the only title she wanted.

She crouched in the lee of a chimney stack above Candlemaker's Lane, dripping, heart slamming, and looked back at the herbhouse district glowing with its few stubborn lamps. Somewhere down there Corvess was probably already planning how to explain her absence. Somewhere down there Mother Ives would be worried, or pretending to be. And somewhere out there in the wet dark of Veymark, a man with grey in his beard and a liar's kind eyes was spending the reward for her skin.

"Fine," Ulyana said to the chimney, because saying things out loud made them true, and she needed this one true immediately. "Fine. I never needed him anyway."

The chimney, being a chimney, did not argue. It was the first conversation in years that hadn't ended with someone wanting something from her blood.

* * *

The first day was the easy one, because the first day you still have money.

She had eleven coppers sewn into her boot heel — old habit, older than the Remnant, older than Silas. Eleven coppers bought a meat pie from the pie-cart on Fishhook Row (the vendor didn't look at her face twice; half of Veymark wasn't looking at faces these days), a candle stub, and a place in the sleeping-room above the Broken Keel for the night, wedged between a snoring fishwife and a boy who talked in his sleep about eels.

She lay awake listening to the rain and doing sums, because sums were steadier than feelings.

Eleven minus four left seven. Seven minus two for tomorrow's food left five. Five wouldn't buy another night anywhere with a roof, which meant by tomorrow evening she'd be back to the old arithmetic: doorways, drain-grates, the warm grates behind the bakeries, the church porches if the vergers were slow. She knew all of it. Her body knew it the way a sailor's body knows rope. There was a horrible comfort in that — the streets hadn't changed, even if she had. The streets were honest. The streets had never once pretended to love her.

*He did, though,* said the voice behind her ribs. *Or something shaped like it.*

"Shut up," Ulyana told the ceiling, quietly, so the fishwife wouldn't wake.

The boy dreamed about eels. The rain came down. She slept in scraps, and in the gaps between scraps she dreamed too — of lightning answering her voice, of a flooded cellar where she had shown a man the worst and best thing about herself and he had knelt down on the wet stone and said, *well, that explains the weather*, and laughed, actually laughed, and she had felt for one moment like the most extraordinary creature alive instead of the most dangerous.

It had been a good trick, she thought bitterly, drifting off. The laughing. The kindness worn so long it fit him like his own skin. You couldn't fake ten years. Could you?

She decided you could, and hated how much room for doubt she'd left.

* * *

Day two, the money ran out and the education began.

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving alone: it is mostly bookkeeping. Hunger is not one continuous agony; it comes in waves, and between waves there is strategy. Ulyana spent the morning of the second day relearning hers.

Rule one: never beg near the markets, because the market guards knew her face now — not as the storm-heir, thank all the drowned gods, but as *Fairweather's girl*, and Fairweather's face was currently nailed to every wall in Veymark beside the words WANTED FOR HIGH TREASON. She'd seen the poster that morning, pasted crooked over a chandler's shutters. The likeness was bad. They'd given him a villain's jaw he didn't have. She had stood in front of it longer than she meant to, hating it, and hating that part of her hatred was grief, and hating most of all that some stupid loyal corner of her chest had thought, *they drew him wrong.*

Rule two: work beats begging. She carried crates for a fishmonger until he noticed her hands were quick and paid her two coppers and a herring. She swept a barber's step. She told a fortune for a drunk sailor using entirely invented methods and got a bun out of it, which she considered fair, since the fortune — *you will be betrayed by someone you trust, but survive it* — was at least accurate.

Rule three, the hard one: don't use the gift.

This was the rule that sat on her chest all day. Because here was the thing — she could feel it now, always, the way you feel a full bladder or a loose tooth. The storm sat in her sternum like a second heartbeat, and every time a cart nearly hit her, every time a guard's hand brushed her shoulder checking for stolen goods, it *pressed*. Wanting out. Wanting to answer her fear with thunder.

And she understood, with a clarity that felt like growing a new organ overnight, why Silas had spent ten years selling luck-knots that did nothing. Not cowardice. Arithmetic. Every flash of real magic was a shout in a city full of listeners. She had lit up the sky once already, running across the rooftops, and look what it had cost: the Saltwrights hunted, the Remnant exposed, Silas's face on walls.

*His face on walls,* she corrected herself. *Not mine. He traded my secret to keep his name smaller than mine.*

Except the poster said TREASON, which was a big word for a man who sold fever-charms, which meant the crown already knew what he was, which meant — her thoughts went around this loop eleven times before noon — either he'd been caught protecting her after all, or he'd sold her and gotten caught at something else, and she refused, absolutely refused, to care which.

She cared. That was the problem. Caring was the wound; everything else was just weather.

By evening she'd found a dry culvert under the aqueduct where three other street kids had already claimed territory. She negotiated entry with the herring and half the sailor's bun, and learned their names — Wick, who was nine and missing teeth; Sef, who was maybe twelve and lied about his age upward; little Ashen, who didn't talk but could whistle like a kettle. None of them asked her business. Street kids never did. Asking questions was how you got answers, and answers were heavy to carry.

They shared the culvert in a pile for warmth, and Sef, chewing, said conversationally, "You're the one what's worth a hundred crowns."

Ulyana went very still. "What?"

"The storm-girl. Proclamation went round this morning. Hundred crowns, alive. Dead's no good, they said, which is funny, 'cause usually it's the other way." He shrugged with the magnificent indifference of the well-informed poor. "Nobody's going to turn you in, mind. Hundred crowns sounds like a lot till you remember what happens to folk who talk to the Inquisition. My da informed on a neighbor once. Got his copper and got his house burned by the neighbors' friends same week. Economics," he said sagely, "don't favor it."

"I'm not—" she started.

Wick, nine and toothless, patted her arm without looking up from his crust. "Your hair does the thing when you're scared," he said. "Like sparks. It's grand."

Ulyana pulled her hood up and lay staring at the dark curve of the culvert, and did not sleep much, and somewhere past midnight made the decision that day three would be different — because a hundred crowns meant everyone in Veymark was now a hunter, and hiding was a losing game, and she had never in her life won anything by standing still.

If the world wanted Ulyana of the storm-line, then Ulyana of the storm-line would decide where and when she was found.

* * *

Day three, she stopped running and started thinking, and discovered that thinking was its own kind of power — quieter than lightning, but it traveled further.

Her assets, she listed on her fingers in the grey dawn light, sitting on the seawall with the harbor wind trying to steal her hood: one, she was invisible among thousands of poor; two, she had eleven years of city-knowledge in her feet; three, she had the Remnant, who wanted her as a weapon — useless, unless she wanted to be one; four, she had the crown, who wanted her as a symbol — worse; and five—

Five was the problem. Five was the empty space where Silas used to be, the person whose whole job had been wanting nothing from her except that she eat something.

Unless the empty space wasn't empty. Unless Tansy's coal-hot gossip was wrong, or half-wrong, or wrong in the specific way that mattered. Petch had said *bargain*. Petch was a Saltwright, and Saltwrights said whatever kept their skins stitched on. And Silas — Silas, who had called a true storm after ten years of silence, burning himself bright for every spy in the city to see, purely so she could get away —

*Purely so he could collect later,* said the cold voice.

*Or purely so I could get away,* said a newer voice, smaller, stubborn, that sounded annoyingly like his.

She sat on the seawall and argued with herself like a madwoman while gulls screamed their opinions overhead, and finally did what she'd learned to do in the flooded cellar with the lightning: stop fighting the noise and listen underneath it.

Underneath the hurt, underneath the anger, there was a fact, and facts were the only currency the streets respected: Silas Fairweather had taught her every card-game trick, every lock-picking angle, every "look up" gambit — and he had taught them all *to her*, freely, for years, asking nothing back. Men planning to sell you don't make you valuable. Men planning to sell you keep you ignorant and grateful.

So either he'd changed in the last month, or Tansy's coal was cold.

Which meant the question wasn't *did he betray me*. The question was *who benefits from me believing he did*. And that question had an answer with a face: smooth-voiced Corvess, who spoke of destiny the way other men spoke of dinner, and Mother Ives's frightened girls, and a Remnant that had been hunting a storm-heir for a generation and had finally caught one — and would very much prefer she stayed angry, stayed pliable, stayed *theirs*.

Ulyana stood up on the seawall, wind shoving her hair back, and felt something settle in her chest that wasn't lightning and wasn't warmth. It was colder and better than both. It was the feeling of a debt being converted into a plan.

Three days ago she'd been a foundling with a famous bloodline, passed between factions like a purse. Today she was a girl with eleven coppers' worth of experience, a hundred-crown price tag, and nobody's permission needed for anything ever again.

"Right," she said to the gulls. "New rules."

Rule one: trust actions, never words, including her own. Rule two: the Remnant thought she'd run in panic; let them. Panicked things get chased, and chasing things leave their backs open. Rule three — and here she smiled, thin and sharp, a smile Silas would have recognized with pride and dread in equal measure —

Rule three: find out the truth herself. Which meant finding the one person in Veymark who knew both sides of every bargain, the man whose face was on every wall, the traitor-or-not who had gone to ground somewhere in this city with the whole crown hunting him.

Finding Silas Fairweather.

Behind her, out over the grey water, thunder rolled — distant, unhurried, almost conversational. The gulls scattered. Ulyana didn't flinch. She turned toward the sound, tilted her head, and listened the way the storm had taught her to listen, and beneath the thunder, faint as a whisper under a door, she heard it answer her attention. Not with words. With direction. A pull, south and down, toward the old palace hill and the drowned places beneath it.

"Well," she said. "Aren't *you* full of opinions."

She pulled her hood up, checked her boot for coppers — three left, spend them wisely — and set off along the seawall toward the ruined palace, a lone figure against the rain, hunted by everyone, belonging to no one, and for the first time in her life entirely, dangerously free.
