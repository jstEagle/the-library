## 18. Reunion in the Rain

Silas had spent three days learning the shape of his own infamy.

His face was on every wall in Veymark, rendered by some clerk's imagination as a gaunt, hook-nosed villain with eyes like coals. The real Silas Fairweather—tired, rain-damp, and currently eating a stolen plum behind a fishmonger's awning—found the portrait flattering in the wrong places and insulting in the right ones.

"Coals," he muttered to himself. "I have *kind* eyes. Marta said so."

Marta had also said he was a fool for going out at all, but Silas had a debt to settle that no wanted-poster could outweigh. Mother Ives was dead. The herbhouse was ash. And somewhere in this drowned, frightened city was a girl who believed—because Corvess had made sure she believed—that Silas Fairweather had sold her name to the Saltwrights for passage out of Veymark.

He'd spent three days chasing rumors of her. A baker swore a girl with storm-grey eyes stole a loaf off his sill. A lamplighter swore lightning followed someone down Tanner's Row like an angry dog. The rumors moved toward the harbor, which meant she was moving toward the sea, which meant—

The seawall. Of course. It was where she went when the world got too loud. He'd found her there a hundred times over ten years: after scrapes, after nightmares, after the winter her fever-charm failed and Silas had sat up all night holding her small burning hands and lying about how easy it was to fix.

Silas finished the plum, wiped his hands on his coat, and walked out into the rain toward the end of the seawall, where a small figure stood with her back to him and the whole grey ocean arguing at her feet.

* * *

Ulyana heard him before she saw him. That was new. Three days on the streets had rewired something in her; she could feel people now, faintly, like heat off stones. And this particular heat she knew better than her own heartbeat—a lopsided, limping warmth, like a fire that never quite caught properly.

She didn't turn around.

"You walk loudly for a man hiding from the entire crown," she said.

"Years of practice," Silas said, stopping six paces back. Close enough to talk. Far enough, he hoped, to survive what came next. "You've gotten good at this. The sensing."

"Don't." Her voice cracked like ice on a pond. "Don't you *dare* stand there making small talk like you're teaching me a lesson. Like it's any ordinary day."

"It isn't?"

"You *sold me*, Silas."

The words came out of her with force enough that the rain seemed to flinch. Ulyana turned at last, and the sight of her stopped his breath. She was soaked through, hollow-cheeked, a bruise going yellow along her jaw—and crackling. Literally crackling. Little blue threads of static crawled up her arms and vanished into her wet hair, lifting it slightly, so that she looked like a girl standing inside a thunderhead wearing a thunderhead's temper.

"Ives is dead because of you," she said. "Corvess told me everything. Bren named his price and you paid it. You gave me to the smugglers to buy your own boat out of Veymark, and I—" Her breath hitched. "I believed you. Ten years I believed you were the one person in this city who wasn't for sale."

"Ulyana—"

"*No.*"

The sky answered before she finished the word.

Lightning came off her in a raw, unshaped bolt—not aimed so much as *released*, the way a kettle releases steam. Silas had perhaps half a heartbeat to throw up the only defense he had left, a warding knot he'd woven into his own coat collar years ago and never once needed until now.

The world went white. Then blue. Then sideways.

He came to on his back on the seawall stones, ears ringing, coat smoking gently at the collar, staring up at Ulyana's horrified face.

"Oh no," she whispered. "Oh no, oh no—I didn't mean—Silas? *Silas!*"

"I'm fine," he wheezed.

"You're *smoking*."

"Just the coat. The coat had it coming. It's been smug for years." He sat up carefully, testing his limbs the way a man tests ice. Everything worked. His hair, he suspected, now stood entirely on end, which would at least match the portrait on the walls. "Good arm. That's real progress, actually. Last month you'd have taken my eyebrows too."

"This isn't funny!" She dropped to her knees beside him, hands hovering, afraid to touch him. Static jumped between her fingers and his sleeve in tiny indignant sparks. "I could have killed you."

"No," Silas said quietly. "You couldn't have. You pulled it wide the moment it left your hand. I felt it go past my shoulder instead of through my chest. You've never once hit anything you loved, Ulyana. Not even when you were trying."

She stared at him. Then her face did something complicated—rage and grief fighting for the same doorway—and lost to both at once.

"Don't," she warned, voice wobbling. "Don't be kind to me right now. I'm very angry with you."

"I know. I can see that. My coat can see that."

"*Silas.*"

"Ulyana." He held up a hand before she could summon another bolt. "Before you fry me properly, hear one thing. Ask Corvess—no, don't ask Corvess, he'll lie beautifully. Ask yourself this: if I sold you to Bren, why am I here? In the open. With my face on every wall in Veymark. Walking straight to the one place I knew you'd be, in the rain, alone, with nothing but a plum in my pocket and a coat that just saved my life."

Her brow furrowed. Static still danced along her shoulders, but slower now, thinking.

"...What plum?"

"The plum is not the point."

"Why would you bring a plum to a confrontation?"

"Because I hadn't eaten since yesterday and I assumed either I'd die quickly or find you hungry. Both outcomes required preparation." He fished the second plum from his pocket—slightly bruised, entirely sincere—and held it out between them like a treaty.

Ulyana looked at the plum. She looked at Silas. And then, with the terrible precision of a girl raised on the streets, she said: "Bren told Corvess you took the deal. He showed the bargain. Your mark was on the paper."

"My mark was on the paper," Silas agreed. "Because Bren put my mark on the paper. I signed for forged passage for two—one berth for me, one for a 'niece.' He changed the terms after I signed. Or he tore up the true terms and wrote new ones. Either way, the paper Corvess showed you was bait, and you swallowed it, and so did I, because I've spent three days believing *you'd* run to the Remnant willingly, that maybe you'd chosen them over me, and do you know how that felt?"

Ulyana's mouth opened. Closed.

"They used us," Silas said. "Both of us. Bren sold you the lie and Corvess bought it, because a grieving orphan makes a much better weapon than a trusting one. Grief doesn't ask questions. I should know. I've been grieving for ten years and it's only now anyone's bothered to lie to me directly."

The rain fell harder. Out past the seawall, the sea grumbled its agreement.

"That's—" Ulyana started, stopped. Her hands had gone still in her lap. "That's the stupidest plan I've ever heard. Why would Bren bother forging papers just to—"

"Because Corvess paid him more than the crown would. Because a weapon needs a wound first. Because it's cheap." Silas shrugged. "Evil is usually cheap, Ulyana. That's how you spot it. The expensive thing—the hard thing—is what we're doing right now. Sitting in the rain, being honest."

They sat in the rain, being honest, for approximately four seconds.

Then Ulyana burst into tears.

Not dainty tears. Great, gulping, ten-years'-worth sobs that shook her whole small frame, while the static around her flickered wildly—lightning stuttering across the clouds overhead in confused little pulses, as though the sky itself couldn't decide whether to comfort her or join in. She collapsed forward against Silas's chest, and he wrapped his arms around her, one hand cradling the back of her head the way he had when she was seven and feverish, and let her cry out what must have been three days of terror in one long, soaking, electrifying minute.

Every few seconds a spark popped against his shirt. He didn't flinch. He'd earned a few scorch marks. They matched the rest of him.

"I thought you sold me," she sobbed into his collar. "I thought—I hated you, I hated you so much, and hating you was worse than being hunted, do you understand? Being hunted was *easier*—"

"I know." His own eyes were stinging, though he'd have denied it under torture. "I know exactly. I've been doing it for a decade."

"I burned your coat."

"The coat forgives you."

"I nearly killed you!"

"You missed. Deliberately. Which means somewhere in that terrifying little thunderhead of yours, you knew." He tipped her chin up so he could see her face—blotchy, furious, relieved, all at war. "You never doubted me. Not all the way down. You doubted the story, because the story was rotten, and you're too sharp to swallow rot without tasting it."

"I swallowed it plenty."

"You spat most of it out. You ran instead of staying. You went to the seawall instead of letting them aim you." He smiled, tired and crooked. "Three days alone on these streets, using that gift everyone wants to own, and you survived. Do you realize what that means? Corvess says you need the Remnant. Crane thinks you need catching. The crown thinks you need burying. And you need none of it. You needed a plum and someone to tell you the truth."

Ulyana hiccupped. Wiped her nose on her sleeve, which sparked faintly in protest. "The truth being?"

"Neither of us betrayed the other. We were both played by men who profit from orphans' grief." He paused. "Also, you really must learn to aim. If you'd hit me, I'd be a rumor."

A wet, snorting laugh escaped her—the first in three days, judging by how surprised she sounded by it. "Your hair looks ridiculous."

"It matches the posters. I'm finally famous correctly."

* * *

They stayed on the seawall as the rain gentled, sharing the plum in solemn alternating bites, because Ulyana declared that after attempted murder and reconciliation, property lines were meaningless.

"So what now?" she asked eventually. Her head was on his shoulder. The static had settled to a faint warm hum, like a cat purring against him. "Corvess will send hunters. He won't accept losing his weapon."

"He already has. I passed two of his people near the tannery quarter. They had your description and my poster and a very poor understanding of stealth." Silas turned the plum pit over in his fingers. "And Crane's closing in too. Ives gave her everything about me before she died. Everything except you."

"She died protecting me." Ulyana's voice went small. "Corvess said she died because of me."

"Corvess says a lot of things. Here's what I say: Ives died holding a door so children could live. She chose that. Nobody made her, and nobody gets to spend her death like coin—not the crown, not the Remnant, and not you. Grief is yours to carry, Ulyana. It's not theirs to spend."

She was quiet a long moment. When she spoke again, something had shifted in her voice—harder, clearer, the sound of a girl deciding rather than enduring.

"Then I want to see it. What Ives died for. What everyone's lying about." She looked up at him, and her eyes had lightning in them, patient now instead of wild. "Corvess talks about the storm-crown like it's a hammer. The crown talks about my bloodline like it's a disease. Somebody in this city is telling the truth, Silas, and I'm done taking their word for it."

Silas felt the riddle stir in his memory—Mother Ives's last gift to him, pressed into his palm the night before the raid, along with a smile he hadn't understood until now.

*"When the archives drowned, the truth learned to swim. Go down where the old kings kept their paper, storm-caller. Paper rots. Truth keeps."*

"There's a place," he said slowly. "Under the old palace. The drowned archive. Ives gave me a riddle and I thought she was delirious." He looked down at Ulyana—at the heir the crown exterminated, the weapon the coven sharpened, the girl who'd just tried to kill him and then shared his plum. "It's flooded, it's guarded by thirty feet of black water, and every spy in Veymark is looking for us. But if there are answers anywhere, they're down there."

Ulyana stood, pulling him up by the hand, and a low roll of thunder crossed the harbor—so perfectly timed that they both looked up.

"Was that you?" Silas asked.

"No." She grinned, and for a moment she looked ten years old again, standing on this same wall, daring the sea. "But I think it agrees with me."

Hand in hand, the storm-caller and the storm-heir turned their backs on the city that wanted them—crown and coven alike—and walked toward the water that kept the truth.
