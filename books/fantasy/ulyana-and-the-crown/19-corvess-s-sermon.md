## 19. Corvess's Sermon

Corvess had been a schoolmaster once, in the years before the Cleansing, and he had never entirely stopped being one. It showed in the way he stood at the front of the catacomb chapel with his hands folded like a man about to take attendance, and it showed in the way the survivors of the Remnant arranged themselves before him—not as soldiers before a general, but as children before the one teacher who had refused to look away when the king's men came asking which of them could do *things*.

He was sixty-one years old, lean as a winter branch, with white hair cropped close to a skull that had once borne the tonsure of a scholar's guild and now bore only an old scar where an Inquisitor's chain had glanced off it. He wore grey wool and soft boots and carried no weapon heavier than a piece of chalk, which he used to mark the slate board he had hung on the catacomb wall twenty years ago, back when the Remnant numbered nine frightened people and a goat.

Tonight the chamber held better than two hundred.

"Sit, sit," he said gently, though they were already sitting. "Marta's people have brought bread. There is enough. There will always be enough, if we are careful." A small lie, told kindly. He had gotten very good at those.

The bread went around. Corvess watched them eat—the tanner with his burn-scarred hands, the twins from the fishmarket who could still water, barely, if they held hands and concentrated, the old ferryman who had rowed Remnant couriers under the city for so long that his arms were more rope than flesh. Children too. There were eleven children in the Remnant now. He counted them every night the way other men counted coins or sins.

And there, near the back, sitting between Mother Ives's herb racks because Ives had insisted she stay close: the girl. Ulyana. Storm-blooded. The last heir of a line the crown had spent a generation trying to make into a rumor.

She was laughing at something the younger twin had whispered, and her laugh was quick and unguarded, and Corvess felt the familiar ache behind his sternum that he had long ago decided was love, because it certainly was not doubt.

*You will understand,* he thought at her, the way he thought prayers. *One day you will understand that everything I do, I do so that no child ever again has to learn magic in a hole in the ground.*

He tapped the slate. The room quieted the way classrooms had quieted for him thirty years ago—a sound like rain beginning.

"Tomorrow night," he said, "we stop running."

***

Afterward, when the crowd had gone and the candles had burned down to stubs, Corvess sat with his lieutenants around the trestle table that served the Remnant for a war council. It was an absurd thing to call it. Half of them were bakers' apprentices. One had been a midwife. The war council consisted of seven people and a pot of barley tea, and Corvess loved each of them with the specific, terrible love of a man who intended to spend them.

"The registration depot on Candle Street," said Wren, who had been a quartermaster's daughter and was the closest thing the Remnant had to a strategist. She spread her charcoal map across the table. "It's the largest holding facility east of the river. Three hundred souls waiting to be processed. Processed," she repeated, and spat, delicately, into the rushes.

"Guard strength?" Corvess asked.

"Sixty regulars, two engine-teams, and—" Wren hesitated, and Corvess watched her hesitate, and knew what was coming, and let her say it. "Crane's justiciars rotated through last week. They may be back."

"Dame Crane," Corvess murmured. "Our courteous wolf."

"You've never met her."

"No. But I've read her reports." He had stolen copies through three layers of bribed clerks, and read them all by candlelight, and found them more frightening than any screed, because they were *fair*. Crane wrote down mitigating circumstances. Crane noted when a confession seemed coerced. And then Crane signed the warrant anyway, every time, in a hand as neat as a hymnal. "A woman who does evil beautifully is worse than a woman who does it sloppily. Sloppiness can be exposed. Beauty has to be *outlived*."

"And the girl?" asked Hask, the ferryman, in the tone of a man asking about weather he did not like the look of. "Is she coming?"

Corvess folded his hands. Here it was, then. The question underneath the question, surfacing at last after weeks of circling.

"She is coming," he said, "as our standard. Our proof. When the gates open and we stand in the palace square, the people of Veymark will not see ragged fugitives. They will see the storm-line returned. They will see legitimacy."

Hask frowned into his tea. "Legitimacy for what, exactly? Forgive me, Corvess, but you've said 'the crown must fall' so many times I've stopped asking what goes in its place."

"I've answered this."

"You've *preached* this," Hask said mildly. "Preaching and answering aren't the same animal. I've hauled both across the river in my boat and they behave very differently."

Someone laughed. Corvess permitted it. He had learned long ago that a leader who cannot bear laughter is a leader who will soon forbid everything else.

"A regency," he said. "Transitional. Ulyana takes the crown—as a symbol, a living seal—and governs through a council drawn from the districts until such time as the realm is settled."

"Until such time," Wren repeated, tasting the phrase.

"You object?"

"I object to nothing," Wren said. "I'd simply like to know how long 'such time' is, so I can write it down. I find that things written down get done, and things left vague get... extended."

Corvess smiled at her, warm as banked coals, and gave the answer he had prepared, the answer that was true in every particular except the one that mattered. "Until Veymark is safe. Not one day longer. You have my word, and you all know I have never broken my word to this circle."

This was even true. Corvess had kept every promise he had ever made to the Remnant. It was one of the quiet supports of his soul, load-bearing, inspected nightly. What he had *not* promised anyone—including himself—was that the regency would end while Ulyana still drew breath to dispute it. But that was not deception, he told himself. That was prudence. A ship's captain does not tell the passengers about the rocks; he simply steers around them.

*Around them,* said a small dry voice in him that sounded unpleasantly like Hask. *Or onto them, if it gets you to the far shore faster.*

He poured the tea.

***

He went to check on the girl before he slept, as he did most nights. It was not surveillance, he assured himself. It was pastoral care. The distinction mattered to him. Almost everything mattered to him; that was the trouble.

Ulyana was awake, sitting on the herb-bench with her knees drawn up, turning Silas Fairweather's little copper luck-knot over and over in her fingers. Corvess had seen it before. She wore it on a cord at her throat like a saint's medal. He had opinions about that charm which he kept, with great effort, behind his teeth.

"Can't sleep either?" he asked, settling onto the bench beside her at a respectful distance. Old habit. Frightened creatures needed room to bolt, and however much she belonged here now, some part of her was always measuring the exits. He respected that. It was good sense. He had taught a hundred children to measure exits.

"He's alive," she said. "Silas. Ives won't say much, but she doesn't have the face people have when someone's dead. I'd know that face. I've worn it." Her thumb moved over the copper knot. "They took him, didn't they. After the raid. After I ran."

"That is my understanding."

"And nobody's going after him."

Corvess was quiet for a moment. This was the part he hated—not the lying, which had become nearly effortless and therefore hardly counted, but the arithmetic of care. Every hour spent on Fairweather was an hour not spent on the three hundred in the Candle Street depot.

"The storm-caller made his choices," he said at last, gently. "He called a storm over a crowded market quarter. He worked charms illegally for ten years. He consorted with smugglers. I do not doubt he believed himself justified in every instance. Most men do. But we cannot pull every stone out of the crown's net, child. If we stop to save every individual, the individuals in the depot die waiting."

"They're not stones," Ulyana said. "Neither is he."

"No," Corvess agreed softly. "They're not. Neither are you, which is why I will not spend you on one man when you might buy freedom for thousands." He heard the sentence leave his mouth and winced inwardly at the shape of it—*spend*, *buy*—and then smoothed it over with warmth, the way he smoothed everything. "Sleep, Ulyana. Tomorrow we make history, and history keeps poor hours. Ask anyone who's tried to attend it."

She almost smiled. "You talk like a schoolmaster."

"I was a schoolmaster."

"What happened?"

Ah. There it was. The question he answered differently depending on the listener, and tonight, looking at her storm-grey eyes in the candlelight, he found he wanted to give her the true one—or the true-shaped one, which was nearly as good and safer to hold.

"I stayed," he said. "When the king's men came to my schoolhouse in Greyford and asked me to point out which of my students were gifted, I stayed, and I lied, and I hid eleven children in the cellar, and I got caught, and I watched them take the ones I couldn't reach. And I swore then that I would never again be a man who merely *hid* people from the fire." His voice did not rise. It never rose. "Hiding is not enough, Ulyana. Kindness is not enough. Bread in the Nightmarket and charms sold for copper and one good smuggler's mercy—they are all beautiful, and they are all insufficient, and the fire does not care how beautiful your insufficiency was. Only power stops the fire. Only the fire stops the fire. Do you understand?"

She looked at him for a long moment. "I think you're the saddest person I've ever met," she said, "and I grew up on the streets of Veymark, so that's saying something."

Corvess laughed—a real laugh, startled out of him—and patted her shoulder once, lightly, and left her to her sleeplessness and her copper knot.

In the corridor, alone, he stopped and pressed his hand flat against the cold stone and breathed until the shaking stopped.

*She is the saddest person I've ever met.* No, child. I am the most *resolved*. Sadness is what resolution looks like from the outside. From the inside it is clean. From the inside it is the only clean thing left.

***

Dawn found him on the catacomb steps above the harbor, where the Remnant's watchers kept their post behind a false wall in a cooper's shed. Below, Veymark woke: gulls screaming over the fish-stalls, the first ferries crossing the grey water, smoke rising from a thousand chimneys of a city that did not yet know what tomorrow would bring it.

Wren came up beside him with two cups of chicory and handed him one without asking. Twenty years of mornings in that gesture.

"The depot garrison changed shift patterns," she reported. "Crane's justiciars are confirmed for tomorrow evening. Which means if we hit the depot, we hit it while she's watching."

"Good," Corvess said.

Wren turned to stare at him. "I'm sorry—*good?*"

"When she watches us fail, it breaks the city's fear of her. When she watches us succeed, it breaks the crown's faith in her. Either way, Dame Rosalind Crane becomes a problem for her masters instead of for us. There is no outcome tomorrow in which she is not diminished." He sipped the chicory. It was terrible. Everything the Remnant made was terrible, and he loved it all. "Besides, Wren—we will need a spectacle. Symbols require audiences."

"You're enjoying this," she said slowly, not with accusation but with the wonder of a woman discovering a new species. "You're actually enjoying this."

"I am enjoying," Corvess corrected, "being *useful*. You should try it sometime. It's better than sleep."

But when she had gone down the steps, he stayed, cup cooling in his hands, and let himself look honestly at the feeling underneath his ribs, the one he kept in a locked drawer the way other men keep love letters.

It was joy. He could admit that much, alone, to the harbour wind. Tomorrow he would stand in the palace square with the storm-line's heir beside him and watch three centuries of burning end in a single night, and some vast organ-note of gladness would go through him that no hymn had ever managed. He had waited thirty years for it. He had earned it. The dead of Greyford had earned it for him.

The only cost was a girl's choice, and girls' choices, historically, had never once stopped the machinery of history. She would thank him later. Or she would hate him later. Both were acceptable. Gratitude was preferable, but hatred, properly directed, was also fuel.

He set down the cup and spoke to the watcher behind the false wall without turning.

"Send for Dree and the Saltwater twins. Quietly. Before the others wake."

"What for, Elder?"

"There is a matter I want handled personally, ahead of the assault." He watched a ferry cross the brightening water, low in the hull, riding heavy with morning cargo. "The storm-caller is in the White Tower. After tomorrow, everything in this city changes shape—and I will not have Silas Fairweather loose in the cracks when it does, telling the girl stories about mercy and third paths. Find where they're keeping him. Watch the tower. And if the opportunity presents itself before the assault..."

He paused, and chose the words with a craftsman's care, because words were the only tools he had never once misused.

"...see that the story ends quietly."

Behind him, faint through the floor, he could hear the Remnant waking—barley porridge, coughing, a child laughing somewhere in the dark—and Corvess closed his eyes and let the sound wash over him like a blessing, and began, silently, to rehearse tomorrow's sermon.
