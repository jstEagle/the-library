## 20. Crane's Doubt

The dead of the Tannery Quarter were catalogued in three ledgers.

That was the first thing Dame Rosalind Crane noticed, standing in the ash-stinking doorway of the coroner's hall on Fetter Lane, and it was not even the thing that would keep her awake that night. Three ledgers. One for property losses, kept by the Guild of Mercantile Interests, because of course the merchants had someone counting spoons before anyone had finished counting children. One for the wounded, kept by the surgeons, whose handwriting suggested they had been writing while their hands shook. And one for the dead, kept by a clerk named Pellowe who had been assigned to her office six months ago and who, she was beginning to suspect, was the only person in the entire Inquisition who had ever read a book all the way through.

"Forty-one," Pellowe said, when she reached his desk. He said it the way a man announces weather he doesn't like. "As of this morning. There may be more under the collapsed roofline on Cordwainer's Row. The masons won't dig until the walls are shored."

"Names?"

"Mostly." He turned the ledger toward her. "Some we'll never get. The fire took—" He stopped, adjusted his spectacles, and tried again. "There were families in the upper rooms of the tannery itself. Workers' housing. By the time the water-carts arrived..."

Crane took the ledger and read.

She had read death lists before. She had written them, in the early years, back when she believed — still believed, she corrected herself firmly, *still* — that registration protected people, that a name in a ledger was a shield rather than a target. She knew how to read a list of the dead without letting it reach anything vital. It was a professional skill, like picking a lock or lying to a bishop.

She got as far as the eleventh name.

*Tobias Wren, charm-seller, aged 54. Registered 12th of Harvestmonth, Year Nine of the Cleansing. Registration number 4471.*

She read it again. Then a third time, as though the ink might rearrange itself into something survivable.

"Pellowe," she said. "Tobias Wren. Charm-seller. Is this the Wren who kept the stall by the Nightmarket gate? Little man, grey beard, sold luck-knots and—"

"Fever-charms, I believe." Pellowe had the particular stillness of a clerk who has already anticipated where a question is going and does not wish to be standing there when it arrives. "He was registered. Lawfully. His papers were found on him. Or what was found of him." A pause. "His papers were in better condition than he was, madam."

"That is not funny, Pellowe."

"No, madam."

"He complied. He signed the edict. He paid his fee every year — I've seen the receipts, they cross my desk for audit — he wore the registration badge, he sold charms that couldn't light a candle if you begged them to. He was the edict working *correctly*. He did everything we told him the law required, and the law rewarded him by burning down his street at two in the morning because the Remnant chose a depot four doors from his bed."

Pellowe said nothing. Pellowe was very good at saying nothing. It was, Crane reflected, possibly why he had survived six months in her office, where saying something was frequently fatal to one's career prospects.

She closed the ledger with exaggerated care, the way one closes a door on a room where something is crying.

"I want the depot file," she said. "The Tannery Street registration depot. Target assessment, garrison strength, casualty projections. Everything the Remnant operation was built on."

"It's with the operational command, madam. Under Lord-Commander Vess's seal."

"I know whose seal it's under, Pellowe. I can read. Fetch it anyway."

* * *

Lord-Commander Aldous Vess received her in an office that smelled of beeswax and self-regard. He was a broad, pink, comfortable man who had risen through the Inquisition's administrative ranks by the simple expedient of never once having been present when something went wrong. His hands were soft. His voice was warm. Crane had watched him comfort a widow at a funeral once with such evident sincerity that she'd nearly believed he cared, right up until she saw him laughing about it in a carriage afterward.

"Dame Rosalind." He beamed at her. "Come in, come in. Sit. Wine? It's from my cousin's vineyard, you simply cannot get it in the city anymore, the roads being what they are."

"No wine, thank you."

"More for me, then." He poured anyway, two cups, and pushed one toward her side of the desk in the manner of a man who considers refusal a charming eccentricity rather than an answer. "You're here about the Tannery business. Terrible affair. Terrible. The Remnant have gone too far this time, haven't they? Burning their own quarter. Shows you what we're dealing with. Animals."

"The Remnant struck a military target," Crane said. "A registration depot. The deaths were caused by the reprisal — the crown's fire-engines, which were deployed without assessment of wind direction or adjacent structures. The depot sat in the middle of a residential block. Someone ordered engines into a street full of timber houses and pitch stores."

Vess's smile remained exactly where it was, but something behind it went very still, like a pond deciding whether to freeze.

"Well," he said. "War is untidy."

"This wasn't war. This was a Tuesday. Forty-one civilians are dead, including a registered charm-seller whose compliance file I personally audited last spring. Tobias Wren. Number 4471. He did everything right, my lord. We tell these people: register, obey, and you will be protected. That is the entire moral architecture of the edict. Take away that promise and every registered mage in Veymark has exactly one remaining reason to cooperate, and it isn't loyalty."

"You're very passionate this morning." Vess sipped his wine and studied her over the rim with small, pleasant eyes. "It's one of the things I admire about you, Rosalind. That fire. Of course, fire needs containing, doesn't it? Otherwise it spreads." He set the cup down. "What exactly is it you want?"

"The operational file. The order authorizing the engine deployment. The name of the officer who gave it."

"Why?"

"Because if our own conduct killed forty-one people, then our own conduct should answer for it. That's what justice is. If it only runs one direction, it isn't justice, it's just violence with paperwork."

The silence that followed had texture. Crane had interrogated smugglers, poisoners, and one genuinely memorable horse-thief who had cried throughout and stolen her knife while doing so, and none of those silences had felt quite like this one — the silence of a superior recalibrating his opinion of a subordinate in real time.

Vess leaned back. "Do you know what I was doing at Greyford, Dame Rosalind?"

"I understood you were in supply logistics."

"I was. Which means I signed the requisitions for the pyres." He said it lightly, conversationally, the way another man might discuss a harvest. "Eleven thousand storm-folk burned or broken in that campaign, and do you know what I learned? That the ledger balances either way. Order or chaos, mercy or severity — the empire continues. What matters is which way the fear points. Right now, the fear points outward, at the gifted, and the city holds together. You start pointing it inward — at officers, at orders, at the crown's own decisions — and you don't get justice, Rosalind. You get a crack. And cracks spread. You said so yourself."

"The difference—"

"The difference," Vess said, still pleasantly, "is that you are asking questions that have answers nobody wants, and I am telling you, as your commander and, I hope, your friend, to stop asking them." He slid a paper across the desk. Her own transfer request form, from three years ago, requesting reassignment to the coastal circuit. Denied. "I kept you here because you're the best investigator I have. Don't make me regret finding you interesting. Drop the Wren matter. Drop the engine order. Write your report on the Remnant cell, pursue the terrorists, and let the arithmetic of the city take care of itself."

"And the forty-one?"

"Are a tragedy," Vess said, "that occurred during a legitimate action, and will be remembered as such, in the official record, which I am sure you'll draft with your usual precision." He smiled again, warm as a hearth. "Drink your wine, Rosalind. My cousin really would be offended."

She looked at the untouched cup. Then she stood, thanked him with flawless courtesy, and left the wine sitting on his desk like an accusation.

* * *

She went, that evening, to the Tannery Quarter.

Not in uniform. She had a plain grey coat for occasions like this, though occasions like this were rare enough that the coat smelled faintly of its cedar chest. She walked Cordwainer's Row where the masons had finally shored the walls and the digging had begun, and she stood at the edge of the work site with her hood up like any mourner, watching men pull beams out of ash.

Wren's stall had been near the Nightmarket gate, but he'd lived above a chandler's shop on Cordwainer's Row, second floor, the window box still clinging to the charred sill with a few stubborn stalks of something green in it. Neighbors had left things at the ruin's edge: a boot, inexplicably; a jar of pickles; a child's drawing done in charcoal on board, depicting a small stick figure behind a market stall with a smile drawn so wide it wrapped around the head twice.

Crane picked up the drawing before she could decide not to.

"You knew him?" said a voice at her elbow. An old woman, soot to the elbows, holding a shovel like a soldier between engagements. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady.

"I — audited his registration," Crane said. It was true and insufficient and she heard both truths leave her mouth at once.

"Ah." The woman looked at her a long moment. "The lady from the crown."

"Yes."

"He came round after his inspection last year, terrified, hands shaking. Thought he'd be taken. Your people had flagged his charms for review." She said it without heat, which was somehow worse than heat. "I told him, Tobias, you're a law-abiding man, they've no cause. And he said — I remember it exact — he said, 'Marta, the law has no memory of causes. Only of names.'" She nodded at the ruin. "He was right, wasn't he."

Crane could not make her mouth produce a denial. She stood there holding a dead man's memorial drawing in a gloved hand and discovered that her voice had resigned without notice.

"There's a girl missing from the row," the old woman went on, softer now. "Liss. Eight years old. If your digging finds her..." She didn't finish. She didn't need to. She just looked at Crane the way people look at institutions when they are hoping, against all evidence, to find a human being inside one.

"We'll find her," Crane said. "I'll see to it personally."

"Marta!" someone called from the rubble line. "Second floor! You'd best come!"

The old woman dropped her shovel and ran. Crane followed, telling herself she was doing no such thing, she was merely walking briskly in the same direction, and then stopped pretending entirely.

They brought Liss out at dusk. Alive. Wedged in the space beneath a stair with a wet blanket over her head, dehydrated, smoke-scarred, furious at everyone, and — Crane noted with a part of her mind that filed details automatically and another part that was simply kneeling in ash holding a child's hand — alive. The girl clutched something in her fist and would not release it for anyone until Crane crouched down to her level and asked, quietly, what it was.

"A luck-knot," Liss croaked. "Mr. Wren give it me. For keeping safe." She held up a scrap of braided cord, scorched at one end, intact at the other. "It worked."

Crane looked at the knot. A harmless charm, the kind Wren had sold by the hundred, the kind that worked only because people believed in them — and a child had pulled it from the ashes of her home believing exactly that, and here she was, breathing.

*It worked,* thought Crane, and something in her chest that had been load-bearing for twenty years made a sound like ice on a pond.

* * *

She wrote the report that night. All of it. The engine deployment order, the absence of structural assessment, the wind, the pitch stores, the forty-one names including number 4471. She wrote it in her precise, unimpeachable hand, sealed it, and addressed it to the Office of Judicial Review, bypassing Vess entirely, which was not insubordination exactly but was certainly its close cousin.

Then she sat with it on her desk for a long time, candle burning down, the city quiet outside her window except for the distant sound of the harbor bells counting the hour.

Her father had been a magistrate. A minor one, a country one, a man who had spent thirty years ruling on boundary disputes and stolen geese, and who had told her once, when she'd gotten her commission and been insufferable about it: *"Rosalind. The law is a map, not the country. The day you mistake the map for the country is the day you'll walk happily off a cliff, following lines."*

She had thought it a quaint sentiment at nineteen. At forty-one, sitting across from a report that would either end her career or begin her conscience — possibly both, probably both — she understood that he had been giving her the only inheritance that mattered.

She thought, too, of the storm-caller. Fairweather. Ten years in her files, ten years of small infractions: unlicensed charms, unpaid fees, a pattern of kindness so consistent it had begun to look less like criminality than like a philosophy. She had assumed she was hunting a fugitive. Standing in the ashes of Cordwainer's Row, she wondered whether she had instead been building, entry by patient entry, a case against everything she served.

The candle guttered. She sealed the report a second time, unnecessarily, the way anxious people check locks.

And it was in that hour — past midnight, the ink dry, the die cast — that a knock came at her door. Soft. Unofficial. The kind of knock that meant someone had climbed stairs they shouldn't have known how to find.

She opened it on a soaked, shaking wreck of a man: Bren of the Saltwrights, smelling of bilgewater and terror, glancing over his shoulder at the empty street.

"Dame Crane," he rasped. "I've information. About the storm-caller. And the girl." He swallowed. "But I want a pardon in writing first. Because what I'm about to sell you... it's worth more than my life, and I mean to survive spending it."
