## 21. The Saltwright's Revenge

Bren had sold a great many things in his forty-odd years, and he liked to think he'd never once been proud of any of them.

He'd sold salt cod that was mostly salt and only nominally cod. He'd sold passage to folk fleeing debts, wars, and marriages — sometimes all three at once, which made for lively voyages. He'd sold his own crew's labor to the harbor guild at rates that would have made a slaver blush, if slavers blushed, which in Bren's experience they did not; they were too busy counting.

But this — sitting in Dame Rosalind Crane's rented rooms above the Nightmarket at one o'clock in the morning, dripping on her good rug while she poured him tea like he was an aunt come for a visit — this was the first time selling something had ever felt like swallowing broken glass.

"Your pardon," Crane said, sliding a document across the table. It was written on real vellum. Bren touched it the way other men touched relics. "Signed under my seal as Inquisitor of the Crown Edict. Full remission of all crimes past and future committed by yourself or your crew, save treason against the crown itself."

"And what counts as treason?"

"Selling me false information," she said pleasantly, "would be a start."

Bren laughed. It came out wrong, like a cough with ambitions. He took the tea instead. It was good tea. Of course it was good tea. Everything about this woman was good — her manners, her posture, the way she listened as though you were the most interesting thing she'd encountered since breakfast. That was precisely what frightened him about her.

"You understand," he said, "that I'm not doing this out of malice."

"I'm sure you're not."

"The Saltwrights are finished. You know that. Your people burned the Tannery Street depot, and now every guard in Veymark knows our faces, and every dock we ever used is watched, and half my crew's in your cells singing songs they learned from each other." He wrapped both hands around the cup because they wouldn't stop shaking otherwise. "I've got eleven people who eat because I feed them. Eleven. Some of them have children. I'm not selling the girl. I'm buying their lives."

"Of course," said Crane, in the tone of a woman agreeing with the weather.

That tone got under his skin worse than anything else could have. He set down the cup.

"She's not with Silas anymore. They split after the Remnant started burning things — I don't know why, and I don't care, and you can write that down. But I know where she went." He leaned forward. "The old herbwife's place. Mother Ives. The crooked house behind the tannery ruins, the one with the blue door everyone thinks is boarded up. The girl's been sleeping there three nights running. There's a cellar underneath with a tunnel that goes down toward the river gate."

Crane didn't write any of it down. She just looked at him, and Bren had the peculiar sensation of being read like a ledger, line by line, and found full of small errors.

"How do you know this?" she asked.

"Because I pay people," Bren said. "It's what I do. I pay the fishwife whose window faces the blue door. I pay the boy who sweeps the tannery lane. I paid a drunk who sleeps in Ives's woodshed and doesn't know he's useful, which is the best kind of informant there is." He shrugged. "You don't survive twenty years of smuggling on charm. Charm gets you hanged. Bookkeeping gets you rich."

Something flickered across Crane's face — not quite approval, but its cousin. "And the storm-caller? Fairweather?"

"Gone to ground. Nobody's seen him since the fire. My guess is he's dead in a ditch somewhere, or hiding so deep even the rats have lost track of him." Bren hesitated. This was the part he'd rehearsed on the walk over, the part where he told himself it wasn't really lying, just... editing. "If he surfaces, I'll send word. That's worth something too, isn't it?"

"It's worth something," Crane agreed, and pushed the pardon fully across the table. "Take it. And, Captain—" she smiled, warm as a hearth, "—thank you for your patriotism."

Bren took the vellum and fled.

* * *

Three days later, he stood on the roof of the customs house with a spyglass he'd bought off a dead man years ago and watched the crown's soldiers surround the herbwife's street at dawn.

He'd told himself he wouldn't watch. He'd told himself he'd be on his boat, gone upriver before first light, drinking bad brandy in some town where nobody knew his name. But boats needed repairs, and repairs needed coin, and coin needed time, and somehow — the way things somehow always went for Bren — he was still in Veymark, still watching, telling himself it was due diligence. Making sure the job was done clean. A professional interest.

The soldiers came quiet, which was worse than loud. No drums, no criers, no theater. Just thirty men in grey moving through the morning fog like ink through water, surrounding the crooked house with the blue door while the city slept around them.

Bren found himself gripping the parapet.

*Nothing's going to happen to her,* he told himself. *She's the last heir of the storm-line. They won't kill her. They want her breathing — she's worth more alive than the whole city.* And that was true, and it should have comforted him, except that he'd seen what the crown's interrogators did to valuable prisoners, back when he'd run cargo for men who knew such things, and *breathing* was a low bar. Breathing was something you could arrange for a person with hooks and hot irons and patience.

*She's a child,* said a voice in his head that sounded annoyingly like his late mother.

*She's a weapon,* said the voice he'd been using for three days, the practical one, the bookkeeping voice. *A loaded crossbow somebody left lying in a gutter. Better the crown holds it than the Remnant burns another street with it.*

*You watched her steal apples from your deck when she was nine,* said his mother's voice. *You let her keep them.*

*I let her keep them because throwing her overboard would've been more trouble than the apples were worth.*

*Liar,* said his mother.

Below, the soldiers reached the blue door. An officer knocked — politely, Bren noted, the way Crane's people did everything, courtesy laid over violence like icing over a brick. The door opened. There was a brief conversation Bren couldn't hear, and then—

Then nothing happened.

The officer turned and spoke sharply to his men. Two of them came out of the house escorting someone — a slight figure, hooded, hands bound, stumbling theatrically. Even from the roof, even through cheap glass, Bren could see the figure was walking wrong. Too tall. Shoulders too square. And when the hood slipped in the fog-light, the face beneath was a young man's face, sullen and terrified and entirely, gloriously, not Ulyana's.

"What," said Bren, out loud, to no one.

The soldiers marched their prisoner away. The officer stood a moment longer at the blue door, staring at it as though it had personally insulted him, and then followed.

Bren lowered the spyglass. His hands were shaking again, though this time not from fear.

The drunk in the woodshed. The fishwife. The sweeping boy. Somebody had talked — not to him, but past him, or over him, or through him. Mother Ives had known she was watched — of course she had, she was an old woman who'd survived thirty years of witch-hunts by being smarter than everyone hunting her — and she'd known exactly which mouths were for sale, and she'd fed them a story. Fed *him* a story. Let him carry it to the crown like a good little courier, and put some poor fool of a nephew or neighbor in a hood to take the arrest meant for the girl.

He'd been played. By an herbwife. With a broom closet and a hood.

Bren sat down hard on the parapet and laughed until his ribs hurt, and the laugh curdled halfway through into something else, something that sat in his chest like swallowed seawater.

Because here was the thing. Here was the ugly, bilgewater truth of it: for one moment — one bright, stupid moment — when the hood slipped and it wasn't her, relief had hit him like warm rum. She was safe. The child was safe. And then, right behind the relief, marching in its shadow like a debt collector, came the question:

*What happens tomorrow?*

Because Ives's trick worked once. Maybe twice. But Crane wasn't the officer at that door — Crane was the mind behind it, and Bren had sat across a tea table from that mind, and he knew it didn't make the same mistake twice. She'd go back. She'd dig. And next time there'd be no hooded decoy, because next time the crown wouldn't knock politely at dawn. Next time they'd burn the whole street and sift the ashes, and they'd call it justice, and the pardon in Bren's pocket would protect him from none of it, because he'd seen how the crown treated useful tools once they'd been used.

His mother's voice again: *You sold a child for paper, Bren. Paper burns.*

*Everything burns,* he told her. *That's the whole trick of living — be somewhere else when it does.*

But he didn't move. He sat on the cold parapet as the fog burned off and the city woke up below him — bakers and fishwives and sweep-boys, the whole ordinary miracle of Veymark getting on with its morning — and he thought about eleven mouths that ate because he fed them, and one mouth that might stop eating because he hadn't.

He thought about the night, ten years back, when a storm-caller had appeared at his dock soaked to the bone and asking for work, and Bren had taken him on because a man who could calm weather was worth ten sailors, and then kept him on long after the usefulness wore thin, because — though he'd have denied it under torture — he'd liked him. Quiet, careful Silas, who mended nets better than anyone and never cheated at cards and gave half his wages away in small kindnesses Bren pretended not to notice.

And Silas had loved that girl. Anyone with eyes had seen it. Fed her, taught her, lied to her face about her own safety the way you lie to children about monsters — gently, and badly.

*And you sold her anyway,* said his mother.

*Yes,* said Bren. *Because I'm a small man, Mam, and small men survive. That's the whole of my genius.*

His mother's voice, for once, said nothing. Which was somehow worse.

* * *

By evening he'd made his decision, though he'd have fought anyone who called it a decision. Decisions were grand things, made by admirals and inquisitors. What Bren did was smaller. What Bren did was this:

He went down to the tannery lane after dark, alone, no crew, and he stood in front of the sweeping boy — Tam, eight years old, gap-toothed, terrified of him as usual — and pressed a coin into his hand.

"Message," Bren said. "For the blue door. You don't knock. You chalk it on the step. Three lines, like this." He crouched and scratched in the dirt with a stick: three short strokes, crossed. "They'll know what it means. Can you remember that?"

"Yes, sir," said Tam, pocketing the coin with the speed of long practice.

"And Tam." Bren caught his shoulder. "After tonight, you don't sweep this lane anymore. You tell the fishwife, and the drunk, and anyone else on my list — I'll give you the list — that Bren pays them to forget his face. Understand? Whatever happens here next, you weren't ever paid by anybody."

The boy looked at him with those sharp eyes that saw everything on this street, and nodded slowly. "Is it the soldiers, sir?"

"It's the soldiers, lad."

"They took someone this morning. From the blue door. My da says it was the herbwife's nephew, come visiting at the wrong time." Tam's voice dropped. "My da says the real ones are still inside. All of them. And that the lady with the grey coat will come back."

Bren's stomach dropped through the cobblestones. "What lady with the grey coat?"

"Dame Crane. She walked the lane yesterday evening. Bought a bun from the baker. Asked him who delivered bread to the blue door." The boy shrugged, matter-of-fact as death. "Everybody knows, sir. Everybody always knows. That's what my da says."

*Everybody always knows.* Twenty years of bookkeeping, and Bren had forgotten the first rule of Veymark: there were no secrets in a city, only prices. He'd priced his information and sold it, and the whole street had priced theirs and given it away free, to whoever cared enough to ask kindly and buy a bun.

He straightened up. His knees cracked. Somewhere across the rooftops, thunder muttered — far off, out at sea, the kind of grumble that might be weather and might be something else, these days.

"Run along, Tam," he said. "Chalk the door. Then disappear."

The boy ran. Bren stood a moment longer in the dark lane, looking at the blue door with its fresh board nailed over it, and felt something move in his chest that he refused, absolutely refused, to name.

Shame was for men with souls. He had a ledger. And the ledger said he owed.

Tomorrow, maybe, he'd work out how to pay. Tomorrow he'd get his crew out, and find the storm-caller, and tell him everything — the pardon, the sale, the soldiers coming — and take whatever Silas chose to give him, which knowing Silas would probably be forgiveness, which was infinitely worse than a knife.

Tonight, though, tonight he heard boots in the distance. Many boots. Moving fast, and not toward the harbor.

Toward the tannery quarter.

"Oh, you clever, terrible woman," Bren breathed, and started to run.
