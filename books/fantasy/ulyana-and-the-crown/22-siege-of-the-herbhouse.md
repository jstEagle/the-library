## 22. Siege of the Herbhouse

The rain had stopped an hour before dawn, which was how Silas knew something was wrong.

Ten years in Veymark taught a person to read weather like scripture. Rain meant cover. Rain meant the watch stayed by their braziers and the Inquisition's lanterns drowned in the wet dark. A clear morning in the season of storms was either a gift or a trap, and Silas had never once been offered a gift by this city.

He stood at the herbhouse window with a cup of Mother Ives's bitter willowbark tea going cold in his hands and watched the street below, where a milk cart sat parked at an angle no milk cart would ever choose. The horse was calm. Too calm. Horses that had been standing since before dawn got restless.

"Your tea's stewing," Ives said behind him, not looking up from her mortar. She ground dried feverfew with the patience of a woman who had outlived three kings and considered it a personal accomplishment.

"Your milk cart's been there since the fourth bell."

"Marta Osgood takes her milk early."

"Marta Osgood's cart has one wheel painted blue. This one's all brown." He set the cup down. "And its driver is wearing boots worth more than my stall."

Ives's pestle paused — just for a heartbeat, just long enough. Then she resumed grinding. "You always did have a suspicious mind, storm-caller. It's why you've lived so long."

"It's why I've lived so long *so far*," Silas said. "There's a difference, and the difference usually arrives with soldiers."

She laughed at that, low and unbothered, and went back to her herbs, and Silas loved her a little for it, the way you love someone who refuses to be afraid on your behalf. The herbhouse smelled of rosemary and tallow and the particular warm-animal smell of too many children sleeping in too few rooms. Nine of them tonight. Remnant brats whose parents had been taken in the registration sweeps, plus two little ones fished out of the Tannery Quarter ash, plus a boy called Pip who technically belonged to nobody and had appointed himself everybody's older brother at the advanced age of eleven.

Silas counted them the way he counted everything now: what could be carried, what could be hidden, what would have to be left behind if the door came down.

The door came down at half past the fifth bell.

* * *

It did not come down quietly. Nothing about the crown had ever been quiet.

The battering ram hit wood like thunder's ugly cousin, and the herbhouse shuddered around them, jars rattling on their shelves, dust sifting down through the rafters like gray snow. The children were awake instantly — children of Veymark learned to wake fast and cry quiet — and Silas was already moving, throwing back the rug in the back room, hauling up the trapdoor to the cellar stairs that didn't lead to a cellar at all but down into the old sewer gallery, brick-arched and ankle-deep in water that smelled like regret.

"Pip." He crouched, took the boy by both shoulders. "You know the way to the tanner's grate?"

"I know every grate in this quarter," Pip said, with the fierce pride of a child who had survived by knowing things.

"You're second. I'm last. Nobody passes me, nobody stops, nobody talks. If we get separated, you take them to Marta Osgood's and you tell her the storm-caller sent you. She'll know."

Pip nodded, swallowed, and turned to the smaller ones with a gravity that made Silas's chest hurt. "Hold hands. Hold *hands*, Tam, I mean it—"

The door splintered on the third blow.

What happened next happened in pieces, the way terrible things do, each fragment sharp and separate: Ives stepping into the front room with her apron still dusted in feverfew. The first soldier coming through the broken frame, young under his helmet, his spear held wrong. Ives saying, pleasantly, as though greeting a customer, "Mind the floor, love, I've just swept it." And then light — not lightning, nothing so clean — the ugly amber flare of a suppression ward detonating off her outstretched hand, the kind of working that cost the caster days of strength and bought everyone else minutes.

"GO," Ives said, over her shoulder, without turning around. "Storm-caller, if you're standing in my kitchen when they come through that door, I will haunt you personally, and I am *very* tedious as a ghost."

"Ives—"

"*Go.*"

He went. It is the thing he would carry longest, longer than the sound of the door, longer than the light: that he went, because she told him to, because nine children needed him more than one old woman needed witnesses, and because some part of him — the part that had run from Greyford ten years ago, the part that never stopped running — obeyed before his heart could vote.

He pulled the trapdoor shut above him and dropped into the dark, into the smell of wet stone and worse, and took up the rear of a crocodile of frightened children wading through sewage water by the light of a charm-glow cupped in his palm.

Behind them, muffled through brick and earth, the herbhouse roared.

* * *

The sewers of Veymark were older than the city above them, laid by engineers who had believed in drainage the way other men believed in gods. They ran in great concentric rings beneath the streets, and somewhere in their branching the crown's maps simply gave up, marked HERE BE FLOOD, and moved on. Which meant that for once, the advantage belonged to people who had spent years learning the dark.

Pip led them true. Silas would remember that too — the boy standing at each junction with his chin up, pointing left or right with total certainty, hoisting the smallest girl onto his hip when the water deepened, whispering encouragement in the flat practical voice of a dockhand. *Nearly there. Mind your step. Don't drink anything, obviously.*

Twice they heard boots overhead, marching in the streets above, and twice they froze in the black until the sound passed. Once, far behind them, there came a crack of amber light through a grate, and a shout, and Silas felt it in his teeth — a suppression ward, spent. Ives's work, or another Remnant hand. Either way, someone up there was buying time with themselves.

The littlest girl, Tam, started crying around the third ring, silent tears rather than noise, which was somehow worse. Silas crouched beside her in the shallows.

"Do you know what a luck-knot is?" he asked.

She shook her head, hiccupping.

"It's a knot tied wrong on purpose. Three loops instead of four. Anyone can tie a proper knot — but a wrong one, a *kind* wrong one, takes knowing." He fished the cord from his sleeve, the one he'd braided that morning out of pure habit, and looped it clumsy and deliberate around her small wrist. "This says: nothing bad gets to find you today. It's not magic. It's better than magic. It's a promise somebody bothered to make."

Tam looked at the knot. Then at him. "Did it work? For other kids?"

"Every single time," said Silas Fairweather, liar, and Tam nodded solemnly, satisfied, and walked on holding Pip's hand, and Silas got up and followed with his throat full of glass.

That was the whole trade, he thought. That had always been the whole trade. You couldn't give children safety — no charm in the world sold that — so you gave them the shape of it, a knot, a story, a hand to hold, and prayed the world was too embarrassed to contradict you.

Tonight the world was not embarrassed. Tonight the world had sent a hundred men and a ram.

* * *

They came up through the tanner's grate into a gray drizzle and the smell of hides and lime, and Marta Osgood was already waiting, because of course she was; word traveled through Veymark's grandmothers faster than any courier the crown could buy. She herded the children inside with the brisk tenderness of a woman who had buried a husband and raised six alone and considered armed soldiers a household nuisance on par with rats.

"The little ones go in the root cellar," she said, counting heads with a finger. "The big ones can sit quiet in the loft. Pip — bread's on the table, and don't let Tam near the butter, she knows what she did."

"Yes, ma'am."

"And you." Marta turned on Silas, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious. "Sit down. Eat something. You look like something the tide refused."

"I can't stay—"

"I didn't say stay. I said *sit*. There's a difference, and the difference is bread." She shoved a heel of dark loaf into his hands and softened, just slightly, the fury banking to grief. "Was it bad?"

"It was Ives," he said, which answered it.

Marta closed her eyes for a moment. When she opened them they were dry, and that was its own kind of terrible. "She pulled my daughter out of a fever when the physicians wouldn't come. Charged me a jar of honey and told me I'd overpaid." Her jaw set. "So. What do you need, storm-caller?"

"Boats, eventually. Word to the Remnant cells that the herbhouse is burned and the children are here. And—" he hesitated, hating the word even as he reached for it, "—Ulyana. If anyone's heard where she is. Corvess has her, or had her. After today he'll want to move her, and if the crown finds her first—"

"After today," Marta said quietly, "everyone in this city will be choosing sides whether they want to or not."

She was right. Even then, even in the tannery's shadow, you could hear it beginning: bells ringing wrong, the wrong rhythm, the alarm pattern; voices in the street; a crier already bawling something about sedition and sheltering traitors. The herbhouse raid wasn't a raid. It was an announcement. The crown had found the Remnant's heart and put a torch to it, and now every sympathetic soul in Veymark had to decide, before breakfast, what they were willing to lose.

Silas ate the bread standing at the window, watching the smoke rise from the direction of the Nightmarket, thin and gray and final.

* * *

He went back. Of course he went back. Marta shouted something after him about fools and funerals and he pretended not to hear, hood up, face down, moving through streets that had gone strange overnight — shuttered shops, watchmen in pairs, a wall where someone had torn down his own wanted poster and left the nails gleaming like small accusations.

The herbhouse was a shell. That was the only word for it. Fire had taken the roof and most of the upper floor, and the front door hung off one hinge like a broken wing, and the soldiers were gone — called away to the next target, the next cell, the next announcement — leaving the ruin to steam gently in the drizzle.

Mother Ives lay in the doorway.

Not inside. Not collapsed somewhere private. In the doorway itself, on her back, arms folded across her chest with a dignity no one had arranged posthumously, because she had arranged it herself, in the last seconds, while the life ran out of her. Around her the floor was scorched in a perfect fan — the residue of every ward she'd had left, spent all at once, a working that should have killed three men and had evidently killed none, because she hadn't aimed it at them. She'd aimed it at the doorframe. To hold it. To hold the line a few minutes longer, so that a man and nine children could get thirty yards further down a sewer.

The soldiers hadn't killed her, Silas realized slowly, kneeling in the wet ash. They hadn't needed to. She had poured herself out to the last drop holding that door, and when the door finally gave, there was simply nothing of her left to stop.

On the inside of her wrist, written in the ash-fingered way herbwives kept their records, in a hand gone shaky at the end:

*Tell the storm-caller: the girl is the crown's better heir. Make sure she knows it. Not Crane's crown. Not Corvess's. Hers.*

And beneath that, smaller, almost amused even in dying:

*You owe me a jar of honey.*

Silas Fairweather knelt in the ruins of the Nightmarket's oldest kindness and did not weep, because the grief arrived not as sorrow but as fuel — cold, dense, patient, the kind that burns in a forge rather than a fire. He thought of Greyford, and the vow he'd made in the mud there, and how completely and how gladly he was about to break it. He thought of Ulyana, somewhere in the catacombs with a man who wanted to make her a banner, and of Dame Crane, somewhere in the White Tower with orders that grew uglier by the day, and of a city full of grandmothers and smugglers and children with luck-knots on their wrists, all of them about to find out what side they were on.

He folded Ives's hands more neatly. He touched two fingers to the ash on her wrist, taking the message into himself the way stone takes heat.

Then he stood, and the clouds that had been sulking over Veymark all morning gathered lower, and lower, and began, very softly, to growl.

Somewhere across the city, in the dark beneath the palace, Ulyana lifted her head as though someone had spoken her name — and the storm, for the first time in her life, spoke back.
