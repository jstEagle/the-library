## 23. What the Storm Knows

They buried Mother Ives at dawn, in the herb garden she had tended for forty years, beneath the rosemary she'd always said would outlive them all.

Ulyana dug the last of the grave herself. Her hands were blistered from the shovel, and her eyes were swollen shut almost, and she did not care. The others had offered—the Remnant survivors who had scattered back to the herbhouse after the raid, the ones who had hidden in root cellars while Ives held the door—but she had waved them off. Some things a body owed. This was one of hers.

*The crown's better heir.* That was what Ives had said, at the end, with soldiers hammering at the door and her old hands braced against the wood like she could hold back the whole kingdom by stubbornness alone. *The crown's better heir.* Not to Corvess. Not to the dead king's ghost. To Ulyana.

She pressed the rosemary sprigs into the turned earth with both palms, as if she could push the words down into the roots where they might grow into something true.

"You're supposed to say something," said Pip, the smallest of the herbhouse children, tugging at her sleeve. He was seven and missing two teeth and had watched Ives die without understanding why nobody could fix it.

"She hated speeches," Ulyana managed.

"Say it anyway."

So Ulyana stood over the fresh grave in the grey morning light and said, "You told me once that thyme grows best when you're rough with it. And that people are the opposite." Her voice cracked. She pushed on. "I'm going to be better at people. I promise."

Pip nodded solemnly, satisfied, and went off to bother somebody else, which was probably exactly the eulogy Ives would have wanted—short, honest, and followed immediately by breakfast.

The Remnant fed them all porridge that tasted of ash and grief, and then Corvess arrived.

***

He came through the garden gate like winter coming through an open window. The other survivors bowed their heads. Ulyana did not.

"Ives is dead," he said. It wasn't an accusation, quite. It was worse: it was arithmetic. "And Silas Fairweather is in the White Tower. And the edict still stands. Three facts, child, and only one of them can we change."

"Then change that one," Ulyana said. Her voice came out flat and strange. Everything inside her was flat and strange since the raid, since the sewers, since she had heard Ives's last breath rattle out through a door she hadn't been allowed to hold. "Go get him."

"That is being arranged." Corvess folded his hands into his sleeves, patient as a stone that intended to outlast the river. "But hear me first. What happened at the herbhouse will happen again, and again, until we finish this. The crown has shown us its heart. We must show it ours."

"Your heart is fire," Ulyana said. "I saw the tannery quarter."

Something flickered behind the elder's eyes—not guilt, never guilt, but recalculation. "The depot burned. The depot needed to burn."

"The *street* burned. Marta's neighbor's boy pulled a baby out of the ash. Did the depot need that too?"

Corvess regarded her for a long moment. When he spoke, his voice gentled, which was somehow more frightening. "Grief makes us cruel with the ones who remain. I understand. Rest, child. Eat. When you're ready, your gift is waiting, and it has work to do."

He left her standing among the rosemary, holding words she hadn't said and fury she didn't know where to put.

That night she climbed onto the herbhouse roof, because roofs were where she thought best, and the sky overhead was churning like a pot about to boil.

She had been fighting the gift for three days. Every time the pressure built behind her ribs—that hum, that itch, that feeling of a door rattling on its hinges—she shoved it down. Swallowed it. Sat on it. Because every time she let even a thread of it loose lately, something burned or someone screamed, and she couldn't bear for her hands to be the shape of that.

But the storm wouldn't be swallowed tonight. It pressed against the inside of her skin like floodwater against a door, and somewhere far off thunder rolled, long and low, like something enormous clearing its throat to speak.

*Ives is dead,* Ulyana thought, and the thought had lightning in it. *Silas is in a cage. And everyone keeps telling me what my power is for.*

Her grandmother—Ives, not by blood but by everything else—had taught her herbs. *Listen to the plant,* she used to say. *It'll tell you what it wants to be. Tincture or tea or salve. Stop deciding for it.*

Stop deciding for it.

Ulyana sat down cross-legged on the wet slate tiles, closed her eyes, and for the first time in her life, stopped fighting.

***

It was like opening a door she'd been leaning against for ten years.

The storm poured in—not as pain, not as the white-hot crackle she knew, but as *sound*. Vast, layered sound, like standing inside a bell while it rang. Rain on ten thousand roofs. Wind through a hundred thousand trees. The slow groan of clouds shouldering each other across the sky.

And underneath all of it, threaded through it, voices.

Not words at first. More like... presences. Warmths and weathers. She felt them the way you feel a room full of sleeping people—each one distinct, each one dreaming. Old ones and young ones. Fierce ones and gentle ones. A chorus so large it should have been deafening and instead felt like being *held*.

"Oh," Ulyana whispered, and the rain around her softened, listening with her.

They were storm-callers. All of them. Everyone who had ever carried the gift, reaching back further than names—and they were all still here, in some way that made no sense and perfect sense at once, because storms don't end, they just pass their water along. Every rain that had ever fallen had fallen through *them* first.

And then, closer than the rest, familiar as bread:

*Mother.*

Not her own mother—she barely remembered her own mother, just flour-dusted hands and a humming tune. This was another mother entirely. This presence came wrapped in the smell of ozone and woodsmoke and something sweeter, like honey cakes left to cool on a windowsill in a house that smelled always faintly of rain.

*You're the boy's mother,* Ulyana thought at it, wonder cracking her open. *Silas's.*

The presence rippled. If a warmth could laugh, this one laughed—a sound like sun breaking through a squall.

Images came then, not in words but in weather. A small kitchen in a coastal town, rain lashing the windows while a woman danced a toddler across the floorboards, both of them laughing at the thunder as if it were music written just for them. A boy—Silas, small and round-faced and gap-toothed—standing in a doorway holding up a drawing: a scribbled family under a scribbled sun, except the sun had been drawn with lightning bolts for rays, and the woman had kissed his head and said, *that's us exactly, little spark.*

A lesson, later. Older Silas, maybe eight, sitting on a seawall while his mother held his hands palm-up to catch the rain.

*Don't fight it,* she was saying—her voice arriving now not as memory but as instruction, direct, warm, close as breath. *Everyone fights it. The crown taught us fear and we taught ourselves shame. But the storm isn't a weapon, lovey. It's a conversation. It's been talking since before there were ears to hear it. Your job isn't to command it. Your job is to listen, and then answer honestly.*

*What does it say?* young Silas asked, in the memory.

*Everything,* his mother said. *It remembers everything. Every hand it's passed through. Every heart. We don't own it—we carry it awhile, and then we hand it on richer than we found it. That's all inheritance is.*

The images shifted. Darker weather now, and Ulyana flinched—but the mother-presence steadied her, wrapped around her like a cloak against the rain. She saw Greyford. She saw the battle from above, from inside the storm itself, saw a woman standing on a ridge with her arms raised, holding back a wall of lightning meant for civilians, holding it, holding it, until it burned through her—

*No,* Ulyana tried to pull away. *No, I don't want—*

*Look,* the mother said gently. *This is the part they'll never put in songs, so someone has to know it truly. Look.*

Ulyana looked.

And she saw that the woman wasn't fighting the enemy army. She was fighting the *storm*—her own side's storm-casters, wild with battle-fury, whose lightning would have drowned friend and foe alike. She caught it all in her own body. She redirected it skyward in sheets, buying time, buying lives, spending herself coin by coin.

The last thing, before the memory dimmed: Silas's mother turning her face toward the sky—toward *her*, across all the years, as if she could see the girl sitting on a rooftop decades in the future—and smiling.

*Now you know how he learned sacrifice. He didn't learn it from losing me. He learned it watching me choose it. And he's been choosing it ever since, the dear foolish boy, every charm sold for copper, every kindness hidden, every year of exile borne like a coat too heavy to take off.*

*Why are you telling me this?* Ulyana asked, and her voice in the storm-space was very small.

*Because you've been asking the wrong question, child. You keep asking what your power is FOR—as if it were a sword someone handed you and pointed east. But power like ours doesn't come with directions. It comes with company.*

*Company?*

*Every caller who ever lived is in this weather. When you listen—truly listen—you're never alone with the choice. You're surrounded by everyone who ever made it. Some chose wrong. Some chose beautifully. And none of them, not one, ever had to choose alone.*

The storm breathed around her. And Ulyana understood, suddenly and completely, what Corvess would never understand: he wanted to give her a purpose, a target, a war. He wanted to point her east.

But the storm had never once pointed anywhere. It had only ever *asked*: what do you see? what breaks your heart? what do you want to protect?

*I see Veymark,* Ulyana answered, before she knew she was speaking aloud. *I see the Nightmarket and the widow Marta and Pip's missing teeth and the harbor lights and all of it, all of it, the whole loud unfair alive city. That's what I want to protect.*

The chorus stirred. Warmths pressed close—not steering her, just... standing with her. An entire lineage of weather leaning in to hear a girl decide.

*Then that,* said Silas's mother, and her voice was thick with something like pride, *is the truest thing any of us has heard in a generation.*

***

She came back to herself soaked through and kneeling on the roof tiles, tears indistinguishable from rain, laughing and sobbing in the same breath.

Below her, a door banged. Voices—urgent, hushed. She wiped her face and slid down the drainpipe in time to meet Pip barreling into her legs.

"Ulyana! Ulyana, there's a man at the gate, he's all wet and limping and he's got a message, he says—" Pip grabbed her hand and pulled. "He says the storm-caller broke out of the White Tower. He says Silas is free."

The rain fell soft around them, gentle as a blessing, and Ulyana stood very still in it, hearing beneath the rainfall that vast and patient chorus, and somewhere in it, unmistakably, a mother's laughter.

"He's hurt," she said. It wasn't a question; she could feel it now, could feel things in the weather she'd never felt before, a new ache on the edge of the storm's attention, familiar the way your own heartbeat is familiar. "And he's heading for the water. The flooded archive. Under the old palace."

Pip stared at her. "How d'you know that?"

Ulyana looked up at the churning sky, at the whole breathing inheritance overhead, and smiled through the last of her tears.

"Family told me," she said. "Come on. Fetch Corvess. And fetch my boots."
