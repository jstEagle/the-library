## 24. The Prisoner Exchange

The trap was, Crane had to admit, a workmanlike piece of nonsense.

A "reliable informant" had reported that the storm-caller would be at the tannery docks at moonrise, alone, to meet a contact from the undercity. The informant was reliable in the way that all of Bren's people were reliable — which was to say, reliably for sale — and the report had the unmistakable texture of bait. Silas Fairweather had evaded three patrols, two bounty crews, and one embarrassingly thorough sweep of the Nightmarket by hiding inside a shipment of pickled herring. A man with that kind of luck did not stand alone on an open dock at moonrise.

So Crane went herself, and she did not go to the dock.

She went to the widow Marta's house.

It had taken her eleven days to see it, which she considered eleven days too many. Every report of Fairweather's near-captures traced back along the same crooked line through the lower city, and every crooked line ended, eventually, within four streets of the same door. The old woman's grandson wore a tower guard's uniform and had filed three conflicting duty rosters that month. And the widow bought more bread than one household could eat, which in Veymark meant exactly one thing: someone was hiding in her cellar.

Crane came alone, out of uniform, in a plain gray coat, carrying a basket of apples she did not intend to eat. She knocked the way a neighbor knocks.

Marta opened the door, looked at her for a long moment, and said, "You're the Inquisitor."

"I'm told I'm recognizable even out of uniform."

"It's the way you knock." Marta stepped aside without being asked. "Like you own the house already. Come in before the street sees you standing there. You'll have tea, and you'll drink it, because I made it before you knocked. I heard your boots stop at the corner."

Crane stepped into the warm little kitchen, where a pot of stew breathed on the stove and a cat regarded her with open suspicion from atop a cupboard. She set the apples down. "Madam—"

"Marta. If you're about to arrest me, you'll want to know I'm seventy-one years old and my knees are bad, so we should get it over with quickly either way."

"I'm not here to arrest you." Crane sat, because refusing the chair would have been ruder than anything else she might do tonight. "I'm here because I believe you know where Silas Fairweather is, and I believe he is not at the tannery docks where my informant says he will be, and I would very much like to be proven right, because if I am wrong, then he is standing on an open dock at moonrise in front of forty soldiers."

Marta poured the tea. Her hands did not shake, though Crane noted they wanted to. "And if I tell you nothing?"

"Then I go to the docks, and we find out together whose information was better."

The cat jumped down, crossed the kitchen, and — with what Crane could only describe as editorial intent — rubbed against her boot. Marta looked at the cat, then at Crane, and something in her face shifted from defiance to a tired kind of arithmetic.

"You've been asking around the market," Marta said. "About him. About who he was, before the edict."

"I read his file. All ten years of it. It reads less like a criminal record than a ledger of small mercies." Crane turned her cup. "Fever-charms. Luck-knots sold at cost or given away. A girl fed off his own purse. Do you know what strikes me most about it?"

"That he was careless?"

"That he was *kind*, madam, in a city where kindness is now a capital offense, and he knew it, and he did it anyway, for a decade." She met the old woman's eyes. "I have burned people for less in the files of men far worse than him. And I find I cannot stop reading this one."

Marta sat down across from her. For a while there was only the stew and the wind against the shutters.

"He's not here," Marta said at last. "He moved on two nights ago. He said staying put was how people got caught, and he'd stayed put too long already." She hesitated. "But the girl comes by. Ulyana. She checks on me, brings water up from the well so I don't have to manage the stairs. She'll come tomorrow, near midday, if the streets are quiet."

"Then tomorrow at midday," Crane said, rising, "I will arrest her."

Marta stood too, faster than her knees should have allowed. "You just spent five minutes telling me you couldn't—"

"I didn't say what I'd do after I arrested her." Crane pulled her hood up. "Thank you for the tea. It was terrible. Don't change it; I'll know something's wrong if it isn't."

She let herself out. Behind her, she heard the cat yowl once, as if filing a complaint.

* * *

They took Ulyana at the well, gently, which somehow made it worse.

There were six soldiers, and Crane had instructed them twice that the girl was fifteen and frightened and not to touch her harder than necessary, but instructions only stretch so far when the prisoner's hair stands on end with static and the well-water begins to tremble in its bucket. One soldier's sword-arm went numb to the elbow before they got the leather cuffs on her — cuffs lined, Crane noted grimly, with the dampening salt the armory had finally started issuing.

Ulyana did not scream. That was the thing Crane would remember afterward, in the long accounting she was beginning to keep of such things. The girl planted her feet on the cobbles, looked at the six armed men ringing her, looked at Crane, and said:

"You're making a mistake."

"People keep telling me that," Crane said. "Lately I've begun to wonder if they're coordinating."

"They'll trade me for him." Ulyana's chin came up. "That's what you want. Him. So here's the offer: take me instead. Let him walk. He's worth more to you anyway — he's the storm-caller, he's the one on the walls. I'm just—" She stopped herself. Whatever she had been about to say — *just an orphan, just nobody* — she swallowed it, and Crane watched her swallow it, and thought: *there goes a girl deciding not to make herself small.*

"You're the last heir of the storm-line," Crane said quietly. "By our own records, you are considerably more valuable than he is."

"Then you'll want me more." The static crackled. Somewhere above them, clouds that had been minding their own business began to gather with suspicious speed. "Take the deal, Dame Crane. Before I do something we'll both regret."

The nearest soldier shifted nervously. Crane raised one hand, and the squad stilled.

"Cell nine," she said. "The dry cell, not the flooded one. And someone get her supper — the actual supper, not whatever Cook is passing off as soup this week."

Ulyana blinked. "You're — feeding me?"

"You'll fight better negotiations on a full stomach," Crane said, and turned away before her face could betray anything at all.

* * *

Silas walked into the White Tower three days later, of his own free will, because Ulyana had sent him a note.

The note had been smuggled out folded inside a heel of bread, in handwriting that wobbled with fury: *They have me. They're feeding me, which is somehow scarier. Come trade yourself before I escape on my own and ruin everything. Also the soup really is terrible. — U*

He had read it four times, laughed once — a short, cracked sound that startled him — and then sat very still for a long while, doing the arithmetic of love, which never balances.

So now he stood in the receiving hall of the White Tower, hands bound in front of him, hair plastered flat with rain, facing Dame Rosalind Crane across a table bearing a single candle and two cups of tea. Only one cup was poured.

"You came," Crane said.

"You left a message in a dead-drop I didn't even know you knew about. Very tidy. I almost felt flattered."

"I try to extend professional courtesy." She pushed the second cup toward him. "Drink. You look half-drowned."

"I was. There's a storm coming in off the sea. I can feel it in my teeth." He wrapped his bound hands around the cup anyway. "Where is she?"

"Safe. Fed. Insulting my cook, according to the duty sergeant, who reports that she has organized the other prisoners in her block into a card game and is winning." Crane sat. "She's fifteen, Master Fairweather, and she offered herself in exchange for you before I had asked her a single question. I have interrogated smugglers, assassins, and one archbishop, and I have never seen anyone negotiate that fast or that badly."

"She gets it from me," Silas said. "Badly, I mean. I taught her everything I know about self-sacrifice, which is mostly how to do it without asking anyone's permission first." He looked up. "Name your terms."

"The terms are simple. You confess fully — the charms, the storm at the harbor, the escape of the Saltwright fugitives. In exchange, the girl walks free. Unregistered, unmarked, out of Veymark if she wants, with papers I will forge myself."

Silas stared at him — at her. "You'd forge crown documents."

"I've recently discovered I'm capable of a great many things I'd have called impossible a year ago." Crane's voice was level. "Here is what you need to understand about this arrangement, Master Fairweather, and why I want you to understand it. Three weeks ago, the reprisal after the tannery depot fire killed thirty-one people in a single block. Among them was Alden Moss. Charm-seller. Registered, law-abiding, licensed, taxed. His name was in our rolls, in good standing, for six years." She laid a paper on the table between them. "I have read the casualty list eleven times. His name does not become less dead no matter how I read it."

Silas said nothing. The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"The Edict of Cleansing was written," Crane continued, "on the premise that magic unchecked burns cities down. And yet every death on that list except the arsonists' targets was caused by *our* response. I have begun keeping a different ledger, Master Fairweather. Mine has columns the Edict doesn't account for." She tapped the table once. "Do you accept the terms?"

"I accept," Silas said slowly, "and I don't trust them. Which I suspect you respect more than trust."

"Immensely more." For the first time, something that was almost a smile touched the corner of her mouth. Then it faded. "One more thing, and then the sergeant comes to collect you. The confession you give tomorrow, in the great hall — give it truthfully. All of it. Even the parts that shame you."

"Why?"

"Because a lie would be easier for them to dismiss. But a man who stands in front of the tribunal and says *yes, I broke the law, and here is every kindness it made criminal* — that man makes the law defend itself out loud. And laws," Crane said, gathering her papers, "sound very different when they have to speak."

* * *

The exchange happened at dawn on the tower steps, before witnesses, because Crane insisted it be witnessed — "An exchange done in shadow can be undone in shadow; one done in daylight becomes a matter of public record, and public record is the one thing chancelleries fear more than storms."

Ulyana came down the steps in borrowed boots two sizes too big, saw Silas standing bound between two guards, and broke into a run. She caught herself two feet away, remembering the cuffs, the soldiers, the audience — and settled for glaring at him with an intensity that could have cured fish.

"You absolute *idiot*," she said.

"Good morning to you too."

"I told you to stay away! I had a plan!"

"You had a plan?"

"I had the beginnings of a plan!"

"Children," Crane said, and both of them shut their mouths with the identical guilty expression of people who have been caught sharing habits. She nodded to the guards. "Release him. Give the girl the road-papers."

The cuffs came off. Silas flexed his fingers, wincing. Ulyana snatched the packet of forged papers from Crane's hand, checked it with the brisk competence of a girl who had learned document-reading from smugglers, and frowned.

"These are good fakes," she said suspiciously.

"I don't do anything badly," said Crane.

"You arrested me."

"And released you within the week. Consistency is for weather." Crane turned to Silas. "Master Fairweather. Your confession is scheduled for tomorrow morning, in the great hall. You will be held in the east cell overnight. The east cell," she repeated, with the faintest emphasis, "is on the third floor. Its window latch has been reported faulty — I filed the report myself, this morning, and maintenance is notoriously slow."

Silas went very still.

"A faulty latch," he said carefully.

"Catastrophically faulty. Practically decorative. A strong gust — and there is a storm coming in off the sea, I'm told — might conceivably blow it open in the night. If a prisoner were foolish enough to leave through such a window, he would drop into the courtyard, cross to the postern gate, which is also under-maintained, and find the gate-watch changed at midnight. The new watch," Crane said, examining her fingernails, "consists largely of young guardsmen whose grandmother lives on Candlemaker's Row and whose little sister once recovered from a fever no one could explain. They are, I'm afraid, extremely unreliable men."

Ulyana looked from Crane to Silas and back again, her mouth opening and closing. "You're — you're letting us *escape*. You arrested us to let us escape. Why did you even bother arresting—"

"Because the exchange had to be witnessed, child, and because—" Crane paused. For a moment, the Inquisitor's armor slipped, and what showed underneath was tired, and older than her years. "Because I needed to know whether mercy still worked. Whether it was still *possible*. One does experiments."

"And if your superiors ask what happened?" Silas said quietly.

"Then I will report that the prisoner escaped through negligence, and I will accept whatever punishment follows, and I will sleep better for it than I have in months." She straightened her coat. "The storm arrives tonight, Master Fairweather. Try not to drown on your way out of my custody; the paperwork for prisoners lost to weather is unbearable."

* * *

The east cell was exactly as advertised: cold, sparse, and equipped with a window whose latch gave up its duties at the first persuasive tug, sighing open onto the courtyard below like a door that had been waiting all evening for permission.

Silas climbed out onto the sill and paused, looking back at the little stone room, at the candle Crane had left burning on the ledge — left, he noticed, deliberately, so that anyone glancing up would see flickering light and assume occupation.

"Mercy as rebellion," he murmured to himself. "She's going to hang for us someday."

Below, in the shadows by the postern gate, a figure in borrowed boots waved both arms with the urgent gracelessness of someone who had already bribed two unreliable young guardsmen and was rapidly spending the remainder of her patience.

"Hurry *up*!" Ulyana hissed up at him. "The storm's coming and I can feel it in my teeth, and if we're caught I am *not* trading myself for you again, I'm telling you now, my negotiating days with that woman are over—"

Silas dropped from the windowsill into the dark, laughing — actually laughing, for the first time in ten years — and ran for the gate, and behind them the promised thunder rolled in off the sea like applause.

Neither of them saw Dame Crane watching from the tower window above, candle in hand, counting them out of her custody like coins she was glad to lose.

And neither of them knew that in the great hall below, the tribunal had already convened early — and that the questions waiting for a missing prisoner had a way, in Veymark, of finding whoever loved him instead.
