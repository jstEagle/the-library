## 25. Interrogation by Candlelight

The cell they gave Silas Fairweather was, by White Tower standards, a kindness.

Rosalind Crane had personally chosen it. Third sublevel, no drain in the floor, no chains bolted to the wall — she had ordered those removed an hour before the prisoner arrived, and had endured the look her clerk gave her for it. A single cot. A stool. A candle on the table between them, because she had found over eleven years of work that people confessed differently in candlelight than under the flat white glare of a mage-lantern. Lanterns made men feel like specimens. Candles made them feel like guests who had stayed too long at dinner and were about to say something regrettable.

She wanted Silas Fairweather to feel like a guest.

Not that he looked like one. He sat across from her with his wrists still ringed in suppression-iron — standard, non-negotiable, and she'd checked twice that it was fitted properly, because storm-callers were notorious for what they could do with badly fitted iron and a bad attitude — and he was looking around the cell with the air of a man appraising secondhand furniture.

"No window," he observed.

"You're a security risk."

"I'm a *shopkeeper*."

"You're a registered fugitive with a decade of undocumented charm-craft behind you, currently held in the most secure facility in Veymark." She folded her hands on the table. "And your shop was in the Nightmarket, which means you paid protection to the harbor guard, which means you have opinions about corruption, which means we'll get along famously once you stop pretending to be boring."

Something flickered at the corner of his mouth. Not quite a smile. The ghost of one, come to pay its respects.

"Inquisitor Crane," he said. "I've heard about you."

"Only good things, I hope."

"Mostly that you're polite right up until the moment you ruin someone's life."

"Well." She tilted her head, conceding the point. "Everyone needs a reputation. Mine keeps me from having to raise my voice."

She opened the ledger.

It was a real ledger — not the case file, which lived in her office and ran to four hundred pages now, but a working notebook, leather-bound, its corners soft from handling. She had learned early in her career that prisoners watched what you wrote down, and that the illusion of completeness could accomplish more than actual completeness ever did. In truth there were gaps in her knowledge of Silas Fairweather wide enough to drive a salt-cart through. He didn't need to know that.

"Silas Fairweather," she read aloud. "Exiled from Greyford following the Battle of—"

"Don't," he said quietly.

She looked up. His face hadn't changed, but his hands had gone still on the table, and stillness, in her experience, was louder than any flinch.

"The Battle of Greyford is public record," she said.

"It's public *propaganda*. There's a difference."

"Then correct the record. That's what this interview is for." She turned the page. "You were seventeen."

He said nothing.

"You were seventeen," she repeated, softer now, "and they put you on a boat out of the harbor with nothing but the clothes you were wearing and a name that followed you like a debt collector. And you came here. To a city where your kind are illegal. And instead of doing — frankly, Silas, anything — you sold fever-charms to dockworkers' wives and luck-knots to fishermen for copper, and you paid off guards so nobody looked too hard at you, and—" she paused, and let the pause do its work, "—and you fed an orphan girl bread every morning for years and pretended you didn't care whether she ate it."

The candle flame leaned, though there was no draft.

"Where is Ulyana?" Crane asked.

"I don't know."

"That's the first lie you've told me, and I want you to know I noticed, and I want you to know I'm going to be gracious about it, because I think you actually don't know where she is *right now*, which is different from not knowing where she is." She tapped the ledger with one finger. "But we'll come back to it. First I want to talk about the widow Marta."

His jaw tightened. "What about her?"

"Her daughter had marsh-fever when she was six. Fever-charms are a Class Three working — technically harmless, technically within the tolerance the edict allows for folk remedies. But yours weren't Class Three. Our artificers examined the residue in your old stall. Your charms didn't suppress fever. They *negotiated* with it. That's living magic, Silas. That's a conversation between two wills, and one of them was yours." She leaned forward. "You broke the edict every day for ten years. Small breaks. Kind ones. But the law doesn't have a column for *kind*, and neither do I, officially."

"And unofficially?"

The question hung there between them, and Crane found, to her mild alarm, that she did not have an immediate answer prepared.

* * *

She had interviewed forty-one gifted persons since taking the Veymark posting. She kept a private tally in her head: nineteen had broken quickly, twelve had broken slowly, seven had said genuinely nothing worth hearing, and three had frightened her — not with power, but with certainty, the kind that has no doors or windows in it.

Silas Fairweather was shaping up to be none of these. He was doing something worse. He was being *reasonable*.

"Let me tell you how I see it," he said, when the silence had gone on long enough. "You've read my file. You know what I did before Greyford. You know what the covens were doing in those years, and what the crown did about it. So you tell me, Inquisitor — when the Edict of Cleansing went up on every wall in this city, how many mages do you think it stopped?"

"Stopped?"

"From being dangerous. That's the word the edict uses, isn't it? Dangerous." He gestured with his bound hands, an odd, careful motion, as if the iron itself were an argument. "Because from where I sat in the Nightmarket, all it did was make us hide. And hiding doesn't make a person less dangerous. It makes them *desperate*. Desperate people don't negotiate with fevers. They burn things."

"Some of them do."

"All of them might. Given the right year." His voice was even, almost conversational, and that was the trap of him, Crane realized. He wasn't preaching. He was *bookkeeping*. Laying figures side by side on the table and inviting her to check his sums. "Your law says: register, and be watched forever, or run, and be hunted. Those are the options. Where in that arithmetic is the widow Marta's daughter? Because that child is alive because of a crime. Somebody should account for that."

Crane looked at him for a long moment.

"Do you know what I was doing during the war?" she asked.

He blinked. It was, clearly, not the response he had budgeted for. "I — no. You'd have been young. A girl."

"A cadet. Sixteen. I stood guard at the registration depot in Greyford for eight months." She kept her voice level, the way she kept everything level; it was the discipline of her life. "I watched families sign their names into a book that some of them never came back for. I watched a woman argue with a clerk about whether her son counted as gifted, because his gift was small, just a warmth in his hands, useful for setting bones, and if she said yes he'd be taken to the tower and if she said no and someone found out later they'd both hang. She said no. I wrote it down. I was sixteen and I wrote it down, and I told myself it was mercy, because hanging was worse than the tower."

"And was it?"

"I don't know," Crane said. "That's the honest answer, and I'd thank you not to look so satisfied about it. The boy went to the tower. I don't know what happened to him after. I've spent some years not knowing, on purpose, because knowing would require me to decide something, and deciding something would require me to believe that the edict I serve is either right or wrong, and I am not yet permitted to believe either."

"Not permitted by whom?"

"By myself." She smiled, thin and dry. "You'd be surprised how much of the law lives inside a person's own permission, Silas. Break the law and they can only hang you once. Break your own belief in it and you hang every morning for the rest of your life."

The candle guttered and steadied. Somewhere above them, muffled through stone, the Tower bells rang the third hour past midnight.

Fairweather was quiet for a while. When he spoke again, the bookkeeper's evenness had cracked somewhere along a seam, and what showed through was tired, and older than his face.

"My mother used to say the storm doesn't ask whose roof it takes off," he said. "It just comes. That's what she thought power was — weather. It happens to people. The only choice anyone gets is whether to be the rain or the roof." He shook his head. "I was seventeen and I thought she meant it as poetry. She meant it as a warning. The crown heard 'storm-line' and saw a threat. The covens heard 'storm-line' and saw a banner. Nobody, in thirty years, has asked the storm what *it* wants."

"And what does it want?"

"If I knew that, Inquisitor, I'd be a very different problem for you."

* * *

She tried the direct approach then, because she was a professional, and because the indirect approach had somehow become a confession booth, and one of them had to steer.

"The Remnant," she said. "Mother Ives wouldn't give me names, but she confirmed the network exists. Catacombs, safehouses, a chain of herbwives and smugglers running messages and mages beneath the city. You were part of it."

"I was part of a bargain I regret."

"What bargain?"

"Protection," he said. "For someone. In exchange for services."

"Services rendered at the tannery quarter? Eleven dead in that reprisal fire, Silas. Two of them children."

"I know." His voice went flat and hard, and for the first time she saw the thing underneath the shopkeeper — the thing that had once been a storm-caller at Greyford, the thing the crown had feared enough to exile rather than kill, because killing storm-callers had historically gone poorly for everyone involved. "You think I don't count them? Every night. Eleven, plus the four the Remnant lost, plus Bren's crew scattered, plus Marta's grandson beaten half to death getting me out of your tower. I keep accounts too, Inquisitor. Mine are just worse than yours, because mine are all people, and half of them I can name."

"Then help me end it. Give me Corvess. Give me the Remnant's leadership, and the assault they're planning, and I give you my word—" she paused, because she chose her words the way sappers chose their footing, "—my *personal* word, not the Tower's, that Ulyana is processed gently. Registered under a false designation. Hidden in the system. No throne, no coven, no war. Just a girl who gets to grow up."

Something moved through his whole body then, like weather passing over water. She watched him weigh it — actually weigh it, honestly, the offer against the cost — and she understood in that moment why Dame Rosalind Crane, who trusted no one, had begun against her will to trust this man: because he considered betraying everyone he loved to save the one he loved best, and she could see him doing it, and she could see him choosing not to, and both halves of him were visible at once, without shame.

"No," he said.

"Why not? It's the rational answer. You've done the sums your whole life."

"Because your system isn't a place to hide a girl. It's a place to lose one." He leaned forward, iron clinking softly. "You'd process her gently today. And in five years some clerk with a new superior pulls her file, and in ten years some edict revision reclassifies her designation, and in fifteen years some frightened minister reads the words *storm-line* in an old report and starts asking where the last heir went. Systems don't protect people, Crane. People protect people. That's the whole secret. Everything else is filing."

"Spoken like a man with no respect for filing."

"Spoken like a man whose entire life is in one of your files." He almost smiled. "I've read the summary they read aloud at my arrest. Do you know what it called me? '*Charm-seller, minor offender, long-term noncompliant.'* Ten years of my life, and I fit on an index card. Yours would too, you know. If they fell tomorrow — the ministers, the Lord Justiciar, the whole rotten scaffolding — do you think your service would protect you? You arrested your own superior. There are people in this Tower who call you traitor behind their hands already. When this ends, however it ends, you'll find out how much the law loves the people who enforce it honestly."

The candle burned low. Neither of them moved to trim it.

* * *

Near dawn — she judged by the ache behind her eyes, not by any clock — Crane closed the ledger.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "because you're going to hear it soon anyway, and I'd rather you hear it from me. There's a movement in the ministry to move you. Deep cells. The kind with no interviews."

Fairweather went very still. "When?"

"Soon. Days, perhaps. The order hasn't been signed." She rose, gathered her papers, and paused at the door with her hand on the frame. "I shouldn't tell you that. It compromises the security of this facility, and if it goes wrong, it ends my career, possibly my neck."

"Why tell me, then?"

She considered the question honestly, because he deserved honesty and because, more dangerously, she had begun to want to give it to him.

"Because you asked me earlier what I was, unofficially," she said. "And I've been turning it over all night, and I find that I still don't have an answer. But I noticed something, Silas. Every question you asked me tonight, I've been asking myself for years, alone, at night, in an office full of files. Order versus mercy. Law versus justice. What the storm wants." She looked back at him — the exhausted charm-seller in the suppression-iron, the exile who had spent a decade committing small kind crimes. "You didn't win tonight. Neither did I. But I think we finally understand each other's ledgers, and I find that I can't un-read them."

"And that changes what, exactly?"

"I don't know yet," Crane said. "Ask me again in a few days. Something tells me the answer is going to arrive sooner than either of us expects — and that when it does, this cell door is going to matter a great deal."

She left him sitting in the candlelight, watching her go with an expression she would spend the next several days refusing to name — and took the stairs two at a time, because somewhere above her, in the signing rooms of the White Tower, an order was moving through the ministries faster than any order had a right to move, and Rosalind Crane had perhaps three days to decide, once and for all, which side of her own ledger she intended to stand on.
