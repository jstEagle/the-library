## 26. Escape from the White Tower

The trouble with prisons, Silas had decided by the third day, was not the walls or the chains or even the gruel, which was honestly no worse than what he'd eaten during certain lean winters in Veymark. The trouble was the waiting.

Waiting had never been his talent. He could call lightning out of a clear sky, could taste a storm three days before it broke, could read the pressure in a sailor's bad knee better than any physician — but patience? Patience had always been Ulyana's department. She could crouch behind a fish stall for an hour without twitching, waiting for exactly the right moment to lift an apple. Silas, meanwhile, had spent ten years telling himself *not yet, not yet* every single morning, and it had taken everything he had.

Three days in a cell in the White Tower, and his hands had begun to shake from the absence of weather. The tower was warded, of course — cold iron threaded through the mortar, salt in the drains, little prayers scratched into the stones by some pious mason a century dead. His gift sat inside him like a swallowed thunderhead, pressing against his ribs, going nowhere. It hurt in a way he hadn't anticipated. He'd imagined losing his magic would feel like losing a limb. Instead it felt like holding his breath.

*Not yet,* he told himself again, and then laughed, quietly, at the ceiling. "You know," he said to the empty cell, "I really did think I'd die doing something noble. Or at least something interesting."

The wall declined to comment.

* * *

Tam found him on the fourth night.

Silas knew the boy was coming before the door opened — not through any gift, but because he'd spent ten years learning the Nightmarket's rhythms, and Tam walked like his grandmother, quick and apologetic, as if constantly asking permission of the floorboards. The lock turned. The door swung. And there stood a tower guard of perhaps nineteen years, wearing a helmet a size too large and the expression of a young man who had rehearsed this speech all day and intended to deliver all of it.

"Master Fairweather," Tam whispered. "My name is Tamwell Ostrey. My gran is Marta Ostrey, of Lantern Row. You sold her—" he fumbled at his belt pouch, "—you sold her a fever-charm. Nine years ago. When my sister Nell had the sweating sickness."

"I remember," Silas said. He'd remembered the moment the guards dragged him past Marta's window, actually, and had thought: *there's one debt I'm glad I owed.*

"Nell would have died." Tam said it flatly, the way you state a fact that has organized your entire life since. "Gran says so. Everyone said so. The physician said so. But she didn't die, and Gran never forgot, and when your face went up on the walls she cried, and then she stopped crying and started *planning*, which is worse." He held out a ring of keys. "So. Here's how it's going to go."

Silas took the keys. "I notice you've prepared instructions."

"Gran made me memorize them. There are eleven steps. Some of them are just 'don't panic.'"

"That sounds like her." Silas tested the first key in the ankle shackle. It fit. Of course it fit; Marta Ostrey had probably bribed, charmed, or emotionally devastated half the tower staff by now. "Step two?"

"Don't take anything from the cells that isn't yours. Step three is about the privy shaft, but I don't think we need it if the keys work."

"The privy shaft," Silas repeated, with feeling. "She wanted me to escape down a privy shaft."

"She wanted you to escape. The shaft was her backup plan." Tam paused. "There were also candles involved in step seven, but honestly, Gran's plans have more atmosphere than engineering."

Silas got the shackles off and stood, swaying, legs full of pins and needles and four days of fear. He put a hand on the boy's shoulder. "Thank you. And thank your grandmother. And tell her—" He stopped, because there wasn't a word big enough, and settled for: "Tell her the charm worked because she believed in it. That's not false modesty. That's just true."

Tam looked at him with wide, earnest eyes. "Was it true about the fever-charm? Did magic save my sister?"

Silas hesitated. The truth — that the charm had been a knot of string and river clay and one small, illegal, desperate thread of real working, the kind of thing he'd sworn never to do again and done anyway because a dying child was a dying child — rose in his throat. What came out was gentler than either lie or confession.

"It saved her because someone loved her enough to try everything. The rest is details."

Tam nodded slowly, filing this away somewhere permanent. Then he straightened. "Right. Steps four through eleven. Keep close, walk like you belong here, and whatever happens, don't look at Sergeant Bray's boots. He polishes them during evening watch and if you compliment them we'll be here an hour."

* * *

The White Tower at night was a cathedral of paperwork.

That was the joke nobody in Veymark appreciated until they saw it: the crown's great engine of cleansing, the terror of every hedge-witch and charm-seller in the city, ran on paper. Mountains of it. Ledgers, registries, interrogation transcripts, requisition forms for lamp oil and manacles. The Inquisition generated documents the way a storm generated rain, and it stored them the way a miser stored coins — jealously, obsessively, in long galleries of shelving that climbed into the dark like scaffolding around a god.

Silas and Tam moved through those galleries by the light of one shielded lantern, and it was Tam who stopped first.

"This way," the boy breathed. "Inquisitor Crane's office. We pass it on the route. Gran's step nine says don't go in, actually—"

"Gran," Silas said, already drifting toward the door, "is a wise woman, and I am ignoring her."

He'd been thinking about it for four days, in the gaps between despair and gruel. Dame Rosalind Crane carried her case files everywhere; he'd seen the leather folio at her hip during the interrogation, had watched her open it and lay out his decade like a surgeon laying out instruments. Every charm. Every kindness. Every rule broken. She had reconstructed ten years of his life from gossip and receipts, and she kept it all *close*, which meant it lived in that office at night, which meant—

Which meant that somewhere in this building was the complete account of everything the crown had done, written in its own meticulous hand.

"I can't read most of the cipher," Tam whispered, glancing nervously down the gallery. "But I can carry things. Gran says I have good shoulders for a scholar."

"You're not a scholar?"

"I wanted to be. Then the edict happened, and now I arrest people instead." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "It pays for Nell's schooling. Gran pretends it doesn't shame her. They argue about it every Sunday."

Silas tried Crane's door. Locked. He tried Tam's borrowed keys. Third one turned it, and he made a mental note to build Marta Ostrey a shrine.

Inside, the office was exactly what he'd expected and nothing he'd expected. He'd braced for austere cruelty — iron fittings, whips on the wall, portraits of stern ancestors. Instead: a tidy room smelling of ink and lavender. A cot, neatly made, in the corner — she *slept here*. A shelf of books on law, theology, and, unexpectedly, poetry, spines cracked with use. A single window looking east over the harbor, where dawn would come. On the desk, a half-eaten apple and a letter begun in Crane's precise, upright script:

*Mother — you asked whether the work troubles me. I find I no longer know how to answer questions of that kind—*

The letter stopped there. Silas stared at it a moment too long, then made himself turn away. *Don't,* he told himself. *Don't start seeing her as a person with a mother and doubts. It'll slow you down.*

It didn't slow him down. That was the problem.

The case files were in a locked drawer beneath the desk, and Tam's shoulders proved their worth immediately: the boy hauled out folio after folio, and Silas stacked them into a satchel with hands that shook harder now, though whether from weakness or anticipation he couldn't say. Registry ledgers. Edict drafts. Correspondence between the Inquisition and the Privy Council. And beneath everything, wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord, a folder marked in Crane's own hand:

**GREYFORD — ORIGINS OF THE STORM-LINE WAR — RESTRICTED — EYES OF THE COUNCIL ONLY**

Greyford. The word hit him like cold water. Greyford was where his old life had ended — the battle, the pyres, his mother's voice calling the storm down on soldiers who had come to burn a village of storm-callers, and the exile decree that followed, signed by men who had never stood in the rain. For twenty-six years — his whole life, and then ten more besides — everyone had known why it happened. The storm-line had risen against the crown. The crown had answered. Tragedy, necessity, order restored.

He untied the cord.

Tam hovered at the door, keeping watch, while Silas knelt on Crane's floor with the lantern low and read.

The first document was a memorandum, dated three weeks *before* Greyford, from the Privy Council to the Crown Marshal. Silas read it twice, because the first time his eyes refused to cooperate.

*"...the storm-line remains popular in the coastal provinces and cannot be moved against openly without cause. It is therefore proposed that cause be manufactured. A staged massacre, attributable to storm-caller violence, will provide the necessary pretext. Casualties among our own will be regrettable but proportionate..."*

Regrettable but proportionate.

His mother had died defending a village against an attack that had never needed to happen, because the attack *was* the point. The soldiers who burned Greyford had been ordered to burn it by their own king. The war that had exterminated the storm-line — that had left a baby girl abandoned on a doorstep in Veymark with lightning sleeping in her blood — had been invented. Conceived in a committee, drafted in fair hand, approved with a signature.

Silas sat back on his heels. The lantern flame didn't waver, but everything else did. The room tilted gently, like a boat. Twenty-six years of grief picked itself up, examined its foundations, and found them rotten.

"They lied," he said aloud. His voice sounded strange, very far away. "The whole war. The whole — it was *forged*, Tam. Like a counterfeit coin. Like one of my charms." He laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "They made the whole thing up. My mother died for a forgery."

"Master Fairweather?" Tam's voice was thin. "Someone's coming."

Boots in the gallery. Two sets, unhurried, conversational. Silas swept the remaining papers into the satchel, rewrapped the Greyford file, and killed the lantern. In the sudden dark, he heard the office door across the hall open and a familiar precise voice say, "—no, leave the lamps, I'll work late. Has the prisoner been fed?"

Crane. Coming home to her cot and her poetry and her unfinished letter to her mother, and her desk drawer was about to be empty.

Tam grabbed his sleeve. "Window," he mouthed. "Step ten."

There was no step ten that involved windows, but Silas was beyond Gran's itinerary now. The window overlooked the harbor wall — three stories of rough stone, a drainpipe older than both of them, and below, mercifully, the dark water of the moat channel rather than cobbles. Silas got the casement open with a squeak that stopped his heart, and then the door handle across the hall began to turn.

"Through," he whispered. "Go."

Tam went — the boy dropped down that pipe like a squirrel, which explained several things about his childhood — and Silas followed, satchel strapped tight, boots braced against wet stone, lowering himself hand over hand while above him the office door opened and lamplight spilled out across the floor he'd just vacated.

He was six feet from the ground when he made his mistake. He looked up.

Through the window, framed in gold, stood Dame Rosalind Crane, lantern raised, staring at the open casement. Their eyes met across twelve feet of night air. She didn't shout. She didn't reach for the alarm bell hanging by her door. She stood perfectly still, her face unreadable, and then — deliberately, unmistakably — she stepped back from the window and let the curtain fall across it.

Silas dropped the last six feet, landed knee-deep in freezing water, and stood there gaping upward at the curtained window while Tam tugged frantically at his arm.

"What?" the boy hissed. "What's wrong? Did she see—"

"Yes." Silas tore his gaze away. "Come on. Before she changes her mind about whatever that was."

* * *

They went out through the tannery drains and up through a coal merchant's yard and along Lantern Row under a sky that couldn't decide whether to rain, and the whole time Silas held the satchel against his chest like a newborn and said almost nothing.

Marta Ostrey was waiting at her door with soup, a change of clothes, and the particular fury of a woman whose plan had gone off-script. She took one look at them, hauled them inside, bolted the door, and only then demanded a full report, which Tam delivered breathlessly while she ladled barley broth as if feeding fugitives were a weekly occurrence.

"—and then the window, Gran, and Master Fairweather froze, and the Inquisitor *saw him*, I saw it on his face—"

"And she let you go." Marta set down the ladle. She was a small woman built like a doorstop, with Nell's same stubborn chin, and she fixed Silas with a look that had probably overruled kings in its time. "Why would she do that?"

"I don't know." Silas wrapped his hands around the bowl, needing the warmth. "Maybe she's sloppy. Maybe she wants me to run so she can follow me to whatever I'm protecting. Maybe—" He stopped. He thought about the letter. *You asked whether the work troubles me.* He thought about the curtain falling shut like a door closing on a decision not yet made. "Maybe she read the same files I did. Or maybe she wrote them down herself and hasn't decided yet what she thinks they mean."

"You stole from her," Marta said. "A woman like that doesn't forgive theft."

"No," Silas agreed quietly. "She doesn't. Which means next time we meet, one of us ends the other. Unless—" He looked into the soup, at his own warped reflection. "Unless she arrests herself first. Don't ask. I've had a strange week."

Nell appeared in the doorway in her nightdress, thirteen years old and solemn, and studied Silas with frank curiosity. "Are you really a storm-caller?"

"I was. Mostly I'm a charm-seller with delusions."

"My brother says you're going to start a revolution."

"Your brother," Silas said, "reads too much. I'm going to drink my soup and sleep under a roof that isn't a cell, and tomorrow I'm going to bring these papers to someone who can use them." He hesitated, then added, because the girl deserved honesty after saving his life by proxy: "And maybe, someday, if the world gets kinder, I'll sell charms again. The honest kind. The kind that only work because you believe in them."

Nell considered this. "Those are the best kind," she said, with the unassailable authority of the young, and went back to bed.

* * *

Later, alone in Marta's back room with the candle guttering, Silas laid the Greyford file open on the floor and read it through a second time, properly, line by line, letting each page land like a stone dropped into deep water.

The staged massacre. The forged evidence — letters attributed to storm-caller leaders that had been composed by a crown scrivener, dated backwards, seeded into the record. The execution orders drawn up *before* the pretext existed. Page after page of careful, bureaucratic murder, and at the bottom, the signature that had started it all, the old king's own hand, and beside it a marginal note in different ink that made Silas's blood run cold all over again:

*"If any of the line survive, the lie requires their extinction. Search must continue indefinitely. No heir of the storm may live to contest the record."*

No heir of the storm.

Silas sat very still in the candlelight, listening to the rain finally arrive against Marta's window, soft and steady, and felt vertigo sweep through him — not the dizziness of fear but of a world rearranging itself. Ulyana wasn't just the last spark of an old bloodline. She was the living refutation of a quarter-century of official history. Her existence, her single heartbeat, proved the crown's founding war was a fraud. If the Remnant understood that — if *Corvess* understood that — she wouldn't be their banner anymore.

She'd be their proof. And proofs get pressed flat into pages, or burned.

He closed the file and pressed his palms against it, as if he could hold the weight of it down.

"Ulyana," he said to the empty room, to the rain, to a girl somewhere out in the dark city who believed he'd sold her away, "I'm coming to find you. And you're going to want to sit down."

Outside, thunder answered — distant, uncertain, like a question being asked for the first time.
