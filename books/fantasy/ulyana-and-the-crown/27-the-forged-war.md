## 27. The Forged War

The widow Marta's back room smelled of lavender and old wool, which Ulyana decided was the correct smell for committing treason.

She sat at a table scarred by forty years of darning needles, the stolen case files spread out before her in three uneven stacks, and she did not touch them for a long moment. She just looked at them. Crane's seal was stamped on every folder — the crowned eye of the Inquisition, staring at her from wax the color of dried blood — and it stared with the same patient courtesy Dame Rosalind Crane herself had used when she'd bought a luck-knot from Silas all those months ago, holding his wrist a heartbeat too long.

*You're not afraid of paper,* Ulyana told herself. *You've slept in gutters. You've fought Remnant hunters in flooded tunnels. You once ate a pie off a windowsill while its owner watched.*

The pie had been disappointing. The papers were going to be worse. She could feel it the way you feel weather coming, in the ache behind her teeth that had nothing to do with lightning and everything to do with dread.

Silas came in carrying two cups of Marta's chicory brew, which tasted like someone had boiled a boot and then apologized for it. He set one down beside her without comment. Ten years of selling charms to strangers had taught him exactly how much comfort a person could offer before it became unbearable, and he always stopped precisely one kindness short.

"Whenever you're ready," he said, and sat down across from her, and did not say anything else, because he understood — as Corvess never had, as Mother Ives had only begun to — that this had to be her reading, not his telling.

Ulyana drank the boot-water. Then she opened the first file.

* * *

The early pages were almost boring. That was the trick of it, she realized later. Bureaucracy is a kind of anesthesia; you read supply requisitions and garrison rosters and your eyes glaze, and by the time something terrible arrives you've already stopped flinching.

She learned the shape of the Inquisition's archive: neat hands, careful dates, a clerk somewhere who had spent his whole life filing other people's ruin. She learned that in the year of the Greyford Massacre — *her* massacre, the one every schoolchild in Veymark could recite, the one that justified everything, the edicts and the burnings and the ten years of Silas eating bread that tasted like guilt — the crown's own records told a different story than the proclamations did.

The proclamation said: *On the third night of Harvestmonth, storm-witches of the old line rose against His Majesty's peace and slaughtered four hundred loyal subjects at Greyford Bridge.*

The file said: *On the second night of Harvestmonth, by order of Lord Chancellor Aldous Vane (counter-signed, His Majesty's hand), a company of the Fourth Regiment, dressed in the grey cloaks of the storm-line, proceeded to Greyford Bridge.*

Ulyana read that sentence four times. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. She'd expected shaking. Instead there was a stillness in her chest like the air before a strike, and the lamp flame leaned sideways though there was no draft, and Marta's cat, an enormous gray beast named Turnip, got up from the hearth and left the room with great dignity.

"Silas," she said. Her voice came out flat. "Read this."

He read it. He'd known, of course — he'd pulled these files out of the White Tower specifically because he suspected — but knowing a thing in your bones and seeing it in a clerk's careful ink are different animals. Ulyana watched his face do what faces do when an old wound is finally named: not shock, but a kind of grim homecoming.

"Go on," he said quietly. "Keep reading."

* * *

The next files were worse, because they were thorough.

There were orders for grey cloaks — sixty of them, tailored to specifications copied from captured storm-line garments, paid from a discretionary fund that didn't appear in any public ledger. There was a memorandum discussing, in the bland language of men arranging flowers, *the desirability of a visible atrocity to secure the Assembly's assent to the Cleansing Act.* There was a list of the dead at Greyford — four hundred and eleven names — and beside many of the names, in a later clerk's hand, small annotations: *tenant of Lord Vane.* *Debt forgiven posthumously.* *Widow resettled at crown expense.*

They hadn't even been random people. They'd been inconvenient people. Debts erased, tenancies cleared, a riverfront quarter emptied of its poor and its protesters in a single night, and the bill stamped *storm-witch atrocity* and presented to a horrified nation.

And the storm-line — Silas's line, Ulyana's line, the bloodline the crown had spent a generation hunting into extinction — had been blamed for a crime committed against them, wearing their stolen colors, by the king's own soldiers.

"They weren't afraid of us," Ulyana said. The lamp flame stood up straight again, and she made herself let it. "We were never a threat. We were a *convenience.*"

"No," Silas said. "Not quite." He turned a page toward her, one he'd marked earlier with a thread from his sleeve. "Look at the date on this deposition."

It was the testimony of a surviving storm-caller, taken years after the massacre, during the last purges. A woman named Edda Voss — and Ulyana felt her whole body go cold when she saw the name, because Edda Voss was in the songs, was in the stories the Remnant told in the catacombs, was the martyr whose death Corvess quoted at every gathering like scripture. Edda Voss, who had supposedly died cursing the crown with her final breath.

The deposition said otherwise. It said Edda Voss had surrendered. It said she had offered, in exchange for her coven's lives, to publicly confess to Greyford — to stand in the square and give the lie its face. And it said the crown took the confession and burned the coven anyway, all of them, and put Edda Voss to death slowly, and sealed the deposition because even then, some clerk with a conscience couldn't quite throw it away.

"She tried to save them," Ulyana whispered. "She gave them everything they asked for, and they killed everyone anyway."

"That's what I needed you to see," Silas said. "Not just that the war was forged. That it was forged *knowingly*. Vane wasn't frightened. He wasn't protecting anyone. He wanted the mages' lands, their charters, their harbor rights — and the storm-line was the excuse he built, brick by brick, out of other people's blood."

Ulyana thought about Corvess. She thought about his sermons, his certainty, his beautiful burning conviction: *the crown fears us because we are powerful, therefore power is our shield, therefore more power, always more power.* She thought about the tannery quarter, the child in the ash, the way Corvess had said *regrettable* the way other men said *Tuesday.*

Corvess had built his whole church on a foundation of ash. And the ash was fake. The fire had been lit by the man who sold the matches.

Her rage, which had lived in her chest for weeks like a swallowed coal — rage at Crane, at Bren, at the soldiers, at the whole grinding machine of the crown — turned over, slow and enormous, and found its true address.

* * *

She read until the candle guttered. Silas replaced it twice without being asked. Somewhere past midnight Marta shuffled in, looked at the two of them bent over the papers like conspirators in a very dull play, and set down a plate of seed bread and hard cheese without a word, which was Marta's way of saying *I know what you are and I'm feeding you anyway.*

It was the widow's own file that broke Ulyana open.

Marta Hesse. Listed under *Cleansing Act registrations, Veymark district.* Her daughter Lise, registered at fourteen as a hedge-talent — nothing dangerous, the annotation said, *minor gift, weather-sense, harmless* — registered, law-abiding, obedient. And then, two years later, a warrant. Signed by Aldous Vane himself. Reason given: *unregistered association with known gifted persons.* The girl had been seen speaking with a storm-caller in exile. A charm-seller. A man who fixed fevers and tied luck-knots.

Lise Hesse had died in the White Tower's cells before her trial came up. *Fever,* the record said. The cell records said the fever began the night of her interrogation.

Ulyana closed the file very gently, the way you'd close a door on a sleeping child, and looked across the table at Silas, who had gone the color of tallow.

"I didn't know," he said. "All those years. I knew they'd taken people, I knew the risk I put my customers in just by knowing them, but I never—" He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I told myself the charms were harmless. Small magics. That I was keeping my head down, keeping *her* head down. And the whole time, the machine didn't care how small the magic was. It cared that I existed. That was enough to kill a fourteen-year-old girl."

"You didn't kill her," Ulyana said sharply. "Don't you dare carry that one too. You've got enough."

"It's not a sack I can set down, little storm."

"No," she agreed. "But you can stop adding to it." She pushed the widow's file aside and laid her hand flat on the stack of the rest — the forged cloaks, the bought dead, the sealed confession, the whole rotten architecture of a lie that had eaten a generation. "This is what we add to instead. This is what we do with it."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Something in his face shifted — pride, maybe, or relief, or the particular grief of watching a child grow into the exact person you were afraid to hope for.

"And what is it we do with it?" he asked.

"We don't burn it down," Ulyana said. "That's Corvess's answer, and it's Vane's answer too, if you think about it. Fire is the one thing they both agree on." She tapped the topmost file. "Vane won with a *story*. He wrote one lie and killed four hundred people to make it true. So we write a truer story, and we make *this* one true instead — in front of everyone, with names and seals and dates, so no one can ever say it was witch-talk or rebel propaganda. Let Crane read it aloud if she has the spine. Let the city see whose hand signed the grey cloaks."

"The city might not thank us," Silas said. "People don't like learning their grief was inventory."

"People don't like burning either," Ulyana said. "They'll choose."

* * *

It was nearly dawn when she reached the last file, and it was the smallest — a single sheet, unsealed, tucked inside the folder like a bookmark someone had forgotten. No heading. Just a fragment of a letter in Vane's own hand, unsent, or perhaps sent and copied by a clerk who wanted insurance:

*...and should the storm-line prove to survive, so much the better. A living fear is worth ten dead ones. See that the search continues indefinitely. Fear is the surest foundation of a chancellorship, and fear requires maintenance.*

*A living fear is worth ten dead ones.*

Ulyana sat very still. Outside Marta's window, Veymark was waking — carts in the lane, a fishwife shouting prices, the ordinary music of a city that had no idea what its peace was built on. Somewhere in that noise, Corvess was doing the same thing she was doing: reading the truth, and deciding it changed nothing. She knew him well enough now to know it. He would move the assault up. He would send hunters. He would come for her with ropes and sermons and call it rescue.

Because to Corvess, this evidence was fuel — proof the enemy was evil, proof the war was holy, proof that power was the only answer. He would never see what Ulyana saw: that the lie worked because everyone, crown and coven alike, had agreed to believe the storm-line was dangerous. Vane had needed them monstrous, and Corvess had needed them mighty, and between the two of them nobody had ever simply asked whether a girl who sang to thunder might want something smaller than a throne.

Well. She was asking now.

Ulyana gathered the files into order, squared their edges, and tied them with the thread from Silas's sleeve. Her hands were steady. Behind her teeth, the old ache had resolved into something cleaner — not the wild static panic of the rooftops, not the borrowed fury of the catacombs, but a aimed thing, a lightning bolt looking for the tallest tree.

"Aldous Vane," she said. "Is he still alive?"

Silas went very quiet. "Chancellor Emeritus. He keeps a townhouse on the Sable Ring and gives lectures on civic virtue."

"Of course he does." Ulyana smiled, and it was not a warm smile, and she was almost glad of it. "Then we'd best hurry. Because I promise you Corvess read the same page tonight — and he won't be writing letters."

Down in the street, the first bell of morning rang out over the rooftops, and far to the east, over the palace, the sky went suddenly, unmistakably dark.
