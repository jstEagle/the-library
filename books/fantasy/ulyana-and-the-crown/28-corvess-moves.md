## 28. Corvess Moves

The candle had burned down to a stub by the time Corvess finished the last page, and he sat for a long while afterward in the catacomb dark, listening to the drip of water and the small sounds of forty sleeping mages above him, and he did not move.

He was not a man who moved quickly. That was the first thing anyone learned about him. In another life — a life that felt like it belonged to someone else, some younger and more foolish man — he had been a schoolmaster in the Tannery Quarter, a thin, patient, ink-stained man who taught arithmetic to children whose parents tanned hides until their hands went black. He had been good at it. He had liked the work. He still kept the habit of grading everything, even now: arguments, plans, people. *Adequate. Promising. Fails to show his reasoning.*

The file before him, stolen from the White Tower along with a hundred others, deserved a grade.

*Fails to show its reasoning,* he thought. *But shows something better.*

The old king's own seal, on orders dated three years before the Massacre at Greyford. Instructions for staging an atrocity that could be blamed on the storm-callers. A list of names — farmers, weavers, two children — marked *expendable* in a clerk's tidy hand. And beneath it all, in the king's own writing, one line that Corvess read four times because he could not quite believe a man would write it down:

*Let them be monsters. It is simpler than letting them be neighbors.*

"Well," said Corvess aloud, to the empty room, in the mild tone he had once used on boys who cheated at sums. "There it is."

He had expected to feel vindicated. He had spent thirty years telling anyone who would listen — and many who would not — that the war against the gifted had been built on lies, that the edicts were theft dressed as law, that every pyre in Veymark was lit by men who knew exactly what they were burning. And here it was, in the crown's own hand: proof. Complete, damning, irrefutable proof.

He waited for the feeling to come.

It did not come. What came instead was a memory, unbidden, of the schoolhouse. Of a boy named Tam Weller, eleven years old, caught copying answers off his neighbor's slate. Tam had wept and sworn and finally confessed, and Corvess had asked him the only question that mattered: *Did you do the work? Did you learn it?* And Tam, sniffling, had admitted he hadn't. The answer was right, but the boy had never earned it, and so it taught him nothing.

Corvess looked at the forged-war evidence and thought: *the answer is right, but I did not need it to know.*

He had known. They had all known, in the way the persecuted always know — not with documents but with scars. Mother Ives had known. Poor dead Ives, whose herbhouse smelled of thyme and whose hands smelled of blood the day they pulled him out of the registration queue twenty-two years ago. Every mage who had ever hidden a birthmark under a glove had known. You did not need the king's handwriting to tell you the fire was lit on purpose. You only needed to have stood close enough to feel the heat.

So the truth changed nothing.

That was the thought that settled over him, cold and clean as well-water, as the candle guttered out. The crown had lied. Yes. The crown had murdered its own subjects to manufacture a war. Yes. And tomorrow morning — this very morning, in fact; dawn was perhaps two hours off — the crown's criers would stand in the Nightmarket and read the Edict of Cleansing aloud again, and the crown's soldiers would drag another dozen frightened people to the registration depots, and if those people were lucky they would be registered like cattle, and if they were unlucky they would burn.

A lie did not stop being a knife because you found the receipt for it.

"Master Corvess?"

He turned. A girl stood in the doorway of his cell — Sef, fifteen, fire-gifted, one of the children they'd smuggled out of the Tannery Quarter after the reprisal burns. She had ash still faintly in the creases of her knuckles; no amount of scrubbing got it out anymore. She held a fresh candle and a cup of barley tea, and she looked at the papers scattered across his table with the particular hunger of the young confronted with history.

"Is it true?" she whispered. "What they're saying? That the old king made the massacre up?"

"It's true."

Her face did something complicated — fury and relief and grief all fighting for the same ground. "Then it's over. We can show everyone. We can—" She stopped, because she was watching his face, and Corvess had never been able to hide things from clever children. "You don't think it's over."

"I think," Corvess said gently, "that Aldous Vane wrote those orders, and Aldous Vane is alive, and comfortable, and gives lectures. I think the man who signed them died in bed with grandchildren around him. I think the soldiers who burned Greyford are pensioners now, drinking beer and complaining about their knees." He took the tea. It was terrible. He drank it anyway; gratitude was a discipline like any other. "Truth is a lamp, Sef. It shows you the room. It does not sweep the floor."

"But if people *knew*—"

"Some would weep. Some would shrug. Some would say it was a long time ago and the storm-callers were frightening anyway, which, between us, they were." He smiled to take the sting out of it, and saw that it did not entirely work. Good. She should carry the sting. "And meanwhile the edict stands. The fires are lit this morning whether or not a dead king's handwriting makes anyone blush."

Sef stood there a moment longer, holding her empty hands, and then she said, in a small voice: "Tam Weller used to be in your class. Before. My mother knew him."

Corvess went still. "Yes," he said. "He did."

"He'd be our age now. If he hadn't been registered."

"Yes," Corvess said again, and found nothing else, and the girl nodded once — solemn as a little judge — and left him alone with his candle stub and his terrible tea and the truth, which lay on the table like a coin someone had dropped and no one had bent to pick up.

* * *

By full light he had summoned the inner circle, all seven of them, into the long chamber the Remnant called the Chapter House — a flooded crypt once, drained now, its pillars carved with saints whose faces the Church had chiseled off during the purges. Corvess rather liked the defaced saints. They reminded him that iconoclasm, too, was a policy, and policies had authors, and authors had addresses.

They came bleary and anxious. Word of the archive files had spread overnight through the tunnels the way word always spread — fast, garbled, hopeful. He let them hope for a quarter hour. He was not a cruel man, whatever Dame Rosalind Crane might write in her neat little ledger. Hope was rations like any other, and he believed in feeding people.

Then he stood, and he told them plainly what the papers proved, and he watched their faces the way he had once watched a classroom: the bright ones lighting up, the tired ones glancing at their hands, old Maren the hedge-witch crossing herself with the wrong hand out of lifelong habit.

"So," said Dree, the smuggler-turned-quartermaster, a woman built like a bollard with a voice to match. "We wave the papers around and the city rises up and apologizes to us."

"No," said Corvess.

"We *show* them—"

"To whom? The criers won't read it. The printers' guild holds its license from the crown. Half the city can't read, and the half that can has mortgages." He folded his hands on the table, schoolmaster to the end. "I have spent the night asking myself one question, over and over, the way I ask my students to ask it: *what follows?* Not what is true. What follows. If every soul in Veymark read these pages at breakfast, what happens at noon?"

Silence. Water dripped somewhere in the dark behind the pillars.

"At noon," Corvess continued, "the registration depots open. At noon the garrison stands where it stood. At noon Chancellor Vane takes his constitutional walk on the Sable Ring, and if someone hands him these papers, he will smile his lecture-hall smile and say that history is complex and order is precious. Truth does not disarm a man, friends. It only tells him where he stands. And we already knew where we stood. We've been standing in the fire for thirty years."

"You're saying lie or no lie, nothing changes," said Maren slowly.

"I'm saying the lie was never the disease. It was the symptom. The disease is a crown that believes some lives are kindling." He let that sit. Then, because honesty was also a discipline: "And I will grant the other side of the ledger, since I make my students keep both. There are those who will say: if the war was forged, then perhaps the hatred can be unforged. Perhaps we should wait. Petition. Publish. Trust in the slow mercy of a shocked conscience."

"Do you?" asked Dree.

"I believe in shocked consciences the way I believe in spring in Veymark. Technically scheduled. Frequently late. Often fatal to anything tender." A few of them laughed despite themselves. Corvess did not. "No. I do not believe we can wait. Because I have read the rest of the file, and there is a page none of you have seen yet. The crown is not waiting either. Crane's requisitions went up yesterday — powder, chains, wagons. They mean to clear the undercity within the fortnight. Every tunnel. Every refuge. Every child."

That killed the laughter properly.

"So we move first," said Corvess. "The assault on the palace comes forward. Not in nine days. In three. Dawn after next, when the garrison changes shift and the tide is low enough to flood the lower gates."

"Three days," Dree said flatly. "Half our people aren't ready. Half our *plan* isn't ready."

"Plans are like sermons, Dree. Everyone admires the long ones and remembers the short ones." He was already moving, pulling the great map of the palace district across the table, weighting its corners with candlesticks. "We cut the plan down to what cannot fail without us failing: the gates, the armory, the tower records. Everything else is weather."

"And the girl?"

Everyone looked at Maren, who had asked it, and then everyone looked at Corvess, because everyone had been thinking it.

Ulyana. The heir of the storm-line. The banner they had raised without ever quite asking her permission — or rather, having asked, having received an answer they had agreed to hear in their own language. She wants to protect people, Corvess had told himself, and protecting people is what we do, therefore she is ours. It had seemed, at the time, like sound reasoning. Lately, in the small hours, it had seemed more like the arithmetic of a boy copying off his neighbor's slate.

"The girl," Corvess said, "is the difference between a riot and a revolution. When she stands on the palace steps, the storm-line stands with her. Every mage who ever hid a gift will know the old blood is not extinct, and every mortal who ever feared us will see the crown's true heir refusing the crown's true cruelty. She legitimizes everything. Without her, we are arsonists with a manifesto. With her, we are a government in waiting."

"She doesn't want to be a government in waiting," Maren said quietly. "She told me. She wants—" the old woman waved a hand vaguely upward, toward the streets, the market, the ordinary roaring world, "—all that. Just that."

"What people want and what they're for are rarely the same thing," said Corvess, and heard, even as he said it, how much of his life fit inside the sentence. He pressed on before anyone could examine it. "And there is the other matter. Silas Fairweather has the rest of the files. Fairweather is clever, wounded, and sentimental in roughly equal measure, and Ulyana loves him, which means Fairweather can talk her into anything — including running. Including disappearing. Including taking the storm-line out of this war entirely and leaving us to burn alone."

"You think he'd run with her?"

"I think he spent ten years teaching her to hide. Hiding is the one lesson she learned perfectly." Corvess straightened. "So. We retrieve her. Tonight, before Fairweather can fill her head with pardons and parliaments. Gently, if she'll come. Firmly, if she won't."

"Firmly," repeated Maren. "You mean by force. You mean we put hands on the last daughter of the line we claim to serve, and drag her to a battle she hasn't agreed to fight."

The words landed harder than Corvess expected, and he examined why, the way he examined all unexpected results. It was because Maren was right, and he had known she was right since roughly midnight, and he had decided anyway, and the space between knowing and deciding was where he now lived, and it was not a comfortable country.

"I mean," he said at last, "that in three days the crown burns every child in these tunnels, and I will spend any coin, spend *anyone*, to prevent it. Including myself. Including my own soul, which I assess, for the record, as already heavily mortgaged." He met the old woman's eyes. "Judge me after, Maren. If we lose, judge me forever. But if we win, there will be mages alive in Veymark who owe their lives to a hard decision made in a wet cellar by a tired schoolmaster, and they will never know my name, and that is the whole of my ambition."

Maren held his gaze a long moment. Then she bowed her head — not agreement, exactly. Surrender to the inevitable, which in wartime looks identical from the outside.

"Choose your hunters," she said.

* * *

He chose six. He chose them himself, walking the tunnels through the grey afternoon, and he chose carefully, because retrieval was delicate work and delicate work ruined by blunt instruments.

Not Dree — too loud, too loyal to him specifically, and loyalty to him was precisely the wrong tool for this. Not Sef — he would not put a child with ash in her knuckles in front of Ulyana, not for this. In the end he settled on Perrin, who could walk through a locked door like rumor through a town; the twins Osk and Vesna, who could quiet a room and a heartbeat both; Halvard, big and gentle and regrettable; and young Wren, because Wren had known Ulyana longest, had shared bread with her in the worst winter, and might be the one voice the girl wouldn't strike lightning out of.

He gathered them in the Chapter House at dusk and gave them their instructions standing over the map, and his instructions were simple, and he made them repeat the simple part back to him twice.

"She is not a prisoner," Corvess said. "Say it."

"She is not a prisoner," they echoed.

"She is the heir of the storm-line, and she is coming home, and the only question is whether she comes home angry. Do not hurt her. Do not frighten her worse than the world already has. Do not — Perrin, I am looking at you — do not *lecture* her. If she refuses, you send word, and you wait, and I will come myself and kneel in the mud if that's what it takes. Am I understood?"

"Understood," said five voices.

Wren, the youngest, hesitated. She was picking at her sleeve the way she did when a sum wouldn't come out. "Master Corvess," she said. "If she asks us — if she says, *why do you need me, why can't you just win without me* — what do we tell her?"

It was a good question. It was, Corvess thought, the question, the one he had been not-answering since midnight, the one sitting in his chest like swallowed river water. He considered giving the hunters the speech, the true-and-incomplete speech about banners and legitimacy and the storm-line's duty to its people.

Instead he heard himself say: "Tell her the truth. Tell her we cannot win without her, and that we are sorry, and that sorry will have to do."

Wren blinked. "That's it?"

"That's it. Honesty costs nothing at the moment of use and everything in advance, and I have paid in advance, so we may as well spend it." He rolled up the map. His hands were steady. He noted, distantly, that he had expected them not to be. "Go at moonrise. The Fairweather man keeps watch badly — ten years of hiding teaches a person to sleep like a guilty thing. Be in the herbhouse quarter before the second bell, and be gone with her before the third."

They filed out. The candles guttered in their wake. Corvess stood alone among the defaced saints with the water dripping its patient count behind the pillars, and he allowed himself, briefly, honestly, the luxury of doubt.

*Perhaps she'll come willingly,* he told himself. *Perhaps she'll understand. Perhaps the hard thing and the right thing have aligned at last, the way the almanacs promise the stars do.*

Above him, muffled through sixty feet of stone and earth, thunder rolled over Veymark — early, out of season, rolling in from the east where the sky had gone dark over the palace. The storm-line's weather. Her weather, or the old storm-caller's, or the sky simply grieving ahead of schedule.

Corvess closed his eyes and listened to it, and graded it, the way he graded everything.

*Promising,* he thought. *Promising — and utterly beyond anyone's control. Which is, of course, the problem.*

Then he snuffed the candles and went up to prepare for a war he had just made three days closer, and far across the city, six shadows slipped out of the undercity into the rain, moving toward an herbhouse that smelled of thyme and ghosts, where a girl was learning to read stolen files by candlelight and had not yet been told that her oldest teacher had decided her future without her.

They would reach her by the second bell.

The second bell rang early that night — rung, though no one in the herbhouse knew it yet, not by the clocktower but by lightning striking the harbor beacon, once, twice, like a hand knocking on a door.
