## 29. Chased Through the Undercity

The undercity had been a harbor once, before the sea changed its mind and moved three streets east. Now it was a drowned cathedral of brick arches and black water, lit by the pale glow of lichen that grew on the old pilings—witchlight, the smugglers called it, though it was only fungus doing what fungus did. Silas had always found that comforting. Even here, magic was mostly just life refusing to be embarrassed about itself.

"Left," Ulyana hissed.

They took the left passage at a run, boots splashing through ankle-deep water, and behind them the sound of pursuit split into two distinct flavors: the disciplined drumbeat of crown soldiers' boots, and the looser, wetter slap of Remnant hunters who knew these tunnels the way rats knew grain stores.

"Wonderful," Silas panted. "We're popular in *both* directions."

"You're the one who said the undercity would be safe."

"I said it would be *complicated*. There's a difference, and I'd like it noted for posterity."

Ulyana shot him a look that could have curdled milk at forty paces, which was impressive given that she was also sprinting, ducking under a low arch, and holding her satchel above the waterline all at once. The girl had grown up on Veymark's streets; she could multitask grievance like other people breathed.

They burst into a wider chamber—a flooded market square, by the look of it, stall-posts still standing in rows like drowned men waiting patiently to be counted. The witchlight lichen hung from the vaulted ceiling in soft green curtains. And there, silhouetted against the far arch, came the Remnant hunters: four of them, moving fast, led by a broad-shouldered woman Silas recognized with a sinking feeling as Wren Corvess's own niece, sent to retrieve Ulyana "by any means."

And through the arch they'd just come from, torchlight bloomed. Crown soldiers. Six of them, wading in, swords drawn, an officer barking orders about taking the storm-caller alive.

Silas did the arithmetic he'd been dreading since the first splash behind them. He couldn't fight both. He couldn't outrun both. Ulyana could probably out-storm both, but every time she used her gift openly, she painted a target on the city above them, and after the tannery quarter, after the herbhouse, after Ives—

"Silas," Ulyana said quietly. She'd done the same arithmetic. Her hand found his sleeve. "What do we do?"

He opened his mouth to say something brave and useless.

Then the ceiling tried to kill everyone equally.

* * *

It began as a groan in the masonry, deep and structural, the kind of sound a building makes when it has been holding its breath for two hundred years and has finally decided to exhale. The soldiers stopped. The hunters stopped. Everyone looked up with the universal expression of people realizing that water, gravity, and architecture do not take sides.

The eastern arch let go.

It didn't collapse so much as *pour*—a section of vaulting sloughing off in a sheet of brick and mortar and two centuries of accumulated silt, directly onto the narrow spit of causeway where four Remnant hunters stood. Wren's niece shoved the youngest hunter clear and went down under the edge of it herself, pinned to the waist beneath a slab the size of a dinner table, the black water surging up around her.

For one long moment, nobody moved. Soldiers looked at hunters. Hunters looked at soldiers. Somewhere overhead, water dripped with insulting serenity.

"Oh, you cannot be serious," Silas muttered—to the universe, to the arch, to whatever god oversaw dramatic irony—and waded toward the rubble.

"Fairweather!" the officer snapped. "Step away from the traitor!"

"She's not a traitor, Captain, she's a drowning woman, and unless your edict has expanded to include gravity, I suggest you make a decision quickly." Silas got his shoulder under the slab's edge. It didn't move. It weighed approximately as much as his entire decade of bad decisions. "Ulyana! A little help!"

She was already there, bracing beside him, teeth gritted. Between the two of them the slab groaned upward an inch. Two inches. Underneath, the hunter—her name was Sera, Silas learned later, though at the time she was mostly a muddy face and a stream of curses in three dialects—wriggled free to the hips, then the knees, then hauled herself out entirely as the slab crashed back down.

The soldier nearest them had his sword half-raised and an expression of profound professional confusion, like a dog that had been told to fetch and handed a philosophy lecture instead.

"Well?" Silas said, still panting. "Are you going to arrest us, or are you going to help me get the rest of them clear before the next arch goes? Because I promise you, the next arch is going."

The soldier looked at his officer. His officer looked at the ceiling, which chose that exact moment to emit another groan, longer and less patient than the first.

"Corporal Hesk," the officer said at last, heavily, "assist the... persons."

"Persons," repeated one of the remaining hunters, spitting water. "That's the nicest thing the crown's called us in years."

"Save it," said Corporal Hesk, and waded past her to grab a second hunter by the collar and haul him out of a collapsing alcove with more strength than courtesy.

* * *

They got everyone clear thirty seconds before the second arch went down, and then there was nothing to do but stand in the rising water, eleven enemies and two fugitives, all of them soaked, filthy, and breathing hard, while the witchlight lichen glowed its gentle idiot green over the whole absurd tableau.

The officer—Lieutenant Dace, according to the name Hesk kept using in the tone people use when they want someone to feel guilty—surveyed the flooded square. "That causeway's gone. We can't go back the way we came, and neither can you."

"No," Silas agreed.

"There's a service tunnel," said Sera the hunter, simultaneously.

They looked at each other.

"The one behind the old customs house," Sera went on, chin up, defiant. "Runs north under Tanner's Row. Comes up by the coal chutes." A pause. "You don't know about it because it's a *Remnant* route."

"And you're offering it to us why?" Dace asked.

Sera opened her mouth. Closed it. Looked at the youngest hunter—the one she'd shoved clear, a boy of maybe sixteen, still shaking, his arm bleeding where the brick had caught him. Then she looked at Ulyana, standing hip-deep in black water with her satchel clutched to her chest, looking for all the world like exactly what she was: a girl who'd been hunted by everyone for a month straight.

"Because the boy needs a physicker," Sera said finally, gruffly, "and the only one I trust is Mother Marta's, up top, and if your lot arrest us on the way there I'll haunt you, Lieutenant, I swear it on my grandmother's bones."

"Marta's the widow on Candle Lane," Ulyana said suddenly. Everyone turned. She shrugged, defensive. "Her grandson's a tower guard. She patches anyone who knocks. She doesn't ask questions, she asks if you've eaten." A beat. "She'll feed you too, if you're polite. She fed *me* for years and I bit her twice."

"That was once," Silas said.

"It was twice and you know it."

Dace rubbed his face with a hand that left a streak of mud across his brow, which did wonders for his authority. "I am," he said slowly, "standing in a flooded hole in the ground, collaborating with insurgents, to escort a wounded insurgent to a known harborer of fugitives. My career is flashing before my eyes and honestly, Fairweather, it's a short film."

"Add a musical number and sell it in the Nightmarket," Silas offered. "I know a fellow who does broadsheets."

Hesk snorted. Dace glared at him. Hesk pretended he hadn't.

* * *

The service tunnel was exactly as advertised: narrow, dripping, and mercifully empty of anything trying to kill them. They traveled in an uneasy procession—hunters in front, soldiers at the rear, Silas and Ulyana in the middle like coins in a purse, everyone watching everyone else with the wary sideways attention of cats sharing a sunbeam.

It was the boy who broke the silence. He'd been limping along cradling his arm, and now he peered up at Ulyana with open, guileless curiosity.

"Is it true you made lightning come out of your hands at the tannery?"

"Sable," Sera warned.

"What? It's true, isn't it? Tam says you lit up the whole quarter."

"Tam," said Sera, "says a great many things, most of them wrong."

"It *is* true," Ulyana admitted, and then, because the boy was looking at her with such naked wonder and no fear at all—which was so rare lately it seemed to knock something loose in her—she held out her palm. A thread of static crawled between her fingers, blue-white, delicate as spider silk, throwing flickering light up the tunnel walls.

The boy's whole face opened like a window. "That's the most beautiful thing I've ever seen."

"It's also why six governments want me dead," Ulyana said, closing her fist, but she was almost smiling.

"Three," corrected Hesk from the rear. "Crown, coven, and whoever's paying Bren Saltwright this week. I keep the ledger." A pause. "Sorry. Poor choice of profession, that sentence."

Ahead, Sera glanced back at Silas. "You're really him, then. The storm-caller from Greyford. My mother sang about you. Bad songs. Very rude songs."

"I've heard them," Silas said. "The rhymes are criminal. 'Fairweather' does not rhyme with 'traitor,' no matter how hard the ballad-writers wish it did."

"They weren't wrong, though, were they? You called the storm that broke the crown's line at Greyford. Killed a hundred men."

Silas walked several steps in silence. The water lapped. Somewhere far above, muffled by stone, the city went about its terrified evening.

"Yes," he said at last. "And I spent ten years making luck-knots for fishermen to apologize to the sea for it. Neither thing cancels the other. That's the part the songs leave out."

Sera considered this. "My brother died at Greyford," she said. "On the crown's side." Another few steps. "But the crown burned my aunt last spring for keeping bees that found lost children. So." She shrugged, a small, exhausted motion. "I don't know where that leaves us."

"Knee-deep in a sewer," Hesk supplied helpfully from behind.

"Together," said Ulyana.

Nobody argued with her. She was, after all, the one with the lightning.

* * *

They reached the coal chutes as the witchlight gave way to honest grey dusk filtering down through a grate. Above them, faintly, came the sounds of Veymark at curfew: a bell, a dog, a shutter banging. The world where people lived.

At the foot of the ladder, the group arranged itself into the strangest farewell Veymark's undercity had ever hosted. Sera pressed a chalk mark into Silas's hand—"draw this on any door in the low quarters and a Remnant runner will bring food, no questions"—while Hesk, with the furtive air of a man committing treason in installments, slipped Dace's patrol schedule for the next three days into Ulyana's satchel.

"This never happened," Dace announced to the group at large. "Officially, we pursued you into the tunnels and lost you in the flooding. Unofficially—" he looked at the boy's bandaged arm, at Ulyana, at Silas, and something tired and human moved behind his eyes— "unofficially, if the council that everyone says is forming actually forms, tell them some of us in the white coats remember whose side the law is supposed to be on."

"And if your superiors ask where you were tonight?" Silas asked.

Dace managed something that was almost a smile. "I'll tell them I was inspecting drainage. Which, technically—" he gestured at his ruined boots, his mud-streaked uniform, the entire reeking tunnel— "no one will be able to dispute."

They climbed. At the top of the ladder, Ulyana paused and looked back down at the hunters and soldiers standing together in the glowing dark, up to their knees in the drowned past, and something in her chest went loose and strange.

"Mother Marta's is on Candle Lane," she told them. "Third door with the blue shutters. Knock loud—she's deaf in the left ear." A grin, quick and bright as her namesake weather. "And tell her the girl who bit her twice sent you. She'll feed you extra."

Then they were up and out, into the smoky evening air of Tanner's Row, leaving below them four hunters and six soldiers standing in the ruins of their certainties, listening to each other's footsteps fade, each privately wondering how they were going to explain tonight to the people who gave them their orders—and each privately suspecting that, for the first time, they might not bother explaining at all.

Silas pulled his hood up against the drizzle and nodded east, toward Candle Lane, toward Marta's door, toward whatever came next.

"Come on," he said. "If we hurry, we'll beat the supper rush. And Ulyana—if Marta offers the eel pie, take the eel pie."

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow," Silas said grimly, "we go to find the drowned throne. And nobody fights well on an empty stomach."

Above them, thunder rolled—not angry, not yet. But gathering.
