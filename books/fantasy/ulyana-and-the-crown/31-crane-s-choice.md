## 31. Crane's Choice

Dame Rosalind Crane had kept a ledger of her life since she was nine years old. Her father—a customs officer with ink-stained cuffs and a horror of imprecision—had taught her that a thing written down could be weighed, and a thing weighed could be judged, and a thing judged need never keep you awake at night.

For thirty years the system had worked beautifully.

She was awake now. She had been awake since the fourth bell, sitting at the desk in her rooms above the Nightmarket with her ledger open before her and a candle burned down to a puddle of tallow, reading entries she had made in her own neat hand over the past season and finding that they no longer balanced.

*Item:* one fever-charm purchased from Silas Fairweather for two coppers, held wrist a moment too long to test for resonance. Found: nothing but warmth.

*Item:* widow Marta Kell, registered, law-abiding, taxed punctually. Daughter alive of a lung-rot winter because of said charm. Widow Kell currently sheltering wanted persons. Widow Kell's grandson a guard at the White Tower who salutes me every morning.

*Item:* Tannery Quarter. Forty-one dead. Among them Odile Vasseur, registered charm-seller, whose paperwork I personally reviewed and found impeccable. Burned by reprisal fires lit on the Crown's order to punish an attack the Crown's own registration depots provoked.

The candle guttered. Crane dipped her pen and wrote, in the margin, in smaller script than usual:

*The arithmetic does not balance.*

It was, she reflected, a strange kind of grief—discovering that you had been wrong not through error but through diligence. She had done everything correctly. Every procedure followed, every suspect catalogued, every interrogation conducted with courtesy and thoroughness. She had been so *good* at it. And the sum of all that correctness was forty-one bodies in the Tannery Quarter and an order waiting on her desk that she had read eleven times hoping it would say something different on the twelfth.

It did not. It said:

*To Dame R. Crane, Inquisitor, Third Circle: The Remnant captives taken at the herbhouse raid, being minors trained in proscribed arts, are to be disposed of per Edict protocol. No registration. No trial. Efficiency is mercy. By order of Lord Justiciar Aldous Vane.*

*Efficiency is mercy.* Crane had known Vane for twenty years. He signed his letters with a flourish like a dead bird. He had never once visited the cells beneath his own tower, because the screaming carried, and Lord Justiciars were expected to sleep.

She put down the pen. She picked it up. She put it down again.

Outside, rain was beginning again—the soft, persistent drizzle that Veymark wore like an old coat—and somewhere under it the city was holding its breath. Everyone knew what was coming. The Remnant was massing; the palace had doubled its watches; the harbor was closed to everything but naval traffic. Two storms converging, and Crane standing precisely where they would meet.

There were six children in the cells.

That was the whole of it, when the ledger was stripped of its careful columns. Six children aged nine to fourteen, taken in the raid on Mother Ives's herbhouse, muddy, terrified, and gifted. One of them—a boy called Pim, all elbows and freckles, who could coax light out of a dead lantern—had spent the first night of his captivity politely asking whether the guards might possibly have something to eat that wasn't porridge, and thanking them when they didn't.

Crane had interviewed him herself, because she did not delegate what mattered. He had sat across from her with his wrists chafed raw and told her, with the grave honesty of a child who has been taught that lying gets people hurt, exactly what Corvess had promised him: that mages would rule again, that the crown would burn, that he would be somebody important someday.

"And do you want that?" Crane had asked. "To be important?"

Pim had considered this with great seriousness. "I want," he said finally, "to not be cold. And I want my sister back, but she got out, so." A shrug. "Important would be fine."

*Fine,* Crane had thought. *This is what we execute children for. Fine.*

She rose, dressed in her formal grays, pinned the silver quill of her office to her collar, and went down into the rain to commit treason.

* * *

Lord Justiciar Aldous Vane received her in his office atop the White Tower, a room designed, Crane had always felt, by a man who believed natural light was a form of insubordination. The windows were tall and narrow and grimy; the desk lamps burned yellow; Vane himself sat behind a mountain of paper like a toadstool grown through a woodpile.

"Crane." He did not look up. "You have the disposal order. Good. I want it done before dawn. Quietly. The city's volatile enough without—"

"I'm not going to do it."

The pen stopped moving. Vane looked up, and Crane watched him assemble his face into the expression he used on subordinates—mild disappointment, the face of a man inconvenienced rather than angered. She had seen it work on dozens of officers. It had worked on her, once, years ago, when she'd objected to the burning of a hedge-witch in Saltmarsh who had done nothing worse than cure a bailiff's gout.

"You're not going to do it," Vane repeated.

"No, my lord."

"They are enemy combatants. Trained. Captured in a fortified position belonging to a hostile organization."

"They are children," Crane said. "The oldest is fourteen. The youngest cannot yet write her own name—I checked, she asked me how, she asked *me*, my lord, as though I were someone worth asking. They were fed porridge twice a day and lectured nightly by a fanatic. That is not training. That is an orphanage with ambitions."

Vane set down his pen with great care, which Crane knew from long experience was the most dangerous sign he offered. "Inquisitor. You have served the Crown with distinction for—"

"Nineteen years. Yes."

"—and I am prepared to regard this moment as fatigue. Grief, perhaps. I understand the Tannery Quarter weighed on you. It weighed on all of us." He spread his soft hands. "But the edict is the edict. Gifted persons who take up arms against the Crown forfeit registration protections. You know the law better than anyone in this building. You *wrote* half of it."

And there it was—the trap she had built for herself, brick by brick, over nineteen years. Every regulation, every protocol, every elegant clause distinguishing lawful registration from unlawful practice, drafted in her own hand, enforced by her own quill. She had built the machine believing it was a scale.

"I know the law," Crane agreed. "I also know what the files in your vault say about Greyford. About the massacre that started all of this. About who signed the orders that manufactured the war we've spent a generation paying for." She watched him very carefully as she said it. "You knew, my lord. All along. The storm-line wasn't exterminated because they were dangerous. They were exterminated because a frightened king needed an enemy, and dead men make excellent enemies—they can't correct the record."

Something moved behind Vane's eyes. Not guilt—Crane doubted the organ existed—but calculation, rapid and cold. "Those documents are sealed."

"They were sealed. They are considerably less sealed now."

"Then you've committed espionage as well as insubordination." He rose. For the first time in nineteen years, Lord Justiciar Aldous Vane raised his voice, and the room's shadows seemed to flinch. "Guards!"

"Before you call them," Crane said pleasantly, "you should know that I anticipated this conversation. Copies of the Greyford file exist in six locations. If anything happens to me—if I am arrested, if I die of a sudden fever, if I trip on the stairs—my instructions will see them delivered to every crier, every temple, every dockside tavern in Veymark within a day. The war is coming to this city regardless, my lord. The only question is whether it arrives while the truth is still a secret or after it's common knowledge. I would strongly recommend *after*."

Silence. Rain ticked against the glass. Somewhere below, the tower bells marked the hour, indifferent as arithmetic.

"You always were the cleverest officer I ever had," Vane said at last, quietly. "It's why I gave you the Nightmarket. I thought a decade among charm-sellers and smugglers would blunt you." He smiled, and it was almost fond, which was somehow worse than anger. "Instead it taught you their sympathies."

"It taught me their names," Crane said. "There's a difference, my lord. I recommend learning it. I believe you'll be seeing quite a lot of these people shortly."

"You're relieved of command."

"Yes."

"You'll be confined to quarters pending tribunal."

"Of course." She unpinned the silver quill from her collar and laid it on his desk, beside the dead-bird signature. "Under the circumstances, my lord, I won't be needing this. But I'd think carefully before confining me anywhere too comfortable. Tribunals require testimony. Testimony requires witnesses. And I intend to testify about *everything*."

She turned and walked out of his office, past the guards who had arrived too late and too confused to stop her, down stairs that smelled of stone and lamp oil, and only when she reached the bottom—only when the door was shut behind her and the rain met her face—did she let her hands shake.

Then she opened her ledger, right there under the archway, shielding the page with her coat, and wrote:

*Item: resigned commission, 5th bell, this morning. Item: committed treason, same hour. Item: slept poorly, expect to continue sleeping poorly.*

She considered the page a long moment. Then, beneath it, in the smallest script she owned:

*Would do again. Note for future review: this is either corruption or conscience. Determine which. Proceed accordingly.*

* * *

Finding Silas Fairweather was not difficult, which was itself an indictment of the Crown's intelligence apparatus. Crane simply went where a hunted man would go when he was done running: to the one door in Veymark that had never turned him away.

Marta Kell's house sat crooked at the edge of the Tanner's Walk, leaning toward its neighbor like an old drunk telling a secret. Crane knocked. A shutter cracked. An eye appeared, vanished, and then the door opened on its chain.

"Dame Crane." Widow Kell's voice was flat as a skillet. "Come to arrest my boarders? Or come to burn the house? I only ask so I can fetch the good linens out first."

"Neither, Marta. May I come in? I'm wet, unarmed, and unemployed, which makes me about as dangerous as a Sunday sermon."

A pause. Then the chain rattled off and the door swung wide. "Unemployed," Marta repeated, letting her pass. "That's the first honest word I've heard out of a crown official in ten years. Sit. There's tea, if you don't mind it weak enough to apologize for itself."

The kitchen smelled of bread and yarrow and the particular damp sweetness of a house sheltering more people than it admitted. Crane sat. Marta poured. And from the shadowed doorway beyond, Silas Fairweather emerged—thinner than Crane remembered, gray to the temples, moving like a man whose body had been borrowed and returned damaged. His eyes went to her collar first, found no silver quill, and narrowed.

"Dame Crane."

"Formerly."

"That's not reassuring. In my experience, former inquisitors come in exactly two varieties: retired, and renegade."

"The second, I'm afraid. Though I prefer 'conscientious objector.' It fits better on a headstone." She wrapped her hands around the teacup, mostly for warmth. "Fairweather. I arrested you. I interrogated you. I let you go and told myself it was procedural error." She met his eyes. "It wasn't. It was the first entry in a ledger I've been refusing to read. I've read it now. It doesn't balance. None of it balances. The edict, the burning, the war—it was built on a lie your king told before either of us was born, and I have spent nineteen years sharpening the knife they used."

Silas lowered himself onto the bench across from her, slowly, the way a man sits when he doesn't trust furniture. "You have the files."

"I stole my own government's secrets and blackmailed my superior with them. This week has been very educational." She drew a breath. "Here is what remains of my authority, such as it is: the North Gate. Captain Ehrling commands the watch there—his brother died at Greyford, he's suspected the official story for years, and last month he asked me, over drinks, whether I thought history was ever going to catch up with us. I believe he can be persuaded to look elsewhere between the fifth and sixth bells tomorrow. After that, I can promise nothing."

Marta set down the teapot with a clack. "You're telling them the gates will be open."

"I'm telling them the gates will be *unguarded*, which is a different thing and legally much worse for everyone involved." Crane produced a folded scrap of paper and slid it across the table. "Ehrling's signal. Show it to no one you wouldn't trust with your life, which given your recent history, Fairweather, ought to narrow the list nicely."

Silas stared at the paper without touching it. "Why?"

It was the question she had been dreading, because she had spent three sleepless nights asking it herself, and the answer was still forming.

"Because of a boy called Pim," she said. "Fourteen years old. We have him in a cell, and my former lord and master has ordered him executed along with five others, and I discovered—sitting there with the order in my hand—that I would rather be hanged than sign it. That surprised me. I had assumed I was the sort of person who signs things." She smiled, and it hurt. "It turns out I am the sort of person who keeps ledgers. And the ledger says the crown stopped being the law some time ago. It became merely the largest gang, with better stationery. I find I can't work for stationery."

Silas looked at her for a long moment. Whatever he saw seemed to settle something, because he finally touched the paper, folding it away into his coat with the care of a man handling a live coal.

"There's going to be a battle tomorrow," he said. "Corvess is marching. Ulyana—" he hesitated on the name, and Crane noted it, filed it, understood it—"Ulyana means to stand between all of them and the throne. If we take the North Gate, civilians can flee east before it reaches the Tannery Quarter. If we don't..."

"Forty-one became four hundred," Crane said quietly. "Yes. I've done the arithmetic."

Marta snorted from the stove. "Arithmetic. You people and your arithmetic. My husband ran numbers for the customs house for thirty years and he never once counted how many cups of tea a tired woman pours for free." She thumped a second cup in front of Silas. "Drink. Both of you. Traitors need their strength."

They drank. Outside, the drizzle thickened toward rain, and far off—east, toward the hills—thunder muttered like an army clearing its throat.

Crane finished her cup, stood, and buttoned her coat. At the door she paused.

"Fairweather. Tomorrow, when it starts—when you see what the Remnant does and what the Crown does and what falls in between—remember that I chose my side over a cup of weak tea in a crooked house, and I have regretted nothing except the delay." She pulled up her hood. "Tell the girl with the lightning that the North Gate opens at fifth bell. And tell her—" Crane searched for it, the phrase she'd been carrying for three days without knowing its owner, "—tell her she was never the threat they said. Tell her she's the correction."

She stepped out into the rain and did not look back, because looking back was for people whose ledgers still balanced.

Behind her, faint through the closing door, she heard Marta Kell say: "Well. I've sheltered smugglers, storm-callers, and now a disgraced dame. If a bishop shows up next, I'm charging rent."

And Silas Fairweather laughed—short, rusty, startled out of him—and the sound followed her down the wet street like a small light she intended to carry all the way to the gate.

Fifth bell. Dawn. Treason, she thought, turning north into the rain, was simply integrity that had finally run out of patience.
