## 32. March on the Palace

The smugglers came up out of the undercity at dawn smelling of bilgewater and gunpowder, and Bren stood at their head feeling like a man who had walked into a story he hadn't read the ending of.

There were perhaps three hundred of them—Saltwrights and Ferrowmen and half a dozen crews that had spent the last ten years pretending they didn't exist, armed with whatever the flooded tunnels hadn't ruined: boarding pikes, rusted pistols, harpoons meant for river-wyrms and now, apparently, for crown soldiers. Old Tamsin from the *Kestrel's Grace* had sharpened a fish-gutter and named it after her first husband. Bren chose not to ask why.

"Right," he said, and his voice cracked on the word, which was not the rousing start he'd planned. He cleared his throat and tried again. "Right. You all know why we're here."

They knew. Everyone in Veymark knew by now. The gates unguarded at dawn—a message that had traveled through the undercity faster than rumor through a fish market—and then the files, the forged war, the massacre that had never happened the way the history books said it did. Bren had read the pages himself, or as much of them as he could manage before the letters started swimming. He'd sold people to the crown over those years. Small people. People with names he'd told himself he didn't remember.

He remembered them now. All of them. In order.

"So here's how it goes," he said. "We take the harbor garrison, we open the armory to anyone who asks nice, and we don't—" he looked at Tamsin's fish-gutter, "—we don't do anything we'd be ashamed to tell our mothers about. Assuming you've got mothers who'd still claim you. Mine wouldn't."

A ripple of laughter. Good enough.

They moved up Rope Street two hundred strong, and Bren kept waiting for someone to stop them—the watch, a wall of pikes, some officer with sense—but the streets were empty except for civilians standing in their doorways with candles lit against the grey morning, watching them pass. An old woman pressed a heel of bread into Tamsin's hand without breaking stride. A boy ran alongside for three blocks carrying nothing, contributing nothing, grinning like this was a festival.

Bren had smuggled brandy, silk, fugitives, and once, memorably, an entire bear. He had never smuggled hope before, and it was heavier than all of it, and it kept shifting its weight, and he was terrified of dropping it.

Then the sky over the palace district turned green-black, and the bells began to scream, and Bren understood that they were late to a party already on fire.

* * *

Corvess had not waited for the council of ash and rain, or the widow's truce, or any of the careful arrangements being stitched together in back rooms across Veymark. Corvess had waited twenty years, which is to say he had stopped waiting entirely.

He led the Remnant up the Processional himself—an old man in storm-grey robes gone threadbare at the cuffs, walking at the head of four hundred mages and mortal sympathizers as though he were strolling to morning prayers. The crown's engines opened fire at the Fountain of Nine Kings. Corvess raised one hand and the volley broke apart mid-air, iron balls hanging like hail deciding whether to fall, until he flicked his fingers and sent them back the way they'd come.

"Twenty years," he said, and his voice carried without effort, the way it always did, pitched for crowds and courtrooms and the ears of kings. "Twenty years we have hidden in cellars and sewers while they burned us for existing. Today we stop hiding. Today we take what should have been ours."

Behind him, the Remnant surged forward, and it would have been a magnificent thing if every soul in that column had believed exactly the same thing. But they didn't. Some of them fought for the dead. Some fought because there was nothing left to lose. And some—Corvess noted them, catalogued them, the hesitaters, the questioners—some fought because they thought they were fighting for something other than a new king with an old name.

He would deal with those later. There was always a later, in revolutions. Later was where the bodies went.

The palace gates came down at the third attempt, and Corvess walked through the smoke into a courtyard littered with the wounded of both sides, and he stepped over a dying guardsman no older than Ulyana without slowing, because the future does not pause for the present.

"The girl," he said quietly, to the hunters flanking him. "Find her. Before the crown does, before the storm-caller does. She belongs at the front of this march—not hiding behind it."

"And if she refuses?"

Corvess smiled the smile that had kept the Remnant alive through the worst years, warm as a hearth and about as safe to touch.

"She won't refuse," he said. "She's a good girl. That has always been the difficulty."

* * *

Silas came at the palace from the west, at the head of a column that wasn't really a column at all—it was Veymark, or the parts of it that had decided enough was enough, and it refused to march in formation. Charm-sellers and dockhands and the entire congregation of Mother Ives's old herbhouse, carrying her memory and her recipes and, in three cases, actual weapons. Silas had a quarterstaff he'd picked up off a fallen guardsman and no particular plan for using it.

Beside him, Marta walked with a lantern though the sun was nearly up, because Marta did everything her own way or not at all.

"You could stay behind," Silas said. Not for the first time.

"And let you get yourself killed without witnesses?" She snorted. "I've buried enough people who died brave. I intend to watch this one live."

The fighting near the west gate was chaos in the purest sense—Remnant mages hurling fire at the walls, crown engines answering, and between the two of them a thousand ordinary people trying to reach the palace without being murdered by either army. Silas pulled a family flat behind a fountain as a chain-shot screamed overhead, and a young mage from the Remnant—barely older than Ulyana, hands still smoking—grabbed Silas's sleeve.

"You're Fairweather. The storm-caller." His eyes were too wide. "Corvess says you're a traitor to the cause. Says you want to put the girl on a leash instead of a throne."

"Corvess says a lot of things," Silas said. "Have you seen her?"

The boy shook his head. Then, quieter: "Is it true? About the forged war?"

"It's true."

Something shifted in the boy's face—grief arriving ahead of schedule, grief for a cause he'd only just lost. "Then what are we even fighting for?"

Silas looked past him, at the courtyard, at the burning, at Marta kneeling to bandage a crown soldier's leg with the same care she'd give a smuggler's.

"That," Silas said. "That's what we're fighting for. Everything else is just weather."

* * *

Ulyana was not with any of the columns.

She had left the herbhouse before dawn, when the first whispers of the march were still being passed door to door, and she had dressed in the plainest clothes she owned—Marta's castoffs, a coat two sizes too big, boots with a hole in the left toe—and she had taken nothing but a knife, the memory of Ives's voice, and a fury so clean it felt almost peaceful.

Every faction in Veymark wanted her at the front of their march. Corvess wanted her crowned; Silas wanted her safe; the city wanted her to be the thing they pointed at when it was over, the reason, the symbol, the banner. Even Crane, somewhere in the wreckage of the White Tower, presumably wanted her alive and useful.

And Ulyana stood in an alleyway off the Processional, listening to four armies converge on a single building, and understood with perfect clarity that if she joined any of them, she would belong to all of them forever.

So she didn't join.

She went around.

The palace had a servants' postern on its north face, half-hidden behind the ruins of the royal aviary—Ives had told her about it years ago, in the way Ives told her things, casually, between lessons, planting knowledge like seeds and never explaining what would grow. The postern's lock was old and the lock's keeper was dead or fled, and Ulyana's knife did the rest.

Inside, the palace was strangely quiet. The battle roared at its edges, but the heart of the building had emptied—servants gone, courtiers fled, and the throne room's great doors standing open onto a hall where dust drifted down through shafts of light like snow that had forgotten which way was up.

Ulyana walked the length of it alone.

Her footsteps echoed. Her hands shook. Somewhere above her, lightning struck the palace spire and thunder filled the hall like a held breath, and she felt her gift rise to answer it, static crawling along her arms, and she breathed through it the way Silas had taught her—*the storm isn't asking permission, and neither are you.*

She stopped before the throne.

It was smaller than she'd imagined. Gilded, yes, draped in the storm-line's colors out of some curator's dutiful habit, but small—a chair, essentially, built for a man, occupied by no one. Twenty years of blood and fire over an empty chair.

"Ives said you'd better heirs than any of them," Ulyana told it. Her voice sounded very loud in the empty hall. "I'm starting to think she was flattering me."

"You think quite highly of yourself, then, to assume she was."

Ulyana turned.

Corvess stood at the far end of the hall, alone—or seeming alone, though she could feel the hunters in the shadows the way she could feel pressure before rain. He was smiling. He was always smiling.

"There she is," he said softly, spreading his arms. "The last daughter of the storm-line, come home at last. Do you see, child? No one brought you. No one carried you. You walked here on your own feet, through your own doors, to your own throne. This—" he gestured at the hall, the light, the thunder still rolling overhead like applause, "—this is destiny. All I have ever done is clear the road to it."

Ulyana looked at the throne. She looked at the old man. And she found, to her surprise, that she wasn't afraid.

"No," she said. "You cleared the road to a lie."

The smile faltered—one heartbeat, no more, but Ulyana saw it, and she would remember seeing it for the rest of her life.

"The war was forged," she said, walking toward him, slow, deliberate, each step an argument. "The massacre was staged. My family died for a king's convenience, and you've spent twenty years building an army on top of their graves, and now you want to set me on that chair so their deaths finally mean something. So your whole life means something." She stopped an arm's length away. Up close, Corvess was just an old man with tired eyes. That was somehow worse. "But I won't wear a throne built on a lie, Corvess. Not theirs. Not yours."

For a long moment the elder of the Remnant said nothing. Lightning flashed through the high windows, and in its white light his face looked carved from something older than stone.

"Child," he said at last, gently, terribly, "you misunderstand. It was never a question of whether."

The shadows moved. Four hunters stepped out of them, and the great doors at the end of the hall boomed shut, and outside, the battle for Veymark reached the palace walls like the sea reaching a seawall—

—and somewhere beneath Ulyana's feet, deep in the flooded dark where drowned archives slept, something ancient began, very quietly, to wake.
