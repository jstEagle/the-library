## 33. The Battle of Veymark

The bells of Veymark had rung for coronations, for funerals, for the king's birthday and the queen's mourning. They had never rung like this.

Corvess stood on the roof of the tannery that had burned a season ago and was rebuilt badly, listening to the alarm peal out of order—three strikes, silence, three strikes—the signal the Remnant had waited thirty years to hear. Around him, in the grey light before dawn, forty mages knelt in a circle on the tar-paper roof, palms flat against the shingles, drawing up the city's old sleeping power through their hands. He could feel it moving beneath them like a tide returning: the deep veins of magic that ran under Veymark, the ones the crown had dammed and diverted and denied for a generation.

"Remember what we fight for," Corvess said, and his voice carried without effort; the wind liked him this morning. "Not vengeance. Not the throne. *Her.* The girl who is the last true thing this kingdom ever made. We put her where she belongs, and every child born after this day will grow up unafraid of their own hands."

They murmured assent. He believed every word. That was the trouble with Corvess, an old smuggler named Dree had once said—he was never lying, not even when he was wrong.

He raised his arms, and the sky over the eastern quarter went the color of a bruise.

* * *

Three miles away, at the harbor gate, Sergeant Odo Pell was eating a boiled egg when the first engine spoke.

The crown's war-engines were called thundershields, though no one who had stood near one called them anything but *the machines*. They squatted along the city walls like iron beetles, and when they woke they drank lightning from the ley-anchors and spat it back out in ordered, obedient bolts—magic made lawful, licensed, and lethal. Odo had served beside them for eleven years and had privately concluded they were less weapons than arguments: the crown's way of saying *this is what happens when magic belongs to everyone instead of us.*

The first bolt from the rooftops took the top off the gatehouse tower. Not destroyed it—took the top off, cleanly, the way you'd take the cap off a bottle—and dropped it into the harbor with a sound like the world clearing its throat.

"To your posts!" someone screamed, uselessly, because everyone was already running.

Odo abandoned his egg—a small tragedy he would mourn later, in the way soldiers mourn eggs—and hauled himself toward the wall stair as the second bolt came down and the third, and then the thundershields answered, and the sky over Veymark became a conversation conducted entirely in shouting.

* * *

In the Nightmarket, Marta Voss threw open her shutters, looked at the burning sky, and did the most Marta thing imaginable: she started making tea.

"You're mad," said her grandson Tam, pulling on his guard's uniform with shaking hands. He was supposed to be at the palace barracks. He was instead standing in his grandmother's kitchen because some part of him had known, since the night he'd helped a storm-caller escape the White Tower, that this morning would come and he'd have to choose which side of the door he stood on.

"I'm practical," Marta said. "There'll be wounded. Wounded people want tea. It's not complicated." She set the big copper kettle over the fire with a clang that sounded, in the trembling air, almost martial. "Go do whatever it is you've decided to do. But come back by midday if you can, because I'm not letting good tea go cold twice in one lifetime."

Tam kissed her forehead, hesitated at the door, and said, "If anyone comes—anyone at all—"

"They'll get tea," Marta said, "and I'll decide about the rest after."

It was, she reflected as the door banged shut behind him, more or less the family motto.

* * *

The battle did not go the way either side had planned, because battles never do, and because Veymark itself had opinions.

Corvess's mages came down through the Tannery Quarter in a wedge of stormlight, expecting the streets to empty in terror. Instead the streets filled—with people running *toward* the fighting, dragging children out from under collapsing eaves, hauling neighbors from doorways, forming bucket chains against fires nobody had set yet but everybody knew were coming. A fishwife stood in the middle of Cordwainer Street with her arms spread and screamed at both armies that her stall was *right there*, and did they propose to pay for what they broke?

A young Remnant mage named Sef, seventeen years old and full of Corvess's sermons, raised his hand to strike a squad of crown soldiers—and found himself looking down the street at a line of civilians between him and them: a cooper, two grandmothers, a boy holding a dog. The soldiers were trying to go around. The civilians wouldn't let them pass, and also wouldn't let the mages pass, and stood there in the rain of cinders insisting, in the universal language of stubborn people, that everyone just *stop*.

Sef lowered his hand. He would think about that moment for the rest of his life, which turned out to be long.

* * *

Silas Fairweather felt the battle begin the way a sailor feels weather: in the teeth, in the old break in his ankle, in the low ache behind his eyes where the storms lived.

He was in the flooded archive when it started, one last desperate search of the drowned shelves for anything about the crown's choosing—but the water trembled, and dust sifted down from the vaulted ceiling, and he knew. He climbed the long dark stairs two at a time, heart hammering, and came out into a city at war.

From the hill above the Saltwright docks he could see all of it: the Remnant's stormlight in the east, the thundershields' white fire answering from the walls, and between them—the whole sprawling, smoking, screaming middle of it—the city. Tenements. Markets. The orphanage steps where he'd first seen a sharp-eyed girl stealing bread. The widow's street. The herbhouse ruins where Mother Ives had died holding a door.

And in the streets between the two armies, thousands of people, running everywhere and nowhere, because no one had told them where was safe, because nowhere was.

Silas stood very still on the hilltop and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding for ten years.

A full storm—not a squall, not a shield, not the careful rationed trickles he'd lived on since Greyford—a full storm would take everything. Every reserve. Every scrap of the gift he'd hoarded like a miser, spent down to the dregs, and when it was gone it might be gone forever. He had watched his mother burn herself hollow doing exactly this, at exactly his age, and he had sworn on her grave he would never be so foolish.

Below him, a tenement caught fire from a stray bolt, and a chain of neighbors passed buckets while the thundershields kept firing over their heads, and somewhere in that smoke were people whose fevers he'd charmed, whose luck-knots he'd sold for copper, whose bread he'd slipped to orphans.

"Well," Silas said aloud, to no one, to the wind, to his mother wherever she was. "You always did say I'd end up foolish."

He walked to the crest of the hill, raised both arms, and opened every door inside himself at once.

* * *

What came down was not weather. Weather has patterns; this had intent.

The storm Silas called rose out of the sea in a black wall half a mile high, and it did not fall on the armies—it fell *around* them, a ring of roaring rain and howling wind that wrapped the whole of central Veymark like a cupped hand. The thundershields fired and their bolts wandered, bent, grounded harmlessly into the deluge. The Remnant's fireballs guttered and drowned. Under the storm's canopy, the streets cleared of fire and smoke, and the fleeing crowds found something they hadn't had all morning: cover. Direction. Mercy, wearing the shape of thunderheads.

Silas held it the way you hold a drowning man—as long as you can, and then longer than you can.

It hurt. That was the part nobody sang songs about. It felt like being pulled apart slowly, like every vein in his body had become a riverbed and the river was leaving. He laughed, actually laughed, because underneath the pain was the other thing, the thing he'd forgotten: it was *joyful*. Ten years of rationing himself down to charm-sized sips, and now the whole ocean at once, and even dying felt briefly, terribly like flying.

*Hold,* he told the storm. *Just hold. Give them time to get clear.*

The storm, which had known him since before he was born, held.

* * *

On the seawall road, Ulyana stopped running.

She had been running since dawn—away from Corvess's hunters, away from the crown's soldiers, away from everyone who wanted to carry her somewhere on their shoulders like a banner—and she had gotten very good at not being found. But when the great storm rose over the city, she stopped, because she knew that storm. She knew its signature the way you know a voice through a wall.

"He's killing himself," she whispered.

The rain reached her a moment later, warm and strange, tasting of ozone and something older. And in it—in the storm itself—she heard what she had learned to hear in the catacombs: memory. Voices braided into the water. Her ancestors. His mother. And underneath all of it, fraying at the edges like a rope under too much weight, one familiar thread singing itself out to nothing.

Every instinct she had learned on the streets said *survive, hide, live*. She stood in the downpour and let those instincts shout themselves hoarse, and then she turned toward the palace, where the storm was thinnest, where Corvess was even now driving his mages toward the throne room, where the crown waited in its drowned vault for someone to choose.

"Not yet," she told the storm, and the storm—gently, the way you'd steady a child learning to walk—parted around her and let her run.

* * *

Dame Rosalind Crane stood in the White Tower's command room and watched the reports come in, and understood, with the cold clarity that was her particular curse, that the edict had already lost.

Not the battle—the battle was still anyone's. The *argument*. Because her own officers were coming to her with the same report from a dozen quarters: the storm-caller was shielding civilians. Both sides' civilians. Crown loyalists and Remnant sympathizers alike were sheltering under his rain, and the soldiers under her command were beginning to ask each other, in the low voices soldiers use, why the outlaw was the only one on the field protecting the people the law claimed to serve.

"Inquisitor," said Captain Verel, her superior, gray-faced at the map table. "The thundershields are at sixty percent. If we concentrate fire on the storm-caller's position—"

"You'll level three districts to kill one man who is currently saving your soldiers' lives," Crane said.

"The edict—"

"The edict," Crane said, "was built on a lie, Captain. I have read the files. Have you?" She let the silence sit. Verel's jaw worked. Around the table, six officers studied the map with tremendous fascination. "No? Then here is my order, and you may court-martial me for it afterward: the thundershields hold position and defend the walls. Nothing fires into the civilian quarters. Nothing fires at the storm."

"And if the council overrules you?"

"Then the council can come down here and give the order themselves, face to face, and we'll see which of them can look a soldier in the eye while they say it."

Verel stared at her for a long moment. Then, quietly, he gave her orders to the room.

Crane turned back to the window, to the impossible storm holding its circle over the burning city, and allowed herself one thought she would never say aloud: *Ten years ago I would have called this treason. Today it feels like the first honest hour of my career.*

* * *

The cost arrived on schedule, as costs do.

By midmorning Silas was on his knees at the hilltop, arms still raised, holding a storm that had begun to slip its leash. The edges frayed. Bolts escaped downward, wild, and he wrestled each one back with hands that no longer fully belonged to him. His vision had gone silver at the borders. Somewhere far away, someone was shouting his name—Marta, he thought, Marta and Tam and a dozen others who'd climbed the hill to drag him to shelter—and the words reached him underwater, delayed and distorted.

"Almost," he gasped. "Almost done. The east quarter's clear. Just—give me the west—"

His mother's voice, or his memory of it, said: *You don't have to hold the whole sky, little storm.*

"Just a bit longer," he told her, and meant it, and poured out the last of himself like a man emptying his purse into a beggar's bowl—gladly, completely, keeping back nothing, not because he was brave but because below him a grandmother pulled a child through a window, and a soldier dragged a wounded mage behind a wall, and a fishwife saved her stall, and all of it, all of it, was still possible.

Then the last reserve went out of him like a candle in wind, and the storm, released, rolled away over the sea grumbling like a servant dismissed without thanks, and Silas Fairweather folded up onto the wet grass of the hilltop, lighter than he had ever been in his life, and emptier, and smiling.

"Got him!" Tam shouted, catching his shoulders. "Grandmother! He's breathing!"

"Of course he's breathing," Marta said, arriving with the kettle somehow still intact and a flask of tea that had survived artillery. "Stubborn as a barnacle, this one. Drink, love. You've earned the whole pot."

Silas tried to lift his arm to take the flask and discovered his arms had resigned. He settled for leaning against Tam and mumbling, "The palace. Someone has to—the girl—"

"Rest," Marta commanded.

But his eyes had found the skyline, where above the old palace a new light was rising—stormlight, wild and young and unmistakable, climbing the tower like ivy.

Someone else had heard the storm's last message. Someone else was running toward the throne.

* * *

Corvess stood in the antechamber of the throne room with blood on his sleeves and victory in his grasp, and sent his final sermon ahead of him like an arrow:

"She will come. She always comes to the storm. And when she arrives, we crown her, and thirty years of ash becomes a kingdom."

Behind the great doors, in the empty hall where a false dynasty had ruled on a lie, Ulyana wiped the rain from her face, squared her shoulders like a girl who had learned courage on street corners instead of in palaces, and pushed them open.
