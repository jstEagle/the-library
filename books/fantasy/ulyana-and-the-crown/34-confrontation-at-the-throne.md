## 34. Confrontation at the Throne

The doors to the throne room were taller than the house Ulyana had grown up in—taller than three houses stacked on top of each other, probably—and they were open.

That was the first strange thing. All through the palace she had climbed over rubble and stepped around the bodies of men who had chosen the wrong side of a door when Corvess's mages came through it, but here, at the heart of everything, the great bronze doors stood wide as an invitation. As if the room had been waiting for her specifically.

She stood in the doorway a long moment, rain dripping off her elbows, lightning still crawling faintly under her skin like ants with somewhere to be. Behind her, down the grand stair, the sounds of the battle rolled and cracked—the crown's fire-engines answering Corvess's storm-mages, glass breaking somewhere far away, a bell that wouldn't stop ringing no matter how much everyone clearly wanted it to.

Ahead of her: silence, and an empty throne, and Corvess standing beside it like he'd been posed there by a sculptor with strong opinions about posture.

"You came alone," he said. He sounded pleased. That was somehow worse than if he'd sounded angry. "I told them you would. I told them: *she has her mother's sense of duty.*"

"My mother," Ulyana said, walking forward across a floor so polished she could see her own warped reflection in it, "died on a road outside Greyford when I was four years old. You never met her."

"I knew *of* her." Corvess spread his hands. He was an old man, older than he looked in the catacombs, and up close she could see the seams—the fine white scars at his wrists where manacles had once sat for years, the way his left hand trembled unless he pressed it flat against his robe. A lifetime ago the crown had done that to him. She tried to remember that. It was important, she thought, to remember what had made him before she decided what he was. "I knew of every storm-caller they burned. I kept their names. When the lists were read out in the square, I wrote them down afterward, from memory, in the dark, because even paper was dangerous." His voice went soft and reverent. "Yelena Voss. Your mother. Third on my list."

Something in Ulyana's chest pulled tight. She hated him a little for that—for being kind to her mother's memory in a way her mother would never have asked for.

"Well," she said. "You have me now. So say whatever it is you've been building toward for thirty years, and then I'm going to tell you why you're wrong, and then we can both get out of this drafty room."

A flicker crossed his face—surprise, or maybe delight. "They said you were funny," he said. "The children from the herbhouse. They said you made jokes even during drills."

"Drills are terrifying. Jokes are how I keep my hands steady." Which was true, and also not true, and both at once, which was the most honest thing she'd said all day.

She stopped ten paces from the throne. Close enough to talk without shouting. Far enough, she hoped, that whatever Corvess did next, she'd have room to be fast. Her gift hummed along her arms, restless. Outside, thunder dragged its long grey coat across the sky, and she felt it the way you feel a dog pulling at a leash—familiar, eager, hers if she wanted it.

Corvess gestured at the throne.

It was an ugly thing, up close. Gilded iron shaped into waves and storm-clouds, the old royal iconography, and beneath the gilt she could see scorch marks—old ones, a generation old, from the night the last storm-king's line had been dragged out of this room. Someone had polished the armrests recently. The rest was dust. Nobody had sat there in thirty years, and yet somebody had been keeping it ready, like a guest room for a ghost.

"Do you know why the crown never melted it down?" Corvess asked. "Thirty years. They burned us, they hunted us, they scrubbed our names from the registers. But the throne stayed. Because the throne is a *claim*, child. It says: whoever sits here rules by right older than law. The Veymarks feared that right. And now their edict has failed, their soldiers are running, and their palace is ours—" his hand swept the ruined hall, the broken chandelier hanging by one chain, the banners half-burned on the walls— "*ours*, and the world is watching to see what we build in the ashes."

"We're not building anything standing in a room this dusty," Ulyana said. "You'd want to start with the roof."

"*Ulyana.*" He said her name gently, the way Silas used to say it when she'd skinned her knee and wanted to be brave about it. That comparison hurt more than she expected, and she set it aside carefully, like a hot pan. "You are the last of the storm-line. The blood of the kings who ruled before the Veymark usurpers, the blood they tried to burn out of existence. Every mage who died in the purges died believing someone like you existed. You are their answer. Sit, be crowned, and everything they took comes back—not just for you. For all of us. Legitimacy. Protection. *Meaning.* Thirty years of graves become thirty years of martyrs instead of murders." His trembling hand pressed flat against his chest. "All I ask—all any of us ask—is that you let your suffering mean something."

"That's a lot of words," Ulyana said, "for 'sit down.'"

"Yes," he agreed, with terrible patience. "It is."

Outside, something enormous exploded—a fire-engine, she thought, one of theirs or the crown's, impossible to tell anymore—and the whole hall shuddered. Dust sifted down from the rafters and landed on the throne like the room itself was trying to bury it.

Ulyana looked at the old man for a long time.

She had liked him, once. Three weeks ago, which felt like another life, he had sat with her in the catacombs and taught her the old forms of shaping lightning, and when she got one right he'd smiled like her grandfather might have, if she'd ever had one. He'd remembered things about storms that even the archive hadn't recorded. He'd known what her mother's technique looked like, and when Ulyana's hands finally found it, he'd wept. Actual tears. She had believed, in that moment, that grief like that couldn't lie.

She still believed it. That was the horrible part. Corvess wasn't lying. He believed every word, the way a stone believes in falling.

"Can I tell you what I saw two nights ago?" she said.

"If you wish."

"The tannery quarter. After the depot burned." She kept her voice level. It took work. "There was a boy pulled out of the ash. Six years old, maybe. Not registered, not gifted, not anything except asleep upstairs when the reprisal started. A guard pulled him out and just—stood there holding him. Looking at the street. Everyone on that street had lost something, and half of them were people your Remnant says we're doing this *for*." She took a breath. "And I know what you'll say. You'll say the crown lit the fire, and you're right. You'll say the war was forged on a lie, and you're righter than you know—I've read the files, Corvess, the old king staged the massacre himself, your whole grievance is real, I'm not arguing with your grievance." She stepped closer. "But that boy didn't light any fire. And neither did the charm-seller they arrested for selling luck-knots, and neither did the widow Marta's neighbors, and neither did the smugglers who are fighting on *your* side tonight and losing people too. Everybody in this city keeps getting sacrificed to somebody else's *meaning*. The crown did it for order. You're doing it for justice. From underneath, I promise you, it looks exactly the same."

"War is cost," Corvess said quietly. "I have never pretended otherwise. Ask me what the price is and I will tell you honestly, because these people deserve honesty after thirty years of lies. The price is blood. Some of it innocent. I carry each name."

"And what do they get?" Ulyana asked. "The innocent ones. What do they get for carrying yours?"

"A better world."

"That's what the edict said." She let that sit. "Register or burn, and the city will be safe. Same sentence. Different god."

His jaw tightened. For the first time, the sculptor's pose slipped, and underneath it was just an old, tired, furious man. "You are a *child*. You have survived on the streets and in cellars because people kinder than either of us hid you. You do not understand what it costs to build anything that lasts."

"No," Ulyana agreed. "I really don't. I know what it costs to sell charms for copper. I know what it costs to run across rooftops in the rain. I know what it costs when someone buys you from your friend like a sack of flour." Her skin sparked; she reeled it back in. "That's my entire education. But here's the thing nobody with a big education ever seems to figure out, so maybe it takes a girl with none." She pointed at the throne. "*That* is a lie."

"It is the oldest truth in Veymark—"

"It's a chair." Lightning popped between her fingers, unbidden, and she didn't stop it this time; she let the light of it fill the hall, blue-white, throwing both their shadows huge against the scorched banners. "Silas went into the flooded archive. Read the records. Do you know what they say? The storm-crown isn't inherited. It isn't won. It *chooses*. And do you know what else? The war that killed my family—the war you've spent your whole life avenging—the old king *staged it*. Burned his own district. Wrote his own atrocity. My mother's people weren't a threat, Corvess. They were an *excuse*. There is no ancient right waiting in that chair. There's just a room where a liar sat, and a story he invented, and everyone who died because it was convenient." Her voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "So no. I won't wear a throne built on a lie. Not his lie. Not *yours*. You want to put me up there so the killing gets a flag to rally around, and I'd rather drown than be a flag."

For a moment the only sound was the battle outside, distant and rhythmic, like the city's heart stuttering.

Then Corvess laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry as old paper, with nothing warm anywhere in it.

"They told me the files were destroyed," he said. "Thirty years ago. I helped try to save them, in the first days. We lost people." He shook his head slowly. "If it's true—if the war was forged—then it changes nothing. Do you understand that? Nothing. The crown burns mages *today*. The edict is real today. Whatever lies founded it, the fires are not lies. The graves are not lies." He straightened, and though his hand shook, his eyes didn't. "You think I want you crowned because of blood. I don't care about your blood, child. I care that the people who survive tonight need something to believe in besides fear, and you are what they have. If not the crown, then the symbol. If not the symbol, then the weapon. One way or another, the storm-line serves the Remnant. That is not cruelty. That is arithmetic."

"There it is," Ulyana said softly. "Finally something honest."

"Ulyana—"

"No, thank you. Really. Thirty years of martyrs' names, and it turns out what you actually want is a *battery*." She backed toward the doors, keeping her eyes on him, feeling for the storm outside the windows the way you'd feel for a knife on a table in the dark. "You're not the opposite of Dame Crane. Did you know she refused an order to execute children? A crown inquisitor has a better spine than the liberator of the mages. Think about that in your next sermon."

Something moved at the edge of her sight—figures in the shadow of the colonnade behind the throne. Remnant hunters. Four of them, maybe five, the ones he'd sent ahead, the ones she'd known would be there because Silas had taught her years ago that an empty room is never empty, *check the corners first, little bird, always the corners.*

"Take her gently," Corvess said, and meant it, which was the worst part. "She's tired and grieving. No harm comes to her. When the battle's done, she'll understand—"

"She'll what?" Ulyana said. "Understand?"

The nearest hunter lunged. Ulyana dropped flat, and the lightning she'd been letting crawl under her skin all this time—patient, coiled, polite—finally got to speak. It went out of her in a flat white sheet that threw all five hunters backward across the marble, smoking, alive—alive, she made sure of it, she *chose* alive, that was the whole point, that was the only point—and blew out half the windows in the eastern wall so the storm could lean in and look at what she'd done.

Rain swept across the throne room floor. The chandelier swung on its one surviving chain, creaking.

Corvess had stayed on his feet. Of course he had. He looked at her through the rain with an expression she'd seen once before, on Dame Crane's face, in the candlelit hour when they'd talked past midnight: the look of someone recalculating everything.

"You would have made a magnificent queen," he said.

"Yeah," Ulyana said. "People keep telling me what I'd make. Magnificent queen. Perfect symbol. Good little weapon." Thunder answered her, rolling up from the harbor, and beneath it—faint, almost lost, but unmistakable to a girl made of storm-blood—another sound. A roar of wind from below the palace. From beneath it. From the direction of the flooded vaults, where a certain stubborn, self-sacrificing, infuriating exiled storm-caller had gone hunting for a crown that chose its bearer.

And the wind sounded wrong. Strained. Like someone holding open a door against a gale with nothing left in their arms.

Her stomach dropped through the polished floor, through the foundations, all the way down into the dark water where Silas was.

"Hold her!" Corvess snapped, and the hunters rose, singed and grim, and Ulyana turned and ran—not away, not this time, but *down*, toward the vault stairs, toward the water, toward the only person who had ever fed her bread and lied about not caring—while behind her Corvess's voice chased her down the dark:

"You cannot refuse destiny forever, child! It waits! It *waits!*"

"Good," she shouted back into the dark, already taking the stairs three at a time, lightning lighting her way. "Then it can wait for me. I'm busy saving my family!"

* * *

The vault door was open. The water beyond it was glowing.

Ulyana stood at the threshold with rain in her hair and her heart going like a drum corps, looking down into a flooded hall lit from within by a slow golden light, and at the center of it, barely above the surface, a figure knelt with his arms spread wide, holding back a power that was not his anymore and never had been, and even from here—even through the water and the dark—she could hear him talking to it. Negotiating. Making jokes.

Of course he was making jokes. He'd taught her that.

"Silas Fairweather," she said, wading in, "if you die before I get to yell at you, I swear I will bring you back *just* to yell at you—"

The light flared. The water rose. Somewhere overhead, muffled by stone and storm, the Battle of Veymark reached its turning point without either of them.
