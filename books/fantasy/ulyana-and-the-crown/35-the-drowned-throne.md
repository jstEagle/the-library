## 35. The Drowned Throne

The water was up to Silas's chest and rising, and he had never felt so much like laughing.

Not because anything was funny. Nothing was funny. His legs had stopped answering questions somewhere around the third collapsed corridor, his hands were shaking so badly he could barely keep the herbwife's riddle in his head — *where the king kept his secrets, the rain keeps them better* — and every heartbeat cost him something he could not name and would not get back. Storm-exhaustion, Mother Ives would have called it, if Mother Ives were alive to call it anything. The body's ledger coming due. Ten years of small magics and one enormous one, all of it written down somewhere, and tonight the collector had come.

He laughed anyway, because the alternative was weeping, and he'd done enough of that for one lifetime.

*Left at the drowned archway,* Ives had said, pressing a cold iron key into his palm with hands that shook from age rather than fear. *Down past where the mirrors used to hang. You'll know the door because it won't want you.*

The door did not want him.

It stood at the end of a corridor that had once been grand — he could tell by the bones of it, the fluted pilasters furred with white mineral bloom, the ceiling fresco dissolved into a smear of blue and gold like a sunset left out in the weather. The door itself was black oak banded in silver gone green, and it sat six inches above the waterline, which meant the vault behind it was dry, which meant the old kings had spent fortunes on wards just to keep their most precious possession high and dry while the rest of the palace drowned around it.

Typical, thought Silas. The crown keeps itself comfortable.

The key fit. Of course it fit; Ives had carried it forty years waiting for someone mad enough to come down here. But when he turned it, the lock spoke — not clicked, *spoke*, a voice like distant thunder rolling through a valley, and the words arrived inside his skull rather than his ears:

***Who calls?***

"Nobody," Silas said. His voice came out as a rasp. "That's rather the point."

The door considered this. Water lapped at his chin. Somewhere far above, muffled by thirty feet of stone and flood, the battle for Veymark went on — he could feel it now, feel everything, the way a man with a fever feels every thread of his bedding. Corvess's mages hurling fire at the palace gates. Crown engines answering with alchemical thunder. And threaded through all of it, unmistakable, a bright wild note that could only be Ulyana, lightning without shape or discipline, magnificent and terrified.

She'd walked toward the throne room alone. Of course she had. He'd taught her better than that — taught her to check the rooftops, count the exits, never trust a smuggler's smile — and she'd walked in alone anyway, because she was seventeen and brave, and bravery at seventeen always looked exactly like foolishness from the outside.

The door opened.

***

The vault beyond was dry as a sermon.

Silas hauled himself over the threshold on arms that screamed, and lay for a moment on cold marble, dripping, wheezing, taking inventory. One novelist's imagination: intact. One exiled storm-caller: mostly spent. One chance to fix what he'd broken: pending.

The room was round, and its walls were lined with niches, and in each niche rested an object under glass — regalia, he realized. The old coronation regalia of the storm-line. A scepter crusted with salt-white crystals. An orb that held, impossibly, a little rolling fog, like a jarred piece of weather. Robes folded with the care owed to dead kings.

And in the center of the floor, on a plinth of black basalt, sat the crown.

It was smaller than he expected. That was the first absurd thing about it. He'd imagined some vast spiked horror, a thing that demanded necks. This was a circlet, slender, worked silver set with stones the color of storm-light — grey, violet, that particular green the sky turns before hail. It looked less like a symbol of dominion than like something a smith had made for a person they loved.

The second absurd thing was that it was humming.

Not sound, exactly. Pressure. The air in the vault pressed gently against him the way a summer sky presses before rain, and beneath the pressure ran a current of attention. He knew that feeling. He'd grown up inside it. Every storm-caller knew the moment the sky noticed you back.

*Ah,* said the crown, in the same valley-thunder voice, and there was something almost pleased in it. ***A caller. At last, a caller.***

"I'm not staying," Silas managed. He got one knee under himself. The marble swam. "I've come to — to make arrangements."

***You are dying,*** the crown observed, with the tactless precision of weather. ***Your channels are burned to wicks. I can mend them. I have mended greater ruin than yours. Kings who came to me hollowed out by war, by plague, by grief — I poured them full again. I can pour you full. Everything you lost at Greyford. Everything you buried these ten years. The storms will answer you as they answered your mother, and your mother's mother, back to the first woman who shouted at the thunder and was surprised when it shouted back.***

Silas closed his eyes.

And oh, it was a lovely offer. That was the trap of it, and he could see the trap clearly and it didn't matter even slightly, because the offer was still lovely. He let himself feel it for three whole seconds — the memory of Greyford rising unbidden, the sky splitting open at his word, the enemy line breaking not because they were beaten but because no army on earth keeps formation under lightning. He'd been twenty-two and invincible for eleven minutes. Then the counter-storm had come, and the field had burned, and the coven's children had burned with it, and he had learned what his gift actually weighed.

Ten years of luck-knots and fever-charms. Ten years of being small on purpose. Ten years of telling himself the exile was penance when really it was just fear wearing a hair shirt.

"You could," Silas said. "Mend me. I believe you." He opened his eyes and looked at the circlet, and made his voice as steady as a dying man could. "But here's my problem with kings. Every one of them took the deal you're offering. Fullness. Restoration. *Everything you lost, returned.* And then they spent the rest of their lives defending the fullness, because once you've been refilled, going empty again is unbearable. Isn't that right? Isn't that why the storm-line fell? Not because the old king feared your power. Because your bearers couldn't bear to be ordinary ever again."

The pressure in the room shifted. Not anger. Something more like interest.

***Your mother refused me too,*** the crown said, quieter. ***Did she ever tell you why?***

"She died before—" Silas stopped. Swallowed. "No. She never told me."

***She said a vessel kept full has no room left for anything new. She said she'd rather be a woman who sometimes commanded storms than a queen who was only ever the storm.*** A pause, long as a held breath. ***I have waited ninety years for a second refusal. I was beginning to think the bloodline had produced nothing but thirst.***

Silas laughed again — a real laugh this time, wet and cracked and startled out of him. "You're lonelier than I am."

***I am a crown,*** it said, with immense dignity. ***Loneliness is in the specification.***

He crawled forward. The last few feet took everything, and he paid it gladly, and knelt — actually knelt, which he hadn't done since childhood prayers he'd only pretended to mean — with his ruined hands flat on the basalt beside the plinth.

"Here are my arrangements," he said. "I'm not taking you. I'm not refusing you either — refusals get passed down like debts, and I'm done handing my girl my debts. What I'm doing is this: I bind you to her *choosing*. Do you hear me? Not to her blood. Not to the Remnant's ambition. Not to Corvess's war or Crane's law or any banner anybody raises over her head. To Ulyana. Whatever she decides — throne, council, ferry-boat to the free cities, nothing at all — you go where her choice goes, and nowhere else. If she says no, you sleep until someone worthy asks properly. If she says yes, it's because yes was hers, not ours."

***And if she chooses wrongly?***

"Then she'll choose wrongly as a free person," Silas said, "which is worth ten thousand correct choices made on a leash." He paused, and honesty wrung one more thing out of him. "Besides. She's better at this than any of us. She's been choosing since she was six years old, alone, on the streets, with nothing but her wits. We just kept getting in her way."

The vault was silent a long moment. The fog in the orb rolled. Somewhere overhead, faint as a memory, thunder crossed the city — and Silas felt it pass through the crown like a shiver through a dog's coat.

***Bound,*** the crown said at last. ***To the chooser. Witnessed by water and stone and one stubborn, half-drowned fool.*** The pressure gentled, became almost warm. ***Storm-caller. Your mother would have liked you. You argue the way she argued — as though winning weren't the point.***

"She'd have denied it," Silas whispered. "Loudly. At length."

***Yes,*** said the crown, and he could have sworn it sounded fond. ***Rest now, if you can. Your arrangement is drawing company.***

"What do you—"

The outer door boomed. Water gushed through the opening in a black sheet, and standing in it, lit by her own stray lightning crawling along the flooded corridor behind her like veins of daybreak, was Ulyana.

She was soaked to the skin, singed at one sleeve, bleeding from a cut above her eyebrow, and her eyes went from Silas — kneeling, grey-faced, shaking — to the crown on its plinth, and back.

"You're *dying*," she said. It wasn't a question. It was an accusation, hurled like the first stone of a very long argument.

"Hello to you too," said Silas. "How was the throne room?"

* * *

Ulyana had left Corvess shouting in an empty hall, and she regretted nothing except the shouting part, which had followed her down three stairwells like a dog that wouldn't stop barking. *Take the crown and legitimize us. Take it and end this in an afternoon.* As though crowns were tools. As though she were a hammer looking for a nail.

She'd come down here following the pull in her own blood — the storm in her chest had turned toward this place like a compass needle the moment Silas's great working tore open the sky above the city, and she'd learned, in the last terrible week, to stop fighting that pull and start reading it.

She had not expected to find Silas kneeling in a puddle of his own making, looking like a coat left out in the rain.

"Don't change the subject," she said, splashing across the vault. Up close it was worse. His lips were grey. His hands, those clever careful hands that tied luck-knots with a fisherman's patience, trembled against the stone like leaves. "You burned yourself out. On purpose. For the evacuation — Marta told me, everyone's telling me things lately, it's very annoying — you held the storm over the harbor quarter for *an hour* so the crowds could run, and nobody can hold a storm for an hour, Silas, nobody, it's not a thing bodies do—"

"It's not a thing sensible bodies do," Silas agreed. "Fortunately I was never sensible. Ask anyone. Ask the crown, it's known me five minutes and it's already filed a complaint."

"The crown doesn't complain, it *rules*—"

"This one complains. You'll see. It's very chatty." Silas's grin flickered and guttered like a bad lamp, and Ulyana's stomach dropped through the floor, through the flood, through the bedrock, all the way down to whatever lies underneath Veymark holding it up.

"No," she said. "No, don't you dare do the joke-until-you-collapse thing, I know that trick, I invented that trick, I learned it from *you*—"

"Ulyana." And the humor went out of his voice all at once, the way cloud goes out of a sky, leaving something clean and bare underneath. "Sit down. Please. I haven't much time and I'd rather spend it honest than funny, which for me is genuinely a sacrifice."

She sat. The marble was cold through her wet clothes. The crown hummed between them, and she felt it noticing her — that pressure, that attention, the sky's eye turning her way — and she understood suddenly, completely, what this room was and what the two of them were doing in it.

"You found it," she whispered.

"I found it. And I've made a mess of the paperwork, so listen." Silas drew a breath that seemed to cost a week of his life. "Everyone wants to give you this. Corvess wants to give it to you so he can hold your arm while you wave. The old king wanted it destroyed so no one could ever prove his war was forged. The Remnant wants it as a flagpole. Even the storm-crown wants to give it to you — it's been sitting down here for ninety years doing nothing but give, poor thing, it doesn't know how to receive." A ghost of the grin. "So I've taken the liberty of ruining it for all of them. I bound it to your choice. Not your blood. Not anyone's plan. If you take it, it's because you woke up one morning and decided, freely, that you wanted the weight. If you don't, it sleeps. Forever, if need be. Nobody — not me, not Corvess, not the whole assembled history of this miserable royal line — can put it on your head but you."

Ulyana stared at him.

"You *bound* it. While dying." Her voice cracked straight down the middle. "You spent your last — your last *anything* — on making sure nobody could use me?"

"I spent it on making sure *you* couldn't be used," Silas said. "There's a difference. It's the whole difference. It's possibly the only difference that's ever mattered." He swayed. She caught his arm — ice-cold, terrifyingly light, like holding a bundle of sticks — and eased him down until his back was against the plinth, his head tipped back, his eyes finding hers with an effort that was visible and enormous.

"Listen," he said. "Last lesson. I promise. After this you're officially smarter than me and I'll stop pretending otherwise."

"Silas—"

"The crown can heal me." He said it fast, before she could hope. "It offered. Everything back. Greyford undone, ten years returned, storms on call like a corner shop. All I had to do was wear it, and wear it forever, because that's the price nobody prints on the label. And I said no." He watched her face carefully. "I need you to understand that I said no *gladly*. Not nobly. Gladly. Because I finally worked out what my mother knew, what took me thirty years and one very long bath to work out: a person who's full has no room left. And I—" his voice caught, and he pushed through it, "—I want room. Room for the market. For Marta's terrible stew. For watching you argue with a city council made of mages and fishermen. For all the small weather. The big storms nearly killed everyone I love. The small ones are where I live now."

Ulyana was crying. She noted this distantly, the way you note rain starting — it was simply happening to her face, and there was nothing to be done.

"So don't you dare," Silas said softly, "sit there thinking you can trade yourself for me. Don't pick it up to save my life. That's just Corvess with kinder eyes. If it heals me, let it be because you chose it — chose *this*, custodian, keeper, whatever word fits — and chose it knowing you could also walk away and let a tired old charm-seller nap forever, and I would bless you for walking away. Do you hear me? Either answer is a good answer. That's what I bought with the last of the big magic. The right for both answers to be good ones."

For a long moment the vault held only water-sounds and the crown's low hum and Ulyana's breath going in and out in pieces.

Then she laughed — a wrecked, sodden, furious laugh — and wiped her face with a sleeve that was already beyond help.

"You absolute *lizard*," she said. "You manipulative, self-sacrificing, infuriating lizard. You've made it impossible to refuse you, do you realize that? If I walk away, I'm the wise girl who honored your stupid beautiful choice. If I stay, I'm the loving daughter fulfilling your dying wish. There's no option where I get to be *angry* at you properly!"

"There's always anger," Silas said. "Anger keeps. I've got ten years' worth in storage myself. We can compare inventories when I'm upright."

"When," she repeated, and seized the word like a rope thrown to a drowning swimmer. "*When* you're upright."

"When," he agreed, and his eyes were closing, and the crown's hum deepened, and Ulyana understood that the decision was now, and had perhaps always been now, since the night a sharp-eyed orphan had sung to a storm in a flooded cellar and the storm had sung back.

She reached out and lifted the circlet from its plinth. It weighed almost nothing. That was the last trick of it, she thought — the terrible things never weigh anything until they're on you.

"I'm not doing this for the bloodline," she told it. "Or the Remnant. Or the crown, or the war, or Veymark's glorious future, or any speech anyone has ever given me. I'm doing it because there's a stubborn, drowning lizard on the floor who gave up everything so I'd have a real choice, and I choose *him*, and I choose this city, and I choose the version of the world where people stop deciding things on behalf of other people. If you can't serve that, put me down and go back to sleep."

The crown warmed in her hands like a bird.

***Bound and witnessed,*** it murmured. ***Custodian. Not queen.***

"Custodian," Ulyana agreed. "Not queen."

She set it on her brow, and the storm came home.

It poured into her — not as fire, not as the raw crackling terror of the rooftops, but as *memory*, generations deep, every storm-caller's joy and grief and argument with the rain, and at the bottom of it all, warm as a banked coal, a woman's voice saying *a vessel kept full has no room left for anything new* — and Ulyana laughed aloud, because even the dead in her blood were on Silas's side.

Then she knelt, and laid both hands over Silas's cold ones, and let the crown pour through her and into him, healing, restoring, filling the burnt channels like spring rain filling a cracked riverbed — and watched color climb back into his face like dawn climbing a wall.

His eyes opened. Found hers. Went, very slowly, to the slim silver band on her brow.

"Oh no," he said. "I've created a monster. I can see it already. First it's custodian, next thing you'll be regulating the Nightmarket's stall fees—"

"Shut up, Silas."

"—there'll be *forms*, there'll be a committee on luck-knot standards—"

"*Shut up*, Silas." She pulled him into a hug that was half embrace, half throttling, and held on while the water rose gently around them both, and above them, far above, the battle for Veymark still roared — but underneath it now, threading through it, the thunder had changed its tune. It sounded less like a warning.

It sounded like a beginning.

"We still have a war to finish," Ulyana said into his shoulder. "Corvess is upstairs. Crane's somewhere in it. And I intend to give every single one of them a piece of my mind."

Silas winced. "Mind if I sit the next one out? I've recently taken up napping as a discipline."

Together, crown and charm-seller, they turned toward the door and the flood and the burning city above — and found, standing waist-deep in the corridor with her sword drawn and her expression caught somewhere between duty and doubt, Inquisitor Dame Rosalind Crane.

"Well," said Crane. "This complicates my report."
