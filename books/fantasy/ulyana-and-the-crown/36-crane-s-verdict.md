## 36. Crane's Verdict

Rosalind Crane had rehearsed the reading of the evidence eleven times.

She knew this because her clerk, a meticulous young man named Pellow who survived the fall of the White Tower by the simple expedient of being in the privy when it happened, had counted. Eleven rehearsals, conducted at odd hours of the night in the ruined records room, her voice steady, her hands steady, everything steady except the part of her that kept insisting she was about to make the largest mistake of a career built on never making mistakes.

"You could simply not do it," Pellow offered, for the twelfth time, holding the lantern while she shuffled the case files one last time. "The city's in chaos. No one would blame you for—"

"That is precisely the problem, Pellow. No one would blame me." She squared the pages against the table edge. "I have spent twenty years ensuring that people *would* blame me if I did something wrong. It seems a shame to waste the habit now."

He didn't laugh. He rarely laughed. But he handed her the second folder without being asked, which from Pellow was practically applause.

* * *

They gathered in the palace courtyard three days after the fighting stopped, because it was the largest space still standing and because there was a certain poetry to it that Crane refused to acknowledge out loud. The same courtyard where, nineteen years ago, King Aldric Vey had stood before his assembled court and announced that the storm-line had butchered a village of loyal subjects at Greyford, and that the crown would answer fire with fire until every last spark of that traitorous blood was stamped out.

She remembered watching it as a young justiciar-in-training, standing in the third row, feeling the righteousness roll over her like heat from a forge. *This is what law is for*, she had thought. *This is what order means.*

Now the courtyard held survivors. Mages with singed sleeves standing next to smugglers who smelled of bilgewater and gunpowder. Guardsmen who had thrown down their arms, some weeping, some looking profoundly embarrassed about the whole affair. Civilians pulled from the rubble of the Tannery Quarter, still gray with ash. A cluster of Remnant children sitting cross-legged on the flagstones under the supervision of an herbwife's apprentice who kept shushing them, mostly without success.

And at the front, in a chair carried down from the infirmary because he could not walk, sat Silas Fairweather.

Crane had seen him two days ago, properly, for the first time since the prison exchange. He looked like a man who had been struck by lightning from the inside out — which, she supposed, was not far from the truth. His hands trembled when he lifted them. His hair had gone white at the temples in a way that ten years of exile had never managed. And yet when she'd entered the infirmary he'd given her a small, crooked smile and said, "Inquisitor. Come to arrest me again? I'd argue, but I haven't got the strength."

"I've come to ask you something," she'd said.

"Ask."

"Did you know? About Greyford. About the forgery."

And Silas Fairweather, storm-caller, exile, the most wanted man in Veymark for the better part of a month, had looked at her with an expression she was still trying to name, and said: "I knew my mother didn't do it. Knowing *why* — no. I spent ten years thinking I was a monster who deserved his exile. Turns out I was only a man who was lied to." A pause. "It's strange. The lie was almost kinder."

That conversation had settled something in her. Or perhaps unsettled it. With Crane it was often difficult to tell the difference.

* * *

She took the steps of the old dais at midmorning, the stolen files in a leather satchel at her hip, and the crowd went quiet the way crowds only go quiet for two reasons: grief or judgment. Today, she suspected, they expected both.

"People of Veymark," she began, and then stopped, because the words felt wrong in her mouth, too much like a proclamation, and she had come here to end proclamations. She tried again. "My name is Rosalind Crane. For nineteen years I served the Crown Edict of Cleansing. I registered the gifted. I burned the unregistered. I told myself every night that I was holding back chaos, that law was a wall between you and the dark, and that anyone who broke the wall deserved what came through it."

Somewhere in the crowd a child asked loudly what a Cleansing was, and was shushed by three different adults.

"Three weeks ago," Crane continued, "I arrested a woman named Mother Ives. She gave up nothing to save herself. She died holding a door so children could escape through a sewer. I have spent a great deal of time since then asking myself what kind of law requires a grandmother to die holding a door." She let that sit. "I found no good answer. So I went looking for a bad one instead. And I found it."

She opened the satchel.

"What follows are documents recovered from the White Tower archives. They were classified under seal by order of King Aldric Vey, may whatever gods he prayed to judge him accordingly. They concern the events at Greyford, nineteen years past — the massacre that justified the extermination of the storm-line." Her voice did not waver. She had rehearsed eleven times; it would not waver now. "The first document is a requisition order, dated nine days before the massacre, authorizing the payment of forty crowns to a company of mercenaries operating under crown charter. The second is their captain's receipt, signed and witnessed. The third—" and here she made herself slow down, made herself read each word clearly, because these words were going to be repeated in every tavern and temple and street corner for a hundred years, and they deserved to be said correctly— "the third is a letter in the king's own hand, instructing that the village be burned and the dead arranged to resemble the work of storm-magic, 'so that the question of the old blood be settled once and forever.'"

The silence afterward was enormous. Not the quiet of shock — the shock would come later, in pieces, over months and years — but the quiet of a wound being opened so the poison could finally drain.

"The storm-line did not burn Greyford," Crane said. "The crown did. Your king murdered his own subjects and used their corpses to justify a generation of slaughter. Every registration. Every pyre. Every family torn apart in the Nightmarket while their neighbors watched and said nothing, because the law told them it was right." She closed the folder. "The law was wrong. I enforced it anyway. That is the whole of my confession, and I will answer for it."

Then she turned to the side of the dais, where four men stood under guard — Lord Chancellor Hesse, whose signature appeared on the requisition; Archivist Tolliver, who had maintained the forgery for two decades; General Marren, who had commanded the Greyford operation; and Prelate Ossian, who had assured the faithful that burning mages was holy work. Three of them stared at her with hatred. Ossian stared at her with pity, which was somehow worse.

"Lord Chancellor Hesse. Archivist Tolliver. General Marren. Prelate Ossian." She named them like a litany. "By the evidence contained herein, you are accused of conspiracy, mass murder, and the fabrication of a war. You will be tried by whatever court this city builds for itself, and I will testify against you with every document, every deposition, and every hour of my own complicity laid bare beside yours."

Hesse found his voice first. It was thin and reedy, the voice of a man discovering that power is largely a rumor. "You have no authority, Crane. The crown—"

"The crown is a smoking hole in the east wing," Crane said, "and its authority died with the men who forged a war. What remains is us." She gestured at the courtyard — the mages, the smugglers, the guardsmen, the ash-gray civilians, the shushed children. "All of us. Which brings me to my final order of business."

She unbuckled her sword belt. This she had not rehearsed, because some things cannot survive rehearsal. Her hands shook doing it, and she let them.

"For nineteen years I executed the edict. I registered the gifted. I burned the unregistered. Whatever tribunal judges Hesse and Marren and the rest must also judge me, and it must judge me by the same measure, or none of this means anything." She laid the sword on the dais steps, the hilt pointing outward toward the crowd, toward all of them. "I place myself under your judgment. I will accept whatever sentence you decide — banishment, imprisonment, or worse. I ask only that it be decided openly, by people who were harmed, and not quietly, by people like me."

* * *

What followed was, in Crane's professional assessment, the least orderly proceeding she had ever witnessed, and the most just.

A smuggler — Bren, she learned later, though everyone just called him *that bilge-rat* with surprising affection — stood up and demanded she hang. A guardsman stood up and demanded she not, on the grounds that Crane had once reduced his fine for smuggling fever-charms to his sister, and "a woman who does that isn't hanging material, whatever else she's done." An old widow named Marta, whom Crane recognized from a decade of surveillance reports she had filed and never acted upon, stood up and said, in a voice that carried across the entire courtyard:

"She came to my door with papers that could have taken my grandson. She looked at the boy sleeping in the corner, and she put the papers away, and she said 'mind you register him when he comes of age,' knowing full well he never would. I've thought about that day for six years." Marta folded her arms. "I don't forgive her. But I'll say this — the ones who wrote the law never once put their papers away. Hang the writers. Let the one who hesitated stand trial and see what she says for herself."

Crane stood very still on the dais and discovered that being defended by the people you had hunted was a sensation roughly adjacent to being flayed. She also discovered, somewhat to her alarm, that her eyes were stinging, and fixed her gaze on a point above the crowd with the discipline of twenty years' practice.

A trial was agreed upon — a real one, with advocates drawn from both the mage community and the mortal quarters, to convene once the council finished forming. Until then, the four architects would be held in the cells beneath the courthouse, which had survived the battle intact, presumably because even artillery recognized the architectural value of a good dungeon.

As the guards led Hesse away, the old chancellor paused beside the dais. Up close he smelled of ink and fear.

"You could have buried this," he said quietly. "You found the files weeks ago. You could have burned them and no one would ever have known. Why?"

Crane considered the question honestly, because he deserved at least that much.

"Because I spent nineteen years believing that order was worth any price," she said. "And then I watched a city full of people I was ordered to despise pull each other out of the rubble. There was no edict making them do it. No law. No wall against the dark." She looked at him steadily. "They were simply good. It turns out that was the thing my ledger never accounted for."

* * *

Evening found her in the records room, drafting — of all things — her own indictment. Pellow had gone home, or to whatever passed for home now; half the clerks' quarter was rubble and the other half was full of clerks arguing about whether the new council should use quills or, as one enthusiast insisted, "enchanted self-writing pens, now that nobody's arresting us for it."

A knock. She looked up expecting Pellow, and found instead Ulyana.

The girl stood in the doorway with rain in her hair and something vast and careful behind her eyes, the way she always did lately, like someone carrying a lit lantern through a paper house. Word had already spread through the city — word traveled fast in Veymark, faster than law ever had — that the storm-crown existed, that it waited in a flooded vault, and that the last heir of the storm-line had been seen coming and going from the old palace grounds. People looked at her differently now. Some with hope. Some with hunger. Crane recognized both expressions professionally; she'd worn them herself, in the old days, toward the files that promised answers.

"Inquisitor," Ulyana said.

"Former Inquisitor. Currently awaiting trial. You may call me Rosalind, or 'you there,' whichever is more comfortable."

Something flickered at the corner of the girl's mouth — not quite a smile, but the foundation of one. "I watched today. From the wall." She came further into the room, dripping on documents that very much deserved it. "You put yourself on trial. Nobody does that. Especially not people who were only following orders."

"Especially them," Crane agreed. "It's the following-orders people who most need the practice."

Ulyana studied her for a long moment. Then: "There's going to be a vote. On the council. About what happens to the crown itself — whether it stays locked in the vault, or gets destroyed, or..." She trailed off, and Crane heard everything she didn't say. *Or someone takes it. Or someone is made to take it.*

"And you wanted to see me why?" Crane asked gently.

"Because Silas is dying."

The words landed in the quiet room like a dropped glass.

"The storm-burning. He poured too much of himself into shielding the crowds during the battle, and there's nothing left to pour back. The healers can keep him comfortable. They can't keep him." Ulyana's voice stayed level, which told Crane exactly how much it was costing her. "There's one thing that might. One thing. And everyone keeps telling me it's my choice, my burden, my bloodline, my destiny—" her jaw tightened— "and I am so tired of destiny. Destiny sounds like something Corvess would say."

"It is precisely something Corvess would say," Crane said. "Why come to me?"

"Because you're the only person in this city who reads things all the way to the end before deciding what they mean." Ulyana stepped closer, and the lantern light caught the faint blue shimmer beneath her skin, the storm waiting patiently inside her. "The vault records. The crown's terms. You read them, didn't you? When you had the files."

"I read them."

"Then tell me the truth. All of it. Even the parts I won't want to hear." The girl's eyes were bright and terrified and absolutely steady. "Because tomorrow I walk into that vault, and I need to know exactly what I'm choosing — and I need to hear it from someone who won't tell me it's destiny."

Crane set down her quill. Outside, far off over the harbor, thunder rolled — soft, patient, almost conversational, like something old clearing its throat before speaking.

"Sit down," Crane said, pulling a chair toward the lantern. "It's a long ledger. But you're right. We'll read it to the end."
