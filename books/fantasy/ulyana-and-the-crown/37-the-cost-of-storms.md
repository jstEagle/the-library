## 37. The Cost of Storms

The room they had given Silas smelled of salt and candlewax and the particular sweetness of herbs steeped too long. It was a healer's room in what had been, until three days ago, a wing of the White Tower, and it still bore the marks of its old life: iron rings in the walls where prisoners had been chained, a window too narrow to climb through, a door that locked from the outside. Marta had hung lavender from the bars anyway. "If he must die," she'd said, tying the bundles with the brisk efficiency of a woman who had buried a husband and two sons and refused to be sentimental about a fourth death, "he'll do it smelling like something other than fear."

Ulyana sat beside the bed and counted his breaths.

She had gotten very good at counting breaths over the past two days. Silas breathed the way a gutter did after rain — shallow, stuttering, each inhale a small argument with the body about whether to continue. His hands lay on the blanket, and she could not stop looking at them. They were a storm-caller's hands, or they had been: long-fingered, restless, always half-curled as if holding invisible lightning. Now they were gray at the fingertips, and when she took one in both of hers it was cold all the way down to the wrist.

"You're staring," Silas said, without opening his eyes.

"I'm counting."

"Counting what?"

"Breaths. You owe me forty-one since I started."

A ghost of his old dry smile moved across his face. "And if I stop paying?"

"Then I'll come find you in whatever afterlife takes smugglers and charm-sellers and stubborn old liars, and I'll make you pay up there instead."

His eyes opened then — pale gray, washed out, but still his. "Ulyana."

"Don't." She said it fast, before he could get going, because she knew that tone. Everyone in this tower had used that tone with her for three days. The healers used it. Crane used it, which was somehow worse, because Crane's version came wrapped in perfect courtesy like a blade in silk. Even Bren had shuffled in yesterday, smelling of tar and guilt, mumbled something about debts, and left a pouch of silver on the sill that nobody had asked for. "Don't do the voice. Don't say my name like you're saying goodbye. You're not allowed to die, Silas. I forbid it. I'm going to be in charge of things soon, apparently, so consider that an official decree."

"The council hasn't even—"

"I don't care. Decree issued. Sealed with thunder. Very binding."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough went on too long, and Ulyana held his hand tighter and hated how small it felt in hers.

* * *

The truth was that she had already been to see the crown.

She hadn't told anyone. Not Marta, not Crane, not the council members who kept appearing at her elbow with titles on their tongues — Custodian, Regent, First Voice, as though she were a hat they could put on the city's head. On the second night, while Silas slept and the tower settled around her, she had gone down alone to the flooded vault beneath the old palace, carrying a lantern and her own terrible courage, and she had stood in the ankle-deep water before the drowned throne and asked it, out loud, in a voice that shook:

"What will it cost?"

Because she had read the archive pages now. All of them. She knew what Silas had found down here weeks ago, what he had never quite told her straight: *the crown chooses its bearer, and it cannot be worn by force.* And she knew what the storm had whispered to her in the catacombs, in the voice of every storm-caller who had ever lived — including, once, briefly, terribly, the voice of Silas's mother, who had sounded so much like him that Ulyana had wept into the rain for an hour afterward.

The crown had answered her question the way storms answer everything: honestly, and without mercy.

*Everything,* it said. Or rather, it showed her. It showed her the shape of full acceptance — not the borrowed spark she had carried all her life, not the wild gift she had learned to aim, but the whole of it, the deep well, the thing that had lived in kings and killed them young. It showed her what pouring that power outward would mean. Healing was not free. Nothing the storm gave was ever free; it only ever moved, from sky to sea to blood to sky again, and if she wanted to move enough of it into one dying man to knit a body back together that ten years of hoarded power had finally burned through —

Well. The storm was very clear about arithmetic.

She had stood in the water until her lantern guttered, and she had said, "I need to think," and the storm, to its credit, had let her think.

* * *

On the third morning, Silas was worse.

Marta found Ulyana asleep in the chair with her head on the mattress edge and woke her with a hand on her shoulder, gentle as snowfall. "He's asking for you," she said. Then, because Marta was Marta and kindness from her always arrived disguised as practicality: "Wash your face first. He'll fuss otherwise."

Silas was propped against pillows, and someone — Crane, probably, or one of the tower guards who had decided overnight that the exiled storm-caller was a hero rather than a criminal, people were funny that way — had opened the shutters. Gray light fell across the bed. Outside, Veymark was making its ordinary noises: gulls, cart wheels, someone arguing about fish prices two streets away. The city went on. That was the whole point of everything they had done, Ulyana reminded herself. The city went on.

"Hey, troublemaker," Silas said.

"That's my line."

"I've been thinking." He patted the edge of the bed, and she climbed up beside him the way she had when she was nine years old and scared of thunder, which was a joke between them now so bitter it was almost sweet. "About the crown."

Her heart stopped, restarted badly. "Have you."

"Don't do the innocent face. You have the worst innocent face in the history of faces. You've been down there. To the vault." He watched her, and she felt seen all the way through, the way she always had with him, from the first day he'd caught her stealing bread off his stall and given her the whole loaf plus a lecture on proper theft technique. "I know that look. Your mother had it too, when she'd made up her mind about something enormous and was waiting for everyone else to catch up."

"You never talk about her."

"There wasn't time. There was never time." He closed his eyes. When he spoke again, his voice had gone quiet, the teaching-voice, the one he used for the important things, the things he'd disguised as casual remarks for ten years. "Listen to me. The crown can save me. I know that. I've known it since the vault, and I know you know it too, because you've got the same stubbornness I do and you went and asked it yourself. Didn't you."

It wasn't a question. Ulyana looked at her hands.

"It wants to give me everything back," Silas said. "That's the trap of it. That's what it offered me, down there, when I was dying on the floor with Corvess's people coming. Everything I lost. Ten years of exile undone. My storms returned, full strength, no cost." A pause. "And I said no."

"Why?" Her voice cracked on it. "Why would you say no?"

"Because it wasn't mine anymore. Because I'd spent ten years learning what I was without it, and I liked — " he huffed, almost laughed — "all right, tolerated, most days, the person I'd become. And because taking it would have meant keeping it. Holding it. Being the thing the crown wanted, a vessel, forever." He turned his head and looked at her, and his eyes were clear and steady and absolutely certain. "But you're not me, Ulyana. You never were. I hid from my gift for ten years and it nearly destroyed me twice. You ran toward yours. You argued with it. You made it explain itself. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Nobody in the whole history of that bloody thing has ever made it explain itself."

"So you want me to take it." She heard her own voice go flat and hard. "You're dying, and your grand plan is for me to strap a cursed crown to my head and spend the rest of my life being a vessel, and you want me to be *grateful*—"

"No." He said it gently, and the gentleness stopped her cold. "I want you to understand that it's yours to choose. Not mine. Not the Remnant's. Not the council's, not Crane's, not the storm's. Yours. If you take it, take it knowing what it costs. If you refuse it, refuse it clean, and sit here while I die, and I will haunt you cheerfully and without resentment for the rest of your long life." His mouth twitched. "I make an excellent ghost. Very annoying. Ask anyone."

"Silas—"

"And if you take it," he went on, softer, "then hear the last thing I have to teach you, which is the only lesson that matters, and which I should have taught you years ago instead of luck-knots and pickpocketing. Power isn't for ruling. It was never for ruling. Every person who wore that crown thought it was a throne, and every one of them died alone on top of it. It's not a throne, Ulyana. It's a *hand*. It's for reaching out. That's all. That's the whole secret. Reach out."

His hand found hers on the blanket. Cold fingers. Counting breaths again, forty-two, forty-three.

"I'm tired," he admitted, and it was the most frightening thing he'd said yet, because Silas Fairweather did not admit tiredness, Silas admitted nothing, Silas sold charms and paid guards and lied beautifully for a decade. "So whatever you're going to decide, decide it soon. And Ulyana — " his eyes were closing — "whatever you choose. I was glad. Every single day. Even the bad ones. Especially those."

Forty-four. Forty-five.

She kissed his forehead, salt-stung and fever-hot, and walked out of the room, and did not let herself cry until the stairwell, and only for exactly one flight of stairs, because she had work to do.

* * *

The vault again. This time she brought no lantern. She didn't need one; the water glowed faintly where she stepped, answering her before she asked.

The drowned throne rose out of the black flood, and on it, wreathed in slow-moving light like weed in current, lay the storm-crown — not gold, not metal, but something older, something that looked like weather given shape, like the moment before lightning made permanent. It had waited a generation for her. It had waited, she understood suddenly, for someone who would come down these stairs afraid and ask questions anyway.

"I'm not doing this to be queen," she told it. Her voice echoed off wet stone. "I'm not doing it for the Remnant, or the council, or the dead, or revenge, or any of the beautiful reasons everyone keeps offering me. I'm doing it because there's a man upstairs who taught me that magic is just another way of feeding people, and he's dying, and I am *not done* needing him."

The storm-crown pulsed. Listening.

"And I know the cost." She waded closer, water climbing her thighs, her waist, cold as honesty. "I know what 'fully' means. I know I won't get to be careless with it ever again, that every storm I call will pass through me first, that I'll feel every drowning sailor and every burning field and every frightened child under open sky until the day I die. I know it doesn't come off." She stopped before the throne. "I accept it anyway. Not as a ruler. As a custodian. As a hand, reaching out. Take it or leave it."

The crown rose.

It settled onto her brow like recognition, like a name spoken correctly for the first time, like coming home to a house she'd never lived in — and the world *opened*. Every storm everywhere, every memory, every debt and gift, all of it suddenly hers to hold, heavy beyond bearing and bearable anyway, the way grief is bearable, the way love is. She gasped. She laughed. Both at once.

Then she reached out.

* * *

She poured.

Not struck, not blasted — poured, the way Marta poured tea, carefully, generously, with her whole attention. The storm's power moved through her and out through her joined hands, into the gray cold body on the bed, and she felt it work: knitting what ten years of buried lightning had unraveled, banking the fires that burned in his blood, coaxing breath back into rhythm the way you coax a fire in wet wood, patiently, endlessly, refusing to give up.

The healers came running when the light spilled under the door. Marta came. Crane came, hand on her sword out of pure habit, and stopped dead in the doorway, and did not draw, and did not leave.

Ulyana knelt with her forehead nearly touching Silas's, hair floating around her as though underwater though the air was dry, lightning crawling gentle as ivy along the walls, and she poured, and poured, and somewhere in the middle of it she started talking, because that was what you did, that was what he had taught her without ever meaning to — you fed people and you talked to them, that was the whole of magic.

"—and you're going to keep selling charms, real ones this time, honest ones, and you're going to complain about the rent, and you're going to tell embarrassing stories about me to the council until they beg you to stop, and you are going to *live*, do you hear me, you stubborn, impossible, wonderful old man, you are going to live—"

The light crested. Held. Began, slowly, like a tide going out, to ebb.

When it was done, she sagged forward, and arms caught her — strong arms, warm arms, arms that smelled of salt and lavender and ten years of small kindnesses.

"Well," said Silas Fairweather, alive, his voice rough but whole, his heart hammering steady under her ear. "That's the last time I fake my own death to get your attention."

"You didn't fake anything, you absolute disaster, you were *dying*—"

"Mm. Mostly. Sixty percent." She felt him smile. She lifted her head and looked at him, really looked — color in his cheeks, gray gone from his fingertips, eyes bright and wet and fixed on the faint corona still shimmering above her brow. His expression was something she had no word for. Pride and fear and love and farewell, all braided together.

"How much did it take?" he asked quietly.

She considered lying. Decided against it. He'd taught her better than that, too.

"All of it," she said. "Every day, from now on. But Silas—" and she smiled, and outside the window, far over the harbor, thunder rolled soft and easy, like a cat settling down, "—it turns out there's more than I thought. There's always more. It just needed someone willing to give it away."

He pulled her close, and for a long moment neither of them said anything, and the storm hummed contentedly through the stones of the tower, through the streets of Veymark, through the whole waking city.

Then the door banged open and Bren stood there, soaked, wild-eyed, pointing back down the corridor. "Council's convened. Emergency session. Crane's already there — and Ulyana, you'd best come quick, because half of them want to swear fealty to you and the other half want to arrest you, and they're *arguing*—"

Ulyana sighed, stood, and smoothed her rain-damp clothes. Some things, it seemed, no amount of ancient power could spare you from.

"Politics," she said darkly.

"Welcome to ruling," said Silas, grinning.

"I'm *not* ruling." She was already moving for the door. "I'm custodian-ing. There's a difference. Write it down."

And she went, barefoot, crowned in quiet lightning, to go and argue with a room full of people about the future of a free city — while behind her, in a small room that smelled of lavender, the man who had saved her first sat up in bed, reached for the bread someone had left on the sill, and began, at last, to live.
