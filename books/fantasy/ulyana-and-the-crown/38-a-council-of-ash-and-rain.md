## 38. A Council of Ash and Rain

The first meeting of the Council of Veymark was held in a burned-out granary, because it was the largest roofed building still standing, and because Mother Ives had once said that the best magic happened in ruined places, where nobody expected anything to work.

They had swept the ash into corners. They had dragged in benches from three different chapels and a tavern, so that no god or drunk could claim the furniture favored one faction. Someone—Silas suspected Ulyana, though she denied it with the particular innocence of the guilty—had hung a charm above the door made of knotted string and river glass, which did absolutely nothing except catch the light.

Forty-one people took their seats on the first morning. Nine mages. Fourteen guildsmen and guildswomen. Six harbor folk. A tanner, two widows, a former tower guard who kept his helmet on the table like an apology, an herbwife's apprentice named Tansy who was seventeen and terrified and had volunteered anyway, and one smuggler captain who had come "strictly to observe" and had somehow been given a chair at the front.

Dame Rosalind Crane stood at the head of the room with a ledger under her arm and the expression of a woman about to do something she considered both necessary and slightly indecent.

"Before we begin," she said, "I want it recorded that I argued against this."

"You argue against everything," said Marta the widow, from the second row. "It's how we know you're paying attention."

A ripple of laughter went through the hall—the first laughter any of them had trusted themselves to feel in months—and Crane waited it out with the patience of a woman who had once interrogated a smuggler for eleven hours and gotten not names but a recipe for pickled herring.

"I argued against this," she repeated, "because I spent nine years enforcing the old order, and the old order is what got us a burned tannery quarter, a dead herbwife, and a city that smells like smoke and river rot. You should not hand power to someone whose hands look like mine." She turned her palms up, briefly, as if displaying evidence. "But you voted. And I have learned, recently and painfully, that when people vote against my advice, they are sometimes right."

"So you'll take the post?" asked Alderman Petch, a flour merchant with flour still permanently in his eyebrows, as if he'd been mid-bake when the world ended and never quite finished.

"I will serve as justiciar," Crane said. "Not as the crown's justiciar. There is no crown. As *yours*. Which means," and here her voice went flat and cold in the way Silas remembered from an interrogation room lit by one candle, "that when I come for one of you—and someday I will have to—you will remember that you gave me the authority yourselves, and you will not act surprised."

Another silence. Then old Widow Sorrel, ninety if she was a day, raised a gnarled hand.

"Question," she said. "Do you still burn people?"

"No."

"Do you still drown them? Because my late husband always said the Inquisition drowned more than it burned, only quieter."

"Widow Sorrel, I have never drowned anyone in my life."

"Hm." The old woman looked unconvinced but sat down. It was, everyone would later agree, as close to a unanimous confirmation as Veymark had ever managed.

* * *

Bren Saltwright did not attend the second session, because Bren Saltwright did not attend things. He attended barges.

The ferry-fleet began, as most honest enterprises in Veymark did, as a dishonest one. With the bridges down—two of them cracked by Corvess's assault, one simply gone, sunk into the canal with the palace guard's armory aboard—the harbor districts were cut off from the market districts, and the market districts were where the food was. People tried swimming the canals for exactly one day, until a boy named Pip nearly drowned and was fished out by a Saltwright deckhand who swore afterward he'd only done it so the boy wouldn't die owing him money.

By the third day, Bren's boats were running the canals for copper a crossing. By the fifth, he'd posted rates on a board outside the Fishhook: *COPPER A CROSSING. CHILDREN FREE. NOBODY WHO FOUGHT AT THE WALLS RIDES FREE FOREVER BUT RIDES FREE THIS MONTH.*

"He's profiteering off a disaster," said Alderman Petch, scandalized, at the fourth session.

"He's feeding half the east bank," said Tansy.

"He's charging widows."

"He's carrying widows' goods for free and pretending he isn't," said Marta. "My granddaughter watched him do it. He told her to keep her voice down about it."

Petch opened his mouth, closed it, and moved on to the agenda item about grain tariffs.

Crane visited the Fishhook herself that week—not officially, she told herself, though she wore her justiciar's sash, which in Veymark now meant roughly what it had always meant, which was whatever the wearer wanted it to mean. She found Bren on his flagship barge, the *Honest Return*, coiling rope with the concentration of a man avoiding conversation.

"Dame Crane." He didn't look up. "Come to arrest me?"

"For what?"

"Give us a minute, I'll think of something. You always were better at that part than me."

She leaned against the rail and watched the barge behind his load passengers—a tanner's family, two Remnant children with singed sleeves, a tower guard off duty, all of them sitting shoulder to shoulder without anyone checking anyone's papers, because there were no papers anymore, and the absence of them still made people flinch like a missing tooth.

"I sold a girl's location to the crown," Bren said, to the rope rather than to her. "You know that."

"I know you sold a location. The girl wasn't there. A decoy was."

"I didn't know that at the time." He set the coil down. "I'd like to say I would've stopped if I'd known. I'd like to say a lot of things. Mostly I'd like to say the fleet runs free for anyone who fought at the walls, forever, starting now, and I'd like it noted somewhere official that I said it first, before you asked."

"Noted," Crane said. "Though I'll point out I wasn't going to ask."

"You were going to ask eventually. You've got a face for asking eventually." He finally looked at her, and there was something underneath the smuggler's grin that she recognized, having seen it in mirrors during the worst years: a man trying to find out whether he was still worth anything. "One condition. The board stays up. Copper a crossing. If the council votes it free, fine, but while it costs copper, everybody pays it. Rich, poor, mage, mortal. Nobody rides special. That's the whole point of the thing."

"That's the most political thing you've ever said."

"Don't tell anyone. I'll lose my reputation." He picked the rope back up. "Now get off my boat, Dame Justiciar. Some of us are working."

* * *

The question of titles took six sessions, two near-brawls, and one memorable afternoon on which Alderman Petch proposed that the storm-line heir be named Queen-Protector and was answered by every person in the room talking at once, including, briefly, the cat that had taken up residence in the granary rafters.

Ulyana attended every session. She sat in the back, on the bench nearest the door, and she listened the way she had once listened in the Nightmarket—completely, quietly, missing nothing. When the debate turned to her, as it did with the regularity of weather, she would stand, say some version of *no*, and sit back down.

"No" came in many flavors. To Petch's Queen-Protector: "No. I've seen what a throne built on a lie does to a city. I lived under it." To the Remnant's proposal—Corvess was gone, but his followers remained, chastened and searching—that she serve as First Voice of the Gifted: "No. Your last First Voice decided he knew what was best for everyone, and I buried friends in the rubble of his certainty." To the harbor delegation's offer of Lord-Admiral of the Rebuilt Fleet: "I can't swim. Ask Bren. He thinks it's hilarious."

"But the people need a symbol," Petch persisted, in the seventh session, wringing his hat. "Something to gather around. Something *above* the factions."

"They have one." Ulyana nodded toward the front of the room, where Crane sat taking minutes in her small, merciless handwriting. "You elected her. Gather around her."

"She arrests people!"

"She arrests the right people, and then she goes home and can't sleep, and Marta brings her soup, and she eats it because refusing would be rude. That's a better symbol than a crown. Crowns don't eat soup."

The hall laughed. Even Crane's mouth twitched, which those who knew her understood to be the equivalent of another woman weeping with joy.

Afterward, in the granary doorway, with rain coming down soft and steady over the burned quarter—rain that fell on mage and mortal alike and, these days, put out fires instead of hiding lightning—Marta caught Ulyana by the sleeve.

"You could have it, you know," the widow said. "Any of it. They'd give it to you tomorrow."

"I know." Ulyana watched the water run silver down the gutters, past the herbhouse ruins, where someone had planted something green in the ash and it was, impossibly, coming up. "That's why I won't take it. Anything they'd give me tomorrow, they could take away the day after. I'd rather be useful than important. Useful lasts."

"And what will you be, then?"

Ulyana smiled—the sharp-eyed street smile Silas had fallen for years ago, softened now around the edges by everything she'd survived. "Present," she said. "I'm going to be present. At every session. Listening. And when somebody proposes something cruel or stupid or lazy, I'm going to stand up and remind them I once carried the last spark of the storm-line in my chest, and even *I* couldn't make lightning fix a grain tariff, so maybe we should listen to the people who can."

* * *

Silas came to the ninth session, which surprised everyone, including Silas.

He sat in the back now too—he and Ulyana had an unspoken arrangement about the back bench, sharing it on alternating weeks like a pair of cats dividing territory—and he spoke exactly once, when the council reached the matter of the gifted registry.

The old registry, of course, was ash. The edict was repealed, the White Tower's records burned or stolen or both. But names were needed now for a new purpose: a census of the gifted, voluntary, for the allocation of healing duties and storm-shield rotations through the winter.

"There will be people who don't sign," Silas said. His voice was quiet, and the hall went quiet to hear it; ten years of hidden living had taught him how to fill a room without raising his voice. "People who hid through the worst of it and don't believe the worst is over. Some of them are right to hide. Fear doesn't repeal itself just because a law does." He looked down at his own hands, which had once called storms and now ached in cold weather like any dockworker's. "Leave a way in for them. Not a door—a window. Somewhere they can climb through when they're ready, without having to knock first and explain themselves."

Tansy, the apprentice, raised her hand. "What kind of window?"

"A stall," Silas said. "In the Nightmarket. Where a person might buy a luck-knot, and mention, casual-like, that they've got a gift, and walk away knowing somebody knows, and nothing happens. No ledger. No name. Just... one person less alone than yesterday."

The council passed it without a dissenting vote. Widow Sorrel abstained on principle, having abstained on every vote since the founding, on the grounds that "someone has to keep an eye on the lot of you by disagreeing."

* * *

On the last evening of the month, the rain cleared, and the council adjourned early, and Veymark did something none of its forty-one council members had planned or authorized.

They lit lamps.

All of them. Every lamp in the Nightmarket, the harbor district, the lanes below the seawall—lamps that had gone dark a year ago when the edict came, when light meant being seen and being seen meant the pyre. Now the lamplighters went street by street, and people came out of doors to watch, and nobody sent soldiers, because there were no soldiers anymore, only a ferry-fleet on the dark water and a justiciar walking home with soup-stain on her sash and a city full of windows glowing like embers banked for morning.

Ulyana stood on the seawall—her seawall, the one she'd fled along once in a thunderstorm, certain the sky itself had betrayed her—and looked out at all that stubborn light.

Silas found her there. He usually did; they had a talent for finding each other in high places during weather.

"They'll want to give you something again," he said. "Tomorrow's session. Petch has a proposal. I saw him writing it. There were ribbons involved."

"Poor man. Someone should save him from himself."

"You could let him. One title. Honorary. It would cost you nothing."

Ulyana considered it, watching a fishing boat come home through the harbor mouth, guided in by Bren's ferry-lamps strung along the breakwater like a necklace of small mercies.

"No," she said at last, comfortably. "Let them keep trying. It's good for them. A council that's busy arguing about what to call me is a council that isn't arguing about grain tariffs." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Besides. I already have the only title I ever wanted."

"Which is?"

"The person Silas Fairweather sells charms to." She grinned. "Even if they don't work anymore."

"They work perfectly," Silas said, with dignity. "They work because people believe in them. That's the oldest magic there is, and I'll thank you not to slander it."

Below them, across the shining streets, thunder rolled—far off, out over the open sea, where storms belonged now. It sounded less like a warning than a greeting.

And in the Nightmarket, in a stall that did not yet exist, an empty table waited for a luck-knot seller, and a sign waited to be painted, and the morning was coming the way mornings came now in Veymark: not promised, not safe, but chosen—by the people who stayed to build it.
