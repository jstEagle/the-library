## 39. What Silas Buried

The doctor—such as Veymark still had, after everything—had told Silas that the numbness in his hands might pass. He had said it in the tone of a man telling a farmer the drought might break, which is to say: kindly, uselessly, and while looking anywhere but at his patient's face.

It had been four months. The numbness had not passed.

Silas had learned to live with it the way one lives with a bad hip or an old debt: by pretending, most hours of the day, that it wasn't there. Some mornings he woke reaching for the storm the way a man reaches for a wife who has died, and found only his own pulse, ordinary and mortal and stubbornly continuing. Other mornings he woke and simply made tea, and that was better.

The tea helped. Most things, he was discovering, helped less than people promised, but they helped.

The stall in the Nightmarket had cost him eleven coppers to rent from a woman named Odile who ran the fishmongering two spaces down and had opinions about everything, including, at length, the price of canvas awnings, the moral character of pigeons, and Silas himself.

"You're the storm-caller," she'd said on his first morning, not as a question, squinting at him over a barrel of eels.

"I'm a charm-seller," Silas said.

"You're *the* storm-caller. My nephew saw you call lightning off the harbor during the battle. Said it stood up like a wall." She sniffed. "And now you're selling luck-knots."

"Luck-knots are respectable."

"Luck-knots are *rubbish*," Odile said fondly, and gave him a discount on the awning anyway.

That was the strange arithmetic of the new Veymark. Half the city knew exactly what he had done—the storm that had shielded ten thousand fleeing souls through the burning streets—and the other half knew him only as the man who'd once sold them fever-charms that were, strictly speaking, knotted string. Both halves kept coming back. He had stopped trying to reconcile them. The city, he was learning, did not require its heroes to be consistent. It only required them to show up.

So he showed up.

* * *

The stall opened at dawn, because the market's rhythms had survived kings, edicts, fires, and one moderately apocalyptic battle, and would survive Silas's sleeping habits too. He set out his wares on a board worn smooth by forty years of other sellers' goods: luck-knots in three colors, "warding" braids for doorposts, small clay beads fired in a neighbor's kiln and sold as protection against nightmares, and—for the ambitious—a jar of river stones labeled, in his own careful hand, *Stones That Have Been Near Water.*

"That last one's cheating," said Ulyana, who arrived every morning at roughly the time the light came over the rooftops, which is to say whenever she felt like it, and who now leaned on the stall's counter as though she owned it, which in some legal sense she might have.

"It's honest cheating," Silas said. "The label makes no claims beyond geography. Those stones have, in fact, been near water."

"They've been near your *bucket*."

"Which contains water. Geography is geography."

She laughed—that bright, unguarded laugh that had taken a year of peace to come back, and that Silas privately considered the finest thing the revolution had produced—and stole an apple from the basket he kept for exactly this purpose, and sat herself down on the stool beside him like a person settling into a chair she intended to occupy for the rest of her natural life.

This was the arrangement. Nobody had proposed it aloud. On the first morning after he'd reopened the stall, she had appeared, scowled at his inventory, corrected his knotwork, and stayed until dusk. She had done so every day since. The council she nominally served could summon her; the city needed her for ceremonies and disputes and the occasional diplomatic thunderclap; but between all of it, she came to the Nightmarket, and she sat on the stool, and she ate his apples, and if anyone had asked either of them why, they would have received two entirely different non-answers delivered with identical stubbornness.

"Corvess's trial starts next week," she said, after a while, working the apple's stem loose with her thumbnail.

"I heard."

"He's going to plead that he was right."

"He was right about the crown," Silas said. "He was wrong about nearly everything else. The tribunal will sort it out. They're sorting out a lot of things these days." He tied off a knot—badly; his fingers were slow now, and he'd stopped minding—and added it to the pile. "Crane is presiding."

Ulyana glanced at him. "Does that bother you? After the tower. After—" She gestured vaguely, a gesture that encompassed candlelit interrogations, case files, and one very memorable arranged escape.

"Rosalind Crane hounded me across a decade and then tore down her own government to make it right," Silas said. "I don't know whether to fear her or build her a statue. Possibly both. A statue that follows you around."

"She'd hate that."

"She'd arrest the statue."

* * *

The customers came slowly at first, and then, as markets do, all at once.

There was Tam the rope-maker, who bought a luck-knot every month and had done so since before the war, and who now, Silas suspected, understood perfectly well that the knots were string, and bought them anyway, because Tam's father had bought them, and because some purchases are prayers wearing coats.

There was the widow Marta, who came every eighth day and refused to pay, and whom Silas paid instead, in the form of bread slipped into her basket when she wasn't looking, though she was always looking, and always let him believe she wasn't. Her grandson Joren—the tower guard whose keys had opened the White Tower's back stair—had been promoted twice and visited rarely, and when he did visit he stood too straight and called Silas "sir," which Silas found unbearable and secretly treasured.

There were new faces, too. Families from the burned quarter, rebuilding. Fishwives who'd seen the storm-wall and wanted, of all things, *storm-charms*—little knotted cords meant to honor what he'd done, not to replicate it. Silas sold those at cost and donated the proceeds to the orphan house on Tanner's Lane, which had been Mother Ives's idea once, years ago, back when she'd been alive and scheming and magnificent.

He did not sell magic. There wasn't any left to sell, or almost none—a flicker, sometimes, on stormy nights, a pressure behind his eyes like weather remembered rather than weather felt—but even if there had been barrels of it under the stall, he would have sold the string.

Because here was the thing he had buried, down deep, in the flooded vault of himself where he kept the Battle of Greyford and the look on Bren's face and the sound of Ives's last breath: he had spent ten years selling charms that worked only because people believed in them, and he had thought of it as a con. A survival. A shameful little trade for a fallen man.

And then he had watched this city burn and bleed and bury its dead, and he had watched it get up the next morning anyway, and the morning after that, and he had finally understood what he'd actually been selling all along. Not luck. Not protection. Permission. Permission to hope a little, cheaply, without shame. Every copper spent on a knot of string was a copper spent saying *tomorrow might be kind*, and there was no magic in the world worth more than that, and no magic required.

His gift had burned itself out shielding strangers. His living, it turned out, never needed it at all.

* * *

On a grey Thursday in late autumn, Odile came by at midmorning with two cups of fish-broth, which was her version of tenderness, and stood watching Ulyana re-string a display of nightmare-beads with the brisk competence of someone who had appointed herself quality control.

"You know," Odile said, in the carrying whisper of the professionally indiscreet, "half the market thinks she's your daughter."

"We met when she was nine," Silas said.

"And the other half thinks you're sweet on each other."

"The other half should mind their eels."

"Mm." Odile sipped her broth. "I think you're family. Family's rarer than either of the others. Hold onto it." And she went back to her barrel before either of them could answer, which was also her version of tenderness.

Silas looked at Ulyana. She was pretending very hard to be absorbed in the nightmare-beads. Her ears had gone slightly pink.

"She's not wrong," Ulyana said, to the beads.

"No," Silas agreed. "She's not."

They let it sit there, warm and unexamined, the way you let a fire sit in a hearth—not because it needs tending in that moment, but because poking at it would only scatter sparks.

* * *

The trouble—if you could call it trouble, and Silas had learned to be suspicious of anything that arrived looking calm—came at closing time, with the light going amber over the rooftops and the market emptying into the evening streets.

A woman stopped at the stall. Middle-aged, travel-worn, dressed in the plain wool of the hill country north of Veymark. She looked at the luck-knots for a long moment, and then at Silas, with an expression he couldn't read.

"You're Fairweather," she said. "The storm-caller."

"I'm a charm-seller," he said, automatically, and then, because the old reflexes were dying hard but dying nonetheless, "Yes. That was me."

"My village sent me." She reached into her coat and drew out a folded paper, soft with handling, and laid it on the counter. "We've had word there are others. Storm-callers. Up in the hills, in the valleys—people who hid for twenty years, same as you. Now the edict's gone, they're coming out of hiding, and they're frightened, and half of them can't control what they carry, because nobody ever taught them. Because everyone who could have taught them is dead or scattered." She looked up. "They asked for you by name. They said if the exiled Fairweather could stand in a market square and sell string, then maybe they could learn to live in daylight too."

Silas looked at the paper. It was a list of names. Eleven names, in different hands, some of them written carefully like children practicing, some scrawled fast like people afraid to be caught holding the pen. At the bottom, in a shaky script: *Please.*

"I'm not what I was," he said quietly. "What I carried—it's mostly gone. Spent."

The woman shrugged, hill-country style, the shrug that says *that's weather, not argument.* "We're not asking for your storms. We're asking for your ten years. You lived hidden and whole. You learned how. Teach us that."

Behind him, he heard Ulyana go still on her stool.

He stared at the list for a long time. The numbness in his hands, the ache that lived where the storm used to, the decade of fear and small kindnesses and buried grief—all of it rose up at once, and he let it, and then he set it down again, gently, the way you set down something heavy that you've finished carrying.

"I'd need to think," he said. "About the stall. About—"

"Take the winter," the woman said. "Spring's soon enough. Growing things keep their own schedule." She nodded once, turned, and was gone into the evening crowd before he could offer her broth or lodging or anything at all, hill folk being what they were.

Silas stood there a long moment, the paper in his numb hands.

"Well," said Ulyana, from the stool. Her voice was carefully light, and underneath the lightness ran something else—something that sounded almost like hope wearing its work clothes. "You always said the string was the honest trade."

"It is."

"So teach them the string first." She hopped down, took the list from him, read the eleven names, and something in her face shifted—settled, the way weather settles before it turns fair. "Then whatever comes after."

She rolled the paper and tucked it into her coat, next to the council seals and the smugglers' ferry-tokens and all the other improbable cargo of her life, and she looked up at him with the seawall wind of her whole history in her eyes.

"Besides," she said, "you'll need someone to carry the apples."

Far off, past the harbor, past the drowned vault, past the crown locked in its vault belonging to no monarch, thunder rolled—soft, unhurried, almost companionable—and Silas Fairweather, charm-seller, exiled storm-caller, teacher of nothing yet, felt the old pressure stir behind his eyes like a sleeper turning over, and smiled.

"Spring, then," he said.
