## 1. The Date and the Dead

The bone told Freya Okafor everything before the widow finished her first cup of tea.

It was a knucklebone from a bull's hind leg, boiled clean and bleached to the color of old milk, and it sat on her workbench like a small patient moon. Maren Ashwell had brought it wrapped in a handkerchief along with a lock of her husband's hair, because that was how it was done in the valley — you brought the bone and the relic of the dead, and the carver did the rest. Freya had set down her tea, taken up her finest knife, and begun.

The first curl of shavings fell away and the whisper started.

*Tell her the blue dress,* said the grain. *She thinks I hated it. Tell her I bought it for her birthday before—*

Freya's knife paused. The whisper was faint today, worn thin, the way voices got when the dead had been dead a long while. Corren Ashwell had been in the ground eleven months, and his voice had gone soft as river-smoothed stone. But it was still him. Still stubborn. Still circling back to the same unfinished sentence he'd been trying to finish since winter.

"I hear him," she said quietly, not looking up. It was kinder to say it early. "He's faint, but he's there."

Maren's teacup rattled against its saucer. "What does he say?"

"He wants you to know about the blue dress." Freya turned the bone under the lamplight, studying the grain the way other people read faces. "The one hanging in the back of your wardrobe with the tags still on it. He bought it before the fever took him. He was going to give it to you at the harvest dance."

The sound Maren made was not quite a sob and not quite a laugh. It was the sound Freya had learned to live inside of, the sound that paid for her bread and her lamp oil and the roof over her head, and which she had never once gotten used to. She kept carving. Thin curls of bone fell away like snow, and with each one the voice came clearer, and she gathered what it gave her: the name of the money hidden under the floorboard by the stove — forty crowns, not twenty, Maren should dig deeper — the apology for the quarrel about the fence line, the way he'd watched her kneading bread every morning of their marriage through the window and thought, every single time, *there she is, there's my whole life.*

By the time the sun went down behind the western ridge, the keepsake was done: a small carved wren perched on a branch, wings folded, head tilted as if listening. Freya pressed it into Maren's hands and closed the widow's fingers around it.

"When you miss him," Freya said, "hold this. He'll be quieter than he was today, but he'll be there. Bones remember. That's all my gift is, really — bones remember, and I've learned to listen."

"And you?" Maren asked, shrugging into her coat at the door. Her eyes were red but steady. "Who do *you* listen to, girl? You spend all day in other people's dead."

Freya smiled the smile she gave everyone who asked that question. "I listen to the living," she said. "Tonight, in fact. There's a man coming to dinner."

"A man!" Maren's eyebrows climbed. "Whose idea?"

"Old Sela at the bakery. She says he's a historian passing through, quiet, respectable, that he asked after me at the market." Freya shrugged, though her stomach had been doing slow, uncertain turns all afternoon. "Sela says a woman can't carve bones forever with no one to come home to."

"Sela meddles," Maren said fondly, "but she's usually right. Wear something nice. Not the apron."

Then she was gone, and the workshop was quiet except for the tick of cooling shavings and the faint, fading murmur of Corren Ashwell's last unfinished sentence drifting up out of the scrap bin where Freya had swept him. *Tell her tell her tell her.* They always ended that way. The dead never ran out of things they wished they'd said.

Freya looked at herself in the small mirror by the door. Bone dust in her hair. A smear of white across her jaw. Twenty-six years old, hands scarred fine as lace from thirty years of knives — no, twenty years; she'd started at six, when the first bone had whispered to her and she'd screamed for a week straight until her mother taught her that the voices were not ghosts in the room but memories in the material, harmless as echoes.

"Something nice," she muttered, and went upstairs to find the green dress.

* * *

The Lamplight Inn sat at the crook of the valley road where the millstream bent, and by half past seven its common room smelled of roast onions and woodsmoke. Freya arrived ten minutes late, which she considered punctual by the standards of someone who had spent the afternoon elbow-deep in a dead man's knucklebone.

Tomas was already there.

She spotted him by the window table because he was the only person in the room not eating or drinking or talking — just sitting, very upright, with his hands flat on the table as if the table might bolt. He was younger than she'd expected, maybe thirty, with ink-stained fingers and the kind of face that looked like it spent most of its time indoors. A leather satchel leaned against his chair leg, and he held one arm curled protectively around a large book on the table, the way a farmer holds a lamb in bad weather.

"You're Freya," he said when she reached the table. Then, immediately, wincing: "Obviously. Sela described you. I mean — not described. Mentioned. I'm Tomas. Tomas Vey. Please sit down, I've been rehearsing this for an hour and it's going worse than rehearsal."

Despite herself, Freya laughed. She sat. The barmaid brought wine without being asked — Sela's doing, certainly, and probably Sela watching from somewhere behind the kitchen door.

"So," Freya said. "A historian. What brings a historian to a valley with no history?"

"That's exactly what I used to think." He smiled — a quick, self-deprecating thing. "Everyone said the valley was a dead end. Nothing happened here, ever. No battles, no kings, no ruins. But that's why I came, actually. A place with no recorded history is either hiding one or has been deliberately left out of one, and both possibilities are interesting." He caught himself. "Sorry. That's a terrible opening. Sela told me not to lecture."

"She told you right." Freya sipped her wine. Up close he wasn't handsome, exactly, but there was something in his face she liked — a kind of careful attention, as if he listened the way she listened, wholly and without hurry. "And did you find anything? In your dead-end valley?"

"No," he said. And then, softer, with a strange hesitation: "But something found me. A week ago. At a book stall in Marrow's Crossing, on my way here."

His hand moved to the great book on the table. Now that she looked properly at it, Freya saw it was ancient — cracked leather boards, brass corners gone green, pages so swollen with age they wouldn't close flat. A ledger, maybe, or a chronicle. The kind of thing that belonged behind glass in some southern archive, not on a tavern table in a valley where nothing had ever happened.

"It's a prophecy collection," Tomas said. "Compiled about a century ago by a court astrologer named Ilvane. Most of it is the usual nonsense — comets, doom, the return of this or that. But the last section..." He trailed off. His thumb moved along the page edge without opening it, back and forth, back and forth, like a man petting something that frightened him.

"But the last section?" Freya prompted.

"The final prophecy appeared overnight." He said it quickly, as if speed would make it sane. "Three nights ago. I'd read the book cover to cover on the journey — I'm a scholar, I read everything twice — and the last page was blank. Just blank vellum. When I woke the next morning at the inn, it wasn't."

"Someone wrote in it."

"Ink that matches my own handwriting, Freya." His eyes came up and held hers, and she saw that beneath the awkwardness he was genuinely afraid. "Not similar. Identical. I compared them for hours. The slant, the pressure, the way I cross my sevens. It's my hand. And it's dated tonight."

The common room noise seemed to recede, the way sound does underwater. Freya set down her glass very carefully.

"Tomas. Open the book."

He hesitated — one breath, two — then drew it toward him and opened it to the final leaf.

The handwriting was indeed elegant and cramped and utterly unremarkable, the handwriting of a man who wrote all day. But it was the words that made the hair rise on Freya's arms, and she read them twice, three times, her lips moving silently:

*On the night the carver dines with the key, the Ember stirs. Let it be written plainly, for plainness was our failure: Ulyana lives. Ulyana, true king, unbound from his own body these hundred years, crowned in flame he cannot remove. The Ember wears him as a crown wears gold — and it grows hungry, and it will march. The bone-carver Freya shall hear what the dead have hidden. The historian Tomas shall be the key that turns. On this night they meet. On this night it begins.*

Below the words, in fresher ink, still smelling faintly of the reed pen: tonight's date.

The inn went very quiet around her, or perhaps it was only her own blood in her ears. Freya became aware, distantly, that her gift was singing — not from any bone on the table, but from the book itself, from the vellum, from the skin of whatever animal had died a century ago to hold these words. It was murmuring. Old, dry, papery, but unmistakable: the parchment remembered being written on, and it remembered more than once.

*Tonight,* the vellum whispered. *Tonight tonight tonight.*

"Tomas," she said, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "This isn't funny. If Sela put you up to this—"

"I swear on my mother's grave I've never met Sela before Tuesday." He pushed the book across the table toward her, and his hand was shaking. "Read it again. Read your name. Read mine. I didn't write it. I *can't* have written it — I didn't know yours until Sela told me, and I'd never heard of anyone called Ulyana in my life until three days ago, and now I've dreamed about him every night since. A man under a mountain of ash, wearing a crown that's eating him alive, screaming without a mouth." He swallowed. "Do you understand? The book knew I would buy it. The book knew I would bring it here. It's been writing itself ahead of me like — like a road laying itself down under my feet."

Freya looked down at the page. At her own name sitting there in a stranger's handwriting, written a hundred years before her birth, waiting for her like a trap waits, like a grave waits.

Every bone-carver in the valley's long unwritten history had known one rule above all others, the rule mothers taught with the alphabet: *the dead tell you what they want you to know, and nothing more.* She had built her whole life on the gentle half-truths of the dead. And now a dead book — a book that had been dead a century — was telling her far too much, and none of it gentle.

Outside the inn's window, far off past the dark fields, something flickered on the ridge line. Once. Twice. Like a signal fire answering another.

Tomas saw it too. He went pale.

"Freya," he whispered. "There's one more thing on the page. Under the date. I didn't want to say it in front of—" He turned the book fully toward her and pointed with a trembling finger at the bottom margin, where four words stood in that impossible familiar hand, smaller than the rest, almost apologetic:

*Riders cross at midnight.*

The firelight on the ridge flickered a third time — and began to move downhill.
