## 2. Ink That Wasn't There Yesterday

Tomas Vey had handled four hundred and eleven rare books in his career, and he had catalogued every one of them in a ledger he kept with the devotion other men reserved for prayer. He knew the number because he had counted again that morning, standing in his rented room above the chandler's shop, holding the four hundred and twelfth at arm's length as though it might bite.

It was a small book. That was the first absurdity of it. Prophecies, in his experience, arrived bound in calfskin the color of dried blood, or chained shut with iron, or wrapped in seven layers of warning. This one was barely taller than his hand, its boards scuffed gray-green, its spine re-sewn sometime in the last century by someone with more haste than skill. The title page read *Observations on the Succession of the Ember Throne, Being a True Account*, and beneath it, a bookseller's pencil note: *dull. incomplete.*

He had paid six coppers for it a week ago, haggled down from eight, from a stall in the Salt Road market where the vendor was selling off a dead man's library by the crate. It had seemed, at the time, like a modest little disappointment — the kind of purchase a historian makes out of obligation rather than hope. A provincial chronicle, probably. Half-finished. The kind of book you read once to be thorough and then shelved spine-in so no one asked about it.

He had read it twice anyway, because thoroughness was the one virtue he could claim without lying. And it *was* dull. A hundred years of court records compressed into a merchant's diary: grain tariffs, coronation dates, the price of tallow. The author — one Aldric Fen, scribe of the palace under the last true king — had a flat, dutiful hand and no gift for narrative whatsoever. Tomas had noted with mild professional sympathy that the book simply stopped mid-sentence, two-thirds through, on a page dated the night of the old king's death. *The king has called for his crown to be brought up from the vault, though the physician begs him to wait until the fever breaks, and I have told him—*

And there it ended. No more entries. Just forty blank pages, and then the back board.

That was how it had been when he bought it. He would have sworn it on any altar they cared to name. Forty blank pages. He remembered thinking it a shame Fen hadn't lived to finish; even dull men witnessed interesting times.

Then, three nights ago, he had opened the book to check a date against another source, and the pages were not blank anymore.

* * *

He had not slept properly since. He sat now at the narrow table by his window, the book open before him, and read the final entry for perhaps the ninetieth time, hunting for the flaw, the trick, the seam where a forger's work must show if you looked long enough. There was no seam. That was the horror of it. The ink had soaked into the paper the way century-old ink soaks into century-old paper — bloomed slightly at the edges of each letter, oxidized to the same soft brown-black as Fen's earlier entries, sitting in the same faint trough pressed into the page by a quill held at the same angle.

The handwriting matched too. Not approximately. *Exactly.* Tomas had spent eleven years learning to read dead hands; he could distinguish Fen's *g* from a dozen other scribes' across a dark room. The final entry was Aldric Fen's handwriting to the last flourish — except that Fen had been dead for a hundred years, and Tomas had owned this book for a week, and the entry was dated tonight.

*The eleventh day of the Dying Moon,* it began, in that patient, clerkish script, *in the year the valley keeps no count of.*

*The bone-carver Freya Okafor will meet the historian Tomas Vey at the hour the lamps are lit, though neither of them chose the meeting, and neither of them will believe what follows.*

His own name. His own name, set down in ink older than his grandfather, in a hand that had stopped moving a century before he was born. He had read past it the first time in a kind of numb disbelief, telling himself: coincidence, a common name, a parlor trick. But the entry went on, and it knew things.

*The historian will have bought this book for six coppers and will have read it twice, and he will believe himself a careful man, and he is, which is why he will check the ink against his own writing and find them alike, and this will frighten him more than the prophecy itself.*

Tomas looked down at the scrap of practice parchment beside the book, where that morning he had written the alphabet three times in his own hand, pressing hard, comparing letter after letter against the page. The *y* that dipped below the line with its tail curled left. The *k* with its straight armed leg. His hand. Not similar. His.

*The prophecy names a king,* the entry continued. *Not the usurper who wears the crown now, but the true one, Ulyana, whom the Ember took. Hear me, whoever finds this: the king is not dead. The king is bound beneath his own crown, and the flame that wears him rules in his shape, and it is coming for the free cities, and there is one who can loose him and one who can anchor the loosing, and they are named above, and I am sorry for it, truly, for I have seen what it costs them both.*

*I did not write this page. I want that recorded plainly, in whatever hand survives me. I woke and it was here, written in my own ink, my own hand, describing a night a hundred years distant, and I have been a scribe all my life and I know every forgery there is, and this is not one. Something used me to write it. Something is using me still. If you are reading this, Tomas Vey, then it found you the same way, and I beg you — do not do what I did. Do not sit alone with it, checking the ink. Go to her. She will be frightened. Be gentle. The dead do not lie to her, but they lie to everyone else through her, and she does not yet know the difference.*

*The valley cannot hold. Take her out of it.*

The entry ended there. Below it, the remaining pages were blank again — but Tomas had checked an hour ago, and he would check again before he left, because yesterday morning there had been one line more, added in the night:

*She carves the dead into bone so she can hear them. You will hear nothing. Learn to be her ears' courage instead.*

* * *

He walked to the meeting because his legs refused any other instruction. The lane down to the miller's bridge was doing its ordinary evening work — lamplighters moving door to door, a dog arguing with a cart wheel, the smell of onions from the cookshop — and every ordinary thing felt like scenery arranged around him by an author he couldn't see. He kept catching himself watching his own hands swing at his sides as if they belonged to a character whose next line he hadn't been given.

That was the vertigo of it, and it did not fade with repetition; it deepened. Every man secretly believes his life is his own composition. Tomas had built his whole self on that belief — the careful scholar, the deliberate man, the one who checked sources. And now a dead scribe's hand, wearing Tomas's own handwriting like a stolen coat, had informed him that his life had been outlined a hundred years ago, that his purchase of a dull book for six coppers was a plot point, that his fear itself had been predicted with such precision that the prediction included the checking of the ink. He was not living a life. He was *adhering to a text*, and the text was already finished, and somewhere a century back a tired clerk had apologized to him in advance for the cost.

*I am sorry for it, truly, for I have seen what it costs them both.*

What cost? The entry never said. That omission was somehow worse than anything stated. He had spent three nights constructing theories and dismantling them: a hoax by the bookseller (impossible — the ink was old, the paper uncut and resewn, and no forger alive could reproduce Fen's hand that perfectly); a hallucination (he had shown the page, carefully worded as a palaeography question, to a colleague, who pronounced it "period-consistent throughout" and asked where he'd gotten the scan); a dream (the book was still there every morning, and so was the new line, and this morning there had been a smudge of candle soot on the corner of the page that he had put there himself).

Which left the theory he did not want. That the world had margins, and something wrote in them, and it had chosen him — not for his courage, clearly, nor his strength, but for his *thoroughness*. Because a careful man, once convinced, stayed convinced. Because he would do exactly what the page said: go to her, be gentle, take her out of the valley.

Because he would come to the bridge at the hour the lamps were lit, precisely as written, hating himself for how completely he obeyed.

The miller's bridge came into view, lamplit, ordinary, a girl fishing without bait off the near piling. His appointment — his *arranged meeting*, he corrected himself fiercely, people had those all the time, his aunt had arranged it, blind dates were a mundane human institution and not a fulfillment of prophecy — was with a woman named Freya Okafor, a bone-carver, recommended by his aunt with the breezy confidence of a woman who had never once considered that her matchmaking might be canonized in a century-old manuscript.

A bone-carver. He had thought nothing of it when his aunt first said it. Now the phrase rang against the final page like a struck bell. *She carves the dead into bone so she can hear them.*

He stopped at the foot of the bridge, took out the book — he had brought it, of course he had brought it, obedience again — and read the last line one more time in the failing light.

*You will hear nothing. Learn to be her ears' courage instead.*

"Tomas?" said a voice behind him, wary and low, a stranger's voice that the page had already introduced to him by name. "Freya. We haven't — I don't think we've met."

He turned. She was smaller than he'd imagined, sharp-eyed, with fine white dust still caught in the creases of her knuckles, and she was looking at him the way people look at a sound in an empty house.

"No," he said, and heard his own voice shake. "But I think we've been introduced." He held up the small gray-green book, open to the final page, to tonight's date. "A hundred years ago. By your name and mine."
