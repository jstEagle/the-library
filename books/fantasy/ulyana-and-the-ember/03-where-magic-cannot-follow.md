## 3. Where Magic Cannot Follow

The widow's name was Marta, and her husband had been dead eleven days when Freya finished carving his jawbone into a keepsake comb.

She worked by lamplight in the back room of her shop, the bone warm under her chisel even though it should have been cold. That was how it always began — the warmth first, then the whisper, rising out of the grain like water through cloth. The dead did not haunt their bodies. They haunted what was made of them. Freya had learned that at nine years old, when she carved a button from her grandmother's finger bone and heard her grandmother humming a lullaby through the whole of the following winter.

*Tell her,* said Ivo Marta's son, from inside the comb, in a voice like dry leaves settling. *Tell her the blue jar. Tell her I wasn't angry about the roof.*

"I'll tell her," Freya murmured, and blew bone dust from the fine teeth of the comb.

The whisper faded as the last cut closed, the way it always did — the carving sealing the voice inside itself like amber seals an insect. Finished work went quiet. Unfinished work spoke. It was why she never left a piece half-done overnight if she could help it, and why her shop at dusk was the only silence she had ever known that didn't frighten her.

Marta came at closing time, wringing her hands, and left weeping and holding the comb against her sternum like a second heart. *The blue jar. He wasn't angry about the roof.* Freya watched her go down the lane through the window, small and bowed and lighter somehow, and felt the old familiar ache that was the closest thing her gift ever gave her to pride.

Then she remembered the date, swore, and ran for her good shawl.

* * *

Tomas was already at the Hearthstone Inn when she arrived, breathless, twenty minutes late. He sat at the corner table with his long frame folded awkwardly onto a stool built for smaller men, and the prophecy book lay open on the table before him like a wound he couldn't stop looking at.

He'd shaved, she noticed. Someone had told him to shave. There was a smudge of ink on his left cuff and the particular hunted look of a man who has spent the day arguing with himself and losing.

"You came," he said, standing too fast. The stool scraped. Two farmers glanced over.

"You sound surprised."

"I've been surprised since Tuesday." He turned the book toward her with both hands, careful, the way you'd handle something venomous. "I wanted to be wrong about this. I've read it forty times. I brought it so you could tell me I'm wrong."

Freya sat. The page facing up was dense with that cramped antique script, but across the bottom third, in fresher ink — brown-black, slightly raised, as if written yesterday — ran lines she had already memorized against her will:

*On the night the valley's hearths burn twice, the carver shall hear the king. The historian carries the key. The Ember comes for its crown, and the crown sleeps beneath the mountain of the free.*

"The valley's hearths burn twice," she said quietly. "That's tonight. The harvest fires. Every year, same night, same fields." She looked up at him. "It's not prophecy, Tomas. It's a schedule."

"That's what terrifies me." He laughed, and there was no humor in it, only vertigo. "A week ago this book ended at page three hundred and twelve. Now it doesn't. And the handwriting—" He held up his right hand, flexed the fingers. "It's mine. Or it will be. The ink matches my hand better than my hand does."

Outside, down the valley, someone lit the first harvest fire. Orange light bloomed against the early dark, and a cheer drifted faintly up the road. Freya watched it and felt the hair rise on her arms.

"Second fire," Tomas whispered.

She hadn't seen anyone light it. Neither, she realized, had anyone else. But there it was, on the far ridge above the outlying farms — a second bloom of orange where no harvest fire had ever been set, burning wrong, burning *upward*, flames leaning against the wind like grass in a gale.

The screaming started before she understood what she was hearing.

* * *

It came from her satchel. She carried bones the way other women carried talismans — a knuckle of her grandmother's, a hawk's wishbone she'd carved with a spiral, three beads of sheep vertebra she used for counting stitches. All of them unfinished. All of them, until tonight, murmuring their small eternal murmurs: fragments of lullabies, complaints about the weather, the dead gossiping gently across the grain.

Now they screamed.

Not words at first — a raw harmonic shriek that made her clap both hands over her ears and still hear it, because it wasn't coming through her ears. It was coming through the marrow. Around the inn, dogs began to howl. A glass cracked behind the bar. Tomas was saying her name, her name, her name, and she couldn't answer because beneath the shriek the dead had found words, all of them at once, every voice she had ever carved speaking in terrible unison:

*RIDERS. RIDERS ON THE BORDER. TAKE THE HISTORIAN AND RUN.*

"Run," she gasped. "We have to run. Now."

"Run where? Freya, what is—"

She grabbed his wrist and hauled him out the door into the cold, and the valley showed her everything at once.

The second fire was not one fire. It was seven. The outlying farms — the Hendricks' stead, the wool barns, old Sef's orchard cottage — were burning along the northern border, and between the flames moved riders. Even at this distance she could see the sigil on their banners: a crown rendered in black thread, and rising from the crown's circlet, stitched in ember-orange, a flame. The banners didn't flutter. They *leaned*, the way the fire leaned, toward the valley, straining against an invisible wall.

And at the border itself — the old standing stones, the mossy line where the valley's quiet magic simply stopped — the lead rider had dismounted and was walking straight at the boundary stone. Walking confidently. Walking into nothing.

He struck the line and stopped as though he'd hit glass. Freya saw him stagger, press his palm flat against empty air that shimmered faintly, like heat over a forge. He tried again. Again. Then he threw back his head and laughed — she couldn't hear it at this distance, but she saw the shape of it, saw him turn and say something to his men, gesturing lazily at the burning farms.

*They can't cross,* said the dead in her satchel, quieter now, sadder. *So they burn what they can reach. They cannot have the valley. So they take everything around it.*

"They're firing the farms," she said aloud. Her voice sounded far away. "The border won't let them in, so they're — they're making an example. Burning everyone who lives close enough to be a temptation."

Tomas had gone the color of tallow. "The Ember," he said. "That's the sigil from the book. The parasitic flame. It wears the king's crown — Ulyana's crown — it's been ruling through him for a hundred years, and now it's here, at your valley, because—"

"Because of us." The words came out of her like teeth being pulled. "Because of the book. Because the prophecy names this valley, this night, and something on the other side of that border can *read*." She was shaking. "My grandmother is buried two fields from the Hendricks' stead. Old Sef taught me to carve. That's not a border, Tomas, that's a cage with us inside it, and they're burning everything they can't reach to smoke us out."

"Would it? Smoke you out?"

"No." She almost laughed. "The valley doesn't let magic in. That's the whole point. That's why I've never left. Whatever the Ember is, it can't follow us here. But it can wait. It can burn the world down to the stones and then wait, forever, for the moment we get hungry enough or brave enough or stupid enough to walk out."

Behind them, the inn door banged open. The innkeeper's wife stood in the light, face gray. "Riders on the north road," she called to the lane. "Everyone to the meeting hall — armed men at the border, the farms are lost, the Hendricks are—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. The whole valley would know by morning which of its children had died in the first fire, and whose fault the second one was.

Hers. However you counted it, hers. The book had come to *her* town. The prophecy named *her* hands. The riders had ridden for *her*, and the Hendricks' boy — sixteen, always whittling outside her shop, always asking her to teach him — the Hendricks' boy was ash now, or worse than ash, because she knew what the Ember's soldiers did with bones. She'd read the histories. They made trophies. They made *music*.

The dead in her satchel had gone silent again, all except her grandmother's knuckle, which pulsed once, warm, like a hand squeezing hers.

*Take the historian and run,* the dead had said. Not save the farms. Not warn the valley. Run, and take him.

"Why me?" Tomas asked. He was watching her face instead of the fires, and there was something in his expression beyond fear — a scholar's hunger, awful and bright. "The prophecy says I carry the key. The key to *what*, Freya? To freeing him? To freeing Ulyana?"

"I don't know."

"But you'll take me anyway."

She looked at him — this soft-spoken stranger with ink on his cuff and a century-old sentence wrapped around his life, this man she'd met four hours ago hoping for nothing more than pleasant conversation and maybe, if the gods were kind, a second date. The fires painted his face orange. Somewhere behind them, the valley's bells had begun to ring, calling everyone to the hall, and Freya knew that in an hour the elders would vote to seal the passes, keep everyone in, trust the border, trust the silence, trust the magic that had protected them for three hundred years.

Three hundred years of protection, and it had never once been tested by something patient.

"Yes," she said. "Tonight. Before the passes close. Because whatever that thing wants, it wants it badly enough to burn children for it, and I'd rather meet it on my feet than starve behind a wall." She tightened her grip on his wrist. "But understand me, Tomas. Out there, past the stones, I can't hear the dead. I've heard them every day of my life. Out there I'm deaf, and you're carrying a book that rewrites itself, and neither of us knows what we're walking into."

He swallowed. Nodded. "Together, then."

"Together," she said, and hated how much comfort she took from the word.

Above the northern ridge, the false fires burned higher, and the rider at the border stone had stopped trying to cross. Instead he had drawn something from his saddlebag — small, pale, long. He raised it to the light of his own flames, examining it the way a jeweler examines a stone.

Even at a distance, even in the dark, Freya knew bone when she saw it.

The dead in her satchel woke one final time, and this time they did not scream. They spoke in a chorus soft as snowfall, and every voice was afraid:

*He is holding one of ours. He knows exactly what you are, carver. Take the historian and run — run now, run tonight, and whatever he offers you at the border, whatever he shows you — do not answer him.*
