## 4. Crossing the Silence

The border was not a wall. Tomas had expected a wall — some palisade of blackthorn, a river that ran backward, at least a standing stone with warnings carved in a dead alphabet. Instead the valley simply ended, the way a sentence ends, and beyond it the world resumed.

He saw it first as a change in the light. The valley's dusk had always felt thick to him, lamplit and close, air like old paper. But twenty paces past the last fence post of the Okafor farm, the evening opened into something vast and blue and cold, and the stars came out so suddenly it felt like an ambush.

Freya stopped walking. She had the book under one arm and her carving kit slung across her back, and behind them, far down the valley road, the outlying farms were still burning — orange blooms against the dark, too distant now for heat, close enough for grief.

"Here," she said. Her voice was flat. "This is where it stops."

"Where what stops?"

"The magic." She said it the way you'd say *the rain* or *the cold* — a fact of weather, not a wonder. "It can't cross. Whatever the Ember sends, whatever it is, it dies at this line. That's why we're safe here. Were safe." She looked back at the fires. "They can't come in. So they burn everything they can reach from outside."

Tomas understood then why she'd made him walk through the night instead of waiting for dawn, why she'd taken only what fit in one bag, why she hadn't said goodbye to anyone. The valley wasn't a home. It was a lifeboat, and lifeboats are not meant to be comfortable, only to float.

"And if we cross," he said slowly, working it through, "we're outside the lifeboat."

"We're where the prophecy needs us to be." She turned the book over in her hands without opening it, as if it might bite. "You've read the rest of it?"

He had. Three times, by candlelight while she slept badly in the next room. The final page named him — *the historian, the key, the hand that turns* — in language that made his skin crawl precisely because it was so specific. Not *a scholar*. Not *one who reads*. His name. His mother's maiden name, which he'd never told anyone in the valley because no one had ever asked.

"I've read it," he said.

"Then you know there's no version where we stay." She started forward before he could answer, and he followed, because following Freya Okafor had apparently become the organizing principle of his life sometime in the last forty-eight hours without his consent.

The crossing itself was nothing. One step: valley. Next step: not-valley. He braced for a sensation, a shudder, the snap of some invisible membrane.

What he got instead was a flood.

It hit him between one heartbeat and the next — a pressure behind the eyes, a ringing in the teeth, the sudden certainty that the ground beneath him was not ground but the skin of something enormous and sleeping. He staggered. Freya caught his elbow without looking, steadied him as if she'd expected it.

"Breathe," she said. "First time out, everyone staggers."

"You knew this would happen?"

"I knew the valley keeps things out." She glanced at him, and in the starlight he saw something almost like apology. "I didn't know what it kept out of *you* specifically. Everyone feels it differently. My mother said it felt like drowning upward."

Tomas breathed. The flood did not recede; it settled, the way silt settles in water, finding levels. And then he became aware of a weight against his chest that had changed.

The book.

He pulled it from inside his coat. In the valley it had been inert — beautiful, ancient, maddeningly fixed, its final prophecy sitting on the page like a stone at the bottom of a well. Now the pages stirred against his fingers though there was no wind. And on the page after the prophecy — a page that had been blank an hour ago — new ink was blooming.

Not appearing. *Blooming*, the way frost blooms on glass, letters unfurling from nothing in a handwriting he recognized with a lurch of nausea because it was his own.

*They crossed at moonrise,* the fresh line read. *The bone-carver wept once, briefly, and denied it.*

Tomas looked up. Freya was walking ahead, face averted, jaw tight.

"This is happening now," he said. "It's writing down what we're doing. As we do it."

"Then stop reading and start walking."

"It's not just recording." He turned the pages faster, scanning ahead of the wet ink, and felt the vertigo open beneath him like a trapdoor. "Freya — there's more. It's writing *ahead*. Choices I haven't made yet. It says—" his voice caught— "it says I choose the eastern road when we reach the ford. And I haven't chosen anything. Have I? Have I said anything about a ford?"

She stopped. Turned. For a long moment she studied him the way she studied bone, he thought — looking for the grain, the place where it would split.

"No," she said quietly. "You haven't."

"Then how does it know?"

"Because it's not your handwriting." She took the book from him gently, the way you take a blade from a child. "It's wearing your handwriting. There's a difference. A century ago someone wrote down what you would do, and the book has been waiting for you to catch up to it." She closed it and handed it back, and her hand lingered on the cover a half-second longer than necessary. "The question isn't whether it knows. The question is whether knowing changes what you'd have done anyway."

"That's not comforting."

"I'm not a comforting person." But her mouth did something at the corner — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile lived — and she turned east, toward the ford, and Tomas realized with a cold, dizzy thrill that he had already decided to go east, had decided it somewhere below language, and the book had merely been honest enough to say so first.

They walked. Behind them the valley sank into dark, and the fires with it, and Tomas did not look back because he suspected if he did he would never stop looking back.

* * *

It happened an hour later, at the ford.

The eastern road bent down through alder trees to a shallow crossing, water loud and silver in the starlight, and halfway across the stones Freya stopped so abruptly that Tomas nearly went past her into the current.

She stood perfectly still. Water broke white around her ankles. Her hands hung at her sides, and they were shaking.

"Freya?"

Nothing. Not a flinch, not a blink. She stood like a woman listening to a sound so enormous it had erased all other sound — and then Tomas understood, with a horror that arrived whole, that it was the opposite. That she was hearing nothing at all. That the constant murmur he'd watched her live inside since the night they met — the tilt of her head toward her carvings, the way she answered questions no one had asked aloud, the patience of someone perpetually mid-conversation — all of it had stopped at the border, and she was standing in the ruins of her own silence.

"My mother," she said at last. Her voice came out strange, unmodulated, like an instrument played for the first time in years. "When she crossed, going the other way. Coming home. She said the voices came back all at once and she fell to her knees in the road." A breath. "I used to think that sounded awful. All of them at once. Forty years of the dead, talking."

"And now?"

"Now it's quiet." She laughed, and it was the worst sound Tomas had ever heard, because there was nothing underneath it. "Do you know how long it's been quiet? I was six years old when I carved my first piece. A bird, out of a goose bone. And my grandmother's grandmother spoke through it before I'd even set down the knife. I have never—" Her voice cracked and reformed. "There has never been silence. Not in sleep. Not underwater. Not once."

Tomas did the thing he hoped was right, which was to say nothing, and wade back to her, and stand beside her in the cold water with the alders rattling overhead. The book pressed against his ribs like a second heart.

"They're gone?" he asked finally. "Or just... far?"

"Gone. Or muffled. Or I'm deaf to them now." She stared downstream. "Everything I am, everything I know how to do — it worked because they talked to me. Grieving women brought me their husbands' bones and I gave them back their husbands' voices. Out here I'm just a woman with knives and a bag of animal parts."

"You're also the person the prophecy names by name."

"The prophecy can go drown itself."

Despite everything, Tomas smiled. "Noted."

She looked at him then — really looked, the first time since the farms began burning — and something passed between them in the starlight that neither of them would have been able to name. Recognition, maybe. Two people standing in each other's disaster. He had lost the authorship of his own life; she had lost the company of her dead; and the book in his coat had arranged both losses with the tidy precision of a century-old plan.

"We keep each other honest," she said. "That's what this is. You can't hear them either. So when I start trusting ghosts more than people, you tell me. And when that book starts telling you who you are—" her eyes dropped to his chest— "*I* tell you."

"All right," Tomas said.

"Say it properly."

"I can't hear them either. We keep each other honest." He held out his hand, absurdly formal, knee-deep in a river at midnight. "On my word as a historian, which I understand is worth very little."

She took it. Her grip was a carver's grip, strong and precise, and she held on perhaps a moment longer than a handshake required.

They crossed.

On the far bank the land climbed away from the valley in long folds of moorland, and somewhere beyond those folds, according to a book that wrote itself, waited a capital city ruled by a crowned thing made of ash. Freya shouldered her bag. Tomas checked the new pages one last time — and stopped.

The ink had moved again. Below the line about the ford, below the line about the weeping she'd denied, fresh letters were still cooling:

*At the city gate, the bone-carver will ask the historian what the book says of her death. He will lie to her. It will be the first kind thing he ever does, and the beginning of every trouble that follows.*

Tomas closed the book very carefully, the way you close a door on a room where someone is sleeping.

"Tomas?" Freya called from up the slope. "What does it say now?"

He stood in the dark with his heart hammering, holding a hundred years of somebody else's certainty, and discovered that lying was easier than he'd feared.

"Geography," he said. "Mostly geography."

And he climbed after her toward the capital, carrying the truth like a stone he intended, for as long as possible, never to put down.
