## 5. The Court of Cinders

The capital announced itself by smell before sight: woodsmoke and roses, burning and perfume braided together until you couldn't tell which was the sickness and which was the cure.

Freya stood at the crest of the last hill on the pilgrim road, Tomas a warm weight at her shoulder, and looked down at Vhalen's Rest sprawled in its bowl of hills like a coin at the bottom of a well. White walls. Copper roofs gone green with age. And above it all, rising from the palace at the city's heart, a column of pale smoke that did not disperse — it simply climbed, straight as a plumb line, into a sky that had no clouds anywhere near it.

"That's not a hearth fire," she said.

"No," Tomas said quietly. "That's the Ember."

She had spent eleven days beyond the valley's border now, eleven days in a silence so total it felt like drowning in reverse — water everywhere except her ears. In the valley she had never once been alone; the dead murmured through every carved thing she touched, a constant tide of other people's unfinished business. Out here there was nothing. Bone stayed bone. Wood stayed wood. She had caught herself three times pressing her thumb to a dead bird's hollow skeleton by the roadside, waiting for the whisper, feeling the silence answer like an empty room.

Tomas had become her hearing. He read the book aloud each night as it rewrote itself around their choices, its ink shifting like something alive under the vellum, and she had begun to think of his voice the way she'd once thought of the dead: as the thing that kept the dark from being total.

"Charm and ash," she said now, remembering what a refugee woman had told them two days back, huddled in a waystation with her children. "That's how she runs it. You'll love the king, or you'll burn for someone who does."

"The Ember-crowned," Tomas said. "In the histories I studied, before—" He stopped. He'd been doing that more often, stopping mid-sentence when the book in his satchel contradicted everything he'd spent his life learning. "The histories called the old king Ulyana the Just. They never mentioned he had a crown that ate him."

They walked down into the city.

* * *

Vhalen's Rest was beautiful, and that was the horror of it.

Freya had expected ruin, or at least fear — a city living under a parasite's crown should have had the look of a bruise. Instead the streets were swept. The fountains ran clean. Children chased a hoop down the Processional while their mothers sold honeyed pears from carts, and guards in ash-grey livery smiled at passersby, actually smiled, and directed lost pilgrims to the temple of whatever god they favored. Every corner held a brazier, and every brazier burned with that same pale, patient flame, and no one looked at the braziers longer than they had to.

"It's real," Tomas murmured, turning slowly in the great market square. "The prosperity. The order. Look — grain dole queues, but they're *short*. Courts that meet in public." He had his historian's face on, the one that lit up at primary sources, and Freya watched him catch himself wearing it and put it away like something shameful. "Anyone could rule through terror. This takes work."

"This takes a lie," Freya said.

But even as she said it, she wasn't sure. That was the thing she couldn't stop noticing: nothing here was a lie, exactly. The laws were good laws. A merchant who shorted his weights was fined double and made to stand in the square with the scales hung round his neck until he apologized to every customer he'd cheated — publicly, humbly, and then it was over, his name clean again. A noble who evicted a widow at midwinter found his own estate taxed to house her. Justice, tidy and visible and almost kind.

And threaded through all of it, small as a hairline crack in porcelain: the executions. Once a week, on Ashday, in the square beneath the palace walls. Always rebels, the criers said. Always traitors to the peace. And always, afterward, the pale column of smoke rising a little thicker for an hour, and the braziers burning a little brighter, and the whole city smelling faintly of roses.

"They're feeding it," Freya said. "The executions aren't punishment. They're *meals*."

"Then we need to know what happens to what's left over." Tomas glanced at her, and she saw him arrive at the same thought she did, the thought they'd been circling since the valley: *she needed bones.* Carved ones, whispered-through ones. Out here the dead were silent in everything — but maybe not silent in everything. Maybe silence was just what uncarved bone sounded like.

They went looking for a carver's row, because every city had one, and because the book — open in Tomas's hands as he walked, the way it seemed to want to be — had that morning grown a single new line at the bottom of its final page:

*The court remembers what the crown burns.*

* * *

Master Ilvet's shop smelled of tallow and chalk dust, and its proprietor was a small, quick-eyed woman with sawdust in her grey braid who took one look at Freya's hands — knife-scarred, steady — and said, "You carve. Valley work, that grip. We don't see valley folk past the border."

"We have business with the dead," Freya said, because lying to another carver was like lying to a mirror.

Ilvet's eyes flicked to the window, to the ash-grey guard walking his circuit across the square, and then back. "Business with the dead is legal here," she said. "That's the joke of this place. Mourning is protected. Grief has a charter. You can remember anyone you like, as loudly as you like — so long as the person you're remembering isn't on the list." She wiped her hands on her apron. "There's a list. There's always a list."

"What's on it?" Tomas asked.

"Names. Rebels, they say. Last Ashday they burned a chandler named Brecc Odo — young man, organized the dockworkers, refused to break a strike. Very lawful trial. Three days, public testimony, a magistrate who wept when she pronounced sentence. The whole city agreed it was fair." Ilvet's voice was flat as planed wood. "His mother came to me after. Wanted something carved to hold his memory. I told her mourning goods are licensed. His name is on the list. Carving his likeness carries a fine. Carving his *words* carries worse."

"And did you?" Freya asked.

Ilvet reached beneath her workbench and set a wrapped bundle on the counter, long and narrow, the weight and shape of it unmistakable to Freya's hands before the cloth was even drawn back.

"I'm old," Ilvet said. "Fines frighten me less than they used to. But my hands shake now, and the work deserves better than shaking hands." She unwrapped it.

A femur. Human, cleaned and pale, and Freya felt her breath go strange in her chest, because out here bone was supposed to be *silent*, bone was supposed to be only bone —

— and yet the moment her fingers closed around it, something moved in the grain. Not the tide she knew. Not the murmur of the valley dead, layered and constant. This was a single voice, thin and furious and fresh, like a man shouting up through floorboards.

"You can feel him," Ilvet said, watching her face. "I thought so. Valley gift. Listen, then. He's been talking to the bone since they burned him, and I am too old to be the only one hearing it."

* * *

She carved that night in the rented room above a dye-house, Tomas keeping watch at the shuttered window, the prophecy book open on the table between them like a third guest.

She worked the femur with the smallest blade in her kit, and Brecc Odo came up through the grain like a diver breaking water.

He was angry. Of course he was angry — he was twenty-six years old and the last thing he'd seen was ten thousand of his neighbors watching him die with sad, satisfied faces, agreeing it was fair. For the first hour he shouted. Freya let him. She'd learned long ago that the dead need to shout first, that grief arrives dressed as rage and won't take off its coat if you rush it.

Tomas sat across from her, transcribing, his hand cramping, his face grey in the lamplight. "He's describing the trial," he said hoarsely. "It really was fair. The evidence was real. The magistrate gave him every chance to recant. Freya, the *lawyers were good*—"

"I know," Freya said. "Keep writing."

Because that was the horror, and Brecc's ghost understood it better than any living person could: the Ember didn't corrupt the law. It *used* it. It wore justice the way it wore Ulyana's crown — snugly, patiently, from the inside. Every execution was lawful. Every rebel was guilty of something, because the Ember wrote laws that made resistance guilty, and then it fed, week after week, on the deaths its own fairness produced. A monster that hid in tyranny would be hunted down in a year. A monster that hid in *due process* could eat a kingdom forever, and the kingdom would thank it for the meals.

"He keeps saying the same phrase," Tomas said suddenly. "Over and over. 'Ask about the binding. Ask about the binding.' Freya — he knows something. About Ulyana?"

"Ask him," she said, though her hands already knew, already moving, the blade finding the shapes the voice wanted: a crown, a crypt, a circle of candles burned down to pools.

Brecc Odo's fury gentled into something colder and clearer, and he spoke through her carving in a rush, as if he had very little time and knew it — and maybe he did; ghosts out here felt thin to her, secondhand, like voices carried through a wall.

*They didn't kill the king,* the dead chandler said. *Everyone thinks the Ember murdered Ulyana and wears his corpse. Wrong. Wrong. You can't kill a true king — the land won't allow it, the crown won't allow it. So they did something older and worse. They bound him. There's a ritual — the Ember's priests keep it in the undercroft beneath the throne room, cut into the foundation stones themselves. Nine anchors. A living anchor at the center. The king isn't dead, carver. He's underneath his own body, pressed down under the flame like a man under ice, awake, every hour of every year, watching his own hands sign their laws.*

Freya's knife stopped.

"How do we undo it?" she whispered. "Tell me how to undo it."

*You don't,* Brecc said, and for the first time his voice cracked, and he sounded young, and afraid, and sorry. *That's the part they don't put in the songs. The unbinding needs an anchor of its own. Something living, willing, holding the circle open from inside while the king climbs out. The ritual doesn't end, carver. It transfers. Whatever holds the door —*

The voice frayed. The bone went cool in her hands, the grain settling into ordinary silence, and Freya sat staring at the half-finished crown in the femur's surface, her heart slamming.

At the table, Tomas looked up from his notes, pen dripping. "What did he say? What's the ritual? Can we—"

"The book," Freya said. Her voice came out wrong, scraped. "Tomas. Read me the last page. All of it. Every word, including the parts you've been skipping."

He went still. Outside, far off toward the palace, the pale column of smoke bent slightly, as if something enormous had turned over in its sleep.

"Freya," he said carefully, "the fine print is just — it's archaic formula, invocations, names of—"

"*Read it.*"

He drew the book toward him across the table, and the lamplight caught the ink, and Freya watched her friend's face as he read — watched the color leave it line by line, watched his lips form a word she couldn't hear from where she sat, a word she was suddenly, terribly certain she already knew.

It was his name.
