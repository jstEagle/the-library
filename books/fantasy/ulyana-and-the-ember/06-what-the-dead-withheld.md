## 6. What the Dead Withheld

Tomas had spent his whole life believing that books were honest. Not truthful — he knew better than that; truth was a country with disputed borders and every mapmaker drew it to flatter himself. But honest, at least: a book could not pretend it had not said the thing it said. Ink stayed put. That was the entire covenant of scholarship. You could argue with a book for forty years and lose, but you would never catch it changing its mind behind your back.

He caught the prophecy book changing its mind on the fourth night out of the valley.

They had made camp in the ruin of a waystation on the Salt Road, a low stone shelter whose roof had half fallen in and whose walls were furred with frost. Freya slept sitting up, as she always did now, her back against the wall and her bundle of carved bones wrapped in oilcloth against her chest like an infant. The fire was small. Outside, the dark went on for miles in every direction, and somewhere out in it, Tomas knew, the Ember's armies were moving toward the free cities like a tide that had been told about the shore.

He sat with the book open across his knees, reading the final prophecy again by firelight — the hundredth time, or near enough — because he had begun, lately, to feel like a man rereading a letter from a dead friend, searching it for something he might have missed.

The ink moved.

Not much. A single line beneath the passage describing the unbinding ritual, a line he was certain had not been there when they crossed the border, certain the way you are certain of your own name. It had simply arrived, the way the first prophecy had arrived overnight a week before everything began, written in ink that matched his own hand. He held the page close to the flame. He tilted it. He touched it with one finger and felt nothing but paper, cool and ordinary and lying.

The new line read:

*The unbinding requires an anchor of living flesh, who shall hold the door while the king passes through, and the anchor's price is all he carries of what he loves.*

Below that, in smaller script, cramped into the margin as if the book itself were embarrassed:

*The anchor is the historian.*

* * *

Tomas did not sleep that night. He sat by the dying fire and read the fine print of the last three pages the way a condemned man reads the terms of his pardon, looking for the loophole, the clause, the asterisk that would make it mean something else.

There was no loophole. There was never a loophole. That was the thing about prophecies that he should have understood from the beginning, as a historian: documents this old did not describe the future so much as they described a machine, and machines did not care whether you liked what they were built to do.

An anchor of living flesh. He turned the phrase over. The book was specific in the manner of legal contracts: the anchor holds the door while the king passes through, and the anchor's price is all he carries of what he loves. All he carried. He thought of the contents of his own heart laid out on a table for some cosmic customs officer to confiscate, item by item, until the tray came back empty. His mother's kitchen. His doctoral thesis, which had taken nine years and cost him a fiancée. The smell of the archive room where he had spent his thirties. And—

And Freya.

He looked at her sleeping against the wall, her face slack and younger in the firelight, her hands curled loosely around the oilcloth bundle. Four days ago she had walked out of the only place in the world where magic could not follow her, where the dead could not reach through her hands and speak, because a book with his handwriting in it had said her name. She had left her sanctuary for a stranger. She was walking toward a usurper's capital armed with bone knives and ghosts, for a king she'd never met, because the dead had asked it of her and she trusted them the way other people trusted gravity.

And the dead, Tomas realized, watching the fire eat down to coals, had known all along. They had known the price. They had sent their instructions through her carvings — *take the historian and run*, *the historian opens the way*, *the historian is the key* — and not once, not in any carving, in any whisper through the grain, had they mentioned that the key was also the kindling.

They had not lied. He understood that with a scholar's cold clarity. Lying was clumsy; omission was elegant. You simply declined to mention the part that would make the listener refuse, and you let love do the rest of the work. The dead had been guiding Freya since childhood. They knew precisely how much she would do for them, and how little she needed to be told.

He closed the book softly, so the sound wouldn't wake her, and found that his hands were shaking.

* * *

In the morning he almost told her.

It was the sort of morning that made telling seem easy — clear and cold, the road running white between frosted fields, her breath and his hanging together in the air like two arguments refusing to resolve. She walked beside him with her hood back, letting the light hit her face, and after a mile she said, without preamble, "You didn't sleep."

"I was reading."

"You're always reading." A pause. "You read differently when something's wrong. You hold the book closer, like it'll confess if you squeeze it."

He laughed, and it came out wrong, too thin, and he saw her notice that too. She noticed everything. It was the curse of a woman who listened to things that never spoke aloud — she heard the silences under sentences, the grain of a voice, the places where words had been sanded down to hide something. He watched her watch him, and he watched her decide not to press, and he understood that this, too, was a form of kindness he was about to repay with silence.

"I'm trying to work out the ritual," he said. "The mechanics of it. What the binding actually was."

"The ghost told me. The executed rebel." Her voice flattened slightly; she still hadn't washed the memory of that carven bone out of herself, and perhaps never would. "A crown of living flame set on a living king. The Ember eats outward from the mind. The unbinding tears it loose, but a tearing needs two hands — one inside, one out."

"One inside," Tomas repeated. "That would be the anchor."

Her step hitched — barely, a fraction of a stutter in her stride, but he had spent four days learning to read her body the way she read bone, and he caught it. She knew. The thought landed in him like a stone dropped down a well: she already knew, and she had decided, sometime in the night or the week or the moment the riders burned the farms, that he was not to know.

Or — no. He made himself consider it fairly, because fairness was the only dignity left available to him. Perhaps she didn't know yet. Perhaps the dead had withheld it even from her. Perhaps she was carrying some other weight entirely.

But he remembered the way she had gone quiet yesterday evening when the rebel-ghost's instructions came through her knife, whispering details she had relayed to him in careful summary, edited summary, summary with a shape to it. And he remembered that she had looked at him afterward the way people look at someone they are memorizing.

"Freya," he said. "What aren't they telling me?"

She kept walking. Three steps. Five. The frost crunched under her boots like tiny breaking bones.

"Nothing," she said. "They tell me everything they can."

*Everything they can.* Not *everything*. He heard it. She heard him hear it. Neither of them said anything else for a mile, and the silence between them changed species — it stopped being companionship and became a territory, claimed by both, patrolled by neither.

* * *

That night he dreamed of the archive.

In the dream it was the crypt beneath the palace instead — the same shelves, the same dry smell, but the ledgers were bones and the bones were labeled in his own handwriting, and Freya stood among them carving, and each curling shaving fell to the floor and stood up as a person he used to be. His mother. His advisor. The fiancée whose name he still couldn't say without a small internal flinch. They all looked at him with the patient, terrible tenderness of the dead and said, in unison: *you were always going to be useful. That's why we let you find the book.*

He woke with frost on his blanket and the book open on his chest, though he had no memory of taking it out. The fire had gone to ash. Freya was awake, watching him across the embers, and there was something in her face that he had never seen there before — a war being fought behind it, visible like fish under ice.

"Tomas," she said quietly. "When we get to the crypt. Whatever happens in there. I want you to know—" She stopped. Started again. "I want you to know I didn't choose this. Any of it. If I could carve a different ending into this story, I would. I swear it on every dead person I've ever loved."

Every word was true. That was the horror of it. He could hear the truth in her voice the way she heard lies in others' — total, ringing, unmistakable — and it changed nothing, because the sentence had a hole in its center exactly the size of a name. *I didn't choose this.* But she had chosen not to tell him. Somewhere in the last day she had learned the word *anchor*, and she had weighed it against whatever the dead had promised or demanded, and she had chosen their silence over his consent.

Loyalty to the dead versus truth to the living. He had written those words once, years ago, in a footnote about some forgotten schism — *the perennial tragedy of faith communities: loyalty to the absent outweighing truth to the present* — and had felt very clever, very safe, very academic. Now the footnote was standing over him wearing Freya's face, and it was not clever at all.

"I know," he said. And then, because he was a historian and historians are cowardly curators of other people's catastrophes, he added: "Whatever happens, we see it through together."

She exhaled, and something in her shoulders let go, and she believed him. He could tell she believed him, and he hated how easily the lie had come, how naturally his mouth produced comfort the way the book produced ink — without his permission, on someone else's schedule.

* * *

On the sixth day the capital rose out of the plain ahead of them, white-walled and black-crowned, and the roads filled with pilgrims and soldiers and merchants hauling wagons of ash-gray ore. At the gate, guards checked papers and faces. In the queue, pressed against Tomas's side, Freya's hand found the bundle of bones and gripped it, and he saw her lips move — receiving, listening, translating.

"What are they saying?" he asked.

Her eyes came up. For a moment he thought she would finally break, would spill the whole architecture of her omissions here in the shadow of the gate with the Ember's sigil glaring above them. Her mouth opened.

"They say the crypt is open," she said instead. "They say the king will receive us tonight."

And below her words, so quiet that Tomas almost missed it — so quiet that maybe he imagined it, maybe it was only the wind off the salt flats — he heard her murmur, half to herself, in the tone of a woman making a bargain she already regretted:

"Not yet. Let him have a few more days happy. Please."

The gates opened. They walked in.
