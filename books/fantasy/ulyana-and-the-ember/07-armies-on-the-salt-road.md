## 7. Armies on the Salt Road

The dust came first.

Freya felt it before she saw it — a tremor in the ground that traveled up through her boots and into the bones she carried in her satchel, so that every carved keepsake in her possession rattled like teeth. She stood on the ridge above the Salt Road with a spyglass she didn't need. The column was visible from horizon to horizon: ten thousand soldiers in ash-grey livery, supply wagons, siege engines broken down into their component parts, and at the head of it all, banners bearing the sigil of the Ember — a crown wreathed in flame, stitched in thread that seemed to smolder even in daylight.

"They'll reach Vellmark in six days," said Sera Doss, the rebel commander, squinting against the wind. She was a broad woman with a burn scar down one side of her jaw, and she spoke of armies the way other people spoke of weather — flatly, without wonder, because wonder was a luxury commanders couldn't afford. "Seven if the rains slow them. The free cities have maybe four thousand fighting men between them, and half of those are fishermen with pitchforks."

Freya lowered the spyglass. "And you want me to wait."

"I want you to *win*." Sera turned to face her. Up close, the burn scar was older than Freya had thought — childhood, maybe, some kitchen accident or worse. "The ritual takes what it takes. You told us yourself. The crypt will be sealed for however long the unbinding needs, and you'll be inside it, and Tomas will be inside it, and if the Ember's army reaches the capital while you're down there—"

"Then Ulyana will be free to command an army that no longer exists."

"Then everyone dies," Sera said simply. "The cities burn. The rebellion ends. And a freed king stands alone in a crypt under a palace full of corpses, holding a crown nobody's alive to kneel to." She let that sit in the wind between them. "Delay the ritual. Help us break this army first. Then unbind him."

* * *

That night, in the rebel camp below the ridge, Freya took out her bones.

She had learned, in the weeks since the capital, to listen differently outside the valley. In the valley, the dead had spoken through her carvings as clearly as living voices. Out here, past the border where magic flooded back into the world, the dead were everywhere — thick in the air like smoke — and they needed less invitation. The bone was just a door she happened to be holding.

Tonight's bone had come from a soldier executed three years ago for refusing to fire on a grain riot. His name had been Aldric Vane. His ghost was patient, dry-humored, and dead — in every sense — certain about military matters.

"Ten thousand," Aldric said through the grain of the femur in her palm. "But look at the road. Look at the wagons. They're carrying their own grain, girl. That means they don't trust the countryside to feed them, which means the countryside hasn't surrendered, which means—" he paused, savoring it, a man who'd been starved of good news for three years, "—they're overextended. An army that hauls its own bread is an army afraid of ambush."

"The rebels want me to delay the ritual."

"And I want you to delay it too." The voice grew quieter, and she felt the familiar pressure behind her eyes that meant the dead were gathering — more than one now, crowding toward the door. "Every day you wait is another day the Ember roots deeper into that poor man's spine. We've told you this. But hear the arithmetic, carver. If you free Ulyana tomorrow and the army burns the cities next week, you have traded ten thousand living souls for one dead king. And the Ember wins anyway. It doesn't need the crown if there's nothing left beneath it."

Freya set the bone down on the camp table. Her hands were steady. They always were; it was her insides that shook.

"And if I fight first," she said to the empty tent, "and the ritual comes too late?"

No one answered. The dead, for once, had nothing to say.

* * *

Tomas found her at the fire an hour later. He looked better than he had in the capital — the road had put color in his face, and the book rode in his pack like a sleeping animal, quiet since the last rewriting. He sat down across from her without asking, which was new. A month ago he would have asked permission to exist near her.

"Sera's called a war council at dawn," he said. "She wants you there."

"I know."

"She also wants the ritual moved to after the battle. She told me first." He said it carefully, watching Freya's face. "Before she told you. Because she thought I might talk you around to it."

"Would you?"

Tomas fed a stick into the fire. For a long moment the only sound was the crackle and the distant noise of the camp — men sharpening things, horses shifting, someone singing low and badly.

"I read the fine print again," he said finally. "All of it. There's a passage about timing — the binding strengthens with each moon. The Ember isn't just wearing Ulyana, Freya. It's *growing into* him. Like a tree growing around a fence post. Wait long enough and there won't be anything left to pull free. The post becomes the tree." He looked up. "So no. I wouldn't talk you into waiting. But I'm not the one who has to choose, am I? The prophecy named me the key. It didn't name me the hand that turns it."

"You could refuse," Freya said. "You could tell Sera you agree with her. Make it two voices against mine."

"Do you want me to?"

"No."

"Then I won't." He smiled, small and tired. "Besides. I did the math too. Ten thousand lives against one king. It's not close, is it? Any reasonable person waits." He poked the fire again. "Which is exactly why the Ember wrote its laws the way it did. Everything reasonable. Every execution justified. Justice inside the law, serving monstrous ends. You taught me that phrase, standing in that square in the capital." He met her eyes across the flames. "Reasonable is how monsters win, Freya. Sometimes the unreasonable thing is the true one."

* * *

The war council convened at dawn in Sera's command tent, around a table map weighted with knives and river stones. Freya stood at the edge of it, listening to men argue terrain she'd known for five days and they'd known for fifty years.

Sera laid it out plainly. The Salt Road narrowed at a place called the Kessing Narrows, where the road ran between a salt marsh and a limestone bluff. Four thousand rebels could hold that gap against ten thousand — for a while. Long enough, maybe, to bleed the column, scatter the wagons, force the Ember's generals to winter short of the free cities. But "for a while" was doing heavy work in that sentence, and everyone in the tent knew it.

"We need something the enemy can't plan for," Sera said, and then, reluctantly, like a woman paying out her last coin: "We need her."

Every face turned to Freya.

"The dead know things," Sera said. "Where the officers sleep. Where the grain is stored. Which captains are loyal and which are bought. You could walk our scouts through that army's skeleton, carver. One night of that, and we don't hold the narrows — we *break* them there."

"It would take days," Freya said. "Carving enough bones, finding enough dead who died near enough to matter, cross-checking what they tell me against what's true. The dead lie sometimes. Not often, but—"

"Days we might not have."

"And every one of those days," Freya said, and her voice came out harder than she intended, "the Ember digs deeper into the only man who can end this permanently. You're asking me to trade his soul for your battle."

"I'm asking you to trade one soul for forty thousand," Sera shot back. "Because that's what's in those cities. Fishermen with pitchforks, carver. Children. Old women who remember the last time the Ember-crowned came east." She planted both fists on the table. "I buried my brother after the Vellmark riots. I know exactly what that thing does when it reaches a town. So yes. I'm asking. And I'll keep asking until you say yes, because I am *right*, and being right is all I've got."

The tent went silent. Somewhere outside, a horse screamed briefly and was calmed.

Freya looked down at the map. The Kessing Narrows. The little knife marking the rebel line. The river stones marking everything that would drown if the line broke.

*One soul for forty thousand.* Aldric's arithmetic. Sera's arithmetic. Even Tomas's, offered gently by firelight. Everyone could count except her, apparently — or everyone could count except the dead, who kept whispering *hurry, hurry, hurry* in her ear every night, and whom she had promised, in the valley, in another life, to trust.

Loyalty to the dead. Truth to the living. She was so tired of the scale.

"Three nights," she said.

Sera blinked. "What?"

"Three nights of work with your scouts. Three nights, and I'll give you the army's skeleton — officers, stores, weak captains, all of it. Enough to break them at the narrows or die trying." She held up a finger. "But the ritual happens the morning after the battle. Win or lose. If you lose, I go into the crypt anyway, because a freed king is worth more than a lost cause. And if you win—" her voice caught, and she hated it, and pushed through, "—if you win, you'll have earned it. But I don't wait for the next moon. I don't wait for reinforcements. The morning after, Tomas and I go underground, and whatever comes out of that crypt comes out, and the kingdom gets to live with it."

Sera studied her for a long moment. Around the table, the other commanders exchanged glances — relief and doubt in equal measure.

"Four nights," Sera said. "The battle plan needs four."

"Three."

"Three and a half."

"*Three,*" said a voice from the tent flap, "or nothing at all."

They all turned. Tomas stood there, the prophecy book open in his hands, and Freya's stomach dropped, because she knew that posture — the slight sway, the unfocused eyes — the book was reading *him*.

"What is it?" she asked. "What does it say?"

Tomas looked up at her, and his face was pale as salt.

"It's rewritten itself again," he said. "There's a new passage. About the narrows." He swallowed. "Freya — it says the battle is already lost unless the ritual begins *first*. Tonight. Before the army arrives. It says—" his voice cracked, and he steadied himself against the tent pole, "—it says the Ember knows where we are. It has always known. And it is marching here faster than anyone believes."
