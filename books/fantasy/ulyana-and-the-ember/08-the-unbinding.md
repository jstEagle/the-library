## 8. The Unbinding

The crypt beneath the palace smelled of salt and old iron, and Tomas thought, absurdly, of the valley — of Freya's workshop with its bone-dust and its lamps, where he had first shown her a book that knew his handwriting better than he did. That had been forty-one days ago. He had counted. He suspected she had too.

Below the Ember-crowned throne room, below the halls of ash and charm, the old king's dead lay in rows of stone. Ulyana's own tomb stood at the center of the crypt: not a sarcophagus but a bed, carved from a single block of pale rock, and on it lay a man who had been dying for three hundred years without ever being permitted to finish.

Tomas had seen him twice before, from a distance, under rebel guard. Up close, Ulyana was worse. The Ember showed through him like fire through paper — veins of orange light crawling beneath the skin of his throat, his wrists, the hollows of his eyes. His chest rose and fell with terrible patience. He was beautiful the way a burning house is beautiful, and Tomas had to look away.

"Anchor positions," Freya said. Her voice was flat, workmanlike, the voice she used when the dead were talking through her and she didn't want them overheard.

Around the tomb, six rebels knelt at chalk marks Sable had drawn — the ghost of the executed carver, speaking through a sliver of his own shinbone that Freya carried against her heart. The rebels were frightened. They were also out of options. The Ember's vanguard would reach the palace by dawn.

Tomas took his place at the head of the tomb, because the book said he must. It lay open on a stand beside him, and it had stopped pretending to be an antique. The pages moved now, faintly, like something breathing. The final prophecy filled the last leaf in ink that was still drying.

*The key shall kneel at the king's head. The key shall hold the flame's name in his mouth. What the key gives, the flame will take — not blood, not breath, but all he carries of the one who frees him.*

He had read it forty times. He had read it aloud to himself in the dark of his tent until the words lost meaning and became only shapes. He had decided, each time, to tell her.

Each time, he hadn't.

Because she would stop. Because she would find another way, or refuse this one, or turn the ritual on herself, or let the Ember march into the free cities rather than pay this price — and the price was not his life. He could have given his life gladly. He had rehearsed that sacrifice on the road, polished it like a stone, offered it up silently every night as if some god of historians might accept the substitution. But the book was specific. The book was always specific.

*All he carries of the one who frees him.*

Every memory. Every moment. The blind date in the valley where magic couldn't follow, where he'd arrived clutching a book open to tonight's date like a madman, and she'd looked at him with wary tenderness and made tea anyway. The crossing of the border, when silence had struck her deaf and he had taken her hand and she had let him. The night in the rebel camp when she'd laughed — really laughed — at his impression of the Ember's courtly drawl, and he had understood, with the vertigo of a man falling, that he loved her.

The flame would take all of it. And Tomas would wake tomorrow knowing only that a bone-carver had saved a king, that he had served as anchor, that duty was done. A stranger to her. A stranger to himself, in the one way that mattered.

"Tomas." Freya stood across the tomb from him, her carving knife in hand, a length of antler bone in the other — the last piece of the executed rebel, the piece she'd saved. "Say it back to me. All of it."

"The flame wears his crown," he recited. "The flame is not in the crown; the crown is in the flame. To tear one from the other, the binding must be given somewhere to go. I am the somewhere."

"And?"

"And I speak the flame's name, and you cut the name out of him, and—" he faltered.

"*And*," she said.

"And what I carry of you goes with it."

Her eyes held his for a long moment. In the candlelight they were dark and steady and full of everything he was about to lose, and he understood suddenly that she had known. Had known for days, maybe — since the passage appeared, since the night she'd asked him *what does it say* and he'd lied with his whole face. She was a woman who heard truth out of dead bones. Of course she'd heard the lie in living ones.

"You should have told me," she said quietly. Not angry. Worse than angry. Grieving.

"I know."

"I would have found another way."

"There is no other way. There's never been another way. There was only ever tonight, and you, and me, and this." He gestured at the burning man between them. "You taught me that the dead don't lie, Freya. They just withhold. I learned from the best."

Something broke in her face and reassembled itself into resolve, which was the most Freya thing he had ever seen. "When it's done," she said, "you won't remember any of this conversation."

"No."

"So I'm telling you now, while you can still carry it." She came around the tomb and took his hands, and her grip was a carver's grip, strong and certain. "You chose this. Nobody made you. You read the fine print of a century-old prophecy and you stayed anyway, and that is the bravest thing I have ever witnessed, and I have heard the last words of ten thousand dead. Whatever you feel tomorrow when you look at me — whatever hollow thing — I want you to remember that I will remember enough for both of us. That's how it works. Someone has to keep the record."

"That's very historian of you," he managed.

"Shut up, Tomas."

And then she kissed him, brief and fierce, like sealing a letter, and went back to her place at the foot of the tomb.

* * *

The ritual began with names.

Freya spoke the rebel's name first — Sable Vane, carver, traitor by law, honest man by every measure that mattered — and the antler bone in her hand began to glow like a coal. Then she spoke Ulyana's name, three hundred years of it, the coronation name and the childhood name and the secret name his mother had given him, which the ghosts had kept like a pressed flower all this time. The light beneath the king's skin surged. He arched off the stone bed, mouth opening on a sound that was not a scream but a word — the same word, Tomas realized, that was written on the final page.

*Vhal-eth-karuin.* The Ember's true name. Older than the kingdom. Hungry as winter.

"Now," Freya said. "Tomas. Now."

Tomas knelt at the king's head and put his palms flat against the pale rock, and he said the name.

It did not feel like speaking. It felt like swallowing the sun. The syllables tore up through him from somewhere below memory, and the candles guttered, and the chalk circle blazed white, and the book on its stand turned its own pages shrieking toward the end. Heat poured out of the king's body and into the circle and *looked* for a door, and the door was Tomas — the anchor, the vessel, the somewhere.

He had expected pain. There was none. There was only loss, beginning gently, at the edges.

The smell of bone dust. Gone. The sound of Freya saying *shut up, Tomas*. Going. Her laugh in the tent — he reached for it and felt it slide through his fingers like water through a net, and he panicked then, truly panicked, scrabbling through his own mind for anything, the tea she'd made, the hand she'd let him take at the border—

*Hold,* said a voice that was not his own and was not the flame's. It sounded like many voices. It sounded, impossibly, like the dead of the valley, the ones whose whispers lived in her carvings, and he understood that they had come with her, that they had always been coming with her, that they were holding the doors of him open so the fire would pass through clean.

*We will keep what we can,* the chorus said. *We are sorry. We can keep so little.*

The flame poured through him like a river through a broken dam, taking its toll as it went, and Tomas knelt in the flood and felt himself become a stranger in real time. He watched his love for Freya Okafor leave him the way color leaves a face in frost — gradually, then all at once — and the last thing he thought, before the thought itself burned away, was:

*Someone has to keep the record.*

* * *

Then it was over, and there was a silence in the crypt that tasted like snow.

Ulyana lay still on the stone bed, and the fire was gone from him. His veins were quiet. His breathing was just breathing. Slowly, wincing like a man waking from a century's sleep, he opened his eyes — grey eyes, human eyes, empty of everything except the vast bewilderment of the returned — and looked at the ceiling of his own tomb, and wept without sound.

At the head of the tomb, Tomas swayed on his knees. He felt fine. He felt light. He felt like a house after the furniture has been carried out — all the rooms still standing, all the rooms empty.

He looked down at the woman kneeling at the foot of the tomb, the bone-carver, her knife still raised, her face wet and blazing with triumph.

She was looking at him the way people look at graves.

"Do I know you?" he asked, and heard his own voice with mild surprise, as if it belonged to a colleague. "I'm sorry — you're the carver. Freya. You freed him. It worked?" He looked around at the shattered chalk, the smoking book, the weeping king. "It worked."

"Yes," Freya said. Her voice did not break. He noticed that, distantly, professionally, the way he noticed everything now — catalogued, filed, unattached. "It worked."

"Then why are you crying?"

She smiled at him, and it was the worst thing he had ever seen, though he did not know why, though he would never know why, though some door in him stood open onto darkness and wind blew through it forever.

"Get some sleep, historian," she said. "Tomorrow we break a siege."

Above them, muffled by thirty feet of stone, the first horns of the Ember's army sounded against the walls of the city.
