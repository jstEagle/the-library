## 9. The Ember's Last Argument

The crypt was still smoking.

Freya knelt where Tomas had fallen, her hands pressed flat against the cold stone, as if she could hold him to the world by touch alone. He breathed. That was the miracle the ritual had left them — he breathed, his heart beat, his skin warm under her fingers when she checked. But when she'd said his name, he had looked at her with the polite blankness of a stranger at a funeral, and asked, gently, *Do I know you?*

Ulyana stood apart, swaying on legs that hadn't borne his weight in a hundred years, the torn stump of his crown still smoking in the brazier where Freya had hurled it. The Ember was not in it anymore. She could feel that much even without her gift — a wrongness in the air, a pressure like the moment before lightning.

*It's still here,* said a voice inside her skull, and for one wild heartbeat she thought her hearing had come back, that the dead had returned to her, that the price had been refunded.

Then she understood whose voice it was.

"You should be dead," she said aloud, to the empty air, to the smoke, to the dark.

*Dead is such a small word,* the Ember answered. It did not speak in sound. It spoke directly into the place where her gift used to live, filling the silence the ritual had carved out of her, and Freya realized with a lurch of horror that this was why it had waited — because her deafness had made a room, and it had moved in. *I have worn a hundred crowns before this one. I have been burned, drowned, buried under altars, sealed in lead. I am patient the way stone is patient.*

In the brazier, the blackened crown shifted. Not burning. *Breathing.*

Freya got to her feet. Her knees were unsteady; the ritual had taken more than her hearing. It had taken something deeper, some floorboard of her self, and she was still learning to stand on what remained. Behind her, Ulyana spoke — his voice rusty, cracked from disuse, but steady.

"Do not listen to it, bone-carver."

*I am not speaking to you, king.* The Ember's attention slid over Ulyana like oil over water, unable to find purchase. *You are an empty house now. I am speaking to the one who can hear.*

"I can't hear anything," Freya said. "That was the price. You know that. You felt it leave me."

*Did it?* Something almost gentle entered the voice. *Or did your little ghosts simply go quiet, and you assumed the worst? I was there, Freya Okafor. I have been in every fire this kingdom has ever lit. I heard the dead whisper through your carvings long before you knew they whispered. And I tell you: nothing was taken from you that cannot be returned.*

The brazier flared. In its heart, images bloomed in the flame — and Freya's breath caught, because she saw herself, younger, in her valley workshop, a sparrow's wing-bone between her fingers, and she could *hear*, in the memory, the sparrow's small ghost chirring about the warmth of the nest it had built and lost. She heard her mother's voice. She heard the widow's husband from that first night, the one who had told her *tell Marta the blue coat, the one with the brass buttons, tell her I never minded the cold.*

"Stop," she whispered.

*Hear me out,* the Ember said, and laughed at its own joke, a dry crackle like paper catching. *Here is my argument. The historian lies three steps behind you, alive, whole, and hollow. He does not remember your name. He will not remember it tomorrow, or in ten years, or on his deathbed, unless someone gives it back to him. I can do that. I held his memory while the ritual tore it loose — I hold it still, curled up inside me like a coal in ash. Give me a crown, and I give him back to you. Every evening in the tavern, every argument about the dating of manuscripts, every hour he spent reading to you on the Salt Road. All of it. Word for word.*

The flames shifted. Now she saw Tomas — laughing, arguing, asleep with a book open on his chest — and beneath the images, faint as a watermark, she heard his voice saying her name the way he said it, *FREY-a*, with the soft emphasis that had made her stomach flip the first time.

"And the other thing?" Her mouth was dry. "You mentioned my hearing."

*I restore what I take. It is the only law I keep.* The flame dimmed, almost modest. *Your gift returns. The dead return. Every carving you make will sing again. You would be whole, Freya. Both halves. The woman who lives among the living AND the woman who speaks for the dead. No one has ever offered you that. Your ghosts certainly never did — they took and took and gave you riddles in return, didn't they? They hid the anchor clause. They let you walk into that crypt believing the price was only yours to pay.*

"That wasn't them," she said. "That was the prophecy. That was the book."

*The book your ghosts chose. The path your ghosts set you walking.* The voice softened further, became almost a lover's murmur. *They lied by omission because they needed you. I lie by omission too — everyone does — but I am offering you the truth upfront, written in fire: two prices, one payment, and it isn't even yours. A crown. Just a crown. Put it on, and the man who loves you remembers loving you. Put it on, and you hear your mother's voice again. What is a headpiece, Freya, weighed against a soul?*

Silence. Real silence, the kind she was going to have to live inside for the rest of her life.

She looked back at Tomas. He had propped himself against the crypt wall, watching her with careful, courteous eyes — the eyes of a man being polite to a stranger who was clearly distressed. There was a smear of soot on his cheek. His hands, the hands that had turned a century-old book's pages with such reverence, were folded in his lap like a student's.

"Is he—" Tomas began, nodding toward the brazier. "Should we be worried about the fire?"

And that — that small, brave, oblivious question — nearly broke her.

Because here was the arithmetic the Ember had laid out, and it was beautiful, and it was monstrous. Wear the crown: Tomas remembered, the dead sang, and the Ember rode her body out into the world wearing her face, her hands, her gift weaponized at last. Refuse: the Ember stayed loose in the world, hungry, and Tomas lived out his days beside a woman he did not know, and she lived hers in silence.

*Choose quickly,* the Ember murmured. *Even now my children ride for the free cities. Even now the siege lines close around Vellmark. A crowned bearer could stop the war by morning. An uncrowned woman with no gift can only watch.*

"She's choosing," Ulyana said. He had crossed the crypt to stand beside her, this king who had been a prisoner in his own body for a hundred years, and his voice was quiet and iron. "Bone-carver. May I tell you what I learned, wearing that thing for a century?"

Freya nodded.

"It argues beautifully," Ulyana said. "That is all it is. An argument, wearing a flame. And every bargain it ever offered me was true — every single one. It restored what it took. It kept its law. And it ate me anyway, year by year, because the crown is not the price. The crown is the *mouth*. Everything it gives you back, it gives back inside itself. Your dead would sing again — inside the Ember. His memories would return — inside the Ember. You would be whole the way a fly is whole inside amber." He smiled, and it was a terrible, tired, magnificent thing. "It is not lying to you. That is the horror of it. It is telling you the truth about a door that only opens inward."

The brazier flared, furious. *Liar—*

"Yes," Ulyana agreed pleasantly. "Me. I've had a hundred years to practice."

But Freya was barely listening, because something else was happening — something in the walls, in the floor, in the old executed rebel's bone still tucked against her heart where she'd carried it since the Court of Cinders. A vibration. A pressure. Like a thousand voices drawing breath at once.

She couldn't hear them. Her gift was gone; the silence was total, permanent, hers forever.

But she could *feel* them. Through the bone against her sternum, through the stone under her feet, through the marrow of the world itself — the dead of the crypt, the dead of the palace, the dead of every farm the riders had burned, the widow's husband, the sparrow, the rebel with his gentle ghost-voice, all of them, all of them, pressing in against the membrane of her silence like light against a closed eyelid.

Not speaking to her.

*Charging.*

"They're not gone," Freya said slowly, understanding arriving like dawn. "They were never gone. They're just... waiting for somewhere to go."

*Don't,* said the Ember, and for the first time its voice cracked, lost its velvet. *Bone-carver. Freya. Think about what you're refusing—*

"I thought about it," she said. "For exactly as long as it deserved."

She pulled the rebel's bone from her shirt. She held it in both hands, the way she'd held a thousand bones, and though she could not hear a single voice, she knew — the way a conductor knows without hearing each violin — that every dead thing within reach of her grief was gathered behind it, a choir holding its final note.

"You want a crown," she said to the Ember. "Fine. Here's mine: I choose truth over loyalty. I choose the living over the lovely lie. I choose a silent life with a man who doesn't know me over a singing life with a parasite in my skull. And I choose —" her voice broke, and she let it, and kept going anyway — "I choose to spend every ghost I ever loved on making sure you never argue with anyone again."

She snapped the bone in half.

The chorus came through her like a river through a broken dam — not sound, not anymore, but pure pressure, a hundred years of the unheard and unsaid pouring through the one open channel left in her: her own refusal. The dead had lied to her by omission, yes. They had hidden the anchor clause. They had used her.

And they had also, every one of them, chosen to spend their last existence on this — on her — and she forgave them in the same instant the forgiveness stopped mattering.

The light in the crypt went white.

There was no scream, or if there was, it was made of every voice at once and therefore belonged to no one. The brazier shattered. The crown in it came apart like a dandelion in a gale, and the Ember — the argument, the flame, the patient hungry ancient thing — came apart with it, thread by golden thread, unwoven by a chorus of sparrows and farmers and rebels and kings' forgotten dead, until there was nothing left of it but a smell like snuffed candles and a fine grey ash that settled, gently, over everything, like snow.

Like mercy.

When Freya could see again, Ulyana stood in the ruin of the brazier, bareheaded, blinking, a living man in a dead king's crypt. He picked up a shard of the broken crown, considered it, and dropped it.

"The siege," he said. "How long has Vellmark been surrounded?"

"Nine days," Freya said. "Maybe ten."

"Then there's no time to raise an army." He was already moving toward the stairs, ash falling from his shoulders like he was shedding a century. "But there's time for a king to arrive. Armies break when the thing they're fighting for turns out to be alive. Get me a horse, bone-carver, and get yourself one, and fetch the historian — he'll want to see this. He writes things down."

Freya turned last to Tomas. He was standing now, brushing off his coat, looking at her with that courteous stranger's face, and she felt the old grief rise like water.

"You don't know me," she said. It wasn't a question.

"No," he admitted. "But you clearly know me, and you look like someone who could use a friend on a long ride." He hesitated, then offered his hand, formal and absurd and unbearably kind. "Tomas. Historian. Apparently I'm important to prophecy — there's a book about it, I'm told. Perhaps you could tell me the story on the road? I find I remember things better when they're told to me."

She took his hand. His grip was warm.

"Perhaps I will," said Freya Okafor, and followed her king up into the light, carrying her silence like a sword she'd finally learned to hold.

* * *

They rode east through the ash-fall, three riders and a story, toward the fires of Vellmark.
