## 1. Salt Roads

There are three ways to disappear in the Thornfells, and I've spent my whole life learning all of them.

The first is altitude. Get high enough, into the scree and the cloud, where even the eagles can't be bothered to look, and you stop existing to anyone below. The second is boredom. Tollmen, inquisitors, tax assessors—men whose whole trade is noticing things—will look straight through a salt caravan on its ninth day of the same road. Salt is heavy, salt is cheap, salt is dull. Nobody dreams about salt.

The third way is me.

I was born with a face like a closed door and a walk that makes eyes slide off me the way rain slides off slate. It's not magic. I want to be clear about that, because later—well. Later matters. But at the time, I believed it was just a knack, the way some men can whistle through their teeth or guess a mule's weight by looking. Baba Sef called it my *smallness*. "You're not invisible, boy," she told me when I was twelve and sulking about it. "You're *economical*. You take up exactly as much world as you need and not one grain more." She said it like a compliment. From her, it was.

So there I was, three days out of the salt pans at Vessik with eleven wagons, nineteen mules, four hired drovers who thought I was a toll-clerk's cousin, and myself—which is to say, nobody in particular, a thin man in a gray coat driving the rear wagon and being forgotten by everyone including, occasionally, himself.

The road out of Vessik climbs through the Kettle Gorge, then switchbacks up the shoulder of Mount Halloway before dropping toward the free towns on the far side. The empire holds the low country with garrisons and the high country with paperwork. Every traveler through Thornfell proper carries a transit seal stamped with the imperial double-eagle, and every seal costs money, and every coin goes back to a capital none of us will ever see. The guild's whole business model rests on the fact that the empire counts roads but not paths, and that a man carrying a seal-stamped manifest is never asked what else might be riding under the salt.

That morning we were carrying, under nine hundred pounds of rock salt, forty bolts of Vessiki dye-cloth, two crates of unlicensed surgical steel, and a letter for Baba Sef that I hadn't read and very much didn't want to.

"You're doing the face again," said Dren, the eldest of the drovers, falling into step beside my wagon. He was sixty if he was a day, built like a root vegetable, and had been smuggling since before the empire drew its pretty new borders. He was also, in his own estimation, hilarious.

"I don't have a face."

"Everyone has a face. Yours is just set to *closed*." He spat expertly over the wheel. "Toll station ahead. Halfmile. There's a new one posted—big fellow, red sash. Been waving people down since dawn."

My stomach did the small cold drop it does when routine develops a complication. "New how?"

"New as in not on the rotation. Red sash means inquisitorial auxiliary, not customs." Dren glanced at me sideways. "They don't post auxiliaries for salt, Yara."

"No," I agreed. "They don't."

Auxiliaries were the empire's answer to a question nobody had asked: what if we gave bored young men with grudges a badge and permission? They couldn't levy taxes, technically. They could only *inspect*, which is a word that means the same thing but takes longer and sometimes ends with a fire.

I called a halt at the bend below the station, on the pretext of a loose harness trace, and walked the line of wagons while the drovers drank from their skins and complained about the mules in the elaborate, affectionate way drovers complain about everything. The station itself sat in a notch of the pass—a stone hut, a barrier arm, a brazier, and the red-sashed auxiliary, who was indeed big, and indeed bored, and had already flagged down a tinker's cart and was making the tinker unload it into the mud.

Under the salt, our cargo sat quiet. Under my coat, so did I.

Here's the trick of it, and it isn't much of a trick: people see what they expect to see, and most of the time, they expect nothing at all. A tollman expects a manifest, a seal, a driver, a yawn. So I gave him a manifest—the guild kept a shelf of beautifully aged forgeries, tea-stained and thumb-worn—and a seal that would survive a casual glance, and a driver's yawn that I have been practicing since I was old enough to drive. What I didn't give him was anything to remember. No jokes. No eye contact held a heartbeat long. No interesting scars above the collar. When he looked at me, I made sure he was already finished looking.

It works because it's boring. Boring is the guild's true patron god. We leave offerings daily.

The auxiliary waved the tinker through eventually—or rather, waved the tinker's scattered goods back onto the tinker's cart, minus a "handling fee"—and turned to us with the particular enthusiasm of a man who has found that annoying people is the best part of his day.

"Caravan origin?"

"Vessik pans," I said. "Contract haul for the Merrow factors, free side."

"Manifest."

I handed it over. He read it the way auxiliaries read everything, which is slowly and with visible suspicion of the alphabet. His eyes came up once, found my face, and slid off. I watched it happen—watched his gaze hit me and skid, like a boot on ice—and felt the familiar nothing-flutter in my chest. Not pride. Pride implies effort. More like the satisfaction of a key turning smoothly in a lock.

"Cargo?"

"Salt. Dye-cloth under the salt, declared and bonded." A lie wrapped in a truth wrapped in tedium. Auxiliaries die of boredom faster than anything; I was administering a mercy.

He grunted, stamped the manifest against his knee, and was reaching for the barrier arm when the second thing happened.

Not the mountain. That came later. First: a sound from off the road, up the slope—a voice, thin and cracked, coming from the little roadside shrine that squats in the notch above the toll station. Every pass in the Thornfells has one. They predate the empire, predate the maps, maybe. Little stone niches, weathered smooth, holding nothing now. The garden faith tended them once, a century ago, back when anyone tended anything. Now they were just rocks with hollows, and everyone knew it, and travelers still left bread in them anyway, the way you'd toss a coin in a dry well. Habit. Nothing more.

A woman had climbed up to this one. Pilgrim by her dust, poor by her shoes, and she was on her knees in front of the empty niche with her hands pressed together so hard her knuckles had gone white, and she was praying. Not the polite mumbled kind. The loud, broken, past-caring kind—the prayer of someone who has run out of everything except words.

"*Please.* Oh please, oh please, whoever's listening—my daughter, my girl, she's got the lung-rot, the physickers won't come without coin and I haven't—I haven't got—" Her voice cracked apart. "*Please. Anyone. Anything.*"

The auxiliary rolled his eyes. Dren muttered something about madwomen. The drovers stared at their mules.

And the mountain shuddered.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left of that moment. It was not an earthquake. Earthquakes have a direction, a logic, a rolling violence. This was smaller and worse—a single deep tremor, like something enormous turning over in its sleep beneath the stone, and then stillness. The brazier flame bent sideways though no wind moved. The mules screamed—all nineteen of them, at once, a sound I have never heard before or since—and then went silent as if a hand had closed over their mouths.

And the shrine—

The shrine *answered*.

I don't know how else to put it. The empty niche filled. Not with light, nothing so clean. With a kind of *pressure*, a green-dark fullness, like the moment before summer thunder, and the air above the stone thickened and bent and I swear on Baba Sef's name that something leaned out of that hollow rock and *looked at the woman*, and the woman stopped crying, and her face—

Her face went slack with wonder. And then, a heartbeat later, with something else. Something that knew better.

"*No,*" she whispered. "*No, I didn't mean—you're not—*" She scrambled backward down the slope, fell, got up, ran—not along the road, but straight up into the scree, away from everything, and the green-dark pressure in the niche folded after her like smoke deciding to be hungry, and then it was gone, and the shrine was just a rock with a hollow again, and the birds started up as if nothing had happened, and the mules stood trembling and sweating, and the auxiliary was staring at the shrine with his mouth open, his stamp still raised, having entirely forgotten I existed.

Which, under other circumstances, would have been my cue to vanish with the whole caravan while he stood there. That was the play. Any competent smuggler would have taken it.

Instead I stood in the road holding a mule's bridle and listened to the mountain settle around me, deep down, like a house creaking, and I felt—this is the part I've never told anyone, the part I barely admitted to myself—I felt *recognized*. For half a heartbeat, as that pressure folded away up the slope, it had brushed past me on its way, and something in it had paused. Something vast and starving and old had glanced at a thin gray man in a gray coat on a salt road, and something in *me* had glanced back, and for one impossible instant I had known its hunger the way you know your own heartbeat.

Then it was gone, and I was nobody again, and I have never been so grateful and so horrified to be nobody in my entire life.

"Yara." Dren's hand on my arm. His face was the color of old tallow. "You felt that."

"It was a tremor," I said. "Shallow one. Happens."

"That wasn't a tremor." He was looking at the shrine. "The old stories say—my gran used to say, before the empire burned the story-tellers' rolls, she used to say the shrines weren't empty. Just sleeping. She said you should never pray at them desperate, because desperate prayers get *answered*, and the things that answered aren't—" He stopped. Swallowed. "They're not the garden-kind. They're the older kind."

"The garden faith is dead, Dren. Hundred years dead. Everyone knows that."

"Everyone knows that," he agreed, in the tone of a man agreeing with a child about the honesty of tax collectors. "And everyone knows the shrines are empty rocks. So explain the mules."

I couldn't. So I did what the guild pays me for, which is to make problems small enough to carry. I got the caravan moving. The auxiliary stamped our manifest without looking at it—he'd developed a sudden thirst for the interior of the toll hut and a sudden lack of interest in salt—and we ground up through the notch and past the shrine, and I made myself look at it as we passed. Empty stone. Weathered hollow. A crust of old bread in the bottom, going green.

Going green. In this cold. At this altitude. In autumn.

I drove on and told myself it was moss.

* * *

We made the free-side descent by nightfall and camped in the lee of a fallen watchtower the empire had abandoned two border-redraws ago. The drovers built a fire, argued about dice, and conspicuously did not talk about the morning. I checked the cargo, checked the mules, checked the letter in my coat—the one for Baba Sef, the one I hadn't read—and finally, because I couldn't think of another way to be useful, sat down across the fire from Dren and let him pour me a cup of the terrible plum brandy he carries for medicinal purposes and morale.

"Baba Sef know you drink that?" I asked.

"Baba Sef taught me to drink that. Said a smuggler should always carry one vice he can name." He studied me over the rim of his cup. "You're going to see her tomorrow."

"Day after. We deliver to Merrow first, take the cut home."

"She dying, Yara?"

The fire popped. Somewhere up-slope, a stone clattered—just frost-heave, probably, just the mountain doing ordinary mountain things, though I noticed both of us flinched.

"She's tired," I said. Which was true and insufficient. Baba Sef had run the guild of Thornfell for forty years, and the guild was thirteen people and a crumbling waystation and a ledger of debts so old half the debtors were dead, and she had held all of it together with spite and bookkeeping, and for the last two years I had watched the spite burn lower while the bookkeeping stayed sharp as ever, which was somehow worse. A mind that stays sharp inside a failing body is a lamp burning in an empty house. You keep waiting for it to go out, and instead it just keeps showing you the rooms.

"Tired," Dren repeated. "Right." He knocked back his brandy. "You know what she asked me last month? Asked if I remembered my grandmother's maiden name. My *grandmother's*. Out of nowhere. Then she wanted to know if I'd ever seen the old seal—the Warden seal, the one from before. I told her the Wardens were a story. She laughed at me." He shook his head. "She doesn't laugh right anymore. It's like laughing's a chore she's doing to keep us calm."

I said nothing, because there was nothing to say, and because the word *Warden* had gone into me like a splinter I couldn't find the edge of. I'd heard it before—everyone in the guild had, the way you hear family ghosts, in fragments, in Baba Sef's occasional mutterings about *before*—but hearing it twice in one day, once from a frightened drover and once from a dying woman's questions, felt less like coincidence than like a door I'd been walking past my whole life suddenly standing ajar.

"And another thing," Dren said, quieter. "That woman on the mountain. The praying one. Patrols found her pack at midday, torn open, supplies scattered. No body." He looked at me. "No body, Yara. On a slope a goat couldn't fall off of."

"Frost took her somewhere soft."

"There's no soft anywhere on Halloway." He poked the fire. "Get some sleep. You've got the dawn watch."

I took the dawn watch. Of course I did. I always take the dawn watch, because dawn is when the world is smallest and most manageable, when the light comes up gray and honest and there's nothing yet that needs hiding from.

Except that morning, sitting on the fallen tower with the caravan breathing below me and the peaks going rose-gold around the bowl of the sky, I caught myself doing something I hadn't done since I was a child: I caught myself listening. Sitting absolutely still, ears straining, listening *down*—down through the stone, down under the roots of the mountains, the way you'd listen at a wall for movement in the next room.

And I don't know what I expected to hear. Silence, probably. The ordinary mineral silence of a dead faith and empty shrines.

What I heard—or almost heard, or dreamed I heard, I've gone back and forth on it every day since—was a stirring. Far down. Many things, far down, shifting in their sleep the way a sleeper shifts when a door opens somewhere in the house and lets in a draft.

One of them, I could have sworn, sighed a name.

It sounded like mine.

I got up, stamped feeling back into my feet, and told myself firmly that plum brandy is a liar and dawn is a theater for hallucinations, and that I was Yara Reyes, salt-caravan guide, toll-dodger, economical user of the world's attention, and that I had a delivery in Merrow and a cut to bring home to a dying woman, and that was the whole of me, and plenty.

Below me, the drovers were waking. The mules were complaining. The road went on down toward the free towns, ordinary as bread.

Behind us, up in the notch of the pass, the little shrine sat in the morning light with its crust of bread going green, and I did not look back at it, and I thought about Baba Sef asking after grandmothers' names, and I did not look back at it, and I rode down out of the mountains with the letter against my ribs like a second heart, and I did not look back.

I should have looked back.
