## 2. The Guild of Small Debts

The last mile into the guildhall was always the hardest, because it was the only part of the road where I had to be somebody.

Out on the salt roads I could be anyone—a drover, a pilgrim, a tax collector's nightmare. The passes didn't care what name you carried; they only cared whether your boots knew the stone. But the guildhall sat in a fold of the Thornfells that didn't officially exist, which meant every soul inside it knew my face, my habits, and exactly how much salt I'd failed to declare. You can't be invisible in a house with thirteen windows, all of them lit.

I came down through the birch stand at dusk, when the waystation's chimneys made their crooked smoke against a sky going bruise-colored, and I counted the lights out of habit. Eleven. Two dark.

"Vess and Odo still out?" I asked the mule, who ignored me with the practiced indifference of a creature who'd heard every question I owned.

The hall itself had been a tollhouse once, back before the empire decided Thornfell was a rounding error and stopped collecting tolls from a country it insisted wasn't there. The empire was very good at that kind of arithmetic. They'd redrawn the maps, struck the names off the ledgers, and declared the whole range administratively empty—which suited us fine, because you can't smuggle through a place that exists, but nobody checks the paperwork of nowhere.

The front door stuck, as it always did, and gave, as it always did, with a groan like an old man being asked to dance. Warmth rolled out to meet me—stew-smoke, tallow, wet wool—and with it the particular noise of thirteen people pretending they weren't waiting for someone.

They were terrible at pretending. They always were.

"You're late," said Kettri, not looking up from the boot she was re-soling, which was how she said *we were worried*.

"Road was late," I said, which was how I said *I'm fine, don't fuss*.

"Mm." She bit a thread clean through. "There's stew. There's also a rumor the tollmen on the Kessick road have started weighing wagons twice, so if you brought anything interesting, bury it before dawn."

"I never bring anything interesting."

"No," she agreed. "That's your entire value as a person."

That was Kettri. Affection, in the Guild of Small Debts, came wrapped in insult like medicine in honey. I took the bowl Marta handed me without sitting, because I'd learned years ago that if I sat down first, the questions would come all at once, and if I stayed standing and moving, they'd come one at a time, and I could survive almost anything one at a time.

"Where is she?" I asked.

Marta's ladle paused over the pot. Just a pause. Half a heartbeat. But in our house, half a heartbeat was a paragraph.

"Back room," she said. "She ate some. She asked for you twice."

Twice. Baba Sef asked for things maybe four times a year, and each asking was treated by the guild with the gravity of a royal summons. I put the bowl down untasted—I could feel Kettri watching me do it and saying nothing, which was its own kind of kindness—and went down the hall past the ledger room, past the shrine niche where we kept a chipped statue of no god in particular because Baba Sef said a house should have somewhere to put its worries.

Her door was open a hand's width. It was always open a hand's width now. She'd stopped closing it months ago, and none of us talked about why, the same way we didn't talk about the cane leaning against her chair, or the way she'd started letting other people count the take.

"Baba," I said. "It's me."

"Obviously," said the shadow in the bed. "Who else walks like a man apologizing for existing? Come in, boy, and bring the light. My eyes are done pretending."

I brought the lamp in and set it where she liked it, low and to the left, so the light caught her face without glaring. She looked smaller than she had in autumn. That was the thing nobody warned you about with old women—you think they're finished shrinking, and then they find another size beneath. Her hands lay on the blanket like two knotted roots, and her eyes, when they found mine, were as sharp as ever, which somehow made everything else worse.

"The cut," she said.

I dug the purse from my coat and set it in her palm. She weighed it without opening it, the way she weighed everything, and something in her expression did arithmetic I couldn't see.

"Light," she said. "Tollmen?"

"Tollmen, weather, and a shrine I had to detour around."

Something moved behind her eyes at that. "A shrine."

"Roadside one. Old style, pre-imperial. Somebody'd left prayers there fresh enough to still be wet." I kept my voice easy, the way you keep a boat easy in chop. "Odd thing, though. One of them got answered while I was standing there. Audibly."

The silence that followed had texture. I'd grown up in this house; I knew all its silences—the one after a job gone wrong, the one before Kettri told a joke she'd been saving, the one when someone mentioned the war. This was none of those. This was new, and it fit badly, like a coat cut for someone bigger.

"Answered how?" she said.

"Mountain shook. Voice like gravel in a barrel. The woman praying ran off convinced she'd been blessed." I shrugged. "Probably a rockslide. Probably coincidence."

"Boy." She patted the blanket beside her, an invitation she hadn't extended since I was small enough to need it. "Sit. And stop lying to me with your shoulders."

I sat. The mattress dipped, and the smell of her—dried herbs, lamp oil, the ghost of pipe smoke—rose up around me, and for a moment I was eight years old again with a fever, and she was the only fixed point in a spinning world.

"You felt it," she said. Not a question.

"I felt something." I looked at my hands. "Like the mountain cleared its throat. Like something turned over under the floor of the world and went back to sleep."

"And your grandmother—" she began, and stopped herself, and I watched her rebuild the sentence brick by brick, careful as a sapper. "Your grandmother. What was her name?"

The question landed wrong. Not dangerous-wrong, just wrong, the way a familiar stair isn't where your foot expects it. "You knew my grandmother. You buried her."

"I know who I buried." Her voice had an edge now, thin and bright. "I'm asking what you called her. What she called herself. What name she gave you, if you ever asked, if there was ever a night by a fire when she spoke of her mother, and her mother's mother, and the line that goes back before the maps changed."

"Baba Sef." I laughed, and it came out thinner than I wanted. "She was Nana. She sold dyes and lied about her age. There's no line. There's no fire. There's a dye merchant who raised a stray and died owing money to everyone in three valleys, which is the most honest inheritance anyone in this house ever got."

"Owing money," Baba Sef repeated softly. "Yes. Debts. We are the guild of small debts, aren't we. Salt owed, favors owed, names owed." Her fingers closed over mine, dry and strong despite everything. "And some debts are older than coin, Yara. Some debts sit in the ground and wait a hundred years for the creditor to wake up hungry."

Outside, the wind came down the pass and worried the eaves, and somewhere below us Marta dropped a lid, and the ordinary sounds of the house went on, and I held onto them like a rail.

"Why are you asking me this?" I said.

"Because I'm dying, and dying men get to be rude." She said it flatly, without drama, the way she announced weather. "Because something answered a prayer in my mountains yesterday, and the last time that happened, my grandmother's grandmother burned a letter rather than let it be read aloud. Because you walked in tonight smelling of cold stone and green things, boy, and you've smelled faintly of green things for a month, and I've been telling myself it was the moss on the north road."

I pulled my hand back. "You're ill. Illness makes—"

"Don't." For a moment the sharpness in her was the old sharpness, the one that had stared down imperial captains and rival guildmasters and one memorable bear. "Don't manage me, Yara. I taught you that trick. I know every seam of it."

We sat in the lamplight, the two of us, and the distance between us was the width of the bed and the length of thirty years, and I hated it.

"There's a seal," she said at last, quieter. "Old. Pre-imperial. A circle of thorns around a seed, if the stories are right. Have you seen it? On anything of hers? On the dye-stamps, the ledgers, the bottom of a cup?"

"Nana's stamp was a flower," I said. "Five petals. She used it to mark her good dyes. Everyone in the market knew it."

"Five petals." Something crossed her face that I couldn't read, grief and relief fighting for the same chair. "Show me sometime. Show me the stamp."

"It's in a box under my cot. It's a dye stamp, Baba."

"Mm." She settled back against her pillows, and the effort of the conversation settled onto her visibly, like snow taking a branch. "Go eat your stew. It's been on the fire twice; it'll be terrible."

I stood. At the door I stopped, because leaving felt like agreeing with her, and staying felt like finding out, and both were unbearable.

"She was just a dye merchant," I said.

"Yes," said Baba Sef, closing her eyes. "And I'm just an old woman, and this is just a tollhouse, and the empire is just administrating an empty mountain. Go on, boy. Sleep. Tomorrow we'll all go on being exactly what we say we are."

* * *

I didn't sleep. I ate the terrible stew instead, second helpings, while Kettri dealt cards to a losing table and Vess—back at last, mud to the knees—described the Kessick weighbridge with the loathing it deserved. Ordinary sounds. Ordinary people. I held the evening around me like a coat against rain.

But later, when the house had gone dark and breathing, I knelt by my cot and dragged out the box.

Nana's box. Cedar, cracked at one corner, smelling of cedar still underneath the dust and the dye-stains. Inside: three spools of thread gone brittle, a river stone worn smooth, a lock of hair tied with wire, and the stamp.

I turned it in the moonlight from the window. Wood, dark with age and ink. Five petals, yes—but I'd never really looked, because children don't look, and adults see what they expect. Five petals arranged around a center, and the center wasn't a center at all. It was a seed. And the petals weren't petals, they were thorns, curved inward, guarding.

A circle of thorns around a seed.

My hands were steady, which surprised me. Somewhere far off, or maybe not far off at all, the mountain made a sound—not quite a groan, not quite a word—and the lamp flame in the hall guttered though no draft had come.

Under the box, pressed flat against the bottom where I'd never thought to look because I'd never had reason to, was a paper. Folded small, sealed with wax gone the color of dried blood, and stamped with the same thorned seed.

On the outside, in handwriting I knew better than my own, my grandmother had written four words:

*For the boy. When it wakes.*

The mountain shuddered again. Closer, this time. Or deeper.

I sat on the cold floor of the only home I'd ever had, holding a letter from a dead woman who had apparently known this night was coming, and upstairs—or down the hall, wherever she was—Baba Sef coughed once, long and wet, and did not stop for a worryingly long time.

In the morning, I decided, I would ask her everything.

In the morning, the empire arrived instead.
