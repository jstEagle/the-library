## 3. What the Shrine Gave Back

The shrine at Hollowmere had been dead for eleven years, which made it the most honest thing in my life.

Everything else I tended lied a little. The herb garden behind the almshouse pretended to be charity when it was really penance. The villagers' prayers pretended to be faith when they were mostly bargaining. Even my own hands, kneeling in the dirt each morning with their careful devotions, pretended they were serving the Garden rather than hiding from it.

But the shrine was honest. Cracked basin, choked spring, moss eating the names off the dedication stone. Dead as doctrine. I came every third day because the order of Saint Vessal—my former order, though my tongue still stumbled on *former*—required that deposed priestesses perform acts of humble maintenance within their assigned parishes, and because scrubbing moss off a dead god's porch was the closest thing to peace I could afford.

I should explain what I mean by dead, since I'll be contradicting myself shortly and I'd rather you know I knew better.

In the garden faith we do not say the old gods are gone. We say they are *sleeping*, which is the kind of word theologians use when they want to avoid saying *buried*. A century ago—so the archives say, the open archives, the ones they let novices read—the gods of Thornfell went silent. No answered prayers, no signs, no root-tremors in the sacred groves. The garden faith did not die so much as it was widowed. We kept tending shrines for spouses who never came home. It is either the most faithful thing imaginable or the most foolish, and after six years of exile I still hadn't decided which side of that line I stood on.

So: the shrine was dead, the spring was silted, and I was on my knees in the mud with a trowel, clearing the drainage channel the way you clear a grave you're not sure anyone's in.

That was when the soil moved.

Not shifted. Not settled. *Moved*—with intent, the way a sleeper turns toward a voice. I felt it through my knees before I saw it, a slow muscular ripple under the earth like something rolling over in bed. My first thought, embarrassingly, was *mole*. My second thought was *landslide*. My third thought arrived late and cold:

*Nothing in this valley has been alive for eleven years.*

I sat back on my heels and watched the ground where the spring used to rise. The silt there had darkened, gone from grey to a green so deep it looked wet even though it wasn't. And out of that green, thin as thread, a shoot came up.

Now. Here is what you must understand about me, so that you may judge me accurately rather than generously. I am, by the order's own finding, a heretic. They stripped my vows, took my name from the rolls, and assigned me to Hollowmere specifically because it was the parish furthest from anything holy. The charge was *unauthorized opening*. The truth was worse and I will get to it eventually, or I won't; heretics are allowed some secrets. But the relevant fact is this: when I saw that shoot rise from dead ground, every rule I had ever broken sang in me at once, and instead of fear—

Instead of fear, I leaned closer.

"Hello," I said. To a plant. In a dead shrine. Alone. If the village had seen me they'd have added *mad* to *heretic* and been done with it.

The shoot grew as I watched. Not fast—not the obscene speed of miracle—but steady, the way good things grow, which is exactly why I should have known it wasn't one. It put out two leaves, then four, and the leaves were wrong in a way I couldn't name until I did: they were shaped like hands. Small ones. Cupped, as if asking.

Every gardener-priestess learns the taxonomy of growth in her first year. There is *green growth*, which is the world being the world. There is *blessed growth*, which is the world being generous. And there is *rooted presence*, which is what happens when something vast puts down an anchor in small soil and begins, leaf by leaf, to remember how to be a body.

This was rooted presence. This was a god taking root in the mud of a shrine whose god had been dead since before I was born.

I did the correct thing. I want that on the record. I stood up, wiped my hands, and walked away at a dignified pace for nearly forty steps before I broke into a run, and even then I only ran as far as my cottage, where I threw the bolt, sat down hard on my cot, and laughed until I cried, which is either dread or wonder and I have honestly never learned to tell them apart.

Because here is the heresy at the center of me, the one the order never fully dug out: I had *missed them*. The gods. The sleeping ones. Six years of exile and I still caught myself praying into the silence the way you talk to an empty chair. And now something had heard—or half-heard—and come home to a shrine I had been keeping clean out of spite, and part of me, the part I hated, was *glad*.

*

I went back at dusk, because of course I did. Heretics are not known for their restraint.

The plant had become a bush. That was my first warning. Things that grow honestly take days; things that grow hungrily take hours, and this had taken five. It filled the spring-basin now, a low mound of deep-green leaves, each one still shaped like a cupped hand, and the air around it smelled the way a chapel smells after incense—except no one had burned anything here in a decade, and the smell was coming *up out of the ground*, like breath.

I knelt at the edge of the basin, far enough to be prudent, close enough to be curious, which describes most of my life.

"I know what you are," I said quietly. "Or near enough. I'm going to ask you questions, and if you can't answer, don't. If you can—" I hesitated. "Be gentle. The last thing that spoke through soil to me was a turnip, and it said nothing, and I'd like to keep that record intact."

The leaves turned. All of them, together, slowly, like faces.

I will not pretend my courage held. It didn't. But courage is just fear that keeps working, and my hands kept working, so I counted it.

"What are you called?" I asked. "Your true name. The old one."

The bush shivered. And then the answer came—not in sound, nothing so crude, but in the roots, in the ground, in the bones of my knees pressed against the earth. A pressure. A shape. A meaning wearing the shape of a word too large for any mouth.

*Forgotten,* it said. Or: *the one who was forgotten.* Or possibly: *forgottenness itself.* Old gods do not translate well. Imagine trying to write down the smell of rain using only numbers.

"You were one of the hundred," I said. "The garden's hundred roots. You slept when the others slept." I swallowed. "Why are you awake?"

The pressure again, longer this time, and underneath it something new—a hunger so patient and so enormous that I felt it in my teeth. It showed me, without words, an image: a door. Stone, ancient, set into a mountainside, sealed with a sigil I did not recognize but my body did—my spine arched away from it before my mind understood why.

And then, threaded through the image like wire through clay, three words. Not spoken. *Insisted.*

*"The Warden's blood."*

I sat very still. Somewhere behind me a nightbird started up and stopped, as if it too had heard.

"Warden," I repeated. My voice came out flat, which is what my voice does when I am terrified and refuse to show it. "There's no Warden. There's no such line. The Wardens are a story the archivists use to frighten novices away from the sealed stacks."

Which was true, and also a lie, because I had read about them anyway. Of course I had. You do not strip a woman's vows and expect her to stop reading.

Here is what the forbidden archives say, the pages I copied by lantern-light in my last year before exile, the pages I burned the morning they came for me and have regretted burning every day since: before the empire drew its maps over Thornfell, before the gods went silent, there was a family whose duty was the seal. Not priests—priests pray *to*. These prayed *at*. They stood between the hungry old powers and the world, generation after generation, and the door in the mountain was theirs, and the blood in their veins was the lock's oil and the lock's chain both. And then, roughly a century ago, right around the time the gods went quiet, the Wardens ended. Not died—*ended*. Erased themselves. Unnamed themselves. Scrubbed from every record so thoroughly that even the gods, waking groggy after a century, might not remember whom to hate.

The archives end there. The archives always end where the interesting part starts; that is what makes them forbidden.

But I remembered one more thing, a marginal note in a dead archivist's hand, cramped and hurried: *If the seal weakens, the roots will wake hungry, and the first thing they will ask for is the blood that shut the door. Pray the line is truly dead. Pray harder than you've ever prayed.*

I looked at the bush with its hand-shaped leaves, and the bush looked back with all of them at once, and I understood that I was kneeling inside someone's prayer. Just not mine. Not anymore.

"The Warden's blood," I said slowly. "You want it. Why? To be free?" I thought of the door, the sigil, the way my body had flinched. "Or to be *fed*?"

The leaves cupped upward. Asking. Always asking. And beneath the asking, that patient hunger turned over like something huge in shallow water, and I felt—for one vertiginous second—what it would feel like to answer it. Warmth. Plenty. Every seed sprouting, every wound closing, every prayer answered *kindly*, the way the old stories swore the garden gods once answered them, before the sleeping, before the silence.

It would have been so easy to say yes to whatever it wanted. That is the trap of gods, and I speak with authority: they never offer you horror. They offer you the thing you already want, grown to monstrous size.

"No," I said. My voice cracked on it, which I choose to interpret as conviction. "Whatever you're offering, whoever you're asking for—I'm not the Warden, and I won't help you find them. Whatever's left of that blood, whatever poor soul carries it without knowing—they've done nothing. The debt isn't theirs."

The bush went still. Completely still, in a way plants are not, in a way living things are not, and the smell of incense thickened until I tasted it.

Then, softly, in the roots, in the ground, in the marrow of me: a feeling I can only describe as *being filed away*. Catalogued. Remembered. The way a creditor remembers a debtor's face.

I got up. I walked back to my cottage at a dignified pace for nearly forty steps, and then I ran, and this time I didn't laugh at all.

* * *

I did not sleep. I lit my lamp, dragged out the loose stone under the floorboard where I keep the things that would hang me twice over, and pulled out the one item I'd saved from the burning: a scrap of archive paper, no bigger than my palm, on which I had copied—in my arrogance, in my hunger for the forbidden—one line from the sealed annals.

*The last Warden of Thornfell kept a guild of small debts, and hid among the thirteenth of them.*

Thirteenth. Thirteen smugglers, the road-talk said, in a crumbling waystation somewhere in the high passes, the last free company in a country the empire insisted didn't exist. I'd assumed it was allegory. Archives love allegory; it lets them lie beautifully.

But gods do not speak in allegory. Gods speak in hunger, and the thing in the shrine had asked for a specific blood, and there was exactly one place in Thornfell where a hidden bloodline might plausibly have hidden for a hundred years: among people whose entire trade was not being found.

I sat with the scrap of paper until the lamp burned low, doing arithmetic I didn't want to do. If the seal was failing—if the roots were waking one by one, hungry, half-remembering—then somewhere in those passes was a person who had no idea what they were, and every god in the mountains was beginning to smell them. And I knew, with the sick certainty of someone who has read the ending of a story she's standing in, that my former order would have reached the same conclusion. They kept the sealed stacks. They knew the annals. And they had exiled me to Hollowmere, of all parishes, the nearest habitation to the high passes, with nothing but time and a dead shrine to tend.

They hadn't exiled me. They'd *planted* me.

I almost admired it.

Dawn came grey through my one window. Below the village, in the valley, the shrine waited with its hand-shaped leaves, and beyond the valley the passes rose into cloud, and somewhere up in that cloud was a smuggler's road and a guildhall and, if the annals were true, the last drop of the Warden's blood, beating unaware in someone's chest.

I am a heretic. I have opened one door I should not have opened, and three pilgrims paid for it, and I have spent six years scrubbing moss to pay a debt that cannot be paid. I know precisely what wakes when gates open, and I know what it costs, and I know that the correct response to a hungry god asking for a stranger's blood is to bar the door and pray.

But I also knew this: the order would send someone to kill the Warden heir. That is what orders do—it is cleaner than saving, cheaper, and it looks identical from a distance. And if they sent anyone, they would send word to Hollowmere eventually, because I was their plant, their spare, their deniable knife in the drawer.

I could wait to be used. Or I could walk up into the passes first, find this smuggler with the doomed blood, and decide for myself what a life was worth.

Neither option was good. Both were mine.

I packed a bag. I took the knife I'd sworn never to take up again.

On my way out of the valley, I passed the shrine one last time. The bush had grown overnight—of course it had—and its leaves had begun, very slightly, to resemble a door.

"Don't wait up," I told it, and left the only honest thing in my life standing guard over a lie the whole country was built on.
