## 4. The Prayer He Didn't Mean

The thing about being invisible is that you have to keep practicing at it.

Most people think invisibility is a gift. It isn't. It's a discipline, like keeping your knives sharp or your debts paid. You practice walking so that no one's eye catches on you. You practice standing in doorways, in the gray space between belonging and not. You practice being the man who was carrying the salt sacks, wasn't he? Wasn't there someone? I can't quite recall.

I'd been practicing for twenty-six years, and I was good at it. Baba Sef liked to say I could walk through a wedding unnoticed and leave with the bride's purse as a tip. She said it fondly. Baba Sef said most things fondly, even the ones that should have frightened me.

So when I took the north pass out of Thornfell three days after delivering my cut to the guildhall—alone, because the winter salt run was a two-man job and the other man had a cough that wouldn't quit and a wife who'd finally made him stay home—I was doing what I always did. Being small. Being useful. Being no one in particular, hauling no one's particular salt through no one's particular mountains.

The pass above Kettle Hollow is called the Widow's Teeth, which tells you everything you need to know about it. The switchbacks are steep enough to make mules philosophical, the scree fields are the size of parishes, and the wind up there doesn't blow so much as it files complaints. The empire never bothered posting tollmen that high, which was the whole point. The empire didn't believe anything worth smuggling existed above the tree line, and for a hundred years the mountains had politely agreed with them.

I knew every stone of that pass. That's not poetry. I mean I knew them individually, the way you know the loose tooth in your own mouth—the slab by the second cairn that tips when frost gets under it, the shelf where the snow bridge rots out every Thawmonth, the overhang near the saddle where the rock does this thing, this slow groaning thing, like an old man getting out of a chair, and you learn to just not be under it.

I'd crossed the Widow's Teeth maybe forty times. I'd been caught in weather twice, in a whiteout once, and in a lie told by a business partner exactly once, and I'd walked away from all three. The mountain and I had an arrangement: it did its slow tectonic grumbling, and I stayed small and quick and unremarkable, and neither of us made a fuss.

That arrangement ended on the fourth day, a little past noon, at a place I have since come to think of as the beginning of the end of my ordinary life.

* * *

It started, as these things do, with something small.

A sound. A click, deep under the trail, like a knuckle cracking in a very large hand. I stopped. The mule—I'd named him Chancellor, because he was stubborn, expensive, and only worked when properly bribed—stopped behind me and gave me the flat, judgmental look of his office.

"Nothing," I told him. "Probably."

Chancellor did not believe me. Chancellors never do.

I listened. Wind in the teeth of the pass, the creak of my pack straps, the distant tick-tick-tick of freezing meltwater somewhere below. And underneath it, faint but unmistakable, a settling. That deep, grinding whisper of stone deciding to go somewhere else.

Now, a smart smuggler turns around at that point. A smart smuggler knows that when a mountain starts rearranging its furniture, you don't stand in the room. But the weather window was narrow, the salt contract was already late, and I have already confessed that my defining talent is staying small, not staying sensible. So I did what forty crossings had taught me to do: I read the slope, picked the line, and moved fast and quiet across the bad stretch, keeping Chancellor's lead short, keeping my weight off the slabs that hummed.

I was ten steps from solid ground when the mountain sneezed.

There's no better word for it. One moment the slope was a slope; the next it was a decision, and the decision was *down*. The whole face of the cut above the trail let go at once—not a rockfall, not a slide, but the entire hillside shrugging off a century of patience. I remember thinking, with the strange clarity of the doomed, *so that's what the clicking was*, and then the world went white with dust and deaf with noise, and something the size of a chapel hit the trail six feet behind Chancellor, and I went down, and the sky disappeared.

When the dust settled enough to breathe—and I use "breathe" generously; it was more like inhaling flour—I took stock. This is a smuggler's habit. Broken bones: none that I could find. Blood: some, from my scalp, cosmetic. Position: wedged in a pocket between two boulders, both of which had, in the mountain's casual malice, landed in such a way as to form a little stone room around me. Above me, maybe eight feet up, a slab the size of a barn door lay across the gap, propped at an angle. Light came through the crack along its edge. Air came through the same crack. It was, all things considered, a merciful trap.

But it was a trap.

I won't pretend I was calm. I want that on the record. I did the sensible things first—tested the boulders, tested the slab, shouted until my voice cracked against the stone and came back to me sounding small and stupid. No one was coming. The nearest living soul was four hours back down the pass, and the nearest helpful soul was considerably further than that. Chancellor, bless his bureaucratic heart, had been on the far side of the slide. Alive, presumably. Standing in the clear, eating something he shouldn't, waiting for me to produce a bribe.

Eight feet of barn-door granite overhead. Two boulders pinning me in a pocket barely big enough to kneel in. My hands free, my knife sheathed, my pack crushed somewhere behind me, and the light in the crack going from noon-white to afternoon-gold while I watched.

I did the arithmetic. Digging out: two days, minimum, without tools, and the slab would come down on me long before then. Waiting: the cold would take me tonight, or thirst tomorrow. Shouting: done, failed, embarrassing.

And here is the thing I need you to understand, because it matters, because everything after this moment flows out of it like water from a cracked jar: I was not praying. I want to be absolutely clear about that. I am a smuggler. We don't pray; we negotiate, we bribe, and when those fail, we curse. Prayer implies you believe someone's listening, and the whole theology of my life was built on the comfortable certainty that nobody was.

But I was angry. Trapped, cold, bleeding slightly, watching my one life get spent on a bad stretch of trail between two lumps of rock, and I was *angry*, and anger in the mountains has nowhere to go except up, and so I cursed.

And here's where it stops being a story about a rockslide.

Because I didn't curse the way a normal man curses. A normal man says *damn this mountain* or *curse the luck* or invokes whatever saint his grandmother kept a candle for. What came out of me, kneeling in the dark in my little stone room, spitting dust and fury at eight feet of granite, was this:

"*Veyra of the Deep Roots, I curse you. Ashkaval who Walks the High Snows, I curse you. Mother-of-Thorns, Third-Named, Keeper of the Green Door, I curse you and your silent house!*"

Three names. Three names I had never learned, never read, never heard spoken in my life—names that poured out of me in a voice that wasn't entirely mine, older and lower, the way a river sounds different when it runs under ice. I knew them the way you know how to flinch. I knew them the way you know your own heartbeat: not as information but as inheritance.

And the mountain *moved*.

Not fell. Not slid. *Moved.* The barn-door slab above me groaned—a sound I felt in my teeth, in my sternum, in the wet bottoms of my lungs—and lifted. Rose, actually rose, the way a lid rises off a pot, hinging up along its upper edge, shedding grit, letting in a bar of gold afternoon light that fell across my hands like something spilled. The boulder at my left shoulder rolled aside with a grace no boulder has ever possessed, grinding itself into the scree with the deliberate care of a servant stepping back from a table.

I knelt there in my opened grave with dust sifting down around me like dirty snow, and the mountain held its breath, and I swear to you—I swear on Baba Sef's bad hip and Chancellor's crooked teeth—that the silence after was not empty. It was attentive. It was the silence of a very old thing leaning close to see whether I would say something else.

I did not say something else.

I crawled out. Fast, graceless, hands and knees through the gap, out into the blinding ordinary daylight of the Widow's Teeth, where the wind was still filing its complaints and the sun was still just a sun and nothing, nothing at all, indicated that the laws of the world had recently been bent over someone's knee. Behind me, released from whatever attention had held it, the slab came down with a boom that echoed off the far wall of the pass and came back twice before it died.

I stood up. I checked myself over, the full smuggler's inventory, hands moving down arms and ribs and legs on reflex. Unhurt. Not a scratch beyond the scalp. Not a bruise beyond the ones the boulders had already given me. Forty crossings, two whiteouts, one rockslide, and I walked away from the worst of them without a mark, because I had *cursed correctly*.

That was when I looked at my palm.

The right one. The one I'd braced against the stone when the world came down. Across the meat of it, fine as a hairline crack in porcelain, ran a mark in green—the green of new leaves, of moss on the north side of stones, of the first reckless shoots of Thawmonth. It wasn't ink. It wasn't blood. It sat *in* the skin, the way a scar sits in skin, except I hadn't had it an hour ago and scars don't glow. Because it did glow. Faintly. Pulsing gently, in time, I realized with a horror that arrived a half-second late, with my pulse.

I stood in the middle of the Widow's Teeth and stared at my own hand and felt the bottom drop out of twenty-six years of practiced invisibility.

Here's what I knew, in the part of me that had known those three names: that mark was old. Older than the empire, older than the guild, older than the redrawn maps that showed Thornfell as three tidy imperial provinces with no history worth mentioning. It was a sigil, though I'd have fought anyone who used that word—it sounded too much like priests and prophecy, and I was a man whose highest ambition had been to die quietly solvent. It was a signature. Something had signed me. Or—worse, in the way that true things are worse—something had *recognized* me, and the recognition had left a mark, the way a brand leaves a mark, the way family leaves a mark.

Family. The word surfaced from somewhere deep and unwelcome, trailing Baba Sef's strange questions behind it. *Your grandmother's name, Yara. Say it for me. Just the given name.* And the seal she'd asked me to describe, the one on the ring I'd sold at fourteen because it was ugly and heavy and I needed coin for a debt I've since forgotten. I'd described it from memory: a circle of thorned branches, closed. She'd gone quiet in a way Baba Sef never went quiet.

I closed my fist. The green light seeped out between my fingers anyway, patient as sap.

"Chancellor," I said. My voice came out steady, which surprised me, and hoarse, which didn't. "We're leaving."

He was waiting fifty yards down the trail, exactly as predicted, munching alpine flowers with the serene entitlement of the tenured. He looked at me. He looked at my hand. Mules are not imaginative creatures, but Chancellor took one look at the green glow leaking through my fist and laid his ears back and sidled sideways, and if you've never been distrusted by a mule, let me tell you it does nothing for a man's composure.

"It's temporary," I lied to him.

We went down. Fast. Faster than sense allowed, because every instinct I owned had woken up screaming the same message: *be small, be gone, be no one.* Whatever had answered me on that mountain, whatever had signed my hand and moved a hundred tons of stone at the sound of its own name in my mouth—it knew where I was now. Things that answer prayers know where their pray-ers are. That's practically the job description.

By the time the tree line swallowed us, the light in my palm had faded to almost nothing—just the mark, leaf-green and fine, visible when I turned my hand toward the last of the sun. By full dark it was dormant, a secret written on me in a language I couldn't read. I told myself it would fade. Marks fade. Bruises fade, scars fade, bad weeks fade. In a few days I'd be back at the guildhall, back in the smell of lamp oil and drying nets, back to being no one in particular, and this would be a story I told badly over cheap wine, and no one would believe me, and that would be the best possible outcome.

I believed it right up until the moment I crested the last ridge above the valley and saw, in the blue hour, the lights of the guildhall below—and beyond it, far up the eastern slopes where no shrine had any business being, a thin line of green fire burning on a mountaintop like a lit candle in a window.

Like an answer, sent ahead of me.

Like something saying: *we know the road you're coming home by.*

I stood there with the reins in my fist and the mark sleeping in my palm, and I understood, with the calm clarity of a man reading his own death warrant, that I had not escaped the Widow's Teeth at all.

I had just been let out.
