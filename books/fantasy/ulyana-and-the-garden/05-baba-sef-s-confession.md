## 5. Baba Sef's Confession

Baba Sef was dying the way she did everything else: slowly, stubbornly, and with a running commentary about how everyone else was doing it wrong.

I found her in the counting room, which is what we called the smallest, dampest room in the guildhall because it held a strongbox and Baba Sef's delusion that thirteen smugglers constituted an accounting problem. She sat wrapped in two blankets despite the fact that it was barely autumn, her hands folded on a ledger older than the empire's claim to these mountains. The candle beside her had burned down to a stub so far gone it was more wax memory than light.

"You're late," she said.

"I'm early. You said come at moonrise."

"Moonrise was an hour ago."

"The moon came up behind the ridge. From where I was standing, I had another hour of not being lectured."

Her mouth did the thing it did instead of smiling—a twitch at one corner, like a door deciding whether to open. "Sit down, Yara."

There are tones of voice you learn to fear as a smuggler. The tollman's bored *papers, please*. The buyer who says *of course I'll pay* too smoothly. And the quiet, level voice of someone who has been waiting for you specifically, which is the tone Baba Sef was using now. I sat down on the crate across from her because refusing would have required a spine I didn't currently have, and also because my knees were still remembering the rockslide.

She looked at me for a long time. Baba Sef had eyes like wet river stones—gray, smooth, and impossible to see the bottom of—and when she wanted to, she could make you feel sorted through, itemized, entered into a ledger under some heading you wouldn't like.

"Your hands," she said finally.

I looked at them. The right palm still bore the sigil, though it had faded from angry green to something like old moss, a curling mark that might have been a scar if scars grew in spirals. "Rockslide," I said. "You heard."

"I heard you were buried under forty tons of mountain and walked out without a scratch." She leaned forward. The candle guttered, and for a moment her face was all shadow and wet stone. "I heard the mountain *moved*, Yara. Moved out of your way. Do you know how many people have been buried in those passes since I was a girl? Hundreds. Thousands. The Thornfells eat smugglers the way the empire eats everything else. And the mountain moved for *you*."

"Lucky footing."

"Yara." My name in her mouth sounded different than usual. Heavier. Like she was setting it down carefully on a table between us. "What name did you call?"

The room got very small. Or maybe I did. "I don't know what you—"

"*Veshkaran.*" She said it flatly, without reverence, the way you'd say the name of a debt collector. "The Under-Mountain. The Eater of Roads. You cursed him by his true name, in the old tongue, in a voice that half the pass heard even if they don't know what they heard. Where did you learn that name?"

"I—" And here is the humiliating truth: I didn't know. It had come up out of me like water from a cracked well, whole and ancient and *mine*, the way you know your own heartbeat. "It was just there. In my head. Like I'd always known it."

Baba Sef closed her eyes. When she opened them again, something in them had shifted—not softer exactly. More tired. The way a wall looks tired after a siege.

"Of course it was," she said. "It was always going to be you."

* * *

Here is what I knew about my grandmother, before that night: her name was Ilse, she had come down from the high passes thirty years ago with nothing but a mule and a bad attitude, and she had taught me three things—how to read a trail, how to read a man, and how to be quiet so thoroughly that rooms forgot you were in them.

That was the inventory. That was everything. Ask anyone in the guild. Ask anyone in Thornfell. Ilse Reyes, salt-runner, dead eight years, mourned by exactly no institutions.

Except apparently that wasn't the inventory. Apparently there had been a second ledger all along, kept in a hand I'd never seen.

"Your grandmother," Baba Sef said, "was the last Warden of Thornfell."

I laughed. I'm not proud of it, but I laughed—one short, ugly bark, the kind of sound you make when a joke lands wrong. "The Wardens are a story. Old wives' tales the garden-folk tell to scare children away from shrines. There haven't been any Wardens since—"

"Since the empire redrew the maps and pretended a country out of existence, yes." She said it without heat, which somehow made it worse. "And before that, since the last of them sealed themselves into their own legend and let the world forget. That was the point, child. That was the entire *point*. A Warden who can be found is a Warden who can be used. So they erased themselves. Names struck from records. Bloodlines scattered. Children raised knowing nothing, because a secret you don't know you're keeping is the only kind the empire can't torture out of you." Her gaze dropped to my palm. "But blood keeps its own records. Blood remembers what we make our children forget. And when the seal starts failing, the blood answers."

"The seal." I heard my own voice go flat. "You're telling me the stories are real. The gods under the mountains. The door. All of it."

"I'm telling you I've watched the passes for sixty years, and for fifty-nine of them the shrines stayed quiet and the old names stayed dead and the only things moving under the Thornfells were worms and smugglers. And then four days ago, a stranger prayed at a roadside shrine and something *answered*." She fixed me with the wet-stone stare. "And three days ago, the Under-Mountain answered *you*. Do you understand what that means? The cage is rusting, Yara. Whatever your grandmother's people built to keep the old gods shut away—it's failing. And the lock knows your hand."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my legs just went, carrying me to the narrow window where the night lay over the valley like spilled ink. Below, the guildhall yard was dark and ordinary. Ket was repairing a cart axle by lantern-light, humming off-key. Maro's dog was barking at nothing, the way he always did. Thirteen people. Thirteen small, flawed, beloved lives, going about their evening as if the world were still the shape they thought it was.

"This is a lot to put on me," I said to the glass. "On any of us."

"It is," Baba Sef agreed, from behind me. "Which is why I waited until the mountain made the choice undeniable."

* * *

She made me sit back down before she gave me anything else. She was dying, but she was still Baba Sef, and Baba Sef believed that no one receives anything of value standing up—it makes them inclined to leave.

Then she reached into the folds of her blanket and brought out a key.

I want to describe it properly, because I've turned it over in my hands enough times since to know it by heart. It was iron, or had been once; now it was mostly rust holding itself together out of habit. Longer than my hand, heavy as a sin, with a stem wound in bands of some darker metal and a head shaped like—well, that took me a moment. A circle crossed by a line, and inside the circle, a spiral. The same spiral, I realized with a lurch, that was burned into my palm.

"It doesn't open a lock," Baba Sef said, watching me hold it. "Or rather—it does, but not any lock you're thinking of. Your grandmother left it with me the winter before she died. Told me: *when the blood comes home, give it to them, and tell them what I couldn't.*"

"What couldn't she tell me?" My voice came out strange. Distant. "She raised me. She taught me everything. Why couldn't she just—"

"Because telling you would have woken it in you early. Because a marked child asks questions, and questions travel, and the empire has ears in every market town from here to the coast. Because—" and here Baba Sef's voice cracked, just slightly, the first crack I'd ever heard in it, "—because she hoped it would never have to matter. She hoped the seal would outlast her, and outlast me, and outlast you, and that you'd die an ordinary smuggler in an ordinary bed, having never once been interesting. That was her prayer, Yara. Every night, at that little shrine above the kitchen, she prayed to gods she wasn't allowed to name: *let him be dull. Let him be safe. Let him never know.*"

The kitchen shrine. I could see it suddenly, vivid as a slap—the little alcove with its chipped green bowl, the dried flowers I'd assumed were just an old woman's untidiness. I'd eaten ten thousand meals beneath it. I'd never once wondered what it was *for*.

"She bound herself to it, near the end," Baba Sef went on, quieter now. "The seal. Poured what was left of her years into it, the way the Wardens always did. That's why she aged so fast, why she died when she did. She bought you time with her life and asked me to spend it keeping you small. Unseen. Indispensable to no one." A dry almost-laugh. "You made yourself indispensable to *me*, you contrary boy. Ruined the whole design."

"I'm sorry," I said, and meant it, and didn't entirely know what I was apologizing for. For existing loudly, maybe. For being loved.

"Don't be. Being loved was the one part of the plan that worked." She drew a breath that whistled faintly, and pressed on before I could speak. "Now listen, because I won't say this twice and I may not be able to say it again at all. The key goes to the Warden door. The true door—not the shrine-gates the garden-priests used to tend, little garden gates, easily shut. The Door. The one your ancestors built at the heart of the old country, back when Thornfell was Thornfell and not a smudge on an imperial map. Every god the Wardens caged is behind it. Every hunger, every hungering *name*. As long as it holds, they sleep. If it opens—"

She stopped. Not for effect. I watched her wrestle with something and lose.

"If it opens," I said, "they come out."

"They come out *hungry*," she said. "A century of silence, Yara. A hundred years with no worship, no offering, no names spoken in love. What do you think a god becomes, starved that long? What do you think wakes up, when the lock turns?"

Outside, the wind came down off the high passes and moved through the valley, and somewhere in it—I told myself it was only the wind, I *told myself*—something sighed against the guildhall walls like a large body settling closer.

"And the seal is failing," I said. "Because of me. Because the mountain knew my name."

"Because the blood came home," she corrected. "The seal was always going to fail eventually. Iron rusts. Vows thin. But it's calling you now because it wants renewing, and renewal takes a Warden—and a Warden's whole life, given freely, poured out the way your grandmother poured hers." Her hand closed over mine, over the key, folding my fingers around it. Her skin was paper over bone and cold as cellar stone. "Which is why you must *not* go looking for it. Do you hear me? The door will call. It will dream at you. It will offer you roads and riches and every answer you've ever ached for. You take that key, and you keep walking past every door it shows you, and you live your small loud life, and you let the seal fail on some other generation's watch—"

"Baba Sef." I turned my hand over and gripped hers back. "Four days ago a god answered a stranger's prayer. Three days ago one answered *mine*. Two days ago a priestess watched something take root in a shrine and ask for Warden blood by name—you think I don't hear what's coming down those passes? The empire will come for the anomaly. The rival guilds will come for whatever's scaring the empire. And the gods will come for *me*, whether I walk toward them or not." I shook my head. "Small isn't on the menu anymore. You taught me that yourself—never plan around the world staying polite."

For a long moment she just breathed, the whistle in her chest rising and falling. Then she did the thing instead of smiling again, and this time the door of it actually opened, brief and crooked and grieving.

"Stubborn," she said. "Ilse was stubborn too. Right up until the day it killed her." She patted my hand twice, briskly, business concluded. "Go on, then. Get some sleep. And Yara—if a woman comes asking after you, gray coat, garden-sigil at her throat, eyes like she's doing arithmetic on your soul—"

I went cold. "You know about her?"

"I know the order sent someone the moment the shrines woke. They always do. Kill the heir, close the account." She waved a hand, dismissive, exhausted. "If she finds you before you find her, remember: priests of the garden are trained in three arts—lying, gardening, and killing. Only one of those requires mercy."

"That's reassuring."

"It isn't meant to be. It's meant to be *remembered*." She settled back into her blankets, the candle finally drowning in its own wax, and the last thing she said as I reached the door was soft enough that I almost missed it:

"She tried to kill you once already, you know. The priestess. Two winters ago, on the salt road. You never even saw her. Your grandmother's wards turned her aside—and she never reported it, either. Filed it as *target unconfirmed*." A pause. "Ask yourself why, child. Then ask yourself why she's coming back."

I stood in the dark hallway with a rusted key burning cold against my palm and my whole life rearranging itself around me like furniture in an earthquake, and I thought: *two winters ago. The day the rope snapped on the cliff crossing. The day I should have died.*

Someone had cut it.

And someone had chosen not to finish the job.

I didn't sleep that night. I sat in the loft with the key and the sigil and the sound of the wind worrying at the eaves, and I made lists, the way Baba Sef had taught me—assets, liabilities, unknowns. Under *unknowns*, in the end, I wrote only one word:

*Ulyana.*

Somewhere out in the dark, the mountain sighed again.
