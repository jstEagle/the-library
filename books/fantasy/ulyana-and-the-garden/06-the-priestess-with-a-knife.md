## 6. The Priestess with a Knife

They gave me the knife on a Tuesday.

I remember because it was a Tuesday when they took it from me three years ago—my ordination blade, garden-green steel, surrendered along with my vestments and my name in the rolls of the Verdant Order. And it was a Tuesday when Mother Casilde set it back in my hands, hilt first, like a gift.

"You're not restoring me," I said. "You're arming me."

"I'm being practical." Casilde had that way of speaking about murder the way other people discussed grain tariffs. "The Warden heir is a loose thread, Ulyana. Loose threads unravel seals. You found the shrine at Hollowmere before anyone else did. You read what was written in the soil. So you will find this person, and you will cut the thread."

"And then?"

"Then you may keep the knife."

There it was. Not forgiveness—nothing so generous—but a leash dressed up as a gift. I turned the blade over. It had been sharpened since I'd last held it. Someone had been maintaining my weapon while I weeded the penitent gardens at Vesper's Rest, which meant someone had always intended to hand it back. Three years of exile, and I had been less a disgraced priestess than a tool in storage.

I should have refused. Let the record show that some part of me—the part that still said the evening offices to a god who no longer answered—wanted to refuse.

I took the knife instead. Heresy, I have learned, is rarely a single dramatic act. It's a series of small practical decisions, each one reasonable, each one a half-step down a stair you don't realize has no bottom.

* * *

Here is what the Order knew, which was almost nothing: somewhere in the Thornfell highlands, a prayer had been answered that should not have been answerable. A roadside shrine, one of the little moss-eaten waystones the smugglers use, had responded to a desperate voice with actual power—not the thin residual grace we priests squeeze out of consecrated ground, but the old kind, the deep kind, the kind that moves stone and bends weather and remembers names. The same signature, the diviners said, as the shrine at Hollowmere where something had taken root in the soil and asked—for the first time in a century—for *the Warden's blood*.

The archives were explicit about the Wardens. Explicit and nearly empty, which for our order amounts to the same thing. A family that bound the old gods when the gods went mad with hunger. A family that sealed them beneath the mountains and then did the one thing more frightening than any rite: they erased themselves. No graves, no genealogies, no heraldry. The Order's forbidden shelf held exactly one text concerning them—a gardener's manual, of all things, its margins annotated by nine generations of terrified archivists—and the single useful line in it was this:

*The seal knows its own blood. Guard the bloodline, for the door answers kin.*

Which meant that if the gods were waking, and if a prayer had been answered, then somewhere out there walked a person who didn't know what they were, carrying a key in their veins that every hungry thing under the earth wanted to turn.

Kill the heir, seal the accident. That was the logic. Prune the branch before the rot spreads. Very tidy. Very much the kind of gardening metaphor my order loved, right up until you remembered that branches are attached to people.

I traveled north under a pilgrim's cloak, which is the traditional disguise of everyone in the Thornfells who doesn't want to be asked questions, and therefore fools no one, and therefore fools everyone, because assuming everyone is lying is itself a form of camouflage. I followed the echo of the sigil the way you follow the smell of bread through a market—not by looking for it, but by noticing where the air changed.

The trail led up a switchback road salted white with caravan leavings, past three shrines that had gone quiet again, to a fold in the mountains where a river forgot which way it was going and a building squatted against the cliffside like something that had grown there rather than been built.

The guildhall. Everyone in the lowlands called the last smugglers' guild a rumor. Thirteen thieves and their dying matriarch, holding a pass the empire had officially declared uninhabited, which in imperial cartography means *inconvenient*. I had expected a fortress. What I found was a waystation with a sagging roof, herb pots on the windowsills, and laundry strung between two dead fruit trees.

And I found him mending nets.

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because honesty is the one vow I never broke, even at my trial. Especially at my trial, which is why the trial went so badly.

He sat on an overturned crate by the hall's side door, working a net across his knees with the absent competence of someone who has done ten thousand small necessary tasks and expects to do ten thousand more. Younger than I'd imagined. Dark-haired, sun-brown, with the particular stillness of people who have spent their lives being overlooked and have made a profession of it. His hands moved without his attention; his attention was on the mountain.

That was the first thing wrong. Nobody watches a mountain like that unless the mountain has recently done something worth watching.

I checked the echo against the token Casilde had given me—a sliver of shrine-stone from Hollowmere, wrapped in silk, that trembled faintly in my pocket like a sleeping bird. It trembled now. Faintly, steadily, in time with the man's breathing.

Him. Of course it was him. The gods' idea of a joke, presumably: the bloodline that once caged divinity, reduced to a smuggler darning nets outside a building with a leaky chimney.

I am, whatever else can be said of me, a competent killer. The Order trained me in the quiet arts before it exiled me for the loud ones. There is a way to approach a seated target from behind, a way to hold the knife, a way to do the work so that the net falls neatly over the body and the whole thing looks like a man who fell asleep and never woke. I know it the way I know the evening offices. My feet knew it now. They started moving.

And he said, without turning around, "If you're here about the toll dispute, talk to Marta. If you're here to rob us, you're late—there's nothing left worth the climb."

His voice was ordinary. Tired, dry, a little amused. The voice of a man who had been threatened so often it had become weather to him.

I stopped. One heartbeat. That's all I'm claiming—one heartbeat, maybe two, the space between a knife leaving a sheath and arriving somewhere important. In that heartbeat I looked at his hands, and I saw it: a green mark on his palm, fresh, the color of new leaves, shaped like nothing I'd ever seen and exactly like everything the forbidden margin-notes had tried to describe. The sigil. The seal's own signature, written on its heir like a watermark.

He saw me seeing it. He closed his fist—slowly, deliberately, the way you close a door on a room you've decided not to think about—and turned around fully, and looked at me with eyes that had clearly already catalogued the knife, the pilgrim's cloak that fooled no one, and the fact that I stood like a soldier rather than prayed like a pilgrim.

"Ah," he said. "You're the other kind of visitor."

"There are kinds?"

"On this road? Yes." He set the net down, unhurried, and I understood that the unhurriedness was itself a performance—he was buying seconds, counting exits, doing sums behind those flat dark eyes. "There's tollmen, who want money. There's thieves, who want goods. And there's people like you, who want something they can't put in a sack." He tilted his head. "Inquisitor?"

"Pilgrim," I said, because lying to a marked man seemed pointless only after I'd already done it.

"Right." He almost smiled. "And I'm the Abbot of Vesper's Rest. Baba Sef inside makes a tea that'll strip paint, if you've come all this way. Hospitality's guild law, even for assassins. Especially for assassins—it's harder to stab someone who's complimenting the tea."

Every instinct I had said: *now*. He was seated, I was standing, the knife was warm in my grip, and the Order's arithmetic was simple—one death against a hundred thousand, one smuggler against the integrity of a seal older than the empire. The gods waking hungry beneath the mountains was not an abstraction to me. I had knelt at Hollowmere. I had felt the soil breathe. I had heard something vast and starving say *Warden* the way a dog says *meat*.

One heartbeat. I gave it two.

Because here is the thing nobody warns you about recognition: it isn't only recognizing your target. It's recognizing yourself. He sat there with a secret burning in his hand that he hadn't asked for, wearing ordinariness like a disguise that had stopped fitting, and I thought: *I know exactly what that costs.* I had spent three years being a heretic who still said her prayers. He was going to spend the rest of his life being a key that doors and monsters both wanted to turn, and he was mending nets, because what else is there to do with your hands while the world decides what you are?

The knife stayed where it was. Which was, I note for the record, against orders.

"You have until the tea gets cold," he said, watching me not move. "After that I start charging rent on your silence."

"I need to ask you about the rockslide," I said. "Three days ago. The Kestrel Cut. They say a man walked out of a collapsed pass without a scratch."

"Do they." His face did something very careful. Nothing moved except everything.

"They say the mountain opened for him." I took the shrine-token from my pocket, let the silk fall open, and watched the sliver of Hollowmere stone shiver between us like a compass needle finding its madness. "They say the old gods answered a prayer. And I've come a long way to learn whose prayer it was."

For a moment—this is the part I keep returning to, the part I'll be honest about even when it embarrasses us both—for a moment he looked not frightened but *lonely*. The specific loneliness of a man who has been carrying a thing alone and has just seen, walking up his road in a stolen pilgrim's cloak, the first person in the world who might understand its weight.

Then Baba Sef's voice cracked through the open door like a whip made of smoke—"Yara! Is the nice murderer staying for supper or not?"—and the loneliness folded itself away, and the careful face came back, and I understood I had just learned two dangerous things at once: his name, and the fact that he was loved here, which meant that killing him would cost more than one life.

"Supper," Yara Reyes called back, not breaking eye contact with me. Then, to me, quietly: "You came here to kill me. Don't bother denying it, I've met your kind of pilgrim before." He rose, net forgotten, green sigil hidden in his closed fist. "So here's my counteroffer. Eat with us. Watch us. Decide whether thirteen smugglers and a dying old woman are really the threat to the world your order thinks we are." A beat. "And if you still want to use that knife afterward—at least you'll have had a decent meal first."

It was, I would later realize, the most dangerous thing anyone ever did to me. Not the ambush at the ford, not the river god, not any of it. A man with a death sentence in his palm inviting his assassin to soup.

I told myself I accepted for tactical reasons. Intelligence gathering. Closer observation of the target. All true, in the way that small practical decisions are always true.

I followed him inside. The tea did, in fact, strip paint.

And halfway through the bowl, while Baba Sef watched me with eyes like a hawk that had outlived every falconer, the shrine-token in my pocket went suddenly, violently cold—and far below us, deeper than the cellar, deeper than the roots of the mountain, something turned over in its sleep and whispered a word I felt more than heard, a word with teeth in it:

*Found you.*

Not him.

Me.
