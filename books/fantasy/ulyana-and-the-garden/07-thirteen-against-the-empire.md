## 7. Thirteen Against the Empire

The morning the empire came for us, I was arguing with a goat.

This is not a metaphor. Her name was Duchess, she belonged to Vess, and she had developed a firm opinion that the guildhall's store of dried apricots was community property. I was explaining, at length and with some heat, that community property was a concept Duchess had invented to justify theft, when the bell above the gate rang twice—short, sharp—and then stopped.

That was wrong. Our gate-bell didn't stop. It rang until you answered it or it rusted through. It had been rung by tollmen, tax collectors, one very confused bishop, and on one memorable occasion an entire wedding party who'd taken a wrong turn out of Kellin Hollow. It always rang until someone made it stop.

Duchess stopped chewing. Goats are stupid about most things, but they know when the mountain goes quiet.

I set down the apricot tin and listened. Under the wind off the pass, under the creak of the drying nets, there it was: hooves. Not two, not four. A dozen at least, moving in formation, which no honest traveler does because honest travelers don't exist.

"Baba Sef," I called up the stairs. "Company."

She was already at the window, and I already didn't like what I saw in her face. Baba Sef's face had spent seventy years learning every expression from *mildly annoyed* to *deeply unimpressed*, and none of them were on it now. What was on it was something older. Something like recognition.

"Grey cloaks?" she asked.

"Grey cloaks." Six riders at the gate, six more fanning out along the treeline, and a seventh figure dismounting with the unhurried care of someone who has never once been interrupted. Inquisitors don't wear armor. They wear grey wool and small, patient smiles, and they carry warrants instead of swords, which is worse, because a sword only kills you once.

"Yara." She said my name the way she said it maybe once a year, when the ledgers balanced or when someone came home alive when they shouldn't have. "The back stair. The salt cellar. There's a space behind the third shelf."

"I'm not hiding while they—"

"You're not *hiding*." She took my wrist, and her grip was still iron even though the rest of her had gone soft as old paper. "You're doing what this house was built to do. Do you understand me? This house was *built* for this."

I did not understand her. I want that on the record. But her hand was shaking, and Baba Sef's hands did not shake, and so I went down the back stair with the rusted key cold against my chest where I'd worn it on its cord since the night she gave it to me, and I pulled the third shelf away from the wall, and I found a gap in the stone just wide enough for a man who had spent his whole life being nobody.

From inside, I could hear everything.

* * *

Inquisitors knock politely. That's the horror of them. The one at our door—I'd learn later his name was Serel, though at the time he was just a voice like dry paper—knocked three times, evenly spaced, and waited.

"Guildhouse of Thornfell," he announced to the closed door. "We're seeking information regarding an anomalous prayer-signature recorded in these mountains. You'll open, please."

Vess opened it, because Vess always opened doors; it was his one talent besides losing money at cards. "Prayer-signature," he repeated, with the beautiful bafflement of a man whose entire soul is a shrug. "Friend, we're smugglers. We pray to the bottom of a bottle and it never answers."

"May we come in?"

"You may come in and see nothing, same as everyone."

They came in. Boots on our floorboards—eleven pairs, counting the ones who circled around back, whose footsteps I tracked past the woodpile, past the smokehouse, close enough that I could have reached through the wall and touched them. I pressed myself into the dark and did the thing I do best, the thing I'd done my whole life: I became furniture. I became the smell of salt and the silence between floorboard creaks. Nobody finds what isn't looking to be found, and I had made an art of not being found.

"Thirteen of you," Serel said somewhere above me. I heard him counting. He counted our cups. He counted our beds. He counted the names in Baba Sef's ledger, which she surrendered without argument, and I realized with a lurch that the ledger was wrong—that she'd rewritten it, sometime in the night, in her crabbed hand, striking names and adding debts that didn't exist, building us all a paper trail of ordinariness. When had she done that? While I slept? Had she known?

"The signature was logged here," Serel continued. "Three nights ago. A prayer answered at magnitude. Do you understand what I'm telling you? Someone in this valley spoke to something dead, and something dead *spoke back*."

"That'll be the wind," said Vess.

"It is never the wind."

And then Baba Sef's voice, calm as a woman discussing the price of flour. "You're standing on my clean floor, young man. Either buy something or state your business."

There was a pause. I have thought about that pause many times since. I think, in it, Serel looked at her—the tiny, bent, ancient matriarch of a dying guild—and decided she was exactly what she appeared to be. I think that was the mistake that saved my life, and I think she spent her last free breath making him make it.

"Your business," he said at last, "is whatever I say it is. We'll search the grounds. Everyone will remain in the common room."

Everyone remained in the common room. I heard them settle—Vess's chair, Osk's cough, the twins' twin muttering, Marta humming something tuneless to keep her hands from shaking. Thirteen of us, minus one hiding in a wall. Twelve hearts beating in one room, and mine the thirteenth, hammering so loud I was sure it would give me away.

Then boots on the back stair. Then the cellar door.

* * *

I want to tell you I stayed calm. I stayed as calm as a man can be while crouched in a hole behind a shelf of pickled things, listening to an inquisitor breathe ten feet away. He moved along the shelves. He was thorough. He lifted jars and put them down. And then he stopped, and I knew he'd stopped at the third shelf, and I put my hand over my mouth because I understood, suddenly and completely, that I was going to die in a salt cellar smelling of brine, and the unfairness of it made me almost laugh.

"Serel!" One of the others, from upstairs. Urgent. "You'll want to see this. The old woman's got something."

Footsteps receding. My heart trying to climb out through my ribs. And then—because the gods, waking or sleeping, have a sense of humor I've never appreciated—a sound from the common room that stopped everything:

Baba Sef, laughing.

Not her real laugh. Her *performance* laugh, the one she used on tollmen, big and wheezing and utterly disarming. "That? Child, that's a Warden seal. Or what's left of one. It was on the deed when my grandmother bought the place. Superstition. The empire stamped those out before my mother was born—you'd know better than I."

Silence. Long silence. Then Serel, very quietly: "Say that word again."

"What word? Warden?" Another wheeze. "It's on the deed, boy, it's carved over the hearth, it's nothing. A name rich families gave themselves back when names meant something. There hasn't been a Warden in Thornfell in a hundred years. Ask your own archives—they burned the family tree themselves."

My blood went cold, and then colder. She was lying. She was lying magnificently, casually, giving them the word *Warden* wrapped in so much boredom they couldn't taste it—but why? Why hand them the thread at all?

Because they already had it. That was the answer, and it arrived in my chest like a dropped stone. They hadn't come for a rumor. They'd come following the sigil—*my* sigil, the green mark on my palm, the prayer the mountain had answered for me. They had a thread, and Baba Sef was pulling it toward herself, winding it around her own wrists, so it would point at a dead family and a carved lintel and a laughing old woman instead of at the boy in the wall.

"Search the cellars," Serel said finally. "All of them. And bring me the seal-stone."

Boots again. Closer. The shelf groaned—

And then Baba Sef screamed.

I have heard Baba Sef make many sounds. I have heard her laugh, curse, sing (badly), and once, when Osk fell through the ice, make a sound I hoped never to hear again. This was new. This was a scream built to fill a valley, and it worked: every boot in the house turned toward the common room, and the man at the shelf abandoned it, and I sat in the dark with my hand clamped over my mouth while the woman who raised me sold herself at the top of her voice.

I didn't hear the rest clearly. Fragments. The scrape of a chair. Serel's voice going flat and official: *"Matriarch Sefira of the Thornfell line, you are detained under the Edict of Quiet Prayers for obstruction and suspected consanguinity with the Warden bloodline—"*

Consanguinity. Bloodline. They believed her. Gods rot them, they believed her, because she made it easy, because she stood up out of her chair and said, clear enough to reach me through a foot of stone: "Fine. Take me. But you'll find nothing else here, because there *is* nothing else here. Whatever woke in these mountains, it wasn't one of mine."

*"Take her,"* Serel said.

I heard her cross the room. Slow steps—she made them slow, made them stubborn, made them take as long as they possibly could. At the door she stopped, and she said one more thing, in the voice she used for ledger entries and last words:

"Mind the goats. Duchess bites."

Then the door, then the hooves, then the long grey line of them winding down the pass with our matriarch roped among the horses like cargo, and the guildhall holding its breath around me, and me in the dark with a key on a cord and nothing else.

* * *

They didn't leave watchers. That surprised me, then, and doesn't now. An inquisitor takes his prize and moves on; doubt is for priests. By dusk the pass was empty, and I came out of the wall into a house gone strange with absence.

The others came back from the treeline one by one, faces grey. Vess first, then Osk, then the twins, then Marta with Duchess on a rope, because apparently in the chaos someone had decided the goat was a priority, and honestly, fair.

We stood in the common room, twelve of us, and looked at the thirteen chairs.

"They took her east," Osk said. "Toward Kellin. The garrison there has cells under it."

"She'll talk," said one of the twins—not cruelly, just factually, the way you'd note weather coming. "Everyone talks eventually. She'll be hoping we scatter before she has to."

"She bought us a day," Marta said. "Maybe two. Maybe less if they have a truth-reader."

Everyone looked at me. I hated it. I had spent thirty years arranging my life so that no one would ever look at me in exactly that way, and now twelve people I loved were waiting for me to be something, and I didn't know what.

"We scatter," I said. "Like she planned. Vess, take the north road accounts. Osk—"

"No." Vess said it gently, which was somehow worse. "Yara. Look at your hand."

I looked. The green sigil had woken. It lay on my palm like a vein of moss, faintly luminous in the dimming room, and as I watched, it pulsed—once, twice—in rhythm with something far under the mountain. With something that had felt Baba Sef taken and was, in its half-remembered, hungry way, *paying attention*.

"They didn't come for a rumor," I said. My voice came out steadier than I felt. "They came for me. For this." I held up the hand. "She knew. She rewrote the ledger, she fed them the Warden name, she walked out that door so they'd chase a dead family instead of a live one. And if I scatter—if I hide the way I've always hidden—they'll work it out of her anyway, and she'll have gone through all that for nothing."

"So what do we do?" Osk asked.

What do we do. Not *what will you do*. Twelve smugglers, and the question was *we*, and I understood that Baba Sef had done more than buy me time. She'd handed me the guild the way you hand someone a live coal: quickly, painfully, and trusting them not to drop it.

"You scatter," I said. "All of you. Real scattering—names changed, roads split, the works. Vess holds the accounts. If anyone gets a message to anyone, it comes through him and no one else." I took a breath. "I go after her."

"With what?" That was Marta. "An army? A plan? Yara, they're the imperial inquisition. You're—" she gestured at all of me, apologetically, "—you."

"I'm the last of a bloodline that used to lock up gods," I said, "and I've got a key that opens something they're terrified of, and I am *done* being invisible." The words came out of me like they'd been waiting years for the door to open, and maybe they had. "They think they caught the head of the snake. Let them keep thinking it. But I'm not running anymore. Running is how they win. Running is how she ends up in a cell under Kellin while I die safe in a ditch somewhere, and I have watched that woman balance ledgers for twenty years and I will not let her final entry read *debts unpaid*."

Nobody laughed. Nobody argued, either. Vess just nodded slowly, the nod of a man watching weather turn, and said, "Kellin garrison moves prisoners on the third day. Always has. If you're going to intercept a column, that's your window."

"And after that?" Osk asked quietly.

After that. After that was a rusted key and a warning about a failing seal and a country that officially didn't exist, and a priestess who'd tried to kill me and might be the only person alive who knew the old rites. After that was a road I couldn't walk alone and didn't yet know how to walk at all.

"After that," I said, "I find someone who knows what this key opens. And gods help them, because I'm coming whether they like it or not."

I packed nothing but the key, a knife, and the apricots—for Duchess, who bit me affectionately on the way out, which I chose to interpret as a blessing. At the gate I looked back once at the guildhall, at the carved lintel with the worn Warden seal, at the thirteen chairs visible through the window, twelve occupied by ghosts and one by grief.

Baba Sef had told me once that the guild survived a hundred years of empire by staying small. She'd lied to the inquisitors with her last free breath to keep me small a little longer.

But small things get stepped on, and she was in a cage, and somewhere under the mountain something old had marked my palm and whispered *mine*. So I turned east, toward Kellin, toward the garrison, toward the third day—and toward the border town beyond it, where I happened to know a disgraced priestess was drinking away her exile.

I'd need her rites. She'd need my roads.

Neither of us knew it yet. But the empire had just taught me the only lesson worth learning: you cannot outrun a hunt. You can only decide what's worth turning around to fight for.

I turned around.
