## 8. An Alliance of Liars

Three days after the guildhall burned its way into my memory, I found the woman who'd put a knife to my throat in a border town called Vetch's Landing, drinking tea like she hadn't tried to murder anyone recently.

I want it on the record that I did not plan this well. The plan, such as it was, had consisted of: *follow the priestess, don't die.* Everything past that was improvisation, which is how most of my life has gone. Smuggling is ninety percent improvisation and ten percent pretending you planned the improvisation.

Vetch's Landing sits where Thornfell's last real road gives up and becomes mud. It's the kind of town that exists because two empires once argued about whose problem it was, and both eventually decided it was nobody's. That makes it good country for people like me—people who are officially not there. Half the buildings lean. The other half have given up leaning and simply lie down. There's a shrine to a river spirit in the square that nobody tends anymore, and I caught myself giving it a wide berth, which tells you what kind of week I was having.

Ulyana sat at the back of a tea house with her hood down and her back to the wall, which is professional courtesy among people who expect knives. She looked smaller without the order's green sash. Stripped of the vestments she'd worn when she found me mending nets, she might have been any tired traveler with sharp cheekbones and the posture of someone who'd been sitting vigil for years.

She also, I noticed as I slid into the seat across from her, had a knife under the table pointed at my ribs before I finished sitting.

"Hello," I said. "You're pointing that at the wrong smuggler."

"You're pointing yourself at the wrong priestess." Her voice was flat, but her eyes flicked to the door, counting exits. I respected that. I was doing the same thing. "How did you find me?"

"You walk like someone who knows exactly where she's going. Nobody in Vetch's Landing walks like that." I lifted two fingers toward the proprietor, who brought tea for me and pretended very hard not to see the tableau at the back table. Border towns survive by not seeing things. "Also, you paid with imperial coin, and you're the only person in this room who didn't haggle."

"That's it? Coin and posture?"

"And I asked the ferryman. You crossed yesterday and tipped him too much. Generosity is memorable out here."

Something moved behind her eyes—not quite a smile. "You're better at this than you look."

"I look like a salt hauler. Being better than that is a low bar." I wrapped my hands around the cup. It was terrible tea, scorched, the kind Baba Sef would have sent back with a lecture. My chest did something complicated at the thought of her, and I set the feeling aside the way you set down a pack that's too heavy to keep carrying. Later. Grief keeps. "Here's what happens now," I said. "You're going to tell me why your order wants me dead, and then I'm going to make you an offer, and then one of us is going to leave this table alive and it's probably still going to be you, because that knife is closer to me than mine is to you."

"You don't have a knife."

"I have three. One of them is in the tea house wall behind you, placed there while you were watching the door. Old habit. Sorry."

That was a lie. I had two knives, and neither had left my belt. But she glanced back anyway—a half-second, no more—and in that half-second I took her wrist and turned the blade away from me and pinned her hand flat against the table, palm down, my own scarred hand over hers. The green sigil in my palm flared against her skin like a coal dropped in snow.

She hissed. Not from pain. From recognition.

"*Warden*," she breathed, and for the first time since I'd known her—which was one attempted murder ago—she sounded afraid.

"Keep your voice down." I didn't let go. Around us the tea house went on with its small commerce: dice games, a argument about tar prices, a dog under a table working a fish bone. Nobody looked at us. Invisibility is a skill, and apparently I could extend it to other people when I needed to. "Now. Here's what I know, and correct me where I'm wrong. Your order sent you to kill me because I'm the last Warden. The old gods are waking up, they think I'm a door key, and somebody in your hierarchy decided a dead heir is safer than a live one. Yes?"

Her jaw tightened. "Yes."

"And then you found me mending nets, and you hesitated, and now you're here instead of reporting a completed job, drinking bad tea in a town that isn't on any map your order cares about. Which means either you failed and ran, or you *chose* to fail." I leaned in. "Which is it?"

For a long moment she said nothing. The sigil dimmed between our hands. When she finally spoke, it was quiet enough that I had to lean closer to hear, which I suspect was deliberate.

"When I was noviced," she said, "they taught us the garden faith as a living thing. The gods sleep in the soil, we tend them, they dream kindly. Tending, not binding. That was the whole theology. *Tend, do not bind.*" Her free hand turned over, showing me her own palm—unmarked, but she touched it like it remembered being otherwise. "Then I was assigned to the deep archive, and I read what the order actually believes. They don't tend the gods. They never did. The shrines are locks. Every prayer spoken at them is a rivet. And the Wardens—" she stopped.

"The Wardens built the locks," I said. "And then erased themselves so nobody could find the keys. Except the erasing didn't take all the way, because here I am, and the seal is failing, and every god between here and the high passes can smell it."

"How do you know that?"

"Because one of them answered a prayer I didn't mean to pray, and gave me this." I lifted my hand off hers just enough to show the sigil, then pressed it back down. "And because Baba Sef told me the rest before your inquisitors took her."

Something crossed her face at that—genuine, unguarded grief, there and gone. Whatever else Ulyana was, she wasn't hollow. "The old woman. I'm sorry. Corvane doesn't take prisoners gently."

"Dame Corvane. I've heard the name." I filed it away next to the other names I intended to do something about someday. "So. You were sent to kill me. You didn't. Now your order will send someone who will, or worse, someone who'll try to *use* me, and meanwhile the gods are waking up hungry and the empire is hunting a prayer-signature through mountains full of people who trusted me to keep them invisible." I let go of her wrist and sat back. She didn't move the knife. Progress, maybe. Or exhaustion. "Which brings me to my offer."

"There's an offer?"

"There's always an offer. I'm a smuggler; it's how we say hello." I turned my cup slowly on its saucer. "Somewhere in these mountains is a door. The Warden door. The one door out of the sealed country—the only way through the binding that doesn't break it. Baba Sef gave me a key and half a story before they dragged her off. I know the roads. Every pass, every dead trail, every route the empire redrew out of existence. What I don't know is the rites. The words, the seals, the prayers that keep a god asleep versus the ones that wake it up. You spent your whole life learning those. Including the forbidden ones. *Especially* the forbidden ones."

"No," she said immediately.

"You haven't heard the offer yet."

"I know the offer. You want me to guide you to the Warden door. Everyone wants the Warden door. The gods want it open, my order wants it controlled, the empire wants it catalogued and burned, and you—" her eyes narrowed, "—you want it for yourself. An exit. Out of the country, out of the hunt, out of all of it. A smuggler's solution."

It stung because it was true—or had been true, four days ago, before a rockslide and a rusted key and Baba Sef's hands shaking around a cup of tea. I made myself hold her gaze anyway. Lying badly is worse than lying well, and lying well is worse than truth, if you can afford truth. I couldn't quite, yet. But I could get close.

"I wanted that," I said. "Before. Now I've read enough of Baba Sef's story to suspect the door out is also the door that lets everything else out. Open it for me, open it for them. So no—I'm not asking you to help me escape. I'm asking you to help me find it before anyone else does. Because whoever reaches it first decides what happens to every god in these mountains, and I'd rather it be two liars with guilty consciences than an empire with fire or a god with teeth."

The tea house noise filled the silence. Somewhere the dog got the fish bone. Ulyana studied me the way she'd studied me over the nets—with that terrible priestess attention, like I was a text she was translating and the translation kept coming out wrong.

"Why should I trust you?" she said finally. "You held a knife lie over my head thirty seconds after sitting down."

"To be fair, you had a real knife at my ribs."

"You disarmed me in a public tea house."

"You let me get within arm's reach, which means part of you wanted to be caught. Don't argue. I've spent my whole life reading people who don't want to be read. It's the family business, more or less." I tapped the table between us. "Here's why you trust me. You can't. Trust isn't on offer. What's on offer is *alignment*. You need the door found before your order finds it, because if they open it as a weapon, everything you actually believe in—tend, don't bind—burns with the rest. I need it found before the gods reach it, because if they open it themselves, everyone I've ever smuggled, fed, or lied to dies screaming. We don't have to like each other. We just have to want the same thing for different reasons. That's sturdier than trust. Trust breaks. Aligned greed holds."

A muscle twitched at the corner of her mouth. "That's the bleakest thing anyone's ever proposed to me."

"I'm a smuggler. Romance costs extra."

"And what do I get?" She pulled her hand back at last, flexing her fingers, and I noticed she didn't reach for the knife again. "If I say yes. If I walk into the erased country with a marked Warden and a rusted key. What's in it for me besides dying somewhere interesting?"

I'd thought about this during three days of cold camps and colder fear, and I'd come up with an answer I hated, which usually meant it was honest.

"Atonement," I said. "You said you read the deep archive. Then you know the garden faith didn't fail on its own. Somebody tended those shrines wrong for a hundred years. Somebody let the rites rot and called it doctrine." I watched her flinch and knew I'd hit near whatever she carried. "Whatever you did—the thing that got you stripped and exiled, whatever heresy they named you for—you've been paying for it alone. This is a chance to pay for it *usefully*. Fix the lock. Or failing that, die trying, which your order will at least appreciate."

"You don't even know what I did."

"Don't need to. Everybody out here is running from something. I just happen to be running toward the same place you are."

She was quiet for a long time. Outside, rain started—the soft, persistent drizzle that makes Vetch's Landing smell like wet rope and old wood smoke. When she finally spoke, she didn't answer directly. She did something stranger. She reached into her coat and set something on the table between us: a small clay token, cracked, stamped with a garden sigil older and wilder than anything her order wore now.

"My order thinks I destroyed this," she said. "It's a pilgrim's token from the valley shrines. Pre-imperial. There are seven of them in existence that I know of, and each one marks a site where the old rites were practiced *correctly*—before the binding, before the Wardens, before the order decided sleeping gods were safer than waking ones." She turned it over. "One of the seven is in the heart of the erased borders. If your door exists, it's near that shrine. The rites there might still be... intact. Usable."

"So that's a yes?"

"That's a 'yes, and.'" She took the token back and tucked it away. "Understand what you're getting, smuggler. I was trained to kill things like you. I know eleven ways to stop a heart with my hands and nine with words. If it turns out you're lying—if you're leading me to that door to sell it, or open it, or anything other than what you've said—I will end you and feel the order's approval like sunlight on my face."

"Noted. And understand what *you're* getting. I've kept thirteen people alive in empire territory for six years by being forgettable. If you betray me, I won't fight you. I'll simply become impossible to find, and you'll spend the rest of your life looking for a man who was never there. Ask anyone in Thornfell. I'm very good at never being there."

"Your grandmother was there," Ulyana said quietly. "When it mattered."

The words landed harder than any knife could have. I covered it by finishing my terrible tea. "Yeah," I said. "Well. Maybe it runs in the family after all."

We settled terms the way smugglers do—fast, plain, nothing written. She knew the rites; I knew the roads. She'd lead where the old maps failed; I'd lead everywhere else. Neither of us would ask about the other's worst day until it became unavoidable, which, given our luck, would be soon.

"One condition," she said as we stood to leave. "The shrine in the square. Before we leave town. I want to tend it. Properly, for the first time in years. If we're going to wake things up, I'd rather start with something small enough to apologize to."

I looked at her—at this disgraced priestess who'd tried to kill me and couldn't, offering prayers to a river spirit in a town that had forgotten its name—and felt the strange electric thing settle between us. Not trust. Not yet. Something more honest: two people who had seen each other's worst intentions and decided to walk together anyway.

"Fine," I said. "But if the river god asks for a toll, we're negotiating. I don't pay sticker price to deities."

"It's a shrine, not a toll bridge."

"Everything's a toll bridge if the god is hungry enough. Come on. We've got a long way to go and a country to find that doesn't exist."

She pulled up her hood, and we stepped out into the rain together—two liars, one key, and a door waiting at the end of every erased road in Thornfell.

Behind us, unnoticed by either of us, the water in the neglected shrine's basin had begun, very softly, to glow.
