## 11. What Ulyana Buried

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a man deciding not to kill you.

I had heard it once before, years ago, in the Chapterhouse of the Verdant Hand, when Mother Superior Vess read my sentence aloud and the whole assembly held its breath to see if I would weep. I did not weep then. I did not weep now, either, sitting across a dying fire from Yara Reyes while he turned the rusted Warden key over and over in his marked palm like a coin he couldn't decide whether to spend.

But the silence was the same. Heavy. Expectant. The silence of someone waiting for you to be worth the mercy they'd just shown you.

"You're staring," Yara said.

"I'm assessing."

"At what?"

"Whether you're a fool." I fed a stick to the fire. "The evidence so far is not encouraging."

He almost smiled. He'd been doing that more often since the gorge—since he'd given his true name to a river god like a man tipping a ferryman, and the river had bowed. Since Baba Sef's face had worn another god's smile at our fire and I had burned it back with rites I'd sworn never to use again. Something had changed in him after that night. Or perhaps something had cracked, and what came out was harder than what had been there before.

"You've been quiet for two days," he said. "Quieter than usual, which for you is saying something. You keep looking at me like I'm a diagnosis."

"You are. Terminal, most likely."

"Ulyana."

I should have let it go. That was the sensible thing—the *survivable* thing. Confession is a door, and I of all people knew what came through doors once you opened them. But the god wearing Baba Sef's face had asked him questions with her voice, had pried at him with her hands, had taken the one warm memory he had left and used it as a crowbar. And every hour since, Yara had looked at me a little less like an ally and a little more like a stranger he was traveling with out of arithmetic rather than trust.

Arithmetic keeps you alive on the road. It does not keep you alive at the door. And we were three days from the heart of the erased borders, where whatever waited would need us to trust each other with our actual lives, not just our rations.

So I opened the door.

"My name isn't Ulyana," I said.

He blinked. "It's—"

"It is. But it wasn't first." I pulled my knees up, wrapped my arms around them, and made myself look at the fire instead of at him. Easier to confess to a fire. Fires don't flinch. "When I was born, my mother named me for the shrine she wanted me to tend someday. Verel. It means 'the gate that opens kindly.' The order renamed me when I took vows—Ulyana, 'she who tends.' A warning name, as it turns out. Prophetic. They should have charged extra."

Yara said nothing. He was good at that. Most men fill silences like nervous masons filling cracks. Yara just let them stand, which was either wisdom or laziness, and I'd stopped being sure which.

"I told you I was stripped of my order for heresy," I went on. "That's true, in the way that a knife wound is technically a skin condition. Here's the fuller account, since you're about to bet your life on me at a door that only opens for honest blood."

* * *

I was twenty-three, and I was the finest gate-tender my generation produced. I want that on the record, because everything that follows might make you think I was careless. I was not careless. I was *right*, which is infinitely worse.

The garden faith has one doctrine, and it fits in a thimble: the old gods are not dead, only sleeping, and sleep can be tended. We kept the shrines. We kept the small gates—the minor thresholds where a god's attention could seep through like root-water, enough to bless a harvest, heal a fever, tell a shepherd where the lost lamb had fallen. Tending meant pruning. You sang the waking songs quietly. You gave the god a taste of worship and no more. A sip, never a cup.

And for a hundred years, that was enough. Because the Wardens had done their work well, whoever they were—we didn't even have their names anymore—and the great seal held, and the gods dreamed small dreams behind it, and we gardeners trimmed the hedges of their sleep.

Then the dreams started getting louder.

You felt it too—you told me about the shrine in the pass, the stranger's prayer answered out of turn. We all felt it. The gates ran hot. The songs needed singing louder to get the same quiet. And in my valley—in the hidden green place where I was raised, the last flourishing garden of the faith—there was a gate that had been sealed shut for sixty years. Not pruned. *Sealed.* Sealed by my own teacher, Mother Vess herself, with iron and salt and a doctrine I was not permitted to question.

I questioned it anyway. I was twenty-three; questioning was my entire personality.

The village below the valley had a sick child. Fever, the gray-lipped kind, the kind the small blessings sometimes turned but usually didn't. Her mother came up to the shrine every day for nine days and prayed, and every day I sang the waking song at the small gate and got back nothing but silence, because the god behind it was sleeping too deep, or too hungry, or both. And every day I walked past the sealed gate on my way down, and I could *feel* it. Like standing next to a locked room where something breathes.

On the tenth day the child stopped waking up between fevers.

I want to be precise here, because precision is all I have left of my integrity: I did not open the gate to save the child. That's the story I told myself, and it took me four years and one very patient exile to see through it. I opened the gate because I believed the doctrine was wrong. I believed the sealing was fear dressed up as wisdom, that the gods were not dangerous but *starving*, and that a starving thing given bread becomes gentle. I believed I understood the balance better than a hundred years of mothers superior. I was twenty-three, and I was magnificent, and I was wrong in every way a person can be wrong.

The seal was iron and salt and a spoken binding. It took me two nights to unpick. The gate itself was nothing dramatic—a stone arch in a hillside, moss-grown, knee-high, the kind of thing you'd mistake for a sheep crease. I knelt in front of it, and I sang the opening verse of the First Song, the one they only teach you the first half of, and I sang the second half from memory of a text I'd stolen from the forbidden archive, and the arch filled up with light like a doorway full of water.

And something came through.

Not a god. That's what I need you to understand, Yara, because it matters at the door we're walking toward. What sleeps behind the great seal are gods—vast, hungry, half-mad from a century of dark, but still *shaped*, still recognizably the things our ancestors worshipped. But a seal under strain doesn't hold tight. It leaks. And what leaks through first, what comes through the small cracks ahead of the flood, are the things the gods dream. Their appetites, walking around without them. Fragments. Teeth that forgot whose mouth they belonged to.

It looked like a deer, at first. In the light of the gate, everything looks like a deer.

Three pilgrims had come up the valley path that morning—a grandfather and his two grown daughters, come to leave offerings at the spring, ordinary devotion, the whole reason the faith exists. They were fifty feet away when the gate opened. I shouted at them to run. I remember being annoyed, even then, that my voice came out so thin.

I will not describe what happened next. Not because I can't—I can, in perfect detail, that's the cruelty of it—but because you have spent your whole life keeping people safe by staying unseen, and I think you of everyone will understand that some knowledge is a burden you don't hand to another traveler just because the road is long.

When it was finished, the fragment turned and looked at me. It had my teacher's face—it takes faces from your memory, whichever face means *authority*, whichever voice you cannot disobey—and it spoke with Mother Vess's voice, and it said: *more.*

Then the sun came up—or rather, the dawn rites I'd set as a safeguard finally caught, the counter-song, the closing verse—and the gate snapped shut with the fragment halfway through, and what was left on our side of the arch burned away like frost in morning light. The gate sealed itself after that. Permanently. Some doors, once bloodied, lock from their own side.

I buried the pilgrims. I walked down to the village and told the mother her daughter had passed in the night, peacefully, which was even true by then. Then I walked to the Chapterhouse and confessed everything, because the alternative was carrying it alone, and I already knew what carrying things alone does to a person. I'd watched my teacher do it for thirty years.

They stripped my vows. Publicly. And then—here is the part I did not understand until this month—they did not cast me out. They *sent* me. There is a difference. Exile is punishment; assignment is use. Mother Superior Vess came to me the night before I left, and she was old, older than I'd ever allowed myself to see, and she said:

"The seal is failing, child. The Wardens are gone—all but one line, all but one blood, and the order has spent eighty years trying to find it. When we find the heir, the gods will find him too, and they will want him, because his blood is the key and his fear is the handle. Someone must reach him first. Someone who knows what the gates do when they open. Someone who has already paid the price of a mistake, and therefore cannot be tempted twice."

She looked at me with those terrible kind eyes and said, "I am not exiling you, Ulyana. I am arming you."

And then she taught me the burning rites. The ones I used on the thing that wore Baba Sef's face. The ones that are not in any garden hymnal, because they are not for tending. They are for killing what comes through.

"So when I found you," I said to Yara now, "when I traced the sigil to the guildhall with a knife in my sleeve—I wasn't sent to warn you. I was sent to kill you. Cleanly, if possible. Before the gods could use you to open the door. That was my duty. That was the redemption they dangled in front of me: die a faithful woman, wash out the stain, three lives weighed against however many the door would cost." I finally made myself look at him. "I hesitated. One heartbeat. You were mending nets, and you had ash on your cheek, and you looked up at me like I was a customer, and I thought: *this is the key to the end of the world, and he has ash on his cheek.* And then you offered me tea, because you're apparently incapable of suspecting anyone, and I told myself I was gathering intelligence."

"Were you?"

"No. I was deciding." I fed the fire another stick, mostly to have something to do with my hands. "And I have been deciding ever since, every single day, and I am tired of it. So there it is. Three dead pilgrims. One opened gate. One heresy, served warm. I am not the person who tends the garden, Yara. I am the person who broke it. And your grandmother's order trusted me with a knife instead of a watering can, and honestly, they weren't wrong to."

* * *

The fire popped. Somewhere downslope, an owl asked its one question and got no answer.

Yara sat with it. I watched him sit with it, and I realized I was holding my breath like a girl awaiting sentence, and I made myself breathe, because whatever he decided, I refused to meet it half-suffocated.

"You know what I did when I was trapped in the rockslide?" he said at last.

"Told me. The mountain moved."

"The mountain moved because I cursed the old gods by name. Names I'd never been taught. Names I didn't know I knew until they were coming out of my mouth." He turned his marked palm toward the firelight, the green sigil catching it like a stained-glass window. "I've been telling myself since that it was instinct. Blood remembering. But that's not the whole truth, is it? The whole truth is I *wanted* it. I was angry and scared and pinned under half a mountain, and some part of me knew exactly what names to shout and exactly what it would cost, and I shouted them anyway." His jaw worked. "You opened a gate to save a child. I'd open one to save the guild. Don't tell me you're the breaker and I'm the keeper. We're the same person with different weather."

"That is the least comforting thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It's not supposed to comfort you. It's supposed to make you stop waiting for me to condemn you." He leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for a moment the firelight made him look startlingly like the matriarch he'd lost—same stubborn set to the mouth, same refusal to look away from a hard thing. "Here's what I actually heard, in everything you just said. You confessed when you didn't have to. Nobody was making you. The god at the fire didn't pry this out of you; I didn't catch you in a lie. You opened your own gate and told me the worst thing on the other side." He shrugged. "Baba Sef used to say you can smuggle with anyone who tells you what's in the crate. It's the ones who swear it's empty that cut your throat at the border."

"That's a terrible proverb."

"It's a smuggler's proverb. Terrible is load-bearing." He picked up the rusted key, weighed it, and then—to my genuine astonishment—tossed it to me. I caught it on reflex, and it lay in my palm, cold and light as a bird bone. "Hold onto that tonight."

"Why?"

"Because I've been carrying it for weeks and sleeping with my hand around it like it'll whisper the way out. It won't. It's a key. Keys don't work alone." He nodded at the sigil on his own hand, then at me. "Blood and rite. Door and tender. That's how it has to go, if it goes at all. So we may as well start trusting each other with the pieces."

I looked down at the key. Old iron, older ironwork, the kind of thing you'd dig out of a ruin and sell to a collector for a meal. It hummed faintly against my palm—not sound, exactly. More like the feeling of standing near the sealed arch in my valley. Like a locked room where something breathes.

"You understand what you're doing," I said quietly. "You're handing the key to the woman who opened the last one."

"I'm handing the key to the only person I've met who knows what's on the other side and came back to say so." He lay back against his pack, hat over his eyes, conversation apparently concluded. "Wake me for second watch. And Ulyana—Verel."

I went very still.

"The gate that opens kindly," he said, from under the hat. "Your mother had good instincts. Maybe the name's not a warning. Maybe it's instructions."

* * *

I took first watch anyway, though it was his. I sat with the key in my hand and the fire low, and I thought about doors.

My whole life I had believed the lesson of my heresy was *never open anything*. Seal it, salt it, sing it shut, and guard the lock with your own body if you must. Mother Vess armed me with fire and called it wisdom. The order wrote my sin into their archives as a cautionary text, and I let them, because a cautionary tale is at least a shape, and shapeless grief is worse.

But sitting there in the dark with a dead woman's key warming in my traitor's palm, I wondered if I had learned the wrong lesson entirely. Perhaps the fault was never in the opening. A gate that opens kindly—that was the doctrine underneath the doctrine, the one they only whispered to novices who cried at funerals. Doors are not the sin. Doors are the *promise*. The sin is opening them alone, in the dark, for reasons you haven't said out loud, with no one standing beside you who knows your true name and stays anyway.

Yara slept with his marked hand curled loosely open, palm up, the sigil dimming and brightening with his breath like something vegetal and slow. Trusting, even asleep, in a way I had not managed in six years.

Three days to the erased borders. Three days to the door that was not a door.

I fed the fire one last stick, and far off in the high passes—too far to be real, too rhythmic to be wind—I heard something begin, very softly, to sing the first half of the First Song.

Someone else was heading for the door. And they were singing the opening verse.
