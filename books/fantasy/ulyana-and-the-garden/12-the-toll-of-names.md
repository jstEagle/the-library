## 12. The Toll of Names

The gorge announced itself a full hour before we saw it, which in my experience is the only polite thing water has ever done.

You could hear it first—a low grinding roar, like the mountain clearing its throat—and then the smell, wet stone and old iron and something underneath that I didn't have a name for until later. Ulyana stopped at the lip of the trail and went very still, the way she did when her priestess instincts woke up and started shouting over her common sense.

"That's not meltwater," she said.

"It's been raining for three days."

"Yara." She turned to look at me, and rain ran off the edge of her hood between us like a curtain. "Meltwater doesn't sound *angry*."

She had me there. I'd spent my whole life learning to read mountains—the way a scree slope holds its breath before it slides, the way a snow bridge hums when it's about to give—but this was different. This was the sound of something with an opinion.

We came around the shoulder of the ridge and there it was: the Vaskar Gorge, which on every imperial map I'd ever stolen was marked as a dry ravine crossed by a fine stone bridge built some two hundred years ago by engineers who had clearly never met this valley. The bridge was still there. The river was not supposed to be. It filled the gorge wall to wall, brown-green and churning, high enough that its spray reached us where we stood forty feet above what should have been its banks. And through the middle of it, half-drowned but stubborn, the old bridge arched up out of the flood like the back of a sleeping animal.

"Well," I said. "That's new."

"Nothing here is new," Ulyana said quietly. "That's the whole problem. Everything is *old*, and it's waking up, and it's cranky."

I was starting to develop a real appreciation for how she talked about gods the way other people talked about wasps' nests.

* * *

We made camp above the flood line because neither of us was stupid enough to camp below it, and I spent the evening doing what I do best, which is nothing, invisibly. I sat against a boulder with my hood up and watched Ulyana work.

She'd gathered what the storm had knocked down—pine sprigs, a fistful of river stones gone green with moss, one sad white flower that had no business blooming this late in the season and had probably decided to bloom anyway out of spite. She arranged them on a flat rock with the particular fussiness of someone setting a table for a guest who might arrive as a friend or might arrive as a landslide. Then she knelt, and spoke—not loudly, not in any liturgy I recognized, just words, plain ones, like she was talking to a neighbor across a fence.

"We're passing through," she said to the water. "Two travelers, no harm meant. We ask leave to cross, and we'll pay what's fair."

The river roared on. But the roar changed key. I can't explain it better than that—it was the same sound, and it was a completely different sound, the way your own name sounds different when a stranger says it.

Ulyana's shoulders dropped half an inch. "It heard me."

"Is that good?"

"It's never answered me before." She looked back at me, and in the grey light her face had that expression I was coming to know and dread: wonder fighting terror, terror winning on points. "Yara, shrines don't answer. Gods don't answer. For a hundred years the garden faith has been talking into a well and calling the echo a miracle. And now everything I say gets *heard*—"

"Maybe it likes you."

"Nothing that size likes anyone." She turned back to the water. "It wants a toll."

Of course it did. Everything in these mountains wanted something lately. I'd been shaken down by a shrine, extorted by a standing stone, and followed for two days by a crow that I strongly suspected was taking notes. "What kind of toll? Coin?"

"No." She was quiet a moment. "Names."

* * *

Here is what you need to understand about names, if you've never smuggled anything more dangerous than salt.

A name is the first thing you give away and the last thing you keep. I've been Yara Reyes to tollmen, Yara the Quiet to the guild, hey-you to half the border towns in Thornfell, and a dozen other things to a dozen other people depending on what the moment required. A name is a handle. Anyone who has your handle can pick you up.

So when Ulyana explained that the god in the gorge wanted true names as payment for passage, my professional opinion was that we should find another way around. There is always another way around. It might take nine days and involve a goat track and a rope bridge held together by faith and spider silk, but there is always another way around.

Except.

Except Baba Sef was in an imperial cell somewhere south, and every day we spent detouring was a day the burn-teams got closer to the pass, and a day the gods got louder, and a day closer to whatever waited at the heart of the erased borders. Nine days was a luxury we didn't have. I hated that. I hate being rushed into honesty; it's the one commodity I've always hoarded.

"There's another option," Ulyana said, when I told her all this. We were huddled under an overhang with a small fire, because apparently even a river god respects a fire, or at least the smoke gave us privacy. "We don't cross. We go around."

"You said yourself the long route adds a week."

"I said that. I'm also saying—" She pulled her knees up and wrapped her arms around them, and for a moment she looked very young and very tired. "Yara, I've given a god a true name before. Not mine. A pilgrim's. I took three names from three people who trusted me and I fed them to a gate because I believed—I *believed*—it would save more than it cost." Her voice didn't waver. That was somehow worse. "Three pilgrims went into that shrine and something else walked out wearing their voices. So when I tell you a name is a door, I'm not being poetic."

The fire popped. Somewhere below us, the river ground its teeth.

"What happens if you give it your name?" I asked. "Actually. Not the sermon version."

"I don't know. That's the honest answer. The archives say the old river-tolls bound the giver to the water—safe passage, safe return, in exchange for a claim on you. A thread. Something on the other side can always find you by it." She looked at me across the fire. "But you're not going to give it yours, so it hardly matters."

And there it was. The thing I'd been turning over since she said the word *names*, sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone.

Because here's the joke, the ugly punchline my whole life had been building toward: I had already given my name away. Or most of it. When I cursed the old gods under the rockslide—names I shouldn't have known, spilling out of me like water from a cracked jar—the mountain had moved for me, and it had left a green sigil burned into my palm, and Baba Sef had looked at that sigil and said a name out loud that I'd never heard and recognized instantly, the way you recognize your own face in a window at night.

Vesaryan. That was the Warden name. My grandmother's, and hers before her, all the way back to the ones who built the door. And whether I liked it or not, whether I'd agreed to it or not, it was mine now. It had been mine since the mountain answered.

Which meant the choice wasn't whether to give the god my name. The choice was whether to give it honestly, freely—or to stand at that crossing holding a name like a counterfeit coin and hope the god couldn't count.

Gods count.

* * *

Morning came grey and dripping. We went down to the gorge.

Up close the flood was worse—worse and stranger. The water didn't move like water. Oh, it churned and crashed well enough, but underneath the chaos there were patterns, currents that doubled back on themselves in shapes too deliberate to be accidental, eddies spinning in slow spirals like a thinker tapping his fingers. And once, when the spray parted, I saw the bridge properly for the first time, and understood why Ulyana had gone quiet.

Every stone of it was carved. Faces, hundreds of them, worn smooth by centuries of wind—old faces, patient faces, faces with their eyes closed. A bridge of sleepers. And now the river had risen to their chins.

Ulyana set up her little altar on a ledge above the waterline and made her offerings again, and then she stepped back and said, "It's time."

"I know."

"You don't have to—"

"Ulyana." I pulled off my glove. The sigil on my palm had faded to a pale green ghost, but it woke when I flexed my hand, faintly warm, faintly awake, like a cat pretending not to care. "Let me do this. You've paid enough for both of us already."

She stared at me. "You're going to give it the Warden name."

"I'm going to give it *my* name. That's the point, isn't it? True names. Freely given." I laughed, and it came out thinner than I wanted. "Baba Sef used to say the guild runs on small debts. You keep the debts small so nobody owns anybody. Well." I looked at the water. "This one's not going to be small. So it might as well be mine."

I walked to the edge of the ledge. The spray hit me like a thrown bucket. Below, the river heaved and folded over itself, and the carved faces on the bridge seemed, in the shifting light, almost to be listening.

"Hey," I said. Feeling ridiculous. Feeling like a man shouting at weather, which, to be fair, is exactly what I was. "You want a toll. I'm told you take names. So here's mine—freely given, no tricks, no second purse hidden up my sleeve, though believe me the instinct is there."

The river went quiet.

Not calm—quiet. Every current stopped mid-turn. The roar cut off so completely that my ears rang with the absence, and in that ringing silence the whole gorge leaned toward me, the way a room leans toward a confession.

"My name is Yara Reyes," I said. "Smuggler. Salt-runner. Thirteenth blade of the Guild of Small Debts, which makes me the least important person in it, which is exactly how I liked it." My throat tightened. I pushed through. "And my other name—the one I didn't choose, the one that came to me in a rockslide with blood on it—is Vesaryan. Last of the Wardens. Grandson of Maren Vesaryan, who kept a door shut her whole life so the rest of you could sleep."

The silence stretched. Long enough that I started composing exit strategies. Long enough that Ulyana's hand found my sleeve.

Then the river sighed.

There's no better word. The whole mass of it—thousands of tons of angry water—exhaled, and the exhale rolled up the gorge and shook loose a week of rain from the pines, and when the water settled again it had dropped. Ten feet, maybe more. The old bridge emerged stone by stone, sleeper faces blinking free of the flood, and a path opened across it, dry as a road in summer.

And a voice. Not from the water—from everywhere, from the wet stone and the spray and the space between my own heartbeats, low and layered, a voice like a choir heard through a wall.

***Warden's blood.*** A pause, and something moved through the voice that I can only call wonder, though I'd have denied it under oath. ***A century we slept, and the first of the old blood to speak our names does not command. He pays.***

"Funny thing about smuggling," I said, because my mouth does that when my chest is doing something else. "You learn what things cost."

***Few learn it. Fewer pay.*** The water shivered, and I felt it—not heard, *felt*—a thread settle around me, fine as spider silk, tying itself somewhere behind my ribs. The toll, taken. The claim, made. It didn't hurt. It felt like being remembered. ***The debt is accepted, Vesaryan. Cross, and be claimed. What the river knows, the river keeps—and the river knows much.***

We were halfway across the bridge before it spoke again.

I kept my eyes on the stones, because looking down at the churning water through gaps between sleeping faces was a fast way to lose my breakfast, and because I suspected the god preferred to be addressed without eye contact, the way you don't stare at a dog deciding whether to bite. Ulyana walked behind me, murmuring something under her breath—thanks, or apology, or both.

***Warden.*** The voice again, quieter now, almost conversational, which was infinitely worse. ***A gift, since you paid honest. Hear it.***

"I'm listening."

***Another hunts the door you seek.*** The bridge stones seemed to cool beneath my feet. ***It moves fast, and it lies beautifully. It wears a face you trust—an old face, a kind face, a face that fed you and taught you and named you. Do not open for it. Whatever it asks, whatever it promises, whatever tears it weeps—do not open.***

My feet stopped working. Ulyana bumped into my back.

"Whose face?" My voice came out flat and small. "Say it."

***The river keeps what it is given,*** the god said, and there was something in it that might have been pity, if pity could echo. ***And names are given, not taken. Ask her yourself, little smuggler. Ask the one whose shape it stole.***

The rest of the crossing passed in a blur of spray and stone. On the far bank I turned around, and the river was just a river again—loud, indifferent, beautiful in the way disasters are beautiful. The bridge stood empty behind us, the sleeping faces closed-eyed once more.

Ulyana touched my arm. "Yara. What did it—"

"Baba Sef," I said. "It's wearing Baba Sef."

The words tasted like ash. Because here's the thing I hadn't let myself think, not once in three weeks of running: Baba Sef was in an imperial cell. Baba Sef was old, and sick, and the empire did not keep prisoners it considered inconvenient. Every night since the raid I'd carried a small warm coal of hope that she was alive, that we'd get her back, that the guild would sit down to bad stew and worse wine and laugh about the year the empire tried to arrest a grandmother.

And now a god had just told me that the shape I missed most in all the world was walking around out there, hollow, hungry, hunting the same door I was—and that the fastest way to break my heart open was to knock on it wearing her smile.

Ulyana was quiet for a long moment. Then she said, in the careful voice of someone defusing something, "It could be lying. Gods lie."

"No." I pulled my glove back on, over the sigil, over the name. "It just told me the truth for free. That's what scares me." I looked south, toward the pass, toward the erased country, toward whatever was out there moving fast and lying beautifully in the shape of the woman who raised me. "Come on. We need to move faster."

"And if we find her?" Ulyana asked softly. "The real one. Or the thing wearing her. How will you tell?"

I started walking, because the answer was ugly and I didn't want it on my face where she could read it.

I wouldn't. That was the truth of it. I'd walk up to that familiar face with my heart in my hands like an idiot, same as I'd handed my name to the river, because honesty was apparently the only currency I had left—and I could only pray that when the moment came, someone standing beside me would be strong enough to do what I couldn't.

Behind us, the river roared on, keeping its secrets, keeping its claim, keeping me.
