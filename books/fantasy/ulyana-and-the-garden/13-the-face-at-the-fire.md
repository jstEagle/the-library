## 13. The Face at the Fire

There is a particular kind of quiet that lives in the high passes after dark, and I have spent my whole life learning to trust it. The quiet of Thornfell isn't empty. It's the quiet of a country holding its breath, the hush of stone that has been pretending to be only stone for a hundred years. A good smuggler listens to that quiet the way a sailor listens to the sea. It tells you when the road is safe, when the tollmen are sleeping, when the mountain is in a mood.

That night, camped in the hollow below the Widow's Teeth with the river god's warning still sitting in my chest like a swallowed stone, the quiet was wrong.

I noticed it before anything else. Ulyana noticed it too—she was feeding the fire pinches of dried marjoram from her kit, muttering the low words of a warding rite she'd taught me three nights ago, and her hands stopped mid-motion.

"Yara." Just my name. That was enough.

"I hear it," I said. Which was a lie, because it wasn't a sound. It was an absence of sound where there should have been one—the night birds had gone silent all at once, like someone had closed a door on them.

We'd been two days out of the gorge, following the river god's grudging directions toward the erased borders, and we hadn't spoken much about what he'd told us. *Another hunts the door, wearing a familiar face.* Ulyana had wanted to talk about it. I hadn't. Talking about it meant imagining which familiar face, and there were only so many faces I had left, and most of them were either dead or scattered or sold their loyalty to the empire years ago. So I'd done what I do best. I'd stayed small and quiet and carried the thought the way you carry a knife you're afraid to look at directly.

The fire popped. Ulyana finished the warding circle—a lazy thing, half-strength, because we were tired and the herbs were running low and neither of us truly believed anything would come tonight. Gods, in my experience, keep banker's hours. They show up when you're weak, not when you're watchful.

Which is why, when I heard the shuffle of footsteps on the scree above our hollow, my first feeling wasn't fear.

It was relief.

* * *

She came down the slope the way Baba Sef always came down slopes: sideways, favoring her bad hip, one hand out for balance, swearing under her breath in the old guild cant about young people who made camps in places without proper stairs. She was wearing her grey traveling shawl, the one with the mended elbow. Her hair was down, which it never was except at night, and there was ash smudged on her cheekbone the way there was always ash smudged on her cheekbone because the woman could not sit within ten feet of a hearth without collecting some.

"Baba Sef," I said, and I was on my feet before I knew I'd moved.

"Sit down, boy, you'll spill the tea." She lowered herself onto the flat rock across the fire from me with a grunt that I have heard ten thousand times, that specific grunt, the one that started low and ended in something almost like a laugh. "Inquisitors took me through the Kessrin pass. Sloppy work. Their cage-wards were third-rate guild patter—I've seen better seals on jam jars." She held out her hands to the fire. "Walked three days. You know how it is."

I knew how it was. That was the problem. Every word she said was exactly the kind of thing Baba Sef would say. The cadence was right. The cant was right. The way she looked at me over the fire, that appraising squint that had measured every smuggler in Thornfell for forty years—that was right too.

And still the quiet stayed wrong.

"You're staring," she said. "I know I look terrible. Three days walking and no pipe. Do you have any idea what it's like, going without pipe for three days? Your grandmother used to hoard her tobacco like a dragon. Now *there* was a woman who understood priorities."

My heart did something complicated at that. My grandmother. She shouldn't have known to mention her. Except—except everyone in the guild knew Baba Sef talked about the old days, and the old days were full of my grandmother, because my grandmother had been her closest friend before she died, and—

"Ulyana," I said carefully, "this is Baba Sef. Baba Sef, this is Ulyana. Of the garden faith."

"We've met," Ulyana said quietly. "In a manner of speaking."

Baba Sef snorted. "Priestess. They stripped your order off you, didn't they? Heard it from a wool-trader in Vell. Good. Orders are just cages with better singing." She turned back to me, and her eyes caught the firelight and held it, and something about the way they held it made the hair on my arms stand up. "Come here, boy. Let me look at you. Three days thinking they'd caught you too. Three days praying."

"I don't pray," I said automatically.

"No," she agreed, softly. "You never did. And yet."

*And yet.* The river god's voice in my memory: *another hunts the door, wearing a familiar face.*

I sat back down. Slowly. The way you sit down when you realize the floor might not be a floor.

"How did you find us?" I asked. Keep it easy. Keep it small. A smuggler's first rule: never let them see you check the exits.

"The same way I've always found you, Yara Reyes. You leave a trail a blind man could follow. Salt, sweat, and stubbornness." She smiled, and it was her smile, the gap-toothed one, the one that meant she was fond of you and about to make it your problem. "Now. Tell me where you're going. All this rushing about—the priestess, the key, the old roads. Tell me, so I can help. That's what I'm for, isn't it? Forty years of keeping thirteen fools alive. Let me do it a while longer."

And there it was. Not a demand. Not a threat. Just Baba Sef, asking to be useful, asking to be included, the way she'd asked a thousand times, and every instinct I had was screaming at me to tell her everything, because that's what I did, that's who I was—I was the invisible man, but I had never once been invisible to *her*, and if the world was going to take everything else it could at least leave me the one person who—

"Where's your pipe?" I asked.

Her smile flickered. Just for a heartbeat. "What?"

"Your pipe. You walked three days. You complained about it. Where is it?" Baba Sef had not gone a day without her pipe since before I was born. She'd been captured by imperial inquisitors mid-draw; the guards at the guildhall raid had found her sitting in her chair, smoking, waiting for them with the calm of a woman who had already hidden everything worth finding. If she'd escaped, she'd have escaped with the pipe. She'd have escaped with the pipe or died trying, and probably died trying *while smoking*.

"I lost it," she said. "On the road."

"On the road," I repeated.

"Things get lost on roads, boy."

"They do," I said. My mouth had gone dry. "They do get lost."

Ulyana had gone very still beside me. In the firelight I saw her hand drift, casual as adjusting her sleeve, toward the pouch of consecrated salt at her belt. She'd understood before me. Of course she had. She'd spent her life around things that wore shapes.

"Tell me about the night my grandmother died," I said.

Something shifted behind Baba Sef's eyes. It was very small, like a fish turning under ice, but I saw it. "You know how she died. The fever winter."

"Do I? Because I remember it differently. I remember you telling me she went up the mountain alone and came back down changed, and you never said changed *how*, and I was eleven and I didn't ask because I was eleven. So tell me now. Tell me what changed."

The silence stretched. The fire crackled. Somewhere in that crackle, underneath it, I heard—or felt, or dreamed—a sound like a door being tried in its frame. Like something vast and patient pressing its weight against a lock, testing whether the wood had rotted.

"Boy," Baba Sef said, and her voice was still her voice, warm and rough and tired, and that was the worst part, that was the unforgivable part, "you look at me like I'm a stranger. I changed your swaddling clothes. I taught you to read tollmen's ledgers. I held your hand at her grave. Come here. Let me hold your hand now. You're shaking."

I was shaking. I realized I was crying, too, which was humiliating, and I wiped my face with my sleeve and hated myself for both.

Because here is the thing nobody tells you about grief: it doesn't check credentials. Love doesn't ask to see papers. Some deep animal part of me did not care what this thing was, did not care about wards or rites or gods in borrowed skin—it heard that voice say *come here* and every year of my life stood up inside me and wanted to obey. Forty years of smuggler's caution against twenty-five years of being loved by one specific woman, and it was not a fair fight.

If Ulyana hadn't moved when she moved, I would have crossed that fire.

* * *

Later—and I mean later, hours later, sitting wrapped in a blanket with my hands around a cup of tea I couldn't drink—Ulyana told me what happened in the space of about four seconds, because I honestly don't remember most of it.

She said I stood up. She said I took one step toward the fire, toward *her*, and that Baba Sef's face did something no human face can do—everything in it sliding toward joy at once, greedily, hungrily, like a mask being pulled tight from behind. She said the shadows around the hollow leaned inward, all of them, at once, like an inhale.

And she said she threw the salt.

Not the careful, ritual scattering of the warding texts. A fistful, hurled, straight through the fire so that it burst across the thing wearing Baba Sef's shape in a hissing arc of white. And she shouted—not words, not exactly, more like the *shape* of words, the grammar of a language that predates language, the garden-rites her order had forbidden her and then, apparently, failed to burn out of her spine. The fire flared green. The shadows snapped back. And the thing across from us stopped pretending.

It didn't become monstrous. That's what I'll never be able to explain to anyone who wasn't there. It simply stopped being Baba Sef, the way an actor stepping off a stage stops being the king. What sat on the rock was a shape roughly her size, roughly her outline, and its face was smooth as an egg, and where its eyes had been there were two points of light like stars seen through deep water, and it looked at me with those drowned stars and it said, in a voice that was every voice it had ever stolen layered together:

"*So small a vessel. So much old blood.*"

Then it looked at Ulyana—at the salt burning white trails down its borrowed shoulders—and something like amusement moved through it, the way weather moves across a valley.

"*Gardener,*" it said to her. "*You of all of them. You opened a little door once. We remember. We kept what came through.*"

Ulyana's face went grey. I'd never seen her grey before. She threw the second handful of salt anyway.

The thing stood. It was taller standing than Baba Sef had ever been, or maybe it just seemed that way. And it turned to me last, and it tilted the smooth egg of its head, and it said—gently, horribly gently, in a voice that was almost hers:

"*The key turns east of the drowned surveyor's stone, where the borders were cut. Thank you for carrying it so faithfully, Warden. We will not need to ask again.*"

And the fire went out. Not died down. *Out*, all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the dark that rushed in was total and cold and full of the sound of something enormous moving away up the mountain, unhurried, satisfied, taking long strides that shook loose little avalanches of scree.

Then the night birds started again, all at once, embarrassed, and the quiet was only quiet.

* * *

I don't know how long I sat in the dark before Ulyana got the fire rebuilt. Long enough that my tears had dried stiff on my face. When the light came back she was crouched across from me, feeding twigs to the flame with the mechanical patience of someone whose hands needed a job, and she wouldn't look at me, and I understood why.

"It used what I told it," I said finally. My voice sounded like someone else's. "At the river. I gave my name freely, and it—" I stopped. Started again. "No. Before that. It watched us. It's been watching since the gorge. Everything we said, everything I—" I pressed the heels of my hands into my eyes. "It knew about the surveyor's stone, Ulyana. It knew about the drowned marker. That's from the maps. That's from the cartographer's archive. Nobody knows that. Nobody alive."

"It read it out of you," Ulyana said. Quietly. Not unkindly, which somehow made it worse. "When you stepped toward it. When you wanted it to be her. Wanting opens people, Yara. That's the oldest truth in my order's archives and the reason half of them are forbidden. It didn't trick the location out of you. You offered it. A gift. Freely given." She looked up, and her eyes were red-rimmed and steady. "The same way you gave the river god your name."

"So it knows where the door is."

"It knows where the door *is*. It doesn't know how to open it. It needs you for that—your blood, your choice, your hand on the lock. That's why it wore her. That's why it didn't just take the knowledge and leave you a corpse on the mountain." She poked the fire harder than the fire deserved. "It can't force a Warden's hand. It never could. That was the whole point of the seal. But it can *want* you, and herd you, and wait for you to arrive exhausted and grieving and willing to trade anything to make the hurting stop."

I sat with that. Around us the mountains breathed, ordinary and indifferent, and somewhere far off a fox screamed, and it was just a fox, and I flinched anyway.

"She's still alive," I said. "Baba Sef. The real one. It said *we kept what came through*—no, that was about her heresy. But the inquisitors took her alive. She was alive when they took her." I heard my own voice climbing and made it stop. Made it small. Made it flat. "She's alive, and that thing knows her face perfectly, which means it got her face from somewhere, which means—"

"Which means it has seen her recently," Ulyana finished. "Or is seeing her still. Gods that wear shapes need the original close, at least at first. Old doctrine. The fresher the grief, the truer the mask." She hesitated. "Yara. There's something else. What it said about me. About the shrine-gate. About keeping what came through."

"I heard."

"My order sent me to kill you because they believed I was already compromised. Already marked. I told you I opened a gate and three pilgrims died. I never told you what the records said came through, or where it went after." She stared into the fire. "I think it went *down*. Into the old places. I think it's been down there a hundred years with the rest of them, and I think that thing just told me it's still carrying my mistake around like a favorite toy."

For a long moment neither of us spoke. Then I laughed. It came out cracked and awful, halfway to a sob, and I didn't try to stop it, because stopping it would have required energy I did not have.

"So we're even," I said. "You let something through once. I nearly opened the whole door tonight because I missed my grandmother's best friend." I put my head back against the rock and looked up at the stars, which were doing their usual excellent job of being enormous and indifferent. "It learned enough, you said. It knows the place. It'll be ahead of us now. Moving fast, in ways we can't. And when we get there—if we get there—it'll be waiting, wearing whatever face hurts most."

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"And Baba Sef is in an imperial cell somewhere, and the empire wants to burn these mountains, and the gods want to use me as a crowbar, and my own stupid loving heart is a security risk."

"Yes," Ulyana said again. Then, softer: "I'm sorry. About the mask. About what it did to your memory of her. That's the cruelest thing they do. They don't just steal faces. They poison the well, so you can't drink from it again without tasting them."

She was right. That was the worst of it. I would never again hear that grunt, that gap-toothed laugh, that *sit down, boy, you'll spill the tea*, without checking, without doubting, without some cold starlit thing swimming up under the memory. It had taken the one safe room in my house and I would never get it back, not entirely, not clean.

But.

But Baba Sef had raised smugglers, and smugglers learn early that a mark who steals from you leaves fingerprints, and thieves always overplay what they've stolen. The thing had known the surveyor's stone. It had known the maps. But it had gotten the pipe wrong. It had stumbled on a dead woman's tobacco habit, the smallest, stupidest detail, because it had taken her face from her fear and her love and her voice, and it had never bothered to learn what she carried in her pocket.

Which meant the real Baba Sef was still in there somewhere, still herself, still hoarding her small treasures, still waiting.

I stood up. My legs shook. I stood up anyway.

"Pack the camp," I said. "We walk tonight. It thinks it's ahead of us. Good. Let it set its trap at the door while we come at the borders from the north, the long way, the way no god would bother watching because no map shows it." I looked down at Ulyana, and I felt something settle in my chest that I recognized from the worst jobs of my smuggling life—not courage exactly, but its ugly cousin, the thing that shows up when you've got nothing left to lose and a debt to collect. "And Ulyana—teach me the salt rite. All of it. Not the half-version. If it wears another face at me, I want to be the one who throws."

She studied me for a moment, firelight moving on her face. Then she nodded once and reached for her pouch.

Above us, far up the black shoulder of the mountain, something that was not wind moved through the scree—one long, patient stride, then another—heading east, toward the drowned stone, toward the door, unhurried as a debt coming due.

We broke camp and walked the other way, into the dark, carrying our griefs like contraband.
