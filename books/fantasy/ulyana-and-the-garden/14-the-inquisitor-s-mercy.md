## 14. The Inquisitor's Mercy

I had always imagined that if the empire finally caught me, it would happen in some dramatic fashion—a rooftop chase, a knife at my throat, at minimum a decent view of the sunset I was about to be denied. Instead, Dame Corvane caught us on a Tuesday, in a sheep pasture, while Yara was arguing with a ewe.

"You can't just *take* that," he was saying to the animal, which had claimed his bedroll and showed no intention of surrendering it. "That's theft. That's—do you know what I do for a living? I'm a professional thief. You're making me look bad."

The ewe chewed his blanket with the serene confidence of a creature that has never once been audited.

"Maybe let it go," I said from the fire, where I was doing my best with a pot of barley and a bad attitude. "It's clearly starving."

"It's clearly *round*."

"Round is a state of mind."

And that was when the pasture's other occupants—the ones we'd assumed were distant trees, or perhaps very patient boulders—stood up and became soldiers.

Six of them, in imperial grey, moving with the unhurried coordination of people who had been watching us for hours and were only now bored enough to stop. Yara dropped the argument with the ewe mid-sentence and went still in that particular way of his, the way that made him hard to look at even when you were looking directly at him. His invisibility wasn't magic, mostly. It was a talent for being forgettable, for occupying the exact weight of background. It had kept him alive through twenty years of tollmen and toll-gates.

It did nothing against six crossbows already leveled.

"Hands," said one soldier, unnecessarily. Our hands were, by this point, extremely visible.

Yara's eyes found mine across the fire. In them: the question. *Do we run?*

We did not run. Running from crossbows in open ground is less an escape plan than a donation. I raised my hands slowly and said, in my most reasonable voice, "Before anyone does anything regrettable, I should mention that I am a priestess."

"Stripped of her order," Yara added helpfully.

"*Formerly* stripped," I corrected. "There are appeals."

"There really aren't," he said.

The soldiers did not care about our theological employment status. They bound our wrists with cord rather than iron—which I noted, because iron is for heretics and cord is for witnesses—and marched us up the slope toward the treeline, where a grey pavilion stood among the pines like something that had grown there out of bureaucracy.

And beside the pavilion, waiting, stood Dame Corvane.

I had heard of her, of course. Everyone in my former order had heard of Corvane. She was the Inquisition's answer to questions nobody wanted asked: the woman they sent when a problem required either solving or burying, and she hadn't yet decided which. She was older than her reputation suggested—silver at the temples, a face like a closed ledger—and she held herself with the terrible calm of someone who has already read your file, your correspondence, and possibly your diary.

"Ulyana Vess," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like a verdict being notarized. "Disgraced priestess of the Garden faith. Heretic. Apostate." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "And, as of four months ago, accomplice to the Warden heir."

Yara made a small sound beside me, the sound of a man whose entire survival strategy had just been read aloud in front of him.

"I don't know what you mean," he said. "I'm a salt smuggler."

"Mm." Corvane looked at him the way one looks at a lock one intends to pick eventually but isn't in any hurry about. "Show me your hand."

He didn't move. One of the soldiers shifted, and the crossbow spoke its opinion, and Yara, sighing like this was all terribly inconvenient, turned his right palm upward.

The sigil caught the afternoon light. Green, fine-lined, rooted in his skin like something growing there rather than burned there—a vine, or a key, or both. I had watched it fade over months to a faint watermark, and I felt my stomach drop as I saw it now: bright again. Fresh. The gods were getting closer, or he was getting closer to them, and either way the mark knew.

Corvane studied it for a long moment. Then she did something no inquisitor in any story I'd ever been told had ever done.

She sat down.

Not on a throne, not on a judgment seat—on a plain camp stool, beside a folding table bearing a teapot and two cups. She poured tea into both cups, considered, and poured a third.

"Sit," she said. "Both of you. If you try to flee, my soldiers will shoot you in the legs first, because I want you ambulatory. This is not a threat; it is logistics."

We sat. It seemed rude not to, given the tea.

* * *

Here is what I knew about the Inquisition before that afternoon: they burned things. Shrines, books, priests who wandered too far from sanctioned doctrine. I had spent three years after my exile flinching at grey cloaks in market crowds. I had built my whole understanding of the empire around the idea that it was a machine for forgetting—that its great project was smoothing the world flat, paving over anything old and hungry and true.

So when Corvane set down her cup and said, "How long have the gods been waking?" I confess my first thought was that this was a trap with unusually comfortable seating.

"A year," I said carefully. "Perhaps longer. The shrine at Marrowfen took root last spring. Before that—" I stopped myself. Confessing knowledge was one thing. Confessing *my* knowledge was another.

"Before that, the one at Hollow Bridge," Corvane said. "And the river god at the Gorge of Names, which drowned eleven surveyors in autumn and then politely returned their bodies arranged in order of seniority." She watched me over the rim of her cup. "You know these things because your order keeps forbidden archives, and you read them, which is half of why you were exiled. The other half being the incident at the pilgrim gate."

Yara turned to look at me. We had not spoken of it. He knew there was something—I had told him that much, on the road, in the dark, in the language of people negotiating trust in installments—but he did not know the shape of it, and now here it was, laid out on an inquisitor's folding table between the teapot and the biscuits.

"Yes," I said. "That's the other half."

"Good," said Corvane. "Then you'll understand why I believe you. Because you have seen what comes through a gate that opens even a crack. And you are, whatever else you are, the only person in this army's reach who knows what's coming well enough to describe it."

"And what's coming?" Yara asked. His voice was careful. I recognized the tone; it was the one he used with tollmen, the one that said *I am nobody, please continue underestimating me.*

Corvane set down her cup. When she spoke again, the bureaucratic calm had thinned, and underneath it was something I had not expected to find in an inquisitor's voice.

Fear.

"The empire has known for fourteen months," she said. "Since Hollow Bridge. There is a division of the Chancellery—no name, no seal, funded through drainage commissions—that has been cataloguing every answered prayer in the border provinces. Every shrine that takes root. Every child born with green eyes in a village where no one has green eyes. Every rockslide that moves *the wrong way.*" Her gaze flicked to Yara's palm. "They call them anomalies. There are forty-one documented. Forty-one gods, stirring in their graves, remembering that they were once worshipped and deciding they would like to be again."

"And the empire's response?" I asked, though I already felt the answer coming, the way you feel weather in an old scar.

Corvane looked at the mountains. North and east, the Thornfells climbed into cloud, blue-grey and indifferent, hiding in their folds a dead country and a locked door and everything I had betrayed my order to protect.

"Fire," she said.

The word sat there. The ewe, wandering past the edge of camp, bleated once, absurdly, and no one laughed.

"The Chancellery's term is 'controlled sterilization of anomalous terrain,'" Corvane continued. "In practice: burn-teams. Fire-priests of the Imperial Rite, the kind that haven't been fielded since the conquest. They will burn the high passes, the valleys, the shrines, the villages. Everything within the old borders of Thornfell and a generous margin beyond." She paused. "The margin includes approximately nine thousand people. Smugglers, shepherds, a town called Lowmark that I understand makes excellent cheese. The Chancellery's assessment is that the population density is insufficient to justify the risk of leaving any vessel, shrine, or bloodline intact."

Yara had gone very still. Not his invisible-still, his *other* still—the one where I could feel the heat coming off him like off a struck flint.

"Baba Sef," he said quietly. "My guild. The people in those passes—they're not vessels. They're *people.* Old women. Children. A man named Doss who cries at weddings."

"I know," Corvane said. "I wrote the minority report."

* * *

I will say this for Dame Corvane: she did not pretend to be a good person, which in my experience is the closest thing to honesty the powerful ever offer.

"I have served the Inquisition for thirty years," she told us, refilling cups neither of us had touched. "I have burned shrines. Real ones—empty ones, mostly, but I tell myself *mostly* and I sleep, more or less. I believed the old faiths were dangerous superstition and the garden heresy was sentimental nonsense about vegetables." A dry pause. "Then Hollow Bridge happened, and I was sent to assess it, and I stood in front of a god for the first time in my life."

"What was it like?" Yara asked. And I understood why he asked—not for tactics, but because he needed to know whether someone else had felt what he felt. Whether the terror was survivable.

"Like being remembered," Corvane said, "by something enormous." She turned her cup a quarter-turn on its saucer, precise as everything else about her. "It looked at me and it knew my grandmother's name. A woman dead sixty years, whom I never told anyone about, whom I barely admitted to myself I loved. And it said, in a voice like roots splitting stone: *you kept the little fires going. Thank you.*" Her jaw tightened. "Then it asked me to open the bridge gate so it could see the sky, and when I refused, it thanked me for that too. For keeping it safe from itself. Do you understand? It was *grateful.* These things waking in the mountains—they are not simply monsters. They are prisoners who have forgotten why they were imprisoned, and gratitude curdles fast in a cage."

"That's why the burn order frightens you," I said. "It won't work."

"It will work exactly long enough to make everything worse." Corvane's voice flattened. "Fire hurts them. Fire also feeds them—pain, terror, ten thousand prayers screaming at once as the villages go up. You would be delivering the Chancellery a feast and calling it an exorcism. Within a decade we would have forty-one gods fully awake, fully fed, and *furious*, loose in a country whose entire religious infrastructure consists of tax collectors and artillery." She met my eyes. "I have spent a year trying to make the Chancellery understand this. I have filed reports. I have requested audiences. Last month my minority report was reclassified as evidence of my own anomalous sympathies, and I was given a choice: lead the vanguard burn-team into the Thornfells, or be relieved of command and examined."

"And you chose to lead," Yara said.

"I chose to lead," she agreed, "because a vanguard commander can march slowly. Can misfile a valley. Can lose a day here, a road there." Something almost wry crossed her closed-ledger face. "I have become, at fifty-three, precisely the sort of smuggler you are, Master Reyes. I traffic in delays."

The rain started then, a thin grey drizzle that hissed against the pavilion canvas, and the soldiers at the treeline turned their backs to it with the discipline of men pretending not to listen. Corvane rose, went to a campaign chest, and returned with a leather case, which she opened on the table.

Inside: a fragment of map. Old paper, old ink, drawn in a hand that predated the empire's redrawing of the world. I leaned forward despite myself, and Yara forgot entirely to be invisible, and for a moment we were just two thieves and a saint looking at stolen goods.

It was a corner of old Thornfell. I recognized the cartography from the archives—the pre-imperial surveys, the ones that mapped a country the empire had decided, administratively, did not exist. Mountain ranges with their true names. Valleys marked in green ink. And at the fragment's torn edge, half a symbol: a circle broken by a keyway.

"This came from the surveyor drowned at the Gorge of Names," Corvane said. "The river god returned him arranged by seniority, as I mentioned. It also returned his effects. To me. Personally." She let that settle. "A god gave me a piece of a map and, I believe, a message: *some doors should be found by the right people before the wrong ones.*"

"The door," Yara said hoarsely. "You know about the door."

"I know the Chancellery believes it is an exit, and wants it found and destroyed. I know the garden archives—" a glance at me, "—which your former order has been sharing with my division for a year, Priestess, yes, we read each other's heresies—suggest it is something else entirely. A lock. And I know that the man sitting in front of me with a growing sigil on his hand is the last living key." She closed the case and slid it across the table to him. "Which means the most dangerous place in the empire is wherever you stand. And the second most dangerous place is wherever I am ordered to burn."

"Why?" Yara asked. "Why give us this? Why not take me to the Chancellery yourself—turn me in, collect whatever they pay for Warden heirs?"

For the first time, Dame Corvane smiled, and it was not a comforting sight, but it was an honest one.

"Because I have read your file, Master Reyes, and your file is a list of small kindnesses committed competently in secret. Because the priestess beside you opened one gate and has spent every day since trying to close others. And because I am an old woman who has just learned that her employers intend to solve theology with arson, and I find I would rather bet on the heretics." She stood, and the audience was over; you could feel it end the way a door closes. "You have perhaps three weeks before the burn-teams mobilize. Less, if the gods wake faster than projected, and they are. Find your door. Whatever you do with it—lock it, open it, marry it—I don't care. Make the anomaly disappear from my reports. Give the Chancellery nothing to burn."

"And if we fail?" I asked.

"Then I will burn the mountains as ordered," Corvane said, "and I will spend what remains of my life making sure the empire remembers, in detail, what it chose." She turned to her soldiers. "Release them. Return their effects. The priestess's knife as well." A beat. "Yes, all of them, Sergeant, I counted."

* * *

They walked us to the edge of the pasture as the drizzle thickened into honest rain. At the treeline, Corvane paused beside me, and for a moment the inquisitor fell away and there was only a tired woman in a wet cloak.

"The incident at the pilgrim gate," she said, low. "Three dead. I know. It is in the files I am about to lose access to." She looked north, at the hidden valleys. "Guilt is a poor compass, Priestess, but it is better than none. Most people navigate by nothing at all."

Then she turned back to her tent, her tea, her slow march toward a fire she meant to delay, and we were alone in the rain with a dead country's map and three weeks.

Yara stared down at the leather case in his hands like it might bite. "An inquisitor," he said finally, "just gave us contraband, tea, and relationship advice."

"She gave us a deadline."

"She gave us *biscuits,* Ulyana. I'm keeping the biscuits." But his joke had no floor under it, and we both knew why. Three weeks. Nine thousand people. Forty-one gods counting themselves awake in the dark.

He opened the case one more time, and the rain ran down the old ink, and the broken circle with its keyway seemed, in the grey light, almost to turn.

"We need the rest of the map," he said. "And I think I know a dead surveyor who left more than one archive."

Behind us, faint through the rain, the ewe was still eating his bedroll. Some losses, I reflected, follow a man across empires.

We walked north, into the country that officially did not exist, carrying a map of it anyway.
