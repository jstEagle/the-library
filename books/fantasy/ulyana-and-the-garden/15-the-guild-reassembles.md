## 15. The Guild Reassembles

There is a particular kind of silence that follows a raid. I'd heard it twice before in my life—once when the empire burned the Salt Road waystation, once when my grandmother's name was scraped off a family ledger I was never supposed to see—and both times it had sounded like this: like a room holding its breath around an absence.

The guildhall was still standing. That surprised me more than anything else. The inquisitors had taken Baba Sef, overturned every barrel of salted fish, cracked open the false floor where we kept the real ledgers, and left. They hadn't torched it. That was either mercy or arithmetic—burning a building draws attention, and attention is the one thing the empire's inquisitors are trained never to waste.

I stood across the ravine at dusk and watched the windows for light. None came. Thirteen smugglers had lived there, or twelve now, counting what they'd done to Baba Sef, and not one of them would risk a candle until they were certain the hawks were gone.

I counted to three hundred, then crossed the ravine the long way, through the scree, because old habits die harder than old gods.

Inside, the hall smelled of ash and spilled brine. Chairs upended. Baba Sef's chair—the big one by the hearth, the one she'd had carried up the mountain on the backs of four mules because she refused to be carried herself—sat empty in the middle of the room, positioned exactly where the inquisitors had dragged her out of it. Someone had righted everything else. No one had touched that chair.

I understood why. I didn't want to touch it either.

"They took her south," said a voice from the dark of the pantry, and I nearly put a knife through Kessrin's throat before my brain caught up with my hand. He emerged looking like a man who'd been sleeping in a flour barrel, which, judging by the dust on his shoulders, he had been. "Two riders, one wagon. Headed for the garrison at Vell's Crossing."

"How many made it out?"

"Nine." He held up fingers as if I couldn't count. "Denna and the twins went east over the Kettle Pass before the wagons even arrived. Old Muro's holed up in the salt caves—he won't come down for anyone. And Petrik—" Kessrin's face did something complicated. "Petrik didn't make it."

I closed my eyes. Petrik, who taught me to pick a lock with a wishbone when I was nine. Petrik, who talked too much and laughed too loud and was, in retrospect, always going to die of talking too much to the wrong soldier. "How?"

"Fast," Kessrin said, which was the only kindness available.

I nodded and let the grief sit where it fell. There wasn't time to carry it properly. There never is; you just learn to walk with it dragging behind you like a sled full of stones.

"Where are the others?"

"Scattered, like Baba Sef said. She told us—before they came, she must have known, she *told* us—'if the gray coats come, everyone goes to ground and no one plays the hero.' " Kessrin snorted. "She said it looking straight at you, if you're wondering."

"I'm not wondering." I was, a little. Baba Sef had spent sixty years teaching thirteen criminals how to be invisible, and in her last free hour she'd looked at me and said *no heroes*, because she knew better than anyone what I was becoming. What the gods had already decided I was.

"Well," I said. "We're going to have to disappoint her."

* * *

It took me four days to gather them, and I want it noted for posterity that I did it the hard way: walking, in the open, on roads I'd spent my whole life avoiding.

That was the part nobody tells you about leading. Hiding is easy. Hiding is a craft, and I was a master of it—I could walk through a market square and leave no more impression than weather. But you cannot lead from inside a shadow. A shadow can't stand in front of frightened people and say *follow me*. A shadow has no face for them to look at while they decide whether to trust you.

So I walked in the open, and I let myself be seen, and every minute of it felt like walking a ledge over a gorge.

I found Denna first, at a shrine two valleys east—the same roadside shrine, as it happened, where a stranger's prayer had first taught me that the mountains could answer back. She was sitting cross-legged in front of it, feeding dried fruit into the offering bowl with the grim efficiency of a woman paying protection money.

"You know these things work again," she said without turning around. "Saw it myself. Prayed for rain last month and got a flood. So now I only pray for small things. Carefully worded things."

"Denna."

"Don't 'Denna' me, Yara Reyes. Baba Sef said no heroes, and here you come up the road in broad daylight wearing your face where anyone can see it." She finally turned. Her eyes went to my right hand—to the green sigil burned into the palm, faintly luminous even in daylight, like moss grown over a scar. "And there it is. The thing the gray coats are tearing the country apart looking for."

"It's also the thing that's going to save it."

"That so." She stood, brushed off her knees, and looked at me the way she used to look at ledgers that didn't balance. "Say it plain, then. You owe us that much."

So I said it plain. I told her about the door—not where, not yet, but what: a lock older than the empire, built by my grandmother's blood to keep the old gods caged, failing now because the bloodline had thinned to me and me alone. I told her about Ulyana, the priestess with a knife who'd become something else, though I couldn't have told you what, and neither could she. I told her about Dame Corvane and the burn order—the empire's plan to set fire to the Thornfells, all of them, every valley and cave and shrine, to erase the anomaly the way they'd erased the country itself.

"And the plan," Denna said slowly, "is for you to walk into the middle of a dead country that officially does not exist, pursued by gods and inquisitors, and do something with a door."

"Yes."

"While the empire burns the mountains down around us."

"Someone has to hold the passes while I go. Slow the burn-teams down. Block the roads. Be inconvenient." I heard the words coming out of my mouth and recognized their shape: they were Baba Sef's words, the guild's whole doctrine turned inside out. Sixty years of *stay small, stay unseen, stay indispensable*, and I was asking nine smugglers to become the largest, most visible, most inconvenient thing in the Thornfells.

"They'll kill us," Denna said. Not a question. An inventory.

"Some of us, maybe. Probably. If we're good, most of us live." I spread my marked hand open between us, green sigil and all. "But if I don't reach that door, everyone dies anyway. Every valley, every village, every smuggler and farmer and tollman and child. The gods get out, or the fire gets in. Those are the choices now. I didn't make them. I'm just the only one who can walk through the middle of them."

Denna looked at my hand for a long moment. Then she spat in her palm and held it out—an old road oath, older than the guild, older probably than the empire.

"For Baba Sef," she said. "Not for you. Don't let it go to your head."

"It went to my head weeks ago," I said, and shook.

* * *

Kessrin found the twins at a cockfight outside Vell's Crossing, of all the reckless, beautiful places. Muro took more doing—I had to descend into the salt caves alone, in the dark, calling his name in the old cadence we used for signaling, until his voice floated back out of the black: *"If that's the gray coats, I've got a pickaxe and poor impulse control."*

"It's Yara."

A long pause. Then, grudgingly, from somewhere in the deep: "The one the mountain likes. Come in, then. Mind the third shaft, it's been drinking."

Old Muro sat among the salt pillars like a troll in a story, white-bearded and salt-crusted, and listened to the whole plan without interrupting, which from Muro was unprecedented and slightly alarming. When I finished, he was quiet for so long I thought he'd fallen asleep sitting up.

"My grandfather cut salt in these hills under the old kingdom," he said finally. "Before the empire redrew the maps. He used to say there were doors in the high country that weren't for men. Said his own father had helped build a wall once, up past the glaciers, and came home gray-haired and wouldn't say why." He fixed me with eyes gone pale as brine. "You're telling me that wall's coming down."

"Failing. And the empire means to answer it with fire."

Muro nodded slowly, the way mountains nod, which is to say barely at all. "Then I'll hold the low cave mouths. Fire doesn't like stone, and I don't like fire, and between us we should manage." He paused. "Your grandmother. Sef says—said—she was Warden blood."

"She was."

"Then your family owes these hills a debt, boy, and I intend to watch you pay it." He waved me off toward the light. "Go on. Go lead. And Yara—" I stopped at the mouth of the shaft. "When you see her again, wherever they've got her—you tell her the guild held. Tell her we held."

I promised. It was the easiest lie I ever told, because I intended it so completely.

* * *

By the seventh night we were nine, gathered in the ruined chapel above the Kettle Pass—a place the garden faith had abandoned a century ago and the empire had never bothered to notice, which made it, briefly, the safest room in the Thornfells. Rain came down outside in ropes. Someone had found candles. Someone else had found wine, and I chose not to ask how.

Ulyana stood apart from them near the broken altar, hood back, arms folded, watching my people the way a foreigner watches a language being spoken fluently around her. She'd argued against this the whole climb up. *Smugglers can't fight soldiers. Nine knives against burn-teams with flame-casters. This is a eulogy with extra steps.* She wasn't wrong, exactly. She was just measuring the wrong thing.

"All right," I said, and the room quieted in a way that startled me. These were people I'd spent years moving among unseen—slipping contraband past them, covering their mistakes, taking my cut to Baba Sef. They'd never once had to look at me and decide anything. Now nine faces turned toward mine, lit candle-yellow, waiting.

I laid the map fragment on the altar stone. Corvane's gift: a corner of the imperial survey, showing the erased borders of old Thornfell, and at their heart, circled in the inquisitor's own hand, a mark I now knew was the door.

"The burn-teams mass at Vell's Crossing within the fortnight," I said. "Three companies, flame-casters, enough oil to give the mountains a sunset that'll be visible from the capital. They move up the Kettle Pass and the Salt Road both, burning valley by valley, because somewhere in these hills is a prayer-signature they can't explain and an anomaly they can't allow." I tapped the map. "I go here. Through the erased country, to the door. Alone with Ulyana, because whatever waits there answers to Warden blood and no one else's."

"And us?" asked one of the twins.

"You hold the passes. Not win—hold. Rockfalls on the switchbacks. Burned bridges over the gorges. False fires on the wrong ridgelines so their scouts read the terrain wrong. You've spent your lives making small things disappear; now you're going to make large ones appear where they aren't. Every day you slow them is a day I'm deeper into country their maps say isn't there." I looked around the chapel. "I won't dress it up. Some of you may not come home. I'd be lying if I promised otherwise, and after Petrik, none of us has the stomach for more lies than necessary."

Silence, except the rain.

Then Kessrin stood up, raised his cup of stolen wine, and said, "For Baba Sef."

"For Baba Sef," said Denna.

"For Baba Sef," said Muro, who had climbed out of his salt caves to be there, and whose voice cracked on it, and pretended it hadn't.

They drank. All nine. And then the twins started arguing about which bridge would burn best, and Denna began sketching rockfall points on the back of a tithe receipt, and Muro started explaining, at length, to anyone who'd listen, precisely how salt caves defeat fire, and just like that it wasn't a funeral anymore. It was a plan. It was the guild, reassembled around a purpose instead of a profit, and I stood at the broken altar watching them and felt something turn over in my chest that I didn't have a name for yet.

Ulyana came to stand beside me. Close enough that our shoulders almost touched.

"You realize what you've done," she said quietly.

"Condemned eight people to hold a pass?"

"You've stopped hiding." She nodded at the room—at Kessrin laughing, at Denna drawing lines in candlelight. "Sixty years your guild survived by being nothing. By being weather. And you just made them into a wall with a name on it. Walls get besieged, Yara. Weather just moves on."

"I know."

"Do you? Because there's a version of you that walks away tonight. Takes me, takes the key, slips into the erased country invisible, the way you've done everything your whole life. The guild scatters again, the empire burns what it burns, and you survive. You're *good* at surviving. It might even work."

I watched Denna fold her map and tuck it into her coat, watched her catch my eye and give me the smallest nod—the nod of a woman committing herself to a losing hand because the pot was worth it.

"No," I said. "There isn't a version of me that walks away anymore. I checked."

"When?"

"The night they took her." My voice came out rougher than I meant it to. "Baba Sef bought my escape with her freedom. Sixty years of staying small, and the last thing she did with it was make herself enormous—stood in that doorway and lied to inquisitors with her whole chest while I ran. She spent her invisibility on me like it was coin. I'm not going to waste it being careful."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of a woman filing something away that she will absolutely bring up later: "You know she'd hate this. She'd call it exactly what it is—her worst student playing the hero."

"She'd call it worse than that." I smiled despite everything. "She'd call it *family*. And then she'd tell us we were idiots, and then she'd help."

* * *

We moved out before dawn, in the blue-black hour when the mountain keeps its secrets best. The guild split at the chapel door—eight of them toward the passes with their oil and their picks and their lists of bridges worth burning, and two of us toward the heart of the erased country, where the maps gave out and the old names began.

At the fork in the trail, Denna caught my sleeve.

"One thing," she said. "If you find whoever's wearing Baba Sef's face out there—the thing Ulyana told us about—you look close before you believe it. Grief makes stupid eyes of all of us."

"I know," I said. "Believe me. I know."

She squeezed my arm and was gone into the dark, and the sound of eight sets of boots faded up the pass toward the war they'd volunteered for.

Ulyana and I climbed east as the sun came up, into country that grew stranger with every mile—shrines older than the empire, cairns stacked in patterns no surveyor had drawn, and a silence in the air that felt less like absence and more like attention. Somewhere behind us, faint on the wind, I thought I heard the first smoke of the burn-teams reaching the foothills.

Somewhere ahead, at the center of a country that had been unwritten, a door waited with my family's name on it.

And somewhere between those two points, I knew, something wearing a familiar face was also walking.

I just didn't know yet whose.
