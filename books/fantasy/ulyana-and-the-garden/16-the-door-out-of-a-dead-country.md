## 16. The Door Out of a Dead Country

The maps lied about the distance.

Not the Cartographer's Ghost—those were honest, in the way of dead men's papers—but the land itself. Every mile east, the country argued with itself. Rivers ran uphill for a hundred paces and then thought better of it. Cairns appeared where we hadn't left them. Once I counted our own footprints ahead of us on a ridge, pressed into frost that had fallen overnight, going the direction we'd come.

"Tell me you see that," Ulyana said.

"I see it."

"And?"

"And I'm choosing not to have opinions until we're through." I kept walking. "Opinions are how gods find you out here. You think too loud, they answer."

She gave me one of her looks—the flat, priestess-grade look she'd perfected somewhere between her exile and her attempted murder of me—and said nothing, which from Ulyana was practically a hymn of agreement.

Three days past the last real map, we crossed into the erased borders proper. That's not poetry. There was an actual line. The empire had redrawn Thornfell a century ago, folded its heart into three neighboring provinces and burned every record that said otherwise, but the land remembered being a country even if the ink didn't. We stepped over a fallen boundary stone half-swallowed by moss, and the air changed. Not colder. *Older.* Like walking into a room where someone had just finished praying.

Ulyana stopped with her hand on the stone. She didn't pray—she'd burned her vows by then, or was burning them, depending on which hour you asked her—but she stood there a long moment with her eyes closed.

"It's listening," she said finally.

"What is?"

"The ground. The whole valley. It's been waiting so long it's forgotten what for." She opened her eyes. "Yara. Whatever your grandmother did here, she did it thoroughly. This isn't abandoned country. It's *held* country."

I wanted to make a joke. I'm good at jokes; they're load-bearing, most days. But my palm had started to ache again—that green sigil Baba Sef's confession had explained and never once made comfortable—and the ache had a rhythm now, like a second pulse counting down to something.

We camped that night in the shell of a waystation that predated the empire by centuries. The walls were stone gone soft as bread; the hearth still held warmth, which neither of us commented on, because commenting would have required deciding whether that was comforting or horrifying. Ulyana laid out her rite-bones and read them twice, frowning harder the second time.

"Don't," I said.

"Don't what?"

"Frown at the bones like they're wrong. If the bones are wrong, I don't want to know tonight. I want one more night of not knowing."

She looked at me across the fire, and something in her face did the thing it occasionally did—the thing that made me forget, briefly, that she'd once put a knife against my throat in a border-town alley. Softness, badly hidden, like contraband under floorboards.

"The bones say the door knows you're coming," she said. "That's all. It's not bad news. It's just news."

"Everything that knows I'm coming has tried to eat me lately."

"Yes." She fed the fire a stick. "So maybe this time it'll be flattered instead."

* * *

We found the village on the fourth day, or what was left of one.

It wasn't ruins. Ruins have a shape—walls, doorways, the negative space of lives. This was different. The houses were simply *unfinished*, as though the country had been interrupted mid-sentence and never picked up the thread. A loom stood in a doorway with cloth half-woven. A well had a bucket on its rope, frozen mid-draw, the wood unrotted after a hundred years. Bread, fossilized in an oven. A child's toy on a step.

"They didn't leave," Ulyana said quietly. "They were taken out of the story. Your Wardens didn't just hide themselves. They hid everyone who knew too much."

I touched the loom. The cloth was coarse wool, undyed, the kind my grandmother had spun—I knew that suddenly, the way you know things in dreams, without any right to know them. My hands shook, and I told them to stop, and they didn't listen, which was becoming a theme.

"This is what the seal cost," I said. "Not blood. Not the gods' freedom. *This.* A whole country's heart, scooped out and buried, so no one would ever come looking for the door."

"Your grandmother didn't do it alone."

"No. But somebody signed the order." I looked down at my marked palm. "And somebody inherited the debt."

We walked on. Behind us, faintly, the smoke-smell of the burn-teams had followed us over the passes. Ahead, the ache in my hand grew steadier, like a compass needle finding north—or like a leash shortening.

* * *

On the sixth day we reached the center of the erased borders, and there was nothing there.

I mean that literally. No mountain, no ruin, no shrine. A bowl of high meadow ringed by peaks, grass to the knees, wildflowers nodding, a stream chuckling through it like everything was fine. The most peaceful place I'd ever stood in my life. My hand screamed.

"It's here," I said. "Under us."

"You're sure?"

"Ulyana. My hand hasn't been quiet since the rockslide. Right now it feels like it's trying to shake itself off my wrist and go introduce itself to the grass."

She knelt, pressed her palm flat to the earth, and did the thing she did when she wasn't praying but wasn't not-praying—lips moving around words that belonged to no living liturgy. After a while she sat back on her heels, pale.

"There's a stair," she said. "Straight down. It's been sealed since before my order existed. And it's—" She stopped. Started again. "Yara, it's *unlocked*. It has been unlocked for years. Something's been holding it shut from the inside."

The eldest god. Baba Sef's face wearing a smile it hadn't earned. Of course. The door wasn't failing because the lock rusted. The lock was failing because something patient had spent a century learning the mechanism from the other side.

"Then we go down anyway," I said.

"That's the stupidest—"

"Yeah. Probably. Get up."

* * *

The stair opened when my blood touched the first step.

I want to be clear that I didn't plan that part. I knelt to brush moss off a carved lintel—Warden script, the same characters Baba Sef had traced in spilled tea on the guildhall table—and the sigil on my palm split along its old burn-line, and one drop fell, and the meadow drank it like a dry throat takes water. The grass parted in a perfect spiral. Stone steps descended into dark that wasn't empty so much as *attentive*.

"Well," said Ulyana. "That's one way to knock."

We went down. The stairs were wide enough for processions—this had been a road once, a public one, worn smooth by feet—and the walls carried carvings that got older as we descended. The upper galleries showed the old gods as the garden faith had loved them: green-crowned, generous, hands full of grain and rain. Lower, the carvings changed. The same figures, but gaunt now, hungry, their gifts become demands. And lowest of all, near the bottom, a frieze of small human figures standing in a line between the gods and a great closed door, arms linked, faces blank because the carver had run out of stone or hope.

Wardens. Generations of them. A wall made of people.

"Yara." Ulyana's voice came strange in the dark. "Look at the last figure."

The last Warden in the frieze was smaller than the rest—a woman, one hand raised toward the door, the other reaching back out of the frame, out of the stone, toward whoever walked this stair. Her face wasn't blank. Someone had taken trouble to carve it clearly, and even worn by centuries I knew it.

My grandmother. Younger than I'd ever known her, reaching back a hundred years to grab the wrist of a grandson she'd never meet.

I put my hand where hers reached. The stone was warm.

"Oh," I said. Just that. Everything else had left my vocabulary.

Ulyana put her hand on my shoulder and let me stand there as long as I needed, which was longer than either of us liked, given what was waiting below.

* * *

The door was exactly what the maps promised and nothing like what I'd imagined.

No iron, no bars, no grandeur. It was a garden gate. Wooden, silvered with age, set in a frame of living hawthorn that grew out of solid rock and bloomed white in the lightless dark. Beyond its slats: not a view, not a void, but *growing things*—a green so deep it looked like the idea of green, the original draft every other garden had been copied from. It should have been beautiful. It was beautiful. That was the trap of it, I understood then. Everything about the old gods was beautiful the way a snare is beautiful to a starving fox.

Carved into the gate's lintel, in Warden script I could suddenly read as easily as my own name—not learned, *remembered*, the way you remember a lullaby—were the words:

**THE GARDEN IS KEPT. THE KEEPER PAYS.**

And beneath, smaller, added later by a rougher hand:

*I'm sorry, little one. It was always going to be you.*

Grandmother's handwriting, cut into stone.

I stood in front of that door for a long time, and the wry narrator in my head—the one who'd gotten me through rockslides and inquisitors and a god wearing my matriarch's face—finally, blessedly, shut up.

Because I understood it now. All of it. The whole terrible architecture of my life.

The door wasn't an exit. It had never been an exit. It was a *lock*, and the garden behind it was a cage, and the cage held every god the old faith had ever worshipped—the generous ones and the hungry ones, because generosity and hunger had turned out to be the same appetite wearing different weather. The Wardens hadn't banished anything. They'd built a pantry and locked themselves in as the latch. Bloodline as bolt. Generation after generation paying the toll of names, of blood, of whole erased countries, so that the thing behind the gate stayed dreaming instead of waking.

And the seal was failing because the last Warden had died with no heir—except the seal didn't check paperwork. It checked blood. Mine. The gods hadn't mistaken me for the heir. They'd *smelled* me, the way you smell rain coming, and everything since—the answered prayers, the sigil, Baba Sef's capture, the face at the fire—had been the cage rattling, testing its new latch, asking in a hundred voices the only question a lock ever asks:

*Will you open?*

If I walked through that gate, I walked free. Out of the erased country, out of the empire's reach, out of every debt. Somewhere beyond the garden-gate was whatever the Wardens' first magic had promised—an elsewhere, an escape, a country that couldn't be redrawn. My grandmother's dying gift. The door out.

And the moment it opened for me, it opened for everything behind it.

Every caged god. Every hunger. The eldest one wearing Baba Sef's smile. All of it pouring through a gate propped open by Warden blood, into a world that had spent a hundred years forgetting why it should be afraid.

The empire wanted to burn the mountains. The mountains were the *merciful option*.

"She knew," I said. My voice sounded like someone else's. "In the frieze. She knew it would end with me standing here."

"Then she also knew you'd choose right," Ulyana said. "Or she wouldn't have bothered reaching back."

"You don't know that."

"No." She came to stand beside me, close enough that her shoulder touched mine, and looked at the beautiful, terrible gate with the flat calm she wore like armor. "But I've watched you for two months refuse every easy door anyone offered you. So yes. I know that."

The gate breathed. I felt it against my face—warm air, green air, thick with the smell of growing things and underneath it something older, patient, *aware*. From deep in the garden, soft as a lullaby, a voice that was almost my grandmother's began to hum the tune Baba Sef used to sing over the ledgers.

It knew I was crying. It was using that.

"Yara." Ulyana's hand found mine, the marked one, and held it. "Whatever you're about to do—do it with me. That's what the vow is for. That's what I'm *for*."

"Not yet." I wiped my face with my sleeve, roughly, the way Baba Sef would have approved of. "First I want to look at what I'm refusing. Properly. If I'm going to spend my life as a latch, I want to know the shape of the door."

I reached for the gate.

And from the darkness of the stair behind us, unhurried, familiar, came footsteps—and a voice I had buried a week ago with no body to bury.

"Well now," said Baba Sef. "Ain't you a sight for old eyes, little smuggler."

Ulyana's knife was in her hand before the echo faded.

I didn't turn around. Because I'd already learned this lesson once, at a campfire, at a cost I was still paying—and besides, the gate under my fingers had gone taut as a held breath, and I understood, with the terrible clarity the erased country granted its last Warden, that the thing behind the door and the thing behind me had been waiting for exactly this moment.

Both doors opening at once. That had always been the plan.

"Hello, Baba Sef," I said, without turning. "You're late. Or early. I can never tell, with dead women."
