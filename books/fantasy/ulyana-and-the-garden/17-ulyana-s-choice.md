## 17. Ulyana's Choice

They came at dawn, because my order has always believed that holiness is best conducted before breakfast.

I knew they were coming the way you know a storm is coming—not from any sign, but from the way the valley held its breath. The garden shrine where Yara and I had made our camp had gone strange overnight. The moss on the standing stones had turned to face downhill, all of it, every frond, like an audience settling in its seats. The little spring that fed the shrine pool ran backward for the space of three heartbeats and then thought better of it. Even the god we'd refused—the hungry one in the hidden valley, the one who had marked Yara as prey—had gone quiet, and I have learned that when the old gods go quiet, it is not peace. It is the silence of spectators.

"They're here," I said.

Yara looked up from the fire he was coaxing along with the particular tenderness he gave to everything that couldn't run away from him. "Who's here?"

"Mother Vessien. And she won't have come alone."

He considered this while feeding a branch to the flames. "The woman who sent you to kill me."

"The woman who sent me to kill you," I confirmed, "and who, when I declined, will have promoted someone more reliable. That's how the Garden works. Disappointment is just a vacancy with better paperwork."

"You could run," he said. It wasn't an accusation. It was worse—it was an offer, laid out flat between us like a coin on a table. "We know the passes now. We could be over the ridge before—"

"Yara." I crouched by the fire and took his marked hand, the one with the green sigil burned into the palm, the one that had been aching since midnight in the way I knew meant the seal was thinning. "There is a door behind us that is also a lock. There are gods in front of us wearing my mother superior's face if they feel like it. And there is an empire behind *them* planning to burn these mountains down to their geological underwear." I smiled at him, because if you can't be wry at the end of the world, when exactly were you saving it up for? "Running stopped being on the menu somewhere around the flooded gorge."

"That was your line about the stew in that border town."

"And I stand by it. That stew was a crime against several gods, and not the interesting ones."

But he didn't smile back, and neither did I, really, and we sat there holding hands over a dying fire while the moss pointed downhill and waited for my family to arrive.

* * *

They came down the shrine path in single file, which I confess surprised me. I had expected the Garden's delegation to arrive with trumpets, or at least with the smug self-importance of people who believe the universe has been personally notified of their schedule. Instead: nine figures in grey-green robes, hoods back, faces bare, walking the old pilgrim road the way pilgrims used to walk it, softly, as guests.

Mother Vessien led them. She was older than I remembered, or perhaps I had simply never looked properly, too busy being a furious novice memorizing her flaws so I could avoid becoming her. Her hair had gone fully white. Her hands, folded before her, were spotted like autumn leaves. She stopped ten paces from our fire, and her sisters fanned out behind her in a crescent that was either ritual geometry or tactical positioning, and knowing my order, it was both.

"Ulyana," she said.

"Mother Vessien." I rose. Beside me, Yara rose too, and I felt him do the thing he does—that vanishing trick, folding himself small and unremarkable even though he stood six feet tall in a clearing with nowhere to hide. A lifetime of being overlooked doesn't leave you. It just waits for reasons.

"You look well," she said. "Exile agrees with you. That must be infuriating."

"It has its moments. Why are you here?"

"Isn't it obvious?" Her eyes went past me to Yara, and something moved through her face—grief, I think, genuine grief, which frightened me far more than anger would have. "We've come to finish what you wouldn't."

One of the sisters behind her—a tall woman with a burn scar across her jaw whose name I had once known and had deliberately forgotten—stepped forward and unslung a bundle from her back. She unwrapped it with the reverence my order reserves for relics and weapons, which have always been the same category to us. Inside lay a garden knife. Not a working tool. The kind with a blade shaped like a leaf, ceremonial, old enough that the bronze had gone green. The kind you use for pruning.

Or for harvests.

"He's the Warden heir," Mother Vessien said, conversationally, as if discussing drainage. "The last of the blood that bound them. You know what the archives say, Ulyana. You read them when you were fourteen and then lied to me about it for six years, which, incidentally, was the first sign you'd make an excellent heretic."

"Thank you. I did work hard at it."

"A Warden's death, freely given at the door, renews the seal for another century." She said it gently. That was the worst part. She said it the way you tell a child that the dog has gone to live on a farm. "The seal is failing *now*. You've seen it. You've felt it through your feet every morning since you left us—we taught you to feel that much, whatever else you think we failed to teach. Nine days, perhaps ten, before the eldest one gets a claw through the gap. And then it will not matter what anyone chooses, because choice will be a memory, and a brief one."

"So you're going to kill him."

"We're going to ask him," she said, and I heard, beneath the gentleness, the thing that had been eating my order hollow for a hundred years: fear, dressed in liturgy. "And if he refuses—and he will refuse, I've read your reports, you always did write him too sympathetically—then yes. We will kill him. His blood on the door works whether he consents or not. Consent only determines whether it holds for a century or a season."

Behind me, Yara said nothing. I could hear him breathing, slow and deliberate, the breathing of a man doing arithmetic with his own death. I had watched him do this arithmetic before, at the door itself, when he understood what opening it would cost. He was very good at sums that ended in himself. It was, I had come to realize, his most infuriating virtue.

"And me?" I asked. "What's my part in this liturgy?"

Mother Vessien's expression softened into something I hadn't seen directed at me since I was a novice with dirt under my nails and a talent for asking the wrong questions beautifully. "Reinstatement," she said. "Full restoration. Your vows returned, your name spoken in the Chapterhouse again. The heresy struck from the record." She paused. "You would be the one to do it, Ulyana. The knife. The rite. A Warden's death administered by a priestess of the Garden—the oldest form of the sealing, from before the Wardens existed, when the gardeners still tended gods instead of cages. Do this, and everything you lost comes back to you."

I want to be honest, because honesty is the one discipline I never managed to master in thirty years of religious training, and I might as well start now, at the end.

For one full breath, I wanted it.

I wanted the Chapterhouse with its smell of cedar and cold stone. I wanted the archive with its forbidden shelves, where I had spent the happiest illicit years of my life. I wanted my name back—my *name*, the one that meant something, the one that opened doors, the one that had been struck from every ledger the moment I let those three pilgrims die. I wanted to stop being the woman who opens gates that should stay shut. I wanted, more than I have ever wanted anything, to be forgiven.

And then I looked at Yara Reyes, who was watching me with no expectation at all, because that was the other thing about him—he had spent his whole life being invisible, and he had never once assumed he deserved to be seen. He wasn't waiting for me to save him. He was waiting to find out what I was made of, the way you wait out a storm, with patience and a certain grim curiosity.

And I thought: *they sent me to kill him once already.* And I thought: *the last time I chose duty, three pilgrims died, and the order called it tragedy, and I called it penance, and neither of us was right.*

"Here's my counteroffer," I said.

* * *

I walked to the shrine pool, where the water had gone mirror-still, and I knelt, and I did the thing I had rehearsed in dreams for six years without ever believing I'd have the courage.

I recited my vows.

Not the reinstatement version—the original ones, the ones I spoke at nineteen with my hands in the consecrated soil: *To tend what grows. To guard what sleeps. To open nothing that the roots have closed.* My voice cracked on the third line and I kept going anyway, because that's the whole trick of vows, isn't it? They're only real when they cost.

Then I stood, and I said: "These vows, I return. Freely. Completely. Burn them out of me."

Mother Vessien's face went very still. "Ulyana—"

"You offered me my name back if I killed him. So take the name instead. Take all of it. Every vow, every title, every hour I knelt in your cedar-smelling dark and called it devotion. I am done being a priestess of a garden that keeps its flowers in cages." I turned to Yara. "Give me your hand."

He gave it. Of course he gave it; he'd have given me anything, which is a flaw in him I intend to spend the rest of my life exploiting responsibly.

"What are you doing?" Mother Vessien asked, and for the first time in my memory, my mother superior sounded uncertain.

"What the Wardens should have done a thousand years ago," I said. "Something new."

I pressed his marked palm against mine, sigil to skin, and I spoke—not the Warden rite, which required a bloodline I didn't have and a death I wasn't offering, but the oldest rite my order possessed, the one from before the cages, the one the archives kept under triple seal because it was too dangerous to remember: the vow of co-keeping. Two gardeners, one garden. Two keepers, one door. Not a sacrifice. A *partnership*. The kind of binding that no god could corrupt, because it asked nothing of blood and everything of choice.

Green light climbed our joined arms like ivy finding a wall. The sigil on Yara's palm flared—and then, impossibly, bloomed, splitting into two matching marks, one sinking into my own hand like a seed taking root. It hurt. It hurt like truth hurts, like the moment after a confession when you cannot take the words back and discover, to your astonishment, that you don't want to.

The shrine pool boiled. The moss on the stones stood up straight. Somewhere below us, deep in the mountain, something vast and ancient and hungry went very, very quiet—but it was not the silence of spectators anymore. It was the silence of something listening.

"No," Mother Vessien whispered. "That rite is forbidden. That rite *unmade* the last order that tried it—"

"Yes," I said. "Doesn't it feel wonderful?"

The sister with the burn scar raised the garden knife. Yara stepped in front of me—I would have laughed at the absurdity of it if I'd had the breath—and the green light came off our hands in a wave, not violent, nothing like violence, more like spring arriving all at once in a room that had forgotten the concept. The knife-blade, that ancient bronze leaf, sprouted. Actually sprouted, a white rootlet curling off its edge, and the sister dropped it with a sound I will treasure until I die.

Mother Vessien looked at the knife in the dirt. At the two of us, hand in hand, marked and lit and utterly beyond her reach. At the shrine that had chosen us over her.

"You were the best of us," she said quietly. "That was always the problem."

"I know," I said. "Go home, Mother. Tell the Chapterhouse the anomaly resolved itself. Tell them whatever lets them sleep. But tell them this much, and make them believe it: the door is not open, and it will not open, and the thing keeping it shut is not a bloodline anymore. It's a promise. And promises," I added, wry to the last, "are so much harder to kill than people."

They withdrew the way they'd come, single file, soft as guests. All but one. Mother Vessien paused at the edge of the clearing, and she did not turn around, and she said, so quietly I almost missed it:

"When the eldest one comes—and it will come, child, it is already moving—it will offer him everything. Be sure he knows what refusal costs. And be sure he knows he doesn't pay it alone."

Then she was gone down the pilgrim road, and the moss relaxed, and Yara exhaled like a man surfacing.

"Well," he said. "That went better than the stew."

I looked down at our hands, still joined, twin sigils glowing faintly green, and I felt the mountain shift beneath us—not in threat, this time, but in attention. In the deep places, something old had begun to walk toward the door, drawn by the crack in its cage, and it was coming faster than any of us had guessed.

"Yara," I said. "It's coming *now*."
