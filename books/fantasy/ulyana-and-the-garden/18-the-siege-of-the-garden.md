## 18. The Siege of the Garden

The morning the world came to kill us at the door, I woke to birdsong.

That should have been my first warning. Nothing in the Thornfells sings when something old is about to break. The birds go quiet like a room goes quiet when the wrong person walks in. But that dawn the whole valley was rioting with it—larks and thrushes and some fat idiot of a wren hammering away on a hollow branch—and I lay there in the shrine-valley grass listening, and for one whole minute I let myself believe it was just a beautiful morning.

Then Ulyana sat up beside me, looked east, and said, "They're coming."

Not *who*. Not *how many*. Just *they're coming*, in the flat voice you use for weather.

I'd had two days since the river god's warning to prepare, which is one day more than Baba Sef ever gave me before sending me into a tollman checkpoint with a wagon full of contraband and a smile. Two days to dig in at the heart of a country that officially did not exist, beside a door that was not a door but a lock, with an army of imperial burn-teams descending from the south, three waking gods circling from the north wearing shapes they'd stolen from my memories, and thirteen smugglers—the eleven who'd answered my call, plus Kesst's mule, whom we counted because Kesst insisted and because the mule had better sense than any of us—holding a pass against all of it.

Two days. It would have to do.

* * *

Let me tell you what the pass looked like at first light, because somebody ought to remember it properly, and I have a feeling the empire's version will involve a great deal of fire and very little truth.

The Warden road winds up through a notch in the mountains called the Shepherd's Teeth—two stone spires leaning toward each other like drunks propping each other upright, with maybe forty feet of road between them. Below the Teeth, the trail drops through switchbacks into pine forest. Above them, it climbs another mile to the valley where the door waits behind a wall of living hawthorn that Ulyana said hadn't bloomed in a hundred years and was now covered in white flowers so thick the hedge looked like it had been snowed on in high summer.

We couldn't hold the valley. We knew that going in. Thirteen people—or twelve people and a mule—cannot hold a valley against the Ninth Inquisitorial Company and whatever the gods sent walking. What we could hold was the Teeth. Forty feet of road between two walls of stone, and every smuggler trick I'd ever learned turned loose on it.

Kesst rigged the scree slope above the western approach with tripwires and loose rock—"the polite version," he called it, "since we're not actually killing anybody yet." Old Marette and her sister Vess dug a pit trap across the road's narrowest point and roofed it with brush so artfully that I stepped in it myself during inspection, which earned me a full minute of laughter from men who were probably going to die tomorrow and needed it. Dossa built fires that burned green—we'd learned that trick from watching the god-things move; if they liked wearing our shapes, let them choke on our smoke too. And at the center of it all, on a camp stool she refused to surrender even though her hands shook so badly she needed help holding her tea, sat Baba Sef.

She'd come back to us two nights ago out of the dark above the pass, gaunt as a winter wolf, dressed in a prisoner's smock with the imperial brand still healing on her collarbone, and carrying nothing but a knife and a story I didn't fully believe: that she'd talked her way out of an inquisitor's cell by being too stubborn to interrogate. "Corvane got sloppy," was all she'd say. "Left me a door and a moment. I've made a career of doors and moments."

Ulyana didn't believe it either. She checked Baba Sef for god-sign twice, quietly, with rites that would have blistered anything wearing a stolen face. Both times she came away frowning—not because she'd found something, but because she hadn't, and after the thing at our fire she trusted clean results about as much as I trusted clear roads.

But it was Baba Sef. Whatever else the world had done to her, it was her, and when she looked at me across that campfire and said, "You've got your grandmother's chin, boy. Stubborn as a landslide," I decided that if the universe wanted to hurt me one more time by making her real, then fine. Real she was. And real she would fight.

"You understand," she said to me that last night, while the others slept and the hawthorn hedge glowed faintly in the distance like a lamp behind a curtain, "that I'm not staying at the rear."

"I figured."

"The rite needs time. Ulyana told me—an hour, maybe more, once you start. That hour belongs to me." She sipped her tea. Her hands were steadier now than they'd been in a year, which frightened me more than the shaking ever had. People get calm at the end. I'd seen it in dying animals. "I spent forty years keeping this guild small enough to hide. Hiding was the right call for forty years. But hiding has a cost, Yara, and the bill comes due all at once, at the end, and it's paid by whoever's standing nearest." She looked at me over the rim of the cup. "I intend to be standing nearest."

"Baba Sef—"

"Don't." No anger in it. Just a door closing, gently, the way she closed doors on conversations she'd already won. "Drink your tea. Tomorrow's long."

* * *

They came at midmorning, and they came wrong.

I'd expected the empire first—inquisitors in their ash-grey coats, burn-teams with their censer-poles and their hymns of unmaking, marching up the road in good order because the empire does everything in good order, even murder. Instead the forest below the pass started screaming, and what came up the switchbacks wasn't marching.

It was the god from the hidden valley. The one that had offered me power and marked me as prey when I refused. It had stopped bothering with a face. It moved through the pines like a rumor of winter—a tallness, a hunger, a suggestion of antlers where there shouldn't be antlers—and everything it passed bent toward it and went grey. Behind it, scattered through the trees like leaves in its wake, came the things it had caught: pilgrims, soldiers, a couple of my own people who'd been taken in the first scattering weeks, all of them walking, all of them smiling, all of them empty.

And behind *those*, at the treeline, the inquisitors. Watching. Waiting. Dame Corvane's banner among them—I recognized the black crow on white—and I understood the arrangement instantly, because it was the oldest arrangement in smuggling: let someone else go first, learn what kills them, then move.

"Kesst," I called down the line. "Now would be polite."

The scree slope came down on the western approach in a white roar, sealing the lower switchbacks and cutting off half the empty ones. The rest kept climbing. Of course they did.

What followed was four hours of the ugliest work I've ever done, and I once helped unload a cargo hold full of live eels during a storm. The god-thing reached the Teeth and found that forty feet of road held more tricks than it expected—Marette's pit took three of the empty ones, Dossa's green fires made it flinch and circle, and Vess, bless her wicked heart, had strung the gap with nets weighted with salt-iron from the guild's oldest stores, the kind Baba Sef's grandmother might have handled, and where those nets touched the tallness it *hissed*.

It hissed, and it learned, and it stopped sending the empties and started coming itself.

That was when Baba Sef stood up.

She'd been sitting at the gap's edge all morning, small and still, and I'd almost managed to forget her there, which I think was the point. Now she walked out onto the road between the Teeth with nothing but her knife and her tea-stained shawl, and she planted herself in front of that hungry tallness the way you'd plant yourself in front of a debt collector, and she said, in the voice she'd used on me a hundred times when I was being an idiot:

"No. This road is guild property. Toll's owed."

The thing paused. Gods are old, and old things remember manners even when they've forgotten mercy, and somewhere under all that hunger there was perhaps a flicker of recognition—here was a woman who had spent her whole life standing in doorways charging passage, and there is a kind of holiness in that too, if you squint.

It reached for her anyway.

What happened next, I saw only in pieces, because I was shouting and running and being dragged back by Kesst and Dossa both, and grief makes a liar of memory. I saw her knife open her own palm instead of the enemy's. I saw blood hit the road—Warden-line blood, the same blood in my veins, spilled freely on the stones her grandmother's grandmother had walked. I saw the salt-iron nets blaze white. I saw the tallness recoil as if the mountain itself had slapped it, and I heard Baba Sef laugh—that dry, cracked, triumphant laugh she saved for tollmen who thought they'd caught us, the best laugh in the world—

—and I heard her say, clearly, over the roar: *"Go, boy. Finish it. I'll keep the toll."*

Then the light took them both, and the pass shook, and when my eyes cleared there was no tallness and no Baba Sef, only a circle of scorched stone shaped like an open hand, and her shawl, folded neatly at its center, the way she folded everything.

* * *

I don't remember running the last mile to the valley. Kesst says he carried me partway. I don't believe him, but I've decided to accept it, because the alternative is believing I ran it myself, and no part of me wants to know I had that in me.

Ulyana was waiting at the hawthorn hedge, and the hedge was dying.

All that impossible bloom was browning from the roots up, flowers curling like paper in flame, because the seal behind it was cracking and the hedge was the seal's skin, and skin splits before the wound shows. Through the gaps in the branches I could see the door—old grey stone, taller than a man, carved with the spiral-and-key sigil that matched the scar on my palm—and down its center ran a crack as fine as a hair and as bright as a vein of lightning.

"It's been accelerating since dawn," Ulyana said. Her voice was steady. Her hands, gathering the rite-tools—chalk, ash, a bowl of water from the shrine spring, the rusted key Baba Sef had given me—were not. "Yara. Before we start. If this goes wrong—"

"It won't."

"If this goes *wrong*," she repeated, with the patience of a woman who had once tried to kill me and had since learned exactly how often I lied to make other people feel better, "then the last thing either of us should carry is an unfinished argument. So: I'm glad I missed. That night on the dock, with the knife. I'm glad I hesitated. It was the first honest thing I'd done in ten years, and I didn't know it yet, but it was."

Below us, far below, the pass boomed. Something else was climbing. Corvane's company, moving at last, or the second god, or the third. It didn't matter. We had an hour, maybe less, and Baba Sef had bought every minute of it with the only coin she had left.

"Ulyana." I took her hands, chalk and ash and all. "When this is done, I'm going to be terrible at gardening. Fair warning."

"Noted," she said, and kissed me once, briefly, like a signature on a contract, and then we turned to face the door together.

I pressed my bleeding palm—Baba Sef's knife had opened it at the pass, though I don't remember deciding that—to the stone, and spoke the words Ulyana had taught me, the old vow, the freely given vow, the thing my grandmother died rather than say and I chose to say with my whole chest:

*I am the door's keeper. I open it for no one. I close it for everyone.*

The crack in the stone blazed. The hedge shuddered. Somewhere beneath the mountains, something vast and furious and older than the empire threw its whole weight against the lock one final time—

—and the key in my other hand, the rusted little key that had opened nothing for a hundred years, turned.

* * *

The silence afterward lasted nine heartbeats. I counted. It's what I do instead of praying.

Then the boom from the pass came again, closer, and Ulyana grabbed my arm and pointed down the valley, and I saw what was climbing the road toward us now that the gods' assault had broken against the seal: ash-grey coats in good order, censer-poles swinging, Dame Corvane's crow-banner at their head, and Corvane herself riding in front with her visor up and an expression I'd learned to read in border towns—the expression of a woman carrying orders she intends to misplace.

She reined in fifty yards from the hedge. Looked at the dead god-scorch on the pass road behind her. Looked at the browned hedge, the silent door, the two of us standing bloody-handed in the flowers.

"The anomaly reading's gone," she called up. "As of"—she consulted a brass instrument on her saddle without much interest—"nine minutes ago. Curious." She looked at me a long moment. "Whatever you did, Warden, the mountains just went quiet for the first time in a year. My orders authorize me to burn this valley regardless." A pause. "My orders also assume the anomaly would still be active. They don't fit anymore. I hate paperwork that doesn't fit."

"And so?" Ulyana called back.

"So I'm going to ride back down and report that the valley was already burned when I arrived." Corvane gathered her reins. Then, quieter, almost to herself: "An old woman held that pass alone, they'll say. Give her name to whatever god keeps smugglers. It seems to be listening lately."

She turned her horse. The column turned with her, grey and orderly, and began the long march down out of a country that was starting, very slowly, to exist again.

Ulyana's hand found mine. Below us, Kesst and the others were coming up the road, slow and battered and alive, carrying a folded shawl none of them would let anyone else touch.

"We should dig the grave ourselves," I said. "Not the guild. Us."

"Yes," Ulyana said. "Tomorrow. Tonight—" she looked at the door, whole and silent and locked, and at the first honest stars coming out over the Teeth, "—tonight we're going to sit very still and not save the world. I think we've earned one evening of it."

She was wrong, as it happens. We got about twenty minutes before the hedge put out its first new blossom—but that, as Baba Sef would have said, is a bill for another day.
