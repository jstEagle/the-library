## 19. The Warden's Blood

The door was not what I expected, which is the only mercy I can report from that whole terrible day.

I had spent three weeks imagining it. In my worst imaginings it was a slab of black iron taller than the guildhall, studded with chains thick as my waist, dripping with a century of rust and old prayers. In my better ones it was something small—a hatch, a cellar door, the kind of thing you'd walk past a thousand times without noticing, because that was how Wardens did things. Small. Unseen. Indispensable.

It was neither. It was a garden.

That's what stopped me at the mouth of the valley, with Ulyana breathing hard beside me and the sound of distant burning still hanging in the air like smoke you couldn't see. The erased heart of Thornfell—the country that no longer existed, the blank space on every imperial map—was a walled garden gone gloriously to seed. Fruit trees grown wild and enormous. Roses that had climbed so long they'd eaten the walls. Paths swallowed by thyme that released its scent under our boots like the ground itself was sighing. And in the center, where any sensible garden would keep a well or a statue of some dead saint, there was a door.

Just a door. Standing alone. No wall around it, no house, nothing behind it but more garden. Oak, iron-banded, weathered silver-grey, with a keyhole shaped like nothing I'd ever seen—except that my hand had seen it, apparently, because the sigil burned into my palm had been aching since we crossed the last ridge, aching like a tooth, like a name being called.

"That's it," Ulyana said. Not a question. She'd been raised on rites; she knew a threshold when she stood in front of one, even one wearing a garden's clothes.

"That's it," I said.

"You're doing the thing again."

"What thing?"

"The thing where you make a joke so you don't have to feel whatever you're actually feeling." She looked at me sideways. Her face was smudged with ash from the pass, and there was a cut above her eyebrow she kept refusing to let me tend, and she was, without question, the most beautiful thing in that valley, which was saying something. "Yara. Whatever happens when you touch that door—I need you to know that I'm glad it was me they sent to kill you."

"Ulyana."

"I'm serious. I've been thinking about it for weeks. If my order had sent anyone else—"

"If your order had sent anyone else, I'd be dead, and you'd be a priestess again, and everyone would be much more comfortable." I turned to face her fully. "So don't say it like a eulogy. Say it like a smuggler. We're here. We made it. Baba Sef is holding the pass, the guild is holding the pass, and all we have to do is—" I gestured at the door. "Whatever this is."

She laughed, once, without much humor in it. "You don't even know what it does."

"I know what Baba Sef said. I know what the river god said. I know what every starving shrine between here and the border has been whispering." I flexed my marked hand. The green sigil caught the light, faint as moss. "It's a lock. And I'm the key. And everything hungry on the other side of it has been waiting a hundred years for someone stupid enough to turn himself."

* * *

We crossed the garden together, and the garden noticed.

I want to be clear about that, because later—when people ask, and they do ask—it wasn't like walking through an ordinary overgrown orchard. Every step we took, the thyme bent toward us. The roses turned their heads. Somewhere high in the oldest tree, something that was not a bird watched us with an attention I could feel on my skin like sunburn. The whole valley was awake, and it was awake *toward* us, the way a pack of dogs goes quiet when the meat walks into the yard.

The door grew larger as we approached. That's the other thing nobody tells you. From thirty paces it looked like a door. From ten it looked like a gate. From five it stood twice my height, and the grain of the wood had resolved into faces—not carved, exactly. More like remembered. A hundred years of Warden hands had worn their likenesses into the oak, and every one of them was looking down at me with expressions I recognized from mirrors: wary, tired, stubborn.

My grandmother's face was near the top. Younger than I'd ever known her. She looked furious.

"Yeah," I told her. "Me too."

"Yara." Ulyana's hand found mine—the unmarked one—and squeezed hard enough to hurt. "Before you touch it. The rite. Tell me you remember the rite."

"I remember the rite."

"Say it back to me."

"'Not by blood, but by vow. Not by inheritance, but by choice. What was taken and hidden, let it be given and kept.'" I'd been repeating it in my head for two days straight, ever since she'd taught it to me out of her forbidden archives, the heresy she'd burned her vows over. "Not by blood. By vow."

"Not by blood," she agreed. "By vow. Because blood can be stolen, Yara. Blood can be spilled. Blood can be *used*. But a vow freely given—" She stopped. Swallowed. "A vow freely given is the one thing in creation the gods can't take. They can only receive it. And receiving creates obligation. Even for them. *Especially* for them."

"So I give them a vow instead of giving them a door."

"You give them a vow instead of giving them yourself."

I looked up at the door, at all those worn faces, at my furious grandmother, and I thought about Baba Sef, somewhere behind us on the pass, buying us time with thirteen smugglers and sheer bloody-minded refusal to die. I thought about a guildhall full of small debts, and salt roads, and a life spent learning how to be invisible. I thought about how the easiest thing in the world—the thing I'd been trained for since birth, without knowing it—would be to stay small right now. To walk away. To let someone else's problem be someone else's problem.

Then I put my marked palm against the oak.

* * *

The world went green.

Not dark. Green—the green of deep water, of new leaves held up to the sun, of the light that comes through a bottle of cheap wine. And standing in that green, filling it, was the eldest god.

I don't have words for its shape, so I'll borrow some: it was like a tree that had decided to be a king. Like winter deciding to be patient. Its voice didn't come through my ears; it came up through my feet, through the roots of everything, and it said:

**WARDEN.**

"That's not my name," I said. My voice sounded very small in all that green. Good. Small was familiar. Small was home turf. "My name is Yara Reyes. You can use it or shout at the door."

**WARDEN'S BLOOD,** it said, with the patience of something that had waited a hundred years and could wait a hundred more. **THE DOOR IS WEAK. THE SEAL IS OLD. THE HANDS THAT MADE IT ARE DUST. BUT YOU—YOU ARE NEW BLOOD ON OLD ROOTS. OPEN IT, AND BE REPAID.**

"Repaid with what?"

And oh, it was clever, the way gods are clever, the way they've always been clever—it didn't show me power. It showed me Thornfell.

It showed me the salt roads open and free, no tollmen, no empire, no redrawing of borders. It showed me the guildhall rebuilt in stone instead of rotting timber, thirteen smugglers become a hundred and thirty, the small debts of Thornfell collected and honored under a banner everyone could see. It showed me Baba Sef young again, laughing on the steps, and my grandmother's name spoken aloud in market squares instead of scratched out of records. It showed me a country un-erased. Thornfell, restored. Thornfell, remembered. Thornfell, *mine*—

All I had to do was open a door.

"No," I said.

The green shivered. **CONSIDER.**

"I've considered. I've been considering my whole life. Do you know what I was? Before all this?" I held up my marked hand. "Invisible. That was the job. Stay small, stay unseen, stay indispensable. And it worked, mostly, except for the part where the whole country I loved got wiped off the map while I kept my head down and my cut small and called it survival." I took a breath. "You're offering me everything I ever wanted. I know that. That's why I know it's a lie. Because countries don't come back from doors, they come back from people. From staying. From *keeping*. And if I open that door, there won't be a Thornfell to restore—there'll just be a hole where it used to be, with you pouring out of it."

**WE ARE HUNGRY.**

"I know." And I did know—that was the worst part. A hundred years of silence, a hundred years of shrines crumbling and names forgotten and prayers going unanswered until the answering became desperate and strange. They weren't monsters. They were starving. "I know you're hungry. So here's my counteroffer."

The green went very still. Even gods, it turns out, are curious.

"Not by blood," I said, "but by vow. Not by inheritance, but by choice." The sigil on my palm flared, and I pressed it harder against the wood, and I felt Ulyana's hand close over my shoulder from the waking world, felt her voice join mine, thin and human and absolutely steady: "What was taken and hidden, let it be given and kept."

**WHAT VOW?** the eldest god asked. And beneath the patience, for the first time, I heard something else. Hunger, yes. But also—hope. Terrible, ancient, embarrassed hope.

"A garden," I said. "Not a cage. A garden. You don't get the world, and the world doesn't get to forget you again. We tend you. We feed you—prayers, offerings, tending, the old rites done right, the way they were meant to be before everyone got scared. Ulyana knows them. She burned her whole life for them. And in exchange, you stay. You stay *behind*, and you grow, and you learn to be worshipped instead of feared. Not by blood, but by vow. Not sealed away—kept. There's a difference. My grandmother knew it. She just didn't live long enough to teach anyone."

Silence. Long enough that I started counting my own heartbeats and lost track somewhere past a hundred.

Then, quietly, in a voice like wind through leaves that had finally been allowed to fall:

**AND IF WE BREAK THE VOW?**

"Then you break it against two keepers who chose this," Ulyana said, "and every rite we know, and every Warden face in that door watching you, and a faith we will rebuild root by root. Gods starve when they're feared and forgotten. Try being loved instead. See what it feeds you."

Another silence. Then the eldest god did something I will be trying to understand for the rest of my life.

It laughed.

**SMALL,** it said, almost fondly. **UNSEEN. INDISPENSABLE. YOUR GRANDMOTHER'S CUNNING RUNS TRUE IN YOU, WARDEN. VERY WELL. NOT BY BLOOD. BY VOW.**

The green light gathered itself, drew inward, poured toward my palm like water finding a drain—and the sigil changed. The hunger went out of it. What remained was warm, and rooted, and patient as thyme.

The door sighed shut—not locked, I understood suddenly, but *latched*, the way you latch a garden gate when the work is done and the light is going gold. Behind it, something vast settled down to wait, not the waiting of a prisoner but the waiting of a seed.

I came back to myself on my knees in the thyme, Ulyana's arms around me, both of us shaking.

"It worked," she whispered. "Yara. It worked."

"Yeah." I looked up at the door—at my grandmother's face, which no longer looked furious. It looked, if anything, relieved. "Now we just have to survive the people coming to kill us for it."

Because I could hear them, even here. Down the valley, past the roses and the ruined walls—the drums of the burn-teams, the horns of her order, and underneath it all, faint and impossible and utterly unmistakable, the war-cry of thirteen smugglers holding a pass.

Baba Sef was still fighting.

We ran.
