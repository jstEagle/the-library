## 20. After the Sealing

The silence came first, and it was the loudest thing I had ever heard.

For three days after the sealing, the Thornfells did not speak. No groaning of stone, no whisper in the roots, no hungry attention pressing against the back of the neck like a hand. Just mountain-sounds: wind, water, a hawk somewhere over the eastern ridge complaining about its lot in life. Ordinary noise. I lay awake half of the first night listening for what wasn't there, the way you keep touching a tooth that's finally stopped aching, and around dawn I realized I was crying, quietly, into Yara's shoulder, and that he was awake too, doing the same thing in his own stubborn silent way.

We didn't talk about it. We were both too tired to be embarrassed, which is the only condition under which I recommend crying on a smuggler.

* * *

The empire's response arrived before our grief did.

A courier bird reached the shrine-valley on the fourth day—a fat, indignant imperial pigeon wearing a little brass leg-band like it was proud of its job—and delivered itself to Dame Corvane's forward camp at the mouth of the pass. We learned this because Corvane, to her credit or her shame, sent word up the valley herself: a single rider under a white branch-flag, carrying a sealed letter she had apparently written by hand, because no imperial clerk would have permitted the crossed-out lines.

I read it twice by the shrine's green light while Yara pretended not to watch me read it.

*"The anomaly signature has ceased,"* she wrote. *"The burn order is shelved pending further observation. Pending, I should say, indefinitely, as my superiors have discovered that 'indefinite observation' costs nothing and burning mountains costs a great deal, and bureaucracies are creatures of cost above all else. You are, officially, never to have existed. This is, I am told, the highest honor the empire can bestow."*

And then, beneath a line scratched out so hard the paper had nearly torn:

*"The garden faith was not wrong, priestess. It was early. Tell your Warden that some of us kept ledgers too."*

Yara read it over my shoulder afterward and was quiet for a long moment. "She could have taken the valley," he said finally. "After everything. The door, us, all of it. She had the men."

"She had the men," I agreed. "She also had a year of reports about gods eating inquisitors. I suspect Dame Corvane has a very practical relationship with miracles."

"She let us go twice."

"Three times, if you count the map fragment."

He looked at me. "You're keeping count."

"I'm a priestess," I said. "Counting is most of the job. The rest is digging."

* * *

We buried Baba Sef on the sixth day, in the shrine-valley, where the soil was soft and remembered how to grow things.

The smugglers carried her down from the pass themselves—all twelve of them, because one of the thirteen had decided, in the manner of smugglers, that grief was best processed several provinces away and he'd send his share of the funeral costs when he got around to it. They came down the old salt road in a slow procession, no cargo, no disguises, walking openly in daylight for perhaps the first time in their lives, and I watched Yara watch them and understood that something in him had permanently rearranged itself. He had spent twenty-odd years learning to be unseen. Now twelve people were following him because they chose to, and every step of it visibly terrified him more than the eldest god had.

They buried her wrapped in a salt-cloth shroud, because Vess—Vess of the terrible laugh and the knife collection—insisted that Baba Sef had been guild-matriarch before anything else, and guild-folk went into the ground with salt so they'd always find their way home. Nobody argued. You do not argue with Vess about ritual; she once made a toll-collector apologize.

Yara dug the grave himself. I offered. He shook his head without looking up, so I sat on the wall of the old terraced garden instead and did the other thing I knew how to do: I tended the ground. The shrine-valley had been choked for a century—bramble, scree, the gray dust of a faith abandoned—but since the sealing the springs had come back, and where the water touched, the soil woke up black and greedy. I turned it with my hands and planted what we had: cuttings from the shrine-garden below, seeds from my order's last surviving seed-pouch, three wrinkled apples from a smuggler's pack that Kess swore he'd been saving for an occasion.

"This counts," Kess said, watching me press them into the earth over Baba Sef. "Apples count as an occasion."

"Everything counts now," I said. "That's rather the point."

When the grave was filled, Yara stood over it for a long time with his marked palm open at his side, the green sigil faded now to a pale thread of light beneath the skin, like a vein of moss in stone. He didn't pray—I don't think he knows how, even now, even after everything—but he spoke to her, low, in the voice people use when they've stopped caring who hears.

"You told me to stay small," he said. "I'm going to be bad at that. Sorry."

Then he put a rusted key—the key, the one she'd given him, useless now that the door was sealed by vow instead of blood—on top of the mound, and pressed it into the earth until it vanished, and stepped back, and let Vess lead the smugglers in the guild's burial words, which turned out to be a smuggler's prayer I'd never heard: short, profane, and, in its way, more honest than anything in my order's liturgy.

*"May your debts be forgiven, your roads be downhill, and your enemies be lost forever in the passes. And if there's anything on the other side worth robbing, Baba, rob it gentle."*

Twelve voices. Then quiet. Then, distantly, from everywhere and nowhere—from the soil, the springs, the new green pushing up through the ash-gray bramble—something that was not quite a voice answered, warm and slow and old, the way a garden answers rain.

The smugglers all took a step backward. Except Yara. He just closed his eyes and nodded, like a man acknowledging a debt he intended to pay.

* * *

They stayed.

I want to record that clearly, because I have spent my whole life around institutions and I know how rare it is: they stayed. Thirteen became eleven became—we hoped—thirteen again, as word went out along the salt roads in the smugglers' own encrypted fashion, which involved a great deal of beer and coded complaints about tolls. The guildhall up in the pass was burned rubble, but the shrine-valley had a waystation, ruined but sound-walled, and a spring, and arable ground, and—most valuable of all—an imperial classification of *never existed*, which any smuggler will tell you is better than a charter.

"We'll rebuild here," Vess announced on the second evening, with the air of someone granting herself permission. "Guild of Small Debts, new chapter. Valley office. The matriarch would've hated the sentimentality and loved the tax situation."

"There are no taxes," Yara said. "This country doesn't exist."

"Even better."

They set to work the way smugglers do everything—quickly, quietly, and with an immediate black market in each other's tools. By the end of the week there was a roof on the waystation. By the end of the month there were two roofs, a smokehouse, a rebuilt terrace wall, and a heated theological dispute about whether the guild's new seal should feature a key, a gate, or, per Vess's increasingly strident campaign, "a small dignified turnip."

"Why a turnip?" I asked her once.

"Because everyone expects a key," she said. "Keys get hunted. Nobody hunts a turnip."

I have thought about that a great deal since. I believe Vess may be the finest practical theologian I have ever met.

* * *

As for the two of us—

There was no ceremony. My order did not arrive to defrock me formally; they simply struck my name from the rolls, which I learned through a thirdhand letter that managed to be simultaneously an excommunication and a request for gardening advice. I burned the letter in the shrine brazier and used the ashes for the seedbeds, which felt appropriately liturgical.

Yara and I fell into a life the way water falls into a channel: without discussion, because the shape was already there. He handled the roads—guiding travelers who found their way to the valley by means none of them could later explain, taking a tithe of salt and stories, teaching the younger smugglers the passes. I handled the garden, and the shrine, and the slow delicate work of listening.

Because that was the change, the real one, the one I still catch myself marveling at when I'm elbow-deep in soil: the gods were still here. The sealing hadn't caged them—it had *taught* them. A freely given vow, freely accepted, had done what a century of blood and bars never could: it had given them a shape to live inside that wasn't hunger. The god of our valley still stirred in the springs sometimes, still pressed its vast slow attention against the world, but when it did now, it asked. And when we answered—with tending, with planting, with the small daily liturgy of keeping things alive—it settled, satisfied, like an old animal in the sun.

"They're learning," I told Yara one evening, kneeling by the spring. "Or remembering. Worship as conversation instead of consumption. It's what the garden faith was always supposed to be, before everyone got frightened."

"And if they forget again?"

"Then we remind them. That's the job now. Forever, probably."

He considered this. "Baba Sef said the Wardens kept the seal."

"The Wardens kept a cage. We're keeping a garden." I looked up at him. "It's a promotion."

He laughed—actually laughed, the rare unguarded one—and for a moment the exhaustion lifted off both of us, and the valley smelled of turned earth and woodsmoke, and somewhere below the terraces the smugglers were arguing about turnips, and I thought: *this*. Not victory. Not peace, exactly. This—tired hands, shared ground, work that would outlast us.

* * *

On the last evening of that first season, we planted a rowan tree over Baba Sef's grave, because the garden-memory in the soil suggested it, gently, the way you suggest something to a friend.

Yara tamped the earth down with his palms—the marked one and the ordinary one alike—and I knelt beside him and gave the tree the only blessing I had left, the one I'd written myself, heretical and unranked and mine:

*"Grow tall enough to see the road. Grow kind enough to shade the traveler. Grow stubborn enough to outlive the empire."*

The spring murmured its assent. High overhead, on the pass road, a lantern swung—Kess, coming home late with salt and gossip—and the light bobbed down toward the valley like a small determined star.

Yara stood, brushed the dirt from his knees, and looked out at what we'd made: the graves and the gardens, the roofs and the road, the whole improbable living country that fit inside a place the maps insisted was empty.

"Well," he said. "Somebody has to answer the prayers."

And far below, faint but unmistakable, a traveler's voice rose from the dark of the salt road—not afraid, not desperate, just lost and hopeful and asking—and the valley, our valley, leaned toward the sound like a plant toward sun, ready to answer kindly.

I picked up my spade.
