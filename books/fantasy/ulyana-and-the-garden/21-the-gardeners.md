## 21. The Gardeners

The first thing you learn about keeping a door that was never meant to be a door is that it still wants to be opened.

Not the way a prisoner rattles bars. Nothing so dramatic. It's quieter than that—a lean in the stone, like a dog leaning against your leg. Like something patient that has decided, after a century of silence and one very eventful autumn, that patience is no longer required. Some mornings I put my palm flat against the Warden seal and feel it breathing, slow as tide, and I think: *you and me both, old friend.*

Then Ulyana hands me a hoe and tells me to stop talking to architecture.

"The tomatoes won't stake themselves," she says, which is a lie, because in this valley everything stakes itself if you ask nicely, but she likes saying it. She likes the ordinary verbs of it—*watering, weeding, staking*—the way a woman who once burned her vows off her own wrists might like the weight of a plain wooden spoon. Ritual without ceremony. Holiness with dirt under its nails.

I am, officially, a gardener. Unofficially, I am the last Warden of Thornfell, keeper of the lock at the heart of a country the empire erased from its maps, co-teacher of a faith everyone said was dead, and the only man alive who has told an elder god *no* to his face and lived to complain about the weather afterward.

Mostly I shovel compost. It's better than it sounds.

* * *

Let me back up, because Baba Sef would box my ears for starting a story in the middle, even a story about tomatoes.

We buried her in the shrine-valley in the spring, on the south slope where the light comes down like honey poured slow. The guild came—all twelve of them, because thirteen had been our number and grief does arithmetic badly. Dossa the twins' mother brought salt from the old roads. Ferro, who cries exactly once a year whether he needs to or not, spent his on her grave. We stacked stones the way smugglers stack them at crossroads, which is to say crooked, which is to say honest.

Ulyana spoke the garden rites over her—the real ones, the ones from before her order pruned them into obedience—and where her hands touched the turned earth, green came up. Not weeds. Green. The kind that means something is listening.

"She'd have hated this," I said.

"No," Ulyana said. "She'd have hated being *buried by an empire's schedule*, in a potter's field, under a false name. This—" she patted the mound, "—this she'd have called good smuggling. Getting a body past the empire's ledger into somewhere it can't be taxed."

Baba Sef would have laughed herself into a coughing fit at that. Then she'd have charged us both for the joke. Found family runs on small debts; that was always the guild's whole theology.

After the burial, nobody wanted to leave. That's the truth of it, stripped of poetry. The pass was quiet, the gods were—newly, uneasily—quiet, and the burn-teams had gone home because Dame Corvane filed a report so dry it could absorb moisture from the air: *anomaly resolved, no further imperial interest recommended.* Twelve smugglers stood around a fresh grave in a hidden valley and discovered that home was not, strictly speaking, a place any of them had.

Dossa said it first. "So do we go back to running salt, or what?"

And I heard myself say—not planning it, the way I never plan anything that matters—"Or you stay. And we build something."

Ferro looked at me the way you look at a mule that has begun quoting scripture. "You? Lead?"

"I've been informed," I said, "by several gods and one dead woman, that hiding is a full-time job with no benefits. I'm considering a career change."

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about the old gods, now that I know them the way you know a difficult neighbor: they were never monsters. They were *hungry*. There's a difference, though I'll grant you the difference matters less when you're the meal.

A god is a hunger that learned to answer prayers. That's Ulyana's definition, and she should know—she fed one pilgrims once, or so her guilt insists on telling the story. Mine is simpler: a god is what happens when enough people want something in the same direction for long enough that the wanting stands up and starts walking around.

For a hundred years the Wardens kept them shut behind the door, and shut things get strange. Shut things forget what they were for. The eldest god offered me my country un-erased, my guild restored, everything I'd ever stolen back plus interest, and underneath the offer I could feel the shape of the thing making it: not cruelty. *Hunger.* A stomach the size of a mountain range, dreaming of being full.

You can't fight that. You can't seal it forever either—we tried the forever option, and look how that went. What you can do, Ulyana says, is feed it properly.

"Prayer," she told the assembled smugglers, our first week, standing in the shrine-valley with her burned wrists folded like a lecturer's, "is just attention with manners. The gods went feral because we stopped paying attention. So we're going to pay attention. On purpose. With structure."

"So," said little Sefa-the-younger, named for a dead woman whose debts she now inherits, "we're going to *pet* the gods."

"We are going to establish," Ulyana said with tremendous dignity, "a liturgical calendar."

Which is how the last smugglers' guild of Thornfell became, without anyone voting on it, the first garden congregation in a hundred years.

* * *

I'll tell you what the work actually looks like, because the ballads—if ballads ever reach this valley, which they won't, we're not on any maps, which suits us fine—will get it wrong.

It looks like Ulyana walking the boundary stones at dawn, touching each one, speaking the name of whichever god sleeps nearest. Not binding names. *Greeting* names. There's a river-god two valleys over who took a toll of true names from me once and then, moved by the payment, warned me I was hunted; when Ulyana greets him now, he sends fish upstream against the current, which is either devotion or showing off.

It looks like teaching. Ferro, of all people, turns out to have a voice for the evening litany—low and steady, the voice of a man who has talked his way past a thousand tollgates. He leads the dusk prayers while the rest of us kneel in the tomato rows, and the ground hums, gently, like a cat deciding not to bite.

It looks like me, some nights, sitting alone at the door with my palm on the seal, doing the part of the job that can't be delegated. Listening. Answering. The eldest god speaks to me sometimes—not in words anymore, we're past words—but in pressure, in weather, in the particular ache behind the eyes that means *I remember what you refused me.*

"I know," I tell it. "I'm still here anyway. That's the whole point of me."

And slowly—this is the part I still don't trust, the part I check every morning like a man checking that his purse wasn't cut in the night—slowly, the lean in the stone eases. The breathing slows. It is learning, I think, what Ulyana promised it: that worship freely given fills differently than blood taken by force. Thinner, maybe. But it doesn't run out. It compounds. It's the difference between robbing a caravan and building a road that caravans want to travel.

Baba Sef would have understood immediately. Everything she ever built ran on the same principle. Small debts, honestly kept, outlast empires.

* * *

Not everything goes well. I'd be lying if I said otherwise, and the valley punishes lying—it makes the nettles come up wherever you've shaded the truth, which Ulyana swears isn't her doing but I have my suspicions.

In midsummer, one of the minor gods got impatient. A hill-thing, half-remembered, the kind that used to be paid in milk and bread at crossroads shrines. It started taking offerings without being asked—first a lamb, then, when the congregation's fear fed it nicely, a scarecrow that had been dressed too well. Fear is food too. That's the trap we're always working against: people meet a hungry god, they panic, the panic feeds it, and the feeding teaches it that panic works.

Ulyana caught it before I did. Of course she did. She found the hill-thing wearing the scarecrow's coat, squatting in the barley like a child caught in the pantry, and instead of drawing the knife she still carries—habit, she says, though I've noticed she keeps it sharp—she sat down in the dirt across from it and began, of all things, to negotiate.

"You're owed," she told it. "That's true. Your people forgot you, and that was wrong, and I'm sorry for it—I'm sorry for all of it, I was there when the forgetting started." Her voice did the thing it does, the thing that once made three pilgrims follow her through a gate that killed them, the thing that made me follow her through worse. "But you take what isn't given, and then you're a thief, and thieves get sealed. You remember what sealing is like. Would you like another hundred years of it?"

The scarecrow-coat thing shuddered. And then—it laid the coat down. Folded, more or less. The way you'd set down something heavy you'd been carrying wrong.

Now there's a shrine at the edge of the barley, a plain bowl on a flat stone, and every planting-day the whole congregation walks past it and leaves something: milk, bread, a button, a song. The hill-thing keeps the crows off. It's become, in its small hungry way, a member of the household.

"That's the job," Ulyana said that night, mud to the elbows, wine in hand, looking more like herself than I'd seen her since the day she tried to kill me. "Not heroes. Not wardens with swords. Gardeners. You don't cage a garden and you don't let it run wild. You *tend* it."

"And if it fails?" I asked. "If tending isn't enough someday?"

She leaned her shoulder against mine, which is as close to an embrace as she gets before dark. "Then we fail loudly, together, and the nettles can grow over both of us. But Yara." She turned and looked at me with those priestess's eyes that see straight through a man to whatever he's hoarding. "It's working. Look at it. *Look.*"

So I looked. Firelight on twelve faces that had been scattered and hunted and were now arguing amiably about irrigation. Green coming up along the boundary stones in patterns no one planted. The deep, slow breath of the door beneath the valley, no longer leaning, merely resting.

It was working. Gods help me, it was working.

* * *

Autumn came, the first full year turning over, and with it the first travelers.

They came down the old salt road—which is strange, because the empire unmapped that road years ago—and there were three of them, dusty and hollow-eyed, a grandmother and two children, refugees of some border squabble the empire hadn't gotten around to erasing yet. They'd followed a rumor: a green valley in the Thornfells where prayers are answered kindly. Where the road finds you when you're lost.

Dossa found them at the boundary stones and brought them in, and Ulyana fed them, and I gave the children the job of scaring crows, which the hill-thing resented professionally but tolerated.

The grandmother watched us all evening—at the litany, at supper, at Ulyana pressing a sprig of valley-green into each child's palm with instructions to plant it where their new home would be. Finally she came to me by the fire.

"You're the keepers, then," she said. Not a question.

"We're the gardeners," I said. It felt important to be precise.

"Mm." She looked into the fire. "My mother prayed to the garden faith, before the empire burned the shrines. I thought it was all gone." Her voice did something complicated. "Is it true, what they say? That the old ones wake here, and no one gets eaten?"

"It's true that they wake here," I said. "As for the rest—that depends. On us. Every day. Forever, probably." I shrugged. "It's not a place where nothing bad happens. It's a place where we pay attention."

She considered that for a long time. Then she nodded, once, the way Baba Sef used to nod when a deal was struck, and I felt the debt of it settle onto my shoulders—light, and permanent, the way the best debts are.

* * *

Tonight I walked up to the door alone, as I do, and put my hand on the seal, and listened.

Resting. Breathing. Learning, grain by grain, season by season, what it is to be attended to instead of imprisoned. Somewhere in the deep beneath the stone, the eldest hunger turned over like an old man in bed, and I felt—not words, never words—something that I have decided, after much deliberation, to translate as:

*Spring is coming. What will you plant?*

"Everything," I told it. "Same as every year. Same as forever."

Behind me, boots on the path—Ulyana, unhurried, carrying two cups of the terrible tea she refuses to improve on principle. She stopped beside me and put her hand on the stone next to mine, her burned wrist against my marked palm, and the sigil on my skin answered her warmth the way it always does, green rising faint as dawn under the skin.

"They'll keep coming," she said. "Travelers. Lost ones. Word spreads."

"Good," I said. "That's rather the point."

"There'll be hard ones too. Hungry gods we haven't met. Days the tending fails." She sipped her tea. "Empires, possibly. Empires are like nettles."

"Then we'll tend through them," I said. "Same as every year."

She smiled—that rare, unguarded smile, the one that cost her more than any vow she ever burned—and handed me my cup, and we stood together in the dark above the sleeping valley, two liars, two exiles, two gardeners, keeping the door by keeping the faith.

Below us, the first boundary stone of the path down had begun, very quietly, to bloom out of season.

Ulyana saw it before I did. She went very still.

"Yara," she said slowly, "that's not one of ours."

The flower opening on the stone was white as bone, and it was growing toward the north—toward the passes, toward the world beyond the valley—like a hand reaching out of the earth to point the way.

Something else had found the garden. And it was asking, politely, to be let in.
