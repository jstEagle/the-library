## 22. A Country That Remembers

There's a story they tell on the salt roads now, and I'd like to say I had nothing to do with it, but that would be a lie, and lying to you at this point would be poor manners.

They say there's a valley in the Thornfells where the fog parts for travelers instead of swallowing them. They say the shrine there is tended by a man who was once a smuggler and a woman who was once a priestess, though nobody agrees on which sins they're doing penance for. They say if you pray at the gate—really pray, not the bargaining kind but the honest kind, the kind that costs something—the mountain answers. Kindly. Which is a word people use carefully now, the way you'd handle a blade someone handed you point-first.

And they say there's a road through the passes that always finds you home.

That last one is true. I know because I built it, in the sense that Baba Sef would have understood: not with stone or surveyor's chain, but with debts. Every traveler we've guided through, every lost pilgrim pointed back to their village, every child carried over the high snow-line on Ulyana's shoulders—they all owe the road a small kindness, payable forward, no interest, no collection date. Thirteen smugglers' worth of favors, compounded across six years, until the whole country is stitched together with them like a net mended by someone who never learned when to stop.

I learned from the best. She just wasn't alive to see it.

* * *

This morning began the way most of them do: with Ulyana throwing a pinecone at my head.

"Wake up," she said, from somewhere above me. "The gate's been ringing since dawn."

"The gate doesn't ring."

"It does now. You taught it to." Another pinecone. She has excellent aim for a woman who spent her first year here insisting she was too dignified for garden work. "Also, there's a girl at the threshold who's been standing there since before light, and she looks like she's deciding between knocking and walking into the river gorge, and I would very much like her to choose neither."

That got me up. It usually does.

We keep the gate at the mouth of the valley, where the old Warden door used to be—not the lock itself; that stays buried deep under the shrine, under three layers of vow-stone and one layer of extremely territorial roses—but the outer gate, the one travelers can actually find. It's made of the original timber, salvaged from the guildhall after the empire burned it, which means it's held together mostly by stubbornness and iron brackets that Kess forged as an apology for the time he set fire to our kitchen. The wood remembers being a door that smugglers slipped through in the dark. Now it remembers being a door that opens for anyone who asks honestly.

Ulyana says the difference is liturgy. I say the difference is that we repainted it. She says that's the same thing. We have this argument weekly. It's the only argument we've never gotten tired of.

The girl at the threshold couldn't have been more than sixteen, thin as a winter branch, with the particular hollow look of someone who has walked farther than her body agreed to. Mud to the knee. A bundle on her back wrapped in oilcloth with the care you give either a baby or something holy, and in my experience the two get confused out here more often than you'd think.

She saw me coming and went rigid—the old reflex, the one country folk still have toward strangers on mountain roads. Six years ago I would have melted into the treeline without a word, let her pass or turn back without ever knowing I'd watched her. These days I just walk up and hold out my hand.

"Yara," I said. "This is my valley. Well. Mine and hers." I jerked a thumb at Ulyana, who had planted herself slightly behind me with her arms crossed, radiating the specific patience of a woman who has killed gods-adjacent entities and found teenagers only marginally less exhausting. "You look like you've been walking a long time."

"I'm not—" The girl swallowed. "I don't want trouble."

"Trouble lives two valleys over and doesn't visit. What's your name?"

A pause. The long pause of someone weighing whether a name given away can be taken back. I knew that pause. I wore it for twenty years.

"Tamsin," she said finally.

"Well met, Tamsin." I stepped aside from the gate. Not opening it wide—that comes later, when they're ready—but enough. Enough to show the path down into the green, the shrine roof just visible past the apple trees, smoke rising from a chimney that hadn't existed when we arrived because Ulyana had decided the valley needed a chimney and the valley, these days, tends to agree with her. "Come in. There's bread. There's soup. There's a bed that doesn't argue about whose side it sleeps on. Whatever you're carrying"—my eyes flicked to the bundle, and she flinched, and I looked away again, deliberately, the way you do—"you don't have to explain it today. Or ever. But if you need it kept safe, we're good at that. It's sort of the family business."

Her chin wobbled. She hated that it wobbled. I remembered hating it too.

"What is this place?" she whispered. "The innkeeper in Marrowford said—I asked him how to find it, and he laughed, and he said *the road will take you*, and then it did. The road took me. I didn't even know which way I was going and the fog just—" She stopped, embarrassed, as if admitting the mountain had helped her was somehow worse than admitting she'd been lost.

"The road takes care of its own debts," I said. "Someone helped you once. Maybe you don't remember it. Maybe it was before you could walk. Doesn't matter. You're paying it forward now, by letting us feed you." I smiled. "That's the toll. That's the whole toll. Eat something and be kind to somebody later."

Tamsin looked at me for a long moment with those hollow eyes, and then she did the thing that still undoes me, every time, after all these years.

She believed me.

* * *

Afternoon. Tamsin asleep in the guest loft, her bundle tucked beside her—we didn't look, though I'll admit Ulyana stood near it for a while with her eyes half-closed, listening, the way she listens to growing things. Whatever was in there, she came away from it looking thoughtful rather than alarmed, which from her is practically a blessing.

I was mending nets on the shrine steps when the god arrived.

He doesn't knock anymore. He used to, in the early years, arriving with the theatrical dread of something that had spent a century in a box and wanted everyone to know how much he resented it. Now he just settles into the shade of the apple tree like an uncle at a wedding, all long shadows and the smell of rain on hot stone.

"You're staring at your hands again," the river god observed.

"I'm examining my hands. There's a difference."

"There isn't." He flicked water from his fingers that never touched anything and yet everything near him got damp. "Show me the scar."

I turned my palm up. The green sigil had faded over the years—from a burn to a mark to something more like a freckle, a whorl in the skin like a fingerprint left by the mountain. On cold mornings it ached. On prayer-heavy nights it glowed faintly, and children who visited swore they could see it move, turning slowly, like a key finding its lock.

"It's healing," the god said, with what I suspect was meant to be reassurance.

"That's what worries me. When it finishes healing, what happens?"

"Nothing happens. That's the point of what you did." He settled deeper into the shadow. He was wearing the shape of an old ferryman today, gray-bearded, patient-eyed. He liked this shape lately. I suspected it was borrowed from a memory of mine—Baba Sef's brother, maybe, the one face from the guildhall I'd never quite managed to mourn cleanly—and I suspected he knew I suspected, and I suspected we were both polite enough never to say so. "The seal was blood, and blood runs out. Your seal is a vow, and vows renew themselves every morning you choose to keep them. You've made the cage into a garden. Nothing heals out of a garden. Everything grows."

"You rehearsed that."

"I have had six years and nothing but time and your endless questions. Of course I rehearsed it."

I laughed, and the sound went up through the apple branches, and somewhere in the deep places under the shrine, under the stone, under the roots of the oldest rosebush in the Thornfells, I felt the other gods stir and settle. Not hungry anymore. Not exactly tame, either—let's not flatter ourselves. But *listened to*. That was the whole revolution, really, the one Ulyana and I had stumbled into at the door with the eldest god's offer still burning in our ears: the Wardens had caged the old gods because no one would speak to them. And speaking turns out to be cheaper than chains, and holds better.

Most days.

"Some of the others are restless again," the god said, quieter now. "The one under the eastern ridge. She dreams of the years before the binding. She dreams loudly."

"She's welcome to come talk about it."

"She is a storm front, Warden. She does not *talk*."

"Then she can sit in the valley and be weathered at until she feels better. Ulyana's good at that. Ask the roses."

He made a sound that might have been laughter in some older grammar, and faded the way he always did—not gone, just folded back into the water table, into the melt and the mist, everywhere the valley drank from. I sat with my mended nets and my aching palm and thought about how strange it was that this was my life now. That the boy who'd survived by being unremarkable, by being a smudge on the imperial ledger, by making himself so small the world forgot to look—he'd ended up as the most looked-at thing in the mountains. The keeper of the green valley. The man at the gate.

Baba Sef would have laughed herself sick. Then she'd have put me to work.

* * *

Evening. Ulyana found me at the gate, because she always knows where I am, which remains the single most disorienting luxury of my entire life.

"You gave the girl the east room," she said.

"I gave the girl the east room."

"The room with the window facing the shrine."

"She looked like someone who needs to see the lights at night."

Ulyana considered this, then leaned against the gatepost beside me, shoulder to shoulder, watching the last light go amber over the ridgeline. Six years, and I still catch myself cataloguing these moments like contraband—*this is real, this is mine, no one is coming to take it*—the old smuggler's inventory, run out of habit on things that can't be stolen.

"She told me what's in the bundle," Ulyana said.

"Yeah?"

"Her grandmother's ashes. And a letter. The grandmother was from a village that the empire erased off the maps during the redraw—same campaign that wiped Thornfell proper. The girl's been carrying her home around for a year trying to find somewhere it could be put down." Ulyana paused. "She heard the stories. About a valley that remembers countries that aren't supposed to exist anymore."

I was quiet for a moment. Down in the shrine-yard, the lanterns were coming on, one by one, the ones Kess wired and the ones that lit themselves because they'd been prayed over so many times they'd developed opinions.

"We'll bury her at the north wall," I said finally. "Where the old boundary stone is. Under the Thornfell marker."

"You carved a marker for a village you've never seen."

"I carved a marker for every village the empire erased. Took me two winters. You helped."

"I helped because you refused to sleep until it was done, and I refuse to be married to a man with permanent eye-bags."

"*Married,*" I said, savoring it. She still said it flatly, matter-of-factly, like reporting the weather, and it still landed in my chest like the first warm day of spring. "Say it again."

"No. You're insufferable when I say it." But she was smiling, that crooked private smile she thinks I don't notice, and I noticed, and I always will.

We stood there while the dark came up out of the trees, and the valley did what the valley does—breathed, listened, kept its small lights burning against the big dark of the passes. Somewhere below, Tamsin slept with her grandmother close by for the first time in a year. Somewhere east, a storm-god dreamed loud dreams that we would sit with, eventually, the way you sit with any grieving thing. Somewhere south, the empire's clerks had quietly shelved a burn order and filed the anomaly as *resolved*, because empires can forgive almost anything except being unnecessary.

And somewhere out on the roads, right now, tonight, someone lost was standing at a crossroads in the fog, saying a name they half-remembered hearing in an inn, in a market, from a grandmother's mouth—*the green valley, the smuggler's road, the place where prayers are answered kindly*—and the road was already turning beneath their feet, already bringing them home.

"Another one coming," Ulyana said suddenly, tilting her head. She felt them before anyone—she always had, since the day she burned her vows and took up the other half of the rite. "Up the high trail. Two of them. One of them's hurt."

I rolled my sleeves. Checked the lantern. Put my hand flat against the gate, against the old guildhall timber that remembered every soul it had ever sheltered, and felt the sigil in my palm warm and answer, the way it always did, the way it would tomorrow and the day after, for as long as I chose—which is to say, forever, which is to say, freely.

"Then let's open the gate," I said.

And the valley, remembering everything, opened with me.
