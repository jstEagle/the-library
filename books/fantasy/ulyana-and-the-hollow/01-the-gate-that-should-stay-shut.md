## 1. The Gate That Should Stay Shut

The mountain had been quiet for eleven years, and Dominic Lockridge had learned to distrust quiet the way other men learned to trust it.

He rose before dawn in the waystation below the pass, as he did every morning, and performed the small liturgy of a gatekeeper's life: banked the fire, filled the kettle, checked the salt stores against the damp, and took down the long iron key that hung on its hook by the door. The key was older than the kingdom. Older, the archives claimed, than the idea of kingdoms. It weighed as much as a sleeping cat and was cold to the touch in every season, even now, at the height of summer, when the stones of Thornfell should have held the sun's warmth like a cupped hand.

Dominic carried it out into the grey light and began his patrol.

The path up to the gate was a mile of switchbacks cut into the mountain's shoulder, and he walked it slowly, not from age—though there was plenty of that settling into his knees—but because speed was disrespect. His father had taught him that, and his father's mother before him. You did not hurry past the Gate of Thornfell. You approached it the way you approached a sleeping bear, or a grieving widow, or any other old and powerful thing that had earned the right to be taken seriously.

Mist clung to the pines in ragged banners. Somewhere below, a marmot whistled and fell silent. Dominic counted his breaths the way the old wardens had taught him to count them—one for the valley, one for the mountain, one for whatever lay beyond—and by the time he reached the last bend, the gate stood before him in the early light, and he stopped, as he always stopped, to look.

It was carved directly into the living rock of the pass, forty feet high and thirty wide, framed in stone so ancient that no chisel-mark remained, only smoothness, the way river stones are smooth. No door swung within it. There was nothing to open. The gate was itself the seal: a single slab of mountain, fused shut by hands that predated memory, covered edge to edge in script that no living scholar could read entire. The letters were deep-cut, deliberate, and arranged in spirals that drew the eye inward toward the center, where a single emblem dominated everything—a closed circle, perfect and unbroken, worn by two thousand years of weather but never once cracked.

Eleven years Dominic had kept this post. In all that time, the circle had been whole. His father had kept it twenty-two years before him, and his grandmother thirty-one, and in all their combined watching, through avalanches and wars and the long slow rot of the kingdom's attention, the seal had never failed. It was the one fixed point in a world of drift. Kings came and went. Borders moved. Gods—

Gods had gone silent, centuries ago, and stayed silent.

But the circle was the circle. That was the covenant of Dominic's whole life. Whatever else might happen in Thornfell, the gate would hold, and a Lockridge would stand watch over it, and each morning the warden would press his palm to the stone and speak the words his grandmother had taught him—not a prayer, exactly, because prayers needed someone to hear them, and nothing beyond that stone had answered anyone in living memory. A greeting, then. A courtesy between neighbors who never spoke.

*Still here,* the words meant. *Still watching. Still shut.*

He climbed the last steps, set his hand against the stone, and felt the cold of it bite through his glove.

Then he saw the crack.

It ran through the center of the circle like a hair through amber. Thin—thin enough that if he had come an hour later, or if the mist had been thicker, or if his eyes had been ten years older, he might have missed it entirely. But it was there. It ran from the top of the closed circle to the bottom, splitting it cleanly in two, and as Dominic stared, his breath gone shallow and loud in his own ears, he understood with a certainty that bypassed thought altogether:

The crack ran *outward*. From the inside.

Stone does not crack from the inside. Stone is not hollow. But the fracture before him had the unmistakable quality of something that had pushed, gently, patiently, from the far side—as though the seal were not a wall at all but an eggshell, and something within had turned over in its sleep.

"No," Dominic said aloud, to the gate, to the mountain, to the morning. "No, you don't."

His voice sounded absurdly small. He pressed his palm flat over the crack, the way a man presses a hand to a wound, and spoke the warden's greeting, and his voice shook on the second word.

That was when he heard the prayer.

It came from everywhere and nowhere—from the stone under his hand, from the air, from somewhere behind his own ribs. It was not spoken in any tongue he knew, and yet he understood it the way you understand weeping, or laughter, without translation. It was a woman's voice, young and frayed at the edges, repeating the same few phrases over and over with the desperate rhythm of someone who has been praying a long time and has stopped expecting an answer:

*Let me rest. Let me rest. Please, whoever's listening. Let me rest.*

And then—Dominic would spend the rest of his life trying to describe what happened next, and he would never manage it—the prayer was *answered*. Not by him. He had opened his mouth to say something, anything, some warden's platitude about endurance, and the words died in his throat as another voice moved through him like water through a channel. It used his mouth. It used his breath. It said, in a language that made the mountain's oldest script seem like a child's scrawl:

***Rest is coming. Hold.***

Two words. Three syllables of sound that had not crossed this threshold since before the kingdom had a name. And when they left his lips, they did not echo off the gate—they went *through* it, through the crack, into whatever darkness lay sealed on the other side, and the mountain shuddered once, deeply, like a dog shifting in its sleep.

Dominic stumbled back from the gate. His hand came away from the stone marked with frost.

Frost. In midsummer. White crystals blooming across his palm where he had touched the seal, spreading even as he watched, and then, mercifully, sublimating away into nothing, leaving his skin red and stinging as though scalded.

He looked at the crack. He looked at his hand. He looked at the empty pass, the empty path, the empty world, and understood that none of it was empty anymore.

Something had heard. Something had answered. And neither the hearing nor the answering had come from beyond the gate.

They had come from *this* side.

* * *

He searched the pass for three hours.

He told himself it was procedure—any disturbance near the seal warranted investigation; that was in the warden's charter, written in a hand four hundred years dead—and not terror wearing procedure's coat. He worked the switchbacks methodically, checking the shrine-niches where travelers left offerings, the cave overhangs where smugglers sometimes sheltered, the cairns that marked the safe road down into the low country. The frost-voice had seemed to come from everywhere, but voices, even impossible ones, had sources. Everything had a source. That was the bedrock of Dominic's worldview, the thing he clung to as the morning warmed and the mist burned off and the sun rose shamelessly bright over a world that had apparently stopped making sense while he slept.

He found the camp just past noon, tucked into a fold of the mountain below the tree line, hidden from the honest road by design.

It was a smuggler's camp—he knew the signs the way he knew his own handwriting. Fire pit dug deep and ringed with stone to kill the smoke. Bedrolls pitched under a rock shelf. Saddlebags packed for a fast departure. And goods: bolts of undyed wool, a case of southern wine, a lockbox that probably held salt or tin or something the crown taxed too heavily for honest folk to afford. Smugglers crossed Thornfell's high passes every season, and Dominic's charter required him to report them and his conscience had long ago negotiated that clause down to *leave them be unless they trouble the gate.* They were, most of them, just poor people moving goods around the greed of great houses. He'd shared tea with half of them.

But this camp was wrong.

There were five bedrolls. Four were occupied—or had been. Four people lay sprawled in unnatural sleep, breathing slow and deep, faces slack with something that was not ordinary slumber. When Dominic knelt and shook the nearest one, a wiry man with a beard full of burrs, the man did not stir. His skin was cool. Not frozen—cool, like a house in the evening after the fire dies. His pulse, when Dominic found it, beat once every few heartbeats, slow as a tortoise's walk.

And around each sleeper, the ground was white.

Frost. In July. It furred the grass in delicate feathers, rimmed the stones of the fire ring, traced silver ferns across the leather of the saddlebags. It radiated outward from the sleepers in rough circles, and the circles overlapped, and where they overlapped the frost grew thickest, as though the cold were not coming from the sleepers but passing *through* them, the way light passes through windows.

Four sleepers. Four frosted circles. And a fifth bedroll, empty, at the center of them all—at the point where every circle of cold converged, where the frost lay deepest, where the grass beneath the ice had blackened as though burned by winter instead of summer.

Someone had lain there. Someone had gotten up—or been taken—or had simply ceased to be there, leaving behind a patch of earth colder than any cellar in the duchy.

Dominic stood very still in the ruined camp and listened.

At first: wind. Pine. The distant idiot commentary of a jay. Then, threading beneath it, so faint he might have invented it, a sound like many voices murmuring behind a thick door. Not words. Not yet. Just the sense of speech, of congregation, of a crowd gathered somewhere just out of sight, waiting.

The sound was coming from downslope. Toward the stream.

He followed it with his hand on his knife—not because a blade would help, he suspected, but because a man needs to do *something* with his hands when the world tilts—and picked his way down through the boulders to where a narrow stream cut through the trees. The murmur grew. The air grew cold. By the time he reached the water, his breath was fogging, and the stream's edges wore lace of ice that could not possibly exist in this season, and the jay had gone silent, and the whole forest was holding its breath.

She lay on the bank, half in the shallows.

A young woman, dark-haired, dressed in the layered practical clothes of someone who lived on the road—smuggler's clothes, road-worn boots, a knife sheathed at her hip with the grip worn smooth. Her face was turned toward the sky, eyes closed, lips slightly parted, and she was unconscious in the way the others were unconscious but more so—deeper, further away, as though she had traveled a very great distance and her body had simply been left here for convenience.

The frost around her was not in a circle. It was in *rings*, concentric, spreading from her body like ripples from a dropped stone, and each ring was etched with characters—small, precise, glowing faintly blue-white—that matched the script on the gate. The script no living scholar could read.

The murmuring was coming from her.

Dozens of voices. Hundreds. All speaking at once, all praying, in tongues dead and buried and older than burial, and woven through them, holding them together the way a keystone holds an arch, one voice answering—calm, vast, patient, using no mouth Dominic could see, a voice like the space between stars:

***Hold. Rest is coming. Hold.***

The girl's fingers twitched. Frost crawled up the bank and touched the hem of her cloak and did not melt.

Dominic had served the gate for eleven years. He had buried his father. He had watched the kingdom forget the mountain and had kept watch anyway, faithfully, stubbornly, because someone must and because the alternative—admitting that the world's oldest lock might someday fail—was unthinkable.

The lock had failed. He had seen the crack with his own eyes. And lying in the shallows of a mountain stream, radiating winter in midsummer, wrapped in the voices of every god the world had ever buried, was a stranger he had never met.

Not the breach. Not the intruder.

The *keyhole*.

He knelt beside her, hesitated, then pulled off his coat and draped it over her shoulders. Her skin, when he lifted her, was cold as the gate-stone, and against his chest, through the fabric of his shirt, he felt her heartbeat—slow, vast, spaced apart like the tolling of some enormous bell—and beneath it, faint but unmistakable, the murmur of prayers continued, and one of the voices, younger than the rest, frightened, human, whispered words he could actually understand:

*I didn't mean to wake them. I didn't mean to wake anything.*

Dominic looked up at the mountain, at the pass where the gate stood with its hairline wound, at the bright indifferent sky.

"Neither did I," he said, to no one, to everyone, and carried her down toward the waystation as the first true snowflakes of July began, softly, impossibly, to fall.
