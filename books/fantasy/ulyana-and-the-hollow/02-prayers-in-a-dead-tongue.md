## 2. Prayers in a Dead Tongue

Ulyana woke to the smell of pine smoke and something boiling, and for three full seconds she did not know where she was.

The ceiling above her was rough-hewn timber, dark with age and soot, crossed by a beam from which hung bundles of dried herbs she did not recognize. A wool blanket weighed on her chest—heavy, clean, smelling of cedar and cold storage. Her boots were gone. Her coat was gone. Her knife—

She sat up too fast. The room tilted. Somewhere below, a kettle sang.

"Easy." The voice came from the doorway, and there he was: the gatekeeper, the man from the frost and the camp, holding a wooden bowl of something steaming. He had a face built for weathering—deep lines around eyes the color of wet slate, hair going grey at the temples in a way that suggested it had started early and never asked permission. "You've been asleep two days."

"Two—" Her throat was raw. She pressed her palm flat against the mattress beneath her, feeling for the edge of things, the way she always did when waking in strange rooms. "Where are my things?"

"Dried by the fire. Nothing taken." He set the bowl on a stool beside the bed and stepped back, deliberately, the way you'd move around a spooked animal. "Barley and mutton. Eat. You were half-frozen in midsummer, and I'd like to know why before I decide what to do about you."

*What to do about you.* Ulyana had heard that sentence before, in a dozen voices, in a dozen border towns. It was never followed by anything good.

"I'm not sick," she said.

"No," he agreed. "You're not. I've seen sickness. That wasn't it." He dragged a chair to the foot of the bed and sat, elbows on knees, and looked at her with the particular patience of a man who guarded a door for a living and had learned that most problems could be waited out. "Dominic Lockridge. Keeper of the Thornfell gate. And you are?"

She considered lying. It was her first instinct, honed by twenty-three years of being her father's daughter—lie small, lie fast, lie only as much as the question demands. But her hands were still trembling, and when she looked down at them she saw that the fingernails were rimmed with white, like frost that had climbed out of her own bones.

"Ulyana," she said. Just that.

"Just Ulyana?"

"For now."

Something flickered across his face—not quite a smile, but the ghost of one, tired at the edges. "For now. All right, Ulyana-for-now. You want to tell me what you were doing camped in the high passes alone? Smuggler's fire, smuggler's gear. I know the signs. I've spent thirty years learning them."

"And what does that make me? Contraband?" She reached for the bowl because her body wanted it whether her pride did or not. The stew was simple and hot and better than anything she'd eaten in weeks. She made herself slow down. "I was crossing. Same as anyone crosses Thornfell. There's a buyer in Vaelmoor who pays well for northern silk, and the passes through your gate cost more than my whole cargo."

"The gate's been sealed for two hundred years."

"So I've heard." She kept her eyes on the bowl. "I've also heard the seal's cracked."

The silence that followed had weight. When she finally looked up, Dominic Lockridge was watching her the way men watch a bridge they suspect won't hold.

"How do you know that?"

Because I heard it, she almost said. Because two nights ago, curled against a stone outcropping while sleet hammered the pass, I heard a sound like a mountain exhaling, and then I heard *singing*, and the singing was in a language nobody has spoken since the world was younger, and I understood every word.

Instead she said, "A girl hears things."

"A girl hears things," he repeated flatly.

"It's a professional hazard. Smugglers listen. We listen for patrols, for rockfall, for other smugglers. Sometimes we hear things that aren't there." She shrugged, and hated how small the gesture felt. "Madness runs in families. My mother talked to saints. My father said she got it from his side."

Dominic didn't laugh. That was the trouble with him, she was already learning—he didn't do any of the things that would have made him easy. He just sat there, steady as his own gate, and let the silence do his work until she filled it.

"They kneel to me," she said, before she could stop herself. The words came out low, almost ashamed. "In dreams. Strangers. Hundreds of them, sometimes thousands, kneeling in rows that go back farther than I can see, and they're all facing me, and they're all praying. Not *to* me—I want to be clear about that, I'm not mad enough to think they're praying *to* me—but through me. Like I'm a door they're all trying to walk through at once. And when I wake up, my mouth tastes like candle smoke and old coins, and there's frost on the inside of the window even in summer."

She hadn't meant to say any of it. She'd rehearsed lies for this exact conversation on the walk up from the camp, back when she could walk. But exhaustion is a truth-serum, and the waystation was warm, and some starved part of her had been waiting years for someone to hear this without reaching for a priest or a rope.

Dominic was quiet for a long moment. Then: "My mother heard voices too. Toward the end. The village called it the fading sickness." His jaw worked. "It wasn't that. She heard prayers. Prayers meant for gods that don't answer anymore. I used to think she was the last one who could. I used to hope she was."

"Why hope?"

"Because the ones who can hear them," he said slowly, "tend to be heard *by* them. And nothing good comes of that. Nothing has come of it for two hundred years." He stood, took her empty bowl, and paused at the door. "Rest. If the voices come back, tell me what they say. Word for word. It matters more than you know."

* * *

They came back that night, of course. They always came back.

Ulyana dreamed of the kneeling strangers again, but this time the dream had changed shape. She stood in a corridor of black basalt, and the walls were carved with figures—kings, dragons, women with their arms raised—and the prayers came not as words now but as pressure, a tide pushing against the inside of her skull. And at the far end of the corridor, a door. Old beyond old, its wood grown into the stone around it, sealed with bands of a metal that hurt to look at directly.

Someone had scratched at it. From the other side.

She woke gasping before dawn, frost feathered across the blanket over her heart, and found Dominic already awake downstairs, sitting at his table with an oil lamp and a book so old its leather cover had gone the color of dried blood. He didn't ask if she'd dreamed. He just turned the book toward her, and there, inked in a careful hand generations dead, was a drawing of the door from her dream.

"That's not possible," she whispered.

"Two hundred years ago, the keepers of this gate wrote everything down. What the seal was for. What it held back. What it would mean if it failed." He tapped the page. "And every keeper's journal ends the same way—with a warning about listeners. People who could hear the old gods' prayers. The church called them conduits. The last recorded one died the year the gate was sealed, and the journals say the sealing couldn't have been done without her." He looked up. "You understand what I'm telling you? Your madness has a history. It has a *file*. Which means either you're the first conduit in two centuries, or—"

"Or I'm exactly what I've always feared," Ulyana finished, and her voice came out steadier than she felt. "Inherited."

Before he could answer, someone knocked.

Three slow raps, spaced like a heartbeat. Dominic rose, hand drifting toward the iron poker by the hearth—not toward a weapon on the wall, she noted, which told her something about how many weapons he kept and where—and opened the door on a figure hunched against the pre-dawn grey.

The pilgrim was old, bent nearly double, wrapped in layers of patched wool gone grey as ash. His left hand hung at his side like something dead—a withered claw, fingers curled into the palm, the skin stretched thin over tendons. Frostbite, maybe, or palsy, or one of the hundred slow ruinations flesh was heir to. He lifted his face to Dominic and Ulyana saw that he was crying without seeming to know it.

"Keeper," the old man rasped. "Forgive me. I know the hour. I walked three days. They told me in the valley—" his breath caught, hitched, "—they told me there was a woman here. A stranger. That the frost came when she slept."

Ulyana's blood went cold, and not in the way it had been doing lately.

"They talk already," she murmured. "One night, and the valleys talk."

"They always talked," Dominic said quietly. "We just stopped listening." To the pilgrim: "Come in out of the cold, old father. There's stew."

But the old man shook his head, and dropped—not knelt, *dropped*, knees cracking against the flagstones—to the floor between them, and raised his ruined hand as though offering it up.

"She answers," he said. "Don't she? I dreamed of her. We all did, everyone in my village, the same dream, the same woman standing in a door of black stone. And I heard—" his voice broke apart entirely, and he pressed the withered claw to his breastbone, "—I heard a voice say, *bring him to the threshold*. So I came. Three days. Please. Please, lady. I'm seventy-one years old and I have carried this hand since I was nine, and I am so tired of praying into silence."

The room was very quiet. Outside, wind worried the eaves. Ulyana became aware that she had risen from her chair without deciding to, that her feet had carried her to within arm's reach of the kneeling man, that her right hand was lifting—her *right hand*, moving with a will that was hers and yet not hers, the way a boat is yours and still goes where the river goes.

"Don't," Dominic said. It wasn't a command. It was barely a whisper. "Ulyana. You don't know what it costs. The journals—"

"The journals," she said, and her mouth was shaping words that tasted of candle smoke and old coins, "are two hundred years out of date."

And then she stopped speaking, because something else spoke through her.

It did not take her voice so much as tune it. She felt the difference the way a lute string feels a new hand—same string, same wood, same tension, but a different music moving through it, vast and patient and impossibly sad. The syllables that came out of her mouth were in no tongue she knew, and she understood them anyway, the way you understand thunder:

***LITTLE ONE WHO WAITED. COME OUT OF THE COLD.***

The old god's voice moved through her chest like a bell being struck from inside. Light came from somewhere—or perhaps it was only the lamp, only the fire, only dawn breaking grey through the eastern window, but the whole room seemed to lean toward the old man like wheat toward wind. Ulyana felt her hand descend, felt her fingers close around the withered claw, and the touch was strange—warm, warmer than her own skin had any right to be, warm as a hearthstone at midnight.

And under her palm, bone shifted.

It was not gentle. She would remember that afterward, and be grateful for it—that it was not gentle, that it sounded like green wood bending, that the old man screamed once, a short sharp cry like a man waking from a nightmare, and then went utterly silent. The curled fingers straightened one by one, joints popping like distant gunfire, sinew drawing taut, blood flushing into flesh that had been grey and empty for sixty years. The hand filled. The skin pinked. The nails, yellowed and ridged, gleamed suddenly as though polished.

When it was done, the pilgrim stared at his own open palm as though it belonged to a stranger. Slowly, wonderingly, he flexed the fingers. Made a fist. Opened it again.

"Sixty years," he breathed. "Sixty years I couldn't hold my daughter's hand." He looked up at Ulyana, and his eyes were streaming, and his face was transfigured, and it was the most terrible thing she had ever seen. "Lady. Lady, what's your name? Tell me your name so I can carry it home. They'll build you a shrine. They'll—"

"*No.*" The word cracked out of her, raw and human and entirely her own—the god was gone, receding like a tide, leaving her swaying and hollowed and colder than the frost ever had. She backed away until her shoulders hit the wall. "No shrines. No name. Go home. Hold your daughter's hand. And tell no one—"

But even as she said it, she knew it was already too late. The old man's village talked. The valley talked. By tomorrow the road-talk would run ahead of him like spilled wine, and somewhere in the capital a king sat on a throne wearing a crown that remembered things, and Ulyana understood—with the sudden, vertiginous clarity of a woman stepping off a ledge—that the world had just tilted beneath her feet and would never tilt back.

She slid down the wall until she was sitting on the cold flagstones, and she began, helplessly, to laugh. It came out wrong, edged with something close to sobbing.

"Two days ago I was a silk smuggler," she said to no one, to Dominic, to the ceiling beams. "Two days ago my biggest problem was a tollman named Grigor who cheats on weights."

Dominic crouched in front of her. He didn't touch her. He seemed to understand that touch was the last thing she could bear, that her skin felt like a door with a thousand people pressing against the other side of it.

"You felt it," he said. "What it did to you."

"I felt *him*. Them. Whatever." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "There were others behind him, Dominic. Behind the voice. Dozens. Hundreds. All of them waiting for a turn, all of them leaning on the same door, and the door is—" she swallowed. "The door is me. And they know it. And they are *so patient*."

"Then we need to get you to people who understand patience like that." He was quiet a moment, and when he spoke again his voice had changed—flatter, heavier, the voice of a man setting down something he'd carried a long time. "Vaelmoor. The shrine-city. There are scholars there who study the old faiths. Ruined now, mostly, but the archives survive. If anyone knows what you're becoming—"

"*Becoming?*" She dropped her hands and stared at him. "Not *what I am*? You think there's more of this coming?"

"Ulyana." He said her name carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "That seal on the gate cracked from the inside two nights before I found you frozen in the snow. The journals say the last conduit helped *make* that seal. And you dreamed of a door with scratches on the far side of it." He stood, and offered her his hand—his ordinary, calloused, unmiraculous hand. "Whatever's happening, it isn't happening to you. It's happening *through* you. And I don't intend to find out the difference standing still."

Outside, the sun was clearing the eastern ridge, and the light came through the window and lay across the floor in a long gold bar, and Ulyana thought about doors—about gates that should stay shut, and thresholds, and the small broken prayer of an old man who just wanted to hold his daughter's hand.

She took Dominic's hand and let him pull her to her feet.

"All right," she said. "Vaelmoor. But I'm carrying my own pack, and if anyone kneels to me on the road, I'm tripping them."

It was meant as a joke. Dominic didn't smile. He was looking past her, through the window, down the mountain track where the old pilgrim was limping—walking, *straight-backed* for the first time in sixty years—toward the valley and the talking villages below.

"He'll tell them," Ulyana said quietly. It wasn't a question.

"They always tell," Dominic said. "And word travels faster than we will." He turned from the window, and the morning light caught the worry carved deep around his eyes. "We leave within the hour. Before whatever wears the crown in Vaelmoor's direction hears the same story—and decides it doesn't like the ending."

Far to the south, unseen, unfelt, something ancient stirred in a throne of marble and began, patiently, to listen.
