## 4. Smuggler's Roads

The pass Dominic chose was not on any map worth the vellum it was drawn on, which was precisely why he chose it.

He had learned these roads the way smugglers' daughters learned them and garrison soldiers were never supposed to: by walking them at night, alone, with a purse of bribed silence in one pocket and a knife in the other. Twenty years of patrols along Thornfell's southern face had taught him that the mountains kept two sets of paths—the ones the kingdom drew, wide and honest and watched, and the ones the mountains kept for themselves, narrow as a scar, patient as a grudge. He had sworn an oath to close the first kind. He was about to break every regulation he knew by walking the second.

"You're sulking," Ulyana said behind him.

"I'm navigating."

"You're sulking *and* navigating. There's a difference. Sulking has a rhythm to it." She scrambled up over a shelf of wet granite, breath steaming in the thin morning air, and he noted—again, with the same unease he'd been carrying since the waystation—that she moved like someone who had grown up on exactly this kind of ground. Barefoot-quick. Sure-footed where his boots slipped. "You've been quiet since we left. Quieter than usual, I mean. You have a usual."

"Vaelmoor is four days if we take the king's road," he said. "Six if we take mine. The king's road has tollmen, and tollmen have eyes, and right now I don't know whose eyes those would be."

"And yours don't?"

"My eyes are tired and mostly regretful. That's different."

Something that might have been a laugh escaped her—short, surprised, quickly smothered, as though she hadn't decided yet whether she was allowed to make that sound around him. He heard it anyway. It did something small and unwelcome to the inside of his chest, and he climbed faster to get away from it.

* * *

They made the first camp in a hollow beneath an overhang that some forgotten shepherd had walled against the wind a hundred years before anyone now living drew breath. Dominic built the fire small and shielded, the way you built fires when you didn't want to be found, and rationed out dried mutton and hard bread with the grim economy of a man feeding two people on stores meant for one.

Ulyana ate like a smuggler too—fast, watchful, one hand always near her food. But halfway through the meal her chewing slowed, and she stared into the fire with an expression he was beginning to recognize: the look of a woman listening to a room no one else could hear.

"They're quieter up here," she said.

"The voices."

She nodded. "In the lowlands they're like a market. Everyone praying at once, all of it going nowhere—or so I thought. Up here it's..." She turned her hand over, palm up, as if weighing something invisible. "Like snowfall. Individual flakes. I can almost count them." Her jaw tightened. "There's a woman two valleys east asking her dead mother for forgiveness. There's a boy praying not to be sent down the mines. There's—" She stopped. Closed her fist. "It's not my business. None of it is my business."

"No," Dominic said. And then, because the lie sat poorly even in his mouth: "I don't know what it is anymore. That's the truth of it. The seal on the gate cracked from the *inside*, girl. In twenty years of keeping that door I never saw frost bloom in midsummer, never heard a prayer answered through another living throat. Whatever you are, you started the same week my life's work failed. I'm not leading you to Vaelmoor out of charity."

"I know." She said it without heat, without hurt, and somehow that was worse. "You're leading me because you want to understand what broke. I'm following because I want to know what I am. We're both just chasing our own wounds uphill." She looked at him across the fire, and her eyes caught the light in a way he decided not to think about. "That doesn't mean we can't share the road."

* * *

The second day brought the tollmen.

Dominic heard them before he saw them—the particular careless clatter of men who owned their stretch of road and expected the world to announce itself. Three of them at the mouth of the ravine called Kettlejaw, where the path narrowed to a width of two shoulders and a bad temper. He pulled Ulyana off the trail into a fold of rock and put a finger to his lips, and she gave him a look that said, plainly, *I was born doing this*, which stung more than it should have.

"We go around," he whispered. "There's a goat track above the ravine. Loose stone. We cross single file, quiet as graves, and we're past them by dusk."

"Or," Ulyana said, "we do it my way."

"Your way got you unconscious in the snow in summer."

"*My* way got me twenty years of eating regularly." But she followed him up the goat track, and for three hours they moved together in a silence that was almost companionable—her reading the mountain's mood in the shift of scree, him reading hers in the set of her shoulders, each adjusting to the other without discussion, the way travelers do when they are becoming, without admitting it, a unit.

At the top of the ravine, wind tearing at them both, she paused on a spine of rock no wider than a coffin and looked down at the tollmen's fire, tiny as a spark in a black bowl.

"I used to sell roads like this," she said.

Dominic kept moving. "Everyone sells something."

"Not like this." Her voice came thin over the wind. "Maps. My father drew them—passes, safehouses, which wardens took bribes and which took heads. When he died I kept selling them. And there was a man, once. Paid triple for a map of the coastal villages south of Marrow Bay. Triple, Dominic. Nobody pays triple for geography." Her boot dislodged a pebble; they both watched it fall a long time. "I told myself I didn't know what he wanted with fishing villages. I told myself a lot of things. Six months later, slavers' ships were working that coast, and they knew channels no outsider should have known."

The wind filled the pause. Below them, the tollmen laughed at some joke Dominic couldn't hear and wouldn't have liked.

"What did you do?" he asked.

"Nothing. That's the sin. Not that I sold it—I couldn't have known for certain. The sin is that I *did the arithmetic* afterward. Counted how many villages, how many families, whether any of the names matched faces I remembered. And then I stopped counting, because stopping was easier, and I bought a new coat with the money." She turned to face him on the impossible ledge, steady as a woman stepping off a cliff. "So when you look at me like I'm something that crawled out from under the world's oldest lock, remember that whatever I am now, I was a coward with good penmanship first."

Dominic stood on the rock beside her and found, to his considerable irritation, that he could not summon the judgment the moment seemed to require.

"When I was a soldier," he said, "before the gate, I burned a granary at Coldford. Requisition orders said rebels were storing arms in it. It was grain. It was winter." He looked at the fire below, at the small warm lives around it who would never know two ghosts had passed above them. "Eleven villages went hungry. I filed the report correctly. Correctly is the worst word in the army's language." He started walking again, because standing still on ledges with confessions was a road to nowhere. "So. A coward with good penmanship and a clerk with a torch. We'll make a fine pair at Vaelmoor. The scholars will write papers."

Behind him, after a moment, came that laugh again—longer this time, less smothered, startled out of her like a bird from cover. He didn't look back. Looking back would have meant she'd see his face, and his face was doing something he hadn't authorized.

* * *

The third night the rain came, and they sheltered in a smuggler's cache Dominic had used perhaps six times in fifteen years—a cavity behind a waterfall curtain, dry-floored, stocked with a wax-sealed lantern and a blanket that smelled of cedar and long forgetting. Too small for propriety. They sat back to back for warmth, which neither remarked upon, and shared the last of the bread, which neither counted.

"Tell me about the gate," Ulyana said, when the rain had become its own kind of silence.

"It's a door."

"Everything's a door if you're determined enough. Tell me about *your* door."

He was quiet so long she must have thought he wouldn't answer. Then, slowly, the words came, rusted from disuse.

"My father kept it before me. And his mother before him. Lockridges have held Thornfell since before there was a Thornfell to hold—it's not a posting, it's a bloodline. You're born knowing that somewhere north of you is a door that must never open, and you spend your whole life becoming the kind of person who can be trusted near it." He turned the lantern key between his fingers. "I never wanted it. I wanted to be a soldier, a real one, marching and banners and all the stupid glory. Instead I inherited a hinge and a prayer book and a mountain that hates visitors. And I was *good* at it, Ulyana. Twenty years, and the seal never faltered, and I told myself that was enough. Purpose enough." His voice dropped. "Then it cracked from the inside, and I understood I'd spent my life guarding a lock I never once asked what it locked *in*—or what it locked out. Or why the gods needed a door at all."

Ulyana shifted against his back. He felt her considering her words the way she considered footholds.

"When I was eight," she said, "I told my father about the voices. He looked at me for a long time and said, 'Then you'll always have work, little star, because the world is full of people who need listening to.' He thought I meant I was empathetic." A breath. "Maybe he was right. Maybe that's all any of this is—listening, taken too far." Her head tipped back until it rested against his shoulder blade, light as a falling leaf. "My father sold maps too, you know. But he always gave one away free, every year, to a family that couldn't pay. Said the roads belonged to everyone who walked them honestly. I keep thinking—if I'd given away even one map to the right person instead of selling to the wrong one..."

"Then you'd be a different woman," Dominic said, "and I'd have found someone else unconscious in the snow, and the world would be exactly as broken as it is tonight. Guilt makes us think we're the hinge everything swings on. Mostly we're just weather."

"That's either very wise or very tired."

"In my experience they arrive together and refuse to be separated."

Her laugh moved through her back into his, and for a while neither of them spoke, and the rain came down on the mountain like the world's oldest forgiveness, falling on the guilty and the innocent alike without bothering to check which was which.

* * *

On the fourth day they crossed the shoulder of the mountain called the Old Man's Tooth, and Vaelmoor opened below them—and Ulyana stopped walking so suddenly that Dominic nearly went past her before he understood.

The shrine-city lay in a high valley like a bone left in a bowl: white walls gone grey, spires leaning like tired old men, the great cathedral at its heart roofless and open to the sky. Even at this distance, even to Dominic's ungifted senses, the place had a weight to it. A hush. As though the valley itself were holding its breath.

And Ulyana—

Ulyana stood at the edge of the trail with the wind pulling her hair sideways and tears running freely down her face, and she wasn't sobbing, wasn't trembling. She was simply crying the way stone weeps water, steadily, without volition.

"They're *loud* here," she whispered. "Not the prayers. The—" Her hand rose to her sternum, pressed flat. "Under the prayers. Something underneath. Like hearing a choir through a wall, except the wall is me. Dominic, there's something under Vaelmoor that remembers singing."

He took her arm—not gently, because gentleness would have broken something in both of them—and turned her toward the descending path.

"Then let's hope the scholars know how to make it quiet," he said.

But as they started down, Ulyana glanced back once at the ruined cathedral, and Dominic saw her expression change—saw the wonder in it curdle into something older and colder, the look of a woman who has just realized the room she's entering knows her name.

Neither of them spoke again until the valley swallowed them, and the white walls of Vaelmoor rose up around them like the ribs of something vast that had lain down to die a very long time ago—and was, perhaps, not entirely finished dying.
