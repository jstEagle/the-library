## 5. The Choir of Ash

The road into Vaelmoor smelled of old smoke.

Ulyana noticed it before she saw the city—a ghost of burning that clung to the stones themselves, soaked so deep into the mountain's bones that no rain in two hundred years had washed it out. It was not the smell of a fire still living. It was the smell of a fire that had happened once, terribly, and been remembered by everything it touched.

"Scholars' city," Dominic said behind her, and his voice had gone quiet in the way it did when he was trying not to feel something. "Or it was. They said the libraries here held every prayer ever written down. Every hymn. Every name of every god there ever was." He shifted his pack on his shoulders. "Then the gods went silent, and the scholars started burning books to keep warm. And then they burned the rest because they couldn't bear to look at them."

Ulyana walked ahead of him down the switchbacking road, her boots finding the ruts worn by centuries of pilgrim feet. Below them, Vaelmoor opened up like a cupped hand: a bowl of grey stone cradling a ruined cathedral whose spire had snapped off halfway up its height, leaving a jagged stump against the pale sky like a broken tooth. The city around it was half-empty, half-collapsed, and yet—she could see the movement now, small figures on the walls, laundry strung between shattered columns, smoke rising from chimneys that should have been cold.

"People live here," she said.

"Some people live everywhere," Dominic said. "That's either a comfort or an accusation, depending on the day."

She had learned, over the days since the waystation, to read his silences as carefully as his words. This one meant he was afraid for her. He would not say it. He would walk one step closer instead, close enough that his shadow overlapped hers on the road, and pretend it was about the wind.

The voices had been louder since morning. That was why they had come—the scholars, the archives, someone who could tell her what she was. But the voices had known where she was going before she did. All through the night's camp they had murmured at the edges of her sleep, and when she woke with frost on her blanket in high summer, they had sung, briefly, joyfully, a single line in a tongue she almost understood:

*She comes back to the ash.*

She hadn't told Dominic that part. She told him most things now—that was the strange arithmetic of shared roads and shared fires—but not that. Some sentences you carried alone until you knew whether they were wounds or warnings.

* * *

Vaelmoor's streets were canyons of fallen masonry. They picked their way past a fountain choked with moss, past a statue whose face had been chiseled away—not by weather, Ulyana noted, but by hands; the chisel marks were still sharp—and past doorways bricked shut with a thoroughness that spoke of fear rather than tidiness.

A child watched them from a window, then ducked away. An old man sweeping steps looked up at Ulyana, looked too long, and dropped his broom.

"You," he said. Not *hello*. Not *who goes there*. Just *you*, in the voice of a man seeing a debt come walking down the street.

"We're looking for the Choir," Dominic said, stepping slightly forward, the way he did when he wanted to be the thing people looked at instead of her. "The ones who tend the cathedral."

The old man's eyes never left Ulyana. "There's no Choir in Vaelmoor," he said. "There hasn't been a Choir in Vaelmoor for sixty years."

"But there are people in the cathedral," Ulyana said quietly. "I can hear them praying."

Something moved across the old man's face—fear, recognition, grief, all fighting for the same ground. He bent slowly, retrieved his broom, and said, without looking at either of them: "Third door on the left past the broken bell. Knock twice. Wait. Then knock once more." He began sweeping again, furiously, as if he could sweep them out of existence. "And girl—if you've got any kindness in you at all, turn around and go home."

"I don't have a home," Ulyana said.

"No," the old man said softly. "I suppose you don't. That's how they always find you, isn't it?"

* * *

The cathedral swallowed sound.

Ulyana felt it the moment she crossed the threshold—the way her footsteps died, the way even her breathing seemed to be taken from her and filed away somewhere in the dark. The nave stretched up and up into gloom, its vaulted ceiling lost in shadow, its windows long since shattered so that evening light fell through empty frames in hard geometric blades. Rows of pews lay rotted to lace. The great altar was cracked down the middle, split clean as a loaf.

And everywhere, in the walls, under the floor, in the hollow places where stone had crumbled from stone—prayers. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Prayers pressed into mortar by kneeling hands, scratched into pillars by people who had run out of hope and started writing directly to heaven. She could hear them the way other people heard dripping water: constant, layered, patient. A sea of asking.

"They're still here," she whispered. "All of them. Nobody answered them and they just—stayed."

"That's not poetry," Dominic murmured. "That's a graveyard."

"It's both. It's usually both."

They had gone perhaps thirty paces into the nave when the candles lit.

Not all at once. One, then another, then a slow ripple of small flames waking along the ruined aisles, dozens of them, hundreds, until the darkness organized itself into shapes—and Ulyana saw that the shapes were people. Dozens of them, seated motionless among the ruins, robed in undyed grey, their faces turned toward her with the terrible unified attention of a congregation. Old faces, young faces, faces marked with scars she would later learn to read like text.

None of their eyes reflected the candlelight.

Some had eyes that were simply gone—lidded over smooth, or sunken, or scarred shut. Others had eyes that were open and whole and saw nothing, gazing at her with the blank serenity of statues. But it was more than blindness. Blind people's eyes still caught light. These eyes drank it.

"The Choir of Ash," said a voice from the altar.

A woman stood there, small and straight-backed, leaning on a staff of blackened wood. She was ancient in the way mountains are ancient—not decrepit but worn, her features eroded down to essentials, her white hair cropped short as a penitent's. Her eyes, too, were blind. A scarf covered the upper half of her face, and beneath its edge Ulyana could see the shine of old burns.

"You knocked wrong," the woman said. "Twice, wait, once. You knocked three times fast, like merchants do." Her mouth curved. "Smuggler's daughter."

Ulyana went very still. Beside her, she heard Dominic's hand move toward his knife.

"How—" she started.

"You walk like your mother," the woman said. "I bought brandy from her, twenty years ago, in a town that doesn't exist anymore. Don't look so alarmed, child. I know nothing else about you. Except," and here her voice changed, dropped into something older and sadder, "except what you are. Which I knew before you reached the fountain. We all did." She gestured, and the seated figures inclined their heads in unison, a wave moving through the congregation like wind through wheat. "You are very loud, little speaker. You ring like a struck bell. Every god-speaker in this room felt you coming for three days."

"I'm not a speaker," Ulyana said. "I don't know what I am. That's why we came. There were supposed to be scholars—archives—someone who could—"

"The scholars are dead or scattered. The archives are ash." The woman tapped her staff against the cracked altar, and the sound rang strangely, ringing on and on, longer than wood and stone had any right to sustain. "What's left is us. What's left is always us." She descended the altar steps slowly, feeling with the staff, though Ulyana suspected—watching the sureness of her feet—that the staff was ceremony rather than necessity. "I am Mother Serafim. Come and sit. Both of you. You look like two people who haven't eaten since the last town, which means you ate in the last town, which means you're doing better than most pilgrims."

* * *

They ate in a side chapel roofed with salvaged slate, at a table made from a pew laid across two barrels. The food was simple—barley soup, dark bread, dried apples—but hot, and there was enough of it, and after days of trail rations Ulyana ate like it was a religious experience in itself.

Around them, the Choir moved through the cathedral's dusk on silent errands: tending candles, carrying water, guiding each other through the wreckage with touches on shoulders and elbows. It should have been grim. Instead there was something in the rhythm of it—unhurried, unafraid—that reminded Ulyana of her father's crew working cargo at midnight, that particular grace of people who know exactly where everything is in the dark.

"They burned themselves," Dominic said quietly, watching a young man with seared eyelids stack firewood with perfect accuracy. "Didn't they. That's what the Choir is. Speakers who put their own gift out."

Mother Serafim sat across from them, hands folded around a tin cup of tea she never drank. "Burned is a kind word," she said. "We say *harrowed*. When the gods went silent—truly silent, not sleeping-silent but gone-silent—the speakers were the ones who suffered worst. Imagine a fisherman whose sea dries up overnight. Now imagine the sea screaming at him first. Screaming, and begging, and then going quiet mid-word." She turned her blind face toward Ulyana. "You've heard the silence. You know what I mean."

Ulyana's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth. "It didn't stop," she said. "For me. It never stopped. That's what I thought madness was. Hearing prayers meant for gods that weren't listening anymore. For twenty years I thought my head was just... full of ghosts with nowhere to go."

"And then?" Serafim asked, though her tone said she already knew.

"And then they started answering back."

The chapel went quiet. Even the ambient sounds of the Choir seemed to hush, as if the whole building were leaning in.

"Tell me exactly," Serafim said. "From the beginning. Leave nothing out. The details matter more than you can possibly imagine."

So Ulyana told her. The frost in summer. The dreams of strangers kneeling. The pilgrim at the waystation with the withered hand, and the moment the old god's voice had poured through her throat like water through a broken dam, and the hand had straightened, and the woman had wept, and Ulyana had vomited into the yard afterward because something vast had passed through her and taken a piece of her warmth as toll. She told her about the war-god in the chapel tower—Dominic flinched at that part, and she understood why; she'd leveled a holy place and they still hadn't spoken of it properly—and about the voices growing louder, closer, more *specific*, as if whatever listened on the far side of the silence was leaning nearer with every word she spoke.

Serafim listened without interrupting. When Ulyana finished, the old woman was silent for a long time, turning her cup in her scarred hands.

"Say the line again," she said at last. "The one they sang to you. In the old tongue."

"*She comes back to the ash,*" Ulyana said.

Serafim's breath went out of her slowly. Around the chapel, several of the Choir had stopped moving entirely.

"That's not a welcome," the old woman said. "Child, that is the oldest warning we have. It's carved above the door of every house in this city that predates the burning. *She comes back to the ash.* We thought it meant the gods returning to their burnt-out churches." She set the cup down. Her hands, Ulyana noticed, were trembling now. "It means you. It has always meant you. Or someone like you. The one who comes back."

"Comes back from where?"

"Sit closer," Serafim said, "and I will tell you what the scholars died knowing."

* * *

They talked until the candles burned low.

Serafim spoke of the long silence—not as absence but as siege. Of the generations of speakers born after the gods went quiet, children who heard prayers stacking up unanswered like snow against a door. Of what it did to them. Some went mad. Some became frauds, selling false miracles to desperate villages. And some—the strongest, the ones the prayers loved best—began to hear something else in the silence. Something that wasn't a god at all.

"There is a hunger," Serafim said, "that wears a crown."

The words landed in Ulyana's chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of nothing—told herself she thought of nothing—but beside her she felt Dominic go rigid, and knew he was thinking of the same rumors she was: the king who no longer aged quite right, the court that had closed itself like a fist, the royal processions where the crowds knelt and something in the air felt *wrong*, hungry, attentive.

"Every speaker I ever knew of," Serafim continued, "every true conduit of divine fire born in the last two hundred years—I can name them, there were eleven—they all heard it eventually. It speaks in the silence between prayers. It offers. Oh, how it offers. Purpose. Power. An end to loneliness. It says: *you are a door with no house, little door. Come be part of mine.*" Her voice roughened. "Marta of the Lowlands said yes. She was nineteen. They found her village emptied, every soul gone, and Marta standing in the square wearing a smile that didn't fit her face, and she said, before they burned her, that the king had come for her personally, and that he was so *kind*, and that she had never been so full."

"And the others?" Ulyana asked, though the cold in her stomach already knew.

"Devoured. All of them. Some quickly, some slowly. The crowned hunger drinks divine fire wherever it finds it, and a living speaker is a cup left out in the rain." Serafim leaned forward, and the candlelight moved on her burn-scarred face. "Why do you think we harrowed ourselves? Fire is the only thing that closes a door the gods opened. We took our gifts and we burned them out at the root—pain beyond describing, and worth every hour of it—because a dead conduit cannot be drunk from. We chose ash over appetite." She spread her scarred hands. "This is what survival looks like, child. This is the shape of our victory. Look at it well."

Ulyana looked. At the blind eyes. The ruined faces. The beautiful, terrible discipline of people who had amputated the best part of themselves to keep it from being stolen.

"Why are you telling me this?" she whispered. "You must know what I'm going to ask."

"Because you haven't asked yet," Serafim said. "And because I want one hour of being honest with you before you do."

* * *

Later—much later, when Dominic had been given a pallet in the monks' cells and the cathedral had settled into its breathing dark—Serafim came to where Ulyana sat alone in the nave, wrapped in a donated blanket, listening to the ten thousand unanswered prayers hum in the stones.

The old woman lowered herself onto the pew beside her with a sigh, staff clacking against the floor.

"My teacher," Serafim said, "was the last speaker the Choir harrowed before me. Her name was Ilse. She fought us, when we came for her with the fire. Fought like a wildcat. Said she'd rather be devoured than be less than she was." Her mouth twisted. "In the end we held her down and did it anyway, and she thanked us afterward, weeping, because she could finally hear her own thoughts again. Do you understand what I'm telling you? The gift is real. The power is real. And it is bait on a hook that has never once come up empty."

"You want me to burn it out," Ulyana said. "Like you did."

"I want you to live," Serafim said simply. "What method you choose is your own."

"And if I don't? If I keep it? If I learn to control it—shield myself, ration it, do it *carefully*—" She heard how young she sounded and hated it.

"Then you will be the twelfth name on my list," Serafim said, "and I will carve it into the pillar with the others, and I will mourn you, and the world will go on not noticing." She paused. "Unless."

"Unless what?"

"Unless the crowned hunger gets to you first. And child—" the old woman turned her blind face toward her, and in the dark her scarred features looked suddenly, terribly gentle— "I think it already knows you're here. I think it has known since the day you were born. I think that is why the gods woke up *now*, after two hundred years of silence, answering through *your* throat specifically. The fire is rising on both sides of the board. And you are the square in the middle."

Far above them, in the broken spire, wind moved through empty window frames with a sound almost like singing.

Almost like a choir.

Ulyana pulled the blanket tighter and stared into the dark where the candles had gone out, and for the first time since the waystation, since the pilgrim's hand, since the first impossible miracle, she was afraid—not of what she might become, but of the specific, patient certainty in Serafim's voice, the certainty of a woman who had watched eleven people walk into the same open mouth.

Somewhere below the floor, beneath the prayers, beneath the ash, something vast and buried shifted in its sleep—and every voice in Ulyana's head went silent at once, all together, like a congregation turning to look at the door.

Footsteps were crossing the cathedral square outside. Many footsteps. Unhurried. In perfect step.

Dominic appeared at the end of the nave, knife already drawn, and said the words she would remember for the rest of her life:

"Ulyana. There are pilgrims at the gate. Royal pilgrims. And their eyes don't reflect the candles."
