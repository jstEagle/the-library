## 6. A King's Empty Eyes

Dominic had counted pilgrims for eleven years. It was the first thing a gatekeeper learned at Thornfell: how to read a column of travelers from the height of the wall, how to sort merchants from refugees from soldiers wearing borrowed cloaks. He'd once picked out three assassins in a funeral procession of two hundred mourners, and he'd done it with nothing but the way they carried their weight—too balanced, too quiet, grief performed by men who had never grieved.

So when he looked down from the cathedral's bell tower into the square of Vaelmoor, he knew within ten heartbeats that the thirty-one figures filing through the western arch were not pilgrims at all.

They were too clean. That was the first tell. Real pilgrims came up the mountain road with dust in their seams and blisters changing their stride, and by the time they reached Vaelmoor their devotion looked like exhaustion. These ones moved like men who had bathed that morning and would bathe again tomorrow. Their robes were the undyed gray of royal charity, spotless, and beneath them Dominic could see the line of shoulders that belonged to men who drilled daily with polearms. They walked in step without meaning to. Soldiers always did. It was the last habit to die and sometimes it outlived the man.

But it was the eyes that made his hands go cold on the tower rail.

The square was lit for evening—a hundred candles in glass jars along the colonnade, the great rose window throwing its broken colors across the flagstones, torches guttering at the shrine doors. Light everywhere. And face after face passed through it, and not one of those faces caught it. The light went *into* their eyes and stopped, like rain falling down a well. No glint, no shine, no spark of the candleflame reflected back. Thirty-one pairs of dark holes walking upright through a festival of light.

Dominic had seen dead men before. He had buried friends under cairns on the pass and burned enemies in the moat ditch. Dead men's eyes were worse than this, because at least dead men's eyes were closed.

He took the tower stairs two at a time.

* * *

"They're asking for shelter," Ulyana said when he found her in the nave. She was standing where he'd left her, near the shattered altar of the Choir of Ash, but she'd gone very still, the way she did when the voices pressed close. "The matriarch sent word up. Thirty-one of them. They say they've walked from the capital to pray at the last holy place in Thornfell."

"The capital is six weeks' walk," Dominic said. "They're not sweating."

"Then what are they?"

He hesitated. There was no gentle way to build what he had to build. "I need you to stay away from the windows. Stay away from the doors. If the matriarch lets them in—and she will, because refusing pilgrims is against every law this place has left—I need you somewhere with stone between you and them."

"Dominic." She turned fully toward him, and he saw her take his measure the way she took everything: quickly, hungrily, like a woman reading a map for escape routes. "You know who they are."

"I know what they look like." He crossed the nave, kept his voice low even though the Choir's few remaining members were nowhere near. Old Sera was asleep in the vestry; the blind matriarch sat in her chair by the cold hearth as she always did, hands folded, listening to a house that had stopped speaking years ago. "I served six years in the king's army before they posted me to Thornfell. I know how royal guards move. And I know one of them."

Ulyana went white. "One of the ones outside?"

"Not outside." The words tasted like ash and old wine. "Their captain. He walked into the square ahead of the rest, and he stood under the rose window, and he looked up at this cathedral the way you look at something you intend to own." Dominic's jaw ached; he realized he was clenching it. "His name is Aldric Vane. We shared a tent for four winters. He pulled me out of the marshes at Cael Ford with a rope around his waist and his teeth. When my father died, he sat with me all night because I couldn't be alone and wouldn't ask anyone." He stopped. Started again, quieter. "I drank at his wedding. I held his daughter when she was three days old."

"And now?"

"And now his eyes don't reflect candles."

* * *

They were admitted at moonrise.

Dominic watched from the shadow of the north colonnade as the thirty-one filed into the cathedral's outer court, kneeling in perfect unison on the frost-slick stones, bowing their heads toward the ruined nave. Aldric Vane came last. He had changed, and he had not changed. The same broad frame, the same scar splitting his left eyebrow—the one he'd gotten at Cael Ford, diving back into the water for Dominic a second time. The same easy set to his mouth that had made him the best-liked man in any company he ever served.

But he knelt like a puppet whose strings had been tied slightly wrong. And when he raised his head, Dominic made himself look directly into his eyes from forty feet away, and there was nothing looking back. Not hatred. Not emptiness, even. Emptiness would have been something. This was absence wearing a familiar face, a doorway standing open onto a room where the fire had gone out decades ago and no one had ever come to relight it.

"Matriarch," Vane said, and his voice was Aldric's voice, warm and rough and real enough to make Dominic's chest hurt. "We come seeking the blessing of the Choir. We have walked far."

The old woman turned her blind face toward him. She could not see him—she had told Ulyana she hadn't seen anything since the burning—but her head tracked him anyway, precise as a compass needle finding a wrong north.

"You have walked far," she agreed. "And something has walked *in* you. Further than your feet ever carried it."

A ripple went through the kneeling ranks—not fear, exactly. Adjustment. Thirty-one bodies recalibrating around a complication.

Vane smiled. It was Aldric's smile, the one that had gotten extra rations out of quartermasters and forgiveness out of sergeants. "Grief makes poets of us all, mother. We only want to pray."

"Then pray," the matriarch said. "The gods you serve will not answer. The ones who might are otherwise engaged." She rose from her chair, small and straight-backed, terrible in her plainness. "You may sleep in the outer court. You may draw water from the well. You will not enter the nave. The nave belongs to the ash, and the ash does not welcome the crowned."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment—Dominic thought it would end there. Thought Vane would bow, and smile, and withdraw, and whatever patience moved behind his empty eyes would simply wait for a better night.

Then Vane's gaze lifted, past the matriarch, past the colonnade, and found the shadow where Dominic stood.

It found him the way a hook finds a fish.

"*Dom,*" Aldric Vane said, and laughed, and it was his laugh, it was the laugh from a hundred nights around a hundred fires, and Dominic felt something in his chest tear cleanly down the middle. "Six years I watched the wall at Thornfell and you never once came down to drink with me. And now I have to walk six weeks to find you hiding in a church."

"Aldric." His voice came out steadier than he deserved. "Whoever you were. Whatever's driving you now. Walk away. Take your people and walk away tonight, and I'll never speak of this."

"Walk away?" Vane tilted his head, and the gesture was almost childlike, almost puzzled. "From the most important work in the history of the world? Dom. We're here for the girl. The vessel. The little conduit." He said it gently, the way you'd mention weather. "Give her to us, and no one burns. You remember Cael Ford. You know I keep my promises."

Behind him, in the nave, Dominic heard Ulyana's breath catch. She had disobeyed him. Of course she had. She was standing just inside the doorway, half in candlelight, and thirty-one eyeless faces turned toward her with the smooth simultaneity of a flock changing direction.

"There she is," Vane said softly.

* * *

What happened next, happened fast, and Dominic would spend the rest of his life reassembling it from fragments.

Sera screamed from the vestry—the young Choir acolyte had woken and seen the court through her window. The Hollow Guard rose from its knees as one body, and the pretense dropped off them like a shed cloak: robes torn away, polearms already in hand, moving in the drill-perfect silence of men who had done this a thousand times in a thousand towns that no longer had names.

The matriarch stepped into their path. She did not shout. She simply stood, blind and ancient, in front of the girl, and said, "No."

The guard captain nearest her drove his polearm forward without breaking stride.

Dominic was moving before he decided to move. He came off the colonnade with his knife low and his shoulder leading and put himself between the old woman and the blade, and the world narrowed to the oldest arithmetic he knew: weight, angle, distance, kill. He caught the haft on his forearm—it would bruise to the bone—and drove his knife up under the man's jaw, and the man died like a door closing, folding without sound, and Dominic was already turning to the next one because there was always a next one, that was the whole truth of soldiering, there was always a next one until suddenly there wasn't.

They came through the colonnade in files of three. He killed a second, wounded a third, took a slash across the thigh that soaked his trouser leg hot in seconds. He heard the matriarch dragging Ulyana backward, heard Ulyana screaming his name, heard Sera sobbing somewhere behind the font. And through it all Aldric Vane stood at the edge of the fight and watched, hands loose at his sides, head tilted, studying Dominic's footwork with something that might once have been pride.

"You always were the better fighter, Dom," he called out, conversational, over the clash of steel. "Never could beat you. Isn't that funny? All this, and I still can't beat you."

"Aldric—" Dominic parried a thrust, gave ground, felt the colonnade pillar hard against his back. "Fight it. Whatever has you. You pulled me out of a marsh with your *teeth*, you stubborn bastard, so fight—"

"I remember the marsh." For just a moment—half a heartbeat—something flickered in those empty eyes. Something drowned and far away. "I remember everything. That's the horror of it, Dom. I'm still in here. I'm so tired. I'm so—"

The flicker vanished. The absence reasserted itself, smooth as water closing over a stone, and Vane's voice came out flat and many-layered, several throats speaking through one: "*Take the vessel.*"

They rushed him. Four at once, disciplined, rotating, the way you bring down a man you respect. Dominic got his knife into one's thigh, broke another's nose with his elbow, and then a polearm stock caught him across the temple and the world went white and sideways and he was on his knees in the frost with blood sheeting into his eye, watching a blade rise over him, and his last coherent thought was absurdly small: *I never taught her to run properly.*

Ulyana screamed.

It was not a scream like other screams. It opened something. Dominic felt it in his teeth, in the scar tissue of old wounds, in the fillings of his jaw—and he felt something else rising behind it, something vast turning over in the deep water of the world, something that had been waiting two thousand years to be asked.

"*KHARNETH,*" Ulyana said—or the thing that wore her mouth said, in a voice like siege engines and burning fields, a voice that made the frost jump off the flagstones—and the name was not a word so much as a wound in the air.

The chapel tower of Saint Verene, standing at the cathedral square's corner for nine hundred years, taller than anything else in Vaelmoor, older than the kingdom itself—

The tower leaned.

Stone groaned like a living thing. Then, with a slowness that was somehow worse than speed, the whole nine-hundred-year column of it peeled away from its foundations and fell—not toward the cathedral, not toward the fleeing acolytes, but across the colonnade, across the advancing files of the Hollow Guard, a landslide given architecture, a mountain's worth of anger compressed into one collapsing bell tower.

The sound was beyond sound. Dominic felt it in the wet meat of his lungs. Dust swallowed the square. When it thinned, the tower was a ruin of rubble spanning the colonnade like a fallen god, and beneath it, motionless, lay seven of the Hollow Guard. The rest had been thrown to the edges of the square, broken and crawling. Of Aldric Vane there was no sign at all—he had simply not been where the tower fell, or perhaps the thing inside him had known, had stepped him aside with that inhuman precision, taking its captain out of the jaws of the trap at the last instant.

Silence, except for the settling of stone and the drip of Dominic's blood on the frost.

He got up. It took three tries. He limped through the dust to where Ulyana stood at the center of the square, arms still raised, and the sight of her stopped his heart harder than the blade had.

She was glowing. Faintly, terribly, from inside—light moving under her skin like fire under paper, tracing the lines of her veins in gold. Her eyes were open and they were not hers; they were old and molten and utterly without mercy, and they were looking at the rubble where men were dying slowly, and they were *pleased.*

"Alyana," he rasped. "Ulyana. Come back. Look at me. Look at *me.*"

Her head turned. Slowly. The molten gaze fixed on him, and for one endless moment he stood in the attention of something that had broken cities for worship, and he understood with total clarity that his life hung on whether some fragment of the smuggler's daughter was still home behind those eyes.

Then she shuddered, all at once, like a horse shedding a burden, and the light went out of her veins, and she collapsed. He caught her before the frost did.

She was weeping. Great, silent, convulsive sobs, her face pressed into his bloody shoulder, her fists gripping his jacket like a drowning woman.

"I felt them die," she whispered. "All of them. Under the stones. I felt every one, and it was—Dominic, it was *warm*. It was warm like a meal. Something in me *enjoyed* it." Her voice cracked apart. "That wasn't mine. Tell me that wasn't mine."

"It wasn't yours." He held her, his own vision swimming, the tower's dust settling over them both like grey snow. Behind them the matriarch stood in the wreckage of her square, sightless eyes lifted to the missing tower, and old Sera was vomiting by the well, and somewhere in the dark beyond the walls the surviving Guard was retreating in perfect step, carrying its broken home to whatever had made it.

"It wasn't yours," he said again, and did not know if it was true, and held her anyway.

Because that was the arithmetic he understood best. There was always a next one. But tonight, holding a girl who had just leveled a chapel tower with a dead god's voice, Dominic Lockridge finally understood what the voices must feel like—what it cost to be the instrument of something enormous. Power used in desperation, he thought, was not power at all.

It was theft. Someone always paid.

And far off in the mountains, faint as a heartbeat, he would have sworn he felt the ground shift—once, twice—as though something vast below Thornfell had rolled over in its sleep, disturbed by the noise, beginning to wake.
