## 7. Emberlast Sleeps Beneath

The vision began with ash falling upward.

Ulyana had felt the matriarch's hands settle on her temples like two cool stones, had heard Dominic's protest cut short by the blind woman's raised finger, and then the world had folded inward like a page turning. Now she stood—or seemed to stand—in a cathedral that was not the ruined one around her but its memory, whole and golden, its windows blazing with saints she did not recognize, its floors crowded with kneeling thousands. And everything, everything, was drifting up: ash rising from the ground like snow in reverse, climbing toward a sky that was not sky at all but a vast dark ceiling, close enough to touch.

*You are inside my remembering,* said the matriarch's voice, though the matriarch's body stood beside her still—small, bent, her clouded eyes turned toward Ulyana with the terrible accuracy of the truly blind. *Or what is left of it. Forgive the state of it. Memory burns from the edges inward, and mine has been burning a long time.*

"Where are we?" Ulyana asked. Her voice sounded wrong here—too small, too mortal, a sparrow's voice in a mountain's throat.

*Vaelmoor,* the matriarch answered. *Four hundred years ago. When the cathedral had a roof. When the Choir had voices.* She lifted one hand, and the ash-swarming air stirred, and the kneeling thousands stirred with it, turning their faces upward in unison. Ulyana saw then that their mouths were open. All of them. Every one of the thousands, mouths open, faces lifted, and no sound coming out.

*We were the speakers,* the matriarch said. *Before the silence. When a god wished to be heard in the world of flesh, it needed a throat, and we were the throats. It was not a gift. I want you to understand that first, before anything else I show you. It was never a gift. It was a door, and doors open from both sides.*

The cathedral shuddered. The golden light guttered. And Ulyana felt it—felt it the way she had felt every prayer since childhood, but magnified ten thousandfold, a tide of voices pressing against the inside of her skull, not words now but *weight*, the accumulated longing of a world that had begun, all at once, to pray to gods who no longer answered.

*This is the night the gods went quiet,* the matriarch said, and there was something in her voice Ulyana had never heard there before. Not the calm of the ruined cathedral, not the warning. It was grief, old and worn smooth as a river stone. *Not dead. Never dead. Quiet. There is a difference, child, and the difference is the whole of the story I have to tell you.*

* * *

The cathedral dissolved. The kneeling thousands dissolved. What replaced them was cold and dark and smelled of stone and deep water, and Ulyana understood, with a lurch, that they were underground—far underground, deeper than mines go, deeper than roots, in a dark so total that it seemed less like the absence of light than the presence of something that had eaten it.

*Deeper,* the matriarch murmured, and Ulyana realized she had said it aloud, or thought it, and that here the difference did not matter.

They descended. There were no stairs. The dark simply yielded, the way a dream yields, and they moved through it together, the blind woman walking sure-footed as a cat, her hand resting on Ulyana's wrist with a grip that was gentle and absolutely unbreakable.

*Every speaker in the history of the Choir has had this dream,* the matriarch said. *Every one of us, the night our gift first woke. We thought it was a metaphor. We were young, and arrogant, and we thought everything the gods showed us was a metaphor.* A dry sound, almost a laugh. *It is not a metaphor. It is a place. And the thing that lives here is not a metaphor either.*

"Then what is it?" Ulyana whispered.

The dark answered before the matriarch could.

It began as warmth. That was the first thing—not light, not sound, but warmth, rising through the stone beneath Ulyana's bare feet like the memory of a hearth in a house she had never lived in. Then a pulse, slow and vast, so slow that at first she mistook it for the mountain's own breathing, and then understood with a vertigo that dropped through her like a stone down a well that it was not the mountain breathing at all.

It was a heart.

*There,* the matriarch said softly. *You feel it. Every speaker feels it. The oldest of us could feel it from Vaelmoor on a quiet night, if the wind was right. We told ourselves it was the gods, sleeping beneath the world. We built our cathedral over the place where it beat loudest and called ourselves the keepers of the divine. We were wrong about that too.* Her grip tightened on Ulyana's wrist. *Look, child. I will show you. It will hurt. It is supposed to hurt. The hurting is how you know it is true.*

Light came.

Not all at once—it came the way dawn comes in a deep valley, seeping up from below, and Ulyana saw that the darkness around them was not empty at all. The cavern—they were in a cavern now, though cavern seemed too small a word, cathedral seemed too small a word, *country* might have been closer—was ribbed with stone the color of old iron, arching overhead in curves that were not architecture. They were ribs. She was standing inside something's body, and the body was the size of a mountain range, and the warmth was coming from a coiled shape at the center of it all, a shape her eyes could only take in a piece at a time, the way a man in a valley can only take in a piece of the sky.

A dragon.

It lay coiled around a hollow of black glass, its scales the grey of banked coals, its wings folded like the sails of a ship long anchored. Its head rested on the glass, and its eye—when Ulyana found it, and she could not help finding it, because it was the size of a cart wheel and it was *open*—was the color of a dying fire. Embers without flame. A hearth the morning after the festival, when everyone has gone home.

And in the center of the coil, in the hollow of black glass the dragon's body sheltered, there was fire.

It was small.

That was the thing Ulyana would remember afterward, the thing that would wake her for nights afterward with her heart slamming: it was *small*. A candle-flame. A candle-flame standing alone in a cavern the size of a nation, burning above the black glass, and around it the dragon's body was curled like a wall, like a shrine, like two hands cupped against the wind.

*Emberlast,* the matriarch said, and the name went through the vision like a bell through water. *The last. The only. The fire that would not go out when all the others did.*

Ulyana's mouth was dry. "That's it? That's the fire? It's—" She had imagined infernos. She had imagined, without knowing it, something like the sun buried under the mountains, and what she saw was a candle.

*Yes,* the matriarch said. *That is exactly what it is. A candle. And do you know what a candle is, child? A candle is a wick, and a wick is a promise that the burning will last exactly as long as the wax. Two thousand years ago, that flame was a furnace. It lit the deep places of the world. The gods themselves warmed their hands at it. And it has burned down and burned down and burned down, year by year, age by age, and no one has fed it, because there is nothing left to feed it, and now—* she gestured, and the vision *zoomed*, horribly, and Ulyana saw the flame in truth: saw that it was not steady at all, that it guttered, that it bent and shrank and rallied and bent again, a flame fighting for every second of its own existence— *now it is the size of a candle. And when a candle reaches the end of its wax, child, what happens?*

"It goes out," Ulyana said. The words came out of her like something being pulled.

*It goes out,* the matriarch agreed. *And here is what the gods did not tell us, here is what we burned ourselves hollow trying to learn: that flame is not a symbol. It is a lock's hinge. It is the one thing keeping the prison door ajar.*

* * *

The vision turned, and Ulyana saw the prison.

She saw it the way you see a thing in a nightmare—completely, instantly, without the mercy of gradual understanding. A door. A door in the fabric of everything, vast beyond vast, and upon it a crown was riveted like a seal, and behind the door the old gods pressed—not clawing, not raging, but *waiting*, with the patience of things that do not measure time as flesh measures it. The door was not shut. That was the horror of it. It was not shut, and it was not open. It stood ajar by the width of a candle-flame's light, held by that thin golden line of fire, and everything in the world—the prayers, the voices, the silence, the hollow thing in the capital wearing a king's face—everything balanced on that gap.

*The binders made the door,* the matriarch said. *The binders lit the flame to hold it ajar—ajar, do you understand, never shut, because a door shut on a god does not hold a god, it only makes the god *patient*. The flame was the compromise. The flame was mercy. As long as it burned, the gods could not come through, but neither did they suffocate behind the seal, and neither did the door grow brittle with disuse. A living lock. A breathing lock. And the dragon—* her voice caught, and for a moment the matriarch of the Choir of Ash sounded like what she had once been, a young woman with a gift she had not yet learned to fear— *the dragon was the wick-holder. The keeper of the flame. It has not slept in two thousand years, child. It has *watched*. There is a difference, and the difference is the whole of its suffering.*

"And the hollow king?" Ulyana asked. Her voice shook. "The crowned hunger. What does the fire have to do with *him*?"

The vision darkened. The flame shrank, in the seeing, until it was a single point of gold in an ocean of black, and around that point Ulyana began to see shapes moving—shapes that were not shapes, hunger given a suggestion of form, a vast and patient absence circling the light the way a moth circles in reverse, drawn not to the flame but to the *door* it held open.

*The crown was forged from the binding itself,* the matriarch said. *From the same fire, the same words, the same sacrifice. It was meant to be the key that only a binder could use. But fire does not only warm, child. Fire also *attracts*. Everything that hungers for the divine has been circling that flame for two thousand years, and one of them—cleverer, crueler, more patient than the rest—found a way to climb the light. It crawled up the candle's glow like a moth up a wick, and it found the crown, and it found the man wearing the crown, and it—*

The matriarch stopped. In the ruined cathedral, in the world of flesh, Ulyana could hear the old woman's breath going ragged, could feel her hands trembling against her temples. But in the vision the matriarch's voice went on, steady and terrible.

*It did not devour him. That is what no one understands. It could have devoured him—souls are easy, souls are bread. Instead it kept him. It wears him the way you wear a coat that still holds the shape of the man who owns it, because a hollow thing cannot touch the flame, child. A hollow thing has no fire of its own, and the flame does not recognize it, and the lock does not answer it. It needs a living shape. It needs a king's shape, a binder's shape, a shape the fire will open for. And so it keeps the man, starved and buried and whispering his own name in the dark of himself, and it waits, and it has been waiting for centuries, because it thinks in centuries, the way you think in breaths.*

"And now," Ulyana said, and her voice was very small in the vastness, "it's heard me."

*It has heard you.* The matriarch's blind eyes, in the vision, seemed to hold all the light of the guttering flame. *A new speaker, after two thousand years of silence. A throat the gods are answering. Do you see it now, child? Do you see why we burned out our own gifts, all of us, why we chose ash over voice? Every speaker before you, the crowned hunger found. Every speaker before you, it came for—not to kill, killing is nothing to it, but to *use*. A speaker is a door into the world of flesh. A speaker who stands before the flame, who the flame will recognize—* she shook her head, and the motion carried through the vision like a tremor through stone. *You are not a girl who hears voices, Ulyana. You are the last wick. You are the hand that can feed the fire or smother it or carry it away. And the thing in the crown is coming, and it is patient, and it does not sleep.*

The flame guttered. In the vision, Ulyana saw it bend nearly flat, saw the dragon's vast head lift a fraction—an inch of movement that would have leveled a village—and saw the ember-colored eye turn, slowly, terribly, and fix on *her*.

It saw her. Through two thousand years of stone and silence and the matriarch's dying memory, through the vision and the flesh and the veil between, the last dragon looked at Ulyana the smuggler's daughter, and the look was not wonder and it was not welcome.

It was recognition. The look you give a debt walking through your door.

*It has been waiting for a binder to come back,* the matriarch whispered. *Waiting to be finished, or undone. It does not know which you are. Neither do I. Neither, I think, do you.*

* * *

The vision broke like ice underfoot.

Ulyana came back to herself in the ruined cathedral on her knees, gasping, Dominic's hands on her shoulders—he had not left, he had not let her fall—and the matriarch standing over her, swaying slightly, her sightless face turned toward the broken roof as if she could see the sky through it, or something beyond the sky.

The candle stubs around them had all gone out. Every one. And the air of the cathedral, which had been cold all evening, was colder now, cold in a way that felt less like weather than like attention.

"How long?" Ulyana managed.

"An hour," Dominic said. His voice was rough. "You screamed twice. You said a word I've never heard. The old tongue, the matriarch called it."

"Emberlast," Ulyana said.

The matriarch's breath caught. "You saw it."

"I saw it." Ulyana pushed herself upright. Her whole body ached, as though she had carried the mountain's weight for an hour, and perhaps she had. "I saw the fire. It's dying. It's the size of a *candle*, and it's dying, and the door—" She stopped, because saying it in the flesh made it real in the flesh, and some cowardly part of her wanted to keep it in the vision where it could not touch her. She said it anyway. "The door is held open by that flame. And the hollow king can't touch it himself. He needs a shape the fire will recognize. He needs a binder."

The matriarch lowered herself onto the broken pew beside her, slowly, like a woman setting down a weight carried too long. "Now you understand what the Choir understood, in the end. Why we burned ourselves out. Why we chose silence." Her clouded eyes found Ulyana's face with terrible precision. "A speaker is a wick, child. And everything that hungers will climb a lit wick."

"Then we go to it," Dominic said.

They both turned to him. He stood with his arms crossed, jaw set, looking exactly like the man who had spent twenty years keeping a gate that should stay shut, and Ulyana loved him a little for it, and was afraid for him a great deal more.

"The dragon," he said. "Under Thornfell. That's where the flame is, that's where the door is, and that's where she'll have to be before *he* gets there. So we go. Tonight. Now." He paused, and something crossed his face that was almost a smile and almost grief. "I've been keeping that gate my whole life. Time I learned what I was keeping it *for*."

The matriarch rose. From the folds of her robe she drew something small and pressed it into Ulyana's palm: a sliver of black glass, warm as a coal, and inside it, deep in its dark, a single point of gold light that pulsed—slow, vast, patient.

A scale. A dragon's scale, and it was still alive.

"Emberlast gave this to the first of the Choir," the matriarch said, "with a message for the binder who would finally come. I have carried it sixty years, waiting for a hand it would warm." Her fingers closed Ulyana's around it. "It is warming yours."

Ulyana looked down at the scale, at the ember of light beating inside it like a tiny captive heart, and the message came with the warmth, rising in her not through her ears but through the place behind her sternum where all the voices lived—and for once, for the first time in her life, all the other voices went quiet to let this one through.

*Come,* said the dragon, in a voice like the last coals of the world. *Come and see what your hands have made, binder. And come *quickly*—for the wax is nearly gone, and the moth is already climbing the light.*
