## 8. The Price of Answers

The scale went cold in her palm before the dragon's voice had fully faded, and Ulyana stood in the Choir of Ash's undercroft holding a piece of a god the way she'd once held stolen silver—carefully, guiltily, knowing it was not hers.

Above her, through the broken vault of Vaelmoor's cathedral, the stars were coming out. Somewhere in the ruined cloisters the matriarch was singing the evening office to no one, her blind face lifted toward a heaven that had stopped listening two thousand years ago and had, apparently, just started again.

*Come quickly.*

Ulyana closed her fist around the scale until its edges bit.

"No," Dominic said.

She hadn't heard him come down the stair. He stood at the mouth of the undercroft with a lamp in one hand and his jaw set in the particular way she'd learned meant he'd already lost the argument in his own head and was fighting it anyway, on principle, because principle was all he had left of the man he used to be.

"You don't even know what I'm going to say."

"You're going to say yes." He came down the last steps, boots ringing on old stone. "You've got the yes-face. I've seen it twice now. Once at the toll bridge, once when you channeled a war-god through my chapel tower."

"That tower was already leaning."

"It was *standing*, Ulyana. There's a difference, and I lived in it." He set the lamp on a cracked altar and turned to face her fully. In its light his face looked older than it had in Thornfell, carved by weather and worry into something like the mountains he kept. "Tell me what the dragon asked."

"It didn't ask. It invited." She opened her hand. The scale lay there, dull as river slate except for that single ember pulsing at its heart. "It said come and see what my hands have made. It called me binder."

"And you want to go charging off to answer it. Through whatever door it opens."

"Not charging. Listening. There's a difference too."

"Don't be clever with me. I'm too tired for clever." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and for a moment he looked less like a gatekeeper and more like what he probably was underneath: a soldier who had spent twenty years watching a door and had just watched the world behind it start to open. "The matriarch told you what happens to speakers who let a god all the way in. She told you about the ones who burned. You were standing right next to me when she said it."

"She said they burned because they let gods in *piecemeal*. A sip here, a sip there, over years, until there was nothing left of them but ash and appetite." Ulyana turned the scale over. "She said the ones who died fast—the ones who opened all the way, once—some of them survived. Changed. But survived."

"Some."

"One in nine," Ulyana admitted. "I asked her to be precise. She hates being precise. She says precision is how the Choir flattered itself into thinking it understood the fire."

Dominic laughed, short and without any humor in it at all. "One in nine. Those are good odds for a battle. They're terrible odds for a person."

"They're the only odds I have." She made herself look up at him, made herself hold his gaze the way she'd never been able to hold anyone's—not her father's, not the smugglers', not the strangers who knelt to her in dreams. "Dominic. The moth is climbing the light. That's what the dragon said. Something is eating Emberlast's flame, and when the flame goes out, everything ends or begins again, and either way it ends badly for everyone who isn't wearing a crown. I can hear the gods. I'm the only one who can. If there's a way to find where the dragon sleeps faster than walking every pass in Thornfell—and there is, because the dying god in this scale knows the way, knew it when it still had knees to kneel on—then the price is one hour. One hour of letting it speak through me completely. Not a sip. The whole cup."

"And if the whole cup empties you?"

"Then you'll have one fewer thing to argue with."

He crossed the space between them in three strides and took her by the shoulders, and she let him, because his hands were steady in a way nothing else in the world was anymore. "Listen to me," he said, low. "I spent my whole life keeping a gate shut because someone told me what was behind it. I never saw it. I never asked. I just watched the seal and swept the snow and told myself that duty was the same as understanding. And then the seal cracked, and I found you in the frost, and everything I believed about the shape of the world turned out to be a story told to children so they'd sleep." His grip tightened, gentle as a man handling something he was afraid of breaking. "So I'm not going to pretend I know better than you what the gods will do. But I know what people do. People say *one hour* and mean forever. People say *the whole cup* and find out the cup is bottomless. I found you unconscious in summer frost, Ulyana. Whatever spoke through you before we met—it wasn't careful either."

She wanted to tell him he was wrong. She wanted it so badly her chest ached with it. But the voices were stirring again behind her sternum, waking from their rare silence like birds at dawn, and beneath them all she could feel the scale's ember beating against her palm—patient, ancient, *waiting*—and she knew, with the terrible clarity that had followed her since childhood, that he was exactly right.

"I know," she said. "I'm going to do it anyway."

He held her shoulders a moment longer. Then he let go, stepped back, and did the thing that undid her more than any argument could have: he nodded.

"Then I'm staying in the room."

"The matriarch says no one should—"

"The matriarch burned out her gift hiding from things like this. With respect, she doesn't get a vote." He dragged the altar closer to the wall, cleared a space on the floor, and began pulling his bedroll from his pack with the brisk efficiency of a man building a barricade. "If you burn, I'll be close enough to pull you back. If you change—" he paused, and she saw him choose honesty over comfort, "—if you change into something I don't recognize, I'll still stay. Someone should witness what you become. That matters. It mattered to the binders, whoever they were. Everything I've read since we got here says the oldest rites needed a witness."

Ulyana looked down at the scale, and the ember pulsed, and somewhere very far away and very deep beneath the mountains, something enormous exhaled in its sleep.

"All right," she whispered. "Witness me."

* * *

They prepared the rite in the old baptistery, because the matriarch insisted that thresholds mattered—that a room built for crossing between states would give her a shape to return to. The Choir gathered in a ring around the sunken font, nine of them, gray-robed and gray-eyed, their burned-out gifts leaving them able to feel the presence of divinity the way an amputee feels weather in an old wound. They could not help her. They had made sure of that long ago. But they could sing, and their song was a rope thrown across dark water, and Ulyana was asked to hold one end of it in her mind and not let go.

"Hold the rope," the matriarch said, her blind face tilted toward Ulyana, her voice like paper. "When it pulls, hold. When it burns, hold. When you forget what your name is, hold anyway, because the rope remembers it for you. Do you understand, child? The song is not to bring you power. The song is to remind you that you are *Ulyana*. Everything else is negotiable. That is not."

"I understand."

"You don't. No one does, the first time." The old woman's cracked lips moved in something that might have been a smile. "But you will. Hold the rope."

Dominic knelt at the edge of the ring, outside it, his sword across his knees though they both knew steel would mean nothing. He didn't pray. She noticed that—he never prayed, not even now, not even with gods thick in the air like storm-pressure. Later she would understand why, and it would break her heart a little. For now she only filed it away, one more locked door in the quiet country of Dominic Lockridge, and turned to the scale.

She set it in the font. She put her hands on either side of it. And she did the thing she had spent her entire life doing in fragments, in whispers, in guilty half-measures:

She opened.

* * *

The world went out like a pinched candle.

For a heartbeat there was nothing—no stone under her knees, no cold air, no Dominic, no rope of song—and then there was too much, an ocean of it, pouring in through the place behind her sternum until she felt her ribs creak like a hull in deep water. The dying god came up through her the way floodwater comes up through a cellar: from below, from everywhere, patient and total. It was old. Gods were supposed to be old, she'd known that, but knowing and feeling were different countries, and this god was older than the word for old. It had been young when the mountains were mud. It had loved the world when the world was still deciding what shape to be. And it was dying, had been dying for two thousand years, sealed in the dark with its siblings, and its gratitude at finally being spoken—fully, wholly, without ration—was so vast and so terrible that Ulyana screamed, or thought she did, though her mouth belonged to something else now.

*Little vessel,* the god said, using her throat, her tongue, her breath, and its voice through her voice sounded like wind through a canyon, like the groan of glaciers. *You gave freely. Few ever gave freely. For that courtesy, you shall see.*

And then she was falling—not down, but *back*, backward through time the way a stone falls through water, past centuries, past the memory of centuries, into the god's oldest and most treasured wound.

She saw the world before the crown.

She saw gods walking openly in it—saw them the way the god remembered them, which is to say she felt them, vast presences moving through orchards and rivers and the hearts of kings, tending the world like gardeners tend a border. She felt their love. That was the part no story had ever told her: the old gods had *loved* the world, clumsily, enormously, the way a giant loves a bird. Their miracles were not displays of power. They were gestures of affection, repeated ten thousand times a day, until mortals forgot there had ever been a world without them.

And then she saw the war.

It came like weather. Like a front of black rolling across the green. The gods had quarreled—over what, the memory blurred, the god itself had forgotten, or refused to remember—and their quarrel shook the roots of things. Rivers ran backward. Children were born old. Prayer became a weapon, each answered plea a blow struck in a proxy war fought through mortal mouths, and the world, the beloved world, began to die of devotion.

She saw the binders gather.

Nine of them—they had always been nine, the number sang through the memory like a bell—standing on a mountain that was not Thornfell but was its ancestor, its first draft. Mortals, yet more than mortals: speakers, conduits, men and women through whom the gods had poured themselves for generations until the pouring had changed them, tempered them like steel. They had been chosen by the gods themselves, once, to carry divine fire into the world.

Now they stood together to carry it *out*.

Ulyana watched them work, and the watching hurt, because she understood what she was seeing. Not a prison. Not a theft. A *fusing*. The binders were taking the warring gods—all of them, every name and nature—and binding them into a single vessel, a single shape, a crown of fused divinity that could be placed on one head, ruled by one will, silenced by one discipline. It was mercy. It was triage. The world was dying of a war between gods, and the binders chose to end the war by ending the gods' separateness, folding them together like a surgeon closing a wound with heat.

It should have worked. In the memory, in the god's grief-stricken remembering, she could feel how much it should have worked.

But crowns are worn by heads, and heads are human, and human wills rot.

She saw the first king take the crown. She saw the fusion begin to sour inside it, divinity pressed too close for too long, gods who had hated each other in life now forced into intimacy without end, their hatred fermenting into something new, something hungry, something *hollow*. She saw the crowned hunger wake inside its golden shell and look out through the king's eyes at a world full of unbound prayer, and begin, patiently, century by century, to eat.

And she saw the binders' last act: the sealing of the gate, the burying of the fused crown beneath wards and oaths and the sleeping body of the last dragon, whose fire would keep the prison door ajar—not open, never open, but ajar, a crack through which the gods could whisper, so that the world would not forget them entirely, so that the fuse would not simply smother into silence and leave the hollow alone with its hunger.

The last binder stepped forward to lay the final ward.

The memory gave her the figure whole, at last, after the blur of ages: a woman, tall, frost-pale, with a smuggler's callused hands and a thief's quick eyes and a scar on her left forearm shaped like a river delta—

Ulyana knew that scar. She had carried it since birth. Her father had joked that she'd grabbed a hot pan in the womb.

The woman turned, as if she could feel herself being watched across two thousand years, and Ulyana looked into her own face.

Not similar. Not a resemblance. *Her own.* The same crooked tooth visible when she gasped. The same wary set of the brow. The same eyes that had looked back at her from every mirror of every waystation on every road she'd ever run, eyes that had always seemed, to her, like a stranger's eyes, like borrowed eyes, like eyes waiting for their owner to come collect them.

*I am not chosen,* she thought, and the thought came out of her in the god's voice, in her voice, in the voice of the woman on the mountain, all three braided into one sound that was not a sound at all. *I am returning.*

The memory broke apart like ice on a spring river.

* * *

She came back to herself on the floor of the baptistery, on her hands and knees, and the first thing she knew was cold—real cold, ordinary cold, the honest chill of mountain stone—and the second thing she knew was that her throat was raw and the song had stopped and the nine gray singers were staring at her with their empty eyes wide, which she would learn later was the most frightened any of them had been in forty years.

The third thing she knew was that the hour was not yet over, and the god was still in her, faintly, a tide going out, and it was saying goodbye.

*Little vessel,* it murmured, from somewhere below speech. *You have seen. Now you know what the moth fears: not the flame. The flame is only the lock. It fears the binder who remembers. Tell the dragon its warden keeps faith. Tell it—*

The voice guttered. Went out. And in the font, the scale crumbled to gray dust, and the dust did not blow away, because there was no wind in the undercroft, and somehow that was the worst detail of all.

"Ulyana." Dominic's voice, hoarse. His hands on her arms, hauling her upright, and she realized she'd been lying in the font's basin, that he must have climbed into the holy place to reach her, that Dominic Lockridge had waded into a god's baptismal pool for the first time in his life to drag her out of it. "Hey. Look at me. What did you see?"

She looked at him. She opened her mouth to answer, and what came out was not an answer but a confession, torn straight up from the shattered floor of her:

"It was me."

His brow furrowed. "What was—"

"On the mountain. The binding. The last ward." Her hands were shaking; she watched them shake as if they belonged to the stranger whose face she'd seen. "There were nine binders, Dominic. Nine. And the last one—" She swallowed, and the words came out small and terrible. "The last one had my face. My scar. My *eyes*. I didn't hear the gods because I was lucky, or cursed, or special. I heard them because I built their prison. I'm one of the ones who built it. And I think—" her voice broke, and she hated it, and she said it anyway, "—I think the hollow knows it too. I think that's why it's coming. Not because I'm a conduit. Because I'm a *loose end*. Two thousand years old, and I'm the loose end."

The baptistery was silent. The nine gray singers had bowed their heads. Somewhere above, the matriarch's evening song had stopped mid-verse.

Dominic held her arms. He didn't let go. And when he finally spoke, his voice was rough and quiet and absolutely steady, the voice of a man who had watched a door for twenty years and had just decided, again, what he would do if it opened.

"Then we'd better get you to the dragon," he said, "before the rest of the world remembers you too."

Outside, far to the south, beyond the mountains, beyond the capital, something wearing a king's face smiled in its sleep—and began, at last, to move.
