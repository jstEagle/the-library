## 9. Reincarnation Is a Debt

Dominic had stood watch over the Thornfell gate for eleven years, and in that time he had learned that the worst nights were never the loud ones. Avalanches announced themselves. Wolves sang before they came. The mountain was honest about its violence.

It was the quiet nights that broke men.

This was a quiet night. The waystation fire had burned down to orange coals, and Ulyana sat across from him with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, staring into the embers as if they were a door she was afraid to open. She had been like this since the hour ended—since the dying god of thresholds had finished speaking through her mouth and left her folded on the cavern floor like an empty coat.

She hadn't slept. He could tell by the way her eyes moved: not the slow drift of exhaustion but the quick, hunted flicker of someone cataloguing exits.

"Say it," he said at last.

She didn't look up. "Say what?"

"Whatever you've been chewing on for six hours. You've looked at me four times like you were about to speak, and four times you decided I'd be better off not hearing it." He fed a stick to the coals. "I've carried worse than words, Ulyana. Say it."

The fire popped. Somewhere above them, wind moved through the pass with the sound of a long exhalation—the mountain breathing in its sleep, or pretending to.

"I saw myself," she said.

He waited. She was good at waiting too; she'd grown up among smugglers, people who understood that silence was a currency. But he had learned her silences, and this one had a tremor under it, like ice over moving water.

"In the god's memory," she went on. "The binding. There were nine of them who did it—nine mortals who stood in a circle around the crown and poured the gods into it. The memory wasn't clear, not like remembering. More like... being shown a painting and knowing you'd sat for it." Her voice flattened. "One of the nine had my face."

Dominic set down the stick he was holding. Very carefully, the way his father had taught him to set down anything that might become a weapon.

"That's not possible," he said. "You're twenty-three years old. The binding was two thousand years ago."

"I know what it sounds like."

"It sounds like a god showing you what you most fear in order to use you." He heard the hardness in his own voice and didn't soften it. "That's what gods do. That's what the hollow does. It wears faces, Ulyana. It wore the king's face. Why not yours?"

"Because it would work," she said simply. "You'd believe it. And so would I." She finally lifted her eyes to his, and they were red-rimmed and steady and terrible. "But that's not all I saw. Dominic—I knew the others. In the memory, I *knew* them. Not as strangers in a painting. As comrades. As friends I'd argued with and loved and buried my dead beside. When the ninth binder stepped forward to make the choice, I felt her hesitate, and I felt why. She loved one of the gods. And she did it anyway."

The coals shifted, collapsing inward, throwing her shadow huge against the waystation wall.

"She made the hollow," Ulyana said. "Or she made the thing that became the hollow. The fusion was wrong from the start—we forced living fire into a shape built for a corpse, and everything rotten since has grown from that first rot. Every prayer unanswered for two thousand years. Every village like the Unanswered. Every person who ever knelt to nothing." Her breath caught. "And I helped do it. I'm the reason the world went silent."

* * *

Dominic got up because sitting still had become impossible. He crossed to the narrow window slit and looked out at the pass, at the stars ground sharp and cold above the peaks, at the distant smudge where Vaelmoor's ruined towers caught the moonlight. His hands wanted something to hold. A spear. A rope. Anything with a purpose simpler than this.

Eleven years at the gate taught him about inherited burdens. The Lockridges had kept Thornfell for six generations, and every one of them had inherited a lock they hadn't built, guarding against a war they hadn't fought, for reasons half-forgotten even by the archives. He had spent his whole life inside someone else's decision. He knew exactly how heavy a dead ancestor's choice could be.

But this was different. This was Ulyana saying *the hands were mine.*

"You didn't do it," he said to the window.

"You don't know that."

"I know that the woman standing behind me is twenty-three years old and sold maps on smuggler roads and cried when the pilgrim's hand straightened because she couldn't believe she'd done something kind. That woman didn't bind any gods." He turned. "Whatever soul or shadow or echo sat in that circle two thousand years ago—it isn't you. You don't inherit guilt like a title."

"Don't I?" She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a raw edge. "Your family kept a gate for six generations, Dominic. Did you choose that? Or did you wake up one day already carrying it?"

He opened his mouth. Closed it.

"The Choir told me every speaker gets devoured by the crowned hunger," she pressed on, rising to her feet, and now the words came fast, the way water comes through a cracked dam. "They told me I was a conduit, a vessel, a wick. But none of them told me I might be the *arsonist*. What if the hollow isn't hunting me despite what I am? What if it's hunting me because it recognizes me? Because some part of it remembers the woman who made it, and it wants—" Her voice cracked. "It wants its mother. Or it wants its revenge. I don't know which is worse."

"Ulyana—"

"And if I'm the one who broke the world," she said, quieter now, which was somehow much worse, "then maybe the only honest thing I can do is stop. Stop channeling. Stop listening. Let Emberlast gutter out and let the whole rotten arrangement end, since we're the ones who arranged it. Maybe the kindest thing I can do for everyone is disappear into the mountains and take the voices with me."

"No."

The word came out of him harder than he intended, hard enough that she flinched, and he realized his fists were clenched and his heart was slamming and he was angrier than he had been since the day he found the seal cracked from the inside.

* * *

Here is what Dominic would remember afterward, in the long retelling he never gave anyone: that his anger was not really anger. It was fear wearing anger's uniform, the way the Hollow Guard wore pilgrims' robes.

He was afraid because he had built something fragile and nameless between himself and this woman over smuggler roads and shared fires and confessions in the dark, and she was standing there offering him a reason to drop it. She was handing him the exit. *I am the monster's maker,* she was saying. *You are released from your debt to me.* And some tired, self-preserving part of him wanted to take it—to walk back down the pass to his gate and his routine patrols and his small, survivable griefs, and let the world end without his fingerprints on it.

He thought about his father. Old Aldric Lockridge, who had died at his post with frost in his beard, who had once told him: *A keeper's oath isn't to the gate, boy. It's to whatever's on the other side of it. Anyone can guard a wall. The work is deciding what you'll open it for.*

"What do you want me to say?" he asked. "That I forgive you? For a crime committed before your grandmother's grandmother was born?" He stepped closer. "That I believe you? That I think a god showed you the truth out of generosity?"

"I want you to tell me what to do," she whispered. "Because I have voices in my head telling me everything except that."

"Well, I can't." His voice shook. "I don't know what to do either. I'm a gatekeeper. My entire philosophy of life is *keep it shut until you understand what's knocking.* And right now I understand nothing." He dragged a hand through his hair. "But I'll tell you what I won't do. I won't let you walk into those mountains alone to atone for a sin that isn't yours, because that's not atonement, that's just dying somewhere inconvenient and calling it justice."

"Dominic—"

"You want to carry it? Fine. Carry it. But you carry it *with me*, because that's what I signed up for back at the gate when I found you frost-blooming in a smuggler camp, and I don't recall being given leave to quit." He was shouting now, he realized, and the waystation was small enough that his voice bounced off the stone. "Do you know what I almost did tonight? While you were lying there after the god left you? I almost picked you up and carried you south. Down out of the mountains, away from Vaelmoor, away from Emberlast, away from all of it. Find a farm. Let the world sort itself out." He laughed, bitter. "I had the route planned. I've had it planned for three days. And then you woke up and started talking about disappearing, and I realized—if you go, I go, and if I go, then the hollow wins without swinging a sword, because it will have taken the last keeper off the last wall that matters."

Silence. The fire had burned low enough that the room had gone blue with shadow.

"So that's it," she said. "You're staying out of duty."

"I don't know what I'm staying out of!" The honesty tore out of him before he could dress it up. "Duty, maybe. Fear. Stubbornness. The fact that you're the first person in eleven years who's made me feel like the gate was a beginning instead of a grave. Take your pick. None of them are clean. Nothing about us is clean—you're a haunted vessel with a murderer's face in her past and I'm a man who talks to a sealed door because it's the only conversation I can count on." He crossed the space between them, close enough to see the coal-light reflected in her eyes. "But I'm here. Tonight. Tomorrow. All the way down to whatever's sleeping under this mountain. And if you turn out to be the woman who bound the gods, then I suppose I'm the fool who followed her, and we'll answer for it together."

Her lower lip trembled. "Why?"

"Because two thousand years ago, nine people made a choice alone in a circle," he said, "and look what it cost. I figure the least we can do is make the next one together."

* * *

She cried then—not the dramatic storm he might have expected from a smuggler's daughter, but quietly, horribly, the way people cry when they've been holding a specific weight for days and someone finally says *set it down a moment.* He didn't touch her at first. He'd learned that much from the road: ask with stillness before asking with hands. But when her forehead came to rest against his shoulder, he wrapped his arms around her and held on, and the waystation was very cold and very small and, for a few minutes, exactly the right size for two people.

"I'm frightened," she said into his coat.

"Good," he said. "Frightened means you're still weighing it. It's the certain ones who build crowns."

She huffed something that was almost a laugh. "That's terrible wisdom."

"My father's. He had worse."

They stayed like that until the shaking stopped, and then she pulled back and wiped her face with her sleeve, briskly, the mask of the road coming back up piece by piece—but differently now. Looser. Like a door left unbarred on purpose.

"There's something else," she said. "In the memory. Something I haven't told you."

He braced. "Of course there is."

"The binding wasn't meant to be permanent. The nine of them built a key into it—a failsafe, in case the fusion failed and the hollow was born. Someone with the blood and the sight could reach into the crown and *unmake* the joining. Free the gods. Free the king." She met his eyes. "The memory showed me where the key turns, Dominic. It showed me because it was showing me *myself*. Which means either the god was lying to weaponize me—or I'm the only person alive who can open the lock."

"A keyhole," he said slowly. "At a gate."

She blinked. "What?"

"Nothing." But his skin had gone cold, because he was thinking of the seal on the Thornfell gate, cracked from the inside, and the frost blooming around an unconscious girl in midsummer, and the oldest lock in the world failing on the exact night she walked beneath it. "Nothing. Get some sleep. We move at first light."

"To Emberlast."

"To Emberlast." He banked the fire and took the first watch, because some habits were load-bearing. "And Ulyana—for what it's worth, whoever she was, that woman in the circle? She hesitated. You said so yourself. She loved one of the gods and she hesitated before she damned the world." He looked at the girl wrapped in his spare blanket, already half-asleep, her face finally slack with something like peace. "Hesitation runs in the family, apparently. So does choosing better."

Outside, far up the pass, a sound rolled down through the night—not avalanche, not wind. A horn. Long, low, and wrong, the note bending in a way horns shouldn't bend, like a throat learning to imitate music.

Dominic was on his feet with his spear before the echo faded, watching the dark line of the ridge, where torches had begun to bloom one by one, patient as stars.

They had run out of road.
