## 10. Hollow Court

The king of Thornfell woke before dawn, as he always did, and lay still in the dark of his bedchamber listening to a man whisper.

The whisper came from inside his own chest. It was very small now—smaller than it had been last winter, smaller than the year before—and it said only one thing, over and over, in a voice worn smooth as a river stone: *Aldric. Aldric. My name is Aldric.*

The hollow thing wearing him listened too. It listened every morning with something that was not quite patience and not quite hunger, though it resembled both. Then it rose from the bed, leaving the whisper to its counting, and went to the window to watch the capital kindle below.

Mist lay on the city like a shroud laid out for fitting. The palace gardens were silvered with it. Somewhere beneath the mist, ten thousand people were waking to say their small prayers—to saints, to saints' mothers, to the dead gods whose names had been struck from every temple wall two thousand years ago. The prayers rose like smoke. The hollow felt them pass through the air the way a spider feels flies move in its web: not hearing, exactly. Registering.

It had not eaten this morning. That was the trouble with mornings. The hunger woke first, before thought, before memory, and it gnawed at the hollow's borrowed shape from the inside—the way frost gnaws at mortar, patiently, finding every seam.

*You could stop,* whispered the man inside. *You could let go. You could give it back.*

*Give what back?* the hollow asked, as it asked every morning, though it knew. It liked asking. Asking made the whisper spend itself faster.

*Everything,* said Aldric. *All of it. You were never meant to keep any of this.*

The hollow turned from the window. In the black mirror of the glass, a tall man looked back at it—a broad-shouldered man in his fifties, grey at the temples, with a face that had once been called handsome in the way of old coins, worn but honest. The eyes were wrong. They had been wrong for a long time. They reflected nothing, held nothing, gave back only the room behind them, dim and cold as the far side of a locked door.

*You are remembering again,* the hollow said. *That is wasteful.*

*I remember my wife's name.*

*Do you.* The hollow dressed itself in the king's body the way a hand dresses itself in a glove, flexing each finger, settling each joint. *Say it, then.*

Silence. A long one. And then, faintly, with something that might have been grief or might have been triumph: *Neither do I.*

*No,* the hollow agreed pleasantly. *But I remember yours.*

It did not, actually. Not anymore. But the lie served, and lies were the oldest food it knew.

* * *

The council chamber smelled of tallow and fear, which the hollow found nourishing even before breakfast.

Seven men sat around the long table beneath the banners of Thornfell—the stag crowned in gold, the mountains stitched in silver thread gone brown with age. Lord Chancellor Vessin stood at the head reading dispatches in a dry voice. The Master of Coin was asleep sitting up, which the hollow permitted because his dreams were rich with guilt. The others watched their king with the particular attention of men who have begun, without admitting it to themselves, to notice that something is off about the food they have been eating for years.

"Thornfell," Vessin was saying. "There are reports from the mountain garrisons, sire. Strange ones."

"Reports," the hollow said. Its voice was the king's voice, deep and unhurried, a voice trained over forty years to fill halls. "I love reports. Bring me all of them. Pile them to the ceiling. I will read every word twice."

A few of the councillors smiled. It was an old joke of the king's, from before. The smile cost the hollow nothing; it had learned the choreography of humor decades ago, the way it had learned everything—by wearing it until the seams matched.

"The gate at Thornfell," Vessin pressed, "the old sealed one. Wardens report the seal cracked. From the inside, they believe. And there is—" he hesitated, shuffled papers, "—there is talk of miracles, sire. A girl. A smuggler's daughter who heals with a touch. Who speaks in voices older than the temples. Pilgrims are already walking that way. If the old cults stir—"

The hollow stopped listening after *a girl*, because underneath the words it had caught the other thing, the true thing, the thing it had been tasting on the wind for three days now like blood in water.

Fire. Divine fire, unshielded, unworn, burning in a mortal throat. The first new vessel since the binding. Since itself.

It had known, of course. It had felt the seal crack the way a man feels a tooth loosen—distantly at first, then with the tongue returning and returning to the wound. For two thousand years the divine fire of the world had been banked, buried, fused into a crown and left to smolder, and the hollow had fed on the leavings: scraps of prayer, crumbs of worship, the occasional soul offered up by ambitious men who thought they were bargaining with their king. Enough to persist. Never enough to grow. A fire kept in a thimble.

And now someone had opened the flue.

"Sire?" Vessin said.

"You were saying," the hollow prompted gently, "that a child in the mountains has been answering prayers."

"Not a child. A young woman. The wardens call her—"

"I know what they call her." The hollow folded its hands upon the table, and the councillors, without knowing why, sat straighter. "They call her a saint. They always do, at first. Saints are so much easier than gods. Gods demand temples. Saints ask only for bread and a bed, and then they die, and everyone feels better." It tilted its head. "Where is she now?"

"We—believe she travels toward Vaelmoor. With a warden. Dominic Lockridge, formerly of the Fourth—"

"Lockridge." The name surfaced from the king's drowned memories, and the hollow let it, because old soldiers made useful hooks. "Good family. Dull. He will die well, standing somewhere, being loyal. See that it's arranged eventually. Nothing crude."

Vessin's pen had stopped moving. "Sire. Forgive me. Arranged?"

The hollow looked at him for a long moment, letting the silence do its work. Around the table, seven men discovered seven fascinating places to rest their eyes.

"It was a joke, Chancellor," the hollow said warmly. "You know how I joke. Warden Lockridge may live to be a hundred. Now. The Hollow Guard. Where do they stand?"

"They are massed at the eastern mustering ground, as you ordered. Three hundred picked men. Provisions for a long march. But sire—the Guard answers to you alone; the council has never understood why a royal escort requires—"

"Because," the hollow said, rising, and its shadow fell long across the map of the realm spread on the table, across the mountains rendered in faded ink, "I am going to tell you a story. An old one. You will not repeat it. You will barely remember it, in fact, by supper, and that is a kindness I am doing you."

No one breathed.

"Once," the hollow said, "there were many gods. Too many. They squabbled like dogs over a carcass, and the carcass was us—this world, these mountains, every field and cradle. Men burned whole harvests to feed them. Men gave their children. And the gods grew fat and cruel and careless, because no one had ever taught them consequences." Its fingers walked slowly across the map, northward, toward the mountains. "So certain mortals ended it. They bound the gods—not killed them, killing was never possible—but bound them, fused them, pressed them down into something small enough to carry and quiet enough to ignore. And for two thousand years we have lived free of them. Free to pray to nothing. Free to be nobody's livestock."

"And the girl?" asked the Master of Laws, an old woman with a spine like a pikestaff, the only one who ever spoke.

"The girl has found the lock," the hollow said, "and is picking it. Whether she means to or not. And when it opens—when the fire comes back into the world—it will come back hungry, and it will look for hands to wear, and there will be war such as the binding was meant to end forever. Unless." It smiled, and the smile used muscles that had once charmed ambassadors, and it fit poorly now, like a coat cut for a bigger man. "Unless someone collects the fire first. All of it. Every spark. And keeps it safe."

"Kept where, sire?"

The hollow looked down at its own chest. At the crown lying on its cushion beside the empty throne at the chamber's end—worn only on state occasions now, because the hollow had learned that crowns left impressions in flesh, and flesh was evidence.

"Close," it said. "Kept close."

* * *

The courtier's name was Ser Emmerich Vale, and he had done nothing wrong except be delicious.

He was forty years old, second son of a minor house, master of the king's kennels, a cheerful man with a red face and a laugh like a dog barking at its own echo. He had served the crown faithfully for twenty-two years. He had never prayed to anything but the weather. On the night the hollow summoned him to the private chapel below the east wing, he came gladly, honored, straightening his doublet on the stairs.

The chapel was the oldest room in the palace, older than the dynasty, older than the walls around it. Its altar was bare. Its god had been unnamed for two millennia. Candles burned in iron sconces, and their light did not so much illuminate the room as fail to fully darken it.

"You sent for me, Majesty?" Emmerich dropped to one knee, beaming.

"Rise, Emmerich. Stand by the altar, if you would. There is something I wish to show you."

The hollow closed the chapel doors itself. It liked doing small things with the king's hands. It had spent twenty years learning this body's small kindnesses—the way Aldric had scratched behind the ears of his favorite hound, the way he'd hummed when he carved, the way he'd touched his wife's shoulder passing her chair. The hollow performed none of these anymore. But it remembered performing them, and the memory was the closest thing it had to tenderness.

"Majesty?" Emmerich said, and the beam faltered, because the candles had guttered all at once, and the dark in the corners of the chapel had begun to lean inward.

"Do you pray, Emmerich?"

"To—no, sire. Not since I was a boy. My mother prayed, but she's dead now, God rest—"

"Your mother," the hollow said, "prayed to a god who was already in a box. Did you know that? All of them, in a box. Pressed flat. Folded small. And the box sits under a mountain, and the mountain is guarded by a dying dragon, and the dragon's flame is the only wick keeping the lid from flying open." It stepped closer. Emmerich stepped back and met the altar's edge. "And lately, Emmerich, someone has been rattling the lid. And I find I need more strength than I have. Do you understand?"

"No," Emmerich whispered. "Sire, I don't—I don't understand any of—"

"No," the hollow agreed. "That's rather the point. Understanding is heavy. I'll take that part."

What happened next took eleven seconds, and the hollow experienced every one of them with the slow savoring of a starving man at a feast, because it had been rationing itself for weeks and the march to Thornfell would require fuel—three hundred men to shield, hundreds of miles of road, wards to break, a warden's loyalty to grind through, and at the end of it a vessel of living fire that must be taken whole, unburnt, unspoiled.

Emmerich's soul came out the way breath comes out in winter: visible, briefly, a pale ribbon unraveling from his open mouth. The hollow drank it. It tasted of kennels and rain and a mother's voice singing off-key, of twenty-two years of honest service, of a laugh like a dog barking at its own echo. It tasted, in short, of a man, entire, and the hollow drank him down to the last warm drop and felt the borrowed body strengthen—felt the king's heart beat harder, the king's lungs fill deeper, the king's eyes burn in sockets suddenly too bright for candlelight.

Then it set the body down gently on the chapel floor, folded his hands on his breast, and closed his staring eyes, because it was not cruel. Cruelty required wanting the suffering to matter. To the hollow, Emmerich Vale had simply been a log, and logs do not suffer being burned; they merely cease.

Only afterward, kneeling beside the body it had emptied, did the whisper come again—louder now, the way it always came after feeding, as if the dead man's passage through the hollow's depths had stirred the sediment.

*Aldric,* the whisper said. *His name was Emmerich. Yours is Aldric. Say it. Say your own name. You can still—*

"Hush," the hollow said aloud, to the empty chapel.

*Aldric. Aldric. We hunted together in the Ashvale when we were boys. You fell in the river and I pulled you out and you laughed and coughed and laughed. Aldric. You loved her. You loved her and you—*

"I said hush."

The hollow pressed the king's palms against the king's ears, a gesture so humanly desperate that for one moment the boundary between wearer and worn went thin as ice on a spring pond—and in that moment the hollow saw what the whisper wanted it to see: a boy in a river, brown water, sunlight, another boy's hand hauling him gasping into the reeds. A wedding. A woman laughing, flour on her cheek. A coronation, and the weight of the crown coming down, and something in the crown waking up and saying *yes, this one, this one is strong enough to hold me.*

It had not climbed onto his head, whatever the songs would someday claim. He had knelt to receive it. He had *wanted* it. That was the secret the whisper would not stop telling, and the secret the hollow could not bear: there had been a moment, one single golden moment, when Aldric of the House of—of the House of—

Of the house whose name the hollow had eaten first, long ago, because it was the sweetest thing in him.

The moment passed. The ice refroze. The hollow stood, smoothed the king's doublet, and rang for the servants, who would find their master kneeling in prayer beside a dead kennel-master and would draw the obvious, terrible, correct conclusion: that the king's griefs had grown strange, and were best not discussed.

* * *

At midnight the hollow stood alone on the highest balcony of the palace, facing north, where the mountains were a black serration against the stars.

Three hundred men slept below in the mustering ground, dreaming soldier-dreams. In two days they would march, and the realm would be told the king rode north to inspect the border fortifications, and the realm would believe it, because the realm believed whatever shape the king's face wore when he told it.

The hollow flexed its hands in the starlight. Emmerich's warmth still glowed in them, a banked coal. It would need more before the journey's end—more souls, more prayer, more of the thin gruel of worship a godless realm still unconsciously offered its crown. And at the end of the road, in a caldera under the ice, a dragon two thousand years patient, guarding a flame two thousand years banked.

Devour the flame. Open the box. Wear the gods openly, all of them, fused and folded and obedient, one crown containing every divinity the world had ever dreamed—and rule at last as the only god, the final god, the god who needed no believers because he had eaten belief itself. No more hunger. No more whispering. No more mornings spent listening to a dead man count his own name in the dark.

*You'll still hear me,* the whisper said, very quietly, from the bottom of the well. *Even then. Especially then. Because I'm not a prayer, Aldric. I'm not a god. I'm just what's left of the man who put you on.*

For the first time in a hundred nights, the hollow did not answer.

Far to the north, past the mist and the sleeping city, lightning flickered inside a cloud over the mountains—or perhaps it was not lightning; perhaps it was something answering a prayer at last, after two thousand years of silence, in a smuggler's daughter's voice.

The hollow felt it like a bell rung against its teeth.

"Ready the Guard," it said to the darkness, in a dead man's voice. "We ride at first light. And Emmerich—" it paused, corrected itself, and found it did not care to correct itself after all, "—and *Aldric* is coming home."
