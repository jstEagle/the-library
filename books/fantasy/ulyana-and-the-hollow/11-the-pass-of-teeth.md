## 11. The Pass of Teeth

The mountains did not want them.

Ulyana understood this by the second day, in the way one understands a host's displeasure at dinner—not through any single word but through the accumulation of small refusals. The path narrowed where it should have widened. Scree slid beneath her boots in long, sighing sheets, as if the slope were shrugging them off its shoulders. The wind came down the passes with a sound like a door being closed over and over again, patient and enormous, and every time it died away the silence that followed felt less like peace and more like listening.

Behind them, somewhere below the cloud line, the Hollow Guard was climbing.

She could not see them. She could not hear them. But she could feel them now, the way she had learned to feel the gods—a pressure at the edge of thought, cold and rhythmic, like footsteps counted out by something that never tired. Three days since they'd fled Vaelmoor with the matriarch's warnings still ringing in their ears and the Choir's defector, Serel, walking among them with his burned-out eyes and his careful smile. Three days of climbing, and the pressure behind them had not once faltered.

"They don't sleep," Dominic said that evening, crouched over their cookfire in the lee of a shattered watchtower. He said it without drama, the way he reported weather or the state of his boots. "I've been watching the backtrail. No camp. No fires. They just... keep coming."

"Then we keep going," Ulyana said.

He looked up at her across the flames. His face was three days of stubble and windburn, and there was something in it she was learning to read—the way his jaw set slightly to the left when he was afraid and refusing to say so. "You're limping."

"I'm *walking*."

"You twisted your ankle on the scree field this morning and you've been pretending you didn't for nine hours." He reached into his pack and pulled out the strip of linen he'd torn from his spare shirt two days ago, already stiff with old blood from her palm, which she'd also pretended hadn't happened. "Give me your foot."

"Dominic—"

"*Foot*, Ulyana."

She gave him the foot, because arguing cost more than surrendering, and because some traitorous part of her wanted very badly to be taken care of. He worked in silence, unwinding the bootlace with fingers gone clumsy from cold, and she watched the top of his head and felt the strange, unearned tenderness that had been growing in her chest like moss on a north wall.

The voices noticed, of course. They always noticed.

*Look how he loves her,* whispered something old and feminine, in a tongue that predated tongues. *Look how he kneels.*

*He does not love her,* said another voice, dry as papyrus. *He fears what she is becoming. Fear and love wear the same coat in winter.*

*We could make him love her,* offered a third, younger, hungrier. *One small miracle. One warmth in his chest that never goes out. She need only ask us.*

"Stop it," Ulyana said aloud.

Dominic glanced up, laces half-tied. "What?"

"Not you." She pressed her knuckles against her temple. The voices had been louder all day, and she knew why, and she hated that she knew why. "It's nothing."

"It's not nothing." He finished the lacing, then stayed crouched there, holding her ankle through the boot as if checking the work. "Every time you do something—one of the things you do—they get louder. Don't tell me they don't. I watched you heal that goatherd's boy at the last village and you didn't sleep for two nights after. I heard you talking to people who weren't there."

Ulyana said nothing. Above them the clouds tore open briefly around the peaks, showing stars hard and close as nailheads, and then the mountain swallowed the sky again.

"The boy would have died," she said finally. "His fever. You saw him."

"I saw him." Dominic released her ankle and sat back on his heels. "And I saw what it cost you. And I'm asking you to think about what happens in four days, or six, when the Guard corners us and you have to choose between a small miracle and a large one. Because you won't have anything left for the large one if you spend yourself on every sprained ankle between here and Emberlast."

"That's a cold arithmetic, keeper of the gate."

"It's the only arithmetic that's kept me alive twenty years on that wall." He fed a stick to the fire. "My father taught me locks. You know what he said about them? A lock isn't strong because it's made of iron. It's strong because it's used sparingly. Every time you turn a key, you wear the mechanism a little. The lock that opens every day is the first one to fail." He looked at her, and the firelight moved in his eyes, and his voice dropped into something gentler and much more frightening. "You're a lock, Ulyana. And someone is climbing this mountain to use you until you break."

*He understands,* breathed the oldest voice, almost tender. *Oh, little vessel. He understands better than the priests ever did.*

She closed her eyes. "I know," she said, to both of them.

* * *

The Pass of Teeth earned its name on the fourth day.

They came up out of the valley mist into a corridor of stone where the rock rose on either side in jagged spires—some leaning, some fallen, all of them worn by ten thousand winters into shapes that were almost, but not quite, fangs. The smugglers' maps Ulyana had grown up memorizing called it the Maw. The pilgrims' maps called it the Jaw of Judgment. The old woman in the village of the unanswered had called it, simply, *the place where the mountain chews*, and had refused to say more.

Snow began an hour after they entered it. Not falling so much as *sifting*, fine and grey, filling the air like ash from a fire too far away to see. By midday it lay deep enough to muffle their footsteps, and the world shrank to a white corridor between black teeth, and the only sounds were breath and boot and the distant, tectonic groan of the mountain settling its weight.

Serel walked at the rear. He had walked at the rear for four days, quiet and helpful, and Ulyana had stopped noticing him, which she would remember later and hate herself for.

It was Dominic who noticed the snow.

He stopped mid-stride, head cocked, and Ulyana saw him do the thing he did—read the ground the way she read prayers, patiently, syllable by syllable. Then he turned and looked up-slope, at the great white shoulder of the mountain hanging above the pass, and his face went carefully blank in the way it went blank before bad news.

"Slide," he said quietly. "There's been movement up there. Wind-loaded slab, maybe, or—" He stopped. His eyes went to the rear of their column. To Serel. To the way Serel had, over four days, steered them gently, degree by degree, into the exact throat of the pass. "Get down."

Ulyana didn't question it. Four days of mountains had taught her that. She threw herself flat against the leeward wall of the corridor, dragging her pack over her head, and had just enough time to see Dominic tackle Serel bodily into the snow—

—and then the mountain roared.

It was not a sound. It was a *removal* of sound, a white silence that fell down the slope and filled the pass from wall to wall, and Ulyana felt it hit her like the palm of a god: a pressure that drove the air from her lungs and packed snow into her collar and her mouth and her ears, tumbling her, folding her into darkness that was total and absolute and screamingly cold.

Then it stopped.

Silence. Packed whiteness. Her own heartbeat, enormous.

She dug. That was all she knew for some span of time that might have been minutes or might have been the rest of her life—she dug with hands she couldn't feel, following some animal instinct toward air, until her fingers broke through into blue-white light and she hauled herself out of the drift gasping like a landed fish.

The pass was unrecognizable. The corridor of teeth was half-filled, a white river frozen mid-flow. And forty yards down-slope, where the path continued, the way behind them was simply *gone*—buried under thirty feet of packed slide, a wall of snow smooth and sheer as masonry.

Cut off. The road back, cut off.

Which meant—

"Here." A hand burst from the snow six feet away, then Dominic's head, snow-packed, bleeding from a cut above his ear. He surfaced coughing, hauled himself upright, and his eyes went immediately past her, down-slope, counting. Counting heads.

"One," Ulyana said. "Two. Me. You. Where's—"

They found Serel thirty feet further on, buried to the chest, and at first Ulyana thought he was dead, because he wasn't moving and his face was the color of the snow. But when they dug him out he was breathing, shallow and rattling, and there was blood at the corner of his mouth, and his eyes—those burned-out, ash-grey eyes—were fixed on the sky with an expression Ulyana had seen once before, on the face of the pilgrim whose hand she'd straightened in Dominic's waystation.

Not pain. *Awe.* Terrible, unwilling awe.

"She answered," Serel whispered.

"What?" Ulyana knelt in the snow beside him. "Who answered? Serel, who—"

"The mountain." A bubble of blood popped at his lips. His hand found her wrist with shocking strength. "When the slide came—I prayed. I haven't prayed since the Choir burned it out of me, I swore I never would, but the snow was coming down and I *prayed*, just a word, just *save me*—" His grip tightened. "And it stopped. Do you understand? It stopped. It went around us. Around *me*. Something heard me and the mountain obeyed, and I felt it come through you, through the place where my gift used to be, like water finding an old channel—"

"I didn't," Ulyana started. "I didn't do anything, I didn't even know—"

"You didn't have to." Serel laughed, and the laugh became a cough, and the cough brought more blood. "That's what none of us understood, in the Choir. We thought burning out the gift would free us. But the channel doesn't close, girl. It just waits. And now the gods are awake again, and they pour through anyone who stands near you. Through anyone who *prays* near you." His eyes found hers, and in them was a horror so complete it looked like worship. "You're not a speaker anymore. You're a *spring*. Everything near you is starting to overflow."

Dominic's hand closed on Ulyana's shoulder. She hadn't heard him come close. When she looked up at him, his face was pale under the windburn, and she knew he was doing the same terrible arithmetic she was—that if prayers near her were being answered, then the Hollow Guard, closing in behind them, praying to their hollow king with every step...

"We can't stay here," Dominic said quietly. "The slide took the backtrail, which buys us a day, maybe two. But it also tells them exactly where we are. Avalanches leave scars you can read from miles." He looked down at Serel, and his voice did something complicated. "Can you walk?"

Serel tried. He got halfway to standing, made a sound like tearing cloth, and folded. Ulyana caught him, and her hands were already moving before her mind agreed to it—pressing against his ribs, where something inside had shifted and broken, and she felt the familiar vertigo rise in her like floodwater, the sense of doors opening in her chest—

"No." Dominic's hand covered hers. Not rough. Firm. "Ulyana. Look at me."

She looked at him.

"Rationed is kept," he said. "Remember? You healed a dying man's ribs once and couldn't stand for a day. If you do it now, on this snowfield, we lose you for a day we may not have. So we splint him, and we carry him, and we let his body do what bodies do." His thumb pressed once, hard, against her wrist—over the pulse, over the place where the voices lived. "Let him hurt. Hurt is honest. It'll tell him the truth about what he is faster than any miracle."

*Wise,* murmured the old goddess, and for once there was no mockery in it. *Keep him, little spring. Keepers are rare.*

Ulyana withdrew her hands. It was one of the hardest things she had ever done—to kneel in the snow with a dying man's blood on her fingers and *choose not to*—and she saw Serel understand it, and saw something in his face close like a door.

They splinted him with a tent pole and Dominic's belt. They made a litter of the pack-frame and the oilcloth. And they carried him up out of the Maw while the light failed, four fewer than five, because the fifth was the mountain's now.

* * *

They crossed the bridge at dusk on the sixth day, and the bridge was the last argument the mountain had left.

It was an old thing, older than the kingdom—two spans of black iron chain strung across a gorge so deep the river at its bottom was only a sound, a whispering, a suggestion of water. The planks were ice-glazed and singing faintly in the wind. On the far side, the pass climbed toward the high shoulder where, according to the matriarch's directions, the caldera entrance waited, hidden under the glacier's skirt.

Dominic crossed first with the litter roped to his waist, testing each plank, and Ulyana followed, and it was on the middle of the span, with the gorge yawning beneath her and the wind trying to peel her off the world, that she felt the pressure behind them change.

It had been footsteps for days. Now it was a *run*.

"They found a way around the slide," she said. "Dominic. They're moving fast. Too fast."

He turned on the swaying chain, silhouetted against the bruised violet sky, and looked back the way they'd come. For a long moment he said nothing. Then, very calmly, in the voice of a man who has spent twenty years guarding a gate and knows exactly what gates are for:

"How far?"

"An hour. Maybe less."

"And the anchor points?" He was already looking at the near end of the bridge—at the great iron ring bolted into the living rock, the chain wound through it, the pin that held the whole span taut. Twenty years of gates. Twenty years of locks. She watched him arrive at the answer before he let himself say it, watched him hate it. "If that pin comes out, the bridge drops. Nothing crosses this gorge. Not them. Not us, either, if we're still on it."

Ulyana's stomach dropped with the bridge hadn't. "You'd cut us off from them."

"I'd cut us off *period*. There's no other crossing for sixty miles. Once it's down, we finish this on the far side or not at all." He was uncoiling rope as he talked, working with the economy of long habit. "It's the right call. I've made it before, at Thornfell, with worse stakes and less reason." He paused, and the wind filled the silence, and when he spoke again his voice was quieter. "But I'm not making it alone this time. Your call too, Ulyana. Whatever we burn, we burn together."

She looked back down the pass. Far below, tiny as ants, dark figures were moving up the switchbacks—not hurrying, never hurrying, but relentless as water finding level. And among them, at their head, a shape that walked wrong, that moved with a smoothness no exhausted climber should have, and she felt the crowned hunger look up at her across six miles of freezing air like a man reading a letter addressed to him.

*They will take you apart,* whispered the voices, frightened for the first time. *Little spring. Little vessel. They will drink you to the sediment.*

"Cut it," Ulyana said.

Dominic worked fast. The pin was corroded into its housing, and he had to hammer it with the flat of his axe, each blow ringing out over the gorge like a bell, and with every blow Ulyana flinched because every blow was also a signal, a declaration, a *here we are*. On the fourth blow the pin shrieked loose. On the sixth, the near end of the chain sagged. Dominic got clear, hauling the litter onto solid rock, and then he put his whole body into the final pull and the great iron ring came free of the mountain with a crack like the end of the world.

The bridge fell.

It did not fall all at once. It fell the way a snake dies, in slow collapsing coils, chain singing down into the dark, planks cartwheeling into the whispering river, and the sound it made going was long and mournful and final. Ulyana stood at the lip of the gorge and watched sixty miles of retreat vanish, and felt something in her chest do the same.

"There," Dominic said hoarsely. He was on his knees at the edge, axe still in his fist, chest heaving. "That's done. That's done."

Below, the dark figures had stopped at the gorge's rim. They stood in a line, unhurried, looking up. And though the distance was impossible, though no human eyes could have made out faces at that range, Ulyana felt each of them look at her, one by one, politely, the way guests look at a hostess.

Then the one who walked wrong raised its hand—not in threat, in *salute*—and turned away, and began, with terrible patience, to walk along the gorge, northward. Toward the sixty-mile crossing. Toward the longer road.

Toward arriving exactly when it chose to arrive.

"They'll go around," she said. Her voice sounded far away to her own ears. "Days. Maybe a week."

"Maybe." Dominic rose and stood beside her, and his shoulder touched hers, and neither of them moved away from the contact. Below them the river whispered in the dark like something being told a secret. "Or maybe they don't need the bridge anymore. Maybe cutting it told them everything they needed to know—that we're close enough to be scared, and scared means close." He slung the axe across his back. "Come on. The caldera's a day's climb. Let's not spend our lead standing in the wind."

They climbed. The stars came out hard and pitiless above the Teeth, and behind them the gorge sang its empty chain-song, and ahead the glacier glowed faintly under the moon with a light that Ulyana slowly realized was not moonlight at all.

It was coming from under the ice. A deep red shimmer, pulsing, slow as a sleeping heart.

The voices in her head had gone utterly silent—all of them, every god, every whisper—for the first time since her childhood. And in that silence she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling:

They weren't silent because they were gone.

They were silent because they were *listening*—down through the stone, down through the ice, toward the fire below—and waiting to see what she would dare to knock upon its door.

One day's climb. Then the dragon.

Ulyana pulled her coat tighter, looked at the red glow breathing beneath the glacier, and climbed.
