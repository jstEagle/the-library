## 12. Village of the Unanswered

The village had no name on any map Dominic carried, and when they asked a boy mending a fence where they were, he only shrugged and said, "Here," as though that were answer enough.

It sat in a fold of the mountains like water pooled in a cupped hand — thirty-odd houses of grey stone and turf roofs, terraced fields climbing the slopes in narrow green steps, a stream running cold and quick through the middle of everything. Smoke rose from chimneys. Goats complained from their pens. It was, by every measure Ulyana could see, an ordinary place.

Except that the moment she crossed the first field wall, every head turned toward her at once.

Not toward Dominic, though he stood half a head taller and wore a warden's coat with the Thornfell clasp still glinting at his throat. Not toward Sera Vane, the Choir defector who had walked with them since Vaelmoor, hood up, hands hidden. Toward *her*. Thirty faces, young and old, turning like flowers after a sun they had never seen.

"They know," Ulyana murmured. Her skin prickled beneath her cloak. "Dominic, they *know*."

"Of course they don't." But his hand had already drifted to the knife at his belt, the way it did when he didn't believe his own comfort. "We're strangers. That's all."

An old man came down the lane to meet them, walking with a stick but without hurry, as though he had been waiting years rather than minutes. His face was brown and seamed as old leather, and his eyes — this was what Ulyana would remember afterward, what she would carry into her dreams — his eyes held the particular emptiness of someone who had stopped expecting anything.

"You're her," he said. Not a question. "The one the mountain's been humming about."

"The mountain hasn't been humming about anyone," Dominic said flatly. "Mountains don't hum."

"This one does." The old man looked at Ulyana again, and something moved behind the emptiness, brief and painful as a fish under ice. "Three nights now. A sound under the stone, like singing heard through a wall. My grandmother said it would come before the speaker did. We thought my grandmother was mad." He paused. "She was. But so were we, to hope otherwise."

Ulyana's throat tightened. She could feel it even now, even awake — not the mountain humming, but the *other* thing, the pressure behind her ribs where the voices lived. Since the Pass of Teeth they had grown louder, hungrier, crowding her sleep with names in dead tongues. The god of thresholds still whispered to her sometimes in the quiet hours, small and broken: *let me out, let me out, I will give you rest.* She had learned to hold that whisper at arm's length the way one holds a snake — respectfully, and with both hands shaking.

"We need shelter," she said. "One night. Maybe two. We can pay—"

"You'll pay nothing." The old man turned and began walking, expecting them to follow, because in villages like this the old always expected to be followed and usually were. "There's stew. There's the longhouse. And there are people who will want to talk to you, girl, and I'll tell you now so you're not surprised: some of them will ask you for things you cannot give. Don't hate them for it. They've been asking the sky for sixty years. You're just the first thing that ever answered back."

* * *

The longhouse was low-beamed and firelit, smelling of peat smoke, wet wool, and barley stew. Perhaps forty villagers crowded in — more than the benches could hold, so they stood along the walls and sat on the floor and peered through the doorway, and Ulyana sat at the fire's edge feeling like a relic displayed on an altar.

They told her the story in pieces, the way villages tell stories, each voice adding its thread.

No prayer had ever been answered here. Not once, not ever, going back past the oldest grave in the churchyard — and there was a churchyard, a small one, with a chapel whose roof had fallen in two generations ago and never been rebuilt, because why rebuild a roof over a room where no one listened? Other places had their miracles, or claimed them. Vaelmoor had its cathedral. The capital had its crowned king, blessed by the gods, or so the priests said. Even smugglers' camps had charms and luck-tokens that worked often enough to keep the trade alive.

But this fold of mountain was deaf. The old gods had passed over it. The new faith's saints never dreamed here. Children born here died of fevers that children three valleys over survived. Crops failed in years when every other valley ate well. Sixty years back, a plague had come through and taken one person in three, and the survivors had prayed until their throats bled, and the silence had been total, and the silence had been the only answer they ever got.

"So we stopped," said a woman named Maren, who had lost a son to the river two springs ago. She said it simply, without bitterness, the way one reports weather. "Mostly. You say you stop, but you don't, not all the way. You catch yourself doing it. Kneeling by the bed when the fever's high. Talking to nobody while you dig the grave." She looked at her hands. "You feel foolish. Every time. For sixty years, foolish."

"And then the mountain starts humming," Ulyana said quietly, "and a stranger walks in."

"And then a stranger walks in." Maren raised her eyes, and they were wet, and she was not ashamed of it. "Girl. My son drowned in that river. They pulled him out too late. If there is anything left in you — anything at all —"

"No."

The word came out harder than Ulyana intended, and she saw it land, saw Maren flinch as if struck, and hated herself for the mercy of a softer refusal she hadn't managed to give.

"I can't," Ulyana said, gentler now. "I'm sorry. What I carry isn't mine to spend. Every time I use it, it uses me back, and worse — it *wakes* things. Things that are sleeping right now because people like me, a long time ago, put them to sleep. If I start handing out miracles in your village, I'm not healing your sick. I'm knocking on a door that has to stay shut."

Silence. The fire cracked. Somewhere outside, a goat complained again, absurdly mundane against the weight in the room.

Then a farmer near the door stood up, a big man with burn-scarred forearms, and his voice shook. "My daughter's blind. Born blind. You're telling me you have the power of the old gods themselves sitting in your chest, and my daughter will die blind because of a *door*?"

"Yes," Ulyana said.

"You won't even try?"

"If I try, I might succeed. And then the thing hunting me finds this village, and it doesn't leave villages the way it found them." She thought of Vaelmoor, of the chapel tower lying in rubble, of the Hollow Guard captain with eyes like still water. "I've seen what follows me, friend. I'd be giving you a miracle and a graveyard in the same breath."

"It should be our choice!"

"It shouldn't." Dominic's voice cut across the room, flat and hard as slate. He hadn't moved from his place by the door, and now he came forward slowly, and Ulyana realized he was positioning himself between her and the crowd without making it look like retreat. "I've been a warden twenty years. I know how this goes. One kindness becomes a debt becomes a demand becomes a mob. You'd protect her from the hollow king himself before you'd protect her from each other, and you wouldn't even see yourselves doing it."

"That's easy for you to say—"

"It is," Dominic agreed. "It's very easy. That's why I'm saying it, so she doesn't have to keep being the cruel one alone."

The farmer stared at him. Then at her. Then he sat down heavily, and put his face in his scarred hands, and wept without noise, the way men learn to in hard country.

Ulyana sat with it. That was the whole of what she could do, and she knew it, and it felt like almost nothing: she sat at the fire's edge and let them look at her, let them hate her a little, let them grieve in front of a stranger because grief needed somewhere to go and the sky had never taken theirs. Sera Vane watched from the shadows by the wall, hood shadowing her face, and Ulyana caught the flicker of something in the defector's expression — pity? contempt? hunger? — before it smoothed away.

*You could end this,* whispered the voices, sweet as rot. *One word in the old tongue. One small warmth in the blind girl's eyes. They would love you forever.*

*I know,* Ulyana answered silently. *That's why I won't.*

* * *

She couldn't sleep in the longhouse. Too many breathing bodies, too many hopes pressing against her skin like damp wool. Near midnight she slipped out into the cold and climbed the path behind the village to the ruined chapel, because ruined holy places had become, in the last months, the only rooms where she could hear herself think.

The moon was nearly full. The churchyard grass grew long and silvered around leaning stones whose names the weather had eaten. The chapel itself was a shell — three walls, no roof, stars where the rafters should be. Someone had swept it recently, though. Someone had set a row of stubby candles on the altar slab, unlit, waiting.

And someone was there. An old woman sat on the altar step wrapped in a shawl, small and straight-backed, her white hair braided crown-wise in the old style.

"I hoped you'd come," the woman said. "The restless ones always find the chapel. Sit, child. The step's cold but it holds you well."

Ulyana sat. For a while neither spoke. Below, the village slept; the stream talked to itself in the dark.

"Why do you keep candles if no one answers prayers here?" Ulyana asked finally.

"Who says no one answers?" The old woman smiled without looking at her. "The candles burn. That's an answer. I light one, and it burns, and the light is real whether any god sees it or not. I decided a long time ago that counts." She turned her head then, and studied Ulyana with eyes gone pale as frost but sharp underneath. "They asked you tonight. All of them. The blind girl's father. Maren. Half the village by morning, I'd wager."

"They'll ask again tomorrow."

"They'll ask forever. That's what wanting does." The old woman nodded slowly. "And you said no."

"Yes."

"Good."

Ulyana blinked. Of all the responses in the world, she had braced for every one except that. "Good?"

"You said no because it would cost more than they know, and because you've seen what comes riding after power like gulls after a plough." The old woman's voice was dry and unhurried. "Every miracle-worker I ever heard tell of said yes. Said yes and yes and yes until there was nothing left inside them but the yes. You know what happens to a person who gives away everything they are? They become a hollow, child. A shape with a hunger in it." She reached over and patted Ulyana's knee with a hand like a bird's foot. "I've seen a hollow king in my dreams lately. Big as a mountain, wearing a crown, eating and eating and never full. So when a girl walks into my village carrying fire and says *no* — I say good. Best word in any language. Took me seventy years to learn it."

Ulyana's eyes stung. She looked away, at the candle stubs on the altar, at the stars through the missing roof. "They're disappointed in me. Some of them hate me tonight. And I keep thinking — I have the power to fix all of it, every fever, every drowned boy, every blind daughter, and I'm sitting here refusing, and what does that make me?"

"A person," the old woman said. "A person is exactly the thing that refuses. Gods don't refuse. Gods pour out. That's the whole trouble with them." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and took up one of the candle stubs. "Walk me down. My knees are done with stairs, but they'll manage downhill if I complain enough."

At the chapel door the old woman stopped, struck flint to wick, and lit her candle. Its small flame stood steady in the windless dark.

"For my husband," she said. "Dead forty years. Never got an answer. Lighting it anyway." Then she looked at Ulyana, really looked, and her frost-pale eyes were kind. "Thank you, girl."

"For what? I refused you nothing — I mean, I refused you *everything*, I haven't done a single—"

"You listened." The old woman said it as if it were obvious, as if it were enormous. "Sixty years we shouted at a sky that never leaned down to hear us. Tonight somebody sat in our firelight and heard every word and said no with a face full of grief about it. Do you know what that's worth, to people who've only ever been talked *past*?" She pressed the candle into Ulyana's hand. "Keep it. When the voices get loud — and they will, I can see them circling behind your eyes like crows — you light this, and you remember there's a village in the mountains that thinks you're worth more empty than full."

Ulyana stood holding the little flame and could not speak at all.

* * *

They left at dawn, taking the high track east toward Emberlast's deep country. Dominic walked beside her in silence for the first mile, which was his way of saying he'd noticed she'd been crying and would permit her the dignity of pretending otherwise.

Halfway up the switchbacks, Ulyana glanced back. The village lay below in its fold of morning mist, small and grey and stubborn. On the chapel's roofless wall, tiny with distance, figures were standing — the old woman, Maren, the scarred farmer, others — watching them go. No one waved. No one knelt. They simply stood, the way people stand for a departure they intend to mark.

"They'll be all right," Dominic said.

"No, they won't. People are never all right. They just keep going anyway." Ulyana touched the candle stub in her pocket, its wick still faintly fragrant. "That's better than all right. That's the whole religion, maybe. Keep going, light the candle, thank the girl who told you no."

Dominic made a sound that was almost a laugh. Ahead, the pass narrowed into shadow, and Sera Vane walked point with her hood up, and Ulyana noticed — for the first time, or perhaps only for the first time she admitted it — that the defector kept glancing back down at the village too. Counting roofs. Measuring distances. Memorizing the road they'd taken.

The voices stirred behind Ulyana's ribs, uneasy, like birds before a storm.

"Hurry," one of them breathed, in a tongue older than stone. "*He knows where you sleep now.*"
