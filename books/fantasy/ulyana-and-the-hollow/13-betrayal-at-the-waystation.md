## 13. Betrayal at the Waystation

The waystation at Greyhollow Pass had been built to hold six men through a winter, and it held four travelers through a single night with room to spare—if you didn't count the ghosts.

Dominic counted them anyway. It was a habit of old garrison men, counting what couldn't be counted: the drafts that moved wrong in the rafters, the way the firelight bent where it shouldn't, the faint sweet-rot smell that had followed them since the bridge at Kestrel's Tooth came down behind them with no wind to explain it. He fed the stove another split of pine and told himself the smell was the resin.

Ulyana slept sitting up against the far wall, her pack under her head, her hands folded in her lap like a woman at her own funeral. Even asleep she looked like she was listening. She had looked that way for eleven days now, ever since the Pass of Teeth had begun taking its toll of their strength and she had begun spending her small miracles like a miser spending gold—warmth when Merris shivered, luck when the scree shifted, healing when the mule cut its leg on the wire-grass. Each gift cost her something he could see but not measure: a little more distance behind her eyes, a little more of the voices' chorus pressing at the seams of her face.

Merris slept too, curled near the stove, the mule's blanket around her shoulders. She was fourteen, a tollman's daughter they'd pulled out of an avalanche's aftermath, and she had not spoken more than ten words since. She watched Ulyana the way children watch storms.

And by the door, wrapped in his grey Choir cloak, sat Tam Vess.

* * *

Dominic had never trusted him. That was the thing he would turn over and over in the days after, the stone in the boot of every memory: *I never trusted him, and I let him walk beside us anyway.*

Tam had come to them at Vaelmoor, three days after the tower fell. A defector from the Choir of Ash, he'd said—a speaker who had burned out his gift early rather than be devoured, who had kept his eyes (the matriarch's people lost their sight; Tam's were whole and brown and wet with feeling) and wanted only to serve the last conduit before the crowned hunger found her. He'd knelt to Ulyana in the cathedral dust. He'd wept. He'd carried Ulyana's pack over the worst of the scree without being asked, and he'd sung—badly, cheerfully—in three languages, filling the terrible silence of the high passes with something human.

He'd asked questions, too. Small ones. Where the seal fragment recovered from the Thornfell gate was kept. How Ulyana slept. Whether the voices spoke to her in dreams or waking. Innocent questions, shepherd's questions, and Dominic had answered most of them because Tam Vess had wept real tears over the matriarch's blindness and knew the words to hymns Dominic's own mother had hummed.

That was the craft of it, Dominic thought now, watching the man doze by the door with his chin on his chest. Not lies. Truths, arranged like stones across a river, each one honest, each one leading exactly where the arranger wanted you to go.

The fragment lay in the bottom of Dominic's own pack, wrapped in oilcloth and bound with three iron hasps. A piece of the Thornfell gate-seal, cracked loose from the inside the day Ulyana's prayers first got answered—the day the world's oldest lock failed. The matriarch had warned him: *iron that held a god holds a god still, even broken. Especially broken.* Keep it cold, keep it dark, keep it away from the crowned one's reach.

It was warm. He noticed it as he crossed the room to check the stove—a faint heat coming off his pack where it leaned against the wall, like bread left too near a fire. His hand stopped above the flap.

"Tam," he said quietly. "You awake?"

The man by the door did not stir. But his breathing had changed. Dominic had spent twenty years listening to men pretend to sleep in garrisons and ambush camps, and Tam Vess's breathing was the breathing of a man wide awake, counting seconds.

Dominic turned, slow, letting his hand drift to the hatchet at his belt—and saw that Tam's eyes were open, fixed on the pack, and that they had gone wrong.

Not black. Not glowing. Nothing so generous as that. They had gone *empty*, the way a house is empty when the family has left but the furniture remains—brown irises still there, still wet, but nothing behind them looking out. And in their depths, where a reflection should have been, there was only a depthless grey, like frost forming on the inside of a window.

"Tam," Dominic said again, and reached for him.

* * *

Tam moved faster than a burned-out speaker had any right to move. He rolled under Dominic's grab, came up against the wall, and his hand shot into his own cloak—not for a knife, Dominic realized with a lurch, but for the iron hasp key he'd been entrusted with, the second of three, because the matriarch had insisted the fragment's bindings be shared among them so that no one soul could open it alone.

"No—" Dominic lunged.

Tam smiled. It was the worst thing Dominic had ever seen, that smile—warm and apologetic and utterly vacant, a mask of kindness stretched over a hunger the size of centuries. "Forgive me," Tam said, and his voice was his own voice, gentle, the voice that had sung hymns over the campfire. "He is very patient, and I am so very tired."

The key turned. Somewhere in the walls of the waystation, in the mountain itself, something exhaled.

The third hasp was on Ulyana's belt. Dominic whirled toward her—but she was already awake, already on her feet, and her eyes were wide not with sleep but with horror, because she had heard it coming the way she heard everything now, the prayers and the hungers and the vast slow attention turning toward them through the stone.

"It's not him," she said. "Dominic—it's not *him* anymore—"

But it was him enough. Tam crossed the room in three strides, and Ulyana might have fought him—might have called on some war-god and brought the roof down on all of them—but she hesitated, one heartbeat of mercy, one heartbeat of *he sang to me, he carried my pack, he wept*—and that heartbeat was all the hollow needed.

Tam's fingers closed on the third hasp. The iron did not burn him. That was how Dominic knew, finally and completely, that whatever wore Tam Vess had been wearing him for longer than any of them had guessed—because unbound god-iron seared the faithful and the faithless alike, and Tam's hand rested on it like a lover's.

The hasps fell. The oilcloth unwound itself, patient as a snake shedding skin.

The fragment was the size of a man's palm, a wedge of black iron veined with lines of dead silver—the script of the binding, the same script Dominic had seen in Ulyana's visions, the same script that had held the world's oldest lock shut for two thousand years. In the stove light it should have been inert. It was not inert. It breathed. The dead silver veins pulsed, dim and rhythmic, like a heart remembering its work after a long faint.

And then it opened.

Not broke—*opened*, the wedge parting along a seam no eye could find, and what came out was not light and not sound and not cold, though the stove died instantly and frost raced across the beams and Merris woke screaming. What came out was *attention*. The feeling of being seen by something that had been waiting in the dark beneath the world since before names, that had worn a king's crown and a king's face and a king's patience, and that had just been handed a window into the highest pass in Thornfell by a tired man who had once sung hymns.

The influence flooded the pass like water finding a crack.

Dominic felt it as pressure behind the eyes, as a whisper trying on his name—*Dominic, Dominic Lockridge, keeper of gates, keeper of nothing, why do you guard a door when you could be a door*—and he bit down on it, hard, the way you bite down on a scream, and reached for Ulyana.

She stood in the center of the room with her arms spread, and the frost stopped at the edge of her shadow. Just stopped, curling back like a wave from a rock, because whatever poured out of the fragment recognized her—recognized the divine fire banked in her small mortal frame—and did not dare touch her directly. Not yet. Not until it had worn a body worth wearing.

So it took the one it had.

* * *

Tam Vess screamed.

Later, when Dominic tried to describe it to himself—to make sense of the sound, to file it somewhere in the ledger of horrors he kept—he could not decide whether the screaming had come from Tam's mouth or from somewhere much deeper, from the place where a man's soul goes when it is used up like a wick. Because that was what happened. That was the sickening, simple truth of it. The hollow king did not possess Tam Vess. It *spent* him. All those weeks of gentleness, the hymns and the weeping and the carrying of packs—that had been the investment. This was the return. A spy is a tool, and tools are made to be used up, and Tam Vess had been forged years ago, perhaps in Vaelmoor, perhaps in the Choir itself, perhaps in some cradle where a grieving man's loneliness had been found and filed and fitted with a purpose.

His body arched. The empty eyes filled—not with Tam, never with Tam again, but with grey, a rolling grey like storm-cloud seen from below, and his mouth opened wider than a mouth should, and the voice that came out was layered, a choir of one, a king and the court of hunger that attended him:

"*There you are.*"

Ulyana flinched as if struck. The words went through the room like nails through cloth.

"*Little vessel. Little flame-carrier. Do you know how long I have listened for your voice? Every prayer you answer, I taste. Every miracle, I grow fat. You have been feeding me across a hundred miles, child, and you never once thought to wonder who else was hungry at this table.*"

"Get out of him." Ulyana's voice shook, but she did not step back. Frost bloomed and died at her heels in waves, the gods inside her rising like dogs against a fence. "Get out of him and give him back."

"*Give him back.*" The layered voice did something that was almost laughter, a dry rustling like pages burning. "*There is nothing to give back. There has been nothing of him since the autumn of the year your dragon began to die. Ask him—ah. No. He cannot hear you. He is where I keep the ones I finish with. He is in the crown, little vessel. They are all in the crown.*"

And then—for one moment, one flicker, the way lightning shows you the shape of a whole valley—Tam Vess surfaced.

His eyes were his own. Brown and wet and terrified, and full of a knowledge that had been locked behind them for months, screaming silently while his hands carried packs and turned keys. He looked at Ulyana and his mouth shaped words with no breath behind them, and Dominic read them because he had spent twenty years reading lips across parapets:

*I'm sorry. Run.*

Then the grey closed over him like water, and Tam Vess's body straightened, and the fragment rose from the floor into his outstretched hand, and the hollow king spoke through his ruined throat one last time—not to Ulyana this time, but outward, upward, through the opened seam of the pass, a summons pitched below hearing:

"*Come. The door is open. Bring her to me—or bring me to her. Either will do. I have waited two thousand years. I can wait the length of a mountain.*"

* * *

What happened next happened fast, and Dominic's memory of it would always be fragments: the stove-dead dark, the frost, Merris sobbing, the mule screaming in its tether outside.

Ulyana moved first. She did not call a war-god. She did not level the waystation. She walked through the flooded cold to Tam Vess, took the man's face in both her hands—the face of the traitor, the tool, the friend—and she said, in a voice that was hers and also older than hers, in the dead tongue of the binding-script, one word.

Dominic did not know it. But the fragment knew it. The iron wedge snapped shut between Tam's fingers like a jaw, severing the channel, and the pressure in the room broke apart like fog under wind.

And Tam Vess fell.

Dominic caught him. Of course he caught him—he was a keeper of gates; catching falling things was half his life. He lowered the man to the frozen floorboards and held him, and Tam Vess looked up at him with eyes that were empty now in the ordinary way, the way of dying, and his mouth worked.

"He was in the Choir," Tam whispered. Each word cost him visibly, coins paid out of an empty purse. "Recruited me when I burned out. Said the silence was mercy. Said the crowned one was mercy too, in the end. I believed—I believed right up until I heard her pray." His gaze slid past Dominic, found Ulyana kneeling on his other side, and something in his ruined face eased. "Your voice. It was the first true thing I'd heard in years. I'm sorry. I'm sorry. I stole the key in the night and I'm sorry, I'm sorry—"

"You're forgiven," Ulyana said.

"Don't," Tam said, and almost smiled, and it was almost his own smile this time. "Don't waste miracles on liars. Save them. Save them for the mountain. He's coming up the pass behind the Guard, and the Guard is not men anymore, and the fire—" His hand spasmed, gripped Dominic's wrist with shocking strength. "The fire is smaller than you think. Smaller than anyone thinks. A candle. You'll see. A candle in a cathedral, and every hungry thing in the world is walking toward it, and I opened the door, I opened the door, I opened—"

He died mid-word. Not dramatically. The way a lamp dies when the oil is gone—here, and then simply not here, the grip slackening, the breath stopping between one apology and the next. Used up. Exactly as designed.

For a long moment none of them moved. The frost on the beams creaked as it settled. Outside, the wind had changed direction, and Dominic, who had read mountain weather for twenty years, understood what the new wind meant: it blew from the south, from the low country, from the direction of the capital, and it carried on it the faintest sweetness, like rotting fruit, like a court perfumed over a grave.

They were coming. Not days behind them anymore. Hours, perhaps. The door was open, and everything hungry was walking through it.

Dominic laid Tam Vess flat, folded the grey Choir cloak over his face, and stood. His hands were steady. He noted that with distant surprise, the way you note anything unbearable.

"The fragment," Ulyana said hoarsely. "Can we reseal it?"

"It doesn't matter." He picked the iron wedge up from the boards. It was cold now, inert, a dead thing that had done its living work. "It was never about the fragment. It was about the opening. He knows where we are. He knows what road we're on." He looked at her—at the girl who heard dead gods, who forgave traitors, whose shadow the frost feared—and he made himself say the rest. "And Ulyana. Whatever he put in Tam, whatever he planted—Tam wasn't clever enough to build all this alone. Someone taught him. Someone in the Choir. Maybe someone closer than that."

Her face went white. Behind her, Merris had stopped crying and was staring at the folded cloak with wide, dry eyes, and Dominic felt the thought arrive in his own mind like ice water: *count what cannot be counted. Count your companions. Count them twice.*

Four had entered the pass at Kestrel's Tooth. Four remained.

He shouldered his pack, and the weight of the dead fragment rode against his spine like a hand.

"We move now," he said. "Down is death. So we go up—into the deep teeth, the passes even smugglers don't map. Toward the fire." He met Ulyana's eyes. "Toward Emberlast."

Far below them, down the black throat of Greyhollow Pass, something began to sing—a thin, sweet, many-voiced song, carried on the southern wind, growing slowly nearer.
