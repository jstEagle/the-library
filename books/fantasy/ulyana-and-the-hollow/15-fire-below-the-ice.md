## 15. Fire Below the Ice

The cold came first.

Dominic had climbed mountains his whole adult life—patrols along the Thornfell spine in every season, once a winter crossing that had cost two men their toes and nearly a third his life—and he had never felt cold like this. It was not the cold of altitude or wind. It was old. It rose out of the rock itself, patient as debt, and it did not bite so much as *remember* him, pressing against his skin like something that had been waiting a very long time to be touched again.

"Stop," Ulyana said behind him.

He stopped. The tunnel here was narrow enough that his shoulders brushed both walls, ice-slick stone veined with something that glowed faintly when he looked away from it and went dark when he looked directly at it. His torch guttered low. They had burned through three of them since the pass, since the betrayal, since Sarel's screaming had finally stopped echoing off the ice. He had not spoken of it. Neither had she. Some things you carried in silence or they got heavier.

"What is it?" he asked.

She had her hand flat against the wall, fingers spread, and her face wore the expression he had come to dread and, if he were honest with himself in the dark hours, to lean on: the listening look. The one that meant the voices were speaking.

"It's warm," she said. "On this side."

"That's not possible."

"No," she agreed quietly. "It isn't." She lifted her hand and looked at her palm as though expecting it to be scorched. "Everything past this point is warm. Like standing outside a house where someone's left the oven on for two thousand years."

Two thousand years. She said numbers like that easily now. He still flinched from them.

They went on.

* * *

The tunnel opened without warning. One step he was walking hunched in stone, the next the world fell away beneath him and around him, and Dominic Lockridge—keeper of the Thornfell gate, warden of a door no one crossed, a man who had spent thirty years believing the largest thing he would ever guard was a wall built by dead kings—stopped breathing.

The cavern was a cathedral the way a sea is a pond. It descended farther than his torchlight could follow, tier upon tier of black rock sweeping down into darkness, and the ceiling overhead was lost in a haze of frozen vapor that caught what little light there was and scattered it into something like starfall. Pillars of ice stood in ranks down the slopes, some thin as spears, some thick as watchtowers, and every one of them leaned inward, toward the center, the way worshippers lean toward an altar.

And at the center—

At the center, coiled around a flame the size of a candle, slept a dragon.

Dominic's mind refused the arithmetic. The body before him was a mountain given scales, each plate the size of a cottage door, dulled with age and dusted with frost, rising and falling so slowly that he counted forty of his own heartbeats between one breath and the next. Its neck alone was longer than the waystation he had kept for fifteen years. Its head rested on folded forelimbs like a man asleep at a table, and its eyes were closed, and beneath those closed lids Dominic could see—could *see*, even at this distance—the flicker of light moving, slow as drifting embers.

And wrapped in the coils of its tail, cupped in a hollow of scale and stone, burned the flame.

It was small. That was the thing that broke him open. After everything—the voices, the gods, the hollow king, the Choir's warnings, the whole terrible architecture of the world's secret—it was *small*. A candleflame, white-gold, trembling upright in the dark, no larger than the ones Ulyana lit in their camps each night. It did not cast heat so much as presence; Dominic could feel it against his face from fifty yards away, gentle and enormous at once, like standing near a hearth in a memory of childhood.

"Oh," Ulyana said softly beside him. It was not a word. It was the sound a person makes when the last doubt dies.

"Don't," Dominic said. His voice came out cracked. "Don't go down there yet."

"Dominic—"

"You've been hearing them louder every day. You told me. In the pass, after the bridge—you said the warmth-god wouldn't stop singing. If you walk into that—" He gestured helplessly at the flame, at the mountain of sleeping godfire coiled around it. "If you walk into that, I don't know what comes back out."

She looked at him. In the light of the dying torch and the distant candle-flame, her face was pale and set and more certain than anything he had ever seen on a living person.

"I know," she said. "I think I've known since Vaelmoor. Maybe since the gate." She took his hand. Her fingers were cold; his were colder. "But it's been waiting two thousand years, Dom. The least we can do is say hello."

* * *

The descent took an hour. The ice tiers were staircases if you knew where to look, worn smooth in places as though pilgrims had once walked them by the thousand, and Dominic found himself wondering—who? Who had come here, before the binding, before the silence, to stand in this cathedral of cold and watch a god breathe? There were no bones. No offerings. Whatever had been left here had been left so long ago that even grief had finished with it.

Ulyana walked ahead of him, and the closer she came to the flame, the more she changed. Not visibly—or not only visibly. It was in the way she moved, the way the frost on the steps melted in a ring around her boots, the way the air seemed to bend toward her the way it bends toward a struck bell. By the final tier she was glowing faintly, a shimmer under her skin like sunlight through a held-up hand, and the voices—she said later there were hundreds, thousands, all of them whispering at once—had gone quiet.

All but one.

The dragon's eye opened.

It did not open the way eyes open. It opened the way dawn opens over a valley, gradual and total, gold flooding outward from a slit to a sphere, and Dominic, standing three steps below Ulyana with his sword half-drawn out of pure animal reflex, felt the gaze pass over him like a searchlight and dismiss him—a mayfly, a mote, a rounding error—and settle on her.

"*Binder.*"

The voice did not come through the air. It came through the stone, through the ice, through the soles of Dominic's boots and the marrow of his bones. It was vast and dry and impossibly tired, the voice of a thing that had been counting seconds since before the word *second* was invented.

Ulyana did not flinch. "I'm not—" she began, and stopped, and started again. "I don't know what I am."

"You are the seventh." The great head shifted; frost avalanched from its brow and fell a hundred feet, shattering like glass bells. "I have watched six come to this place since the Binding. Six who carried the fire in their blood and heard the prayers in their skulls. Six who stood where you stand and asked me the same question." The eyelid drooped, then rose again, and there was something in that golden depth that Dominic recognized with a jolt: contempt, yes, but underneath it, worse—hope. Old, scarred, long-buried hope. "Six who failed."

"Failed how?" Ulyana asked.

"They chose," Emberlast said, "and their choosing was too small."

* * *

Dominic had drawn his sword fully now without deciding to, and he made himself lower it, made himself sheathe it, because the absurdity of steel in this place was beginning to curdle into something like shame. He stepped up beside Ulyana instead. If the dragon noticed, it gave no sign. He was, he understood, furniture. He accepted this. He had been a keeper of doors his whole life; he knew what it was to be overlooked by the things that mattered.

"Then tell us," Ulyana said. Her voice shook, but she kept it level, and Dominic loved her for it—loved her, he admitted, in the calm and horrified way a man admits a wound he has been pretending not to feel since the waystation, since the frost, since the first prayer answered in a stranger's voice. "Tell us what the choice is. All of it. No more riddles, no more fragments. The Choir gave us pieces. The god in the cavern gave me a beggar's plea. I have been carrying other people's secrets since I was six years old, and I am done."

The dragon regarded her for a long time. Around them the cathedral creaked and settled, ice groaning in its ancient sleep, and the little flame turned slowly on its wick of nothing, casting shadows that moved wrong, that moved like figures, like a crowd, like a congregation.

"You speak as though the choice were ahead of you," Emberlast said at last. "Child. The choice was made before your grandmother's grandmother drew breath. You carry it in your blood the way you carry your mother's face. The only question—the only question that has ever mattered—is whether you will make the same choice again, or a better one."

"And if I make the same one?"

"Then the world continues as it has continued. A crown that eats. Gods in prison. A fire burning down to nothing while a hollow thing wears a king's shape and grows stronger with every prayer it steals." The dragon's breath washed over them, hot and mineral-smelling, smelling of deep places and older times. "And when my flame gutters out—and it will gutter, binder, look at it, *look*—the door swings wide, and the hungry thing steps through wearing divinity, and there will be no seventh speaker, because there will be no speakers at all. Only the crowned hunger, and everything it devours."

Ulyana looked at the flame. Dominic watched her look at it—at the small, stubborn, dying heart of everything—and saw her hands curl into fists at her sides.

"And if I choose better?"

The dragon was silent for so long that Dominic thought it had fallen back asleep. When it spoke again, its voice had changed. The vastness was still there, but something else had entered it, something quieter, and it took him a moment to name it.

Grief.

"Then you will burn," Emberlast said simply. "Fire does not negotiate, binder. Fire does not weigh and measure. To unmake the Binding—to pour what remains of me through a mortal soul and scour the divine out of the world root and branch—that fire will take everything you are and leave ash and a story. Perhaps not even the story." The golden eye dimmed, flickered, steadied. "I have waited two thousand years to offer this choice to anyone. Do you understand what I am telling you? I have lain in the dark with a candle for a heart, listening to the prayers of a world that forgot me, watching six of your kind come and go, each one smaller than the last, each one asking what the fire could do *for* them." The coils shifted; the whole cathedral groaned. "You are the first to ask what it will cost."

"I didn't ask that either," Ulyana said. "Not yet."

"No," the dragon agreed, and there was something almost like humor in it, dry as old bone. "But you thought it. I tasted it in your blood the moment you reached the last stair. Fear, and under the fear, arithmetic. That is why I woke. That is why I speak." The head lowered, until the great eye hung above them both like a dying sun, close enough now that Dominic could see his own reflection in it, tiny and bent and utterly insignificant. "So. Before I show you the truth entire—the Binding, the war, the crown, the rot—answer me one question, seventh speaker, and answer it honestly, because I will know a lie the way you know cold."

Ulyana lifted her chin. "Ask."

"The voices in your skull. The gods who speak through your throat. The power that leveled a chapel tower and healed a pilgrim's hand and sang to you in the dark passes." The eye narrowed, and the light within it banked low, like a fire being fed carefully, hoarded. "When you imagine a world without them—when you imagine *silence*—"

The pause stretched. Somewhere far above, ice calved from the ceiling and fell, and neither of them flinched.

"—does the silence sound like loss?"

Dominic held his breath. He thought of all the nights he had watched her sit apart from the campfire, hands pressed over her ears, lips moving against the noise only she could hear. He thought of the way she had wept—not from pain—from *relief*—the one evening in the high pass when the voices had gone quiet for a single hour, and she had sat in the snow and laughed at nothing, at the wind, at the plain gray ordinary sky.

He knew her answer before she gave it. He suspected the dragon did too.

"Yes," Ulyana said. And then, because she was who she was, because she had never once in all the weeks he'd known her been able to leave a hard truth lying flat: "But I don't trust that answer. Wanting a thing isn't the same as it being right. My father wanted coin that wasn't his. Half the smugglers I grew up with wanted freedom bad enough to sell other people out of theirs." She spread her hands, and the glow beneath her skin pulsed once, twice, like a second heartbeat. "So don't ask me what I want, dragon. Show me what's true. Let me want it *after*."

For the first time in two thousand years, Emberlast laughed.

It was not a kind sound. It rolled through the cathedral like a landslide, rattling the pillars of ice, setting the little flame dancing, and Dominic clapped his hands over his ears and Ulyana staggered and the dragon laughed and laughed, a sound like mountains grieving, until at last it subsided into long, shuddering exhalations and the golden eye was wet, actually wet, with something that froze before it could fall.

"Oh, binder," it said. "Oh, small fierce mortal child. Your predecessor said almost those exact words, the night she bound the gods into a crown and damned us all." The eye blazed suddenly, terribly bright. "*Almost*. She said, show me what is true, and then she flinched from it, and chose the lie that let her keep her power. So I will show you, seventh speaker. I will show you everything, and we will see together whether you are braver than the woman whose face you wear."

The flame leapt.

It did not grow larger—but it reached, somehow, upward and outward, and the light filled the cathedral all at once, and the shadows of the ice pillars became figures, became faces, became a crowd of the ancient dead turning toward Ulyana with expressions of warning and welcome and sorrow, and Dominic grabbed her arm and she did not pull away, and the last thing he heard before the light took them both was the dragon's voice, everywhere now, inside everything:

"*Look, then. Look, and do not flinch.*"

The world went white.

And in the whiteness, faint and far away, Dominic heard—though he would spend the rest of his life unsure whether he had truly heard it or dreamed it—a sound beneath the roar of the fire. Small. Human. Coming from somewhere deep inside the mountain, deep inside the light, deep inside the story about to unfold:

Someone knocking.

Someone knocking on the inside of a door, calling a name that was not Ulyana's, in a voice that sounded starved and buried and desperately, terribly alive.
