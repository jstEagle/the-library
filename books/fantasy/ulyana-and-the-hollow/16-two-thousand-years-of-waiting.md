## 16. Two Thousand Years of Waiting

The flame was the size of a candle.

That was the first thing Ulyana understood, standing on the black glass shore of Emberlast's caldera: everything—the old gods, the prison door ajar, the hollow king's stolen shape, two thousand years of prayers rising unanswered into the cold—everything hung on a fire no bigger than the one she'd lit in Dominic's waystation hearth to warm her hands. It guttered as she watched it. It breathed. And when it breathed, the whole cavern breathed with it, the walls of veined ice flexing like the ribs of something sleeping.

Emberlast coiled around it. The dragon was vast beyond sense—her head alone was the size of the chapel tower Ulyana had brought down at Vaelmoor—and her scales had gone the grey of old ash, of fires long dead, except where they caught the candlelight and burned briefly gold. Her eye opened. It was the color of deep coals, patient and terribly tired.

*You came back,* the dragon said, and her voice did not arrive through the air but through the marrow of Ulyana's bones, through the same channel the gods used, though this voice was older and lower and did not beg. *I wondered which of you it would be.*

"Which of—" Ulyana's mouth was dry. "I've never been here before."

*No.* The great eye blinked, slow as a season turning. *But you have been here before. Sit, binder. This will take longer than your little life is used to lasting.*

"I'm not a binder."

*You are,* said Emberlast, *and you were, and you will have to decide what that means. But first you must see. You cannot choose well with half a truth. Your predecessor proved that.*

Dominic's hand found hers. He had not let go since they'd come down into the caldera, since the tunnel behind them had sealed itself with a groan of stone, since the Hollow Guard's boots had stopped echoing somewhere far above. His palm was rough and warm and human, and she held onto it like a rope over a drop.

"She doesn't have to," he said. "Whatever you want to show her, there's another way—"

*There is not,* Emberlast said, without heat. *And you know it, gatekeeper. That is why you brought her. Do not make yourself a liar to soften the moment.*

Ulyana squeezed his fingers. "It's all right." It wasn't, quite. But she thought of the Choir matriarch's blind face, of the god of thresholds begging in the dark, of every voice that had ever poured through her like water through a broken jar. She had spent her whole life being a vessel for other wills. If she was going to be one again, she wanted—at least once—to walk into the memory on her own feet.

"How?" she asked.

*The way you have always done it,* Emberlast said. *Open. Only wider. Wider than you have ever dared. I will hold the edges so you do not spill.*

Ulyana sat down on the black glass. It was warm beneath her, faintly, like a stone that had held sun. She closed her eyes. She did the thing she had done since childhood, the thing she had called madness and then a curse and then a gift and then a debt: she opened.

The world went out like a snuffed lamp.

* * *

She stood on a plain of grass under a sky with too many stars, and the stars were singing.

Not metaphorically. They sang—a vast, layered chord, each star a voice, and between them moved shapes of light and weather, beings whose bodies were made of the same stuff as the song. Gods. Not the thin echoes she knew, not the voices in her head, but gods whole and enormous, walking the world like giants through a garden. One bent down and pressed its palm to a river, and the river remembered spring. Another gathered a storm in its cupped hands and set it gently over a dry field like a lid.

*This is before,* Emberlast said. Her presence filled the memory-sky like a second darkness. *Before the crown. Before the silence. Watch.*

War came the way war always comes—not suddenly, but by degrees, the way dusk becomes night while no one admits it. First the gods quarreled over a border of worship. Then over a king who prayed to two of them at once. Then the fields began to burn, and the burning did not stop at the fields. Mortal armies marched under banners stitched with divine names, and where those armies met, the ground did not heal afterward. Rivers ran backward. Children were born remembering their own deaths. The gods fought through human hands because gods could not touch each other without unmaking whole regions of the world, and so they used mortals the way Ulyana had once used a crowbar—levers, extensions, things that could bear force without bearing consequence.

Except mortals bore all the consequence. Ulyana watched a village die in an afternoon because two gods had disagreed about a harvest rite, and she watched it from very close, from inside a woman kneeling in a burning doorway, praying to both gods at once, praying *stop, please, stop*, and neither heard, or both heard, and neither cared enough to stop.

Then the binders came.

They were thirteen. She counted them without wanting to, the way you count the teeth of a wolf that has you cornered. Twelve stood in a ring on a hill above the burning world, robed in colors that hurt to look at, and they had built something between them—an act of working so large it bent the light around it, a knot of intention the size of a mountain range.

And the thirteenth knelt at the center, holding the crown.

The crown was not gold. It was a lattice, a cage woven of oaths, and it was empty, waiting, hungry in the way a mold is hungry. The thirteenth binder lifted it up toward the warring sky, and Ulyana felt the memory lean toward her, felt Emberlast's attention sharpen around her like a hand closing.

*Look at her face,* the dragon said softly. *You asked me once, in the waystation, whether I would judge you. Look.*

The thirteenth binder turned her head, and Ulyana looked into her own eyes.

Not similar. Not a resemblance. Hers. The same narrow jaw, the same scar she'd carried since she was nine years old, earned falling from a smuggler's rigging—except in the memory the scar was fresh, still dark, still new. The same expression she saw in polished bronze sometimes: wary, stubborn, already braced for the cost of whatever came next.

"That's not possible," Ulyana said, and her voice in the memory was small and mortal and shaking. "People don't come back. Souls don't—"

*Yours did,* Emberlast said. *Some souls are knots too tight to untie. Some debts follow their debtor across deaths like hounds. You were the thirteenth binder, Ulyana. You were the one who carried the crown. And now you know why the gods' voices found you first, why the prison door opens at your touch, why the hollow thing wearing the king has hunted you across a kingdom. You are not a stranger to this story. You are its oldest living sentence.*

The memory continued whether she could bear it or not.

The twelve binders spoke the working, and the gods began to fall—not dead, never dead, nothing that old could die—but *gathered*, pulled out of the sky and the rivers and the burning villages and wound into the lattice of the crown like threads onto a spindle. Ulyana watched her past self hold the crown as it filled, watched it grow heavy past any weight, watched the other twelve falter one by one as the working drank them down to fuel itself, until only the thirteenth remained, kneeling in a circle of ash, holding a crown full of screaming divinity above a world gone suddenly, horribly quiet.

The plan had been to bury it. She saw that clearly—saw the intent in her own past mind like reading a letter she'd written long ago. Sink the crown into the deepest mountain, seal it under stone and oath, let the gods sleep off their war for ten thousand years, and give the mortals back their world. A mercy. A tourniquet. The only option left when the patient was bleeding out.

But the fusion went wrong.

Because the gods did not lie quietly side by side in the lattice. They hated each other. They had hated each other for centuries, and hatred does not dilute when you cram it into a small space; it concentrates. The crown became not a prison but a boil. The gods' war did not end—it went underground, inward, a siege conducted in a space the size of a circlet, and the pressure of it warped everything the crown touched. The binders had meant to fuse the gods into peace. Instead they fused them into something else: a single seething amalgam with all their power and none of their wisdom, all their hunger and none of their names.

And the amalgam learned to want a shape.

*It took the first king who came looking,* Emberlast said. The memory was shifting now, rushing forward through centuries like water through a cracked dam. *Two hundred years after the binding. A young king climbed the mountain with his honor guard, seeking the source of the strange dreams that plagued his line. He found the buried crown. He reached out—kings always reach—and it poured itself into him through his ambition like water into a cup. It wore him within a year. It has worn every heir since, passing from body to body down the royal line, growing more practiced, more patient, more hollow. The man it wears now is only the latest. He may even be the last, if the amalgam has its way, because it no longer needs a dynasty. It needs only enough fire to burn the last door open and step out of the crown entirely—into the world, into everything, wearing divinity openly, ruling as the only god left standing.*

"And the real kings?" Ulyana whispered. "The men underneath?"

*Buried. Compressed. Starved.* For the first time, something moved in the dragon's voice that might have been grief. *The one you must reach—he is still in there. Faint. He remembers his name the way a drowning man remembers the shore. When you go into the crown, you will find him. That part, at least, the amalgam cannot digest. Kings are stubborn. It is how they get to be kings.*

The memory dimmed. The burning plain, the thirteen binders, the crown full of screaming—all of it receding like a tide going out, and Ulyana felt herself being carried back along the thread of her own returning soul, through death after death after death, lives glimpsed in flashes: a midwife in a plague year, a soldier who deserted, a scribe who copied forbidden texts by candlelight, a fisherwoman, a thief, a mother. Always moving toward Thornfell. Always, in the last years of each life, beginning to hear voices. Dying with them unfinished in her ears, the debt unpaid, the work incomplete.

Until now. Until a smuggler's daughter on a routine run past a gate that should have stayed shut, unconscious in the frost, the keyhole finally come home to its key.

* * *

She came back to herself on the black glass with a gasp, and Dominic caught her shoulders before she toppled. The candle-flame still burned. Emberlast's eye still watched, coal-patient.

"You saw," the dragon said. It was not a question.

"I saw." Ulyana's voice came out scraped. She pushed upright, out of Dominic's grip, needing to stand on her own legs for this. "I was there. I *made* it. I wove the crown, I fused them, I—" She laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "Every prayer that was never answered. Every village the hollow king burned. Every speaker the Choir lost. Every single horror since—I built the thing that did it. Me. With my own hands, two thousand years ago."

"Yes," said Emberlast.

"You're supposed to argue," Ulyana snapped at her. "You're supposed to say it wasn't really me, that souls change, that—"

*Would it help?* The dragon's voice was gentle in the way mountains are gentle, which is to say not at all, but vast enough to hold what falls against them. *Would a lie help you? You want absolution. I am offering you something better. I am offering you the truth entire, so that whatever you choose next, you choose it with open eyes.*

Ulyana stood there shaking. Dominic had not moved away. He stood at her shoulder, saying nothing, doing the thing he did best: keeping watch, keeping company, being present the way a wall is present—not solving anything, just holding the line.

"The guilt is mine," she said slowly, testing each word like ice on a pond. "But it isn't—she made the choice. The me on the hill. She was standing in a burning world with one bad option and twelve dying friends, and she chose the least terrible thing she could see. And it rotted. It rotted for two thousand years and became the hollow, and none of that rot was her intention." She looked up at the dragon. "But it's still my inheritance. Isn't it? The debt didn't die with her. It followed me through every life because the work was never finished."

*Now you understand,* Emberlast said, and there was something almost like approval in the vastness of her. *Your predecessor chose to save the world by caging its gods. It was a choice made in terror, by firelight, with the sky coming down. You are not her. You stand in a quiet place, with time, with a gatekeeper who loves you, with a dragon who has waited two millennia to finish this conversation. You can make a better choice. That is the whole meaning of return, binder—that is why some souls come back. Not to pay. To *amend*. There is a difference, and it is the width of the world.*

Ulyana wiped her face with the heel of her hand. When she spoke again, her voice was steadier.

"The hollow offered me partnership. Keep my power, share the crown, rule kindly, it said." She barked another laugh, shorter this time. "And I understand it now. I understand it *exactly*, because it's offering me the same thing she took. Power to end the suffering. Order instead of chaos. One will instead of thirteen squabbling ones. It's the hillside all over again, dressed in silk." She shook her head. "It thinks I'll say yes because I said yes before."

*Will you?*

"I don't know." Honesty cost her something, saying it aloud in front of Dominic, but she owed him honesty; she'd learned that on the smuggler's roads, trading sins by firelight. "Ask me tomorrow and I might say yes. Ask me when the voices are screaming and someone I love is bleeding and the easy power is right there. That's the terrifying thing, dragon. She wasn't evil. She was *tired*, and the world was on fire, and the crown was the only tool left. That's all it takes. That's all it ever takes."

*Then remember this,* Emberlast said, and the candle-flame between them flared, throwing gold up the grey scales, and for one moment the dragon looked as she must have looked two thousand years ago, when her fire was a sunrise and the world was new. *When the moment comes—and it is coming, soon, the hollow is already in the passes—you will not choose alone. That is the difference. She stood on that hill with twelve corpses and a screaming sky. You will stand with a keeper of gates who has never once abandoned a post, and with me, and with every prayer in this kingdom rising at your back. Choose with us. That is all amendment asks. Not perfection. Company.*

Ulyana looked at Dominic. He was watching her with an expression she'd seen on him only twice before—once at Vaelmoor, when the tower fell; once in the Pass of Teeth, when she healed a stranger's crushed leg and swayed with the cost. It was not awe. It was the decision to stay, renewed, made again in real time, and it steadied her more than any miracle.

"Together, then," she said.

"Together," he agreed, and his voice was rough, and that was fine, because hers was worse.

Emberlast lowered her great head until her breath stirred Ulyana's hair, warm as a hearth, smelling of iron and old summers. *Then listen well, for we have little time. The flame gutters. When it dies, the door closes forever—or the hollow tears it open and steps through. Either way, the age ends. But there is a third path, one your predecessor never saw because she worked alone and in haste. To find it, you must go into the crown itself, into the place where the true king lies buried, and you must speak his name.*

"I don't know his name."

*No,* said Emberlast, and the coal of her eye banked low, and somewhere far above them, faint through miles of stone, came a sound that froze the blood in Ulyana's veins: the slow, rhythmic tread of many boots descending.

*But he knows yours. He has dreamed of you, binder, every night of his imprisoned life. And he is about to have visitors.*

The boots grew louder.
