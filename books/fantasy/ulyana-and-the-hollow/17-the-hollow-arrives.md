## 17. The Hollow Arrives

The flame was the size of a candle now.

Ulyana had not meant to think of it that way—had tried, in the hours since Emberlast first spoke to them, to hold the image of something grander, something worthy of two thousand years of vigil. But there it was at the center of the caldera: a tongue of fire no taller than her hand, burning atop a cradle of fused glass and dragon-scale, casting light like a dying hearth across a cathedral of stone. The cavern arched above them in ribs of black basalt, hung with frost that had been falling, flake by patient flake, since before her grandmother's grandmother drew breath. Each snowflake melted a foot above the flame and rose again as steam, and rose again as frost, an endless slow breathing that Ulyana realized, with a lurch, was the breathing of the world itself.

*It is smaller than when I slept,* Emberlast rumbled, and its voice did not echo so much as settle, like ash drifting down through still air. *When they bound me here, I carried a sun beneath my breastbone. Now I carry this.*

Ulyana knelt at the edge of the glass cradle, close enough to feel heat on her face, and the gods inside her were very quiet. That quiet frightened her more than any clamor ever had. It was the quiet of a congregation waiting for the sermon to end, of wolves sitting at the forest's edge.

"They're afraid," she said aloud. Her voice sounded small in all that stone. "All of them. Even the loud ones."

*They remember what comes for them.* The dragon's eye opened—a slit of molten gold in a face scaled over like old bark—and regarded her without pity or cruelty, which was somehow worse than either. *You have seen the crown's memory, binder. You know what is climbing down the mountain toward us.*

She did know. She had known since the pass, since the bridge fell behind them, since the defector's scream had come apart into something that wasn't a scream at all but a syllable of the hollow king's long patience. She could feel it now, distantly, the way one feels weather coming in a bad tooth: a pressure against the world's skin, vast and cold and *hungry*, descending through the roots of Thornfell with the unhurried certainty of a thing that had waited centuries and could wait centuries more.

"Dominic," she said.

He was already moving. He had been moving, she realized, for some time—checking the tunnel mouth where they'd entered, wedging the iron pry-bar he'd carried since Vaelmoor under a slab of fallen ceiling, testing the weight of his old army maul with the grim familiarity of a man greeting a friend he'd hoped never to see again. He wore the waystation keeper's coat she'd first seen him in, patched at both elbows, and he looked, in the candlelight of a god's last fire, like exactly what he was: a tired man standing between the dark and what he loved.

"The tunnel narrows fifty paces in," he said. "Past that, it's single file. If they come, they come one at a time."

"If they come," Ulyana said, "there are twelve of them, and their captain doesn't bleed."

"Then I'll have to be very rude about it." He said it lightly, and his eyes didn't match his voice at all. They were the eyes of a man doing arithmetic he hated: one maul, one pry-bar, fifty paces of stone, and everything on the far side of him worth dying for. "Emberlast. How long does she need?"

The dragon's gaze slid from Ulyana to the guttering flame and back. *To unbind? An hour, perhaps less, if she does not falter. To choose? That I cannot say. Some choices take a lifetime even when there is none left.*

"Then we buy you an hour," Dominic said, "or we don't buy anything at all."

"Dominic—" She stood, and the frost-steam swirled around her, and for a moment she felt every prayer she had ever answered stacked inside her like cordwood: the pilgrim's straightened hand, the war-god's terrible thunder, the small warmths and lucks and mercies rationed across a hundred miles of hard road. "If it gets past you—"

"It won't."

"You can't promise that."

"I'm not promising." He crossed to her, and his hand found her shoulder, rough and steady, calluses from rope and iron and twenty years of keeping a gate no one thanked him for keeping. "I'm telling you what I intend. There's a difference. Promises are for things you control." A ghost of the crooked smile. "I intend to be extremely inconvenient."

She laughed—or something between a laugh and a sob—and caught his coat in both fists and pressed her forehead to his, briefly, fiercely, the way you touch someone when you are not certain the touching will be permitted again. He smelled of woodsmoke and cold iron and the road.

"One hour," she whispered.

"One hour," he agreed. Then he turned, took up the maul, and walked toward the tunnel mouth with the unhurried gait of a man going out to mend a fence, and Ulyana watched him go and understood that she was watching a man walk toward the place where the rest of his life would be spent, whatever remained of it, in the next sixty minutes.

She turned back to the flame. Closed her eyes. Reached inward, past the frightened choir, past the small broken god of thresholds still whimpering in its corner of her skull, down to the deep place where Emberlast's ember-warmth met her own mortal pulse.

And began.

* * *

Dominic Lockridge had held worse ground.

He told himself that as he settled into the narrow throat of the tunnel, back to the caldera, maul loose in his grip. He had held the gate-house at Thornfell through the White Winter with six men and a barrel of pitch. He had held a river ford at Cael's Crossing with a broken collarbone and a fever. He had held, for eleven years, a post nobody wanted, in a silence nobody believed mattered, and he had held it well.

But he had never held ground alone against the Hollow Guard, and he knew it, and lying to himself about it felt like a last small luxury, so he spent it and moved on.

The tunnel breathed. That was the wrongness of this place—the whole mountain breathed, slow and vast, the lungs of a sleeping god, and the air that came up from below tasted of hot stone and old grief. Somewhere behind him, faint as a heartbeat through a wall, he could feel Ulyana working. Not hear—feel, the way you feel a singer's note in your sternum. Every few minutes the air shivered with something that was almost a word.

Fifty paces. He counted them off in his head, marked the places where the walls pinched inward, chose his footing on the slick basalt. Single file, past the pinch. One at a time. A maul is a poor weapon in a tight space, but so is a sword, and a man who knows his ground is worth three who don't.

The pressure arrived before they did.

It came up the tunnel like water finding a crack—not sound, not light, just a *wrongness*, a flattening, as though the air itself had been pressed smooth of every living thing. The frost on the walls stopped glittering. His own breath went loud and ugly in his ears. And then, distantly, from the black throat of the passage, came footsteps: measured, unhurried, many, walking in step the way only soldiers do, and beneath them a voice like dry silk sliding over a blade.

"Keeper."

He knew that voice. He had shared a mess-tent with it at Cael's Crossing; he had drunk to fallen friends with it; he had once sewn shut the arm that belonged to it, badly, by firelight, while its owner cursed him with great creativity and forgave him instantly. Captain Edran Voss. Twenty years dead, by every report. Standing now at the mouth of the dark with nothing behind his eyes.

"Voss." Dominic did not raise the maul. Not yet. Raising it early was spending strength you'd want later. "You look terrible."

A pause. Something that might, in a living man, have been amusement. "You always did joke at the wrong moments."

"I've been told it's my best quality." Dominic shifted his weight, found his line. "There were twelve of you at the pass. I count eight sets of boots."

"Four were spent opening the deep ways. Tools wear out." Voss stepped forward into the faint glow bleeding up from the caldera, and Dominic made himself look at the face he'd once trusted with his life. It was Edran's face. The scar through the eyebrow, the broken nose from the tavern brawl at Harrow Ford, the gray coming in early at the temples. Everything was Edran's except the eyes, which reflected the fire-glow like coins reflect moonlight: perfectly, and with nothing behind the shine. "Step aside, Dominic. This is not your gate."

"Everything is my gate," Dominic said. "That's rather the whole point of me."

"He is below. The hollow. It offers terms—it always offers terms; it enjoys the offering—and it has instructed us to deliver you alive if you kneel, and dead if you don't, and it has expressed no preference." Voss's head tilted, birdlike, and for one flickering instant something moved behind the coin-shine, something small and drowning, and was gone. "Kneel, old friend. It will hurt less. It hurts less for everyone, in the end. That is its whole argument, and it has never lost the argument."

"I've heard better arguments from tax collectors."

"Then we do this the old way." Voss drew his sword, and the sound of it was wrong too, too clean, a note scraped off the bottom of the world. Behind him, seven shapes fanned out into the dark, and Dominic set his feet, and thought—absurdly, tenderly—of Ulyana's forehead against his, of woodsmoke and cold iron, of a ledger at a new gate with a blank page waiting for two names written side by side.

"Not today," he said.

The first Guard came at the pinch, where the walls forced him sideways, and Dominic hit him with the maul like a man driving a fencepost, full hip and shoulder, and the Guard went down and did not get up, because whatever animated it needed the body whole and the body was not whole anymore. The second came low. The third came with Voss behind it, patient as winter.

Dominic fought the way he kept gates: stubbornly, economically, giving up nothing he didn't have to, counting seconds instead of enemies. Ten. Twenty. The maul grew heavy. Blood ran into his eye from a cut he didn't remember taking, and he blinked it away and broke a knee and took a blade-graze along the ribs that burned like a brand, and somewhere behind him the mountain shivered with a word that was almost a word, and he grinned through the blood, because that was Ulyana, that was her voice, and as long as he could feel it he was not alone in the dark.

Thirty seconds. Forty. Voss came for him at last, sword moving with the terrible economy of the unliving, and Dominic gave ground, gave ground, gave it grudgingly like a miser parting with coin, until his heel found the stone he'd chosen, the one that rocked—

—and the Hollow Guard captain stepped onto it, and Dominic swung the pry-bar he'd hidden there into the slab above, and half the tunnel's throat came down between them in a roar of dust and black stone.

Silence. Ringing ears. Grit in his teeth. Through the fresh rubble, muffled, came the dry-silk voice, entirely unbothered:

"Clever, keeper. We will dig. It is what we are for."

Dominic sagged against the wall, ribs screaming, and laughed until it hurt, which did not take long.

* * *

In the caldera, the flame had grown.

Not larger—brighter, tighter, whiter, a candle becoming a star becoming a wound in the world, and Ulyana stood within its light with her arms open and every god in creation pouring through her like a river through a broken dam, and she was singing, or the gods were singing through her, a name older than names.

Which was why she heard the other voice arrive.

It did not echo. Sound had no courage in its presence. It simply was, suddenly, everywhere at once, courteous and cold, the voice of a king addressing a court that had long ago stopped being people.

"*Binder.*"

The temperature fell. Frost raced across the glass cradle in feathering white ferns. At the tunnel mouth, the rubble Dominic had brought down began, softly, to shift—as though hands on the far side were patient, and numerous, and in no hurry at all.

"You have done marvelously," the hollow king said, and it descended the caldera wall as it spoke, a figure in a crown of black iron, walking down sheer stone the way a spider walks down silk, robes trailing upward, face a beloved face worn like a mask over an absence. "Two thousand years I have waited for a vessel equal to the work. Every speaker before you was a cup. You are a *well*. Look what you carry. Look what you have already become."

Ulyana held the song. Sweat froze on her brow. She did not turn her head.

"Join me." The voice wrapped around her like a fur cloak, warm and suffocating. "Keep your power. Keep the voices—they are yours now, they answer to *you*, no longer to silence. Together we will rule kindly. No prayer will go unanswered again. No child will die unheard. No village like the unanswered ones will ever want again. I offer you the only honest bargain in creation: everything, in exchange for everything. Refuse, and you burn with the lizard. Refuse, and I drink this fire and wear divinity openly, and you will be a footnote in a scripture no one reads."

Behind her, Emberlast stirred, scales grinding like millstones, gathering the last dregs of two millennia into the coil of its ruined body.

And Ulyana, singing, felt the choice rise in her like floodwater—felt the well of herself tremble at its edges—and through the roar of the gods she heard, impossibly, faintly, from the tunnel: a man laughing through broken ribs.

She held the song. She opened her eyes.

"No," she said, and the word went through the caldera like a bell through glass.

The hollow king smiled with a dead man's mouth.

"Then," it said, "we proceed to the unpleasant portion of the evening"—and the rubble at the tunnel mouth burst inward, and eight shapes poured through the breach with swords drawn, and Dominic Lockridge, alone, bleeding, and grinning, planted his feet before the flame and raised his maul one final time.
