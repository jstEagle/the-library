## 18. Unbinding

The caldera smelled of iron and old snow.

Ulyana stood at the edge of Emberlast's coiled body, and the heat of the dragon's sleeping blood rose through the stone floor in slow waves, like breath from a buried hearth. Above them, the mountain groaned. Somewhere behind, down the throat of the tunnel, Dominic was holding the line alone against the Hollow Guard with a war-axe, a dying god's borrowed strength, and nothing else.

She could hear him. Not his voice—the voices never gave her that mercy—but the shape of him fighting. The gods in her head had gone quiet in the way a held breath is quiet, every one of them leaning toward the tunnel mouth, listening for the moment they would be needed. Or the moment it would no longer matter.

The candle-flame at the caldera's heart was smaller than when she'd first seen it. An hour ago it had been the size of her fist. Now it was a thumbnail of gold trembling above the black stone, and Emberlast lay wrapped around it like a mother around a dying child, scales gone grey as ash at the edges, eyes half-lidded, ancient, patient in the way only something that has waited two thousand years can be patient.

*It is time,* the dragon had said, or the memory of the dragon said, or the god speaking through the dragon said; by now Ulyana could no longer always tell where one voice ended and another began. *The crown is coming down the mountain. You will not get a second chance. Reach into it while the fire still answers you.*

She knelt.

The stone was warm beneath her knees, warmer than it should have been, warm as skin. She pressed both palms flat against it and felt the pulse of the last fire moving under the rock like a heartbeat heard through a wall—a heartbeat slowing.

*I don't know how,* she said aloud, because saying things aloud made them real, and real things could be survived.

*You have always known,* said the voice that was Emberlast, or the binder she had been, or the girl who had grown up hearing prayers meant for dead gods in a smuggler's camp where no one prayed to anything but luck and weather. *Every speaker before you reached into the crown and was devoured. They went in alone, each carrying one god, one fragment, one small light—and the hunger swallowed them one at a time. That was its genius. That was its patience. It never fought the whole choir.*

Ulyana closed her eyes. The voices rose around her like water rising in a locked room.

There was the threshold-god from the collapsing cavern, small and broken, whispering *open, open, open* in a tongue older than doors. There was the war-god whose fury had leveled a chapel tower in Vaelmoor, still hot as a coal in the back of her skull. There were harvest gods and hearth gods, river mothers and road fathers, the nameless thing that watched over hanged men, the twin deities of debt and forgiveness who spoke in perfect unison and had never once agreed on anything. Hundreds of them. Thousands. All the gods the binders had fused into the crown two thousand years ago to stop a war—all of them except the ones already eaten, and the ones that had rotted into the hollow wearing the king's face.

They were all waiting on her. They had been waiting on her for two thousand years, in one life or another.

*If I do this wrong,* she said, *it eats me too.*

*Yes,* said Emberlast, without cruelty. *That is possible. That has always been possible. But girl—if you do nothing, it eats everything.*

Above them, the mountain screamed. Stone split somewhere far up the tunnel, and dust sifted down through the red dark, and Ulyana thought of Dominic—his grey-streaked hair, his hands scarred from twenty years of checking a seal no one thanked him for checking, the way he'd said *I stay* at the lowest point of their road together, furious and frightened and staying anyway.

She opened herself.

* * *

It did not feel like power. She had expected power—she had tasted fragments of it, the war-god's terrible loan, the healer's warmth in the Village of the Unanswered. This was different. This was like being a door kicked open in a storm.

Every god came through at once.

She had braced for noise and got instead a vast, ringing silence, the silence inside a bell the instant before it strikes. Then the strike came, and she was everywhere. She was rain falling on ten thousand roofs that no longer existed. She was the first fire lit in the first cold winter of the world. She was every prayer ever spoken into empty air since the binding—every beggar's whisper, every mother's plea, every soldier's last word—and she felt them land, all of them, at once, in her, because there had been nowhere else for them to go for two thousand years and they had been piling up against the sealed door of heaven like driftwood against a seawall.

She screamed. Or the choir screamed through her. The sound came out of her mouth in a hundred overlapping tongues and shook grit from the caldera ceiling, and Emberlast lifted its great grey head and *breathed*, one long exhalation of its own failing fire, and the breath caught the choir's roar and bent it, focused it, poured it into a single thread of gold no wider than a needle.

*Now,* said the dragon. *While the door is open. Go in and find what is left of him.*

The thread of fire went out of her like an arrow leaving a bowstring, and Ulyana went with it.

* * *

Inside the crown there was no light.

There was no darkness either, properly speaking—darkness implied a place, walls, a floor. This was absence with an appetite. She moved through it the way a thought moves, without feet, without weight, and around her drifted the debris of two millennia: broken oaths, half-eaten names, the fossilized shapes of prayers that had finally stopped hoping. Things brushed past her in the black, things that had once been gods, and some of them whimpered, and some of them did not know anymore what whimpering was for.

And then she saw the throne.

It was not a throne of gold or stone. It was a throne of *wearing*—of faces layered over faces, kings and priests and conquerors fused and compressed until they formed a single seat, and upon it sat the hollow, vast and coiled, wearing the shape of the king the way a coat hangs on a hook. Its crown was not metal. It was the fused gods themselves, hammered into a circlet, still faintly alive, still faintly screaming, so old now that the screaming had worn smooth as river stones.

The hollow turned its head. Its eyes reflected nothing. They had never reflected anything.

*Little vessel,* it said, and its voice was the king's voice, warm and reasonable and terribly tired. *You brought the whole choir to me at once. Do you know how long I have wanted this? Two thousand years I picked at the seams, one speaker at a time, a morsel here, a morsel there. And now you walk yourself into my mouth carrying everything.*

Ulyana stood in the dark before the throne of wearing, and she was afraid—of course she was afraid—but underneath the fear, steady as bedrock, was something else. Something Dominic had taught her without meaning to, rationing his firewood, rationing his hope, teaching her that what is kept is kept by measure.

*You're looking at the wrong thing,* she said.

The hollow paused.

*You see the choir,* Ulyana said. *You see the fire. You're counting it, the way you've counted everything for two thousand years. But I didn't come for the choir.* She stepped forward, into the reach of those empty eyes, and let herself become very small and very quiet, the way she had learned to do in smugglers' camps when soldiers came through—make yourself nothing, make yourself a crack in the wall, and slip through. *I came for what's underneath you.*

And she reached past the hollow, past the throne of wearing, down into the place where the stolen man had been pressed flat and forgotten, and she found him.

He was the size of a child. He was the size of a starved bird. He crouched in a space no larger than a coffin, curled around the last thing he owned, which was his own name, held against his chest with both thin arms the way a beggar holds bread.

He looked up at her with eyes that reflected *everything*—every torch she had ever lit, every face she had ever loved, the whole small mortal world shining back out of them—and he said, in a voice rusted from disuse:

"Do you know my name?"

* * *

Later—hours later, or years, or in the moment before the world ended—Ulyana would try to describe what it was like to hold a king's entire remaining self in her cupped hands. She would fail. There were no words for a soul that small, that patient, that *polite*. Because he was polite. Two thousand years in the dark beneath the thing that wore him, and the true King of Thornfell—Aldric, third of his name, crowned at nineteen, beloved once by a kingdom that no longer remembered why—did not rage at his jailer or curse his fate. He simply asked, again, the question he had been asking the dark since the day the fusion rotted:

"Do you know my name? Does anyone remember it?"

"I do," Ulyana said.

Around them the hollow was rising from its throne, and the dark was filling with its attention, and she could feel it beginning to understand—not yet afraid, because it had never been afraid, but *interested*, the way a spider is interested when the web trembles in the wrong pattern.

"You're going to take me out," the remnant-king whispered. "Or it will eat you, and then no one will ever say it again."

"Then hold on," Ulyana told him. "Hold on to it. Don't give it up even to save me. Promise me."

The ruined king looked at her—at the binder returned, the girl with frost in her blood and a smuggler's manners and a face he might have known once, two thousand years ago, across a council table, before the war, before the crown—and he nodded, once, like a man accepting terms.

The hollow struck.

It came down on her like a mountain learning to fall, all that accumulated hunger, all those centuries of patient picking at the world's seams, and Ulyana did the only thing left to do. She did not fight it. She opened her mouth inside the dark, inside the crown, inside the hollow's own throat, and she spoke the king's name.

Not shouted. Spoken. The way you speak a name to wake someone gently. The way you speak it at a graveside. The way his mother must have said it, once, in a nursery in a castle that was probably ruins now.

"Aldric."

* * *

The shriek that tore out of the crown was heard in three kingdoms.

In the caldera, Emberlast reared its failing body upright and roared back, and the two sounds met above the candle-flame and the air itself split along a seam no one had known was there. In the tunnel above, the Hollow Guard froze mid-blow, and Dominic Lockridge—bleeding, gasping, one eye swollen shut—watched six armored figures collapse like puppets with cut strings, their captain among them, the old army friend's face going slack and almost, briefly, human before the armor crumbled to grey flakes.

And inside the crown, the hollow tore loose.

It came apart the way rot comes away from bone—all at once, shrieking, formless, a shape that had spent two thousand years pretending to be a man and now had nothing left to pretend with. It writhed in the dark, vast and diminishing at once, reaching for the throne of wearing and finding the throne crumbling, reaching for the choir and finding the choir singing—*singing*, for the first time in two millennia, every god at once, harmonized at last around a single mortal voice—and it could not hold. Without the king's shape it was only hunger, and hunger cannot stand in the presence of a name freely given.

Ulyana held the remnant-king against her chest as the hollow dissolved around them, and she felt him weep, and she felt the fused gods in the crumbling crown loosen one by one like knots coming untied after years, and she carried him up and out, out of the dark, out of the crown, back toward the light of a candle-flame that was—

That was almost gone.

* * *

She woke on the warm stone of the caldera with Dominic's hand gripping her shoulder and his voice cracking her name into pieces. The choir was silent in her head—not gone, she could feel them, a congregation settling into pews, waiting—but quiet, so quiet she could hear her own heartbeat for the first time in weeks.

Emberlast lay with its head lowered to the floor of the caldera. Before it, the last fire of the old gods burned no larger than a match flame, guttering, blue at the edges, dying.

"It gave everything," Dominic said hoarsely. "The unbinding. It fed the whole working." His face was a ruin of blood and exhaustion, and his eyes, when they met hers, held something she had never seen in them before: awe, and grief, and fear braided together. "Ulyana. The flame's almost out. And that thing—" He pointed upward, toward the shattered seam in the air where the hollow had torn free. "It's not dead. Look."

She looked.

High above the caldera, in the red dark of the breached mountain, something hung in the air that was not smoke and was not shadow: a formlessness, a hunger with no shape left to wear, drifting like a jellyfish in deep water. It had lost the king. It had lost the crown. It was smaller now, and weaker, and more dangerous for exactly that reason—because a starving thing with nothing left to lose has only one move remaining.

And it was descending slowly toward the candle-flame, and toward the girl who had taken everything it wanted.

Emberlast opened one ancient eye, grey as grave-dust, and looked at Ulyana, and spoke for what might have been the last time.

*It comes to bargain, girl. Hunger always does. And you will have a choice to make before it reaches us—one I cannot make for you, though I have waited two thousand years to offer it.*

The flame guttered. The hollow drifted closer. And Ulyana, mortal and emptied and more alive than she had ever been, rose to her feet.
