## 20. Mortal Again

The hollow's offer hung in the caldera air like smoke that refused to rise.

*Become the vessel,* it said, in the king's stolen voice, in her mother's voice, in a hundred voices Ulyana had carried since childhood. *Rule kindly. End all hunger. No more listening to prayers you cannot answer—every prayer answered, forever.*

And Emberlast, coiled around its candle-flame of a heart, said only: *There is another way. It costs everything you are becoming. It leaves you everything you already were.*

Ulyana looked at Dominic.

He was on his knees at the tunnel mouth, sword broken to a stub, blood sheeting down one arm from where the Hollow Guard's captain had opened him to the bone before the last of them fell. He was not looking at her. He was looking at the hollow king—at the thing wearing Aldric Vane's face and crown—and his jaw was set in the particular way it set when he had already decided something terrible and was waiting for the world to catch up.

"Dominic," she said.

"Don't," he said. "Don't ask me first. Whatever you choose—" His voice cracked, and he laughed at himself for it, a small broken sound. "Whatever you choose, I'll still be here tomorrow. That's the whole point. That's what I've been trying to tell you since Vaelmoor."

*The mortal loves you,* the hollow observed, with something that might have been tenderness if tenderness could rot. *Mortals always love what they cannot keep. I can make you unkeepable, little conduit. I can make you the thing that outlasts grief itself.*

"I know," Ulyana said. "That's why I'm saying no."

She turned to Emberlast.

The dragon was dying. She had known it from the moment they breached the caldera—the great coils gone slack as old rope, scales dull as river stones, the flame beneath its breast no larger than a votive candle and guttering like one. Two thousand years of burning so that a door stayed ajar and a thief stayed bound, and now there was almost nothing left. When Emberlast exhaled, the breath smelled of cold iron and finished things.

*You understand what is asked,* the dragon said. Not a question. Dragons did not waste questions.

"Tell me anyway," Ulyana said. "All of it. I want to hear myself refuse properly."

Emberlast's eye—a slit of amber gone pale as frost glass—regarded her for a long moment. Then, slowly, the vast head dipped until its brow nearly touched the stone before her, an inclination that in any other creature would have been called bowing.

*Pour me into you,* the dragon said. *Not into your gift. Into your choice. The fire does not want a god. It never wanted a god—it wanted a doorkeeper who would one day have the courage to lock it. Your past self built the crown because she could not bear to kill the gods outright. Her mercy rotted into the hollow. Yours must be harder. Take my last fire and burn the divinity out of the world. Every god. Every voice. Every prayer ever left unanswered will simply be unanswered, finally and completely, and no power will remain in Thornfell for anything crowned or hungry to wear.*

"And me?" Ulyana asked, though she already knew. She had known since the Choir of Ash, since the blind matriarch's hands had found her face and trembled. "What happens to the voices?"

*They go where the gods go,* Emberlast said. *Into silence. You will wake tomorrow morning and hear nothing but wind. You will kneel by a dying man and your hands will be only hands. You will pray, someday, perhaps, when the grief is large enough—and nothing will answer, and nothing will ever answer again.* A pause, long as a season. *It is the only mercy I have left to give, and it will feel like a wound. I am sorry. Mercy usually does.*

The hollow had gone very still. In two thousand years of patient hunger, Ulyana thought, it had probably never once been still. Stillness was not in its nature; it was a thing made entirely of reaching.

"You would be nothing," it said quietly. "No power. No purpose. A smuggler's daughter with dirt under her nails and a dead god's debt paid off. Is that what you want? After everything? After they knelt to you in their dreams?"

Ulyana thought about the pilgrim in Dominic's waystation, the withered hand straightening under her touch while terror sang through her like a struck bell. She thought about the war-god's fury in her throat at Vaelmoor, the chapel tower coming down, the feeling—God forgive her, the *feeling*—of being right. She thought about the village of the unanswered, and the old woman who had thanked her anyway, just for listening, just for staying.

"No," she said. "But it's mine. And I'd rather have a small life that's mine than a large one that belongs to everyone's hunger." She smiled, and found to her surprise that it didn't shake. "Besides. You're offering me forever. I've heard forever's voice. It never shuts up."

Something happened to the hollow's face then—something that in a man would have been called flinching.

Dominic hauled himself up the wall beside the tunnel mouth, leaving a red smear on the stone, and came toward her. He didn't argue. She had braced herself for arguing—for *there has to be another way*, for *we'll find it together*—and instead he simply stood beside her, close enough that his shoulder touched hers, and said, "I'm here. However long it takes. However loud it gets."

"It won't get loud," Ulyana said. "That's the whole point."

She knelt before Emberlast.

*Take my breath,* the dragon said. *It is only fire now. It needs somewhere warm to finish.*

Ulyana leaned forward and pressed her lips to the dragon's brow, between the pale eyes, where the scales were worn smooth as a psalter's pages from two thousand years of dreaming.

Emberlast breathed out.

* * *

Fire went into her the way winter goes into a valley—not all at once but everywhere at once, through every seam, every crack, every place she had ever been broken open. It did not hurt. That was the worst of it. It felt like being *remembered*—like every version of herself, the binder in the ancient circle, the child hearing voices in the smuggler's wagon, the woman kneeling in the ash of Vaelmoor's cathedral, all of them suddenly present and burning and *known*.

She opened her mouth and spoke with a voice that was not one voice.

"*Enough.*"

The word went through the caldera like a bell through water. The hollow king staggered. The crown on its head—the fused, screaming, centuries-deep knot of every god the binders had welded into gold to stop a war—began, at last, to come apart.

Ulyana felt each of them leave. That was the price Emberlast hadn't mentioned, or maybe had, in its own oblique draconic way: she would feel all of it. The threshold-god going out of the world like a lamp pinched between wet fingers, taking with it every doorway's small blessing. The war-god's fury draining out of the mountains' bones. The hearth-mother, the road-father, the nameless small deities of luck and lintels and last chances—all of them unwinding into silence, and every thread of them passing through her on the way, brushing her like mourners at a funeral touching the bereaved.

*Thank you,* whispered the threshold-god, the small broken one that had begged her in the dark. *Oh. Oh, thank you. We only ever wanted rest.*

Then it was gone, and the space where it had been was quiet, and the quiet was—

The quiet was kind.

She had spent her whole life thinking of silence as absence, as the shape of a thing missing. But this silence was full. It was the silence after a fever breaks. It was the silence of a house where everyone is finally asleep. The voices did not die screaming; they went out like candles at the end of a vigil, one by one, gratefully, and the darkness they left behind was not empty but *rested*.

The hollow shrieked.

It tore at the dissolving crown with fingers that were losing their definition, that were becoming suggestion rather than substance, hunger without a mouth, reaching without hands. "*No—no, I waited, I was PATIENT, I kept the shape, I kept the SHAPE—*" It turned to Ulyana with the king's face sliding off it like wax, revealing nothing beneath, revealing everything, and made its last offer in a voice fraying to static: "*Take it. Take the crown yourself. Anything. I will serve. I have served for two thousand years—I know how to serve—*"

"There's nothing left to wear," Ulyana said gently. "That's all you ever were. Something looking for a face."

The hollow looked at its own hands, and found them gone, and made a sound that was very old and very small and, for one unbearable instant, sounded exactly like a man.

Then it came apart. Not violently. It simply stopped holding itself together, the way a held breath stops, the way a lie stops when someone finally tells the truth. The last of it drifted across the caldera floor as a grey shimmer, passed through Ulyana like a draft under a door, and was gone.

The crown rang against the stone. Just gold now. Just metal. It rolled once, twice, and lay still.

* * *

Emberlast was smiling.

Ulyana hadn't known dragons could smile. Their mouths were built for other purposes. But the great head lay flat on the caldera floor now, and the amber eyes had gone soft and half-lidded, and the enormous mouth was curved in unmistakable contentment—the expression of a watchman setting down a lantern after the longest night of a very long career.

*Two thousand years,* the dragon said. Its voice was fainter than breath now, more memory than sound. *Do you know what I dreamed about, all that time? Not freedom. Not fire. I dreamed about finishing. About putting the work down.* The pale eye found hers. *You were worth the wait, binder. Both of you. The one who built the cage, and the one who burned it.*

"Rest," Ulyana said. Her voice came out wrecked. "Please. You've earned it."

*Oh, I intend to.* The dragon's ribs rose once, deeply, and settled. *Tell the mountain thank you for holding me. Tell the sky—* A pause. The smile deepened. *No. Tell the sky nothing. Let it wonder.*

And Emberlast, last dragon of Thornfell, keeper of the thin wick, crumbled.

It did not collapse the way ruins collapse. It came apart gently, scale by scale, into fine grey ash that drifted upward instead of down—as if even its weight had been part of the work, and was done now. The ash rose through the caldera's high crack in a slow silver column, catching light that no longer had a source, and Ulyana stood in the middle of it with her arms out and let it settle on her hair, her shoulders, her closed eyelids, until she was dusted grey as a saint in an old fresco.

When she opened her eyes, the cavern was just a cavern. Cold stone. Dripping water. The ordinary dark of underground places.

And silence. Total, bottomless, human silence.

She listened for the voices the way a prisoner tests a door for years after release—out of habit, out of fear the habit is all that's holding her up. Nothing. No whispers in dead tongues. No prayers arriving from strangers' mouths. No gods murmuring behind her sternum. Just her own heartbeat, quick and mortal and hers alone, and somewhere above, wind moving through the pass.

Ulyana sat down in the ash and wept.

Not for the gods—they had wanted what she gave them. Not for the power—she had never trusted it, not really, not in the marrow. She wept because the silence was so kind, and because kindness that arrives after twenty years of noise feels, at first, exactly like loss.

* * *

Dominic found her there. Of course he did. He moved like a man reading a map he'd drawn himself, favoring his ruined arm, and when he reached her he didn't say anything clever. He lowered himself down into the ash beside her, hissed at the state of his arm, and let her lean against him while the grey settled around them both.

"The king," he said eventually. "Aldric. He's—over there. By the tunnel. He's been trying to work up the nerve to come over for ten minutes."

Ulyana wiped her face and looked.

The man standing at the caldera's edge was gaunt as famine. Two thousand years of being worn as a shape had hollowed him to a frame of a man—cheeks like shovel-blades, hair white as the ash, hands trembling at his sides as though he'd forgotten what hands were for. The crown lay on the ground near him and he would not look at it. He could not look away from it either. His eyes were wet and human and utterly lost.

"He remembers," Ulyana said. "Some of it. Enough."

"Does he remember being the hollow?"

"Yes." She watched the ruined king sway slightly, a man learning to stand inside his own skin again. "That's the sentence, isn't it? He gets to live, and he gets to remember everything he did while something else wore him."

Dominic was quiet a moment. "Good," he said, and then, immediately, wretchedly: "No. Not good. I don't know what word I want. He was my king. I swore oaths to him."

"He wasn't anything to you, Dom. That was the whole horror of it." She stood, knees cracking, ash sliding from her shoulders. "Come on. Let's go see what's left of the realm."

They crossed the caldera together, and the restored King Aldric Vane watched them come, and when they were three paces away he did something no chronicle would believe and no court painter would dare paint.

He knelt.

Down into the ash, both knees, the way men knelt to altars in the old days, and bowed his head, and the sound he made was not words at first—just breath, ragged, the breathing of a man surfacing from very deep water. When he finally spoke, his voice was rust and gravel and, underneath it, unbearably young.

"They told me," he said. "In the dark, at the end. The thing wore me and it *talked to me*, all those years, telling me what it did with my hands. I heard every prayer it answered wrong. I heard—" His fist pressed against the ash. "I heard my own name. I said it to myself for two thousand years so I wouldn't lose it. *Aldric. Aldric. Aldric.*" He lifted his head, and his eyes—his own eyes now, tired and terrified and real—found hers. "You said it back to me. In the crown. I heard you say my name, and I remembered which of us was me."

Ulyana crouched down to his level, ash drifting between them.

"Get up," she said. "You're a king. Kneel to no one."

"I'm hardly a king. I'm a ghost with a title." He laughed, and it broke in the middle. "The realm will need—someone will need to—"

"The realm will need a lot of things," Dominic said from behind her. "Starting with a border that isn't a prison. But that's tomorrow's problem."

Aldric looked up at him. At her. At the ash falling like slow snow through the lightless air, settling over the place where a dragon had been, over a crown that was only gold, over two mortals and a ghost, all of them, for the first time in two thousand years, entirely alone in their own skins.

"What do I do?" the king whispered. "What does anyone do now? There's nothing listening."

Ulyana took a breath, and heard nothing in it but wind and her own heart, and found that she was smiling.

"Now," she said, "we answer each other."

Above them, through the caldera's high crack, the first grey light of dawn came down into the dark—and with it, faint and far off along the pass, the sound of bells. Someone in the low country was ringing the temple bells. Not in prayer. There was no one left to pray to. They were ringing them the way people ring bells at the end of a war: wildly, badly, joyfully, just to hear something true.

Dominic offered Ulyana his good hand.

"Come on," he said. "Let's go home the long way."

She took it.
