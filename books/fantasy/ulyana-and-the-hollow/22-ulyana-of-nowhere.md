## 22. Ulyana of Nowhere

The wind on the high pass had no words in it.

Ulyana noticed this every morning now, the way a man who has lived beside a roaring river notices silence once the water is gone. For twenty-six years her skull had been a crowded chapel—whispers in dead tongues, pleas in living ones, the low groan of gods pressed flat under a crown of stolen fire. She had learned to sleep through it the way smugglers sleep through storms, one ear always open, never truly resting.

Now there was only wind. It came off the eastern snowfields and poured down the throat of the Thornfell pass, and it said nothing to anyone, and it was the most beautiful sound she had ever heard.

She walked the old patrol road with her hands in her pockets and her breath steaming, though it was late spring and the lower valleys were green as envy. Up here the seasons kept their own counsel. Frost still clung to the shaded stones, and the pines stood dark and patient, and somewhere below her—she could feel it in her boots more than hear it—the new gate crew was already at work, hammers ringing against stone in a rhythm like a slow heart.

And somewhere behind her, badly, came singing.

*"Ohhh, the warden's daughter loved a man with a crooked nose—"*

"You're flat," Ulyana called over her shoulder without turning around.

"I'm *rustic*," Dominic Lockridge answered, catching up in three long strides. He had filled out since winter, or perhaps she had simply stopped seeing him as a tired man holding a door shut against the end of the world and started seeing him as what he was: broad-shouldered, gray at the temples, with a face that did its smiling reluctantly and therefore meant every one. "There's a difference. Rustic is a style."

"Rustic is an apology you make before you sing."

He laughed—that low, surprised laugh he still seemed to startle out of himself sometimes, as if he'd forgotten he owned it—and slung an arm briefly around her shoulders, warm through her coat. Then his hand found hers, because arms around shoulders were for jokes but hands were for true things, and they walked the rest of the ridge that way, boots crunching, the valley opening below them like a book someone had finally agreed to let them read.

The pass looked different without dread in it. That was the thing no one warned you about, Ulyana thought. Not that the world changes after the end of everything nearly happens. Just that your eyes change, and the world turns out to have been beautiful the whole time, waiting for you to look up from the knife's edge long enough to see it.

* * *

They came down to the waystation by midmorning, and it was not empty.

Pilgrims. Still, after all these months, pilgrims.

There were six of them camped in the lee of the station wall—a mason with dust still in the creases of his knuckles, two sisters who might have been farmers, an old soldier missing three fingers, a girl of maybe twelve leading a blind grandmother by the elbow. They rose when Dominic and Ulyana appeared, the way people did now, that half-bow that made Ulyana's skin crawl and her chest ache in equal measure. They knew who she was. The whole road knew. The woman who had been a god for one hour and chosen to be nobody instead. Stories traveled faster than kings' decrees, and this story had grown a beard and a horse and three impossible endings between Thornfell and the sea.

"We're not asking anything," the mason said quickly, hands raised, when he saw her face tighten. "Truly, lady. We just—we heard you keep candles."

Ulyana let out a breath. "I keep candles," she said.

Inside the waystation, along the eastern wall where the frost had bloomed the day Dominic found her unconscious on the stones, stood a shelf. On the shelf stood candles. Dozens of them, stubs and tapers and one absurd church-candle some merchant had donated, wax pooling and hardening in layers like the rings of a felled tree. Beside them, a clay pot of matches, and a bowl where travelers left copper coins that Ulyana gave back to the road whenever the bowl got heavy, buying bread for the next hungry pilgrim through, so that the coins went around and around like a small loyal dog that kept coming home.

The grandmother was brought forward first, guided by the girl. "She wants to pray," the child said. "But we don't know—to whom. There isn't anyone anymore, is there? The priests all say different things. Some say the gods are dead. Some say they're sleeping. One man in Vaelmoor said they were never real at all and then he cried, which didn't seem like something you'd do if they weren't real."

Ulyana crouched so she was level with the old woman's clouded eyes. Once—she could hardly remember being that person, and yet she was, bone for bone—the voices would already be rising in her, sorting through this woman's unspoken need, choosing which dead god's mouth to borrow. Once, she could have straightened a withered hand, warmed a frozen lung, turned luck like a card sharp turns a queen.

Now there was only her. Only Ulyana, with her two hands and her box of matches.

"She can pray however she likes," Ulyana said. "To whoever she likes. To nobody at all. Nobody's listening up there anymore." She struck a match and touched it to a fresh taper, cupping the flame until it caught and stood. "But I'm listening down here. And I'll light this for her, and it'll burn as long as it burns, and that's a true thing I can promise. It's not much."

The old woman's cracked lips moved. Whatever prayer she made, she made it silently, into the small flame, and Ulyana watched her face and thought: *this is what I am now.* Not a conduit. Not a keyhole. Not a vessel or a binder or a bride of divinity. A woman who lit candles. Who listened. Who stayed in the room while you said the unsayable thing, and did not flinch, and did not fix it, because fixing was never actually the point.

It had taken her most of the winter to understand that. There had been nights—she would not tell even Dominic how many—when she had sat on this floor and grieved the power like a lost limb, phantom-aching, reaching for a god the way you reach for a lamp in a familiar corner and find only wall. The Choir of Ash would have understood. The Choir had burned themselves out on purpose; Ulyana had simply handed the fire back. But grief did not check your reasons at the door.

Then, one gray morning, the mason's sister—younger then, terrified, her son fevered—had taken Ulyana's hand and said, "You sat with me all night. You didn't sleep either. Why?" And Ulyana had started to say *because I owed the world* and heard, instead, coming out of her own mouth, the truth: *"Because you were afraid and I was there."*

That was all. That was everything. Belonging wasn't earned by power. She'd learned that in a village where no prayer had ever been answered, from an old woman who thanked her merely for listening. She'd just needed the whole rest of the war to believe it applied to her too.

One by one the pilgrims came to the candle shelf. The soldier prayed with his three-fingered hand flat on the wood. The sisters prayed together, foreheads touching. The girl prayed longest, screwing her eyes shut with tremendous effort, and afterward asked Ulyana very seriously whether it counted if you weren't sure anyone was there.

"It counts double," Ulyana said. "Faith with nobody watching is the expensive kind."

Dominic stood by the door the whole time, arms crossed, saying nothing, wearing the expression he wore when he was memorizing something. When the last candle was lit and the pilgrims settled outside to share bread, he came and stood beside her at the shelf.

"Sixty-one," he said, counting the flames. "You're going to burn the place down."

"The place burned down once already, in a manner of speaking." She tipped her head at him. "Besides, you're the keeper of the gate. Fire safety is your department."

"The gate," Dominic said, "is a hole in a wall with honest doors in it now. My department is a ledger and a road crew and a partner who sings worse than I do."

"I don't sing."

"Thank the absent gods for that."

* * *

Afternoon took them down to the gate itself.

It was strange, seeing it open. For two thousand years the Thornfell gate had been the oldest lock in the world, sealed from the inside by binders whose names had rotted out of every record, holding shut a door between the world of men and the prison of gods—or so everyone had believed. Ulyana knew better now. There had never been anything behind the door worth locking away, not really. There had been a war, and a desperate fusion, and a crown that rotted, and a dragon who held the last match. The seal had been a bandage over a wound that needed, all along, to be cleaned and closed properly.

So the king—gaunt, gray, human, and learning to eat solid food again like a man relearning a language—had ordered it rebuilt. Not shut. *Tended.* Those had been his words, delivered in a voice like a dry leaf, when he came to the ash of Emberlast's caldera and knelt before a smuggler's daughter who wanted, more than anything, to go home and never kneel to anyone again. *A border crossed honestly,* he'd said. *A door with a keeper, not a lock with a grave.*

The road crew had taken him at his word. The ancient seal-stones, cracked from within on that first terrible morning of Ulyana's story, had been cut free and set aside—half of them shipped to Vaelmoor for the scholars, half left standing in a cairn at the pass head, a monument to the age of secrets. In their place: proper gates, oak and iron, hung on hinges that oiled smoothly, flanked by a customs house barely bigger than a barn, a stable, a bell to ring in fog. A road crew of eleven worked the approaches, widening the switchbacks, laying culverts, arguing cheerfully about drainage the way road crews had argued about drainage since the invention of roads.

The foreman, a squat woman named Brakka with forearms like ship cable, waved them over. "Ledger's ready," she said, and slapped a fat book down on the customs house sill. "First page. Been saving it."

The first page. Ulyana looked at it and felt her throat go tight, which was ridiculous, because it was a page, paper and ruled lines, and she had signed ledgers a hundred times in her father's smuggling days—false names, false towns, her signature disguised three ways on one document. Her father had taught her that a name was a tool, something to sharpen and swap out. *Never put your real name anywhere, little Ulya. A name is a handle. Handles are for grabbing.*

She picked up the pen.

Beneath the printed heading—*THORNFELL GATE, LEDGER THE FIRST, IN THE REIGN OF ALDRIC II, RESTORED*—the first line waited. *Name. Origin. Business.* Travelers would sign it, hundreds a day come autumn, merchants and pilgrims and soldiers and lovers eloping across the mountains, each one writing down exactly what they were, because the king had decreed that at this gate, of all gates, no lie was required and none permitted.

*Name,* said the line.

Ulyana stood there a long moment. Wind moved through the new gate, unhindered, carrying pine smell and hammer-song and the distant bleat of Brakka's cursed goat. Behind her she felt Dominic shift closer—not crowding, just present, the way he was, the way he had chosen to be on the worst night of her life when she told him what she'd seen in the god's memory, her own face among the ancient binders, her own hands building the cage. *You are not what you did,* he'd said, furious and frightened and staying anyway. *You're what you do next.*

Her father would have written *Marla of Greyhollow.* The gods would have written *Vessel.* The hollow thing in the crown would have written *Prey.* The Choir would have written *Speaker.* Even Emberlast, in its final crumbling smile, had called her *binder-returning*, and she had loved the dragon, and still that name had fit like a borrowed coat.

But the gods were gone. Burned out of the world by her own choice, by the last fire poured through her mortal blood, and the silence they left behind was not emptiness. It was room.

Ulyana wrote, in a clear, unhurried hand:

*Ulyana. No family. No god.*

She paused, pen hovering over *Business*, and then smiled and finished:

*Belonging here.*

Brakka read it upside down and snorted. "That's not a business."

"It's the oldest one there is," Ulyana said.

* * *

Evening came up the valley slow and gold. The road crew knocked off, and someone produced a fiddle, and someone else produced a barrel of the thin sour beer that mountain folk called ale only out of politeness, and there was a fire built in the customs house yard out of offcuts and broken pallets, and for a while Ulyana sat among strangers who were becoming neighbors and let the noise wash over her.

Dominic found her at the edge of the firelight, as she'd known he would. He handed her a mug of the terrible beer and lowered himself onto the bench beside her with the groan of a man whose knees had opinions, and for a while they drank and said nothing, watching sparks climb toward the first stars.

"They'll want you at court eventually," he said at last. "The king won't stop asking. Honors. Titles. A seat, even."

"He can put it in the ledger," Ulyana said. "I'll sign *no thank you*."

"And after that?"

"After that—" She turned the mug in her hands. Somewhere past the fire, the girl pilgrim was teaching the road crew a song, badly, everyone laughing. "After that I thought I'd walk the high passes tomorrow. There's a shepherd up past the second switchback whose wife is due to give birth any week, and no midwife nearer than two days. I can't catch babies with miracles anymore. But I can carry water, and boil it, and hold her hand, and run fast if I have to run." She glanced at him sideways. "You could come. If the gate can spare you."

"The gate," said Dominic Lockridge, keeper of the oldest border in the world, "can spare me for anything that ends with me walking next to you." He drained his beer, grimaced at it on principle, and then, because he was Dominic and could not leave well enough alone, began to hum. Then to sing. Low, tuneless, contented: *"Ohhh, the warden's daughter loved a man with a crooked nose—"*

"Dominic."

"—and she had no family, and she had no god—"

"*Dominic.*"

"—but she had a candle, and a road, and a door—"

Ulyana gave up, leaned her head against his shoulder, and let the bad song and the good silence pour over her together, wind and voice and fire-crackle, the whole ordinary music of a world with nothing hiding underneath it.

Above the pass, the stars came out in their thousands, burning for no one, answering no prayers, needing nothing from her at all.

And Ulyana of Nowhere—no family, no god, belonging exactly where she stood—was home.
