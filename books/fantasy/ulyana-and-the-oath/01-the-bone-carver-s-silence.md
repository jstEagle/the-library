## 1. The Bone-Carver's Silence

The whistle was going to be a lark, Edmund decided around midmorning, and by late afternoon it had become a nightingale, and by the time the candles were lit it was simply a small bone pipe with a hole bored wrong and no intention of ever singing again.

He turned it in the lamplight, frowning at the bore. "You're being difficult," he told it.

The dead, who had been murmuring all day like wind through dry reeds, found this funny. They often did. There was a woman — three winters gone, she'd died of fever in the winter house up Coombe way — who liked to laugh at him when he talked to his work. *He scolds the bones,* she'd say to whoever was near her, which was nobody, which was Edmund. *As if the bones are the stubborn ones.*

"The bones are always the stubborn ones," he said aloud. "It's why I like them."

That was the trouble with his gift, if you wanted to call it that. It wasn't dramatic. Nobody rose from graves or walked through walls. The dead simply stayed where they'd lived, faded as old paint on a door, and they talked, mostly about nothing — the price of wool, an unkind word someone had said forty years ago, whether the rain would hold off for haymaking. Edmund had learned to carve while carrying on three conversations he could not afford to be seen having. He'd gotten very good at nodding at nothing.

The child's mother was coming tomorrow for the finished whistle. Little Tam Brindle, drowned in the millrace in the spring flood, six years old, and the whole village still walking softly past the Brindle gate. A memorial whistle was an old custom in Thornfell: carved from the shinbone of a deer, tuned to a song the child had loved, so the mother could carry a piece of her boy's laughter in her pocket. Edmund had made eleven of them over the years. He was good at them. He hated making them.

*Ask her what song,* whispered Tam himself, from somewhere near the window, because children did not understand they were supposed to fade. Tam had not faded at all. He hung about the workshop like dust in the light, bright and impatient. *She'll know. She sings it wrong but she knows it.*

"I know the song," Edmund said, tapping the little pipe against his palm. "Your mother hummed it at the funeral loud enough to raise the roof."

*I want to hear it right.*

"You will." He set down the whistle and flexed his stiff fingers. "Now go bother somebody else. I can't tune a thing with you hovering."

Tam laughed and drifted out through the wall, and Edmund sat alone with the living silence, which was different from the other kind, and rubbed his eyes.

Below him, through the floorboards, he could hear the ordinary sounds of Thornfell at evening: the smith's hammer winding down, dogs arguing over something behind the baker's, Marta Kell calling her sons in for supper in a voice that carried across three rooftops and possibly into the next valley. A good village. A dull village, if you asked the young ones who left for the lowlands every spring. Edmund had spent thirty-eight years making sure it stayed exactly that dull, and he intended to spend thirty-eight more.

Which was why, when the whispers changed their tune, he went cold all the way down to the bench.

The dead didn't gossip about politics. That was the rule of them, one of the few reliable rules. They cared about their own small grievances and affections, and the doings of kings meant no more to them than weather on the far side of the world. But tonight the murmur along the edge of his hearing had shifted, the way birdsong shifts before a storm, and when he let himself listen properly — really listen, head tilted, breath held, the way he never dared do in company — he caught the same word coming from three different voices at once.

*Riders.*

He put down the whistle.

Old Hew, the shepherd dead two generations, whose grave lay up on the ridge where he'd watched for wolves all his life, was the clearest. Hew had never lost the habit of watching. *Riders on the north road,* he was saying, in that flat patient voice of his, *eight of them, maybe ten, gray cloaks, moving fast. Not smugglers. Smugglers don't ride in daylight and don't ride in ranks.* And then, with the peculiar satisfaction the dead took in being right: *They've got paper. Sealed paper. I could see the wax from here, red as blood on snow.*

Edmund stood very still.

Gray cloaks. Crown colors, under the gray. And sealed proclamations riding into mountain country, where the crown's writ ran thin and everyone preferred it that way.

"Tam," he said quietly. "Come back here a moment."

The boy came drifting through the wall, delighted to be summoned. *Did you finish it?*

"Not yet. Listen — did anyone come through the village today? Strangers? A crier?"

*A man on a horse stopped at the well at noon. He nailed a paper to the post and read it out loud, but everybody was talking so nobody heard him proper. Then he rode on toward Millbrook.* Tam frowned, or did the thing that passed for frowning, his face flickering like a candle in draft. *He had a hard face. Like Hew's dog.*

"Thank you," Edmund said. His mouth had gone dry. "Go on now."

*Will it sing tomorrow?*

"It will sing," he lied gently, and the boy went, satisfied, and Edmund stood in his workshop above the sleeping village and felt the first true weight of dread settle onto his shoulders like a yoke.

* * *

He should have gone straight down to look at the proclamation. Any sensible man would have. Instead Edmund did what he had done his whole life whenever the truth came too close: he worked. He trimmed the whistle's bore with a fine reamer, tested it against his lips, adjusted, tested again, until at last — nearly midnight, candle guttering — it gave a thin clear note that hung in the rafters like a held breath.

From the dark beyond the window, faint as frost forming, the dead began to hum along.

He let them. It was the only audience he'd ever allowed himself.

It was while he was polishing the bone with beeswax and oil that he finally admitted what his hands had known since sundown. He knew what the riders carried. He'd known since the word *traitors* started threading through the whispers like a red wire through gray cloth, because the dead along the north road had heard the riders talking as they passed the waymarkers, and the dead repeated everything, and some things once heard could not be unheard.

*A hunt for the gifted.* That was the phrase. The crown had declared a hunt for the gifted, and gifted souls were named traitors to realm and crown, and any soul harboring such a one would share their forfeit.

Edmund set down the cloth very carefully, the way you set down a thing when your hands want to shake and you won't let them.

Gifted. What a soft, official word for it. He thought about how Inquisitors were described in the almanacs and broadsheets that reached even Thornfell: men in gray with kind voices and ledgers full of names. He thought about the peddler's nephew from two valleys over, the one who dreamed true dreams, who had been taken last autumn for questioning and never written home. Everyone had said he'd found work in the lowlands. Everyone had wanted to say it.

And here was Edmund Galloway, bone-carver, respected, useful, harmless — a man who had spent his entire life folding his true self smaller and smaller, like a letter he could never send, until it fit into the space between his ribs where nobody looked. He'd hidden it from his mother, God rest her, though she'd sometimes watched him too closely. He'd hidden it from the smugglers he guided through the high passes, letting them believe his uncanny sense for safe trails was just long experience and good luck. He'd hidden it from the priest, from the neighbors, from every friend he'd ever raised a glass with. Thirty-eight years of nodding at nothing. Thirty-eight years of listening to the dead and pretending the world was quiet.

All of it, he understood now, had only ever been a loan. The debt had come due.

*Run,* whispered old Hew, from his ridge, practical to the last. *North road's watched. Take the shepherd's track past the black tarn. You can be over the pass before they're through Millbrook.*

"And go where?" Edmund asked the empty room. "They'll have papers in every market town from here to the sea. There's nowhere the gray cloaks don't go."

*Then don't be here when they arrive.*

He almost laughed. Fleeing. Him. A man whose whole life was this room, this bench, these tools worn to the shape of his hands; a man whose entire existence was built on staying precisely, invisibly, unremarkably still. Where would he even begin?

But Hew was right, and the dead usually were about simple things. Riders in ranks, in daylight, with sealed paper, did not come to the mountains for the scenery.

Down in the village, a dog barked once and fell silent. The candle burned low. On the bench, the memorial whistle gleamed, finished at last, holding its one perfect note inside it like a secret.

Edmund looked at it for a long time.

Tomorrow morning Mrs. Brindle would come for it, and he would give it to her, and she would weep and thank him and pay him in eggs he didn't need, and it would be the last ordinary thing he did in Thornfell. After that — pack light, take the shepherd's track, get over the pass before the gray cloaks crested the ridge. After that, a life with no shape yet, which frightened him more than the riders did.

He banked the fire, climbed the narrow stair to his loft, and lay awake listening to the dead murmur through the floorboards — the whole whispering congregation of Thornfell's departed, unaware that their one living confidant might soon be gone from among them.

Near dawn, half-dreaming, he heard something new. Not from the graveyard side of the village, but from far away, from the high passes, carried down through stone and snow on whatever strange current let the dead speak to him across distance: a woman's voice, sharp and exhausted and unmistakably alive-adjacent, saying to someone unseen —

*The Oathstone is broken. Do you understand me? It is broken, and everything that follows is my fault.*

Edmund opened his eyes in the dark.

Somewhere above the village, in the souring silence of the mountains, someone else was running too.
