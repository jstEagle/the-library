## 2. The Oath-Keeper's Fall

The garrison at Coldmarch smelled of wet wool and old regret, and Ulyana Vess had learned to tell the two apart.

Wet wool came from the drying racks by the hearth. Regret came from her own quarters, which were less a room than a stone apology — a cot, a stool, a window that looked out on the midden heap because the quartermaster had a sense of humor the crown had never punished him for. She had been at Coldmarch for eleven months. She had counted. Counting was what disgraced people did instead of sleeping.

She was mending a boot she did not like when the courier arrived.

He came up the pass road at a hard gallop, which was itself remarkable; nothing came to Coldmarch at a gallop except weather and occasionally Sergeant Brek's supper. The horse threw foam. The courier threw himself from the saddle and stood in the yard shouting for the commander, and Ulyana, who had once held an office where people ran to *her* with bad news, felt the old reflex rise in her chest like water in a well.

*Someone has died,* she thought. *Or something worse.*

At Coldmarch, those were usually the same thing.

She went down to the yard anyway, boot forgotten, needle still clenched in her teeth like a pirate's knife, and that was how Commander Hesk found her: former Oath-Keeper of the Realm, breaker of the oldest covenant in living memory, standing in the mud with a boot in one hand and a needle in her mouth.

"Vess," he said. He was the only man in the garrison who still used her name rather than her rank or, worse, the absence of it. "You'll want to hear this."

"I rarely want anything," she said around the needle. "It keeps expectations low."

He didn't smile. That was the first wrong thing. The second was the paper in his hand, bearing the royal seal — not the working seal of the Chancery but the great wax sun of the crown itself, pressed deep enough to crack.

Hesk read it aloud because half the garrison couldn't read and the other half pretended not to, so that news would come to them twice and they could charge for it once.

*"By order of His Majesty: let it be known throughout the realm that there walk among us souls gifted with unlawful communion — speakers to the dead, hearers of whispers, carvers of bone-voices. Such gifts are declared treason against the natural order. Let such persons be sought out, taken, and brought before the Inquisition for judgment. So orders the Lord Inquisitor Vael, whose word is the crown's word."*

The yard went quiet in the particular way yards go quiet when everyone is doing arithmetic about their neighbors.

Ulyana took the needle out of her mouth very slowly.

"They're hunting *listeners*," she said.

"They're hunting someone," Hesk said. "Crown's got dead mountains, failing harvests, rivers running gray as dishwater. People want a reason. Reasons are cheaper than remedies." He folded the proclamation with the care of a man handling something that might bite. "Inquisitor Vael passed through Thornfell three days ago. Burning shrines, they say."

Thornfell. The word landed in her stomach like a dropped stone.

"You know it," Hesk said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Everyone knows Thornfell," she said, which was true, and also a lie so large it could have had its own postal address. Thornfell was where the covenant-road began. Thornfell was where the spirits had lived thickest, before the silence. Thornfell was where she had stood, one year and one month ago, with the Oathstone in her hands, and said *no.*

* * *

That night she did not mend the boot. She sat on the cot with her back against the cold wall and let the memory come, because she had learned that refusing it only made it arrive later, armed.

The Covenant Hall had still been beautiful then. That was the thing no one understood afterward — that ruin is easy to look at, but the moment before ruin, when everything is whole and lit and waiting, that is the sight that follows you. The Hall had been lit with spirit-lamps that burned without flame, blue-white and patient, and the Oathstone had sat in its cradle of carved ribs, humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound you can hear is done.

Every seven years, the oath-keeper renewed the covenant. Every seven years, the human realm promised remembrance and the spirit realm promised passage, and the dead crossed the borderlands freely, and the mountains stayed green, and nobody thought about any of it, which was the covenant's greatest triumph: it worked so well it became invisible.

Ulyana had been raised to be invisible in exactly that way. Third daughter of a cadet branch, sent to the Oath-Keeper's college at nine years old because her grandmother said she had "a face that could keep a secret and shoulders that could keep a schedule." She had studied the old words until they lived in her mouth like her own heartbeat. She had knelt before the stone at nineteen, twenty-six, thirty-three. Three renewals. Three times she had spoken the words and felt the realms settle against each other like two hands lacing fingers.

And then, at forty, kneeling for the fourth time, she had looked at the words — really looked, for the first time, the way you look at your own handwriting on a letter you don't remember writing — and she had seen the shape of them.

*The dead shall cross, and the living shall remember.*

But the words didn't say *remember gladly.* They didn't say *remember and grieve and heal.* They said *shall.* The living realm *had* to remember. The dead *had* to linger close enough to be remembered. Nobody rested. Nobody finished. A widow's husband stayed near her hearth for sixty years, watching her grow old beside his ghost of a ghost. A drowned child's voice called from every riverbank, forever, because the covenant required the living to listen and the dead to speak.

Ulyana had spent her whole life believing she was the keeper of a bridge. Kneeling there, she saw it was a chain.

So she had stood up. In front of the assembled elders, the spirit-lamps guttering, the Oathstone humming its patient question, she had said, "No. Not this time. Not again."

She had expected argument. She had gotten silence — the real kind, the human kind, the appalled kind — and then the lamps going out one by one like eyes closing, and the stone going cold under her hands, and somewhere far off in the mountains a sound she would never be able to describe to anyone, a sound like a door closing in a house the size of the sky.

She had believed, that night, that she was setting both worlds free.

She had not known that freedom, for the dead, would feel exactly like being buried alive.

* * *

"You're doing the face," said Sergeant Brek, appearing at her doorway with two cups of something that claimed to be tea.

"What face?"

"The one where you're prosecuting yourself. Verdict's always guilty. Court's always in session." He handed her a cup and sat on the stool without asking, which was either insubordination or friendship, and with Brek it had long since stopped mattering which. "Drink. It's terrible. Consistency builds character."

She drank. It was terrible. She felt marginally more characterized.

"Brek. The proclamation. What do the men make of it?"

He shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Marta's cousin in Lowbridge swears the baker's boy talks to his dead mother. Half the garrison wants to report him. Other half wants him to ask the mother where she hid the good brandy." He sipped. "They think it's a joke, mostly. Crown blaming bone-carvers and hedge-whisperers for the mountains souring. Like blaming the swallow for the winter."

"And what do you think?"

Brek looked at her over the rim of his cup with the flat, unhurried gaze of a man who had survived four commanders and one avalanche through the simple method of noticing things early.

"I think," he said, "that the silence started thirteen months ago. I think it started in the high country, near Thornfell, and spread outward like ink in water. And I think I remember a certain royal procession passing through here fourteen months ago, headed for the Covenant Hall, and a certain officer of mine who used to get letters from the capital who hasn't gotten one since." He set down the cup. "I don't think much, Vess. But when I do, it's in straight lines."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, a sentry coughed.

"If you've deduced all that," Ulyana said quietly, "why are you bringing me tea instead of irons?"

"Because the crown's answer is to burn shrines and arrest children who talk to their grandmothers, and I've decided I'd rather be wrong with you than right with them." He stood, joints cracking like green wood. "Also you make better tea than the mess does, and if you're going to run, I'd like the good kettle back before you go."

She stared at him. "I'm not—"

"You will be," Brek said. "Tonight, probably. Word came with the courier — Vael's riders are sweeping the passes, and they've a list. Your name's on it. Not for the covenant." He paused at the door. "For *harboring knowledge of it.* They want the Oathstone, Vess. And they think you know where the pieces are."

He left. The door swung shut behind him with the soft, final click of a decision being made on her behalf.

Ulyana sat in the dark for a long moment, holding the terrible tea, feeling guilt do what guilt does best: transmute, in the presence of other people's suffering, into something harder and more useful.

Because here was the arithmetic she could not escape. She had broken the covenant to free the dead, and the dead had gone silent — not free, *silent*, extinguished like lamps — and now the crown was burning shrines and hunting innocents to answer a wound *she* had made. Edmund Galloway flashed through her mind unbidden — the smuggler's guide with the careful hands whom she'd met twice on the Thornfell roads, years ago, who had flinched once, just once, at a raven's call, in a way that meant *hearing*, not *fearing*. If the crown's list reached Thornfell, men like him would burn first.

Her failure, wearing other people's faces.

"No," she said aloud, to the empty room, to the memory, to herself. "You don't get to fix this by hiding in a garrison drinking Brek's tea."

There was a locked chest under her cot. There had been since the night she fled the Hall — the night she had gathered what she could carry of the shattered Oathstone while the elders shouted and the lamps died, because even in breaking her oath she could not bear to leave the pieces for anyone else to misuse. Thirteen months she had told herself she was guarding them. Thirteen months of cowardice dressed as duty.

She pulled the chest out. Inside, wrapped in oilcloth, the shards of the Oathstone caught the last light of the fire — dull now, gray as riverbed stones, but when her fingers touched them she felt it, faint as a pulse under snow: *humming.*

Still humming. Still waiting.

"Well," she told the shards, with the gentle humor she reserved for hopeless situations, "it seems we're all going home."

She packed in ten minutes. She took the good kettle, because Brek had earned it, and left a note that read, in full: *The tea was terrible. Thank you.* At the stable she saddled the ugly, sure-footed garrison mare nobody would miss, and rode north into the pass under a sky where the thirteenth moon was already fattening toward full.

Behind her, Coldmarch slept. Ahead, the mountains lay silent as held breath.

And somewhere on those high dark roads, she would find out too late that she was not the only one running.
