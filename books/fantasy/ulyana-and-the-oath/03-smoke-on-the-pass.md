## 3. Smoke on the Pass

The dead woke Edmund before dawn, which was nothing new. What was new was the tone of their voices.

For six years they had murmured to him like neighbors over a fence — complaints about the damp, gossip about who had died badly, the occasional unsolicited opinion on his carving technique. That morning they were sharp. Urgent. A chorus of cold hands on the back of his neck.

*They're coming. They're coming up the river road. The one with the grey eyes — he counts us, boy, he counts us like coins.*

Edmund sat up in his loft above the workshop, heart hammering, and looked out the window toward the village shrine at the bottom of the hill. Smoke was already rising from Tam Wicker's chimney next door, ordinary breakfast smoke, and for one foolish moment he let himself believe it was an ordinary day.

Then he saw the riders.

There were nine of them, moving in that particular formation the crown's inquisitors favored — not a patrol's loose amble but a wedge, deliberate as a blade being slid between ribs. At its point rode a man in ash-grey robes whose horse moved as though it were ashamed to be there.

"Vael," Edmund breathed, and every dead soul in Thornfell said the name with him, a whisper running through the graveyard and the old well and the drowned hollow below the mill where three generations of Galloways had quietly failed to learn to swim.

He dressed fast. He'd packed for this day for years, actually — a go-bag under the floorboards with dried meat, flint, a spare knife, and, because even doom has standards, a small tin of honey candies his mother had sworn by for coughs and crises alike. He took the bag, took his carving tools, and stood a moment over the half-finished memorial whistle for little Maren Cobb, who had gone into the river in spring and hadn't come out.

*Finish it,* she whispered, from wherever drowned children wait. *Please. I want to give my mother something before you run.*

"I can't," Edmund said aloud, hating how it cracked. "If I stay—"

*You never finish anything,* Maren said, with the particular cruelty of the very young dead. *Papa says so.*

"Papa is a drunk and a liar," Edmund muttered, and sat down anyway, and worked.

It took him eleven minutes. His hands shook, but bone forgave shaking; it always had. When he blew across the mouthpiece it made a sound like water finding its way home, and Maren's whisper went quiet in a way that felt, impossibly, like a smile. He wrapped the whistle in oilcloth, left it in the churchwarden's alcove with instructions scratched into the wrapping — *for Cobb's wife, from a friend* — and slipped out the back as the first torch touched the shrine roof.

From the ridge above the village, Edmund watched Inquisitor Vael stand in the square with his hands folded while Thornfell's shrine burned. Vael did not shout. He did not threaten. He simply asked questions of the smugglers Edmund had guided through the passes for six years — Bren of the crooked teeth, old Sela who could drink anyone under any table, the Hollis brothers who argued about everything except sheep.

And Edmund, who heard the dead but had never once been able to hear the living at a distance, could only watch the shape of the interrogation: men kneeling, heads bowed, Vael pacing slowly around them like a man inspecting fruit.

Later — much later, when it no longer mattered — Sela would tell the story in a tavern two valleys over: how the grey-eyed inquisitor had asked each of them, gently, almost kindly, *who carves your markers? Who knows the paths better than a priest? Who talks to himself on the high road?* And how Bren, brave stupid loyal Bren, had laughed and said, "Talks to himself? Half this village does," and how Vael had smiled and thanked him and ordered him taken anyway, as an example.

But Edmund didn't know that yet. All he knew, watching from the ridge with his lungs full of shrine-smoke, was that three of the kneeling figures were put in wagons, and the rest were released, and none of them looked up at the ridge where he stood, and somehow that was worse.

"They'll talk or they won't," said the voice of old Marta Cobb, dead these four years, standing — in the way the dead stand, more memory than shape — beside him at the ridgeline. "Bren won't. Sela might. The Hollis boys will argue about whether to talk until talking doesn't matter."

"I know."

"You always know. It's your whole tragedy." She patted his shoulder, or tried to; her hand went through like wind through a doorway. "Run, bone-carver. Run north. There's someone on the pass you need to meet."

"There's never anyone on the pass I need to meet. There's goats on the pass. There's weather."

"There's a woman carrying a broken stone," Marta said, "and she's about to walk off the edge of Cutter's Ledge because she doesn't know the path turns east. Go be useful. It's the only prayer you're any good at."

* * *

The high passes of Thornfell in autumn were God's own argument against optimism: scree that shifted like bad temper, fog that rolled in without invitation and refused to leave, and a wind that seemed personally offended by hats. Edmund had walked them since he was twelve, first with his father, then alone, then guiding other people's contraband through them for coin. He knew every goat track, every cave, every place the mountain liked to kill the careless.

Which is why, near midday, when he heard footsteps ahead taking the wrong fork at Cutter's Ledge, he broke into a run without thinking.

"Don't—" he gasped, cresting the rise. "Don't go left! Left goes off a cliff!"

The woman ahead of him spun, and what he saw first was not her face but what she carried: a bundle wrapped in grey cloth, clutched to her chest the way a soldier carries a wounded comrade, and from the corner of the wrapping jutted a shard of pale stone that hummed — actually hummed, a note like a struck bell dying — in a way that made every dead thing within ten miles fall suddenly, reverently silent.

Edmund had spent his whole life inside a murmur. The silence hit him like diving into winter water.

"Oh," he said stupidly. "Oh, that's loud."

The woman had a sword out now, which seemed excessive. She was tall, mud-spattered, dark-haired, with the kind of posture that announced military training even through a ruined travelling cloak, and her eyes were doing arithmetic on him — weight, threat, distance to the cliff edge.

"Who are you?" she demanded.

"Edmund Galloway. Bone-carver. Occasionally guide." He raised both hands, palms out. "You were about to walk off Cutter's Ledge. I thought I'd mention it before you got to the interesting part."

Her eyes flicked left, to the mist-shrouded drop, then back. "I wasn't going to—"

"The path turns east at the split cairn. Everyone takes left. The mountain finds it funny." He nodded at the bundle. "That stone's making my head ring. What is it?"

Something closed behind her eyes — a door shutting, quick and practiced. "Nothing. A keepsake."

"It's humming."

"It's a large keepsake."

"It's a keepsake the size of a bread oven."

"And you're a man who shouts at strange women on mountainsides," she said coolly, "which makes us even." But she turned east at the split cairn, which told Edmund she wasn't entirely a fool, and after a moment's visible internal debate, she let him fall into step beside her rather than putting him off the mountain. Progress, of a kind.

They walked in silence for a mile. Below them, thin and far away, a thread of smoke still rose from Thornfell, and Edmund caught her looking at it twice.

"Shrine fire?" she asked finally.

"This morning. Inquisitor came through." He kept his voice light, the way you do when the alternative is screaming. "Burned the shrine. Took three of my friends in a wagon. Very polite about it, from what I could see, which I'm told is his signature move."

"Inquisitor," she repeated. Something in her jaw tightened. "Vael?"

"You know him?"

"Everyone in the capital knows him." She said it the way you'd say *everyone knows the plague*. Then, abruptly: "Why did they take your friends? What are you to Thornfell?"

"Guide. Smuggler's pathfinder. Nothing interesting."

"You're lying."

"Everyone's lying," Edmund said. "It's the national pastime. You're lying too — about the keepsake, about why a woman with a soldier's shoulders and a court accent is hiking the Thornfell passes alone with no escort. So we can either keep lying at each other, which I'll admit I'm very good at, or we can agree to stop asking questions until we're somewhere warm."

She stopped walking. For a long moment she just looked at him, and Edmund felt the strangest sensation — like being read by someone fluent in a language he'd only ever spoken badly. Because he knew that look. He'd seen it in his own mirror for thirty years: the careful face, the pleasant nothing, the mask worn so long you forgot it was a mask until someone looked at you like *that*, like they could see the hinges.

"You hear them," she said quietly. Not a question. "The dead. That's why the stone bothers you. That's why you knew the path. That's why you flinched when I unwrapped it at the ledge — you heard it go quiet."

Edmund's mouth opened. Closed. Thirty years of practiced denials lined up on his tongue and found nowhere to go.

"That's a serious accusation," he managed.

"Yes," she agreed. "So is mine." She began unwrapping the bundle, and the silence deepened around them, vast and ringing, and Edmund realized his hands were shaking. Inside the grey cloth lay shards of white stone — dozens of them, jagged, beautiful, wrong — fitted together loosely like the pieces of a shattered egg. Even broken, the thing radiated presence the way a corpse radiates cold.

"The Oathstone," she said. "Or what's left of it. I was its keeper. I am its keeper. Ulyana Vaschen, formerly of the Royal Order of Oath-Keepers, currently of nowhere in particular." Her chin lifted slightly, defiant, daring him to laugh. "I broke the covenant between the realms. Deliberately. Everything the crown is hunting your friends for — the souring, the silence spreading through the mountains — started with me. And now Vael is burning villages looking for gifted souls to blame for a crime that was mine."

The dead, all around them, held their breath. Edmund had never known them capable of it.

"Well," he said at last, because someone had to say something, and gentle humor was the only tool he'd ever trusted. "When you put it that way, my smuggling habit seems almost quaint."

"This isn't a joke."

"No. It really isn't." He looked at the shards, and at her — this disgraced, exhausted, magnificent liar — and felt the masks between them crumble like wet paper. Two people standing on a freezing mountain with nothing left hidden, which was either the beginning of trust or the end of everything. "I hear the dead, Ulyana Vaschen. Have done since I was a child. And I have to tell you — they've been saying your name for days."

Her face went white. "What do they say?"

Edmund hesitated. Behind him the wind moaned through the pass, and beneath the wind, faint and rising, the voices of the dead began again — hundreds of them, thousands, all speaking at once, all saying the same thing, a tide of whispers rolling down from the high snowfields where no living person stood.

He didn't translate. He didn't have to. Ulyana heard it too — heard it with whatever was left of her broken oath — and her hand crept to the shards of the Oathstone as the whisper resolved into words:

*Thirteenth moon. Thirteenth moon. Bring her home.*
