## 4. What the Dead Remember

Edmund's POV

The dead did not like the high passes. Edmund had known that for years — the wind-scoured heights were thin ground, and voices came through them thin as well, like a song played on a cracked flute. But tonight they came through clearly, urgently, and in numbers.

*Left,* they said. *Not the ridge. Left, down, where the birches lean wrong.*

He stopped so abruptly that Ulyana walked into his back with a grunt.

"If you're going to stop," she said, "perhaps a warning. Some of us are carrying a bag of rocks."

"A bag of very important rocks," Edmund said. The Oathstone fragments clinked in their wrappings on her shoulder, a sound he had come to think of as her personal percussion section.

"The ridge is faster," she said.

"The ridge is a lie."

She raised an eyebrow at him. It was a good eyebrow. He'd been noticing things like that for two days now, and he didn't entirely trust himself to keep noticing them.

"The ridge," he explained, "has a crevasse across it about a mile up, hidden under snow. You'd go through it like a spoon through custard."

"And you know this how, exactly?"

The dead, helpfully, said nothing. They were waiting. He could feel them waiting, the way one feels a room full of people watching a man decide whether to confess to a crime.

"Smuggler's knowledge," he said. "We keep maps in our heads."

"Your head," Ulyana said slowly, "knew about a crevasse that the king's own surveyors missed three years ago."

"Smugglers are thorough."

"Smugglers are *thorough*." She said it the way one might say *the moon is made of cheese*. "Edmund. I was an oath-keeper of the royal court for eleven years. I have been lied to by professionals. You are doing it badly."

He looked down the slope, at the birches leaning wrong, at the faint silver shimmer that no one but him could see — the old covenant-road, waking up beneath the snow like a sleeper turning over. The dead were murmuring along it now, a tide of them, and beneath the murmur he caught a word repeated over and over, soft and patient:

*Come. Come. Come.*

"Ulyana," he said. "Do you trust me?"

"No," she said promptly. "But I've been following you anyway, which I believe says something about my judgment."

"Then keep following me. And I'll show you something I've never shown anyone."

* * *

Ulyana's POV

She had decided, somewhere around the second day, that Edmund Galloway was either the most honest man she'd ever met or the most accomplished liar, and that the two categories overlapped more than anyone cared to admit. She had, after all, been both herself.

Now she followed him down through the birches, over a frozen stream that cracked like breaking crockery under their boots, until the trees opened onto something that made her stop breathing.

A road.

Not a smuggler's track. Not a game trail. A *road* — paved in pale stone, running arrow-straight through the mountains, wide enough for six abreast, and utterly impossible, because she had walked these passes as a girl, as a soldier, as a keeper of the crown's oldest oaths, and there had never been a road here. The mountains kept no roads. The mountains kept *grudges*.

"That's not possible," she said.

"Most of what's happened this week isn't," Edmund agreed. "I find it saves time to skip that part."

The stone under her boots was warm. That was the detail that undid her. Warm, in a pass where her breath froze in her beard — no, her *hair*, she didn't have a beard, she was tired enough to be hallucinating one — warm as a hearthstone, and faintly, faintly singing.

She knew that song. She had heard it once, eleven years ago, in the deep of the Covenant Hall, before she had refused to renew the old words and let the light go out.

"This is the covenant-road," she whispered. "This is — it's supposed to be gone. It's been gone since the realms went quiet."

"It was never gone," Edmund said. He was looking at the road the way other men looked at a beloved's face. "It was only ever *unlistened to*."

And that was when she saw it — saw *him*, really saw him, for the first time since the pass had thrown them together. He wasn't looking at the road the way a man looks at a discovery. He was looking at it the way a man looks at a *guide*. His head was tilted, slightly, the way it tilted when he was listening to something she couldn't hear. It had been tilting that way, she realized, for two days. At the campfire. At the shrine ruin. At *her*.

"Edmund," she said carefully. "What are you hearing?"

The wind moved through the birches. Somewhere below, a fox screamed and thought better of it.

"Everything," he said.

* * *

Edmund's POV

He had rehearsed this confession his whole life, in a hundred imaginary versions, and in none of them had he delivered it standing on a singing road at midnight to a woman with frost in her eyelashes and a bag of sacred rubble on her back. Life had a sense of humor. He suspected the dead had taught it.

"I hear the dead," he said. "I've heard them since I was six. My mother thought I had an imaginary friend until the imaginary friend told me where my father's lost hammer was, and then she thought I was cursed, and then she thought I was lying, and then she mostly just thought I should stop bringing it up at dinner."

Ulyana stared at him.

"The dead talk," he went on, because now that the dam had cracked, the whole flood was coming through, and it was almost a relief. "They whisper. They complain. Some of them are terribly boring — there's a farmer two valleys over who has spent forty years dead explaining crop rotation to anyone who'll listen. They told me about the crevasse — a mule fell in it in '09, and he's still cross about it. They told me the crown was coming. And right now—" he gestured at the shimmering road, at the tide of voices running along it like water along a channel, "—right now they are telling me where to walk, because this road only exists for those who can hear it, and apparently I am the only fool in Thornfell who can."

The silence that followed was long. The dead, unhelpfully, had gone quiet too, which he suspected was their version of leaning in.

"You're gifted," Ulyana said at last. "You're one of the people the crown is hunting."

"I'm one of the people the crown is hunting," he agreed. "Yes. I've been hiding in plain sight my entire adult life, carving bones for the village and pretending the whispers are just my conscience. Which, incidentally, I don't have. I have something much worse. I have *witnesses*."

Something happened to her face then — something complicated. Not horror. He'd braced for horror. It was closer to recognition, the look of a woman seeing her own mask on someone else's face.

"Two liars," she said quietly. "Walking the same road."

"Yours is bigger, though," he said. "I only lied to a village. You lied to a kingdom."

And she laughed — a short, startled, wounded laugh, the kind that comes out of a person before they can decide whether they'd rather cry.

* * *

Ulyana's POV

She sat down on the warm stone, because her legs had opinions and they were no longer consulting her, and she told him.

Not all of it. Not yet. But the shape of it.

"Everyone thinks the covenant broke," she said. "That it frayed, or failed, or was broken *by* something. It didn't. I broke it. Deliberately. I stood in the Hall with the Oathstone in my hands and the old words in my mouth, and I put them down, and I walked out, and I let the door close between the realms."

Edmund said nothing. He had the rare, infuriating gift of silence that didn't press.

"Do you know what the covenant *was*?" she went on. "It was a chain. A beautiful chain, gilded, ancient, sung about in every temple — and still a chain. The spirit realm bound to serve the human one. The dead bound to answer our questions, guard our borders, bleed for our wars. Every oath-keeper for a thousand years renewed it because that was the *duty*, and duty is a wonderful word for not asking questions." Her hands curled around the bag of stone fragments. "I asked questions. I asked what the dead wanted. And do you know what I found in the old records, in the parts the crown doesn't copy? That they had never once been asked. A thousand years of servitude, and no one had ever asked them if they *wished* it."

"So you freed them," Edmund said.

"So I freed them." She laughed again, and it came out wrong. "That's what I told myself. That both realms would be better off unbound — theirs to live as they chose, ours to stop leaning on the dead like a crutch. And instead the mountains soured, the crops failed, the spirits went dark or went mad, and the crown decided it was the *gifted* who'd done it, and started burning shrines and hunting people like you." She looked up at him. "I wanted to unmake a cage. I think I may have unmade a wall instead — the kind that keeps something *out*."

The wind came down the pass. The road sang beneath them, patient, ancient, and somewhere in its singing Edmund tilted his head again, listening, and she watched his face change.

"What," she said. "What are they saying?"

He was quiet a moment longer. When he spoke, his voice was gentle, which was somehow worse.

"They're saying you were wrong," he said. "But they're also saying — and I want you to hear this exactly as they said it — that you were the only one in a thousand years who *asked*. And that the asking mattered. Even now. Even like this."

Her eyes stung. She blamed the wind. She blamed the altitude. She blamed, in a small internal aside, the entire spirit realm, which had a great deal to answer for.

"Where is the road taking us?" she asked, to have something to ask.

"Where it's always led," Edmund said, and his head tilted again, and this time what crossed his face was not wonder but a cold, slow dread. "To the Covenant Hall. They want us to go to the Hall, Ulyana. They're saying the thirteenth moon rises in nine days, and if the covenant isn't reforged by then—"

He stopped. Listened. Went pale in the moonlight.

"—then it won't be the realms that go silent," he said. "It'll be everything."
