## 5. The Thirteenth Moon Rises

The Covenant Hall did not look like the seat of an ancient pact between realms. It looked like a corpse that had been left out in the weather and politely ignored by everyone who passed.

Ulyana stood at the edge of the terrace where the road ended, her breath fogging in the thin air, and let herself feel the full weight of what she had done. The Hall had been magnificent once — she knew this from the histories she'd memorized as a girl, from the engravings in her grandmother's prayer-book, from the one time she'd seen it as a child, riding behind her father's guard with her chin up because oath-keepers' daughters kept their chins up. White stone veined with silver. A roof of blue slate that caught the moonlight like a held breath. Doors tall enough for spirits to walk through standing straight.

Now the roof was open to the sky, the slate fallen in a great fan across the terrace. The silver veins in the walls had gone dull as old pewter. And the doors — the doors still stood, impossibly, though they were blackened and split down the middle, hanging crooked in their frame like a jaw broken and badly set.

"Charming," Edmund said beside her. "Very rustic."

"You don't have to mock it."

"I absolutely do." He shifted his pack on his shoulders. "If I don't mock it, I'll have to think about the fact that I've walked nine days through mountains that are dying, following voices only I can hear, toward a building that looks like it lost an argument with a god. Mocking is how I stay upright."

Despite everything — despite the guilt sitting in her chest like a swallowed stone, despite the riders she knew were somewhere behind them — Ulyana almost smiled. "You're a strange man, bone-carver."

"I'm told the dead find me hilarious."

* * *

Inside, the Hall was worse and better than its exterior suggested. Worse, because the great chamber where the covenant had been sworn was choked with rubble and bird droppings and ten years of blown leaves. Better, because at the center of it all, the Oathstone's pedestal still stood — a pillar of black granite rising to waist height, its surface carved with spirals that Ulyana's fingers remembered before her mind did.

She set the shattered Oathstone on the pedestal. She'd carried the pieces since the garrison: seven fragments wrapped in wool and oilcloth, each one heavy past its size, each one humming faintly when she slept too near it. Bad dreams lived in that hum. She'd stopped sleeping near it three days ago.

Edmund crouched beside the pedestal, head tilted, listening to whatever he listened to. His lips moved once, silently. Then he said, "They're excited. The dead here. It's like... you know when you walk into a room and everyone stops talking?"

"I'm an exiled oath-keeper, Edmund. Everyone stops talking when I walk into rooms. It's my one remaining skill."

"No, it's—" He waved a hand, frustrated, searching. "It's not fear. It's hope. They think we're going to fix it." He looked up at her, and there was something raw under the dry humor now. "Ulyana, I don't know how to fix it. Do you know how to fix it? Because I've been assuming you did, and I would very much like to stop assuming things."

She knelt beside him and put her hands flat on the pedestal, the way she'd been taught, the way she'd refused to do on the night everything broke. The stone was cold. Colder than mountain air could explain.

And something answered.

It wasn't a voice, exactly. It was older than voice — a pressure against her mind, a shape made of law and memory, and it poured into her the way water pours into a dry channel, finding every crack she'd spent ten years pretending wasn't there.

*The covenant is written in blood freely given,* it said, or sang, or simply *was*, inside her skull. *An oath spoken is wind. An oath given is iron. Only royal blood may hold the pen.*

When it released her, she was on her hands and knees on the flagstones, gasping, and Edmund was gripping her shoulder hard enough to bruise.

"What did it say?" he demanded. "What did it—"

"It wants royal blood." She laughed, and hated the sound of it. "Freely given. That's the ritual, Edmund. That's always been the ritual. An oath-keeper can carry the covenant, tend it, renew it year to year — but the founding of it, the reforging of it, needs someone of the blood royal, giving themselves willingly. Not coerced. Not commanded. *Given.*"

Edmund was quiet for a moment. "And you're..."

"Half-royal. Bastard line, third generation removed, but the blood remembers. The stone remembers." She sat back on her heels. "I checked, after I broke it. Believe me, I checked. There are perhaps four people alive whose blood would serve, and two of them are children in the capital, and one is the king himself, who would rather burn the mountains than kneel at a spirit-stone. Which leaves me."

"So we do it," Edmund said. Simply. As if it were simple. "Nine days. You give your oath, the covenant holds, the mountains stop souring, the dead stop whispering warnings in my ear at three in the morning, and we all go home."

"And Vael's riders catch us first," Ulyana said. "Or the crown declares my oath invalid because I'm a traitor twice over. Or the spirits reject me because I'm the one who broke it in the first place." She gestured at the ruined Hall, the dead doors, the dull silver. "I didn't come here to be forgiven, Edmund. I came because running anywhere else felt worse."

"That," Edmund said, "is the most honest thing anyone's ever said to me, and I once heard a dead smuggler confess which crewmate he'd cheated at dice."

* * *

They made camp in the Hall's eastern cloister, where a fragment of roof still held and the walls blocked the worst of the wind. Edmund built a small fire — small enough to hide, large enough to matter — and Ulyana sat wrapped in her cloak watching the flames and trying not to calculate how long they had.

She'd gotten good at calculating, over nine days. Vael's riders moved fast on the high roads. The smugglers Edmund had left behind would talk eventually; everyone talked eventually; she had watched enough interrogations at the border garrisons to know that the only variable was how much pain preceded the talking. Three days, maybe four, before Vael knew exactly where the road led. Two more to ride it.

Five days of margin. Six if the weather turned.

"You're doing the thing again," Edmund said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where your face goes very still and your eyes keep moving, like you're counting exits." He fed a stick to the fire. "My mother did that. Usually right before she told my father the harvest money was gone."

"My face does not—"

"It really does. It's quite impressive, actually. You could teach a masterclass."

Ulyana threw a pinecone at him. He caught it without looking, which was insufferable, and set it on the fire, which was somehow worse.

But the warmth of it — the small, stupid domesticity of it — lasted only until the shadows deepened. Because that was when the cold came.

Not mountain cold. This came from inside the ruin, from the direction of the broken doors, and it arrived the way a name arrives on the tongue of someone you hoped had forgotten you. The fire shrank. The flames went pale and still, like paintings of fire rather than fire itself.

And something stepped out of the dark between the pillars.

It wore a shape, but the shape was a courtesy, or a mockery — a tall figure robed in grey that moved like smoke deciding to be cloth. Where a face should have been there was a suggestion of features, the way clouds suggest faces, and its voice, when it spoke, was beautiful in the way that avalanches are beautiful.

*"Oath-keeper."*

Ulyana was on her feet with her knife out before she'd decided to move, which was absurd — she knew it was absurd — but the body insists on gestures. "Ashvael."

*"You know me."* The spirit lord drifted closer, and the fire bent away from it. *"Good. Then you know I am not the crown's creature, nor the dead's. I am the space between. And I have watched you crawl through my mountains for nine days, little broken covenant, carrying your stones and your secrets."* The cloud-face turned. *"And you. Bone-carver. Listener. You hear them, don't you? Every day. Every hour. The whispers, the pleas, the grief that never ends. How do you sleep?"*

"Don't answer it," Ulyana said.

*"I could make it stop,"* Ashvael said softly, and the softness was the cruelest thing about it. *"All of it. The voices, the hunt, the Inquisitor with his pretty fires. One bargain, Edmund Galloway. Bring me the oath-keeper when the thirteenth moon rises. Let me hold what she carries — her blood, her oath, her surrender — and I will end the hunt. No more running. No more burning shrines. No more dead men chattering in your skull until you go mad as a spring hare. Power enough to walk into Thornfell village in daylight and carve bones for whoever asks."*

The silence afterward was enormous.

Ulyana did not look at Edmund. She found she couldn't. She looked at the fire instead, at its flattened, painted flames, and thought: *this is the moment. This is the moment I find out what nine days were worth.*

Because she knew what those voices cost him. She'd watched him flinch at nothing, tilt his head at empty air, go white at the warnings only he could hear. She'd seen him tired in a way sleep doesn't fix. And Ashvael was offering him the one thing no one else in the world could give.

"Edmund," she said quietly. "Look at me."

He didn't. He was looking at Ashvael, and his face was doing something complicated — grief and longing and a terrible, thoughtful calm.

"How do I know you'll keep the bargain?" he asked.

*"You don't,"* Ashvael said, with what might have been honesty. *"That is what makes it a bargain."*

"And if I say no?"

*"Then the Inquisitor takes you both, the crown forges its puppet covenant, and the mountains die slowly instead of quickly. I am patient, bone-carver. Ruin is patient. But I am offering you the fast road, and the kind one — for you."* The cloud-face tilted. *"Think on it. Nine nights remain. I will ask again."*

The cold withdrew. The fire leapt back up, ordinary and orange and real, and the shadows between the pillars were only shadows.

For a long moment neither of them spoke.

Then Edmund picked up the pinecone from the edge of the fire, turned it over in his fingers, and said, in a voice carefully stripped of everything:

"We should sleep. Riders will come early if the weather holds."

Ulyana lay down with her back to him, cloak pulled tight, eyes open in the dark. She listened to his breathing slow. She listened to the wind move through the broken doors.

And she told herself the tightness in her chest was just the altitude.

But she lay awake a long time anyway, counting the sounds of the night, waiting to hear whether the man behind her would tilt his head toward voices only he could hear — and what, in the dark, they might be telling him to do.
