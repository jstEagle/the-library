## 6. Ashes of the Hunt

They took his hands first.

That was the part Edmund hadn't expected. Not the manacles — he'd expected those, had even rehearsed a dignified speech for them on the long walk down from the pass — but the care of it. Two soldiers in crown grey wrapped his wrists in wool before the iron went on, gentle as midwives, and one of them murmured an apology that smelled of onions and genuine regret.

"Sorry about the wool," the soldier said. "It's all we've got."

"Don't apologize," Edmund said. "Apologize for the iron."

The soldier looked at him as though this were a very strange thing to say, which it was, and marched him down into the valley anyway.

They brought him to the burned shrine at Thornfell's edge, where Inquisitor Vael had made her camp among the charred timbers. She sat on a fallen roof-beam like a woman waiting out rain, her white cloak immaculate against the ash, and she did not look up when they pushed Edmund to his knees in front of her. She was reading a ledger. That was somehow worse than if she'd been sharpening something.

"Edmund Galloway," she said at last, without looking up. "Bone-carver. Guide. Widower." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "And, according to three separate smugglers who sold your name for a pardon within the hour, a man who *hears things*."

"Smugglers," Edmund said. "You can't trust them. It's practically their defining feature."

Now she looked up. Her eyes were pale, patient, and entirely without cruelty, which frightened him more than cruelty would have. Cruelty you could work with. "Master Galloway. I have burned four shrines this month. I have questioned forty-one gifted souls. Do you know what they all have in common?"

"A fondness for talking to people who aren't there?"

"They lie badly," Vael said, closing the ledger. "You will too, I expect. But I'm not here for you, so let's save ourselves the theater."

*

Here is what Edmund learned in the next hour, kneeling in the ashes while Vael circled him with her questions soft as snowfall:

She did not want his confession. She wanted Ulyana.

"The oath-keeper fled the garrison six days ago carrying a fragment of the Oathstone," Vael said. "You were seen together on the high pass. Two fugitives, one road. Coincidence is a luxury I stopped affording myself years ago."

"We collided," Edmund said. "Literally. She has an elbow like a fence post. If we're allies, it's against my will and my better judgment."

"And yet you didn't report her."

"To whom? The crown that's burning shrines?" He shrugged as far as the manacles allowed. "I'm a coward, Inquisitor, not an idiot. There's a difference, though I grant it's subtle."

Something flickered at the corner of Vael's mouth. Not quite a smile. An acknowledgment, maybe, that he was going to be more trouble than the ledger had promised.

Then she crouched in front of him, ash drifting between them, and said the thing that turned his blood to well water.

"You misunderstand the hunt, Master Galloway. We are not destroying the gifted. We are *collecting* them." She tilted her head. "The mountains are souring. The dead have gone silent. The old covenant is broken, and the crown knows it — knows it better than your disgraced oath-keeper does, because the crown has been reading the archives she abandoned. The realm doesn't need the covenant destroyed. It needs it *reforged*. Under proper authority. With a proper oath-keeper. One who understands that oaths flow downward from the throne, not sideways from ghosts."

Edmund stared at her. "You're hunting gifted people to *fix* it?"

"I'm hunting one gifted person," Vael said, rising, brushing ash from her cloak with two precise strokes. "You hear the dead. The dead remember the covenant-road. The dead know the words. You, bone-carver, are going to lead us to the Covenant Hall, and you're going to do it gratefully, because the alternative—" she gestured mildly at the burned shrine, at the whole scorched season of it— "is more of this. More smoke. More wool-wrapped irons. The crown is generous to those who help it be generous."

"And Ulyana?"

"Will be given a choice," Vael said, "between a scaffold and a crown's leash. Most people choose the leash. It's shinier."

They locked him in the shrine's old root cellar, which had survived the fire by being underground, as cellars traditionally are. The door was oak and the guard outside was the onion-scented soldier, who apologized again through the wood, unprompted, for the quality of the straw.

Edmund sat in the dark and listened.

The dead were there. They were always there — thin now, threadbare, the way they'd been since the covenant broke, like voices heard through a wall in winter. But they came closer when he was still, and they whispered the things spirits whisper: the guard changes at moonrise; there are only six riders, not twenty; the horses are picketed downstream where the wind won't carry scent to them; and, from a child's voice that always made his chest ache, *the loose stone behind the third shelf, the one you used to hide the good honey in.*

He almost laughed. Dead children remembered everything.

*Ulyana,* he thought at them, uselessly, the way you shout at someone across a river. *If you're alive, if you're following — moonrise. Six riders. Loose stone.*

The dead couldn't carry messages. He knew that. But the dead could *wait*, and waiting, in the mountains, was half of every plan ever made.

*

Moonrise came gray-gold through the cellar's high vent, and with it came the smell of burning — not shrine-timber this time but wet peat and horse dung, the particular reek of a fire built wrong on purpose.

The guard noticed it too. "Smoke?" he called to his fellows. "Anyone else smell—"

What followed was a masterclass in smuggler craft, delivered at volume. Someone upstream began imitating a stag in rut — badly, magnificently, a sound no stag had ever produced or would wish to — and the picketed horses lost their minds. Boots thundered toward the river. A second voice, deeper in the trees, started a quarrel with itself in two registers, drunk and sober, about whose turn it was to fetch water. And under all of it, quiet as a hand slipping into a purse, the cellar door's bar lifted away.

Ulyana crouched in the doorway wearing a stolen soldier's coat two sizes too big and an expression of profound disapproval.

"Honey," she whispered. "You hid *honey* behind a loose stone and never told me?"

"That information was classified."

"Get up. Your guard's chasing a man named Fenn who is pretending to be two people and a deer." She hauled him upright, then paused, studying the manacles. "Keys?"

"Belt pouch of the onion one."

"Onion one?"

"You'll know him when you smell him."

She got the keys off the onion soldier so smoothly that Edmund felt a flicker of professional envy, and then they were running, up through the trees, past Fenn still bellowing his duet with himself, until the camp noise fell away behind them and the mountain path opened ahead, silver under the thirteenth-moon's early waxing.

Only then did Ulyana stop, hands on knees, breathing hard, and look at him with something raw underneath the smuggler's grin.

"They told me," she said. "In the caves. The dead told me — I felt them, Edmund, for the first time since I broke it, like a hand on the back of my neck. *Loose stone, honey, moonrise.* They shouldn't be able to reach me. I cut the line myself."

"Maybe they forgave you."

"Spirits don't forgive. They *remember*." She straightened. "Which means either the covenant isn't as dead as I thought, or something is using its corpse to talk to us. Both options are terrible. Let's run."

*

They ran until dawn grayed the ridgeline, and then they didn't run anymore, because Vael was already there.

Not with riders. Alone, sitting on a boundary stone at the pass's throat, her ledger closed on her knee, as if she'd been waiting for them the way one waits for weather.

"Six riders," she said pleasantly. "A stag in rut. A man arguing with himself. Very good, all of it. I let you go, Master Galloway, because a fish on a short line teaches you nothing." She stood. "But let us speak plainly now, since plainness seems to be your native tongue. The crown wants the covenant reforged. Not broken further. Not buried. *Reforged* — with the Oathstone, at the Hall, under the thirteenth moon, which is eleven nights away. We need an oath-keeper of royal blood to speak the vow. There is exactly one left." Her pale eyes moved to Ulyana. "And we need someone the dead will answer. There is also exactly one of those."

"You'll make her a puppet," Edmund said. The fury arrived all at once, hot and clean, after days of cold fear, and he welcomed it like an old friend. "A crowned mouth on a throne's hand. You'll chain her oath to your king's word and call it renewal."

"I'll make her *useful*," Vael said. "Which is more than exile ever did. Refuse, and the hunt continues — every gifted soul in every village, every whisperer, every carver, every child who talks to empty rooms. Help us, and the burning stops." She stepped back from the stone, unhurried. "Eleven nights. The Hall remembers its road, and so, I'm told, do you. I'll find you there. Everyone finds everyone there, in the end."

She walked away downhill into the mist, and the mist took her the way mist takes tax collectors: too politely.

Ulyana stood very still beside him. "She's right about one thing," she said quietly. "We were going to the Hall anyway. She's just made our own plan feel like hers."

"No," Edmund said. His hands, still ringed with manacle-bruises, curled into fists. "No, she hasn't. Because she thinks we're pawns she's positioned. And the beautiful thing about being treated like a pawn, Ulyana—" he turned to her, and the fury in him had cooled into something harder and brighter— "is that nobody watches a pawn closely enough to see what it's actually carrying."

Behind them, faint as breath, the dead had begun to hum — low, tuneless, gathering — like a choir clearing its throats eleven nights early.
