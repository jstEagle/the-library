## 7. The Price of a Voice

The caves beneath the Covenant Hall smelled of old rain and older stone, and Ulyana had been counting her heartbeats to keep from counting her fears when the wall began to glow.

Not firelight-glow. Not the sick amber of Vael's lanterns. This was a pale, patient light, like moonrise through milk, and it came from within the rock itself, waking as her fingers brushed it. She snatched her hand back.

"Edmund," she called softly. "Edmund, come look at this."

He was three paces behind her, as he had been for two days, ever since they'd slipped into the deep caves through a smuggler's crack in the mountain that he swore on his mother's grave he'd only used twice, both times while being chased by men with dogs. He came forward now with that careful walk of his, the one that said *I am a simple bone-carver and nothing more*, which she had long since stopped believing.

He touched the wall where she had, and the light spread under his palm like spilled honey.

"Huh," he said.

"'Huh,'" she repeated. "Two days I've been asking you what you hear down here, and all I get is 'huh.'"

"I hear a great many things," Edmund said mildly. "Most of them are complaints about the drainage."

But he wasn't smiling. That was the thing about Edmund Galloway — he smiled the way other men bled, easily and constantly, and you learned to read the wounds underneath. Right now there was a wound in him wide enough to drive a cart through, and he was standing very still so she wouldn't notice.

She'd been noticing for days.

* * *

They had come to the caves because the Hall above was watched. Vael's riders circled the ruin like crows over a carcass, and Ashvael's presence hung in the high passes like weather that hadn't decided yet whether to be a storm. The thirteenth moon was four nights away. Four nights to find how the covenant had first been made, because the fragments Ulyana carried — the shattered Oathstone, the scorched pages she'd stolen from her own order's archive on the night she fled — spoke of a ritual but not of its making.

So they searched. And the mountain, in its grudging way, helped.

It was Edmund who found the first chamber — a low vault where the walls were carved floor to ceiling with figures: men and women kneeling, standing, walking in procession toward a central shape that might have been an altar or might have been a door. Their faces were worn smooth by time, but their postures were vivid, urgent, alive.

"They're going somewhere," Ulyana said, tracing the procession with her torch.

"They're going *in*," Edmund corrected quietly. He stood before the central figure a long moment. "There's someone here who isn't like the others. Carved deeper. Newer." His voice did something strange on the last word. "Someone added later."

She looked. The figure stood apart from the procession, facing away from the altar-door, hands raised as if in farewell or warning. Around its wrists, the carver had cut thin bands — chains, or perhaps something else.

"Who is it?" she asked.

Edmund didn't answer. He was listening — she knew that listening now, the tilt of his head, the way his breath went shallow, the far-off focus of a man hearing voices no one else could. She waited. She was getting good at waiting for Edmund, though every hour of it felt like standing on ice.

"The dead don't remember this room," he said finally. "That's the trouble. They remember everything else — every smuggler's route, every grave, every quarrel my grandmother had with the baker in 1187, gods rest them both. But this place is..." He frowned. "*Quiet.* Deliberately quiet. Like a room someone swept clean."

"Why would anyone sweep the dead out of a room?"

"Because," Edmund said slowly, "they didn't want the dead telling anyone what happened here."

* * *

The second chamber was smaller, and it was there that Ulyana found the record.

It was not a book. It was bone — great curved ribs of some beast long vanished from the world, mounted along the walls and carved with script so fine she needed the torch held close to read it. Old Oath-script, the formal hand of the first keepers, the kind she'd spent ten years learning and six years pretending she'd forgotten.

She read aloud, because reading it silently felt too much like prayer, and she had lost the right to pray.

"*In the year of the Sundering's end, the realms were bound by three hands: the hand of blood royal, who gave the oath; the hand of the spirit lord, who gave the gate; and the hand of the listener, who gave the voice.*"

She paused. "Listener?"

"Keep reading," Edmund said. His voice had gone very flat.

"*For the covenant could not hold by oath alone. It required one who could hear across the veil, to carry the dead's consent to the living and the living's grief to the dead. And the price of that hearing was total. What the listener gave, he gave forever. The dead fell silent to him, as snow falls silent on a field, beautiful and gone.*"

Ulyana lowered the torch.

The chamber was very small. There was nowhere to look except at Edmund, and Edmund was looking at the carved figure from the first chamber — the one set apart, the one with bands at its wrists, the one added later — and his face wore an expression she recognized from mirrors, from the worst night of her own life, from the moment a person decides something terrible and calls it duty.

"No," she said.

"It explains the silence," he said, in the reasonable tone of a man explaining arithmetic to a child. "Why the dead went quiet everywhere when the covenant broke. They weren't silenced by your failure, Ulyana. They were silenced by design. The original binding took the listener's hearing and wove it into the covenant itself. When it broke—"

"*No.*"

"—the weave unraveled, and everything the listener paid went with it. Which means to reforge it—"

"*Edmund.*"

He turned to her then, and he smiled, and it was the worst smile she had ever seen, warm and rueful and absolutely final. "You know what I'm going to say."

"I know what you're *going to say*. I want to hear you not say it."

"That's not how sentences work."

"Edmund Galloway, I will throw you off this mountain myself."

"You'd have to catch me first, and you're a terrible climber. You nearly died on the scree at Hollow Gate, and I had to haul you up by your—" He stopped, because she had grabbed his collar with both hands, and the torch guttered between them, and suddenly neither of them was laughing.

"Listen to me," she said. "I broke the covenant. *Me.* I stood in that hall six years ago and I refused the renewal, and I told myself it was principle, that both realms would be freer unbound, and I let the whole world go dark rather than bend my knee one more time to a crown I despised. Do you understand? The silence is *mine*. Every village that starved when the mountains soured, every spirit that faded, every smuggler your dead used to warn away from rockslides — mine. I have carried it alone because carrying things alone is the only skill I have left." Her voice cracked and she hated it and kept going. "And now you stand there planning to make it worse. To pay *more*. To give up the only beautiful thing about you so that I can be forgiven for the ugliest thing about me. That's not sacrifice, Edmund. That's just me, again, letting someone else bleed for my choices."

"That's not what this is."

"Then tell me what it is."

He was quiet a long moment. The torch hissed. Somewhere deep in the mountain, water moved through stone with a sound like slow breathing.

"When I was nine years old," he said, "I heard my grandfather's ghost arguing with a fencepost. He'd been dead two years. Everyone thought I was mad — my mother wept over me, the priest burned sage in our kitchen until we smelled like a festival bonfire. So I learned to lie. Fifty years of lying, Ulyana. 'No, Mother.' 'No, Father.' 'Just talking to myself.' I built a whole man out of *no*, and I lived inside him, and he fit so well I forgot he was a costume." He looked down at his hands — carver's hands, scarred and precise. "These voices are the truest thing I've ever had. And you think giving them up would cost me everything." He shook his head. "It wouldn't. It would cost me the costume. Underneath, there's just a man who loves—"

He stopped.

The word sat in the air between them, enormous, unfinished.

Ulyana did not let him stop. Six years of silence had taught her exactly what unfinished words cost, and she was done paying that debt. She took his scarred face in both hands, torchlight and cave-light moving on his skin, and she finished it for him.

"A man who loves me," she said. "Say it. Say it out loud, in a room the dead can't hear, so it belongs to only us."

"A man who loves you," Edmund said, and his voice broke on the second word, fifty years of *no* cracking open like river ice. "Gods help us both. A man who loves you, and has since the night you lied to Vael with a straight face and then threw up behind the shrine afterward. I have never once heard the dead say anything half so worth hearing as you swearing."

She laughed, wet and helpless, forehead pressed to his. "You are the most infuriating man in two realms."

"Noted. Repeatedly. By better people than you."

"And if you do this — if you give up your hearing — I will spend every day of the rest of my life knowing I traded your music for my absolution. Don't ask that of me. *Please.*" The word came out raw. She couldn't remember the last time she'd said it. Perhaps never. "Find another way. Take my rank, take my name, take my blood — I'm royal-adjacent enough, the old texts say blood royal, and my grandmother was a king's sister, we can *try*, Edmund, we can try *me*—"

"The text says the listener gives the voice. You can't give what you don't have."

"Then we find another listener!"

"With four nights left? With Vael circling overhead and Ashvael hunting us through every shadow?" He cupped her face now, mirroring her, gentle as he was with the finest ivory. "Ulyana. My love. My impossible, exiled, magnificent love. I have hidden my whole life. Let me spend what I've been hiding on something that matters."

And she saw it in him — the cold clarity she'd seen once before, in herself, in a hall full of candles six years ago — and she understood with terrible certainty that she could not argue him out of this. He had already decided. He had probably decided in the first chamber, before she'd even read the words.

Which meant there was only one thing left to do.

"Then we go tonight," she said. "Before you can talk yourself into ceremony. Before Vael or Ashvael or any of them—"

The torch went out.

Not guttered — *went out*, snuffed as if pinched by invisible fingers, and the pale light of the walls dimmed with it, and from the dark of the passage behind them came a sound Ulyana had hoped never to hear again: the dry, amused, ash-on-marble voice of a spirit lord who had found what he was looking for.

"How touching," said Ashvael. "I do hate to interrupt a betrothal. But we have a gate to open, little oath-keeper — and your carver has a bargain to keep."

* * *

Edmund's hand found hers in the dark and squeezed once — a message, a promise, a goodbye she refused to accept.

She squeezed back: *not yet, not ever, not without a fight.*

Above them, through miles of stone, the thirteenth moon was already rising.
