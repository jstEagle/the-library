## 8. Betrayal at the Hollow Gate

The dead had stopped whispering.

That was the first thing Edmund noticed when they came down out of the caves into the Hollow Gate valley — not the cold, though the cold was remarkable, the kind that got into the marrow and made itself at home like a relative with no departure date. It was the silence. For three days the dead had been a river in his ears, murmuring directions through the covenant-road, warning him of rockfall, complaining — endlessly, pettily, wonderfully — about the state of their own bones. And now, nothing. A hush so complete it rang.

He knew what silence meant. He'd lived inside it for thirty years before Ulyana's broken covenant cracked the mountains open and let the voices leak back through. Silence meant the veil was thinning wrong. Silence meant something on the other side was drinking.

"Edmund." Ulyana stood a few paces ahead of him at the tree line, her hand raised. She'd gone very still, the way she did when she was about to do something brave and stupid. "There are lights in the gatehouse."

"I see them."

"There shouldn't be anyone here. The Hollow Gate has been abandoned since—"

"Since your grandmother's grandmother signed the last renewal," Edmund said. "I know. I've read the stones." He came up beside her and looked out across the valley. The gate itself was an old thing, older than kingdoms — two pillars of white stone leaning toward each other like tired dancers, the road between them worn to glass by centuries of feet both living and otherwise. The gatehouse beyond it was a squat ruin, and yes, lights burned in its windows. Green ones. Nothing natural burned green.

"The dead have gone quiet," he said quietly.

Ulyana turned to look at him. In the moonlight her face was all angles and exhaustion, and beneath the exhaustion something softer that she kept trying to hide and kept failing at, which was one of his favorite things about her. "All of them?"

"All of them. Like someone shut a door."

She exhaled slowly. "Ashvael."

"Ashvael," he agreed.

They had been running from the spirit lord for six days now, ever since the ruined Covenant Hall, ever since Ashvael's voice had slid into Edmund's dreams like oil into water: *Bring me the oath-keeper, bone-carver, and I will end the hunt. I will unmake Vael's writ. I will give you back every voice you have ever lost.* Six days of pretending, even to himself, that he hadn't listened. Six days of watching Ulyana watch him, knowing she could see the temptation sitting on his shoulder like a crow.

He had gotten very good, over the years, at looking like a man who wasn't listening to anything at all.

* * *

They didn't make it to the trees on the far side of the valley.

The mist came up out of the ground between one step and the next — not drifting, but *rising*, deliberate as a curtain going up. Edmund heard Ulyana swear in three languages, two of them dead, which under other circumstances would have delighted him. Then the mist took shape, and shape took weight, and hands of grey smoke closed around them both.

He fought. He would always be a little proud of that. A bone-carver's hands were strong hands, and he broke two of the smoky grips before the rest closed over his arms, his chest, his throat — not squeezing, just holding, patient as a vice. Across the clearing he saw Ulyana twisted in her own bindings, the shattered Oathstone still slung across her back in its leather wrapping, her eyes finding his with a ferocity that said, quite clearly: *don't you dare do anything noble.*

Then Ashvael arrived.

It did not walk so much as arrive, the way weather arrives. One moment the space before the gate was empty; the next it was occupied by something tall and elegant and wrong, wearing a body like a well-tailored coat over a fire. Its face was beautiful the way a frozen lake is beautiful, and its smile had too many gentle curves in it.

"The bone-carver," Ashvael said. Its voice was warm. That was the horror of it — warm, like a hearth, like a hand on your shoulder from behind. "And the oath-keeper. You've led me such a merry chase. I want you to know I've enjoyed it. Very few mortals make me work anymore."

"What do you want?" Ulyana spat.

"What I have always wanted. What I set in motion the day you walked out of that hall and let the covenant die." Ashvael drifted closer, and Edmund felt the temperature drop around it, felt the dead pressing against the thin places in the world, mute and straining. "A renewal. A flawed one. A covenant reforged under my terms, with my hooks in it, binding the spirit realm to a mortal crown I can steer. Your kingdom has been so obliging, oath-keeper. Did you truly think the hunt for the gifted was the king's idea? Kings are such simple creatures. They only ever need a small push and a good story."

Edmund's stomach went cold. "You wrote the proclamation."

"I *suggested* it. Through dreams, through advisors' ambitions, through a certain inquisitor's very personal grief." Ashvael's smile widened. "Vael lost a daughter to the souring mountains, did you know? A gifted child, taken by spirits gone feral after the covenant broke. I made sure the crown blamed people like you instead of the broken oath. Fear is such efficient mortar. And now, at the thirteenth moon, the realms stand ripe for a renewal — mine. All I needed was the last ingredient."

Its gaze slid to Ulyana, and Edmund understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly how long the trap had been closing. Not since the Covenant Hall. Not since the caves. Since the night she refused to renew the oath. Every step of their flight had been a funnel. Every "escape" a corridor.

"You never wanted me to deliver her," Edmund said. His voice came out flat and strange, and he realized with distant interest that it was the same voice he used when carving. Steady. Detached. The voice of a man doing work. "You wanted us both here. Her blood freely offered at the gate, my gift standing witness. A flawed renewal needs a flawed pair."

"Oh, *well done*," Ashvael breathed, delighted. "Yes. The old ritual demands consent — that tiresome clause. But consent given in fear, in desperation, in love twisted toward sacrifice — that counts. The original drafters were sentimental. I have read the fine print for nine hundred years, bone-carver. I know where the ink is thin."

It extended a hand, and the mist holding Edmund loosened — just slightly, just enough to be an offer rather than a cage.

"So. Our bargain stands. Deliver her to the gate. Speak the words I teach you. Bind her oath into my covenant, and I will end the hunt, spare the gifted, and leave your village — your *workshop*, carver, all those little memorials — untouched forever. Refuse, and I take her anyway, and burn Thornfell as an appetizer."

Ulyana was shouting his name. He heard it the way you hear rain on a roof — real, insistent, easy to ignore.

Edmund looked at Ashvael. He thought about the drowned child's whistle, finished and never delivered. About Vael's fires on the pass. About thirty years of hiding in plain sight, carving truth into bone while lying with his whole face. About the way Ulyana said his name when she wasn't angry, which was rare, and worth everything, which was the problem.

Grief, he had learned, was not a fog. It was a whetstone. You could sharpen things on it.

"Yes," Edmund said.

* * *

They bound his hands anyway — Ashvael insisted on theater, and theater required a prisoner being dragged — and marched him up the glass-worn road toward the gate with Ulyana beside him, her wrists wrapped in grey smoke, her shoulder pressed hard against his arm. To Ashvael it looked like despair. To anyone who had spent years reading smugglers' faces by lantern light, it looked like two people leaning close enough to talk.

"Don't," Ulyana whispered. Her voice shook, and he hated that he was the cause. "Whatever you're planning, Edmund, don't. I've seen men bargain with spirits. There's no version where you win."

"There's no version where I lose, either," he murmured back. "That's the trick of it. I'm not bargaining for myself."

"Edmund—"

"He told us the terms, love. Out loud. Spirits are vain; vanity makes them verbose." He kept his eyes forward, his face slack, his voice barely a breath. "Consent given in fear counts. Which means consent *withdrawn* in clarity doesn't. The words only bind if she speaks them afraid. So we make sure she isn't."

Ulyana went very still against his side. "You're going to use the ritual against him."

"I'm going to use *you* against him. As bait." The word tasted like iron, and he made himself say it plainly, because she deserved plainness. "He wants a frightened oath-keeper dragged to the gate. So that's what walks there. Everything else — the words, the timing, what actually gets sealed under that thirteenth moon — that part we choose. He showed me where the ink is thin. I've been reading fine print my whole life. Bone doesn't lie, Ulyana. It just waits for someone patient enough to listen."

"And if he sees through you?"

"Then we die at a gate that matters, together, having ruined nine hundred years of his patience." He glanced at her, and let the mask slip — just a crack, just long enough for her to see what was underneath, which was not coldness at all but a terror so large it had nowhere to go except forward. "I need you to hate me a little, these next few hours. Scream at the gate. Fight him. Mean it. Because if he believes for even one moment that I've truly sold you—"

"—then he won't be watching anything else," she finished softly.

They reached the foot of the gate. Above them the two white pillars leaned together, and between them the air shimmered like heat off a forge, and behind it, faintly, Edmund heard it — the dead, thousands upon thousands, pressed against the veil, silent not because they were gone but because Ashvael was holding the door shut.

Holding it shut from *this* side.

Which meant the door had a hinge. And hinges, Edmund thought, flexing his bound fingers, feeling the old familiar ache settle into his knuckles like a tool finding its grip — hinges could be carved.

Ashvael turned to him, radiant, triumphant, utterly certain.

"Now," it said, "let us begin."

Edmund bowed his head, the picture of surrender, and began to speak the first false words of the oldest lie in the mountains.
