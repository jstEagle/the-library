## 9. The Reforging

**Edmund**

The thirteenth moon rose fat and white over the Covenant Hall, and Edmund thought it looked like a bone held up to the light — the kind he would have chosen himself, if the world had asked his opinion, which it never had.

The Hall itself was a ruin of a ruin. Its pillars leaned like old men gossiping. Moss had eaten the carvings on the lintel, though when Edmund pressed his palm against the stone he could still feel them underneath, faint as scars: two hands clasped, one flesh and one smoke.

"You're shaking," Ulyana said beside him.

"Cold," he said.

"Liar."

"Two liars," he admitted. "That's how this whole thing started, if you recall."

She almost smiled. In the moonlight her face looked carved too — cheekbone and jaw and the long line of her throat, all of it honed by years of standing very straight while everything fell down around her. She wore no royal colors now. She wore smuggler's wool and one of his spare cloaks, and she had never looked more like an oath-keeper in her life.

Below them, at the foot of the Hall's broken stair, Vael's riders waited with torches they did not light. The Inquisitor herself stood apart from them, hands folded, patient as frost. And behind her, spilling out of the dark like ink given ambition, stood Ashvael.

The spirit lord had worn many shapes since Edmund first heard its whisper in the caves. Tonight it had chosen something almost human: tall, silver-eyed, dressed in robes that moved like water deciding to be cloth. It smiled at him across the distance, and the smile was warm, and that was the worst thing about it.

*Last chance,* it murmured, in the voice only Edmund could hear. *Deliver her, and the hunt ends tonight. The crown forgets your name. You keep your gift. You keep everything.*

"I keep everything," Edmund said aloud.

Ulyana turned. "What?"

"Nothing." He took a breath that hurt all the way down. "Time to go down and be pawns with dignity."

* * *

**Ulyana**

The stones of the Hall remembered her feet. That was the cruelest part. Seven years since she had last stood here, the night she refused the renewal, and the floor still seemed to rise to meet her steps like an old dog that had never stopped waiting by the door.

Vael watched her come with those pale, unhurried eyes. "Lady Oath-Keeper. Or should I say Lady Oath-Breaker? The crown has prepared a document." A clerk behind her produced a scroll with the reverence of a man handling a relic. "A full pardon. Restoration of rank. All charges dissolved — provided the covenant is renewed under royal authority, with an oath-keeper sworn to the throne."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we proceed with someone more cooperative." Vael's gaze slid, deliberate and contemptuous, to Edmund. "There are always other gifted souls. The hunt has been *very* productive."

Ulyana felt the old guilt rise in her chest, hot and familiar, seven years of it, and for once she let it burn instead of swallowing it down. Because she finally understood what Vael's careful words had been circling for weeks: the crown didn't want the covenant healed. It wanted it *owned*. A spirit realm on a leash. Dead ancestors answering royal summons. A border that obeyed.

Her failure had nearly unmade both worlds. This would finish the job politely, with paperwork.

"I have read the document," Ulyana said, "in my head, just now, and found it wanting."

The clerk made a small strangled sound.

"The original oath was freely given," she went on, louder now, letting her voice carry up into the leaning pillars where the spirits gathered like storm-light. "Freely offered and freely kept. That is the whole of the law, and every oath-keeper before me knew it, and I knew it best of all because I am the one who broke it." She drew a breath. "I will not swear to a throne. I will swear to the realms. To both of them. As my own woman, disgraced and unforgiven, and let the pardon rot."

Vael's composure cracked, just at the edges. "You understand what you're refusing."

"I've had seven years to practice refusing things. I'm quite good at it now."

Behind the Inquisitor, Ashvael's smile had gone thin as paper held to flame.

* * *

**Edmund**

Edmund stepped forward, and the dead came with him.

They had been gathering all night — he could feel them pressing close, hundreds of them, the drowned child from Thornfell and the smugglers Vael had broken and generations deeper than that, all the way back to the first bone-carver whose story Ulyana had found scratched in the deep caves. They were not silent anymore. They were not singing yet either. They were *waiting*, the way a hall waits when the musician walks onstage.

He knelt at the old altar and took out the whistle.

He had finished carving it weeks ago, in a cave, by candlelight, working while Ulyana slept. It was for the drowned girl, and it was beautiful, and it was also — he understood now — the last thing he would ever make that the dead could speak through.

"Ashvael," he said, and the name rang strangely in the ruined Hall, "you offered me power to end the hunt. I accept."

Ulyana's head snapped toward him. "*Edmund—*"

"In exchange," he continued, over her, over Vael's sharp intake of breath, over the spirit lord's widening silver eyes, "I deliver what you actually wanted. Not her. The flaw in the covenant. The reason your engineered renewal would have failed."

He stood. He held up the whistle.

"The original covenant was sealed by a bone-carver who gave up hearing the dead. That was the price. Not blood. Not surrender. A voice, freely silenced, so the two realms could hear each other without a man in between, taking messages, taking sides, taking *payment*." He met Ashvael's gaze. "You needed an oath-keeper delivered because a bound oath-keeper makes a bound covenant. But the covenant doesn't want a keeper who owns it. It wants a keeper who serves it. And it wants its price paid honestly — which you never intended, because honest prices don't leave anyone to bargain with."

Ashvael's human shape flickered. For half a heartbeat Edmund saw what lived underneath: something vast and starving and very old, wearing hunger like a crown.

"You would be nothing," the spirit lord said. "Silent. Deaf to us forever. You would give up the only thing that has ever made you *you*."

"No," Edmund said quietly. "The voices were never what made me me. They were just the loudest thing about me. There's a difference. Took me forty years to learn it, but there it is."

He raised the whistle to his lips.

* * *

**Ulyana**

She had imagined this moment a hundred times since the caves — since she'd read the old carver's story and understood, with sickening clarity, what Edmund intended. She had rehearsed arguments. She had planned to beg.

Instead she stepped forward and stood beside him, shoulder to shoulder, the way they'd walked every mile of the Thornfell passes together.

"The words are spoken by two," she said. "It's in the carvings. One voice to offer, one to bind. That was always the design — the carver gave his hearing, but the oath-keeper gave the keeping. Freely. Both, or neither."

Edmund lowered the whistle an inch. His eyes were wet and furious and grateful all at once. "You're supposed to take the pardon."

"The pardon can wait in line behind several other things I'm refusing tonight."

Vael shouted something about treason. Riders moved. None of it mattered; the moon had reached the top of the broken roof, and the Hall's dead stones began, very softly, to hum.

Edmund spoke the old words. He spoke them badly, stumbling over syllables no living tongue had used in centuries, and the dead corrected him gently, whispering each phrase a breath before he needed it — one last kindness, one last lesson. When he reached the offering, he put the whistle to his lips one final time and blew.

The note that came out was the drowned girl's laugh. He was sure of it. Then it was gone, and the silence where his gift had lived all his life closed around him like deep water, and Edmund Galloway heard nothing at all.

He almost fell. Ulyana caught his arm.

"My turn," she said, and her voice did not shake at all.

She spoke her oath plainly, no ritual grandeur, just truth laid out flat in the moonlight: that she had broken the covenant believing freedom served both realms better than binding, and that she had been wrong, and that she would keep the mended thing for the rest of her life not because a throne commanded it but because she chose it, daily, the way one chooses anything worth having.

*Freely given,* said the stones.
*Freely kept,* answered the dark beyond the pillars.

And then both realms answered *together*, and the sound was not a sound at all but a vast consent rolling through mountain and grave and growing green thing, and Ashvael screamed — a long unraveling cry, its stolen shape coming apart thread by thread, its engineered hunt dissolving with it, until there was nothing left of the spirit lord but a smell like cold ash and a silence that felt, for the first time in seven years, clean.

In the hills above Thornfell, the sour ground sweetened. In the village below, wells cleared. And everywhere at once, in one great final chorus, the dead sang — a song with no words in it, or all of them, rising through the earth and out of the mountains and up toward the thirteenth moon, and then fading, fading, going home.

Edmund stood in the ringing aftermath and heard none of it.

But he saw Ulyana crying, and he saw Vael's riders kneeling without being told, and he saw the drowned girl's whistle lying whole and quiet in his open hand, and he thought: *so that's what it sounded like. I'll just have to take everyone's word for it.*

"Well," he said. "That's done, then."

Ulyana laughed through her tears and gripped his hand hard enough to hurt. "You absolute fool. You wonderful, stubborn—"

"There's a village shrine needs rebuilding," he said. "And I'm told the record-keeper position in Thornfell pays terribly but comes with excellent company."

She kissed him there among the humming stones, under the settling moon, while the first true dawn in seven years crept gray and gentle over the Thornfell peaks — and far away, in a hollow beneath the roots of the world, something small and patient that had hidden from even Ashvael opened one eye, and began, very softly, to listen.
