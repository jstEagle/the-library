## 1. The Smuggler's Moon

The moon was wrong tonight, and Coral Brightwater knew it before she knew anything else.

She stood at the head of the caravan with her boots planted on a shelf of granite that shouldn't have held her weight, and she looked up through the black lattice of the pines, and there it was — a sliver too many, a brightness where the almanacs said there should be dark. She'd been counting the moon's turns since she was six years old, because counting was the first thing they taught you in a hedge-witch's apprenticeship and the last thing anyone could take away. Thirteen turns to the season. Everyone knew twelve. Everyone had always known twelve.

But Coral knew thirteen.

"Girl." Doss Marrow's voice came up from below, gravel over gravel. The smuggler-master stood at the base of the rock shelf with his lantern hooded and his patience visibly thinning. "You said the pass opens at moonrise. Moon's been up an hour."

"The pass opens when *I* say it opens," Coral called back, not unkindly, because Doss respected nothing so much as a person who didn't flinch at him. "And I say we wait for the wind to turn. You want to walk into Hollowgate with the wind at your back, pushing your smell ahead of you into every cave mouth between here and the far side? There are things in those caves that follow smells."

"What things?"

"Things that eat smugglers." She crouched, laid her palm flat against the stone, and let herself feel what the mountain was telling her. It was a skill she'd never been able to explain properly — not witchcraft exactly, or not only witchcraft, but something older and more embarrassing, like knowing when rain was coming by the ache in a scar. The granite hummed under her hand, low and patient, the way a sleeping animal breathes. "Ten minutes. Maybe twelve. Then the wind swings round out of the north cut, and we move fast and quiet, and by dawn you'll be drinking bad wine in Lowmarsh and telling everyone how clever you were."

Doss grumbled something about clever girls and expensive funerals, but he went back down the line to check the mules' wrappings, which meant he'd accepted it. That was the thing about Doss: he'd hired her three months ago off a roadside in the foothills, half because she'd told him his lead mule was lame before he'd noticed the limp, and half because she'd quoted him a price so low it had to be either desperation or confidence, and he'd gambled on confidence. He'd never once asked her outright why a girl of nineteen with obvious talent was working caravans for coin that wouldn't keep a cat in fish. Smugglers had rules about questions. It was one of the few things she loved about them.

*Wouldn't keep a cat in fish.* Her grandmother's phrase. She caught herself using it more and more lately, and she didn't know why, because she'd never met the woman. Grandmother Brightwater had died — or vanished, the stories disagreed — decades before Coral was born, leaving behind only a name that made hedge-witches go quiet and a cottage in the high valley that nobody would live in.

Coral shook it off. Below her, the caravan waited in the blue-black dark: nine mules, four men, two wagons stripped down to their bones and wrapped in felt so they moved like ghosts. Salted fish, iron nails, untaxed brandy, and one crate Doss wouldn't let her look inside, which was fine, because she already knew it held something that whispered. She'd heard it muttering to itself outside Threeford. She hadn't told him that either.

The wind turned.

She felt it come before she heard it — a pressure change, a shift in the mountain's breathing, the pine tops shivering in a long ripple that ran down the valley like a hand smoothing bedclothes. North cut air, cold and clean and smelling of snowmelt and old stone.

"Now," she said, and dropped from the shelf, landing light as an accusation.

They moved. This was the part she loved, though she'd have bitten her tongue off before admitting it to anyone: the moving. Nine mules and five humans threading a path no map showed, through a pass that existed only if you knew to look for it — a seam in the Thornfells where two ridges leaned together like conspirators, hiding a corridor of scree and stunted birch that went clean through the range. The maps called this stretch impassable. The maps had been drawn by men who asked the mountains politely. Coral didn't ask. She listened.

"Left here," she murmured, and the lead driver hauled his mule left, and thirty seconds later the man behind him saw why: the right-hand trail ended in a drop that would have folded a wagon like wet paper. "Keep the lanterns hooded till we're past the white stone. The stone reflects light. Someone's always watching from the east ridge — don't ask me who, I don't know, but they've watched this pass for as long as the pass has been here."

"You say things like that," muttered Ferren, the youngest of Doss's men, walking beside her mule, "like they're normal things to say."

"They are normal things. For me."

"And the other thing? The thing you won't say?" He was fishing, the way he always fished on night crossings, when the dark made everyone honest. "Why a hedge-witch runs away from her mistress. Aldith, isn't it? Mother Aldith of the Ashvale villages? Everyone's heard of her. Woman can cure lung-rot with a boiled onion, they say. And her own apprentice bolts in the night without so much as—"

"Ferren." Coral kept her voice pleasant. "You see that birch tree ahead, the crooked one?"

"...Yes?"

"If you ask me that question again, I'm going to tell you exactly what lives in its roots, in detail, and then you're going to spend the rest of this crossing imagining it every time we stop to rest. Are we understood?"

He was quiet for a quarter mile. Then, because he was young and couldn't help himself: "Is it true you can hear the mountain?"

Coral sighed. This, at least, was a question she could answer honestly, and honesty was rare enough on the road that she hoarded it like coins. "I can hear *a* mountain. This one. It talks all the time — mostly nonsense, mostly old dreams, the way old things dream. Tonight it's saying—" She stopped.

Because the mountain had just said something new.

It wasn't words, exactly. It was never words. It was a pulling, a lean, the way a dog lifts its head before the knock comes at the door. The deep places beneath them had gone attentive. All along their route, every hollow and cave mouth and buried river they passed, she felt the same sensation: the mountain turning its many ears toward her, listening back.

That had never happened before. Mountains didn't listen. Mountains slept and grumbled and occasionally ate a village, but they did not *pay attention* to a girl leading mules through a pass.

"Coral?" Ferren said. "Tonight it's saying what?"

"It's saying shut up and walk," she lied, and walked.

* * *

They made the far side of Hollowgate two hours before dawn, and Doss paid her at the treeline with the satisfaction of a man who has once again gotten away with something. He counted out the coin slow, watching her face, and she took it without counting it back, which was its own kind of message: *I trust the road, and the road trusts me.*

"You've got a gift," he said, which was as close to affection as Doss Marrow ever came. "Waste of it, hauling brandy. You could be court-witch somewhere. Comfortable. Warm."

"I'd die of comfort inside a month."

"That's what I figured." He chewed on something for a while — tobacco, or a thought. "There's a job, if you want it. Not a run. A *stay*. Old waystation up past Greywater Ford, the one the salt-traders used before the trade died. Somebody's been breaking into it — squatters, maybe, or worse. Owner wants it watched a fortnight, wants a report on who and what. Quiet work. Roof, hearth, no one asking you anything." He shrugged elaborately. "Figured quiet might suit you."

Coral opened her mouth to refuse. Staying still was the one thing she'd never been good at; staying still was how people caught you, how questions caught up with you, how Mother Aldith's letters — three of them now, each one heavier than the last, each one unopened in the bottom of her pack — finally got answered. But the coin was good, and the truth was she was tired in a way that sleep didn't fix, and a fortnight in an empty waystation with a roof and a hearth sounded, just for a moment, almost like rest.

"All right," she said. "A fortnight."

Doss nodded and led the caravan down toward Lowmarsh, and Coral stood alone at the treeline with the moon — the wrong moon, the thirteenth-slivered moon — hanging over the shoulder of the Thornfells like something waiting to be noticed.

She should have looked at it longer. She would think that later, much later, standing in places she couldn't yet imagine. She should have stood there and studied that impossible extra sliver until she understood it. Instead she did what she always did with things that frightened her: she turned her back on it and walked.

* * *

The waystation was two days north, up a track the salt-traders had worn into the mountainside a century ago and the moss had spent a century erasing. Coral reached it at dusk on the second day, and it rose out of the bracken like the ribcage of something enormous — a long hall of grey stone, roof half-collapsed at the eastern end, walls furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Salt-traders had built it, Doss had said. Back when salt came *out* of the Thornfells instead of into them, which was a strange thought, because everyone knew the mountain salt mines had played out generations ago. Everyone knew it the way everyone knows things nobody remembers learning.

The door was swollen shut. She put her shoulder to it, felt the wood give with a groan, and stepped into air that tasted of dust and cold ash and — faintly, unmistakably — the sea.

Coral stopped dead.

She was two hundred miles from any coast. She had never seen the sea in her life. But she knew that smell the way she knew her own heartbeat, because it lived somewhere in her deeper than memory: brine and kelp and storm-wrack, the smell of water that has swallowed everything and keeps it.

"Hello?" she said, to the empty hall, and felt immediately foolish, and then felt less foolish, because the silence *answered*. Not with sound. With attention. The whole building leaned toward her the way the mountain had leaned toward her on the pass, and underneath the leaning was that same patient, buried hum, except here it was layered with something else — something like grief, something like waiting.

Her witch-sense, Aldith would have called it. Her *uncanny knack*, Doss called it. Whatever it was, it was screaming at her now, quietly, the way a kettle screams just before it boils.

She lit the hearth first — habit, discipline, the apprentice's catechism: *fire before fear, bread before grief, work before wondering* — and got a small blaze going with driftwood that had no business being in a mountain hall, pale and porous as bone. Then, because the light was good and the fear needed something to stand next to, she began to search the place properly.

The main room was bare. The storeroom behind it held rotted sacking and the ghost-smell of salt. But behind the hearth, set into the stone where a hundred years of fires should have smoked it black, was a niche — dry, clean, deliberate. Hidden. And in the niche, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a book.

She knew before she touched it that she shouldn't. She touched it anyway. Some part of her had been walking toward this book since before Doss found her on that roadside, since before Aldith, maybe since before she was born, and that part of her took her hands and unwrapped the oilcloth with a care that bordered on reverence.

It was bound in leather gone green-black with damp, and stitched along its spine with cord that crumbled slightly at her touch and smelled overwhelmingly of the sea. No title. No maker's mark. Just a symbol pressed into the cover: a circle, and within the circle a wave, and within the wave — small, almost hidden — an eye.

Coral sat down on the hearthstones with the fire at her back and opened it.

The pages were water-damaged, warped and wavy, ink bloomed into brown flowers across half of them. It was a ledger of some kind, or a journal, or — as she turned the brittle leaves and the style shifted from record to rhyme — a book of prophecies. Century-old verse, most of it gibberish, some of it chillingly specific. *When the miller's second daughter weds beneath a red comet... When the ford at Ashvale dries for seven days...* Small fulfilled things. A village fortune-teller's grab-bag.

Then she reached a page near the end, and the handwriting changed — larger, hurried, pressed hard enough into the paper to score the page beneath — and the date at the top of the page stopped her heart.

Not the year. The year was a century past, written in the old reckoning, meaningless.

The *day*. The season-turn. The moon-count.

*Thirteenth turn,* it read. *Thirteenth sliver, autumn's end.*

Tonight.

Coral's hands had gone very still. Outside, the wind worried at the broken roof, and the fire popped, and she read on, and the verse marched down the page in that urgent scored hand:

*A girl of the Brightwater line shall walk the smuggler's road with salt upon her tongue and no home to her name. She will not know what she carries. She will not believe what she is. On the thirteenth turn she will find this book, for the book has been waiting for her as the sea waits for the river.*

*She is the Salt-Bearer.*

*The Salt-Bearer descends.*

Below the verse, in the same hand, smaller, almost an afterthought — or a plea — someone had written a single line, and the ink of it had run as if the paper had been wept on, or bled on:

*Ulyana. Forgive me. I sold you to buy the morning, and the morning was not worth the price.*

Coral read the name twice. It meant nothing to her. It was just a name, a stranger's name, a century dead.

But as she stared at it, the page grew dark at the edge — a stain spreading inward from the margin, wetting her fingertips where she held it, and she raised her hand to the firelight and saw that her fingers were slick and shining and smelled of the sea.

The book was bleeding seawater. Slowly, steadily, soaking through a century of dryness, as though all the ocean it had ever swallowed was rising up through its pages to reach her.

Coral Brightwater sat alone in a ruined waystation in the heart of the Thornfell mountains, two hundred miles from any coast, holding a drowning book that had been waiting a hundred years to tell her a stranger's name — and outside her window, patient as a debt, the thirteenth sliver of the moon climbed over the ridge and began, at last, to shine.
