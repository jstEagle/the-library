## 2. A Book Bound in Brine

The waystation announced itself the way all dead things do: by smell. Wet stone and old smoke and something sweeter underneath — rotting thatch, maybe, or the ghost of a hearth-fire that had gone out before Coral's grandmother's grandmother was born. She raised a fist and the caravan ground to a halt behind her, eight wagons of contraband salt-cured fish and untaxed iron settling into the kind of silence that only smugglers know how to hold.

"Waystation," she called back down the line. "Old pilgrim road. It'll have a roof, mostly."

"It'll have rats, mostly," said Dagny Holt from the second wagon, spitting into the bracken.

"Rats I can handle." Coral swung down from the lead wagon, boots finding the moss-slick stones without looking for them. "What I can't handle is another night on open ground with that sky coming down. Look east."

They looked. Even the mules looked. Over the shoulder of Mount Cerenne, where the Thornfells went up like broken teeth against a bruising sky, clouds were gathering with an intent that had nothing casual about it. Not weather. *Weather* — the kind that came off the high passes with a sound like a held breath finally let go, the kind that buried roads and drowned travelers and made widows of women who'd kissed their husbands at dawn.

Dagny muttered something uncharitable about mountains and their opinions, but she started unhitching the mules.

The waystation squatted at the crook of the pass like a kneeling pilgrim, its walls furred with lichen the color of old pewter. Coral had known it was there before they crested the ridge — not because it was on any map worth trusting, but because the mountain had told her. That was the thing she never said out loud, the thing she'd run from rather than explain: the Thornfells spoke to her. Not in words, exactly. In leanings. The slope leaned toward water here; the rock leaned away from weight there; and sometimes, on certain nights, the whole range leaned toward her like a dog scenting a hand that might feed it or strike it.

Tonight it had leaned toward shelter, and so here they were.

She got the fire going first — hedge-witch's trick, a pinch of dried rowan-bark and a word she wasn't supposed to know yet, though Aldith would argue she wasn't supposed to know any of them. The thought of Aldith arrived the way it always did, uninvited and sharp under the ribs, and Coral did what she always did with it: fed it to the fire and watched it burn.

Outside, the caravan settled into its evening liturgy. Tarps snapped taut. Mules complained. Marta Voss, who owned the cargo and half-owned the men hauling it, counted crates by lantern-light with the devotion of a woman counting rosaries. Coral heard it all through the waystation's broken door while she explored the interior, and the hearing was habit more than caution. Smugglers' caravans didn't need guarding so much as they needed *steering*, and steering was her job.

The main room was a single vaulted space, its ceiling lost in shadow, its floor a mosaic of flagstones heaved up by frost like a mouth full of bad teeth. Pilgrims had built this place once — you could tell by the alcoves, each one sized for a bedroll and a candle, each one facing the little shrine-niche in the eastern wall. Someone had loved travelers enough to carve them thirty beds out of living mountain. Someone had wanted them safe.

Coral ran her thumb along the shrine-niche's edge and felt the mountain lean again, faintly, the way it did near old holy things. Like a memory pressing up through soil.

That was when she saw the book.

It sat in the niche itself, which was wrong in three ways at once. Wrong because abandoned waystations didn't keep books; books were wealth, and wealth got carried off or burned within a season of any place being left. Wrong because it sat *square*, centered, deliberate, as if placed there this morning rather than a hundred years ago. And wrong because everything else in the niche — the shelf, the dust, the dead moths in the corner — wore its century openly, gray and crumbling, while the book beneath it lay clean as if the dust had simply agreed not to touch it.

Coral knew better than to touch things that behaved wrongly. Aldith had taught her that too, back when teaching her things was still allowed. *If a thing is too neat, girl, ask yourself who tidied it, and why, and whether they're still tidying.*

But the caravan was outside, the storm was coming, and the book was the only interesting thing in forty miles of wet stone.

She picked it up.

It was bound in something that wasn't leather. The cover had the give of skin but the grain of something mineral, and it was crusted at the edges with a rime of white — salt, actual salt, grown into crystals along the spine like frost on a windowpane. When she turned it over, her fingers came away damp. Not wet, exactly. *Brackish.* She touched her tongue to her thumb without thinking, the way you test anything in the mountains, and the taste hit her like a slap: seawater. Clean, cold, unmistakable seawater, three days' hard travel from any coast that maps admitted to.

"Well," she said aloud, to the empty room, because saying things aloud kept the fear at a manageable size. "Well, that's not right at all."

The pages inside were swollen and warped, water-damaged past saving, ink bloomed into gray flowers across half of them. But the front matter had survived. A title, in a cramped ecclesiastical hand: *The Turning of the Thornfell Years, Being a True Account of Things Said Before They Happened.* And beneath it, a date of composition that stopped Coral's breath in her throat.

One hundred and six years ago.

She should have put it down then. She knew that later, in the way you know all the exits of a room only after the fire starts. A century-old book of prophecies, bound in skin and salt, sitting tidy in a dead pilgrim shrine on the exact night she happened to shelter there — every instinct she owned stood up and shouted *no*. But dread and wonder are twins, and the wonder had her wrist, and she opened the book.

The early pages were almanac-stuff, mostly ruined. Tidings of harvests. Notes on comets. A long dull passage about the breeding habits of pike that someone, somewhere in the last century, had presumably found prophetic. Coral skimmed, her fire crackling behind her, the storm beginning to mutter against the eastern wall — and then she reached a page whose damage was different. Not bloomed. *Bled.* The ink hadn't spread; it had run downward, deliberately, as if the words had wept, and the surviving lines stood out stark as bones:

*In the hundred-and-sixth year, when the moon shows a sliver no almanac keeps, a child of the hedge shall come down the smuggler's road with winter in her pockets and no master's name in her mouth.*

Coral read it twice. Then a third time, slowly, the way you touch a bruise to confirm it hurts.

*Winter in her pockets.* Her coat pockets held, at present, a twist of rowan bark, three iron nails, a lump of camphor resin, and a sachet of snow that never melted — Aldith's parting gift, or curse, or both, pressed into her hands on the night she ran. Winter in her pockets. *No master's name in her mouth.* She had refused, flatly and publicly, to call herself anyone's apprentice anymore. She introduced herself as a guide. As nothing.

The storm muttered louder. Somewhere above, thunder cleared its throat.

Her finger found the next line, and her finger was shaking, and she hated it for shaking.

*She is red-haired and river-eyed and carries the mountains' ear in her skull. She will believe herself free. She is not free. She is the Salt-Bearer, and the Salt-Bearer descends.*

The fire popped. Outside, Marta Voss laughed at something one of the teamsters said, a bright ordinary human sound, and it seemed to come from another world — a world where books stayed dry and dates meant nothing and nobody's whole life was written down a hundred years before she was born.

Coral sat down on a fallen roof-beam without deciding to. Her legs had simply stopped being interested in standing.

*Red-haired and river-eyed.* Her mother had been black-haired; her father nobody spoke of. The red came from further back, from a grandmother dead before Coral could walk, a woman who existed now only as a name on a grave-marker in a village Coral had fled at seventeen and sworn never to see again. *Carries the mountains' ear in her skull.* There it was, the secret she'd guarded since childhood, the thing that had made Aldith look at her sometimes with an expression Coral had never been able to name until this moment — and the name for it, she understood now, was *recognition*. Aldith had known. Aldith had read this page, or one like it, and watched a red-haired child talk to rocks, and known exactly what was growing in her garden.

And she'd never said a word.

The fury rose hot and welcome, because fury was easier than the other thing, the vast cold thing underneath it. Coral gripped the book and let herself be angry, and in being angry almost missed the date.

Almost.

Because there, at the bottom of the bled-ink page, in a smaller hand, the same hand but older, wearier, pressed hard enough into the paper to emboss it: a date. Day, month, year. The old reckoning, the one the mountain villages still used, the one Coral had counted in her head every morning on the road.

Tonight.

Not *in* the hundred-and-sixth year. Tonight. This specific night, this specific stretch of hours, this rain just now arriving over Cerenne's shoulder — all of it written down before anyone alive had been born, waiting in a niche for a red-haired runaway to walk in out of the weather and pick it up.

"The Salt-Bearer descends," she read aloud, and her voice sounded strange in the vaulted dark, small and very young. "Descends where? Descends to *what*?"

The book answered.

It was not a dramatic answer. That was somehow the worst part. There was no flash, no voice, no wind through the broken door. There was simply a sound — soft, liquid, intimate — like a drop falling into a full cup. Coral looked down and saw that the book had begun, gently and without hurry, to bleed.

Seawater welled up between the swollen pages the way blood wells through a bandage. It ran over her thumbs, warm as tears, and it smelled of deep water and old ships and something beneath both, something mineral and patient that she recognized with a lurch, because it was the smell of the shrine-niche, the smell the mountain made when it leaned toward old holy things. The drops fell from the book's lower edge onto the flagstones and did not splash. They *sat*, round and perfect, and began — Coral watched it happen, her heart slamming against her ribs — to arrange themselves. Into a line. Then a curve. Then letters.

She couldn't read them. They weren't in any alphabet she knew — angular, flowing, written as if the writer had learned language underwater. But the mountain heard them. She felt it do so, felt the whole mass of the Thornfells shift its attention downward through miles of granite, the way a house shifts when someone opens a door far below. Every hair on her arms stood up. The fire guttered though there was no draft.

Then the letters dissolved, the drops slid apart, and the flagstones were only flagstones, wet with ordinary rainwater that had blown in through the broken door. Except it wasn't raining yet. Except the water tasted of salt.

Coral closed the book. Her hands were steady now, which surprised her; terror, it turned out, eventually ran out of ways to shake you and settled instead into something colder and more useful. She wrapped the book in her spare shirt, stuffed it into her pack, and told herself she was keeping it as evidence, as curiosity, as salvage to sell — told herself three lies in quick succession and let them stand, because the truth was simpler and worse.

The truth was that a book a century old had described her coat pockets, her eyes, her secrets, and her night, and had then spoken in a language the mountain understood. Whatever descended next, it was going to descend with her in it.

"Coral!" Dagny's voice, from the doorway, rough with wind. "Storm's on us. You alive in there?"

"Alive," Coral called back, and was mildly astonished to find it true. She banked the fire, checked the tarps out of pure habit, and stepped out into the first fat drops of the deluge.

The caravan huddled under canvas, lanterns swinging, mules drooping. Ordinary. Human. Marta waved her toward the big wagon and its dry corner, and Coral went, and ate, and answered questions about tomorrow's route with the easy competence that was her armor. *The pass forks at the cairn; we bear left; the left fork floods, so we cross before midmorning.* All true. All safe. None of it the truth that mattered, which sat in her pack like a second heartbeat, seeping faintly, patiently, through the linen.

Sleep took everyone else by degrees. Coral lay awake listening to the rain hammer the canvas and feeling, distinctly, the mountain lean toward her again — closer now, hungrier, or perhaps just lonelier, and unable to tell the difference was perhaps the most frightening thing of all.

Near midnight, when the storm was at its worst, she heard footsteps on the pilgrim road. Unhurried footsteps, in this weather, coming up from the south — from the lowlands, from the direction of everything she'd run from. She sat up in the dark, hand on her knife, and watched a figure resolve out of the rain: a man, thin as a folded letter, carrying no lantern and needing none, walking the flooded stones as if he'd memorized them long ago.

He stopped at the edge of the firelight, pushed back his soaked hood, and looked directly — precisely, inevitably, the way a date looks at you from a written page — at Coral.

"You've got the book," he said. "I've come about the book."

* * *

She should have woken Marta. She knew that too, later, cataloguing exits after fires. Instead Coral stood in the lee of the lead wagon with rain streaming off the canvas above them, knife drawn but angled politely, studying the stranger who had walked out of a century-old prophecy's margins as if the margins were a door.

He was younger than his stoop. Thirty, perhaps, with the pallor of a man who worked indoors and the ink-stained fingers to match — a scribe's fingers, a clerk's fingers, nails trimmed close and one thumbnail permanently ridged from a pen-grip. His clothes had been fine once: a scholar's coat, good wool, now patched with less-good wool and hemmed by someone in a hurry. Water ran off him and steamed faintly in the cold, and Coral noticed that too, filed it away with everything else. Ordinary rain doesn't steam off a man unless the man is warmer than a man should be.

"Who are you?" she said.

"Wren." He said it like a man handing over a coin he suspected was clipped. "Formerly of a court you haven't heard of, and if you have, you'll want your knife closer than that."

"I've heard of every court in the Thornfells."

"No," Wren said, with terrible mildness, "you haven't." He glanced at her pack, at the shape of the wrapped book beneath the flap, and something moved through his face — hunger, grief, recognition, all three wearing one expression like a mask with three faces carved on it. "You read tonight's page."

It wasn't a question, and Coral's silence confirmed it anyway, and she hated how legible she apparently was.

"Then you know what you are," he said. "Salt-Bearer. Red-haired, river-eyed, winter in her pockets. I'd offer condolences, but in my experience the newly prophesied prefer facts." He wiped rain from his eyes with a gesture so weary and practiced it looked ceremonial. "So. Facts. Everything in that book is true. I know because I've spent six years checking, and because I helped copy parts of it, long ago, in a place that doesn't appear on your maps. And because the woman whose soul was sold beneath this mountain a hundred years ago has been waiting all that time for someone with your face to come down the smuggler's road — and the moon, Coral Brightwater, has already begun to show its thirteenth sliver."

The rain filled the pause. Coral became aware, distantly, that her hands had gone cold around the knife, and that the mountain was leaning toward them both now, listening, and that some final door in her life — the one marked *free*, the one she'd believed in with her whole stubborn heart since the night she ran — had just swung quietly shut behind her.

"How," she said, "do you know my name?"

Wren smiled, and it was the saddest thing she'd seen in years.

"Same way I know yours," he said, "is the wrong answer, so I'll give you the right one. Your grandmother sold a soul to the Undercourt to buy this valley's safety. The ledger that records the sale also records the buyer's line. Yours is the last name on it." He turned up his collar against the rain. "Now — are you going to wake your caravan, or are you going to hear the rest?"
