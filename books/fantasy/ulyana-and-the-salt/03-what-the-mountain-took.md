## 3. What the Mountain Took

Wren had been following the caravan for three days before he decided it was time to be seen.

Three days of cold camps and colder arithmetic, counting mules through the fog on the lower slopes of the Thornfells, counting guards, counting the particular way the young woman at the head of the column moved — always a half-step ahead of the lead mule, always with her bare hand pressed flat against the rock as though she were taking its pulse. He had watched her find water where no water showed. He had watched her talk a rockslide out of falling, or at least talk it into falling somewhere else. He had watched her lie to the caravan master about the pass ahead with the easy fluency of someone who had been lying since before she could write.

He knew that fluency intimately. He had once been paid by the word for it.

So on the third evening, when the caravan made camp in the shadow of a ridge the maps called the Grey Shoulder and the people who lived under it called nothing at all because they did not like to name things that listened, Wren walked down out of the pines with his hands visible and his satchel open at his hip, and let them see him coming.

The dogs found him first. Then the guards. Then, last and most important, the girl.

She came around the cookfire with a ladle still in her hand, which he noted with professional appreciation: a weapon no one could confiscate, an excuse no one could question. Up close she was younger than he'd guessed — nineteen, perhaps twenty, with salt-white streaks in her dark hair that were not age but something else, something he had seen only once before, long ago, on a woman who had gone beneath the mountain and never come back up.

"You're lost," she said.

"I'm not." He spread his hands wider, showing his palms empty, showing the ink stains that no amount of lye would ever scrub from them. "My name is Wren. I was a scribe of the Undercourt, before I was exiled from it. And you are Coral Brightwater, formerly apprentice to Mother Aldith of Hollowmere, currently employed by men whose cargo I would very much like to see taxed."

The ladle came up. Not fast — deliberate. Behind her, one of the guards shifted, and the caravan master's voice cut across the camp asking what in all the hells was happening, and Coral ignored every one of them with the practiced ease of a person accustomed to being the only competent thing in any given room.

"Undercourt," she repeated. She said it the way people said *plague* or *debt* — a word kept sharp by disuse. "There's no such place."

"There is," Wren said. "It's under us. Roughly four hundred feet down, if my sense of the stone hasn't rotted along with everything else. It has streets. It has laws. It has a king who drowned so thoroughly that drowning became the family business." He smiled, and knew even as he did it that the smile was wrong, too many teeth, the smile of a man who had spent years among things that smiled back. "And three days ago, in a waystation on the Cold Larch road, you opened a book that was not meant to be opened, and it bled seawater onto your hands, and you have not slept properly since."

The ladle did not waver. But her eyes did — a flicker, quick as a fish turning, there and gone. He had interrogated souls older than kingdoms; he knew what guilt looked like when it tried to stand very still.

"Anyone could know that," she said. "You could have followed me. You did follow me."

"I did follow you. For three days. In weather that would make a saint weep, carrying provisions I can no longer afford, to deliver a message I could have shouted from a hilltop." He lowered his hands slowly. "If I wanted to hurt you, Brightwater, I've had seventy-two hours and a crossbow's worth of opportunity. What I want is stranger than that. I want to help you."

"Nobody wants to help anybody."

"No," he agreed. "Which is why you should ask what I want instead, and then decide whether our wants can be made to wear the same coat."

She studied him for a long moment. The firelight did things to her face — made her look older, made the white streaks in her hair gleam like veins of ore. He had the sudden, unwelcome thought that she looked like her grandmother. He had never met the grandmother. He had read her signature often enough.

"What do you want?" she asked.

"To go home," Wren said, and heard the truth of it land in his own chest like a dropped coin. "Even if home is a drowned city full of dead men's paperwork. Especially then."

* * *

They gave him a place at the fire because Coral told them to, and they gave Coral authority over strangers because the mountains had taught them to. The caravan master grumbled about spies and cutthroats until Coral pointed out, mildly, that a spy would have picked a warmer rock to sit behind, and that settled it. Smugglers were pragmatic people. They respected competence and distrusted enthusiasm, and Wren made sure to display exactly one of those.

He waited until the watches were set and the mules were hobbled and the ordinary noises of a camp had settled into their nighttime grammar. Then he took the book from his satchel — not the girl's book, hers was wrapped in oilcloth inside her bedroll where she thought no one knew about it; this was another copy, or rather the other half of one, pages that matched hers the way a broken plate matches its missing shard — and laid it on the ground between himself and Coral where the firelight could reach it.

Her breath went out of her in a small, controlled sound.

"Where did you get that?"

"The same place you got yours, presumably. From someone who wanted it found." He turned the cover back. The pages were stained brown with old brine, the ink gone the color of dried blood, but the script underneath was still legible to anyone trained in the old hand — and he could see her reading it, lips moving slightly, translating without quite knowing she was doing it. Hedge-witch training. Aldith had taught her more than herbs, then. Interesting.

"It's a ledger of debts," Wren said. "That's all prophecy ever is. Someone writes down what will be owed, and the mountain collects on schedule. Your page — tonight's date, your description, the words *the Salt-Bearer descends* — it isn't a fortune. It's an invoice."

"A soul was sold here." Her voice had gone flat, careful. "You said that. On the ridge. You said a soul was sold beneath the mountain generations ago, and that's why you're here."

"I said it because it's true, and because you'd already half-read it yourself and deserved to hear it from someone who wasn't a book bleeding on your hands." He turned several pages, past columns of names in the old hand, past entries struck through and entries that seemed to writhe when looked at directly, and stopped at one near the middle of the gathering. The stain here was different — darker, fresher, as though the sea had gotten into this page more recently than the others. Or as though the page had been bleeding longer.

A name. A date. A price.

Coral leaned forward despite herself. He watched her read it. Watched her lips form the name — *Ulyana* — and watched something move behind her eyes that was not recognition, exactly. Recognition requires memory. This was older than memory. This was the body remembering what the mind had never been told.

"That name," she said quietly.

"Belonged to a salt-witch," Wren said. "One of the last of them. A hundred and six years ago, give or take a winter, she walked down through the Tollgate of Teeth and did not walk back up. The entry says she was sold. Entries are usually honest; it's the people who aren't." He paused. "The buyer was the Undercourt. The seller—" he hesitated, weighing how much truth a person could carry in one night, and decided she had already been carrying worse alone, "—the seller signed in blood, which is the custom, and the signature reads *Maren Brightwater*."

The fire cracked. Somewhere up-slope, a mule stamped and was still.

"My grandmother," Coral said. Not a question. Her hands had closed in her lap, knuckles pale. "I never met her. Aldith said she died before I was born. Aldith said a lot of things." The flatness in her voice had developed an edge, and the edge had developed a direction, and Wren recognized with some sympathy the particular fury of a person discovering that the ground they stood on had been sold out from under them before they ever learned to stand.

"Maren Brightwater bought something with that sale," he said. "Every village in these mountains stands safe because of it. The pact of the salt — you'll have heard the word spoken carefully, if you grew up here. Crops that don't fail. Mines that don't collapse. Wolves that keep to the high country. The mountain hungers, and the pact feeds it, and everyone sleeps well and doesn't ask what's on the menu."

"And Ulyana was on the menu."

"For a hundred and six years." He closed the ledger gently, the way one closes a door on a sickroom. "Until a book washed up in a waystation and put your name next to tonight's date. The mountain, it seems, has decided the debt is due. Or someone has decided for it."

Coral was silent for a long time. He let her be. Around them the camp breathed — snores, the shift of sentries, wind worrying the pine crowns. He used the silence to study her properly, the way he had once studied documents: for forgery, for fraud, for the tell-tale signs of a thing not being what it claimed. And what he found, in the set of her shoulders and the white in her hair and the way her thumb kept moving over the spot on her palm where the seawater had touched it, was that she was not pretending anything. She was simply a liar of the ordinary human kind — lying to her employers about the roads, lying to herself about why she'd run — and underneath the lies was something inconveniently genuine.

He had been afraid, walking down out of the pines, that she would be a fraud. Frauds he knew how to handle. Sincerity was going to be a problem.

"Why you?" she asked finally. "Why does an exiled scribe care whether some dead witch's debt gets collected? What did the Undercourt do to you?"

"They didn't do anything to me." He laughed, short and unamused. "That's rather the point. I did it to myself, and they were gracious enough to make it permanent. I was an archivist, Brightwater. Keeper of the third gallery of the Ledger. My whole life was names and dates and the careful fiction that recording a thing is the same as understanding it. And then one night I pulled an entry to verify a transcription, and I read the terms of the pact — the actual terms, not the story the priests tell — and I discovered that I had spent thirty years keeping books for a protection racket." He turned his hands over, palms up, the ink stains ghost-pale in the firelight. "I said so. Out loud. To the Herald of the Drowned King. There are smarter ways to resign."

"So you ran."

"I ran," he agreed. "Up through the Tollgate, out into the air, into a world I hadn't touched in thirty years. And I have spent six years telling myself I was free, right up until three days ago, when I felt the mountain turn over in its sleep and remembered that freedom is just exile with better scenery." He met her eyes. "I know the way down, Coral. Every gate, every toll, every street of standing water. I know what the dead trade in and what the living are worth there. And I am offering to take you — because you are going down whether I come or not, and you will die without me, and I will spend whatever's left of my life knowing it."

She looked at him for a long moment. The firelight moved. When she spoke again, her voice had changed — stripped of the caravan-hardness, down to something younger and warier and more honest.

"You're lying about something," she said. "There's a piece you're not giving me. I can always tell. Aldith said I got it from my grandmother — the knack for spotting a false note."

Wren felt the smile try to happen again and this time let it, tired and true.

"Of course there is," he said. "I'm a scribe. We're built out of omissions the way cathedrals are built out of stone. But here's what I'll offer you, since you've earned it: everything I've told you tonight is true. Everything I've left out is true too. And the piece I'm not giving you — you'll have it before we reach the Tollgate, because you'll need it, and because—" he paused, surprised to find the next words cost him something, "—because I think you and I are the same kind of animal, Brightwater. We lie beautifully, and we mean well, and we're both going home to a place that exiled us. Liars recognize liars. It's the one honest thing we do."

Something in her posture eased — not trust, nothing so foolish as trust, but the particular relaxation of a person who has decided that a known dishonesty is safer than an unknown sincerity.

"All right," Coral said. "You walk with us as far as the gate. You carry your own pack. And Wren—" she picked up the ledger, weighed it in her hand as though measuring how much a hundred years of debt might mass, "—when we get wherever we're going, you tell me the rest. All of it. Even the parts that make you look bad."

"Especially those," he said. "They're the shortest list."

She almost smiled. Then she stood, tucked the ledger under her arm with the unconscious possessiveness of someone who had already begun to think of it as hers, and went to check the hobbled mules, and Wren sat alone by the fire and admitted to himself, in the privacy of his own skull, the omission he had promised her.

Not the whole truth. Not yet. But the shape of it: that the ledger she now carried had not washed up in any waystation by accident. That he had placed it there — or rather, that he had paid a river-ferryman a shameful sum to place it there, after six years of searching for a descendant of Maren Brightwater and finding, at last, a runaway girl with salt in her hair and a knack for spotting false notes.

Because the entry beside Ulyana's name had a second line, one he had not shown her, written in the old hand in ink that was still wet after a hundred years:

*The Salt-Bearer descends. The debt is called. And the witness above remembers.*

Wren fed the fire another branch and watched the sparks climb toward a sky he still hadn't learned to trust, and told himself that omission was not the same as betrayal — that he was guiding her, not using her, that the ends wore the same coat as the beginnings.

He was, after all, a scribe. He could make almost anything read like the truth.

Above the Grey Shoulder, low over the notch of the pass, the moon was rising — and Wren, who had spent thirty years keeping the Court's calendars and knew every moon the almanacs predicted, counted its light twice, and frowned, and counted again.

There was a sliver in it that should not have been there.

Thirteen, where twelve belonged.
