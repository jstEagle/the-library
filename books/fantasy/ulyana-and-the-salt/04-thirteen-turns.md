## 4. Thirteen Turns

The moon was wrong.

Coral noticed it first as a smudge of light through the waystation's broken shutters, a brightness that fell at the wrong angle across the floorboards where she'd spread her bedroll. She had slept under open sky for three years, and a body learned the moon the way it learned its own heartbeat — when it should rise, how fat it should be, which shoulder of the mountain it would clear by midnight. This one cleared the ridge an hour early and hung there like a coin someone had bitten and found wanting.

She went out into the cold to look properly.

The valley below the waystation was a bowl of blue dark, the smugglers' fires banked to embers in the hollow where they camped. Above it all the moon rode high — and around its familiar silver disc, faint as breath on glass, curved a thirteenth sliver. A thin crescent nested inside the crescent, turned the wrong way, shining with a light that didn't quite match. Not a halo. Not ice-crystal glare. A sliver, distinct, patient, as if the moon had grown an extra tooth.

Coral stood in the frost until her feet went numb, counting turns on her fingers like a child. Twelve. There were always twelve. The almanacs said twelve; the tide-tables said twelve; every hedge-witch from here to the coast set her charms by twelve, and if there had ever been a thirteenth turn, some grandmother would have sung about it, some crone would have muttered warnings over it, some drunk carter would have blamed his lost mule on it.

Nothing sang. Nothing warned. The extra sliver simply hung there, and when she blinked it did not go away.

Behind her, inside the waystation, Wren stirred in his sleep and murmured something in a language that made the fire gutter. She had heard him talk in his sleep twice now, and both times the words had the same quality: old, wet, formal, like a court summons read aloud underwater. She had decided not to ask. There was a great deal about Wren she had decided not to ask, and he returned the courtesy, which was the only reason their arrangement held together.

*The Salt-Bearer descends.* The book lay wrapped in oilcloth in her pack, and she could feel it from here, or imagined she could — a cold weight against her spine, heavier than paper had any right to be. It hadn't bled again since that first night. But she'd stopped trusting it to stay quiet the way you stop trusting a dog that's bitten once.

She went back inside and did not sleep.

* * *

They came down out of the pass at midmorning into Saltmere, the last village before the deep fells, and Coral felt the change in the air before she saw the smoke. Saltmere always smelled of brine and woodsmoke and the particular sourness of sheep too long indoors, but today there was something else threaded through it — a hush, a held-breath quality, the smell of a village pretending everything was ordinary.

The smugglers unloaded their contraband with practiced speed while Coral walked the one street, taking stock. Doors closed that usually stood open. Children called indoors mid-game. At the well, three women drew water without speaking, and when one of them saw Coral her eyes flicked up — not to Coral's face, but past her shoulder, to the sky, and then away.

"You've seen it," Coral said.

The woman flinched. "Seen what, dear."

"The moon."

"Moons are moons." The woman hauled her bucket up with a creak of rope. "Best get your business done and your people gone before dark, is all I'll say. Old Marta's the one for questions, if you must ask them." She nodded up the street toward a crooked house with a roof of turf and a fence strung with drying herbs, then hurried off with her bucket slopping.

Marta. Coral knew the name the way you know names in small places — the crone who lived at the end of the street, who sold charms against toothache and told fortunes she claimed to invent. Every village had one. Coral had been apprenticed to a better one, once, and had run from her, and the thought of Aldith rose now like bile and she pushed it down where it lived.

But the moon was wrong, and the book was in her pack, and pride was a luxury she'd never been able to afford on the road.

* * *

Old Marta's house smelled of tallow and dried yarrow and something underneath both — a mineral smell, sharp and clean, that Coral's skin recognized before her mind did. Salt. Not cooking salt. Sea salt, or something older than sea.

The crone herself was small and brown and wrinkled as a walnut, sitting by her hearth with a cat in her lap that had one eye and no manners. She looked up when Coral ducked through the low door, and her face did something complicated — surprise, then recognition, then a grief so old it had worn smooth, like a stone in a stream.

"Well," Marta said. "Look what the road washed up."

"You know me?"

"I knew your grandmother." The cat was put aside; the crone's hands folded in her lap, knuckles like river stones. "You have her chin. Her stubborn way of standing in a doorway like the world owes you room. Sit down, girl, before you fall down. You look like you haven't slept."

"I haven't." Coral sat on the offered stool, perched on its edge. "There's a thirteenth sliver on the moon. No almanac predicts it. My whole caravan is pretending they haven't seen it, and this village has shut its doors like a funeral's coming. And three nights ago I opened a book that bled seawater onto my hands and told me—" She stopped. Told me my own description. Told me *descends*. Told me things I haven't said aloud to anyone, including the scribe sleeping in my bedroll's shadow.

Marta watched her stop, and waited, and the silence stretched until Coral heard herself finish anyway:

"—told me I'm the Salt-Bearer. Whatever that is."

The fire popped. The one-eyed cat yawned. Outside, a cart passed, and its driver was singing, and the song cut off abruptly halfway down the street as though he'd remembered himself.

"Your grandmother," Marta said at last, "was named Yeva Brightwater. Hedge-witch, like you were going to be. Like you ran from being." Her eyes were pale and steady. "And sixty years ago, in the winter the fever came down the fells and took one child in three, Yeva Brightwater walked up the mountain alone and did not come back for nine days. When she came back, the fever stopped. Just — stopped. Mid-kill. Children who were grey and rattling at dawn sat up asking for bread by noon."

Coral's mouth had gone dry. "How?"

"There's a court under the mountain." Marta said it flatly, the way you'd say *there's a river under the bridge*, no wonder in it at all, only weight. "The Undercourt. Drowned things, dead things, a king with a crown of whalebone. Older than the villages. Hungry the way winter is hungry — not cruel, just empty. Before your grandmother, the mountain took what it liked. Livestock. Travelers. Once, whole hamlets, back in the oldest days, though folk don't write that part down anymore. Your grandmother went down and made a bargain."

"A bargain," Coral repeated. The word tasted like the book had tasted, like brine.

"She gave them a soul. One soul, bound in service, forever, to keep the pact. In exchange the mountain's hunger sleeps so long as the pact holds. Sixty years, girl. Sixty years of full bellies and safe roads and children living to grow old, bought with one soul." Marta leaned forward. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? Your grandmother saved this whole valley. Everyone in it. Everyone in it *since*."

"And the soul," Coral said. Her voice came out strange, thin, distant, as if someone else were speaking through her. "Whose soul."

The crone looked at her for a long moment, and whatever she saw in Coral's face made her answer gently, which was somehow worse.

"Her daughter's."

The hearth-fire seemed very loud.

"Yeva had two daughters," Marta went on, soft as ash falling. "The elder died of the fever's first wave, before the bargain. The younger — Maren — was strong, healthy, twenty years old, a witch's blood thick in her. A soul like that buys deep. Buys long." She paused. "Maren fought her, they say. Fought her all the way up the mountain. But Yeva was the stronger witch, and grief does terrible arithmetic. One child already buried. One child traded. And thousands saved."

"Maren," Coral said. The name meant nothing and everything. "What happened to Maren? After."

"After?" Marta's brow creased. "There is no after, child. She went under the mountain. She serves there still. That's what sold means."

"No — Maren. Did she have children? Before?"

Understanding moved across the old woman's face like weather. She reached out and took Coral's hand, and her grip was surprisingly strong, and her palm was dry and warm and trembling slightly.

"Oh," Marta said. "Oh, you're hers. You're Maren's line. You're—"

"My mother never talked about it. She died when I was six. The woman who raised me after—" Aldith, and the anger came up hot and fast, crowding out the cold. "She never said a word either. Nobody says a word. Sixty years and everyone keeps this beautiful secret, and I find out from a bleeding book and a stranger's crone that my own blood is chained under a mountain."

"It wasn't cruelty, keeping it quiet. Names have power. If folk forgot the bargain's shape, maybe the mountain would too, someday—"

"The mountain doesn't forget." Coral pulled her hand free and stood, and the little room tilted around her. "The book doesn't forget. It's been waiting a hundred years for me specifically. It has my *name*." She was shaking. She made herself stop. "Why me? Why now? If the pact holds, why does anything need descending?"

Marta was quiet for a moment. When she spoke, she looked at the fire instead of at Coral, and her voice dropped into the cadence of recitation, of something learned and repeated until it wore a groove.

"The pact holds while the moon holds twelve. That was the term. Thirteen turns, and the ledger comes due — the debt renews, or it's called in entire. Yeva's bargain had a mercy stitched into it, because even in the worst winter of her life she couldn't help leaving a door open. The soul that seals the pact can be reclaimed — but only by her own blood. Only by a descendant walking down of their own will, before the thirteenth turn completes. A replacement can be offered. Or the page can be torn out, and everything ends." She finally looked up. "The moon showed its thirteenth sliver last night. You have until it wanes, girl. Days. Maybe less."

Coral stood very still.

Somewhere beneath the fury — and the fury was enormous, a red ocean of it, rage at Yeva Brightwater whom she'd never met, who had fed her own daughter to a drowned court like paying a bill, rage at Aldith for every withheld truth, rage at a whole valley of shut doors and averted eyes — somewhere beneath all that, a colder thing was settling into place. The book had known. Had waited a century on a shelf in a ruined waystation for exactly her, exactly now. Fate wasn't abstract anymore. It had dates. It had her description. It had, apparently, been managing her whole life from behind the scenery — every road she'd chosen, every apprenticeship she'd fled, every restless mile, all of it perhaps just the long approach to a door under a mountain.

"I never met her," Coral said. "My grandmother. I never met my grandmother, and I hate her, and I don't even know if I'm allowed to."

"Grief comes in any order it likes," Marta said. "Hate's a fine place to start. It'll keep you walking when fear would stop you." She rose, joints cracking like green wood, and shuffled to a chest in the corner. From under wool blankets and a bundle of dried eels she brought out a small object wrapped in waxed linen, and unwrapped it with the care of someone handling an egg that might hatch.

A key. Iron, old, black with age — but not shaped like any key Coral had seen. Its teeth were tiny carved letters, worn nearly smooth, and when Marta held it out, the mineral smell of it filled Coral's head: sea-salt and deep cold stone.

"Yeva left this with the village headwoman, to pass down. Headwoman to headwoman, in case a descendant ever came asking. None ever did." Marta pressed it into Coral's hand. It was cold as a fish and heavy as guilt. "It opens the tollgate below. The gate won't take bribes or lies — only memory, they say, and blood. Your blood is the key, but the gate still takes its toll. Be sure you can afford what it asks."

"What did my grandmother pay?"

Marta smiled, and it was the saddest smile Coral had ever seen on a living face.

"She paid the memory of her daughter's laugh. Carried it down the mountain and handed it over at the gate, and walked home lighter, they say, and never smiled quite right again for the rest of her life." The crone folded Coral's fingers closed over the iron. "That's the arithmetic of the Undercourt, Salt-Bearer. Everything costs. Especially the things you love."

Outside, the afternoon had gone strangely dim. Clouds were stacking over the fells in slow grey towers, and beneath them the villagers moved through their shuttered streets like people wading upstream. High above the peaks, patient as a wound, the thirteenth sliver rode its impossible orbit around the moon.

Coral walked back toward the waystation with the key against her sternum and the book against her spine, and between them, like a stone in a sling, her own furious heart. Behind her, Marta stood in her doorway watching, and ahead, in the waystation's shadow, Wren had come out to wait for her — arms crossed, expression carefully blank, the look of a man who already knew everything she'd just learned and was waiting to see how she'd carry it.

"You talked to the crone," he said. Not a question.

"You knew," Coral said. "About the soul. About whose it was. You've known since the pass."

Wren held her gaze, and for once the court scribe's polished mask slipped, and underneath it was something tired and honest and afraid.

"Yes," he said. "And there's worse news than that, if you want it. Because the gate isn't the only thing waiting for you below — and the King already knows your name."
