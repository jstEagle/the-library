## 5. The Tollgate of Teeth

The gate had been waiting for them longer than either of them liked to admit.

Wren felt it before he saw it — a pressure behind the eyes, like the moment before a sneeze, like the hush in a courtroom when the verdict is about to be read. The smuggler's path narrowed to a slot between two walls of black stone, and there, filling the gap from floor to ceiling, stood the Tollgate of Teeth.

He had drawn it a hundred times from memory, back when memory was still his to spend. In the archives of the Undercourt there was a folio — *Survey of the Thresholds*, bound in eel-skin, catalogued under *Do Not Circulate* — and on its ninth page an ink-wash rendering of this very gate, done by some long-dead surveyor whose hand had shaken. Wren had always assumed the shaking was artistic affectation. Now, standing before the thing itself, he understood it was portraiture.

The teeth were real teeth. Fossilized, yes — opalized at the edges, shot through with veins of blue and bone-white — but real. They had belonged to something enormous, something that had died with its jaws open, and the mountain had built its door out of the corpse's last argument. Each tooth was the length of a man's forearm. They interlocked in rows, upper and lower, and between them gaped a passage just wide enough for one person walking sideways, if that person did not mind brushing against things that had once crunched.

Above the arch, carved deep into the stone in the old tongue — the true old tongue, not the courtly corruption Wren had been trained in — ran a single line:

*WHAT ENTERS MUST LEAVE SOMETHING OF ITSELF BEHIND.*

"Charming," Coral said.

She said it lightly, the way she said everything lightly, and Wren had learned enough of her in three days of hard travel to know that lightness was her armor, worn so long it had grown to the skin. She stood with her hands on her hips, chin tipped up, studying the gate the way she studied everything: as though daring it to be more clever than she was.

It was going to win that dare. But he let her have the moment.

"You knew," she said, without turning. "You've known since the waystation. This is why you wanted to come down through Thornfell Pass instead of the salt road."

"I wanted to come this way because the salt road ends in a customs house full of men who would very much like to talk to me." He kept his voice dry. "But yes. I knew."

"And you didn't say anything."

"Would it have changed your mind?"

Coral was quiet for a moment. Wind came down the pass and stirred the loose strands of hair escaping her braid, and beyond her, far below, Wren could hear the caravan's mules complaining in their picket lines — they'd made camp a mile back, because animals would not come near this place. That was another thing the surveyor's folio mentioned. *Beasts refuse the threshold. Handlers must proceed alone.* He wondered what the mules knew that they didn't.

"No," Coral admitted finally. "It wouldn't have." She turned to look at him, and her eyes were the grey-green of shallow water over slate, and they were sharper than any blade he'd ever seen a courtier carry. "But I'd have liked to be asked."

*Two liars recognizing each other.* That was what he'd thought the night he intercepted them on the switchback below Greywater, when he'd watched her lie to the caravan master about the safety of the high route with such fluent, easy confidence that he'd nearly applauded. He recognized the craft. He'd practiced it himself for eleven years in the archives of the Drowned King, lying with ink instead of breath, forging provenance for souls that had no right to change hands.

He owed her better than another lie. Whether he could afford one was a different question.

"The toll is a memory," he said. "One memory, chosen by the gate, not by you. You don't get to decide what it takes. You walk through, the teeth read you, and somewhere in the passage—" he gestured at the narrow dark between the fossilized jaws, "—it takes its payment. Most people don't even feel it happen. They just come out the other side missing something they can't name."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've been through before."

That landed. He watched it land — saw her straighten slightly, recalibrate, file it away. She was quick. Faster than anyone he'd met above ground, and he included the King's own heralds, who were quick the way adders are quick.

"When?" she asked.

"Eleven years ago. Going the other direction." He looked past her at the teeth rather than at her face. It was easier. "I was leaving the Undercourt. I paid my toll and I climbed out into daylight for the first time since I was nine years old, and I got two miles down the mountain before I realized I couldn't remember why I'd left."

Coral frowned. "You forgot your reason?"

"I forgot my *reason*, plural, all of them, every single thing that had driven me up those stairs and out that gate. I remembered that I was angry. I remembered that I was afraid. But the *why* of it — gone. Eaten." He smiled, and knew it was the thin smile, the one that didn't reach anywhere important. "I spent six years trying to reconstruct it. Interviewing people who'd known me. Reading my own letters, which might as well have been written by a stranger. Do you know what it's like to find a document in your own hand describing a crime, and feel nothing? To know, intellectually, that you were furious, and to have the fury itself be missing, like a tooth pulled from your own mouth?"

"Yes," Coral said quietly. "Actually, I do."

And he believed her. Whatever she'd run from — the hedge-witch mistress she never named, the apprenticeship she spoke of only in deflections — there was a pulled tooth in her mouth too. He'd heard it whistling in her silences for three days.

* * *

They told the caravan master they were scouting ahead, which was true in the way most useful lies are true. Then they walked the last mile alone, because the Teeth would not suffer witnesses. The surveyor's folio had been clear on that as well: *Those who watch the toll unpaid may be called upon to pay it unasked.* Wren had no intention of finding out whether that was superstition or statute. In his experience, beneath the mountain, there was no difference.

The light was failing. That was deliberate — he'd timed their approach for dusk, because the teeth glowed faintly in darkness, opal-fire kindling along their edges, and he did not want Coral seeing them lit until she was already committed. Cowardly, perhaps. Kind, possibly. With him the two had never been easy to separate.

At the threshold he stopped and turned to face her one last time.

"Rules," he said. "Don't stop inside. Don't speak inside — the teeth echo, and echoes are how they choose what to take. And whatever you feel, however much it hurts, keep walking. The ones the gate keeps are the ones who stop."

"The ones it *keeps*?" Coral's voice cracked upward. "You didn't mention keeping."

"There's a lot I haven't mentioned. You'll want to save your questions for the other side." He hesitated. Then, because the vulnerability was coming for him anyway, one way or another, he chose to meet it standing: "Coral. If I come out of there wrong — if I've lost something that makes me dangerous, or useless, or cruel — remember that I paid it for you. Try to remember that."

Her expression did something complicated. Suspicion and sympathy wrestling for the same ground, neither winning.

"Why are you doing this?" she asked. "Really. Eleven years running from that place, and you walk back in for a girl you met three days ago on a smuggler's road. Nobody does that."

"No," Wren agreed. "Nobody does."

"Wren."

He looked at her. Dusk had thickened around them; the first faint glow was waking in the teeth, blue as drowned starlight, and it lit her face from below so that she seemed to stand in shallow water. For a moment he almost told her. Almost said: *because I wrote the entry in the Ledger myself, the one that says Ulyana, sold, and I was a child then but a child is old enough to be guilty forever; because every forgery I ever committed was a prayer that someone would come asking after her; because you are the first person in eleven years to ask.*

Instead he said: "Because I'm tired of being a man who knows things and does nothing. Go."

And Coral Brightwater — seventeen years old, runaway, liar, Salt-Bearer, though none of them yet understood what that word would cost — squared her shoulders, turned sideways, and stepped into the jaws of the mountain.

* * *

Wren followed.

Inside, the world became sound. That was the first thing he'd forgotten — or perhaps the gate had taken it from him last time, a small mercy, returned now with interest. The passage between the teeth was lined with them, row upon receding row, and each tooth hummed. Not a note, exactly. A *name*. He could feel them reading him as he pressed through the narrow dark, brushing stone that had been living flesh, the humming rising and falling like fingers leafing through a ledger, searching for the page with his number on it.

*What enters must leave something of itself behind.*

He kept walking. He did not speak. The cold coming off the teeth was not the cold of stone; it was the cold of deep water, of the specific dark at the bottom of a well, and it slid through his clothes as though cloth were a rumor it declined to believe.

Then the humming stopped.

All of it, at once. Silence absolute, silence with weight, and Wren knew — the way you know a hand has closed on your collar — that the gate had found its page.

The taking, when it came, was gentle. That was the obscenity of it. No pain, no tearing. Just a soft unlatching, somewhere behind his sternum, as though a door he hadn't known was locked had swung open and something had walked out of him and not come back.

He kept walking. His eyes were burning. He kept walking.

He came out the far side into purple twilight with his knees half-buckled and caught himself against the lowest tooth, and its opal edge glowed warm under his palm, sated, and he thought, distantly: *it's smiling.*

"Coral—"

She was ten feet ahead, on her knees in the scree, and she turned at the sound of his voice, and her face —

Her face was wet. Her face was wrecked. She looked at him the way people look at a stranger wearing a friend's coat, and Wren felt the bottom drop out of his stomach, because he knew that look. He'd seen it in mirrors, years ago, in the weeks after his own crossing.

"What did it take from me?" she whispered.

"I don't know. You won't know. That's the design." He crossed to her, moving carefully, the way one moves near a spooked animal. "It comes back sometimes. Fragments. A smell, a phrase. Mostly it doesn't."

"It took—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her eye. "There was a song. I think there was a song. My mother used to—" Her voice failed. She tried again. "I can't find it. I know there was a song and I can't find it, it's like reaching into a drawer and the drawer isn't there anymore, the whole *wall* isn't there—"

*Coral.* The name surfaced in him whole, unprompted, and he went very still.

Because he knew that song. Every child raised in the Undercourt's service knew it — the lullaby of the salt-wards, older than the pact, sung to the brine-born children in the generations before the court stopped having children. *Low tide, low tide, the moon goes down to sleep...* It had no business in a hedge-witch's apprentice above the mountain. Unless blood carried it. Unless it had been passed down, mother to daughter, from a woman sold into the dark a century ago.

The gate hadn't taken a song at random. The gate had taken the thread that connected Coral to Ulyana — the one small inheritance the girl hadn't even known she owned.

Or. Or the gate had taken what it took because the mountain was *hungry*, and hungry things eat first from the table set for them.

Either way, it was done, and telling her would not return the drawer to the wall.

"It will hurt less," he said, which was not a lie, exactly. "In time. Come on. We should make distance before full dark — the teeth hunt best at full dark."

"That's a joke. Tell me that's a joke."

"Walk, Coral."

* * *

They walked. Below them, the pass opened onto the long descent into the mountain's root-country, where the pines grew black and crooked and the streams ran faintly bitter, pre-salted, as though the rock itself were weeping brine. Wren led, because he knew these slopes, and Coral followed in silence, and the silence between them had changed texture entirely. Before the gate it had been the silence of two people withholding. Now it was the silence of two people who had both been robbed in the same room and could not look at each other without seeing the theft.

Halfway down, she spoke.

"Yours," she said. "Your toll. What did it take this time?"

He kept his eyes on the path. "I told you. You won't know what's missing."

"Not mine. Yours. What did it take *this time*?" She caught his sleeve, forcing him to half-turn, and in the last grey light her eyes were fierce and frightened and young. "Because I felt it take something from you, Wren. I was behind you. I felt the whole passage go quiet, and then I felt — I don't know how to describe it — like a room somewhere had emptied out. And then you came out of those teeth looking like a man at his own funeral." Her grip tightened. "So don't tell me I won't know. Tell me what it was."

And here was the thing, the terrible convenient thing: he couldn't answer her. He stood there opening his mouth and nothing came, because the reason for his exile was gone again — taken a second time, or perhaps never returned, perhaps the gate had simply collected the debt it had been carrying on his account for eleven years. He reached for it the way he'd reached for it every day since he was nineteen years old, grasping at the shape of the absence, and found the same smooth blank wall, the same drawerless cabinet.

"I can't," he said, and heard his own voice crack on it. "I can't remember. That's what it took. Again. It took the same thing twice, Coral, it took the reason I left, and I had it back — I'd *found* it, three days ago, watching you lie to that caravan master, I remembered everything, all of it, why I ran, what I did, what they—" His breath was ragged now. "And it's gone. It's *gone*. I know I had it. I know it mattered. And I am standing here unable to tell you the single most important thing about me, and you're supposed to trust me, and I can't even—"

He stopped. Because Coral had let go of his sleeve and was looking at him with an expression he had no defenses against at all.

Pity, he could have handled. Pity he'd earned and expected. But this was worse. This was recognition. This was the look of one liar seeing another stripped bare, and finding the same scars in the same places.

"The song," she said slowly. "My song. Your reason. It took the things that tied us to—" She faltered. "To *them*. To whatever's down there. Didn't it. It doesn't take memories. It takes *threads*."

Wren stared at her. Eleven years in the archives, and he had never once thought of it that way. The Tollgate took payment, the texts said. The Tollgate weighed and measured, the texts said. No text had ever suggested the gate was not a clerk but a *shepherd* — trimming away everything that might pull a traveler back toward the surface, or forward toward the court, cutting the threads one by one until only the mountain's own remained to hold you.

"Salt-Bearer," he said quietly, testing the word in the open air for the first time. "It knew what you are. It's clearing the path. Ours, or theirs — I genuinely cannot tell you which."

"Then we find out the hard way." Coral wiped her face with both hands, briskly, the way one closes a ledger, and when she lowered them her jaw was set. "Come on, then. You said the descent takes two days. I want to be at the drowned city before the moon turns, and I want—" she hesitated, and for one unguarded moment the armor slipped and he saw the girl underneath, lonely and furious and grieving a song she couldn't name, "—I want to get her back before it takes anything else from me."

She started down the slope. Wren stood a moment longer in the gathering dark, one hand pressed flat against his own sternum, feeling for the door that had swung open and stayed open, the draft blowing through him from a room he could no longer find.

Far below, past the black pines, past the bitter streams, a light was waking in the roots of the mountain — pale, green-gold, rhythmic, pulsing slow as a heartbeat or a tide.

The Undercourt, lighting its lamps for the thirteenth turn.

He went down after her.
