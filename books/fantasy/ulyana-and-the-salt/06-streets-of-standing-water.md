## 6. Streets of Standing Water

The gate of teeth let them through with the taste of Wren's grief still sour in Coral's mouth, and then there was nothing left to do but descend.

The passage went down the way a throat goes down — not steeply, but with an inevitability that made her want to swallow against it. The walls wept. Not water exactly; something thicker, faintly luminous, that beaded on the stone like sweat and smelled of low tide and old flowers. Wren walked ahead with a lamp of green glass whose flame burned without flickering, as if it had made its peace with the dark long ago.

"Don't touch the walls," he said, without turning around. "It remembers being touched."

"What does?"

"Everything down here." He glanced back, and in the lamplight his face looked older than it had above ground, carved into sharper lines. "That's the whole trouble with the Undercourt, Coral. Nothing forgets."

She had meant to ask him more about the toll — about what he'd paid, about the shape of what she'd glimpsed when the gate drank from him: a desk, a seal, a hand pressing wax, a door closing. But the question kept dying somewhere between her ribs, and he kept not offering, and so they descended in a silence that was itself a kind of payment.

* * *

They came out of the throat of the mountain onto a ledge, and Coral stopped breathing.

Below them, impossibly, was a city.

It filled a cavern so vast the lamplight couldn't find its far wall — a drowned city, yes, but not sunken. The streets were canals of standing black water, perfectly still, each one holding a reflection of lights that had no source above. Buildings rose from the flood in tiers of pale stone, their windows glowing amber and green and a deep bruised violet, and bridges arched between them like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. And everywhere — everywhere — people. Figures moving along the waterfronts, poling flat barges through the canals, gathering on staircases that descended into the water and simply continuing down, walking beneath the surface without a ripple.

"Are they dead?" Coral whispered.

"Some." Wren stood beside her, hands folded behind his back like a man surveying an estate he'd once been proud of. "Some are debts. Some are dreams that outlived the dreamer. The Court doesn't much distinguish. Down here, everything that persists past its natural ending is currency."

"And us? We're neither."

"We're tourists," Wren said dryly. "Which is worse. Tourists get eaten last, but they get noticed first." He reached into his coat and produced two small objects: a coin stamped with a wave, and a ring of braided hair sealed in glass. "You'll wear these. The coin says you're licensed to trade. The ring says whose daughter you are."

Coral took the ring and turned it. The hair inside was silver-white, fine as spider silk. "Whose daughter am I?"

"A soul-broker's. Mother Vess of the Tidemark Quarter — deceased, regrettably, which is convenient, since the dead can't be asked awkward questions. You're her heir, come to collect on accounts outstanding." He said it smoothly, rehearsed, and she realized he'd been composing this story for her since the tollgate, maybe since before. "Your name is Coralin Vess. You've been raised above ground for your health. You know the trade from your mother's ledgers but you've never seen the Court itself. That explains your staring. Stare all you like. Grief-stricken heirs are expected to stare."

"And you?"

"I'm your factor. I handle the arithmetic." A thin smile. "I was good at arithmetic, once. It's why they exiled me instead of drowning me."

She wanted to ask again — *what did the gate take from you* — but the city below shifted, and the question drowned.

Because the lights were moving. All of them, all at once, flowing along the canals in slow rivers of color, converging toward the heart of the city where a single spire rose out of the flood like a needle through cloth. And with the movement came sound — carried up the throat of the mountain to the ledge where they stood — a sound like singing heard through several feet of seawater, wordless, vast, patient.

"The tide-turning," Wren said quietly. "The dead trade at the turnings. Longing flows in, longing flows out. Come on. If we enter with the tide, no one will look twice at us. If we enter after, everyone will."

* * *

The stair down into the city was cut from a single piece of stone and worn smooth by feet that had perhaps never been alive. Coral counted the steps out of nervousness and lost count three times, because each time she looked up, the city had rearranged itself slightly — a bridge that had arched left now arched right; a tower's windows had changed from violet to green. She decided, firmly, not to mention it.

"You'll feel it soon," Wren murmured as they joined the flow of figures drifting toward the waterfronts. "The pull. Everyone does."

"What pull?"

He didn't answer, which was answer enough.

The pull arrived at the bottom of the stairs, at the edge of the first canal, and it was nothing like she expected. It wasn't hunger or fear. It was homesickness — acute, specific, unbearable homesickness for places she had never been. She suddenly missed, with a physical ache, a kitchen she'd never cooked in, a garden she'd never planted, a voice she'd never heard calling her name across water. She gripped the canal's stone lip and breathed through her teeth.

"That's the currency," Wren said softly, watching her with something almost like pity. "Longing. The whole city runs on it. Every soul here is spending what they miss most, every hour, to buy what they need. The dead don't pay in coin, Coral. They pay in wanting."

"Then how do I buy anything?"

"You don't. You *trade*." He pressed the wave-coin into her palm and closed her fingers over it. "You're not spending longing tonight. You're a broker. Brokers hold other people's longing and sell it back to them at interest. Keep your own wanting locked tight behind your teeth, and for the Lady of Tides' sake, don't tell anyone what you miss. Not even me."

"Especially not you?" she said, trying for lightness.

Something crossed his face — quick, complicated, gone. "Especially not anyone."

* * *

They crossed into the city on a bridge called, according to a plaque of corroded bronze, the Widow's Span, and Coral stopped trying to understand what she saw and simply let it pour over her.
