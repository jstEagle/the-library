## 7. The Ledger of Souls

The archives of the Undercourt did not smell of dust.

That was the first thing Wren had forgotten, in the years since his exile — that a library built by the drowned would have no use for dust, no patience for dryness, no tolerance for the slow decay of things left alone. Instead the air tasted of low tide and lamp-oil made from rendered memory, and every breath he drew down here felt like reading someone else's diary without permission. Which, he supposed, was more or less what they had come to do.

"Stay close," he murmured, not turning around. "And whatever you see in the shelves, don't touch it with bare hands."

Behind him, Coral Brightwater made a sound that was half scoff, half swallow. "What happens if I do?"

"The shelves remember you." He kept walking. "And the Court remembers what its shelves remember."

The archive stretched out before them like the ribcage of some god who had drowned standing up. Columns of black basalt rose from water so still it might have been glass poured rather than gathered, and between the columns stood the shelves — great tiered galleries carved directly into the mountain's bone, descending into green-black depths where the lamplight gave up and something older than light went on cataloguing anyway. Each shelf held books. Each book was bound differently: some in leather gone soft as skin, some in slate, some in things Wren preferred not to name too precisely even in the privacy of his own skull. And drifting among them, pale as kelp and patient as tide, were the archivists.

They had been clerks once. That was the polite way to put it. Scribes who had died at their desks and been deemed too useful to bury, their hands too practiced, their eyes too hungry for the next entry. They moved through the flooded aisles without disturbing the water's surface, and when one of them turned its head toward the sound of Wren's boots on the submerged causeway, he felt the old familiar chill of being *catalogued*.

"Don't look at their faces," he said quietly.

"Why? What are they?"

"Colleagues." He let the word sit in the water between them until it curdled. "Former colleagues."

Coral came up beside him, close enough that her shoulder nearly brushed his, and he caught the scent of her — woodsmoke and hedge-magic and the stubborn ordinary smell of a person who sweated and bled up in the world where such things still meant something. It was absurdly comforting. Like carrying a piece of the sun into a cellar.

"You've been here before," she said. Not a question. She'd been doing that all day — taking the things he told her and filing them away behind those bright, suspicious eyes, building her case against him one observation at a time. He couldn't even blame her. He'd have done the same.

"I was apprenticed here," Wren said. "Before I was apprenticed to the Court proper. Every scribe starts in the archives. You learn the ledgers first. Then you learn why the ledgers must never be wrong." He paused at the head of a stair that descended beneath the waterline, where steps of veined marble vanished into green gloom. "Then you learn what happens to scribes who make them wrong."

"And you made one wrong."

"I made one *true*," Wren said. "Which is worse."

She looked at him then — really looked, the way she'd been not-looking at him for two days, the way she studied everything she didn't trust. Her hair had gone frizzy in the archive's damp, copper curls escaping their braid, and there was a smudge of soot still on her jaw from the morning's fire, and Wren thought, with a strange lurch he refused to examine, that she was the realest thing he had seen in eleven years.

"They'll let us just walk in?" she asked.

"No." He smiled, and knew it was the smile that had gotten him through a decade of smuggling secrets past people cleverer than himself. "But they'll let us *browse*. There's a difference, and the difference is about four minutes long. After that we're trespassers. So walk like you belong to me."

"I don't belong to anyone."

"Wonderful. Walk like it loudly, then. Confidence is the best forgery." He started down the stairs, and the water rose around his ankles, his knees, cool and utterly silent. "Keep your hands above the surface. Breathe normally. The Court finds panic *delicious*."

* * *

The Ledger of Souls lived at the bottom of everything.

Wren had known this when he planned the route; knowing it and experiencing it were different animals entirely. They descended for what felt like an hour through galleries of lesser records — the Tide-Books, the Tithe-Rolls, the great damp registries of longing that the dead traded in down here, each volume humming faintly with the wants of people long past wanting. Coral walked beside him with her chin up and her hands clasped tight behind her back, radiating the particular ferocity of someone determined not to be impressed, and Wren watched her fail at it, again and again, and found it the most entertaining thing the Undercourt had offered him in years.

"That one's breathing," she whispered as they passed a shelf of swollen gray volumes. "Wren. That book is *breathing*."

"It's a ledger of unspoken confessions. Of course it's breathing." He nodded toward a distant gallery where the light turned silver-green. "Don't stop. The confession-shelves get lonely, and lonely books make offers."

"What kind of offers?"

"The kind you spend the rest of your life regretting." He glanced sideways at her. "You'd know all about those, I imagine. Running away from apprenticeship. What did she offer you, your hedge-witch? A life of quiet service and dried nettles?"

Something flickered across Coral's face — quick as a fish under ice, gone before he could name it. "I didn't run because of an offer."

"No?"

"I ran because of a *lock*." Her jaw tightened. "A door in Aldith's house I wasn't allowed to open. A door she warded against me specifically. Against my blood." She said it lightly, tossed off, but her knuckles had gone white where her fingers laced together behind her back. "You spend three years scrubbing cauldrons for a woman and she wards her own cellar against you, you start wondering what's down there that smells like you."

Wren said nothing. But somewhere in the back of his mind, a small cold bell began to ring — the bell that rang whenever two pieces of information touched each other and sparked. A door warded against Coral's blood. A soul sold beneath the mountain generations ago. He filed the spark away the way he'd been trained to file everything: carefully, completely, and against the day it would hurt him.

The stairs ended.

The Ledger of Souls lay open on a plinth of white coral in the center of a round chamber, and it was — Wren had forgotten this too, or perhaps had simply refused to remember — *beautiful*. It was the size of a dining table, bound in nacre that shifted through colors as the lamplight moved, mother-of-pearl shot through with veins of deep sea-green. Its pages turned themselves, slowly, at the pace of the tide, though there was no wind down here and no hand upon them. Each page held perhaps forty entries, written in ink that gleamed wetly, and each entry was a name, a date, a price, and a signature.

Around the plinth, the water was perfectly clear and perfectly still, and looking down into it was like looking down into the sky of some drowned heaven. Things moved far below. Wren did not look longer than courtesy required.

"Four minutes," he said. "Starting now."

He approached the Ledger the way he'd been taught — palms open, head slightly bowed, the posture of a servant approaching a master's desk. The pages turned. Turned. Stopped.

Of course they stopped. The Ledger always stopped for its own.

The page facing him bore entries in a fine antique hand, and Wren's eyes went straight to the dates, running backward through decades the way he'd once run backward through them as a boy, racing other apprentices to find the oldest soul in the book. 1847. 1812. 1793. The paper was thick and soft as vellum, and the entries were brief, brutal, businesslike:

*Bellin of Harrowmere. Sold for nine winters of mild weather. Witnessed.*

*Maren Two-Rivers. Sold for the turning of a flood. Witnessed.*

Every entry witnessed. That was the law of the Court — no sale without a witness, no pact without a name attached, because the drowned believed, with the terrible literalism of the dead, that a thing unwitnessed had never happened. Wren had always found it darkly funny. Upstairs, witnesses were supposed to protect the vulnerable. Down here they existed purely to bind the guilty.

His finger hovered over the page. Behind him, Coral had gone very still, and he could feel her attention on his back like sunlight through a lens.

"Ulyana," he said, barely voicing it. "Salt-witch. Sold — the records say sold, not traded, not tithed, *sold*, which almost never happens — roughly ninety years ago. If she's in this book, she's—"

The page turned.

It should not have turned. He had not finished his approach-rite, had not spoken the archivist's phrase that released a page to turn onward, and yet the Ledger moved under his hovering hand with the smooth inevitability of a wave deciding to break, and there it was.

**ULYANA OF THE SALTMARSH.**

The name was written larger than the others. Older ink, deeper pressed, and around the letters the nacre binding had grown a faint iridescent bloom, like frost on a window, like salt creeping up the side of a barrel. Wren read the entry twice before his mind would accept it.

*Ulyana of the Saltmarsh, salt-witch, of the line Brightwater. Rendered into service perpetual. Price: the safety of Thornfell vale, its villages and fields, its children and children's children, against the hunger of the mountain, in perpetuity.*

*Sold by:* — and here the ink had been scratched out, deliberately, furiously, by some clerk's pen driven hard enough to score the page — *sold by [illegible], her own blood.*

*Witnessed by:* 

And there, in ink that had not faded, in a hand that Wren knew — knew the way you know a voice heard through a wall, knew a face seen once in a dream you spent years trying to forget —

*Witnessed by Aldith of the Hedge, of Thornfell village. Living.*

"*Living,*" Coral read aloud over his shoulder, and her voice cracked the word in half. "It says living. Wren. It says—" She went quiet. He could hear her thinking, hear the gears of it grinding against something she didn't want to grind against. "Aldith of the Hedge."

"There are a thousand hedges in Thornfell," Wren said, which was true, and useless, and they both knew it.

"Aldith." Coral's hand came down on the page — bare fingers, damn her, touching the Ledger directly — and the nacre bloomed under her touch, salt-frost spreading outward from her fingertips in delicate fractal feathers. "My grandmother's name was Maren. My mother never spoke of her teacher. She only ever called her *the old woman*. But I grew up in that house. I ate at that table. And there was a woman who came every solstice, who smelled of rue and river-clay, who used to look at me like—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Like she was checking whether I'd ripened yet."

The frost-bloom crept along the page toward the entry. Toward the name.

Wren should have stopped her. Every instinct trained into him across ten years of service and five years of flight screamed that touching the Ledger was death, that the archivists were already turning in their aisles, that the water in the floor below had begun — had it begun? — to move. But he didn't stop her, because he was looking at the witness-line, and the thrill of forbidden knowledge, the old sweet electric joy of *knowing*, was curdling in his chest into something with teeth.

Because he recognized the hand.

Not the name — the name meant nothing to him, or would have, if he'd been anyone else. But the *hand*. The particular forward slant of it. The way the crossbar of the *t* hooked left instead of right. The way the *d* ascenders looped twice, a habit of a writer taught by a teacher taught by a teacher, a lineage of hands going back through the hedge-covens of Thornfell like a braided cord.

He had copied that hand for practice, once. Long ago. In a sunlit scriptorium that no longer existed, in a life that belonged to a boy who no longer existed, he had sat with samples of the surface-covens' scripts — because the Court required its scribes to know the writing of everyone they might someday trade with — and he had filled sheet after sheet imitating a sample labeled, in his master's dry voice, *the hand of the hedge-witch Aldith, our correspondent above.*

*Our correspondent.*

The Court had a correspondent above. Had had one for decades. A living witness, signing sales, renewing terms, feeding names upward through channels Wren had never been cleared to know. And now that correspondent had taken in a runaway apprentice whose blood was the key to the oldest entry in the Ledger, and had warded her own cellar against that blood, and had watched her for years *like she was checking whether she'd ripened yet*—

"Oh," Wren said softly. "Oh, you poor, furious creature. You didn't run away from your apprenticeship."

Coral turned to look at him. The frost-bloom had reached the edge of Ulyana's entry and stopped there, quivering, as if waiting for permission.

"You were *ripening*," he said. "All this time. She's been keeping you like a key keeps in a drawer."

"That's not—" Coral's face did something complicated, fury and fear fighting for the same ground. "She raised me. She taught me everything. She—" And then, quieter, in the voice of a girl who had scrubbed cauldrons for three years and loved the woman who owned them: "*Why would she ward me out of her own cellar?*"

"Because of what's in it," Wren said. "Or because of what you'd become if you saw it." He looked down at the entry, at the scratched-out seller's name, at the living witness's signature gleaming wetly in the lamplight, and the last of the thrill died in him, replaced by the cold specific dread of a man who has just realized how many people will recognize his handwriting on the wanted-notices. "There's something else you need to understand. This witness — Aldith, if it's her — didn't just sign a sale ninety years ago. Look at the ink. Look at the *freshness* of it. This entry has been amended. Renewed. Re-witnessed, over and over, across decades. Someone above has been maintaining this pact her whole life, and the Court knows her, trusts her, *corresponds* with her."

"So?"

"So when we go back up," Wren said, "and start asking questions about a soul sold beneath the mountain — the one person in Thornfell who can answer us is also the one person who will look at me and know exactly what I am. Because I know her hand, Coral. I've *practiced* it. And if she ever practiced mine—" He exhaled slowly. "If she's the Court's correspondent, then she's seen scribes' marks. Exile-stamps. Everything."

The water around the plinth shivered. Just once. Just enough.

Far off in the flooded galleries, something that had been a colleague of his turned its head, all the way around, further than a neck should go, and looked down the long aisle toward the chamber of the Ledger.

"We're done browsing," Wren said.

"But we haven't—" Coral's hand was still on the page. Under her palm, Ulyana's name glowed faintly, salt-white, like a coal refusing to die. "Her soul. Where is it *kept*? The entry doesn't say. It says *rendered into service perpetual* — rendered *how*? Wren, if we don't know where it is, we can't—"

"Coral." He took her wrist, gently, and lifted her fingers from the page. The frost-bloom stayed behind, spreading no further, marking their visit in salt-feathers across ninety-year-old ink. "The Ledger tells you what was sold. It never tells you where the goods are stored. That's not an oversight. That's the *design*." He backed away from the plinth, drawing her with him, his eyes on the dark mouth of the gallery where the archivist still hung, watching, its face a pale smear of drowned patience. "If you want to know where a soul is kept in the Undercourt, you don't ask the Ledger."

"Then what do you ask?"

"You ask for an audience," Wren said, "with the King. And you pray to whatever gods the drowned haven't eaten that he's feeling merciful."

The lamplight guttered. Somewhere above them, deep in the mountain's throat, water began to move with purpose — and beneath the sound, faint and rising, came a tolling like a bell made of teeth.

Coral looked at him. "Wren. What did we just do?"

He was already running. "We signed the guest-book," he said.
