## 8. Court of the Drowned King

The summons came as a tide.

Coral felt it before she saw it — a pressure in her ears, a taste of iodine at the back of her throat, the same sensation she'd known since childhood when a storm was still three valleys away. She had been standing at the window of their borrowed room in the Silted Quarter, watching the drowned city's lantern-fish drift through streets of standing water, when every light in the Undercourt went out at once.

Then the water rose.

Not violently. That was the terrible part. It came up the stairwell with the patience of something that had never once been refused, filling the room to her knees, her waist, her ribs, and Wren — Wren, who had been hunched over a stolen map with ink-stained fingers and an expression of permanent grievance — went rigid as the black water touched his boots.

"Don't fight it," he said quietly. "For pity's sake, Coral, don't fight it."

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. You do that thing with your shoulders." He didn't look at her. He was looking at the doorway, where the water had begun to shape itself into a corridor — walls of it, a ceiling of it, a passage leading down and down into the dark beneath the drowned city, lit from within by a faint green-gold glow like sunlight remembered rather than seen. "That's how they summon. Not with trumpets. With tide. The whole court is one body, and when the King wants you, the King's water comes to carry you."

"Carry me where?"

Wren finally turned, and in the green light his face looked older than his years, carved by something more than exile. "To the throne," he said. "And Coral — whatever he offers you, whatever he shows you, remember that the Drowned King has not ruled this place for centuries by asking for things he cannot take. If he asks instead, it is because taking would cost him too much. Which means what he wants from you is worth more than you think."

"And what if I don't go?"

"The water goes up your nose and stays there until you change your mind." He said it flatly, without drama, the way he said all the worst things. "I've seen it. A smuggler who tried to refuse a summons. They found him three days later, floating, still alive somehow, still drowning. The court doesn't kill you for disobedience. It just makes obedience the only comfortable option."

The water lapped at Coral's collarbone, gentle as a mother's hand. Somewhere far below, something vast turned over in its sleep.

"Well," she said, and hated how small her voice sounded. "Lead on, then, scribe."

* * *

They did not walk so much as sink.

The corridor of water bore them downward through strata of the mountain's drowned history — past doorways bricked shut a thousand years ago, past a chapel whose bell still swung though no wind reached it, past windows that opened onto rooms where the dead sat at tables set for meals no one would ever eat. Coral pressed her hands against the current and watched faces flicker by: a woman braiding rope from kelp, a child chasing a hoop through a flooded square, an old man mending a net with fingers gone translucent as sea glass. None of them looked up. None of them ever looked up.

"They can't see us," Wren murmured. "Or rather — they see everything and nothing. The dead trade in tides, Coral. In ebb and flow, in longing given and longing taken. We're not people to them. We're weather."

"That's cheerful."

"I'm told my cheerfulness is one of my finest qualities."

She almost laughed, and the laugh died in her chest as the corridor opened.

The throne hall of the Drowned King had been a cathedral once — she could see it in the bones of the place, in the ribbed vaults overhead now hung with curtains of living kelp, in the rose window above the altar that filtered moonlight down through forty fathoms of water into shafts of silver-green. But the pews were gone, replaced by terraces of black coral where the court assembled: hundreds of the drowned dead, ranked like choir stalls, their bodies clothed in garments of woven current, their faces turned toward the throne with expressions of such perfect, patient attention that Coral's skin crawled.

And there, at the end of the nave where the altar should have been, sat the Drowned King.

He was not what she expected. She had imagined a monster — something of the deep trenches, all teeth and pressure and blind hunger. Instead he was a man of indeterminate age, tall and spare, seated on a throne of salt-crusted stone with his hands folded in his lap like a scholar between lectures. His hair drifted around his head in a slow halo, white as foam. His eyes were the color of the sea over a shipwreck: beautiful, and utterly without mercy, and very old.

He was looking directly at her. Of course he was. He had been looking at her since before she was born.

"Coral Brightwater," he said, and his voice filled the flooded cathedral the way a bell fills a tower — not loud, but everywhere, resonant in her ribs, in her teeth, in the water itself. "Daughter of Maren Brightwater. Granddaughter of Yeva the hedge-witch, called Yeva Salt-Tongue in certain ledgers. Apprentice runaway, caravan guide, reader of books that bleed." He inclined his head, courteous as a host greeting a guest at dinner. "You are late. The prophecy named tonight, and tonight is nearly spent."

"You've read it too, then." Her voice came out steadier than she felt. Small mercies.

"I wrote the margins." Something that might have been amusement moved across his face and was gone. "Come forward, Salt-Bearer. The water will bear you. It always bears its own."

She let it lift her — there was no dignity in fighting, and less chance — and carried her down the nave between the terraces of the dead while Wren followed in her wake like flotsam behind a keel. Up close, the court resolved into detail she wished she could unsee: the drowned dead had no need to breathe, but some of them breathed anyway, out of habit or vanity, and the sight of chests rising and falling with water instead of air made her stomach turn over.

The throne stopped her ten feet away. The water set her down gently, like a servant setting down a lady's chair, and withdrew to her knees.

"So," Coral said. "You know who I am. Everyone under this mountain seems to know who I am. My grandmother knew. A book written before my birth knew. Do you want to tell me why everyone got an invitation except me?"

"Because invitations are for guests." The King unfolded his hands. "You are not a guest, Coral Brightwater. You are an inheritance."

The word landed in her chest like a stone dropped down a well. She thought of the crone in the last village before the pass, of the story she'd told with her hands shaking around a cup of tea: *your grandmother sold a soul to buy the valley's safety*. She thought of the name she'd been carrying for days now like a coal in her pocket. *Ulyana.*

"The soul," Coral said. "Ulyana. That's why I'm here. That's why any of this — the book, the thirteenth moon, all of it. Her time is up, isn't it? When the moon completes its turn, the debt comes due, and she—" She couldn't finish it. She'd read enough of the Ledger's grammar in Wren's hurried lessons to know what came due.

"She is rendered," the King said simply. "Her soul goes into the salt, the salt feeds the pact, the pact keeps your villages safe, and Ulyana ends. As she agreed to end. As she begged to end, in the end, because a century of service is long, and even the willing grow tired." His wreck-colored eyes held hers. "Unless."

"Unless."

"Unless a new keeper takes up the salt."

The words settled over the hall, and Coral felt the whole drowned court lean in — not moving, the dead never moved unless required, but leaning nonetheless, a thousand attentions pressing against her skin like the pressure of deep water.

"Explain," she said.

"Gladly. I have waited generations to explain." The King rose from his throne, and Coral saw for the first time that he was bound to it — chains of salt ran from his wrists into the stone, delicate as lace, unbreakable as geology. He could stand, but only so far. He could speak, but only to those brought before him. The throne of the Undercourt was also its prison, and had been since before the pact, since before the villages, since perhaps before the mountain had a name.

"Do you know what the salt does, child? Truly does? Your grandmother's bargain bought safety, yes. The mountain sleeps, the passes hold, the floods keep to their channels. But sleep is not free. The mountain dreams, and its dreams are hunger, and the salt is what we feed that hunger so it never wakes to feed itself." He gestured, and the water around them bloomed with images — Coral saw the valley villages, smoke from morning fires, children on the roads, sheep in high pasture, all of it wrapped in a faint shimmer of white that she recognized now, that she had tasted on every wind that came down off the Thornfells her entire life. "Every grain of pact-salt is a soul, rendered fine. Every year, a little more. Every century, another name in the Ledger. Your grandmother understood the arithmetic. One soul, weighed against thousands. She wept when she signed. I have kept the tear in a jar, if you would like to see it. Sentiment amuses me."

"You're a monster."

"I am a bookkeeper." No anger in it. That was worse. "Monsters devour. I maintain accounts. And the account, Coral Brightwater, is coming due. Ulyana's term ends with the thirteenth moon. The pact requires a keeper of the salt — someone living, someone of the seller's blood, someone whose will is strong enough to hold the mountain's hunger at bay without being devoured by it. There is exactly one person in the world who meets the requirements." He smiled, and it was almost kind, which was the most frightening thing yet. "You have already met her in a mirror."

* * *

The water carried her images while he spoke, and she hated how beautiful he made it look.

She saw herself standing in the First Saltworks — she didn't know its name yet, but she would, oh, she would — with light pouring through her hands like she was holding the moon. She saw the villages safe below, generation after generation, weddings and harvests and graves dug in good earth, none of them ever knowing how close the hunger came. She saw Ulyana walking free out of the brine-crypt, whole and breathing, blinking in real sunlight for the first time in a hundred years.

*Her freedom,* the images whispered, in the King's voice, in the water's voice, in a voice that sounded horribly like Coral's own conscience. *Bought fairly. Paid in full. By you.*

"No," Coral said.

The images stilled.

"No," she said again, louder, and the sound rang strangely in the flooded nave. "You're doing the thing my grandmother did. The arithmetic. One soul against thousands, one life against a valley — you make it sound noble, you make it sound like the only decent choice, and that's exactly how it works, isn't it? Nobody sells a soul to a monster. They sell it to a duty. They sell it to the version of the story where they're the hero." Her hands were shaking. She clenched them. "My grandmother took that bargain and it broke her — I've read her letters, I found them in Aldith's house, I know what it cost her, she couldn't sleep near open water for the rest of her life. And you're offering me the same deal with prettier lighting."

"Yes," said the Drowned King.

She blinked. "What?"

"Yes. All of it. The bargain is gilded, the duty is seductive, and the arithmetic is designed to make refusal feel like murder." He folded his hands again, and the salt-chains chimed softly, softly. "I have made this offer four times in a century, Coral Brightwater. To your grandmother's daughter — she refused, and fled, and died on a road far from home rather than choose. To two others of the blood. Each time, I show them the beauty of the sacrifice, because a keeper who comes to the salt believing herself noble serves better than one dragged in chains. Honesty costs me nothing. You will say yes or you will not, and either way the moon completes its turn."

Behind her, Wren made a small sound — protest, warning, she couldn't tell — and the King's gaze flicked past her shoulder with the mild interest of a man noticing weather.

"And your scribe may stop glowering. I have not forgotten his crimes, Wren of the Ninth Desk. Forgiveness remains available. It always has. All you must do is help yourself back into my good graces, and we might discuss restoring your name, your rank, your warm dry study with the window overlooking the Sea of Glass." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "But that is a conversation for later. For now—"

He extended one hand, and from the darkness behind the throne, the water brought something forward.

A jar. Small, ordinary, sealed with wax gone soft with age. Inside it floated a single page, waterlogged and glowing faintly, and even at a distance Coral could read the name written across it in the Ledger's unmistakable hand:

*Ulyana. Term of service: one hundred years. Render upon completion of term.*

"One page," the King said. "One signature. Take up the keeping, and I will strike the rendering myself, before witnesses, and she walks out of here free. Refuse, and the moon turns, and the salt takes her, and the pact holds a little longer until the next name comes due — and there is always a next name, child. There have been eleven before her. Ask your scribe how many pages sit in my Ledger waiting for their turn."

Coral's mouth was dry, which was absurd, standing knee-deep in an ocean.

Eleven. Eleven names. Eleven grandmothers' bargains, eleven souls fed into the white grains that shimmered over every village she'd ever guided a caravan through. And every one of them had stood exactly here, in this water, hearing exactly this offer, feeling exactly this — the terrible gravity of a choice that wasn't really a choice, the way duty dressed itself in love's clothing and asked to be worn.

*It feels noble,* she thought, and the terror of it closed around her heart like a fist. *That's what frightens me. It feels right. He's built it to feel right.*

"I need to think," she said.

"Of course." The King reseated himself, gracious as a bishop. "The moon turns tomorrow night. Think quickly."

The water began to lift them, bearing them back up the nave, and Coral stared at the receding figure on the salt-bound throne — at the chains, at the patience, at the wreck-colored eyes that had watched four keepers refuse and one grandmother weep — and one question clawed its way up her throat before the current swept her out.

"Why do you need a keeper at all?" she called back. "If the pact works, if the salt feeds the hunger — why does it have to be *me*? Why does it have to be anyone?"

The Drowned King smiled.

And for one flickering moment, beneath the courtesy and the calm, Coral saw something in his face that she had never expected to find there — something raw and exhausted and old beyond reckoning, something that looked almost like hope, or almost like despair, or almost like both wearing the same mask.

"Ask your scribe," he said, "what the mountain actually eats."

The water closed over her head, and the throne hall was gone.
