## 9. Salt on Her Tongue

The crypt did not sleep, and neither, strictly speaking, did Ulyana.

She had forgotten what sleeping felt like — the soft surrender of it, the way a body gave itself over to darkness and trusted the world to hold it until morning. In the brine-crypt there was no morning. There was only the salt, and the salt was patient in the way that stone is patient, in the way that grief is patient, and after three hundred years she had learned to be patient too, because the alternative was to go mad, and she had tried that once, early on, and found it tedious.

So she hung in her niche of crystal and listened.

That was all she had, in the end: listening. The crypt was carved deep beneath the mountain's heart, in a place where the rock wept constantly, thin threads of brine sliding down the walls into channels that fed the Saltworks below. The water spoke. It always spoke. It carried the taste of everything it touched — the drowned streets of the Undercourt, the fossil corridors where the dead traded their tides, the long cold veins of the mountain itself, and above all that, far above, filtered through a hundred fathoms of stone, the thin bright rumor of rain on Thornfell soil.

Ulyana drank the water's gossip the way other prisoners dreamed of bread.

Tonight — if it was night; she measured time now by the moon's turns as they echoed through the mountain's tides, thirteen slivers of pressure against the crystal, and tonight the thirteenth sliver pressed close, closer than it had ever pressed — tonight the water tasted different.

It tasted of hawthorn.

She knew that taste. She had known it before she knew her own name, because her mother had planted hawthorn at the door of their cottage on the fell, white blossom in spring, red beads in autumn, thorns sharp enough to draw blood from careless hands. Hedge-magic, the villagers called it. Small magic. The magic of thresholds and protection, of keeping what belonged inside and what threatened outside. Ulyana had been born behind a hawthorn hedge and had died — no, not died; *sold* — with its scent still in her hair.

And now the water coming down from the surface world tasted of hawthorn and woodsmoke and young sweat and fear, and underneath all of it, unmistakable, undeniable, a thread of blood that sang to her own.

*Oh,* she thought. *Oh, you foolish, brave child.*

She could not have said how she knew it was a child, or a girl, or hers. The knowledge arrived the way the tide arrives — not as information but as fact, as gravity. Somewhere above her, descending toward her through the dark ribs of the world, came someone whose blood remembered hers. A descendant. Hers. Which meant the daughter —

Which meant Maren's line had continued. Maren, who had been six years old when they took her mother away. Maren, who had stood at the edge of the salt-circle with her fists clenched and her jaw set exactly the way Ulyana's own jaw set, refusing to cry because crying would give them the satisfaction. Maren, who had lived. Who must have grown, and loved, and borne children of her own, and those children children of theirs, until the blood had thinned and spread like ink in water and yet here it was, three centuries later, still recognizably itself, still singing the same song down through the stone.

Ulyana pressed her forehead against the crystal wall of her niche. It was warm, or she imagined it was warm. After three hundred years a prisoner will imagine warmth anywhere.

*Don't come,* she thought, with all the force of a soul that had nothing left but force. *Don't you dare come.*

Because she knew why they would send someone. She had watched the Court's arithmetic for three hundred years from inside her jar of salt, and she understood the ledger better than most of the clerks who tended it. The pact was old. The pact was fraying. And the thirteenth turn — the moon that no almanac predicted, the sliver that pressed against the mountain like a thumb testing fruit for ripeness — meant the Undercourt was calling in its debts. They would want a renewal. They would want fresh blood to sign where old blood had signed. And who better than the sold woman's own descendant, some green girl out of the hedge-villages who didn't know what she was walking into, who would stand before the Drowned King with his voice like deep water and his eyes like the space between stars, and who would be so *grateful* for whatever small mercy he offered—

*No.* Ulyana pushed the thought away, hard, the way she'd once pushed a fever out of Maren's small body, hand flat against the burning brow, willing the sickness elsewhere. *You don't get to have her. You've had me. That was the bargain, and I've paid it, every hour of every year, and I never once asked for anything back, so you don't get to reach up out of your drowned city and put your cold fingers on my blood.*

The salt around her hummed. It always hummed when she raged — her anger was the one currency she still possessed, and the Saltworks below drank it greedily, rendered it, packed it into the pale crystals that kept the pact alive. Every curse she uttered became a grain of preservation. Every grief became a seal. That was the joke at the heart of her imprisonment, the one nobody above ground understood: the mountain did not feed on villages. It fed on *her*. On her longing, which was the purest longing in the world, because it had nowhere left to go. Three hundred years of missing her daughter, distilled. Three hundred years of wanting home, crystallized. The salt that bought Thornfell's safety was made of a mother's love, ground fine.

They had told her, at the signing, that it was an honor.

* * *

The night they sold her had smelled of woodsmoke and wet wool, and it had rained, and Ulyana remembered the rain because it was the last honest thing she touched for a very long time.

She had been thirty-one. Maren was six. The blight had taken the barley two years running, and the winter had taken old Tam Wetherby and the miller's twins, and the village elders had come to the cottage with their hats in their hands and their shame worn openly, the way poor people wear their best clothes — carefully, because it might have to last.

"There's a way," said Elder Corrick, who had kind eyes and a weak spine, and Ulyana had thought, even then: *there it is. There's the sentence that ends a life. There's a way.*

The Undercourt's brokers had come up through the Tollgate of Teeth three nights later, robed in grey that moved like water, carrying a contract written on skin that was not leather. The terms were simple. The mountain hungered — it had always hungered, since before the villages, since before names — and the hunger could be bound, contained, fed on something subtler than flesh. One soul, freely given, of sufficient depth. In exchange: plenty. Rain in season. Barley that did not rot. Children that lived past their fifth winter. A century of safety, renewable upon terms to be determined.

"Freely given," the broker repeated, and looked at Ulyana, and Ulyana looked back, and between them passed the whole ugly truth: that *freely* was doing more work in that sentence than any word should ever have to carry. Freely, when the alternative was watching her village starve. Freely, when the other option sat across the table in a high chair learning to eat porridge with a wooden spoon.

She signed. Of course she signed. She was a salt-witch; she knew the value of things; she knew that everything true is paid for twice, once in coin and once in regret.

But before she went down — and this was the part she returned to, over and over, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth — before she went down, she had done one small unlawful thing. She had taken Maren onto her lap by the fire, and she had cut a curl of the child's hair, and she had worked it into a knot of salt and spoken over it the oldest binding she knew, older than the Court, older than the mountain, a hedge-craft charm her own grandmother had used for keeping: *what is knotted stays knotted; what is named stays named; what is loved stays findable.*

Then she had sewn it into the lining of Maren's coat, and kissed the crown of her head, and said, "Whatever happens, little fish, you are mine, and I am yours, and no paper changes that."

Maren had nodded, solemn, six years old, understanding nothing and everything, and Ulyana had walked out into the rain and down the mountain and through the gate of teeth, and the gate had taken her memory of her wedding day as toll — she had gone through it not knowing she had ever been loved like that, only knowing that she had been, because the knot of salt in her daughter's coat told her so — and she had descended, and she had endured, and for three hundred years the knot had been her anchor. Somewhere above, a girl wore her love stitched into wool. Somewhere above, the name Ulyana was still spoken, or whispered, or at least not entirely forgotten.

The salt-knot was the reason she had lasted. Not courage. Not faith. A scrap of wool and a lie she had chosen to believe: *I'll see her again.*

She had not seen her again. She had felt Maren's life go on — faint tugs through the blood, a marriage, a birth, the particular bright flare of a grandchild arriving — and then the tugs had faded, generation by generation, until they were too faint to read, until all she could feel was the weather of her descendants rather than their days. And then, tonight, after three hundred years of silence:

Hawthorn. Woodsmoke. Fear. Blood calling to blood down through a hundred fathoms of stone.

* * *

"You feel it."

The voice came from the channel at the crypt's edge, and Ulyana did not startle, because startling required a body's agreement and her body had been the Court's property for three centuries. The speaker oozed into view along the brine-run: a tide-wraith, one of the lesser dead, its face a smooth grey oval with the suggestion of features, like a face seen through fogged glass. It had brought her water-rations for three hundred years and had never once spoken beyond necessity.

"The thirteenth turn," Ulyana said. Her voice came out rusted. She saved it for occasions like this, hoarded words the way misers hoard coin. "I feel it."

"Above," the wraith said. "In the descent-ways. Warm blood. Quick heartbeat." Its featureless face tilted. "It sings, a little. The blood. Old songs. Yours?"

Ulyana said nothing, which was an answer, and the wraith absorbed it with the placid cruelty of all the Court's servants, who had long ago learned that hope was a seasoning and prisoners were best served with generous helpings of it.

"The King knows," the wraith said. "The King has known since the blood first crossed the Tollgate. He is pleased. He says—" and here the wraith paused, savoring, drawing out the moment with the delicacy of a sommelier, "—he says the vintage has come to him at last. The granddaughter of the debt. He says the pact will renew sweeter than ever."

"He would say that," Ulyana said. "He's a salesman."

The wraith drifted a half-circle closer, and though it had no eyes, Ulyana felt it looking at her the way one looks at a dish one intends to order. "You do not ask if the blood is strong. You do not ask if the girl is clever. Most of the long-hung ones beg for news."

"I'm not most of the long-hung ones."

"No," the wraith agreed. "You're the collateral. The principal asset. The salt remembers you fondly." It gathered itself to leave, then paused, and its voice dropped into something almost gentle, which from a tide-wraith was more frightening than menace. "A kindness, then, since you've been such a cooperative vintage. The girl travels with a scribe. Exiled. Wren, he was called. He wrote the King's decrees once, before his fall. He knows the descent-ways. He knows the Ledger." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "He knows what page bears your name."

Ulyana's heart — the slow, tidal thing that beat perhaps once an hour in her salted chest — turned over.

*The Ledger.* The great book in the Court archives, bound in the same unskin as contracts, wherein every soul sold was entered in ink that could not be forged and could not be erased. She had glimpsed it once, at her signing, open on the broker's table like a mouth waiting to be fed. As long as her entry stood, she belonged to the Court. That was the law beneath all laws down here, older than the Drowned King himself: *what is written, is.*

"And if the page were torn," Ulyana said slowly, tasting each word like a stranger's medicine, "truly torn, torn *true*, by the right hand—"

"Then the debt breaks," the wraith said. "And the pact breaks. And the mountain wakes hungry. And every village under Thornfell pays the arrears." It smiled, or suggested smiling. "The King wishes you to know this. He wishes the girl to know it. He finds the choice delicious. Freedom for one, or safety for thousands — he does love a clean dilemma. He says it makes the finest salt."

It poured her ration into the channel and slid away, and the crypt was silent again except for the weeping of the walls and the distant, tidal breathing of the mountain.

Ulyana hung in her niche and did the arithmetic she had been avoiding for three hundred years.

One soul against ten thousand. Her freedom against the barley and the rain and the children past their fifth winter. Everything she had suffered, rendered into salt, holding up the sky over Thornfell — and if she pulled herself out of the architecture, what fell? What crushed whom?

An easy sum, if you were the sort who thought in sums. The Court thought in sums. The King thought in sums. Even the village elders, kind-eyed Corrick with his hat in his hands, had thought in sums: one witch against a valley, and the valley won, and everyone went home to their suppers and called it necessity.

But Ulyana had been a hedge-witch's daughter before she was ever a salt-witch, and hedge-craft did not think in sums. Hedge-craft thought in thresholds. In what belongs inside and what belongs outside. In the difference between a cage and a home, and how the only thing that separates them is whether the door opens from within.

*They never asked me to stay,* she thought. *They asked me to be kept.*

And now they meant to keep her granddaughter's granddaughter — or worse, to make the girl *choose* it, to dress the cage in duty's clothes and call it an honor, the way they had dressed it for her. The King with his clean dilemmas. The King and his delicious choices. As if love were a scale to be balanced. As if a mother's heart were a ledger.

Ulyana closed her eyes, which changed nothing, since there was nothing to see, and reached out through the salt the way she had not dared reach in three hundred years — down through the crystal, down through the channels, following the thread of hawthorn and woodsmoke and fear, following the blood-song to its source, a girl somewhere in the descent-ways with a knot of salt sewn into her coat lining, maybe, if the charm had held, if the wool had survived, if anyone had thought to pass down a dead woman's keepsake without knowing what it was.

*I can't free you,* she told the girl, silently, with everything she had. *I'm sorry. I'm sorry, little fish, whoever you are, whatever name they gave you. I can't reach you and I can't warn you and I can't tell you what I know — that the hunger is a lie, that the pact is a leash disguised as a lifeline, that the King fears one thing only, which is being *remembered wrong*. But listen to me: whatever he offers you, however noble it sounds, however much it smells like sacrifice and salvation — look at his hands. Count his fingers. A king who buys souls always has one finger too many, and it's the one he counts your choices with.*

The blood-thread flickered, faint, like a candle in wind. Closer now. Descending.

And Ulyana, who had refused hope for three hundred years on the grounds that hope was the cruelest instrument in the Court's orchestra, felt it begin anyway — felt it rise in her salted chest like water finding its level, unstoppable, inevitable, absurd — and she did what she had always done with unbearable things.

She sharpened it. She honed her hope into patience, and her patience into intent, and her intent into a single point, fine as a needle, hard as salt, and she aimed it down through the dark at the approaching warmth of her own blood, and she waited.

Above her, far above, the thirteenth sliver of the moon completed its turn.

And somewhere in the descent-ways, a girl with hawthorn in her hair stopped walking, and lifted her head, and said, in a voice that carried strangely through a hundred fathoms of stone, clear as a bell rung underwater:

*"Grandmother?"*
