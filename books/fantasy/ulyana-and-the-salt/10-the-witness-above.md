## 10. The Witness Above

The climb back to the surface took three days, and Wren spent every one of them rehearsing.

He did it the way he'd once drafted petitions for the Drowned King — silently, in the dark, his lips moving through the architecture of a speech he hoped never to deliver. *We need to find the witness. The Ledger says the sale was witnessed. A living witness, above.* Simple words. Clean words. Words that did not mention that the witness's name had been written in the margin in a hand Wren recognized, because he had spent six years of his life copying that hand into the Court's official records before his exile.

*Aldith of Hollowmere. Hedge-witch. Witness to the Salt Pact.*

He had not told Coral yet. He told himself it was mercy. He told himself a great many things were mercy, these days — that was the trouble with being a liar by trade; you got so good at lying to others that your own lies came out polished, lacquered, indistinguishable from truth even to you.

Coral climbed ahead of him through the mountain's throat, her lantern swinging, her braid coming loose in wisps that caught the light like copper wire. She moved the way she always moved on stone — certain, economical, as if the mountain had personally taught her each foothold. Which, Wren was beginning to understand, it might have. Everything about this girl was written somewhere. In a book bound in brine. In a Ledger kept by the drowned. Possibly, if the prophecies were as thorough as they seemed, in places no one had thought to look yet.

"You're doing the thing," Coral said without turning around.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you're quiet and your shoulders go up around your ears like you're bracing for weather." She paused at a shelf of rock, waiting for him to catch up, and studied him with those bright, unfooled eyes. "You've been drafting something for three days. I can hear your teeth moving."

"I don't move my teeth when I think."

"You do. Like a man chewing on a word he can't swallow."

Wren looked away, up the shaft of grey light ahead of them, where the mountain's throat opened onto sky. "When we find the witness," he said, because some truths were easier sideways, "there's a chance they'll know me."

"Know you how?"

"The way the Court knows me." He turned his palms up, an old scribe's gesture, showing empty hands. "I wrote their records for six years, Coral. I have a face people remember, and reasons they remember it that aren't flattering."

She was quiet a moment. Then: "You could have led with that. Back at the tollgate. Before we paid in memories."

"If I'd led with everything, we'd still be standing at the gate arguing about whether to trust me."

"And are we past that now?"

"We're three days deep in a mountain together," Wren said. "I'd say the argument resolved itself."

Something almost like a smile crossed her face and was gone, folded away the way she folded everything away — quickly, competently, before it could be seen. She turned and climbed on. But at the top of the next pitch she said, quietly, not looking back: "Whatever it is you're not telling me about the witness — tell me before they see us coming. Not after. I'm tired of learning things after."

*Yes,* Wren thought. *That's what I'm afraid of.*

* * *

They broke the surface at dusk on the third day, through a cleft in the mountainside that spilled them out above the valley like water from a jug. Below them, Thornfell unrolled in the last light — terraced fields gone gold and grey, smoke rising straight from village chimneys in the windless cold, and beyond it all, far to the south, a single lit window that Wren knew, from maps he had no business knowing, belonged to a hedge-cottage in a place called Hollowmere.

Coral saw him looking at it and went very still.

"That's Aldith's house," she said.

"You know her."

"I *ran* from her." The words came out flat and hard, stones dropped down a well. "Six years apprenticed. Six years of grinding herbs and copying charms and being told I wasn't ready, wasn't ready, wasn't ready — and then one night I found a locked chest under her floorboards and she caught me looking and lied to my face about what was inside. So I left. Took my kit and walked out before dawn and never looked back." Her jaw tightened. "Why are we going there?"

Wren took out the scrap of Ledger page he'd stolen — the transcription he'd made in the archives, Ulyana's entry, the sale recorded in ink that still smelled faintly of low tide. He held it where the dying light caught it and pointed to the witness line.

*Covenanted and witnessed beneath the thirteenth moon, by Aldith of Hollowmere, who set her hand willingly.*

Coral read it twice. When she looked up, her face had done something complicated — anger and grief fighting for the same ground.

"She sold someone," Coral said. "My grandmother sold someone, and Aldith stood there and watched and signed her name to it."

"It looks that way."

"And you knew. You knew her name this whole climb." It wasn't a question. Her voice didn't rise; it went quieter, which was worse. "You were chewing on it for three days."

"Coral—"

"No. You're right. We're past the arguing-about-trust part." She shouldered her pack and started down the switchback trail toward the valley floor, fast, furious, sure-footed even in her rage. "Come on, scribe. Let's go visit an old friend."

* * *

Hollowmere smelled of woodsmoke and drying yarrow, of late autumn pressing its thumb down on the gardens. The cottage sat at the village's edge exactly as Coral remembered it and nothing like she remembered it — smaller, somehow, its shutters crooked, its herb-beds gone leggy and half-wild, as though the woman inside had stopped tending things that grew.

Aldith answered the door before Coral knocked.

Wren would remember that afterward — the door opening on the second knock's echo, as if the witch had been standing behind it for years, waiting. She was older than he'd expected, or perhaps exactly as old as he'd expected and he hadn't let himself expect it: silver hair pinned carelessly, hands stained brown to the wrist with bark-tan and earth, and eyes the same bright, unfooled color as Coral's. Family eyes. Eyes that had seen too much and filed most of it under *later*.

For a long moment nobody spoke. The wind moved the yarrow. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You found the book," Aldith said at last. Not a question. Her gaze went over Coral's shoulder to Wren, and something in her face flickered — recognition, yes, but also a weary kind of confirmation, like a woman checking off items on a list she'd been dreading. "And you brought a scribe of the Undercourt to my door. Hello, Wren Ashgrave. You've gotten thin."

"You know me?" Coral said sharply.

"I know his handwriting better than my own. I helped file half the documents he copied." Aldith stepped back from the doorway, and the gesture was so ordinary, so domestic — a woman inviting guests into a kitchen — that it made the whole scene obscene. "Come in before the neighbors see. There's tea. There will need to be tea."

* * *

The kitchen was warm and close and full of the smell of chamomile gone bitter from steeping too long. Aldith poured three cups with steady hands, and Wren noticed that only hers were steady — Coral had wrapped both of hers around her cup like she was holding it underwater, and his own had developed a fine scribe's tremor he couldn't quite suppress.

"Say it," Coral said finally. No preamble. No softening. "Say what you did."

Aldith set down the pot. Sat. Folded her hands on the table, and Wren saw that they were shaking now, now that the work of pouring was done and there was nothing left between her and the question.

"I witnessed a sale," she said. "Ninety-eight years ago. I was twenty-three, and I thought I understood what I was agreeing to, which is the oldest lie in the world." She looked at Coral, and her voice did not waver, and Wren understood that this steadiness had cost her decades to build. "Your grandmother came to me in the winter. Mara Brightwater. She had the sight, same as you, same as your mother, and she'd dreamed the mountain waking — dreamed it plain, three nights running, the villages burning, the wells turning to blood. And she went down under Thornfell and she bargained. And the court required a witness from above, a hand freely given, someone who would carry the truth of it back up and keep it. That was me. I carried it for ninety-eight years."

"Carried *what*?"

"The pact. The knowledge. The weight." Aldith's mouth twisted. "Every year on the winter solstice I walk to the boundary stones and renew the wards, and every year I tell myself it's penance, and every year I know it isn't. Penance would be telling someone. Breaking it open. Letting the villages decide what their safety costs." She laughed, short and terrible. "I was a coward, child. That's the confession. Not malice. Cowardice, dressed up as duty, for a century."

"You watched my grandmother sell a woman's soul," Coral said. Each word placed like a knife-point. "You signed your name. And then you took an apprentice — took *me* — and never said a word. You let me grind your herbs and copy your charms and you had a soul in a ledger with our family's name on the bargain, and you said *nothing*."

"Yes."

"You could have told me!"

"To what end?" For the first time Aldith's voice cracked, just slightly, a hairline fracture in old stone. "So you could grow up under it? So you could spend your childhood knowing you were born into a debt? I watched what that knowledge did to Mara — it hollowed her, Coral, it ate her alive from the inside, and I swore no child of her line would carry it until they had to. Until the book came due." She reached across the table, slow, telegraphing the movement, and Coral flinched away from her hand. Aldith let her hand fall. "I was trying to protect you."

"You were trying to protect *yourself*."

"Yes," Aldith said simply. "Both things are true. Most cowardly things are."

The silence that followed was long and ugly and necessary. Wren drank his bitter tea and said nothing, because he knew — oh, he knew — the exact texture of that particular shame, the shame of having served the machine faithfully and called it service. He and the old woman were the same creature separated by ninety-eight years and a doorway.

"There's more," Aldith said finally. She rose, went to the dresser, and took down a small iron-bound box that Wren recognized with a lurch — Court make, seal-work along the seams, the kind of box that held things which shouldn't be held. She set it on the table but did not open it. "There's more, and you're going to hate me worse, and you deserve to hear all of it tonight rather than in pieces."

"Then say it."

"When I took you as apprentice — you were seven, do you remember? Your mother brought you, and you had mud on your shoes and you looked at my herb cabinet like you could already name everything in it—" Aldith stopped herself, breathed. "I consulted the brine-book. The prophecy book. Before I agreed to take you. Because your grandmother made me promise, at the end, that I would watch her line, and I wanted to know what I was watching for."

Coral had gone very white. "What did it say?"

"It said the Salt-Bearer would be born of Brightwater blood, and that she would flee her teacher before the moon's thirteenth turn, and that her flight would be the first step of the descent." Aldith's eyes were wet now, and furious with themselves for it. "Do you understand? Your running away — the night you found the chest, the night you left — none of it was a surprise to me. I read it thirty years ago. I raised you toward it. Every lesson I gave you, every charm, every hour in the mountains — I was preparing you for a road I knew you'd take, and I let you believe it was your own choice."

The cup rattled against its saucer in Coral's grip. Wren watched her wrestle with it — watched the fury come and crest and hang there, ready to break — and then watched something else arrive underneath it, something worse.

"Was it?" she asked. Very quietly. "My choice?"

And here was the moment Wren had been dreading, because he knew the answer, had known it since the archives, and it was not a kind answer and it was not a cruel answer but it was the true one, and true answers were the only currency worth anything anymore.

"Read the rest of the page," Aldith said. She slid the iron box across the table. "It's in there. The full prophecy, the parts the water ate out of your copy. And then decide whether it matters — whether any of us were ever choosing anything — or whether the choosing is the only thing the prophecy can't hold."

Coral didn't touch the box. "Open it."

Aldith's hands hovered over the iron lid. Beneath them, Wren could hear — faintly, impossibly, from inside a sealed Court-box in a hedge-witch's kitchen a hundred miles from the sea — the sound of water moving. A tide, turning. And beneath the tide, almost lost in it, a woman's voice singing something old in a language the mountain had forgotten, growing clearer with every second, as if it had been waiting ninety-eight years for someone to lift the lid.

"Before I do," Aldith said, and her voice shook now, all her hard-won steadiness gone to salt, "you should know that the song is hers. Ulyana's. It's been singing in that box since the night I closed it. And every solstice for ninety-eight years, it has gotten a little louder."

She lifted the lid.
