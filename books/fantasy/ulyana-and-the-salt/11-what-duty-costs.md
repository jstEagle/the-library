## 11. What Duty Costs

The fire had burned down to a red heart by the time Mother Aldith finished speaking the first part of it — the part Coral already knew. The sale. The signature. The night her grandmother had walked down into the mountain with her daughter's soul folded in a twist of waxed linen like a parcel of salt pork.

Coral sat with her knees drawn up and her arms wrapped around them, as if she could hold herself together by force. Across the fire, Wren had gone very still, the way he went still when a text said something he hadn't expected it to say. Aldith stood at the mouth of the cave where they'd sheltered, looking out at the Thornfells under the wrong moon, and her shoulders were bent like a woman carrying water up too many stairs for too many years.

"You knew," Coral said. It wasn't a question. It barely qualified as words.

"I suspected." Aldith did not turn around. "There's a difference, girl, though I'll grant you it's a thin one, and I've been standing on it so long I've worn it smooth."

"You *knew* what she was. What she'd done. You helped broker it." The words came out of Coral like stones from a slung pouch, one after another, each one heavier than the last. "You raised me. You taught me to read the mountains, to mind the tides of weather, to be careful and kind and — and you never once said that my own blood was the price of every safe night's sleep in this valley."

"No," Aldith said. "I didn't."

* * *

The silence that followed was long enough for the fire to shift, for a knot of pine to pop and send sparks spiraling up toward the cave ceiling. Coral became aware, distantly, of how loud everything was: the wind worrying at the cave mouth, Wren's controlled breathing, her own pulse ticking in her ears like a second, smaller heart.

She thought about running. It was, she realized with a strange cold clarity, the only thing she'd ever been good at. She'd run from Aldith's cottage with nothing but a satchel and a grudge. She'd run from every question the smugglers asked her. She'd run through half the Thornfells on legs that knew the passes better than they knew her own face. Running was easy. Running was a skill, honed like any other.

But Ulyana was still in the brine-crypt beneath the mountain, hollow and waiting, and no amount of running would carry Coral far enough away from that.

"Tell me the rest," she said.

Aldith finally turned. In the firelight her face looked older than Coral had ever seen it — not merely old but *weathered*, the way driftwood is weathered, shaped by forces that do not care what they make of you. "You won't thank me for it."

"I haven't thanked you for anything yet."

That earned her something that was almost a smile. Almost. "No. I suppose you haven't."

Aldith came back to the fire and lowered herself onto the stone across from Coral with the care of someone whose joints kept their own ledger. She held her hands out to the warmth, palms open, and Coral saw — really saw, for the first time — how the calluses on them were arranged. Not a hedge-witch's hands. A hedge-witch's hands were scarred from thorns and cauldron-rims and the occasional bitten familiar. These hands had ink in the creases that never washed out. Scribe's hands. Broker's hands.

"You signed it," Coral whispered. "The pact. You're the witness."

"I'm the witness," Aldith agreed. "And now you know why I couldn't stop you from running, however much I wanted to chase you down. The prophecy needed you out in the world, angry and unattached and looking for something. If I'd caged you, you'd have stopped being the Salt-Bearer and started being just another girl who died of old age in a mountain village while something ancient starved politely beneath her floorboards."

"*Starved?*" Coral leaned forward. "What do you mean, starved?"

And Aldith told her the rest.

* * *

"The pact isn't a wall," she said. "Everyone above thinks it's a wall — the mountain is hungry, the pact keeps it fed, the villages stay safe. That's the story we tell children, and it's even true, as far as it goes. But walls don't need feeding, Coral. Walls don't need anything. What the pact actually is — what your grandmother bought with Ulyana's soul — is a *meal schedule*."

Wren made a small sound. When Coral glanced at him, he had his journal out, writing fast, his pen scratching like something alive. "The First Saltworks," he murmured. "Souls rendered into salt. That's what feeds it. That's what the salt *is*."

"That's what the salt is," Aldith confirmed. "Every year, on the turning, the Undercourt renders a portion of what it holds, and the salt rises through the mountain's veins, and the hunger sleeps. One soul, ground fine, stretched thin across twelve villages. It doesn't kill the soul — that's the horror of it, if you want horror — it *keeps* it. Keeps it awake, keeps it aware, keeps it working. Ulyana has been the mountain's supper for three generations, and she has felt every crumb of it."

Coral's stomach turned over. She thought of the salt in Aldith's kitchen, the coarse grey crystals in the wooden box by the hearth, the salt she'd sprinkled on stew and rubbed into preserves and scattered across thresholds against ill luck. All those years. Every meal seasoned with a woman's slow unraveling.

"And if it stops," Coral said. Her voice came out flat and strange. "If I tear the page out, if I free her, if the rendering stops—"

"Then the mountain wakes up hungry," Aldith said simply. "Not angry. Hungry. There's a difference, and the difference matters. An angry thing can be reasoned with, or fled from, or killed. Hunger just *is*. It doesn't hate the villages. It doesn't even see them. It sees food. Twelve villages, four thousand souls, give or take, sitting on top of a thing that hasn't eaten a proper meal in a hundred years because your grandmother taught it to sip instead of gulp."

"How many?" Wren asked quietly. He'd stopped writing. "When it last woke. Before the pact."

Aldith was quiet for a moment. "There's a reason the oldest villages in Thornfell are built where they're built," she said. "On high ground. Behind old walls nobody remembers building. There's a reason the low valleys have names like Hollowmere and the Sunken Fields. The mountain woke in my grandmother's grandmother's time. Three days, the histories say. By the fourth morning, when it slept again, there were nine hundred fewer people in Thornfell, and no bodies, and no graves, and the survivors didn't speak above a whisper for the rest of their lives."

The fire crackled. Somewhere outside, an owl called, absurd and ordinary, and Coral hated it for being ordinary.

"So that's the choice," she said. "Her, or them."

"That's the choice," Aldith agreed.

"It's not a choice." Coral heard her own voice rise, cracking on the second word. "It's arithmetic. It's — you're asking me to do sums with a person. With *my* — with the woman whose soul I'm named to carry, whose songs I've been hearing in my sleep since before I knew her name—" She stopped. Pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes until the darkness bloomed orange. "Four thousand people. And one woman who's already been paying for a hundred years. And everyone — the King, the prophecy, the book that bleeds seawater, and now *you* — everyone wants me to be the one who decides which number wins."

"Yes," Aldith said. "Because that's what duty is. It isn't knowing the right answer. Anyone can know the right answer; the right answer is written in the almanac and painted on the shrine-walls and recited at every harvest festival. Duty is being the one who has to *carry* it afterward. Your grandmother couldn't carry it, so she handed it down. I couldn't carry it, so I hid it. And now it's yours, because fate is a coward and always hands its debts to the youngest person in the room."

"That's not fair."

"No," Aldith said. "It isn't. Welcome to it."

* * *

Coral got up. She didn't decide to get up; her legs simply took it upon themselves, carrying her to the cave mouth, out into the cold, where the sky hung low and close and the moon — the impossible moon, the thirteenth-turn moon with its extra sliver of light that no almanac had ever predicted — sat fat and pale over the black teeth of the peaks.

Behind her, she heard Wren say something low to Aldith, and Aldith answer, and then footsteps: Wren coming out to stand beside her, hands jammed in his sleeves against the cold, breath fogging.

"She's right, you know," he said. "About duty. About all of it."

"Don't."

"I spent six years in the Court archives, Coral. Six years copying ledgers and indexing sales and pretending I didn't understand what I was filing. Do you know why I was exiled?" He laughed, short and without humor. "I asked a question. That was all. I found an entry — a soul sold in 1213, witnessed by a hedge-broker whose name matched a woman still living above — and I asked my superior whether the Court had ever considered that keeping a soul might be different from owning one. Whether the Ledger might contain an error of *category*. They exiled me for it. Struck my name from the rolls. Because the whole edifice — the pact, the salt, the King's beautiful drowned city — rests on nobody ever asking that question out loud."

Coral looked at him. His profile was sharp against the moonlight, and there was something raw in his face that he usually kept filed away behind the sardonic remarks and the endless note-taking.

"So you think I should free her," she said. "Even if it kills four thousand people."

"I think," Wren said carefully, "that the question you're asking is the wrong shape. You keep making it *her or them*. But I've read the Ledger, Coral, all of it, every entry for three centuries, and here's what I noticed that got me exiled: the mountain's appetite has grown. Every fifty years, the renderings get larger. The portions get bigger. The salt comes up heavier. Whatever the pact feeds, it isn't satisfied — it's being *trained*. Taught to expect more. And the Undercourt holds more souls than it renders, thousands of them, sleeping in the brine-crypts, and the King calls them reserves." He turned to face her fully. "The pact doesn't protect the villages forever. It protects them *until*. Until the reserves run low, or the hunger grows past what the renderings can cover, or some future Salt-Bearer stands exactly where you're standing and gets handed exactly this arithmetic, except with worse numbers. Your grandmother didn't buy safety. She bought time. And time, in Thornfell, is purchased with interest."

The wind gusted, carrying the smell of snow from the high passes and, beneath it, faint and mineral, the smell of salt.

Coral wrapped her arms tighter around herself. "Then what am I supposed to do?"

"I don't know," Wren admitted. "I genuinely don't. I've read every text, and none of them end well, because every text was written by people who chose the numbers over the person and then had to live with it. But I know this much: the Drowned King wants you to believe the choice is simple, because a simple choice serves him. Aldith wants you to believe it's unbearable, because unbearable choices serve her guilt. And the prophecy wants—" He paused, choosing the word with a scribe's precision. "The prophecy wants to be *fulfilled*. 'The Salt-Bearer descends.' It doesn't say what she does when she gets there. Maybe that's the point. Maybe the descent is the only part anyone could predict, because everything after depends on what you bring down with you."

"And what do I bring down with me?"

Wren smiled then, tired and crooked. "Ask yourself what you've been carrying since the waystation. It isn't the page. It isn't the blood-key. It's the same thing the dead trade in, down below. Longing." He nodded back toward the cave, toward Aldith's bent silhouette against the fire. "Go talk to her properly. Not as the Salt-Bearer interrogating a witness. As her apprentice. You owe her anger, maybe. But you don't owe her silence, and neither does she."

* * *

Coral went back in. Aldith hadn't moved; she sat exactly where she'd been, hands still open to the dying fire, and when Coral sat down beside her — beside, not across, and she saw Aldith register the difference — the old woman let out a breath she might have been holding for a century.

"I'm still furious with you," Coral said.

"I'd be worried if you weren't."

"You let me grow up seasoning my food with her. You let me learn songs that were probably *hers*, that drifted down through the family like heirlooms, and you never said whose voice they came from."

"They were hers." Aldith's voice cracked, just slightly, on the word. "Your grandmother sang them to me when I was small, before the sale, before everything. Afterward she never sang again. I taught them to you because it seemed like the only piece of her worth passing down, and because — gods forgive me — because I hoped the songs would lead you to her someday, the way they seem to have done. I wanted it mended, Coral. I just never had the courage to mend it myself."

Coral stared into the coals. The weight of it all pressed down on her — four thousand lives balanced against one, a hungry god beneath her feet, a hollow woman in a crypt who smelled like home, an old liar who had loved her badly and faithfully in equal measure. Somewhere in her chest, beneath the fury and the dread, she felt the longing Wren had named, vast and patient as an aquifer.

"Aldith," she said slowly. "The pact. The salt. The rendering. Is there any version — any version at all — where the mountain gets fed and Ulyana goes free?"

Aldith was quiet for a long moment. Then she reached into her cloak and drew out something wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapping it with fingers that trembled only a little: a lump of raw salt, grey-white, veined with something darker, older.

"This came up from the deep veins the winter you were born," she said. "It's different. Look at it. Feel it." She pressed it into Coral's palm, and Coral felt it — warm, almost, and humming faintly against her skin like a struck bell that wouldn't finish ringing. "I've shown it to no one in sixteen years. I don't know what it means. But I know this: the mountain made it the season you came into the world, and it sings when you touch it, and I have spent your whole life telling myself that was coincidence." She met Coral's eyes, and there was something in her face that might have been hope, or terror, or both wearing the same expression. "Before you choose, girl — before you hand anyone the arithmetic — go down into the deep places and find out what the mountain itself wants. Because I've begun to suspect that nobody in a hundred years has bothered to ask."

Outside, the thirteenth moon slid slowly behind the eastern ridge, and in the silence that followed, Coral could have sworn she felt the whole mountain lean closer to listen.
