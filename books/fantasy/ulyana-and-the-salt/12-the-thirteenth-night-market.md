## 12. The Thirteenth Night Market

The Undercourt kept its own calendar, and Wren had spent eleven years learning to read it.

Not with ink and almanac — the drowned dead had no use for paper that could rot — but in the tides. The city's streets flooded and drained on a rhythm older than the pact, and every thirteenth turn of the moon above, the waters drew back from the lowest quarter of the city and the Night Market opened like a wound that had learned to smile.

He felt it coming before he saw it: a pressure change in the standing water that lined the canals, a hush in the eternal dripping of the eaves. The dead stopped their slow promenades along the embankments and turned, all together, all at once, toward the sunken plaza where the market stalls would rise.

"They're looking at something," Coral whispered beside him. She had her hood up, her broker's-daughter disguise pinned with a mother-of-pearl clasp she'd stolen from a stall three nights ago, and her hand was nowhere near the knife at her belt because she'd promised him — sworn, actually, with her whole palm pressed flat against the wet stone — that she would not draw steel in the Court unless he said the word.

"They're listening," Wren corrected. "There's a difference. Down here, it's always a difference."

"And what are they listening for?"

"The water going out." He nodded toward the great canal that bisected the merchant quarter, its surface lying flat as poured glass. "Watch."

The canal exhaled.

It was not a dramatic thing, not a whirlpool or a roar. The water simply *left*, sliding away down channels too narrow to see, and within the space of ten heartbeats the canal bed lay bare and gleaming, cobbled with white stones that might once have been bones. And up from the bed rose the market.

Stalls first — they came up out of the mud like drowned ships righting themselves, awnings unfurling, lanterns kindling one by one with flames that burned blue-green and cast no heat. Then the vendors: shapes in robes of kelp and woven reed, faces that were mostly suggestion, hands that were mostly intention. And then the goods, laid out on tables of driftwood and slate — jars of captured echoes, spools of thread spun from last words, bottles of longing in a dozen vintages, each labeled in a script that writhed if you looked at it directly.

Coral made a sound low in her throat. Not fear. Wren had learned to distinguish her fears by now; this one was closer to hunger.

"It's beautiful," she said, and hated herself for it. He could hear the hatred, tucked inside the wonder like a stone inside a peach.

"That's how it gets you," Wren said. "Everything down here is beautiful first. The ugliness is always a second draft."

"You would know. You wrote some of it."

"I transcribed some of it," he said. "There's a difference. Down here—" 

"—it's always a difference," she finished, and almost smiled, and the almost was somehow worse than the real thing, because it reminded him what she looked like when she wasn't afraid, and he needed her afraid tonight. Afraid people were careful. Careful people lived through thirteenth nights.

* * *

They went down into the market with the crowd, which meant going down among the dead.

Wren had forgotten how it felt to walk in a current of them — the cold that came off their bodies like the cold off deep lake water, the way sound behaved strangely around them, arriving a half-beat late as though the air itself were reluctant to carry anything they said. He kept his eyes forward and his gait bored. A scribe who walked like a scribe got remembered. A scribe who walked like a man running an errand for his master vanished.

Coral, to his ongoing astonishment, was better at this than he was. She moved through the dead the way she moved through the Thornfell passes — reading the crowd three turns ahead, drifting left when a knot of mourners stalled ahead of them, never hurrying, never hesitating. Once, a vendor reached out to touch her sleeve, some pale many-jointed thing offering a bottle of *homesickness, aged forty years, mountain-corked*, and Coral smiled at it — a real smile, warm, terrible — and said, "Not tonight, thank you," in the flat vowels of a Court brat, and the vendor withdrew as though rebuffed by a duke's daughter.

"Where did you learn that?" Wren murmured when they'd passed beyond earshot.

"Learn what?"

"That smile. That voice."

She was quiet for three steps. "Aldith used to say I could sell salt to the sea. I thought she meant it as a compliment." A pause. "I don't think she did anymore."

He didn't answer that. There was nothing to say about Mother Aldith yet — not until they'd done what they'd come to do, not until the page was torn or wasn't. Some truths you carried only after you'd earned the right to survive them.

The Ledger was kept in the Hall of Accounts, at the market's far end, beneath an archway carved from a single whale's jaw. On ordinary nights it sat behind three doors and a tide-gate, guarded by clerks who had been clerks so long they'd forgotten their own names and answered only to their ink-stains. But on the thirteenth night, tradition held — and tradition, in the Undercourt, held harder than law — that any soul might come and *view* the Ledger. View. Not touch. The distinction was written into the founding covenants in letters of dried brine, and Wren had copied those letters himself, twice, in his former life, and knew exactly how much room they left a clever man.

"Viewing hall's open till the moon turns," he told Coral, nodding toward the whale-jaw arch, where a line of petitioners shuffled inward, each clutching their token of grief or grievance. "We go in with the next wave. We look at Ulyana's entry. We memorize everything — dates, witnesses, terms, the seal. And then we leave, and we do *nothing else*—"

"But?"

"There's no but."

"Wren."

"There's no *but*," he repeated, and heard how unconvincing he sounded, and hated it. "If we take the page tonight, in the middle of the market, with half the Court shopping — the alarm-tides come. You've seen what the canals do when someone cheats at cards. Now imagine the whole city is the card."

"So we wait. We copy the entry, we climb back up, we find another way."

"There is no other way. That's the point of ledgers." He kept his voice level, his eyes moving over the crowd out of habit. "The soul is bound by the record. Break the record, break the binding. Copying it does nothing. We need the *page*, Coral. Torn true, torn by the Salt-Bearer's hand, on the thirteenth turn. It has to be tonight, and it has to be theft."

"Then why did you just say we do nothing else?"

"Because I wanted to hear myself say it out loud before I talked myself out of being sensible." He exhaled. "Because there's a third option, and it's worse than both, and I've been trying not to think about it since we came down the tollgate."

Coral stopped walking. In the blue-green lantern light her face was all angles and resolve, seventeen years old and older than the mountain. "Tell me."

"The viewing hall lets you view. The clerks let you *petition*. Any soul may file a writ of contest against an entry — challenge the sale, dispute the terms. It goes before a magistrate of accounts. It takes years. Decades. Unless—" he watched her face as he said it, "—unless the petitioner offers collateral. Something the Court can hold while the writ is weighed. A pledge of service. A bond of the body."

"No," Coral said immediately.

"It's how I was going to buy my pardon back, before you. File a writ against my own exile, pledge my service, spend thirty years shelving souls in the archive—"

"*No.* You're not pledging yourself to this place so I can—"

"Not me." He met her eyes. "You're the Salt-Bearer. Your blood opened the tollgate. If anyone's petition carries weight, it's yours. And if you pledge your service as collateral on a writ contesting Ulyana's sale, the clerks must, *by covenant*, produce the original page for examination. Which means the page leaves the Ledger. Which means, for exactly as long as it takes a magistrate to sneeze, the page is *in a room with us*."

The color drained from her face and then flooded back twice as bright. "That's insane."

"Yes."

"They'd never release it to—"

"They must. Covenant of the Open Hand, clause nine. I *wrote* the summary of it for the King's privy council. Well — I copied it. Beautifully. With marginal illuminations." His mouth twisted. "The one useful thing I ever did here."

"And the catch. There's always a catch."

"The catch is that if the writ fails — if the magistrate rules the sale valid — the collateral is forfeit. You'd belong to the Court. Like her." He didn't say *your grandmother sold her*; he didn't have to. The whole architecture of it stood between them, ugly and load-bearing. "And the writ will fail, Coral. The sale was witnessed and sealed by the King himself. No magistrate overturns the King."

Silence, except for the market breathing around them — the lap of returning water against the canal walls, the murmur of the dead haggling over bottled longing.

"So it's not really a plan," Coral said slowly. "It's a way to get the page out of the book for ten seconds."

"Ten seconds is a very long time," Wren said, "if you know what you're doing with them."

* * *

They were three stalls from the whale-jaw arch when Wren saw the herald.

He knew him instantly, the way you know a scar in a mirror. Vessik. King's-herald, fourth rank, voice like a bell rung underwater. Wren had sat across the scriptorium table from him for six years, copying censuses, and had once lost a month's wages betting on ink-drying races against him. Vessik had been dead even then, technically — everyone in the Court's service was — but he'd been the closest thing Wren had to a friend below the mountain, which said more about Wren than about Vessik.

Vessik stood beneath a lantern of green flame, wearing his tabard of office, and he was not looking at the market. He was looking at them.

No — not at them. At *Wren*. And as Wren watched, Vessik inclined his head. Not a bow. An acknowledgment. *I see you. I know you. I am seen seeing you.*

"He's been behind us since the bridge," Coral breathed. She hadn't turned her head; she'd caught it in the reflection off a jar of tears-for-sale, which was a trick Wren intended to ask her about later, if there was a later. "Since before the market opened. Is he—"

"Herald of the fourth rank. The King's voice when the King doesn't want to speak." Wren's mouth had gone dry, which was absurd, given how much water surrounded them. "Which means the King already knows we're here. Has known. Possibly since the tollgate. Possibly since the day I was exiled, if I'm flattering myself."

"What does he want?"

"To be seen wanting something. That's the message. The King doesn't send a herald to arrest you; he sends clerks for that, and they're much less polite. He sends a herald to remind you he's watching. To make you nervous. To make you *rush*." Wren turned deliberately away and began walking toward the arch, unhurried, a man with an errand. "Nervous people rush. Rushing people die in flood-tides."

"And what do we do?"

"We don't rush." They joined the line of petitioners shuffling under the whale jaw. Above them the bone curved, yellowed, ancient, pocked with carvings — names, Wren knew, thousands of names, the first entries ever made in the Ledger, cut into the jaw itself before anyone thought to bind pages. "We go in. We view. We leave. We let him watch us do exactly what any grieving soul has a right to do."

"And the plan? The writ? Ten seconds?"

"Still true. Still insane." He glanced sideways at her. "But now we know the King knows. So we do it anyway, and we do it *beautifully*, and when the alarm-tides come, we run like the sea itself is after us. Because it will be."

Coral laughed — a short, sharp, terrified laugh, quickly strangled. "You're enjoying this."

"I'm a disgraced scribe committing archival fraud against a god-king in a drowned city," Wren said. "Enjoying is the only part I get to keep."

The line moved. The whale jaw swallowed them.

* * *

The viewing hall was smaller than he remembered, or perhaps he had simply been smaller when he'd last stood in it — younger, unbent, still believing that the Court's cruelty was a system he could document his way out of. Rows of reading stands stretched away into gloom, each lit by its own floating wisp of corpse-light. Clerks drifted between them like patient fish. And at the far end, upon a dais of black coral, the Ledger itself lay open on its stand: a book the size of a coffin, bound in hide that no one would name, its pages thick as sailcloth and soft as skin.

They took a stand near the back. A clerk materialized — they always did, drawn by intent the way sharks were drawn by blood — and asked in a voice like sand through an hourglass what entry they wished to view.

"Ulyana," Wren said. "Salt-witch. Sold under the old pact. We'll want the full folio. Terms, witnesses, seals."

The clerk regarded him for a long moment. Corpse-light moved in the hollows where its eyes had been.

"You speak like an archivist," it said.

"I was one."

"Of this Court?"

"Once." Let it chew on that. Let it carry the name of the exile back to whoever collected such things. Distraction was also a tool, and Wren had never had many tools besides words.

The clerk drifted away. Coral leaned close, her shoulder nearly touching his, her whisper barely stirring the air. "Ten seconds. Talk me through it again. Slowly."

"When the folio comes, you read. All of it. Every word — your eyes need to touch everything, because the moment you tear it, everything you read becomes the only copy left in existence. Especially the witness line. *Especially* the witness." He'd turned that over a hundred times since the archives: a living witness to a century-old sale, someone walking around in the sunlight above who had helped bind Ulyana. Finding that person might matter more than the page itself. Might matter more than everything. "Then you say the words. *I, Coral Brightwater, Salt-Bearer, daughter of the line of Brightwater, do file writ of contest against this entry, and do pledge my service as collateral under the Covenant of the Open Hand.* Say it exactly. The clerk must accept it. Must fetch the original page for verification. And when it lays the page on the stand—"

"Ten seconds."

"Tear it. Don't hesitate, don't apologize, don't look at me. Tear it and run for the north stair, and whatever you hear behind you — the water rising, the bells, *me* — you don't stop. You don't stop until you're above the snowline."

"And you?"

"I'm slower than you and I know the flooding patterns of this district better than anyone alive or dead." He smiled, and found that it was mostly true, the smile. "I'll be behind you. Probably. Statistically."

"Wren—"

"Folio's coming," he said, because it was.

The clerk returned bearing a slab of pages bound in cord, and laid it on the stand with the reverence of a priest presenting relics. The top page bore a name in faded iron-gall ink, and beneath the name, a date, and beneath the date, terms written in a hand Wren recognized — recognized with a lurch that went through him like cold iron — as his *own teacher's*. The man who had taught him his letters had written out the sale of Ulyana the salt-witch, neat and precise and damning, a hundred years before Wren was born.

Coral began to read. Her lips moved. Her finger traced the lines, touching each word as though she could press the meaning into her skin, and Wren watched the color change in her face — the fury, the grief, the terrible tenderness — and he watched, too, past her shoulder, through the archway behind them, where the green lanterns of the market burned.

Vessik had not followed them in. Heralds did not enter viewing halls; protocol forbade it.

Instead, the herald had done something far worse. He had raised one grey hand, delicately, ceremonially, and rung a small silver bell.

One note. Clear as ice cracking. It rolled through the market, through the hall, through the water in the walls, and every clerk in the viewing hall stopped moving at once — every clerk, all of them, heads turning in unison toward the dais where the great Ledger lay open.

Toward the empty place in the great Ledger where a folio had been removed.

"Oh no," Wren said quietly. "Oh, you clever, cruel bastard. He's not watching us. He's *herding* us."

The clerk who had fetched the folio straightened. Its voice, when it came, was no longer sand. It was the sound the mountain made before it avalanched.

"Coral Brightwater," it said. "Daughter of the line of Brightwater. The Court acknowledges your presence — and reminds you that the thirteenth turn completes at moonrise tomorrow. Whatever you intend—" the corpse-light in its skull flared, blue-white, furious, "—you should intend it *now*."

Coral's hand closed around the edge of the folio's first page.

Wren, heart hammering, did the only thing left to a scribe in a burning archive: he stepped between her and the clerks, spread his arms wide, and began, in the old tongue, in a voice trained to carry across silent scriptoriums, to recite the Covenant of the Open Hand — clause by clause, word by word, buying her time the only way he had ever bought anything.

With words.

Behind him, he heard the paper begin to tear.
