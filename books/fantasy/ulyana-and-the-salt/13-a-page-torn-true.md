## 13. A Page Torn True

The Thirteenth Night Market smelled of drowned flowers and hot copper.

Coral moved through it with Wren's hand at her elbow and a broker's daughter's smile pinned to her face like a brooch she was afraid would fall off. Around them the Undercourt spilled itself into its once-a-century abundance: stalls of bottled sighs, eel-lanterns swimming in glass, a woman selling memories of rain to creatures who had forgotten what sky was. The water stood waist-deep in the market streets, warm as bathwater, and everything — every awning, every awning-shadow, every face — shimmered beneath it as though the whole city were seen through tears.

*Don't run,* Wren had told her, back in the archive's shadow. *Running is how they know.*

So they strolled. They browsed. Coral let a vendor press a cold pearl against her wrist and pretended to consider buying her own future back from him, and all the while her eyes kept sliding to the far end of the square, where the Court archives rose out of the floodwater like a black tooth.

And all the while, at the edge of her vision, the herald followed.

He did not walk so much as *proceed* — a tall figure in robes the color of deep water over stone, his face smooth as river-polished bone. He never hurried. He never fell behind. When Coral stopped to admire a stall of singing shells, the herald stopped three stalls down and admired nothing at all. When she turned, he turned, in that delayed, courteous way that was somehow worse than pursuit.

"He's herding us," she murmured into Wren's ear.

"Of course he is." Wren lifted a jar of pickled moonlight, examined it, set it down. "The King doesn't send his herald to stop thieves. He sends him to watch them fail. Or—" a pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk, "—to watch them succeed, and follow them home."

"You're very comforting."

"I'm very honest. It's my worst quality."

They drifted on. The tide of the market carried them, slow and glittering, toward the archives, and Coral felt the torn page of the Ledger waiting for her the way you feel a tooth that has to come out — a hot, throbbing knowledge under everything else. Somewhere in that black building was a page with Ulyana's name on it, written in salt-ink a hundred years ago by a clerk who had no idea what he was signing away. Somewhere in that building was the difference between a hollow woman in a brine-crypt and a whole one walking free.

Somewhere behind them, above them, the thirteenth moon was filling out its impossible sliver, night by night, and tonight it hung full and wrong over the mountain's crown, leaking light down through a mile of stone.

"Left," Wren breathed. "Past the shell-singer. There's a service door where the tax-collectors enter. I used it when I was young and stupid."

"And now?"

"Now I'm old and stupid, but I know which doors remember me kindly."

* * *

The door remembered him unkindly.

It was made of whalebone laced with iron weed, and when Wren pressed his palm to it, the whole frame shuddered and exhaled a breath of ancient brine, and somewhere inside the wall something said, in a voice like sand shifting: *Wren of the Third Scriptorium. Struck from the rolls. Struck from the rolls. Why do you knock?*

"Because I'm an idiot," Wren said pleasantly, "and because I've brought a gift."

He had. Coral felt it against her ribs as she drew it out: the false page they'd spent two days preparing in a flooded scriptorium while the market assembled itself around them — parchment aged in tide-water, ink mixed from cuttlefish and grief, Ulyana's name copied in Wren's flawless court-hand. A decoy. If the theft was discovered tonight, the archives would find their Ledger intact but tampered, a page swapped, and the trail would lead wherever Wren chose to write it.

The door considered this. Then it sighed open onto darkness that smelled of dust and drowned libraries, and Coral stepped through before either of them could think better of it.

Inside, the archives were dry.

That was the horror of it, she thought — after days of wading through the Undercourt's standing water, after streets where the dead traded tides like coins, here at the heart of everything the air was parched and still, and the shelves rose up and up into a dark so complete that the little eel-lamp Wren lit seemed to be burning underwater instead of air. Millions of pages. Every bargain ever struck beneath the mountain. Every soul weighed, priced, entered, filed.

Every name.

"The Ledger of Souls is kept in the Vault of Living Entries," Wren whispered, already moving, his lamp held low. "Pages that aren't finished yet. Pages whose bargains are still being honored. Ulyana's will be there — the pact is still paid, so the entry is still open."

"How many pages?"

"Hundreds. Thousands. The vault sorts itself, if you know how to ask." He glanced back at her, and in the lamplight his face looked older than it had on the road, older than exile, older than the Tollgate of Teeth where he'd paid a memory to pass. "Coral. Once we do this, the city will know within minutes. The alarm-tides don't distinguish between thieves and guests. Everything in these streets that can rise will rise, and it will be looking for the heartbeat that took the page."

"I know."

"The water rises fast. Faster than you'd believe."

"Wren." She caught his sleeve. "I have watched you lie beautifully for six days. Don't start telling the truth now just to scare me."

Something flickered across his face — not quite a smile, but its cousin. "You're braver than I was," he said, and turned, and led her down into the dark.

* * *

The Vault of Living Entries opened for Coral before it opened for Wren.

She didn't understand it at first — the great brass doors had ignored Wren's knock entirely, and then she'd simply reached out, meaning only to steady herself, and the metal had gone soft as skin under her fingers and swung inward with a sound like a long-held breath released. Wren stared at her. She stared at her own hand.

"Your blood," he said quietly. "The key. I told you — the crone told you. The pact was sealed with your grandmother's line. The vault knows family."

"It knows *debt*," Coral said, and walked in.

The vault was round, and quiet, and terrible. Pages hung in the air like leaves suspended in amber, each one turning slowly on currents of air that had nowhere to go, each one glowing faintly with its own small light. Coral saw names she didn't know glowing green — bargains honored. One or two glowed red, and those made her chest hurt to look at, though she couldn't have said why. And near the center, alone, hanging lower than all the rest, one page burned white as sea-foam.

Ulyana.

Coral's feet knew the way before her mind caught up. She crossed the vault floor through drifting, whispering paper until she stood beneath it, close enough to read.

*Ulyana of the Saltworks. Sold by Maren Brightwater, hedge-witch of Thornfell, in payment of the Pact of Safety. Term: perpetual. Witnessed: Aldith of the Hollow Road.* And below, in fresher ink, added in a different century: *Heir of the seller's line approaches. Key confirmed. Keeper's succession possible.*

*Keeper's succession possible.* The King's offer, written down before it was ever spoken. Her stomach turned.

"Coral." Wren's voice came sharp and low from the doorway, where he stood watching the corridor. "The herald's left the market. He's coming here. We have perhaps four minutes, and I am being generous with myself."

She reached up.

The page hung just beyond her fingertips, turning, turning, patient as anything that has waited a hundred years. She jumped; her fingers brushed it and it spun away. Again. Again. Her palms were slick. Below her the vault had begun, very softly, to hum — did the pages know? Could they feel what she intended, the way the door had known her blood?

"Stand on my shoulders," Wren said, appearing at her side, setting down the lamp. "Before you argue — yes, it's undignified. Yes, I'll tell no one. Climb."

She climbed. His shoulders were thinner than they looked and steadier than they felt, and she rose up into the drifting constellation of living entries with her arms outstretched, and this time her hands closed on the page.

It was warm. That was the thing she hadn't expected — warm as a sleeping animal, faintly pulsing, and through her fingers she felt it *know* her, felt some vast attention shift and settle, and the humming of the vault climbed half a tone.

"Tear it," Wren said. "Now. Quickly."

But she hesitated — one heartbeat, two — because the page was alive, because tearing it felt like tearing something that could feel it, because a hundred years of patience hung in her grip and she was about to end it violently, in a stolen moment, with alarms rising—

*Coral.* Not aloud. In the salt-taste that had lived on her tongue since the waystation, in the book bound in brine, in the prophecy that had named her. *Salt-Bearer. Descend. Reclaim.*

"No going back," she whispered.

And she tore the page true.

* * *

The sound was not loud. That was the worst of it. The page parted with a soft rip, like linen, like skin, and for one full second nothing happened at all.

Then every page in the vault turned red.

Not gradually — all at once, thousands of entries flaring crimson like a wound opening across the whole round room, and the hum became a bell-tone, became a shriek, became a voice speaking through every page simultaneously in a language Coral's bones understood even though her ears didn't. The brass doors slammed. Water burst upward through the floor drains in black ropes. And beneath her, Wren staggered as the whole mountain shivered around them like a dog shaking off rain.

"What did I say about hesitating," he gasped, catching her as she dropped from his shoulders. "Run."

They ran.

The corridors of the archives had become rivers. Black water poured down the stairwells in sheets, and where it touched the walls, the walls wept, and where it pooled, things began to stand up out of it. Coral saw the first of them clearly as they splashed past a junction: a drowned man unfolding himself from a puddle no deeper than a hand, joints bending wrong, lantern-light passing through him like he was made of fog and grievance. His eye sockets found her. His mouth opened. What came out was not a shout but a tide-sound, deep and rhythmic, and from every direction of the flooding dark, other mouths answered it.

"They're calling the hunt," Wren shouted over the water. "The alarm-tides! Keep left — left, Coral, the left arch —"

The left arch had been a doorway an hour ago. Now it was a waterfall. They went through it anyway, plunging into cold that knocked the breath out of her, and came out into the market streets where the Thirteenth Night Market was drowning in real time.

Stalls collapsed into the flood. Eel-lanterns scattered like sparks. The vendors — the dead ones, most of them, the ones she'd always half-seen through — had stopped selling and started singing, that same tide-call rolling street to street ahead of the water, and the water came obediently, wave after wave pouring down the canyon-streets of the Undercourt, chasing them uphill through a city built downhill.

Coral ran with the page crushed against her heart and laughed.

She heard her own laughter and was appalled at it — high, wild, bubbling up through terror like air through water — but she couldn't stop, because this was it, this was the feeling she'd been chasing since she was nine years old and first ran away, the feeling the road gave her in flashes and the mountains gave her in dreams: *no going back*. Every bridge behind them dissolving. Every choice burned. Just the running, and the page beating against her ribs like a second heart, and Wren beside her keeping pace with a scribe's bad lungs and a traitor's good instincts, shouting directions through the roar.

"There!" He pointed with the lamp, which was somehow still lit, still burning underwater now, a small miracle neither of them had time to appreciate. "The cistern stair — it surfaces near the Salt Road — if we can get above the flood line —"

The drowned dead came out of the alleys.

They didn't chase so much as *converge*, rising from doorways and drain-grates and the flooded windows of tenement-hives, dozens of them, scores, their tide-song layering into something almost musical, almost beautiful, and Coral understood with a lurch that they weren't angry. They were *grieving*. This was what the Undercourt did when something was taken: it mourned, loudly, everywhere, and its mourning drowned.

A dead woman stepped into their path — tall, crowned in kelp, her face eroded smooth as the herald's — and Coral did the only thing she could think of. She held up the torn page.

The woman stopped.

The tide-song faltered around them, a ripple through the ranks of the risen dead, hundreds of hollow faces turning toward that scrap of parchment in a runaway's fist. And Coral, gasping, soaked, half-drowned and wholly terrified, heard herself speak in a voice that did not entirely feel like hers:

"She was sold. She was sold, and the debt is called, and the page comes *with* me."

Silence, except for water.

Then the tall woman inclined her head — slowly, ceremonially, the way one bows to a funeral or a coronation — and stepped aside, and the crowd of the dead parted before them like a wound closing, all the way down the street, all the way to the cistern stair.

"They think you're a broker," Wren breathed, staring. "Or worse. They think you're *authorized*."

"Then let's not correct them."

They ran through the parting, up the stairs, water thundering at their heels, and the last thing Coral saw of the Undercourt was the flood rising to swallow the Thirteenth Night Market whole — the stalls, the songs, the bottled futures — and above it, impossibly far above, through a mile of splitting stone, the thirteenth moon looking down at her through the crack it had waited a century to open.

Behind them, the mountain began, very quietly, to wake.

Ahead, on the Salt Road, someone was waiting with a lantern — a small figure, wrapped in rags, hollow-eyed, smelling of brine and patience.

Smelling, Coral realized, her heart slamming, like home.
