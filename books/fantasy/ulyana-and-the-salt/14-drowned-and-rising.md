## 14. Drowned and Rising

The first thing was the salt.

Not taste — she had no tongue yet, no mouth, nothing so ambitious as a body — but salt as a fact, salt as the whole architecture of existence. Salt pressing in from every direction like an argument that would not end. She had been steeped in it for so long that she had forgotten there was any other substance in the world. Brine filled her where her thoughts should have been. Brine filled her where her name should have been.

*Name,* she thought, or something adjacent to thought, something like a fish nosing at glass. *I had one of those.*

She could not find it. She reached inward, down through the layers of herself, and found only smooth blankness, the way a stone worn by centuries of water is smooth — not empty exactly, but finished, polished past the point where anything could hold on. There had been things carved here once. She was almost sure of it. There were grooves and hollows in her that had the shape of meaning, the negative space of a life: a groove the width of a lullaby, a hollow shaped like someone's hand, a long channel worn deep that might have been grief or might have been love or might have been both, because in her experience — except she had no experience, that was the entire problem — those two wore the same channel in a person.

Then the page tore.

She did not hear it. Distance meant nothing in the brine-crypt; sound died three inches from her skin. But she felt it the way a hound feels its leash snap — a sudden slackness in the thing that held her, a loosening in the great ledgered weight that had pressed on her chest since before she understood what chests were for. Somewhere above her, impossibly far above, paper parted along a line some clerk had ruled a century ago, and the parting traveled down through stone and water and law, and arrived inside her as a single word:

*Rise.*

Her body remembered how before she did.

It began with her hands. They flexed in the dark, fingers uncurling like ferns in spring, and the brine around them shivered and went still, respectful, the way water goes still around a thing it has decided to fear. Then her arms. Then the slow, grinding unfolding of her spine, vertebra by vertebra, each one cracking loose from its century-long sleep with a sound like ice on a warming lake. She rose through the crypt's cold strata — through the layer where the salt was dense as glass, through the layer where it hung like snow that had changed its mind about falling, through the layer where it whispered — and the whispering stopped when she passed, all of it at once, a thousand small voices holding their breath.

She broke the surface of the brine-pool without a splash.

The crypt received her standing form the way a church receives a thief: silently, disapprovingly, with a great deal of shadow. It was a low vault of black stone furred with mineral bloom, lit by veins of pale light that ran through the walls like frozen lightning — the mountain's own slow blood, she understood somehow, though she could not have said how she knew. Niches lined the walls, dozens of them, each holding a vessel of cloudy glass, each vessel holding a curl of light that turned slowly on itself like a moth in amber. Souls, said the knowledge that lived in her body instead of her mind. Rendered souls. The raw stock of the pact.

One niche stood open. Empty. The glass of it still bore the ghost-print of two hands, pressed against the inside, waiting to be let out.

She looked at her own hands. They were white as drowned things, wrinkled at the fingertips, crusted with salt that glittered faintly in the wall-light. They were old hands. That surprised her. She had expected — what? She didn't know. She had nothing to expect with. But the hands were old, and the knuckles were swollen, and there was a scar across the left palm, thin and silver, that her eyes snagged on and refused to leave. Her thumb moved over it, back and forth, back and forth, tracing a shape it seemed to know better than she did. The scar was the shape of a hook. Or a crescent moon. Or —

Nothing. The thought slid off the smooth place inside her and sank away.

*Walk,* said the word that had freed her, still ringing in her bones. *Rise. Come.* And beneath it, fainter, threaded through it like a hair caught in wax: *home.*

So she walked.

* * *

The crypt door had not been opened in a hundred years, but it opened for her the way a mouth opens for a yawn, reluctantly and all at once, and beyond it the Undercourt spread out below her like a drowned star fallen into a well.

She stood on a ledge of black rock and looked down into the city, and even hollow — especially hollow — she could not stop looking. Streets of standing water, perfectly still, perfectly mirror-bright, laid out in a grid older than any kingdom above. Buildings rose out of the flood with their lower stories submerged, their windows glowing a soft corpse-green, and between them moved the dead: figures of varying transparency going about the patient business of eternity, trading in tides, weighing longing on little brass scales, drifting through doorways without troubling to use them. Above everything, far above, hung the underside of the mountain itself, vast and ribbed as a whale's throat, studded with hanging gardens of white crystal that dripped upward.

Beautiful. The word surfaced in her from somewhere, unbidden, and brought nothing with it. She looked at the most beautiful thing she had ever seen and felt the way a coin must feel in a beggar's cup: valued, perhaps, but belonging to no one.

She descended.

The water took her to the ankles, then the knees, then the waist, and it was warm — warmer than the crypt, warm as bathwater, warm as something she had once known. Where she stepped, the dead turned to look. Not hostile. Curious, in the way of fish. One of them, an old man whose transparency showed the street behind him through his chest, drifted close and studied her face with eyes like fogged glass.

"Salt-witch," he said. Not a question. "You're out early."

She opened her mouth. Nothing came. She tried again, and what emerged was a voice like wind through a keyhole, rusted, unused, wrong: "I don't—" A pause while she searched the smooth place. "I don't remember."

"None of us do, at first." He said it kindly, the way you'd tell a new widow that the grief gets quieter. "It comes back in pieces. Usually the pieces you least want." He drifted backward, bowing slightly, the courtesy of the dead. "They'll come for you soon, you know. When they notice the page is torn."

"The page," she said. The words felt borrowed. Everything felt borrowed. "Whose page?"

He smiled, and his smile was the saddest thing in the drowned city, which was saying a great deal. "Yours, I'd have thought. You're the one rising." And he was gone, dissolved into the green-lit crowd, leaving her standing in the warm water with her old hands opening and closing at her sides.

*Yours.* She turned the word over. It fit nothing. It fit the scar, maybe. She touched it again, back and forth, back and forth.

Somewhere above her — streets up, air up, a distance she could feel in her teeth — something tore again. Not paper this time. Water. A great volume of it, moving fast, moving *wrong*, and beneath the sound of it a second sound: footsteps, two sets, running, and a third set that was not footsteps at all but the slap and drag of something following.

The alarm-tides. She knew that without knowing how. The city had woken, and the city was hunting.

And threaded through the panic-noise of the flooding streets, cutting through it like a blade through silk, came a smell.

That was the strange part. She had no reason to trust her nose; she barely trusted her hands. But the smell arrived in her hollow places and *filled* them, the way water fills a cup made to its exact measure, and every groove and hollow in her polished-blank self lit up at once like windows at dusk. Woodsmoke. Wet wool. Crushed thyme. Chalk dust and candle tallow and, underneath it all, under everything, a note of clean river-stone and iron that her body recognized the way a compass recognizes north.

*Home,* said the word in her bones again, and this time it was not an instruction. This time it was a direction.

She ran.

* * *

She ran through streets that were learning to be rivers. The flood came down the terraces in silver sheets, and the dead scattered before it, and she ran through the middle of it all with water tearing at her hem, and she did not know why she was running except that the smell was getting stronger and the smooth place inside her had begun, very faintly, to ache.

That was the worst of it — worse than the forgetting, worse than the old hands and the rusted voice. The ache. Because a hollow that aches is a hollow that remembers being full. She ran through the drowning city chasing a smell, and every step hurt in a place she could not point to, and she understood with terrible clarity that she was not running toward something. She was running toward the *shape* of something. An absence with edges. A person-shaped hole in the world that her body knew and her self did not.

The stairs took her up three terraces. The water chased her up two of them. On the third she rounded a buttress slick with weed and nearly ran them down.

Two people. The first was a man, young-ish, ink-stained to the elbows, carrying a leather tube of papers like a soldier carries a wounded comrade. His eyes went wide when he saw her — wide with recognition, she thought, though recognition of what she could not say — and he stumbled back and put out an arm as if to shield the second person, which was absurd, because the second person needed no shielding.

The second person was a girl.

Young. Red-haired, the red of rowan berries, plastered dark with floodwater. Thin in the way of someone who has been walking mountains on smuggler's rations. A witch's satchel at her hip, a hedge-witch's satchel, patched and mended and loved. She was breathing hard, and there was a cut on her cheekbone, and she was staring at Ulyana with an expression that cracked something in the crypt-smooth center of Ulyana's chest straight down the middle.

Because the girl was looking at her the way you look at a door you've knocked on your whole life, that has finally, finally opened.

"You're real," the girl said. Her voice shook. "You're — oh, you're *real*, you're actually—"

Ulyana knew her.

That was the lie her heart told, in the first half-second, before the smooth place could smooth it over: *I know her.* The certainty was total and instant and completely unsupported by a single memory. She did not know the girl's name. She did not know her face, not as a fact, not as a history. But her body knew her the way a shore knows the tide — knew the weight of her, the rhythm of her, the exact pressure she was meant to exert. The smell of woodsmoke and wet wool and crushed thyme poured off the girl and into all the hollows at once, and Ulyana swayed on her feet like a woman struck.

"I—" she said, and the keyhole-voice failed her, and she tried again. "I know you."

"Do you?" The girl laughed, wet-eyed, wild. "Do you really? I'm Coral. Coral Brightwater. I'm — my grandmother was Maren Brightwater, and she—"

"Maren." The name fell into her and vanished, swallowed by the smooth place, leaving not even a ripple. "I don't—" She pressed her palms to her temples. The scarred palm, the crescent-hook scar, right over her eye. "There's nothing. There should be something and there's *nothing*, I can feel where it should be and it's just — smooth. Like a stone. Like a stone at the bottom of a pool."

The girl — Coral — flinched as if struck. Behind her, the ink-stained man made a small sound, quickly smothered, the sound of a scholar watching a hypothesis die.

"It's the page," he said quietly. "Torn pages free the body, not the soul. The soul stays in the Ledger until it's rendered or returned. I told you, Coral. I *told* you what we'd find."

"I know what you told me." Coral didn't look at him. She couldn't seem to stop looking at Ulyana, and Ulyana found she could not look away either, and the mutual staring was doing something to the ache, feeding it and soothing it in the same breath, like scratching a burn. "Ulyana. Your name is Ulyana. I came down here — we came all the way down here, through the teeth-gate, through the flooded market, Wren stole a *page* off the King's own reading desk — to bring you home."

Home. The word rang the bell in her bones again. Ulyana looked at this girl — this stranger, this rowan-berry stranger with river-stone and iron in her scent — and felt the person-shaped absence in her tilt, orient, align, like iron filings snapping toward a lodestone.

"You smell like home," Ulyana said.

It came out wrong. She heard it come out wrong — too blunt, too bare, the naked statement of a child or a ghost — and she saw Coral's face crumple and reform, crumple and reform, grief and joy fighting for the same ground.

"That's—" Coral's voice broke. She steadied it with visible effort. "That's probably the thyme. I carry thyme. For wards." A wet laugh. "Or it's the woodsmoke. Every inn from here to Thornfell smells of woodsmoke."

"No." Ulyana shook her head slowly. The motion felt ancient, practiced, the gesture of someone who had shaken her head ten thousand times before at ten thousand offers of easier explanations. "It's not the smoke. It's not the thyme." She lifted her scarred hand, helpless, offering it palm-up between them like evidence. "I don't remember you. I want you to understand that. I don't remember *anything*. But whatever I was, before the smooth place — whoever I was—" Her voice frayed. She pushed through it. "Whoever I was, they smelled like you. They knew you. This—" she pressed the scarred palm flat against her own sternum, over the hollow, over the ache, "—this knows you. Whatever else they took, they didn't take this. I don't think they *could* take this."

Coral made a sound that was not quite a laugh and not quite a sob and stepped forward into the floodwater, close enough now that Ulyana could see the salt spray drying white on her lashes, close enough that the warmth of her reached through the crypt-cold of Ulyana's skin like sun through a window.

"Then we'll get the rest back," Coral said fiercely, softly, both at once. "Whatever's missing. We'll go and take it back. That's the whole point of me. That's why I'm here."

Behind her, the ink-stained man — Wren, the name arrived without being offered, another borrowed thing — had gone very still. He was looking past them both, down the flooded stairway, at the water.

The water had stopped chasing them.

It stood in the streets below in perfect stillness, mirror-flat, and in its surface Ulyana saw reflections that did not match the world above them: lights moving where no lights were, shapes gathering, tall and slow, in the drowned avenues. The dead, rising. Not curious now. Summoned. And threading up through the still water, faint as a hymn heard through a floor, came a sound she felt in her new-unwrapped spine before her ears caught it — a low, vast, patient vibration, the sound of something enormous turning over in its sleep and beginning, without hurry, without malice, to consider waking.

"She's hollow," Wren said. He wasn't talking to either of them anymore. He was talking to the mountain, or to himself, or to some old teacher in his past. "A salt-witch walking around without her soul isn't empty, Coral. Emptiness just sits there. A hollow *pulls*. And there is something under this city that has been pulling on the world for a hundred years, and if it notices what she is—"

The vibration deepened. In the mirror-still flood, the false lights went out, all at once, everywhere — snuffed like candles before a draft — and the silence that followed was the specific silence of a held breath.

Ulyana's scarred hand closed, without her permission, into a fist.

* * *

They ran again, all three, up through the terraces while the city behind them filled with a darkness that had weight. But Ulyana ran differently now. She ran with her scarred hand stretched out behind her, and Coral ran with her own hand caught in it, and neither of them spoke, because there was nothing to say that the grip did not say better.

*I don't know you,* said the grip. *But I am not letting go.*

And somewhere below them, in the dark that was learning to be hungry, something old and enormous felt the pull of the hollow woman passing overhead — and began, with the patience of stone, to climb.
