## 15. The Hollow Woman

They made camp in the lee of a fallen pillar three streets below the Saltworks road, where the standing water ran shallow and clear and the drowned dead had not yet thought to look. Coral built no fire. Firelight drew eyes down here — living and otherwise — so she crouched instead over a bowl of river-water and read its surface the way Aldith had taught her, back when she'd still believed the old woman's lessons were kindness rather than rehearsal.

The water showed her nothing useful. It showed her Ulyana.

Ulyana sat at the camp's far edge with her back to a wall of black stone, knees drawn up, hands loose in her lap like a marionette set down mid-performance. She was watching the water too — or watching through it, past it, her gaze fixed on some middle distance where nothing lived. Her hair was still crusted with brine from the crypt. Her skin had the grey-gold pallor of things kept long underwater, beautiful the way driftwood is beautiful: shaped by loss into something elegant.

She had been free for two days. She did not know it.

Coral dipped her fingers in the bowl and let the vision scatter. *Say something to her,* she told herself. *You've said something to her a hundred times since the crypt.* And every time, Ulyana had turned that patient, empty face toward her and answered with perfect courtesy, the way one speaks to a stranger who has been helpful on a long road.

*Thank you for pulling me out of the dark.*

*You knew my name. That was kind.*

*Where are we going? I will follow, if you wish it. I have nowhere else.*

Nowhere else. As if four centuries of service, a daughter left above ground, a soul sold for a valley's safety — as if any of it had never happened at all. The page torn from the Ledger had cut her chains, but it had cut everything else away with them. What rose from the brine-crypt was Ulyana's body walking around without Ulyana inside it, and what remained was worse than grief. Grief at least remembered what it mourned.

Coral crossed the camp. She did it slowly, giving the hollow woman time to see her coming, because there had been one bad moment on the first night when Coral had touched her shoulder unannounced and Ulyana had moved like water finding a crack — fast, silent, a hand at Coral's throat before Coral's heart had finished its next beat. She hadn't squeezed. She had simply held, with a strength that had nothing human in it, and looked at Coral with those tide-grey eyes and asked, *Why did you startle me?* as though violence were a language she spoke fluently but had forgotten the grammar of.

Coral sat down beside her, an arm's length away. Close enough to talk. Far enough to survive a reflex.

"The water's rising behind us," Coral said. "Wren thinks the alarm-tides will reach this district by tomorrow. We should keep moving."

"Whatever you think best." Ulyana's voice was low and even, a well with no bottom. "You've been kind to me. I don't know why, but I trust you."

*Don't say that.* Coral's throat closed around it. *Don't give me your trust like a coin you found in the street. It isn't mine. It's yours, and someone stole it, and I'm trying to give it back.*

What she said instead was: "Do you remember anything? Songs, maybe. You used to sing. In the crypt, the walls were scored with — " She stopped. She had already told her this. She had already watched the same blankness settle over the same face.

"I remember the salt," Ulyana said, after a pause that might have been thought or might have been the simple delay of a mechanism winding through. "I remember that it hurts. Not how. Just that it does." She turned her head, and those eyes found Coral's with terrible directness. "You smell like home."

Coral laughed. It came out broken, half a sob wearing a laugh's clothes. "That's the third time you've told me that."

"Is it? I'm sorry. I don't seem to keep things." Ulyana looked down at her own hands, flexed them, studied them as if they belonged to a stranger whose habits she was politely cataloguing. "There's a shape in me where something used to be. When I try to look at it directly, it's like looking at the sun through water. I know it's there because everything bends around it." A pause. "Is that what having a soul is? Bending around the thing you lost?"

"You haven't lost it," Coral said fiercely. "It's taken. There's a difference. Taken things can be returned."

Ulyana considered this with the gravity of someone being taught a law of nature. "You speak like someone who has decided something very large," she said. "I hope it doesn't cost you what it cost me."

"It won't," Coral said. "I won't let it."

And Ulyana smiled — small, distant, courteous — and Coral felt the smile go through her like a fishhook, because she had read the crypt walls, she had traced the letters Ulyana had carved over four hundred years, and she knew what that mouth looked like when it laughed. She knew it secondhand, carved in salt-scars and desperation, and the woman sitting in front of her would never make that shape again unless Coral found a way to pour her back into herself.

Footsteps behind them. Wren, returning from his circuit of the streets, his scribe's satchel clutched to his chest like a breastplate. He stopped when he saw them sitting together, and something moved across his face — pity, mostly, and beneath it the cold arithmetic he could never quite keep hidden.

"We need to talk," he said quietly. "Not here. But yes, we need to talk."

* * *

They walked while they talked, because Ulyana followed without needing to be asked and Coral could not bear to leave her alone with her own silence. The drowned city opened around them street by street: canals where the water stood vertical like panes of green glass, rooftops crowned with pale coral that had grown in the shapes of chimneys, market squares where the dead had hung their lanterns — spheres of soft blue light that drifted at chest height and dimmed respectfully when the living passed. Beauty everywhere, and none of it meant for her. Coral had felt like an outsider since the Tollgate of Teeth. Now the feeling had sharpened into something with edges.

Wren waited until Ulyana had drifted ahead, drawn — as she always was — toward any running water, standing before a canal with her head tilted like a bird listening for worms.

"She hummed today," Coral said. It came out accusatory, aimed at him, though he'd done nothing. "While we walked. Three notes, over and over. It's from one of her songs. The one about the daughter. She doesn't know she knows it."

"That's not nothing," Wren said carefully. "Residue. The body remembers what the soul carried. It means the connection isn't severed — only stretched."

"Then stretch it back."

"Coral." He said her name the way a physician says the name of a disease he is about to describe. He glanced toward Ulyana's straight back, then drew Coral aside under an archway furred with white stone-moss. "Listen to me. All of it, before you hate me."

"I already know what you're going to say."

"No, you don't. You know the version you've been carrying since the crypt, the one where we find the vessel at the Saltworks and pour her back in and everyone weeps with joy. I've been checking the archives in my head. Every text I ever copied, every marginal note the Court scribes passed around like contraband." His voice dropped further. "A salt-witch without her soul isn't an empty jar waiting to be filled. The soul is what *contained* her. Ulyana's power — the tide-craft, the binding-words, all of it — was held in shape by her soul the way a river is held by its banks. Take the banks away and you don't get an empty riverbed. You get a flood."

Coral's arms prickled. "She hasn't done anything. Two days, and she's been gentle as a lamb."

"Because she's been near you." Wren's eyes flicked to the canal, to the figure standing motionless at its edge. "Salt answers longing. That's the whole cursed physics of this place — the currency is longing, the trade is longing, the pact itself runs on it. Right now the only longing in her is the shape of you. *You smell like home.* Her power has nothing else to organize around, so it organizes around that. Around keeping you near. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

The words arrived slowly, each one a stone dropped into still water. "If anything threatens me—"

"The hunger wakes." He nodded. "The mountain's hunger, the thing the pact contained for a century — it didn't die when you tore the page. It went looking for a vessel. And there is exactly one creature in Thornfell right now with a salt-witch's power and nothing inside her to say no."

Coral looked at Ulyana. The hollow woman had begun, very softly, to hum again. Three notes. Over and over. The water in the nearest canal had begun to move — not flowing, not rippling, but *rising*, a slow swell climbing the vertical glass of it, reaching toward her like a plant toward light. The drifting lanterns in the square had gone out. Every one of them, all at once, as if the city were holding its breath.

"Ulyana," Coral called, gently, and the humming stopped, and the water sank, and the lanterns bloomed back one by one like eyes reopening. Ulyana turned. Smiled that small, courteous, devastating smile.

"Yes?" she said. "Did I do something?"

* * *

They walked on. Coral kept Ulyana on her left side, between her and the open water, and hated herself for it — hated the flinch she couldn't suppress when Ulyana's hand brushed hers on a narrow stair, hated that Wren's words had installed a guard at the door of her own heart. This was the woman her grandmother had sold. This was the sacrifice at the center of everything, the debt Coral had crossed a mountain to pay. And now Coral caught herself measuring the distance between them like a sailor measuring weather.

*This is how it starts,* she thought. *This is how Grandmother must have done it. First you love them, then you fear them, then you tell yourself the fear is a kind of love, and then you sell them and call it protection.* The thought made her sick. She pushed it down where it couldn't breathe and kept walking.

They stopped at dusk — if dusk could be said to exist down here; the light simply thickened, blue going to black — in a chamber that had once been a temple or a counting-house, its columns carved with ledgers in relief, thousands of names worn smooth by water and time. Wren spread their remaining food: hard bread, dried fish, a skin of the fresh water they'd bought at ruinous price from a blind ferryman. Ulyana ate because Coral asked her to, mechanically, thoroughly, like a soldier following orders. Then she folded her hands and sat, and watched Coral with that patient attention, and Coral realized with a lurch that she was waiting to be given a purpose. That was all she had. A body that obeyed, and a longing bent entirely around one girl, and nothing else in the world.

"Tell me about myself," Ulyana said suddenly.

Coral blinked. "What?"

"You keep looking at me like I'm a book you've read and I haven't." No accusation in it. Simple observation, delivered with that bottomless calm. "You know my name. You knew it before you pulled me from the dark — you said it, and it fit, like a key. So tell me. Who was I?"

Coral's heart slammed. Here it was. The opening she had been praying for and dreading in equal measure. She set down her bread. She took a breath.

"You were a salt-witch," she began, and her voice cracked on the second word, and she started again. "You lived in the Thornfells, in the villages above. You sang — you had a daughter, and you sang to her, songs about the sea your grandmother came from. And there was a mountain that was hungry, and people were dying, and someone — someone made a bargain." Coral's hands had become fists in her lap without her permission. "They sold your soul to the court beneath the mountain. They traded you for a century of safety. And you spent four hundred years in the dark, carving your name into the walls so that someone, someday, would remember you existed."

Silence. The lanterns drifted. Somewhere far off, water fell in the dark with the patience of stone.

Ulyana listened to all of it the way one listens to a story about a stranger — attentive, courteous, unmoved. When Coral finished, she tilted her head.

"That's sad," she said. "I'm sorry for her." A pause. "For me." She frowned slightly, testing the word like a loose tooth. "But it doesn't... catch anywhere. It slides off. Is that wrong of me?"

"No," Coral whispered. "No, it's not wrong. It's not yours to carry yet. We're going to get it back for you."

"You keep saying *we*." Ulyana's eyes were steady on hers, and for a moment — just a moment — something moved behind them, deep down, like a shape passing under ice. "You talk about me as if I matter enormously to you. More than the danger you're in. More than whatever bargain brought you down here." The shape under the ice passed again. "Why? Why would you cross a mountain for a woman you've never met?"

Because you're my blood. Because my grandmother sold you and I am the interest on her debt. Because I read your name in a book of prophecies beside my own and I have been falling toward you ever since. Because the crypt walls broke my heart and I have been carrying the pieces down through the dark to give them back to you.

"Because someone owes you," Coral said. "And I intend to pay."

Ulyana studied her for a long moment. Then she reached out — slowly, telegraphing the movement, the way one reaches toward a frightened animal — and touched Coral's cheek with fingers cool as sea-wet stone.

"You grieve," she said wonderingly. "I can feel it coming off you like heat off a hearthstone. You grieve for someone standing right in front of you." Her brow furrowed, and the shape beneath the ice surged close enough that Coral saw it plainly in her eyes — vast, sorrowful, *almost* surfacing. "I wish I knew what I did to you."

"You didn't do anything," Coral managed. "That's the whole tragedy. You didn't do anything at all."

The moment broke. The shape sank. Ulyana withdrew her hand, folded it back into her lap, and became again the courteous stranger, and Coral excused herself to check the perimeter because if she stayed one more second in that gentle blank gaze she was going to come apart in front of everyone.

* * *

She found Wren at the chamber's far end, pretending to study the ledger-columns, which was his way of pretending not to watch her fall apart.

"She almost remembered," Coral said. "Just now. Something surfaced. If I keep talking to her, if I tell her more—"

"And if you push too hard?" Wren didn't turn from the carvings. "I told you what she is now. Longing with a salt-witch's hands. Every memory you force toward that hollow place, you're pouring water into a vessel with no bottom. Some of it will reach her. Some of it will reach the *hunger*, and the hunger is a faster learner." He finally looked at her, and his face in the lantern-light was older than his years. "I've seen the archives' descriptions of a soulless witch coming awake, Coral. The Court keeps records of everything, including the three times it happened before. Villages. Whole villages, Coral. Drowned from the inside, salt in their lungs, smiling."

Coral wrapped her arms around herself. "So what do we do? Leave her hollow forever? That's your answer? We stole her back just to make her a safe, empty pet?"

"No." Wren's jaw tightened. "My answer is the Saltworks, and the vessel, and doing it *fast* — before the thirteenth moon finishes turning, before the King stops hunting us and starts negotiating, and before—" He stopped.

"Before what?"

He hesitated. Wren, who lied the way other men breathed, who had talked his way past tollgates and heralds and the Drowned King himself — Wren hesitated.

"Before she stops asking who she was," he said at last. "She asked you tonight. That's the residue fighting. But it's fading — I've been counting. Yesterday she asked six times. Today, once." He looked back toward Ulyana, seated alone in the lantern-light with her hands folded, patient as the drowned. "When she stops asking altogether, Coral, there'll be nothing left to pour her soul back into. The vessel cracks. And what's left won't be Ulyana, and it won't be harmless either. It'll be the hunger wearing her face, and it will know exactly where home smells like."

Behind them, softly, in the blue dark, Ulyana had begun to hum again. Three notes. Over and over. And the water in the channels along the walls had begun, very quietly, to rise.
