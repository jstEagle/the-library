## 16. The King's Mercy

The tide came in without water.

Wren felt it first as a pressure behind his eyes, the old scribe's ache he'd learned to read like weather — the sense of a great weight shifting somewhere below the world. Then the flooded street around them went still. Not calm; still. The black standing water that filled the drowned avenues of the Undercourt had been moving all night, restless as a crowd before an execution, and now every ripple stopped at once, as if the whole city had drawn a breath and forgotten to let it out.

Coral felt it too. He saw her hand go to the satchel where the torn page lay hidden — Ulyana's page, ripped from the Ledger of Souls with all the grace of a thief and none of a scholar — and Wren's stomach dropped through the wet cobblestones into whatever lay beneath them.

"Walk," he said quietly. "Don't run. Running tells him where we are."

"Running tells *us* where we're going," Coral hissed back, but she matched his pace, boots splashing softly, and they turned down the Street of Nine Bells together.

The hollow woman walked between them. That was what Wren had taken to calling her in the privacy of his own skull, though never aloud, never where Coral could hear. Ulyana moved like someone following music only she could half-remember, her bare feet finding the cobbles without stumbling, her salt-grey hair drifting on air that did not stir. She had spoken perhaps six words since rising from the brine-crypt. All six had been fragments of songs. And twice — twice — she had reached out in the dark and touched Coral's sleeve, just the sleeve, with two fingers, the way a blind woman reads a face.

*She smells like home,* Ulyana had said of Coral, to no one, to the water. Wren had watched Coral's face when she heard it, and had understood then that he was already lost alongside them both.

The bells began as they crossed under the third archway. Nine of them, naturally, though Wren had never once heard nine ring in order. They tolled now in a sequence he knew from the archives — the summons-sequence, the one reserved for traitors and debtors who fled their accounts. His own name had been rung out in it once, eleven years ago, from the other side of a door he had sworn never to open again.

"He knows," Coral said. It wasn't a question.

"The page is singing to him." Wren could feel it too, faintly, through the leather of the satchel: a thin thread of sound, like a wet finger circling a glass rim. Torn ledger-paper did not stop being ledger-paper. It remembered its master the way a dog remembered a whistle. "Every step we take, it's telling him."

"Then we should have burned it."

"And freed nothing? No." Wren shook his head. The page was the tether. As long as it existed outside the Ledger, Ulyana stayed risen — hollow, but risen. Burn it, and she snapped back to the crypt like a fish on a line. He had explained this to Coral twice. She kept suggesting burning it anyway. Grief made arsonists of reasonable people. "We keep walking. We get to the Saltworks gate before—"

The water rose.

It came up around their ankles without warning, warm as blood, and Wren stopped because there was nowhere left to walk to. The street ahead — the long, lamp-lit avenue that led toward the First Saltworks, toward everything — was gone. In its place stood the Drowned King.

He did not arrive so much as assemble: first the crown, a circlet of whalebone and drowned stars; then the shoulders, draped in robes that moved like kelp in a current that wasn't there; then the rest of him, tall and patient, his face the face of a man who had been handsome so long ago that handsomeness had worn away like a coin's profile. His eyes were the color of deep water over deep stone. Wren had seen those eyes across a council table, eleven years ago, when he had been a scribe of the Court and not a fugitive from it, and they had looked at him then exactly as they looked at him now: with the mild, bottomless interest of something that found all living things briefly diverting.

"My scribe," the King said. His voice arrived inside the ear rather than through it, which was worse. "And my thief. And—" his gaze slid to Ulyana, and for one unguarded moment something moved behind it, some sediment of feeling stirred up from the floor of him, "—my salt-witch. Returned."

"She was never yours," Coral said.

"Coral." Wren's warning came too late and would have changed nothing anyway. He had learned that about her within a day of meeting her: warnings landed on Coral Brightwater the way rain landed on the sea.

The King smiled. It was, Wren thought, the most frightening thing he had done yet, because it was practiced. Because he had smiled at supplicants for centuries and gotten very good at it.

"Never mine," the King agreed pleasantly. "Sold. There is a difference, I grant you, though my archivists spent four hundred years arguing where precisely it falls. Come. You are dripping on my streets, and the night market closes at moonset, and I have an offer to make that deserves better than a doorway."

* * *

He led them — there was no question of leading being optional — to a plaza Wren knew from maps older than his exile: the Basin of Accounts, where the Court conducted its gentler business. The water here was shallow and clear as glass, and beneath it lay coins, thousands upon thousands of them, each one pressed flat by the weight of the sea above, each one a longing somebody had paid. Lamplight came from no lamps. The sky overhead was the mountain's roots, veined with mineral light, and against that ceiling the drowned dead drifted like slow birds, watching.

A table stood at the basin's center. On the table: three chairs, a decanter of something amber, and a single sheet of paper, blank, laid square to the table's edge with a precision that made Wren's fingers itch. Scribes noticed these things. The paper was Undercourt stock, heavy, brine-cured, meant for contracts that outlived nations.

"Sit," said the King.

Ulyana sat. She sat the way she did everything, obedient to some instruction Wren couldn't hear, folding her hands in her lap and gazing at the blank page with an expression that might have been recognition or might have been nothing at all. Coral remained standing until Wren touched her elbow, and then she sat too, rigid, her jaw set like a girl called before a magistrate.

Wren took the third chair because refusing it would accomplish nothing and because his knees, after three days of flight through flooding streets, had opinions of their own. Eleven years ago he had sat in a chair very like this one while the Court stripped his name from its rolls. He remembered thinking, then, that he would rather die than sit in such a chair again. Memory was a liar. The chair was comfortable. That was the trap of comfort; it never announced itself.

"You tore a page from my Ledger," the King began, pouring the amber liquor into cups none of them had asked for. "In four hundred years of keeping souls, no one has ever torn a page. They have stolen books entire — fools, the pages went dark in their hands. They have forged entries — poets, the ink refused them. But torn?" He shook his head with something adjacent to admiration. "Only someone who wanted one soul specifically, irreplaceably, enough to wound the book itself. Sentimental vandalism. My favorite kind."

"We'll be going," Coral said, and made to rise, and the water in the basin rose with her, a wall of it, gentle and absolute, and she sat back down.

"Please," said the King, and the word was soft as silt. "Hear the shape of your situation before you drown in it. The thirteenth turn completes tomorrow at moonrise. The pact-salt stands. The Ledger stands. The crypt stands, empty now, but empty is not free — you know this, scribe, you've read the renderings. She walks because the page holds her name above the water. Take the page away and she goes back down. Keep the page and she stays up — hollow, songless, forgetting more each hour, until what's left of her is a hunger wearing a woman's face. You've seen the beginnings of it. Don't lie to me; I can see you've seen it."

Wren had. Last night, at the edge of the night market, Ulyana had stopped before a stall where the dead sold bottled tides, and had stared at the bottles with an expression Wren had last seen on the faces of starving men outside a bakery window. Not wanting. *Needing.* A soulless salt-witch did not merely lack; she *craved*, and craving was the root the pact had been grown from, the hunger the mountain wore like a taproot into the world. He had not told Coral how badly it frightened him. Coral was frightened enough for three.

"So," the King continued, and here he leaned forward, and the lamplight found the coin-profile of his ruined face, "here is my mercy, and mark me, it *is* mercy, offered once, by a king who has not offered anything in a century. Return the page. I will restore it to the Ledger myself, with my own hands, and I will strike the binding-clause — the old sale, your grandmother's bargain, all of it — voided, annulled, done. Ulyana remains above. Free. Walking. Alive in the way of her kind. She will never remember her daughter. She will never remember her songs. She will never remember *you*, child of her child's child. But she will be *here*—" he tapped the table, once, "—and not *there*. And in exchange, the pact stands. The salt flows. Thornfell sleeps safe in its valley for another hundred years, and no village burns, and no child wakes to find the mountain has eaten the road home."

Silence. Somewhere above them, impossibly far, the thirteenth moon was turning toward full.

"And if we refuse?" Coral asked.

"Then at moonrise I come for the page, and I come for her, and I put her back in the dark where she has kept faith with me for four hundred years, and I keep the pact by force, and the mountain keeps its appetite, and everyone lives except the one person you climbed down here to save." The King spread his hands, a gesture of terrible reasonableness. "I am offering you the same outcome with less grief. This is what kings do. We sell you the rope you were going to hang yourselves with anyway, and charge you gratitude for it."

Wren should have been listening to the terms. A part of him was — the old scribe, the part that had spent thirty years learning the grammar of bargains, was already drafting counter-clauses in the back of his mind. But another part of him had gone very quiet, because the King had said *your grandmother's bargain*, and had said it looking at Coral, and had then said *scribe* in a different tone entirely, and Wren had been in courts long enough to know when a second offer was being folded inside the first like a letter inside an envelope.

"There's more," Wren said.

The King's ruined smile widened a fraction. "There is always more. Yes. For you, scribe. Return the page, restore the pact — and come home. Your exile ends tonight. Your name goes back in the rolls, fresh ink over the old. The archive opens to you again — all of it, even the sealed stacks, even the renderings from before the First Saltworks. You will die old, and warm, and respected, with more knowledge in your head than any scribe in the history of the Court, and it will be *yours*, lawfully, forever." He slid a cup across the table. The amber liquid caught the light like trapped honey. "You have missed it. Don't insult us both by denying it. I have watched you walk my streets these three days. You read the plaques. You corrected a misdated cornerstone on the Street of Nine Bells — aloud, to no one. You are homesick, Wren of the Rolls. Homesick men make excellent terms."

And God help him, it was true. That was the obscene thing. It was all true. Wren sat in the comfortable chair with the honey-colored cup in reach and felt eleven years of cold roads and cheap inns and eating his name instead of speaking it rise up in him like floodwater. He had loved the archive. He had loved it the way other men loved women, or gods, or war — the sealed stacks at midnight, the smell of brine-cured vellum, the particular silence of a room where everything ever known waited patiently to be known by him. He had dreamed about it. Actual dreams, waking with his hands curled as if around a pen.

He looked at Coral.

She was staring at Ulyana. Her grandmother's grandmother — no, her grandmother, the arithmetic of it still made Wren's head hurt — sat beside her with her hands folded, humming three notes of a song over and over, a song Coral had been singing snatches of for days without knowing why. And Coral's face, as she listened, was doing the thing Wren had come to think of as her real face, the one under the competent smuggler's mask: open, furious, terrified, hopeful, all of it at once, a heart with no locks on any of its doors. She had run away from everything safe in her life because safe had been built on a lie, and here was the King offering to rebuild the lie and call it mercy, and Wren could see her getting ready to say no in a way that would get them all drowned.

*Don't,* he almost said. *Take the deal. Take the hollow woman and the safe villages and live.* It was the sensible counsel. It was the counsel a friend would give.

But he had watched her tear the page. He had watched her do it knowing what it cost, and he understood suddenly — sitting in the comfortable chair, with his whole lost life held out to him on a salver — that he had not followed Coral Brightwater down a mountain because it was sensible. He had followed her because she was the first person he had met in eleven years who tore pages. Who wounded the book itself. Who believed one soul outweighed an archive.

His whole life, Wren had written what others dictated. Kings. Courts. Prophecies he hadn't believed until they bled seawater onto his hands. Here, finally, was a sentence he got to write himself.

"No," Wren said.

The word came out steady. He was almost proud of it.

The King went still, in the way that water goes still before it remembers it is also ice. "Scribe—"

"I know what you're offering. I want you to know that I know. That's what makes it a refusal instead of ignorance." Wren pushed the cup away, gently, the way you'd return something borrowed. "You're right that I'm homesick. You're wrong about what home is. I thought it was the archive. It turns out it was the *reading* — and there's more reading in one hour beside her than in forty years in your sealed stacks, because she reads people, and mountains, and grandmothers' crimes, and she reads them *true*, which is more than the Ledger ever managed." He stood. His knees had stopped having opinions. "Keep your pardon. Keep your rolls. And keep your pact — because we're going to break it, Your Majesty, properly, at the root, and what replaces it won't need souls at all. You'll hate it. You'll thank us later. Both things can be true; I've read enough contracts to know."

For a long moment the Basin of Accounts held no sound at all — not the water, not the drifting dead, not the three notes Ulyana hummed. Even she had stopped.

Then the Drowned King laughed. It was a terrible sound, rusted with disuse, and it shook mineral dust from the ceiling-roots of the mountain, and every coin beneath the clear water trembled in its pressing.

"Oh, *good*," the King said, and the warmth in it was the worst thing yet. "Oh, splendid. A refusal with *reasons*. Do you know how long it has been since anyone refused me with reasons?" He rose, and the table sank back into the basin, and the blank contract-page dissolved into foam. "Very well, scribe. Very well, little thief. Then hear the terms of *no* mercy: at moonrise, when the thirteenth turn completes, I will come for the page, and for her, and for the pact, and I will not offer chairs. Run to your Saltworks. Sing your songs. See what love maps onto a mountain that eats maps." His eyes found Coral's, and softened by one degree, which from him was a landslide. "You have until moonrise. I genuinely hope you surprise me. It has been *such* a long time."

The water went out of the plaza all at once, and the lamplight with it, and when Wren's vision cleared they stood alone in the dark on the Street of Nine Bells, three figures and a torn page, with the mountain's heart somewhere ahead of them and the thirteenth moon climbing toward full behind.

Ulyana had stopped humming. She was looking at Wren — at him, directly, the first time her eyes had fixed on anything with intent since the crypt — and her mouth opened, and what came out was not a song.

"*Saltworks,*" she said. Clear. Certain. Her first whole word in three days. "*I remember the way.*"

Coral grabbed Wren's arm hard enough to bruise. "Did she just—"

"Yes," Wren said, already running. "Run."
