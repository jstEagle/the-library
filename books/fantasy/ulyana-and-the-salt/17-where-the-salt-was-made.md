## 17. Where the Salt Was Made

The song came back to Ulyana in pieces, the way tide leaves things on a shore.

She hummed it without knowing she was humming — three notes, then a rest, then five more — while they walked the drowned road beneath the mountain, and Coral had learned to listen for it the way sailors listen for weather. The first time Ulyana had produced the melody, standing ankle-deep in a flooded gallery with her hollow eyes fixed on nothing, Coral had nearly wept. It meant something in that ruined head still remembered how to want. And wanting, Coral had decided somewhere around the ninth hour of walking, was a kind of map.

"Again," Coral said softly. "Please."

Ulyana turned her face toward her. In the pale light of Wren's lantern-glass, she looked like a woman carved from sea-foam and left out too long — beautiful, and faintly wrong, as though someone had made her from memory and missed a few details. Her eyes were the color of water over sand. They did not hold anything. But her mouth moved, and the notes came: *three, rest, five.*

"That's the one," Coral breathed.

Wren, trudging behind with the Ledger page wrapped in oilcloth against his chest like a stolen infant, groaned. "You said that at the last fork. And the fork before that. Coral, I love you like a sister I never wanted, but if you tell me 'that's the one' one more time and it turns out to be another dead end—"

"It's not a dead end." Coral crouched at the junction where the road split, one branch descending into blackness, the other curving toward a distant sound of falling water. She pressed her palm flat to the wet stone. "Listen. When she sings the third note, the walls answer. Feel it."

Wren did not touch the wall. He looked at her instead, and his expression did the thing it always did when he thought she wasn't watching — the thing where all his sardonic armor slipped and he looked young, and frightened, and loyal past all sense. "The walls are answering because this whole mountain is a throat," he said. "Everything echoes down here."

"Not like this." She kept her hand on the stone. "It's not an echo. It's a harmony. Echoes repeat what you give them. This is *adding* to it." She looked up at him. "Someone built these halls to sing back. Someone who knew the song."

Wren was quiet for a long moment. Water dripped somewhere far off, patient as a clock. "The First Saltworks," he said finally. "Every archive I ever robbed had a mention of it, buried under forty layers of euphemism. *The rendering halls. The deep kitchens. The place where the pact is fed.* No map ever agreed with any other map about where it was." He gestured vaguely at the dark. "Because you can't map your way there. You have to be *led*."

"And she's leading us." Coral rose, knees cracking, and touched Ulyana's elbow — gently, always gently, the way you'd touch a bird that might startle into flight. "Ulyana. Which way?"

Ulyana looked at the two branches of the road. Her brow furrowed — a small, human motion, the ghost of thought moving across an empty house. Then she began, very softly, to sing again, and walked toward the sound of falling water.

Coral followed. Behind her, after a moment, so did Wren, muttering something in the old tongue that Coral was fairly sure was a prayer and slightly less sure was not a curse.

* * *

They had been descending for two days.

That was the thing no one told you about going down into a mountain: it stopped feeling like travel and started feeling like burial. The smuggler's passes Coral had grown up in were steep but honest — switchbacks, cold air, the smell of pine and stone. This was different. The Undercourt's roads were wide enough for processions, worn smooth by feet that had stopped being feet centuries ago, and they went *down* with the patience of something that had all the time in the world. The air grew warmer, then hot, then strangely cool again, smelling of brine and iron and something sweetly rotten, like flowers left in a locked room.

Coral had marked their way the only way she knew how: with chalk sigils of her grandmother's grammar, ward-marks and way-marks scratched at every turning. It steadied her hands. It also, she admitted in the small hours when they slept in niches carved for coffins, terrified her — because the marks were Aldith's marks, taught to her before she could write her own name, and every one she drew was a small confession that she had run from that woman and was now, in the deepest hole in the world, becoming her.

On the second night — if *night* meant anything in a place where the only sky was stone — they found the first salt.

Wren found it, actually. He had gone ahead to scout a chamber off the road, and his shout brought them running with hearts hammering, expecting drowned dead or worse. But the chamber held no horror. It held a wall.

The wall was white.

Not painted, not plastered. Grown. Crystals the size of a man's forearm jutted from the rock in ridges and whorls, catching the lantern-light and throwing it back in colors that shouldn't have existed — not just white but pearl, rose, a green like shallow water over weed. Salt, but salt as Coral had never seen it, salt with *grain*, salt with pattern, and when she stepped closer she saw why.

There were faces in it.

Small ones. Faint ones. Swirls of crystal that resolved, when you looked too long, into the suggestion of features — a closed eye, a mouth, the curve of a cheek. Not preserved. *Rendered.* The way fat renders, the way ore renders. Something had gone into this wall whole and come out as this.

"Don't look too long," Wren said hoarsely. He had his back to the wall and his eyes on the floor, which was unlike him; Wren looked at everything, catalogued everything, committed everything to the memory he sold so cheaply. "In the archives there were accounts. The old keepers' accounts. They said the salt remembers. They said—" He swallowed. "They said if you listen long enough, it starts talking back."

Coral was already listening.

At first there was only the drip of water, the tick of cooling stone, Ulyana breathing beside her. Then, underneath — so far underneath she might have been inventing it — a murmur. Hundreds of voices, or thousands, layered like sediment, none of them distinct, all of them saying something. The same something. Over and over, in a language older than the old tongue.

Beside her, Ulyana went rigid.

Then the hollow woman opened her mouth and answered them.

It was not the fragment-song. It was fluent, rolling, terrible — a full sentence in the language of the dead, spoken in a voice that did not sound like Ulyana at all but like someone much older, much wearier, someone who had been saying this same thing for four hundred years. The words meant nothing to Coral. But the *shape* of them meant everything: it was a question. A question asked with such exhausted tenderness that Coral's eyes filled before she understood a single syllable.

And the wall answered. A ripple moved through the crystals, light chasing light, and the murmur changed key — and Ulyana slumped, blinking, empty-eyed again, whatever had spoken through her gone back down into the well of her hollowness.

"What did she say?" Coral whispered. "Wren. What did she say?"

He had gone the color of tallow. "I don't know. That dialect predates the Court. It predates the *Ledger*." He wet his lips. "But I know the cadence. Scribes learn cadence before we learn words. That was a liturgy, Coral. Call and response. She called. It responded." He looked at the wall with naked fear. "She's not just a prisoner here. She's part of the service."

Coral stood very still, and let herself feel it — the full weight of it, pressing down like the mountain itself. Four hundred years. Four hundred years of singing to a wall of rendered souls, with no self left to know why. Her grandmother had done this. Her grandmother's bargain had done this. And some stubborn, furious, terrified thing in Coral's chest stood up inside her and said: *no.*

"We're close," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "The salt is fresher here. Look — the faces are clearer near the ceiling. Newer layers on top. We're upstream of the rendering." She turned to Ulyana, who was gazing at the wall with an expression that on anyone else Coral would have called homesickness. "Keep singing, love. Keep singing us there."

* * *

The last stretch of road was a stairway cut into a vein of black stone, and it sang.

That was the only word for it. Every step they took rang a note, low and resonant, and the notes braided together into a chord that shifted as they descended — minor, then major, then something between keys that made Coral's teeth ache and her heart open whether she wanted it to or not. Ulyana climbed ahead of them, sure-footed, humming, and the stair harmonized with her, and Coral understood with sudden total clarity that this stair had been built for exactly one purpose: to announce the arrival of the Salt-Bearer to whatever waited below.

"They know we're coming," Wren said flatly.

"The King has known since the night market," Coral said. "Let him know a little more precisely."

"You're insufferable when you're brave. Has anyone told you that?"

"Aldith. Daily." The name came out before she could stop it, and she felt Wren's glance land on her back like a hand. She didn't turn around. There would be time — above, in the light, if there was an above for people who did what she was about to do — to untangle everything she felt about Mother Aldith, the betrayal and the shame and the strange, stubborn love that had survived both. Down here there was only forward.

The stair ended.

The First Saltworks opened before them like a cathedral that had been drowned and then taught to breathe.

The cavern was vast beyond measuring — the lantern-glass showed them a fraction and suggested the rest in shadows stacked upon shadows. Rows of great basins descended the cavern floor in terraces, each one brimming with slow-turning liquid that gleamed like mercury and moved like grief. Above the basins hung frames of black iron, and from the frames, suspended on chains, hung vessels — hundreds of them, thousands — glass and bone and clay and stranger materials, each one glowing faintly from within, each one pulsing with a rhythm almost like a heartbeat, almost like breathing, almost like crying.

Souls. Rendered souls, hanging in their vessels above the pans, dripping down into the brine. And where the drips struck the liquid, the liquid crystallized instantly, growing salt crusts along the basin rims in delicate, horrible lace. Workers moved among the terraces — not the dead of the streets above, but things older, greyer, faceless, harvesting the crusts with long wooden rakes and carrying them away in sacks toward a tunnel mouth that exhaled a slow, warm wind.

"There," Wren whispered. His voice had gone thin. "That's the wind that comes up through the villages. That's the pact-wind. That's what keeps Thornfell safe." He laughed, a broken little sound. "I used to think the mountain ate souls. It doesn't eat them. It *farms* them."

Coral barely heard him. Because Ulyana had stopped humming.

She stood at the top of the terraces, swaying slightly, her face lifted toward the hanging vessels, and her hands had come up — slowly, involuntarily — to press against her own chest, over her heart, over the hollow where her soul should have been. Her mouth worked. No sound came out. Then, from somewhere impossibly deep, the fragment-song returned — but slower now, transformed, its three-and-five notes stretched into a lament, and Coral realized with a lurch that it had never been a work song at all. It was a lullaby. Ulyana had been singing a lullaby to herself for four hundred years, the only thing left in her, the way a child holds a blanket.

And one of the vessels, high on the left, in the oldest row where the chains had gone green with age —

One of the vessels began to glow brighter.

It was small. Clay, unglazed, cracked and mended with wire, the kind of jar a hedge-witch might keep feverfew in. It swung gently on its chain, though there was no wind, and its light pulsed faster, faster, matching the rhythm of Ulyana's song note for note, beat for beat, until the whole cavern seemed to hold its breath — even the faceless workers stilled their rakes, even the brine stopped turning.

Ulyana took one step toward it. Then another. Tears were sliding down her face, and she did not know why, and that — that was the worst thing Coral had ever seen, a body weeping for a soul it couldn't remember owning.

"Ulyana," Coral whispered, and reached for her hand.

And every vessel in the First Saltworks turned — there was no other word for it — turned upon its chain like a thousand heads, and every point of light within them fixed upon Coral Brightwater, and from the deep tunnel mouth where the harvested salt went, a voice like the grinding of glaciers said:

"*The Salt-Bearer stands in the rendering hall. Let her be weighed.*"

The warm wind rose around them, thick with the smell of the sea, and the lights of the vessels dimmed, all together, like held breath — waiting to see what Coral would do next.
