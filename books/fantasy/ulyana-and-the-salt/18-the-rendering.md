## 18. The Rendering

The songs came back to her in fragments, the way water comes back to a dried riverbed — not all at once, but in pools, in seeps, in places where the ground remembered being soft.

*Down where the salt was born,* one of them went, *down where the dark was torn—*

Ulyana walked ahead of them because her body knew the way even if she did not. That was the cruelest joke of her condition: the hollow girl who had climbed out of the brine-crypt could not remember her mother's face or the color of her own eyes, but her feet knew these passages as a swallow knows the sky it was fledged into. Her bare soles found the worn grooves in the stone where generations of salt-bearers had trodden before her. Her hands found the handholds without looking. Somewhere beneath the nothing that was her, there was a woman who had walked this road ten thousand times, and Ulyana was only the shell she'd left behind, still moving out of habit.

Behind her, the girl with the sea-glass eyes kept close. Coral. The name arrived in Ulyana's mind like a coin dropped into a dry well, and she kept turning it over, listening for the splash.

Coral smelled like home.

That was all Ulyana had. Not a memory — memories were things with edges, beginnings and ends, and everything inside Ulyana was edgeless, smooth as sea-worn glass. But when the wind shifted and carried the girl's scent to her — woodsmoke and hedge-magic and something older underneath, something like bread left rising overnight in a warm kitchen — something in the hollow place where her heart should have been turned over and *ached*. It was not longing exactly. Longing required knowing what you longed for. This was more primitive than that. This was the ache of a root for soil it had been torn from.

"You're humming," Coral said softly, from behind her. "The same song. You've been humming it since the crypt."

"Have I." Ulyana's voice surprised her every time she used it — low and rough, a voice that had clearly sung a great deal once, now rusted from disuse.

"*Down where the salt was born,*" Coral sang, barely above a whisper. "Is that how it goes?"

"I don't know what I know," Ulyana said. "I only know that I know it."

Wren, limping along at the rear with his scribe's case clutched to his chest like a shield, made a small sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. "That," he said, "is the most honest thing anyone has said in this mountain in a hundred years."

* * *

The passage opened without warning, the way a mouth opens.

One moment they were squeezing through a seam of black stone furred with white mineral bloom; the next, the rock fell away and Ulyana stood at the lip of a cavern so vast that the lantern-light died before it found any wall. She felt the space before she saw it — a pressure change, a hush, the particular silence of an enormous room holding its breath.

Then the light caught it, and Ulyana understood why her feet had known the way here better than anywhere else in the world.

The First Saltworks spread below them like a frozen ocean turned inside out. Terraces of pale stone descended in wide steps toward a central basin, and every surface glittered, crusted, wept salt. Great wooden frames — some collapsed, some still standing like the ribs of drowned ships — stretched across the terraces, and from their beams hung sheets of crystal that caught the light and threw it back in slow, prismatic shivers. Channels cut through the floor in patterns too regular for nature, too organic for design, and down those channels moved not water but something thicker, slower, faintly luminous — a tide of grey-gold light that pulsed, very faintly, like a heartbeat.

And the smell. Salt, yes, but under it something else — something sweet and terrible, like flowers rotting in honey. Like preserved things. Like *kept* things.

"Oh," Coral breathed behind her. "Oh, it's beautiful."

"It's a slaughterhouse," Wren said quietly. "It's just learned to look like a garden."

Ulyana said nothing. She was listening to the pulse.

She could feel it through her bare feet — that slow grey-gold rhythm rising up through the stone, through the salt crusts, into the bones of her. And her body, her hollow, habit-driven body, responded to it the way a tuning fork responds to its twin note. Something in her chest vibrated in answer. Something in her chest *leaned toward it*, hungry, homesick—

"No," she said aloud, and stepped back from the edge.

"What is it?" Coral's hand found her elbow, steadying her. Warm fingers. Real ones. Ulyana focused on them like a drowning woman on driftwood.

"That light," Ulyana said. "In the channels. I know it." She did not know how she knew it. Knowing simply arrived, the way the songs arrived, the way the footpaths arrived — deposited in her by whatever ghost of herself still haunted her own skull. "I've stood in it. I've *worked* in it. Those channels—" her hand rose without her permission, traced the pattern below, terrace by terrace, gate by gate, "—brine comes in from the deep springs there, and there, and it moves through the pans in order, and at each stage something is drawn off, and what's drawn off goes *there*—" her finger stopped at the central basin, where the glow gathered densest, "—and what remains is the salt."

"The pact-salt," Wren said. His voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of a man handling something venomous. "The salt that feeds the mountain and keeps the villages safe. Every year, a rendering. Every year, the Court renders souls into salt and carries it up to the boundary stones."

"Yes," Ulyana said. And then, because the knowledge would not stop arriving, because it came faster now, tumbling, a breached dam: "And I rendered it. For three hundred years, I rendered it. I was good at it. I was *proud* of it."

The words hung in the cold air. Coral's grip on her arm tightened.

"You remember," Coral whispered.

"No." Ulyana shook her head, hard, like a dog shedding water. "I don't *remember*. Remembering is mine, remembering is *me*, and I'm not — I'm a coat someone left on a hook. But the coat remembers the shape of the shoulders." She pressed her fist against her sternum, against the hollow ache. "There's a vessel down there. In the basin. There's always a vessel. The soul that's being rendered sits in a vessel of black glass until the last of it dissolves, and the vessel keeps the shape of what it held, the way a cup keeps the shape of the wine." Her voice cracked on the last word, and she did not know why, and the not-knowing was worse than grief. "Mine is down there. Mine has been down there for a hundred years, waiting for the rendering to finish, and renderings are slow, renderings are *merciful*, they stretch a soul across centuries so it never feels itself ending—"

"Ulyana." Coral turned her gently by the shoulders. Sea-glass eyes, close enough now to see the flecks of green in them, the fear in them, the terrible stubborn hope. "Your soul is still whole?"

"A rendering can be interrupted." The knowledge came calm and certain. "If the vessel is broken before the last dissolution, what remains pours back to its owner. Whole. All of it. Even the parts that were already salted." A pause. "Even the parts that were given away."

Something passed over Coral's face then — guilt, quick and sharp, gone almost before it arrived. Ulyana filed it away among the few things she owned.

"Then we go down," Coral said.

* * *

The descent into the works was a pilgrimage through other people's endings.

As they picked their way down the terraces, Ulyana began to see what the glittering crusts really were. Not salt alone. At each pan, at each stage of the channels, the crystal formations took shapes — and the shapes were faces. Hands. A curve of shoulder. A child's footprint pressed into a crust of white. Souls did not dissolve evenly; they resisted, they clung to their favorite shapes, and the salt remembered them. The whole cavern was a graveyard wearing a jeweler's shop for a disguise.

Ulyana walked among them and felt nothing, which was its own horror. These should have been kin to her — the rendered, the sold, the spent. She should have wept for them. Instead the hollow place where her grief lived stayed smooth and silent as a frozen pond, and she thought: *this is what they bought when they bought my soul. Not my labor. My capacity to be wounded by all this. They salted away my suffering first, because a woman who can grieve cannot work forever.*

A woman who can grieve would have refused.

At the lowest terrace, the channels converged, and the grey-gold light pooled thick as syrup, and there it was.

The vessel stood at the center of the final pan, half-submerged in the luminous brine: a jar of black glass taller than a person, rounded as a seed, its surface so dark it seemed to drink the light around it rather than reflect it. And within the glass — faint, deep, like a figure seen through fathoms of dark water — something moved. Slowly. Patiently. Turning over in its sleep.

Ulyana stopped three paces away. Her body, which had led her unerringly to this place, now refused another step. Habit warred with terror, and terror won.

"That's me," she said. Her voice sounded very small in the vast humming dark. "That's what's left of me."

Coral came up beside her. Wren hung back, his eyes moving ceaselessly across the terraces, cataloguing exits, counting shadows. He had been doing that since the night market — since the alarm-tides, since the torn page, since the drowned dead rose through flooded streets hunting them and the three of them had run upward instead of outward, deeper into the mountain, following a dead woman's songs to the one place no one would think to look. He looked like a man who had stopped believing in exits altogether and kept counting them anyway, for comfort.

"There'll be wards," he murmured. "There are always wards on a rendering vessel. Let me look first—"

But Ulyana was already moving.

She did not decide to move. Her body decided — the same body that knew the footpaths, the same body that hummed the songs — and it carried her forward across the final pan, through the shallow luminous brine that soaked her hem and climbed her ankles with warmth, straight to the black glass. Up close, the vessel hummed. Not a sound — a vibration, felt in the teeth, in the marrow. And through the glass, the sleeping shape turned, and turned, and Ulyana saw, with a clarity that had nothing to do with her missing soul, that the shape was a woman. Tall. Long-haired. One hand pressed flat against the inside of the glass, as if it had been pressing for a hundred years.

Her hand rose. Pressed flat against the outside. Palm to palm through two inches of black glass.

The wards, wherever they were, never fired. Perhaps they recognized her. Perhaps they had been waiting for her. Later, Wren would swear the vessel's seal had been broken from the inside years ago, and no one would ever explain it.

Ulyana touched the glass, and the dam broke.

* * *

They did not come back as memories come — in order, with edges. They came back as a flood comes, all at once, everything everywhere, a century and more crashing through the hollow place and filling it so fast and so completely that she felt her knees hit the brine and heard someone screaming and understood distantly that the screaming was hers.

*Salt on her tongue the night she was born — her mother pressing a grain of First Salt between the newborn's lips, the old blessing, taste the mountain, child, taste the mountain and know your keeper.*

*Her mother dying. Her mother's hands going quiet in hers. The winter after, the hunger in the valley, the wolves coming down thin as ribbons, the villagers at her door with their caps in their hands.*

*The Drowned King's herald rising from the well in her own yard, water-black robes, a voice like tide over gravel: The mountain wakes, salt-witch. The mountain is hungry. Give us a soul of power freely offered, and we will feed it, and your valley sleeps safe for a hundred years and a hundred more.*

*And her — proud, grieving, twenty-six years old and certain of her own strength — saying yes. Saying: take me. Take my service. Take my song. Take everything except my name, let me keep my name.*

*The herald smiling with too many teeth: We will take your service, your song, and your suffering. Your name we leave you, as you ask. Names are how we find our way home.*

*The descent. The gate of teeth. The ledger, and her own hand signing, and the ink drinking into the page like blood into snow.*

*And then the work. Gods, the work. Three hundred years at the pans, drawing the tides, tending the renderings, singing the slowing-songs to souls in their vessels so they would not feel themselves end. Learning the craft of mercy inside the machine of cruelty. Telling herself, every single day, telling herself: this is the bargain. This is the price. Because of me, children in Thornfell sleep safe. Because of me, the wolves stay thin and the wells stay sweet. Because of me.*

*A daughter.*

The flood brought her, and Ulyana went down under it.

*Maren. Born two years before the sale, fathered by a carter who died of fever, named for Ulyana's grandmother. Dark-haired, loud-laughing, afraid of thunder. Ulyana had held her the night before she descended and memorized the weight of her, the exact weight, seven pounds and eleven ounces grown to nearly thirty, and she had told herself: a hundred years is nothing, a hundred years is one long watch, I will climb back up and she will be old and I will hold her hand and tell her why, and she will understand, she will understand that I bought her whole world—*

*Except the pact did not end in a hundred years. The pact renewed. The pact had clauses written in a tongue only scribes read, and the herald had smiled too many teeth, and Ulyana had signed anyway, because reading the terms carefully would have meant hesitating, and hesitating might have meant the mountain woke that very winter, and there were children in Thornfell, there was Maren, there was Maren—*

*So she worked. And Maren grew. And Maren married. And Maren had a daughter, and named her—*

*Coral.*

The name detonated in her.

*She had watched from the pans — the salt shows a watcher many things, the salt is a window if you know the songs — she had watched a dark-haired girl with her grandmother's stubborn chin grow up in a hedge-witch's cottage, learning herbs and hexes and the old blessings, and Ulyana had sung to her through three hundred miles of stone, lullabies keyed to the salt-tides, and the girl had grown up restless, grown up hearing music no one else could hear, and Ulyana had thought: she feels me. She feels me and doesn't know what she's feeling. Oh, my love. Oh, my granddaughter. When you are grown, when the hundred years turn, I will—*

*And then the girl ran away from Mother Aldith's cottage, and the almanacs went strange, and a thirteenth sliver crept onto the moon, and Ulyana, in her crypt of brine, had felt the future shift like sand underfoot and had done the only thing left to do: she had pressed her hand against the inside of her vessel, and pushed, and pushed, and pushed, for years, cracking the seal a hair's width at a time, so that whenever her descendant finally came down — if she ever came — the glass would break at a touch.*

*Because a grandmother's love is also a kind of craft. And Ulyana had had three hundred years to practice.*

* * *

Ulyana came back to herself kneeling in the luminous brine, both palms flat against the cracked black glass, the vessel empty now, truly empty, its occupant poured back into the hollow woman who knelt before it. She was soaked to the waist. Her throat was raw. Coral knelt beside her with an arm locked around her shoulders, holding her upright, saying her name over and over — *Ulyana, Ulyana, I've got you, I've got you* — and Wren stood a pace off with his lantern raised, watching her with the expression of a man witnessing something he had only ever read about.

"I remember," Ulyana rasped. It was the truest sentence she had ever spoken. "I remember everything. Coral—" She turned, and took the girl's face in her wet hands, and looked, really looked, at the sea-glass eyes, the stubborn chin, the ghost of Maren in every line of her. "You have her laugh. You have my daughter's laugh. I used to hear it through the stone."

Coral's breath caught. "Your—"

"Maren was my daughter. You are her blood. I sold myself to save your valley before your mother's mother was born, and I have watched you from the salt since the day you drew breath." The words tumbled out of her, a century of hoarded speech. "I sang you asleep. I am so sorry it took me so long to—"

"Don't." Coral's voice broke. "Don't apologize. Don't you dare. I tore a page out of a god's ledger to get to you. Do you think I'd have done that for a stranger?" A wet, disbelieving laugh. "I smelled like home to you. You smelled like home to me. Before either of us knew why."

Ulyana held her granddaughter — her granddaughter, the word fit her mouth like a key fits a lock it was cut for — and for one suspended moment the vast slaughterhouse-garden held nothing but the two of them and the sound of breathing.

Then the knowledge finished arriving. The last of it. The part she had felt circling at the flood's edge, waiting.

She pulled back and looked at the terraces, the pans, the patient channels, the grey-gold pulse. And with her soul restored, with her craft intact, she finally saw the machinery whole — saw what three hundred years of proximity had shown her only in pieces, the way a fish cannot see the water.

"Wren," she said. Her voice came out level and cold, and she felt Coral flinch at the sound of it. "How does the pact describe the mountain's hunger?"

He blinked. "As ancient. Primordial. The hunger predates the Court, predates the villages — that's the entire justification, the mountain was always starving, the pact merely manages it—"

"The mountain was *dormant*." Ulyana rose to her feet, brine streaming from her clothes, and her shadow thrown huge and wavering across the glittering pans. "I rendered the salt for three centuries. I know what's in it. It isn't only soul. It's *appetite*. Every rendering draws off the soul's longing and leaves the hunger behind, concentrates it, feeds it back into the boundary stones, and the stones feed it into the mountain, and the mountain grows hungrier, and so the next rendering must be larger, and so—" She laughed, and the laugh was terrible. "We didn't cage a beast. We built one. Grain by grain. Year by year. The pact created the very hunger it claims to hold back. The Drowned King has been farming his own monster for a thousand years, and every soul in those pans — every face in the salt — was fed to a lie."

The silence rang. Above them, far above, through miles of stone, something shifted — a deep, grinding resettlement, like a sleeper turning toward a noise.

Wren's face had gone the color of the salt. "The thirteenth moon," he whispered. "The King isn't collecting a debt. He's *harvesting*. The largest rendering in history, timed to the moon that shouldn't exist—"

"And Coral is the key," Ulyana said. "And I am the proof." She turned toward the tunnel they had come by, and beyond it, upward, toward the court of the drowned, where a king with too many teeth was surely already aware that his oldest vessel stood empty. "Come. We have until moonrise. And when he comes to claim his harvest—"

She flexed her hands, and salt crackled in the air around her knuckles like small lightning.

"—he is going to learn what three hundred years of practice makes."
