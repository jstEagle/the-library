## 20. The Hunger Answered

The mountain woke the way a drowning man wakes — all at once, and screaming.

Wren felt it first in his teeth. Then in the ink. Every vial in his satchel shivered against its glass, and the black fluid inside them stirred like something alive, like something *listening*. He had spent eleven years in the Undercourt archives learning to read the old tongue, and not one of his masters had ever told him that language could be afraid.

"Coral!" he shouted into the dark. "Coral, where are you?"

The answer came as light — a bloom of witchfire at the heart of the shattered saltworks, green-gold and desperate, and within it two figures: Coral on her knees amid the ruin of the pact-salt, her hands still raised from the act of breaking a hundred years of bargains, and Ulyana beside her, whole now, her soul restored and her face turned upward toward a ceiling that was no longer a ceiling but a throat.

The First Saltworks had been vast once — Wren could see it even now, through the shaking: galleries of rendered stone, basins the size of lakes, chains where souls had been hung like lanterns to dry. Now it was collapsing inward, or perhaps *unfolding*, stone peeling back to reveal what had always been beneath: muscle. Heat. A slow tidal breathing that was not water and not wind but hunger itself, given a body of granite and grievance.

The mountain's voice did not come as sound. It came as pressure, as meaning pressed directly into bone:

*YOU TOOK.*

Wren staggered, caught himself against a pillar that flexed under his palm like the flank of some enormous sleeping animal rudely awakened. Ink sprayed from his dropped pen across the floor, and where it landed, the stone drank it. Of course it did. Everything here drank. That was the whole architecture of the place — the pact, the salt, the drowned court above them with its streets of standing water and its ledger of stolen names. All of it built on one simple transaction, repeated for a century until it seemed like law:

*Take from the villages. Take their fear, their longing, their dead. Feed the hunger below, and the hunger sleeps.*

Except it had never slept. It had only learned patience.

*YOU TOOK,* the mountain said again, and this time the words came with images — Wren saw them whether he wanted to or not, flung across his mind like cards dealt by a furious hand. Villages burning before the pact was struck. A valley of ash. Children carried off into the deep places. And beneath all of it, older than any of it, a loneliness so vast it made the Drowned King's court look like a child's game of pretend: an age when the mountain had been worshipped, sung to, mourned, *named* — and then forgotten, and then feared, and then finally reduced to a thing to be paid off, like a creditor, like a tollgate, like a smuggler bribing his way past a border.

"They didn't understand," Coral was saying, her voice raw, pitched up toward the dark. "We didn't understand — I broke it because I had to, because they lied, because the pact was never—"

*THE PACT WAS A MOUTH,* the mountain said. *AND YOU HAVE STOPPED ITS FEEDING. NOW IT FEEDS ITSELF.*

Wren understood then, with the terrible clarity that only ever arrived too late to be useful, what was coming. The pact had not contained the mountain's hunger. Ulyana had been right about that, standing in the ruins of the rendering-vats with her memories flooding back — the pact had *created* it, had taught a grieving god-sized thing to think of itself as appetite. And now the appetite had nothing left to eat but everything above it: the Undercourt, the passes, every village in Thornfell, every name in every ledger, all of it going down that open throat unless someone gave the mountain something else.

He looked down at his hands. They were shaking. They were also, he noticed distantly, still stained to the second knuckle with the old tongue — the archival ink that never washed out, the mark of his office, the brand of his exile. Eleven years ago he had stood in the Court of the Drowned King and written out his own condemnation in characters so old they predated the court itself, and the King had read them aloud and laughed, and cast him up the mountain like a fishbone.

*You will never write anything true again,* the King had said. *Everything you touch will bend toward your own survival. That is your sentence, scribe: to be a liar with a scholar's hands.*

And Wren had believed him. Had built a whole life on the belief — smuggling secrets instead of writing them, selling other people's histories because he could not bear to make his own, keeping the prophecies like a man keeps a knife he is afraid to use. Even coming down here, even choosing Coral's cause over his pardon, he had told himself it was strategy. Self-interest wearing loyalty's coat. The King's sentence, fulfilled.

But the King was wrong about one thing, and Wren was done being wrong.

"Ulyana!" he shouted. "The songs — the fragments you remembered — do you have them? All of them?"

She turned. In the witchfire's light her face was hollowed and lit and utterly calm, the calm of a woman who had already died once and found it survivable. "Every note," she said. "They came back with everything else." Her eyes went to the heaving dark above them, and something moved in her expression that was not fear. Recognition, maybe. Pity. "They were never feeding songs, scribe. They were *lullabies*. My grandmother's grandmother sang them to the mountain before there was ever a pact. Before anyone thought to sell anything."

"To keep it asleep?"

"No." Ulyana smiled, and it was the saddest thing Wren had ever seen. "To keep it *company*."

* * *

There was no time to explain, and anyway the mountain did not need explanation. It needed what it had always needed, and had been cheated of for a hundred years: witness. Attention. Grief spoken aloud by someone who meant it.

It needed, Wren realized, standing in the ruin of the Saltworks with his ink-stained hands and his exile's knowledge, exactly the thing a scribe was made to give.

"I need a surface," he said, tearing open his satchel. Vials rolled out across the trembling stone — blacks and blues and the one forbidden red he'd bought from a corpse-broker in the night market and never had occasion to use. "Something that will hold the old tongue. Stone drinks ink here — good, let it drink. Coral! Your blood opened the gate of teeth. Will it write?"

Coral stared at him. Around them the dark was gathering itself, the pressure building toward another word, and the word would be *DEVOUR*, and after that there would be no more conversations, only weather.

"Wren—"

"*Will it write?*"

"Yes," she said, and cut her palm on the shard of pact-salt she still clutched, because of course she still held it, because Coral Brightwater kept every promise she'd ever broken close enough to bleed on.

He knelt. He spread the largest sheet of vellum he owned — the one he'd carried out of the archives eleven years ago, blank all this time, waiting, though he'd told himself it was merely loot — and he pinned it flat with four vials at the corners. Then he took Coral's bleeding hand and guided it to the page, and together they wrote the first line.

Not in trade-tongue. Not in court-script. In the old tongue, the true tongue, the one that predated ledgers and pacts and the Drowned King's pretty drowned throne — the language the mountain had dreamed in before anyone taught it to be hungry.

*Here is what we owe you,* the line said. *Not flesh. Not souls. Not salt.*

The mountain stilled. Not calmed — stilled, the way a wolf stills when it hears something unexpected in the dark. Wren's whole body rang with the attention of it. He had never written anything that was *read* like this, by anything like this, and he understood suddenly why the old scribes had gone half-mad, why the archives were built so far underground, why the King kept his scholars behind three locked doors and a river of forgetting. To be read by the mountain was to be known the way a bone is known by the dog that finds it.

He kept writing. His hand shook; the letters came out crooked and human and he let them, because perfection was the court's language and truth had never once been tidy.

*You were sung to once. You were named. You were mourned when the passes closed and thanked when the springs ran sweet, and then the clever people of the valley decided gratitude was expensive, and they bought you off instead. They taught you that love was a transaction. They were wrong, and we are here to unsay it.*

Ulyana began to sing.

Her voice rose through the ruined saltworks — thin at first, rusted from centuries in the brine-crypt, then stronger, finding the bones of melodies she had carried soulless through a hundred years of hollowness. And Wren, writing as fast as his bleeding hand could move, transcribed her. That was the craft of it, the redemption of it: he was not composing. He was taking dictation from grief itself, setting down in permanent ink every fragment of those lullabies, every verse the salt-witches had once sung into the deep places, and around each verse he wrote the mountain's own history as Ulyana's restored memory gave it up — the age of worship, the age of fear, the century of silence, the lie of the pact laid out plain and named a lie.

*This was done to you,* he wrote. *By us. By our grandmothers and grandfathers. We do not ask you to forgive it. We ask you to remember that you were more than what they made you.*

The mountain breathed. The whole cavern swelled with it, a tide-draw of air that lifted Wren's hair and fluttered the vellum and snuffed half of Coral's witchfire, and in the sudden half-dark he saw them: shapes rising out of the stone itself, vast and slow, like shadows of a body the mountain had worn long ago when it still expected visitors. Not mouths. Not hands. Faces, maybe — or the memory of faces, worn smooth by ten thousand years of no one looking.

*NO ONE HAS SPOKEN MY NAMES,* the mountain said, and the voice was different now. Smaller. Which was somehow worse. *IN THREE HUNDRED YEARS. THE KING KNEW THEM AND KEPT THEM IN HIS LEDGERS LIKE COINS. THE WITCHES KNEW THEM AND SOLD THEM WITH THE REST.*

"Then take them back," Wren said aloud. His voice cracked. He let it. "I'm writing them down. All of them. And I'm writing them wrong, probably — my accent is terrible, I've been exiled for eleven years and the archives never let me practice the vowels — but I'm writing them, and I'm giving them to her—" he pointed at Coral, still kneeling, still bleeding, her hand moving under his guidance across the final lines, "—and she's going to carry them up the mountain and sing them in every village that ever paid your toll, and if they don't learn them, she'll teach them, because she's the most stubborn person I have ever met and I include myself, which is saying something."

Coral made a sound that was half laugh, half sob, and did not stop writing.

*WHAT DO YOU WANT FOR THEM?* the mountain asked. And there it was — the old grammar, the transaction-shape, the habit of a century of pacts rising up even now, even wounded, even hopeful. *EVERYTHING IS BOUGHT. EVERYTHING IS SOLD. WHAT IS YOUR PRICE, SCRIBE?*

And Wren Blackwood, disgraced scribe of the Undercourt, liar by sentence and smuggler by trade, knelt in the dark with blood-ink drying on his fingers and found — to his genuine astonishment — that he had no price. Not because he was noble. He knew himself too well for that. But because for the first time since the King had read out his condemnation, he was writing something he did not want anything in exchange for. The words were the want. The record was the reward.

"Nothing," he said. "That's the point. That's the whole point. You were never supposed to be fed. You were supposed to be *remembered*. Those aren't the same thing, and everyone who told you otherwise was lying, and I am professionally qualified to know a lie when I write one."

He set down the last line. It was not a clause. It was not a term. There were no conditions, no penalties, no thirteenth turns, no tolls. It was simply a dedication, the kind scribes put at the front of great works and he had never once had occasion to use:

*To the mountain, who was here first, and will be here last, and deserves better than to be a mouth. We will say your names. We will sing your songs. We will mourn what was taken from you, and we will not call it payment. Signed in blood and ink and grief, freely given: Wren, who wrote lies. Coral, who carries the salt. Ulyana, who remembers. Witnesses: the stone, the water, the moon on its thirteenth turn.*

The vellum drank the blood. The stone drank the vellum. Wren watched a century of scholarship sink into the living rock and felt — nothing break, nothing shatter, nothing dramatic at all. Just a quiet, like a held breath released. Just the sense of a sentence, long imposed, finally served.

The mountain exhaled. The heat dimmed. The vast shapes sank back into the stone like whales settling into deep water, and the pressure in Wren's teeth eased, and somewhere far above them, he would later learn, the Thornfell passes stopped shaking and the rivers ran clear and every almanac in the valley corrected itself with a sound like a sigh.

In the witchfire's returning glow, Ulyana's song faded to humming, then to silence. She looked at Wren across the empty space where the page had been, and her eyes were wet.

"The King will come," she said quietly. "He'll have felt all of it. The pact breaking, the rewriting. He'll come with his heralds and his mercy and his ledgers, and he will offer you everything you lost, Wren. He's very good at offering."

Wren looked down at his hands — ink-black to the knuckle, blood-crusted, trembling, *his*. Eleven years of exile, and the sentence had just been overturned by the only judge whose opinion had ever really mattered.

"Let him come," he said. "I have something true to show him."

Far above, through miles of stone, through the drowned streets and the gate of teeth and the long climb to the surface, a bell began to ring — the alarm-tides, the court roused, the King's answer already descending.

And with it, faint but unmistakable, came footsteps.
