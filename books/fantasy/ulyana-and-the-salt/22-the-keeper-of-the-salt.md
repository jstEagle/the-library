## 22. The Keeper of the Salt

Spring came to Thornfell the way forgiveness comes — slowly, and then all at once.

Coral felt it first in her feet. The smuggler's pass below Hallowmere, which had been a river of grey ice when they'd walked it in winter, had softened into mud that pulled at her boots with every step, warm and alive and faintly smelling of thyme. Above her, snowmelt threaded down the mountain's flanks in a hundred silver veins, and the mountain itself — the great sleeping bulk of it, the thing she was bound to now, bone-deep and salt-deep — hummed beneath her soles like a cat that had finally been fed.

Not fed. *Remembered.* She corrected herself the way she corrected herself a hundred times a day now. The distinction mattered. It was the whole point of everything Wren had written in the old tongue on that terrible, moonlit night, and the whole point of the work they were doing now.

"Stop humming," Ulyana said, without turning around. She walked ahead on the narrow trail, her staff clicking against the stones, her white hair loose in the wind. "You do it when you're happy. It's insufferable."

"I'm not humming."

"You're always humming. You hummed through the whole descent into Hollow Bracken, you hummed while we dug the salt out of their well, and you're humming now." She glanced back over her shoulder, and there it was — the look Coral still wasn't used to, the one where all four hundred years of her showed at once behind eyes as pale as tide-pools. "I spent three centuries in a brine-crypt listening to nothing but my own heartbeat. I have earned silence."

"You spent three centuries in a brine-crypt," Coral said, catching up to walk beside her, "and now you complain more than anyone I've ever met."

"Yes," Ulyana said, with immense satisfaction. "It's wonderful, isn't it?"

And it was — that was the strange, unhealed truth of it. Every complaint, every sharp-edged opinion about the weather or the state of the roads or Coral's cooking, was proof. The hollow woman who had risen from the crypt knowing only that Coral smelled like home would never have complained, because complaining required wanting things, and wanting required a soul. Now Ulyana wanted things constantly. She wanted honey in her tea and her hair braided a particular way and the window open no matter how cold the night. She wanted Coral's hand at specific moments and for no reason she could articulate, and she took it, and Coral gave it, and neither of them made a ceremony of it because ceremonies were for people who hadn't already survived each other.

The village of Hallowmere appeared below them, tucked into its fold of the valley like a coin dropped in a purse: slate roofs, smoke rising straight in the windless morning, terraced fields just breaking green. A year ago, Coral would have skirted it. A year ago, villages were places you passed through in the dark, places where people asked questions and hedge-witches' apprentices learned to lie beautifully. Now she walked straight down the main road, into the smell of bread and dung and thawing earth, and let them see her coming.

They knew her here. That was the other thing she wasn't used to.

"Mistress Brightwater!" Old Tam Hedgerow dropped his rake and came across the yard at a speed his knees clearly regretted. "Mistress Brightwater, it's the well again — we did what you said, we poured the salt round the lip like you drew, but last night the water went bitter again, and Marta's boy swears he saw something looking up at him from the bottom—"

"Something looking up," Coral repeated gently. "What kind of something?"

Tam's hands twisted together. "Eyes, mistress. Just eyes. Patient ones."

She crouched by the well and looked down into the dark water, and she let herself feel what lived down there — not with fear, the way the villagers did, but with the strange double sense the binding had given her. The mountain's hunger was still there; Wren's rewriting had not erased it, only given it somewhere else to go. It pooled in deep places now, wells and caves and the flooded workings of old mines, and it looked up, and it waited, and it was lonely in a way that Coral understood in her bones.

*Hush,* she told it, and pushed a thread of herself down through the water, salt-salt-salt, the way Ulyana had taught her. *I see you. You're not forgotten. But this well belongs to the living.*

The water shivered. The bitterness faded. Somewhere far below, something enormous turned over and settled back into its long attention.

"That'll hold till midsummer," she said, standing, brushing off her knees. "Then I want everyone in the square at dusk, and I want your oldest song — the one your grandmothers sang at the thaw. Doesn't matter if you've forgotten the words. Forgotten words are my specialty."

Tam stared at her. "The thaw-song? Nobody's sung that since — since the pact, mistress. Grandmother said singing to the mountain was how it got hungry in the first place."

"Grandmother," Coral said, "was wrong about nearly everything, and I say that with love, because she was also my grandmother, and it took me a whole winter to forgive her." She smiled, and after a moment Tam smiled too, uncertainly, the way people smile at weather they don't trust yet. "Sing to it anyway. It doesn't want to be fed. It wants to be *known*. There's a difference, and the difference is everything."

Wren was already at the inn when they found him, installed at the corner table with his ledger open, three cups of tea going cold around him. He'd changed over the winter — filled out a little, slept more, lost the flinching look of a man waiting for a door to be kicked in. His scribe's hands were ink-stained to the second knuckle now instead of just the first, because he'd taken to writing everything down: every village, every well, every half-remembered song, every name the pact had taken before Coral broke it. He called it the True Ledger. It had no magic in it that Coral could feel, except the ordinary kind, which she was beginning to suspect was the stronger.

"Hallowmere," he said, without looking up. "Population two hundred and eleven. One bitter well, remedied. Thaw-song pending. And—" he turned the page, and his voice did the careful thing it did when he was being precise about feelings, "—a request from the reeve. They want a festival. For the anniversary. Of the night the mountain woke."

"A festival," Ulyana said flatly.

"They're calling it the Night of Two Names." Wren finally looked up, and there was a wariness in his face that Coral recognized from the Tollgate of Teeth, from the moment he'd paid his memory and shown her the shape of his exile. "There's to be a bonfire, and songs, and — they've asked if the Keeper will attend. And the Salt-Witch. And me." He cleared his throat. "I told them I'd ask."

"We live here," Coral said. "More or less. We keep walking, but we always come back. What's to ask?"

"What's to ask," Wren said, "is whether either of you wants a holiday named after you. I've read enough history to know how holidays treat their namesakes. They get carved into things. Things get built at them. Then, a century later, someone writes a book about it and gets all the details wrong." He tapped his pen against the table. "I'd rather write it right the first time."

Ulyana sat down across from him, slow and deliberate, and Coral watched the two of them — the disgraced scribe and the freed witch, the two people the Undercourt had chewed up between them — arrive at some silent agreement.

"Write it right," Ulyana said. "All of it. Including the parts where we were fools. Especially those."

"The parts where I tore a page out of a Ledger and drowned half a city's streets in alarm-tides?"

"*Especially* those," Coral said. "That's my favorite part."

Wren laughed — really laughed, the sound still new enough that it startled him slightly — and bent back over his ledger, and began to write.

* * *

They stayed in Hallowmere nine days, which was eight longer than Coral had planned, and she stopped minding on the second day.

Nine days of mending. That was the word for it, though the work was stranger than mending a roof or a fence. In the mornings, Coral walked the boundaries of the village with her palms against the earth, finding the thin places where the old pact had scarred the land — the field that never grew wheat, the spring that ran cold in summer, the copse where birds wouldn't land. Each scar had been a payment, once. A tithe of luck, of warmth, of small softnesses, extracted year upon year until the villagers had forgotten the land had ever been generous. Unmaking them meant giving something back, and what Coral gave was herself: drops of her own binding, salt and memory, pressed into the ground like seeds.

In the afternoons, Ulyana taught. Word spread from village to village faster than any of them traveled it, and folk came walking — hedge-witches mostly, and midwives, and one very serious shepherd boy who had dreams of standing water. Ulyana taught them the old craft, the true salt-craft, the thing she'd been sold for: how to listen to deep water, how to bargain fairly with hungry things, how to know the difference between a spirit that needs feeding and one that needs naming. She was a hard teacher. She was also, Coral noticed, happier than she had ever been, in the fierce and unsentimental way of a woman doing exactly what she was made for.

"She was right, you know," Ulyana said one evening. They sat on the inn's roof, watching the sun go down behind Thornfell's western peaks, and Coral had learned to know when Ulyana meant Aldith without saying the name. "Your grandmother. Not about the pact — the pact was a coward's bargain and it cost her everything she loved. But she was right that you'd come for me. She saw it forty years ago and she let you run anyway. That took more courage than selling me did."

"I'm still angry with her."

"I know. Stay angry a while longer. Anger keeps better than grief." Ulyana's shoulder settled against hers. "But write to her. Spring roads are open. Even liars deserve to know how the story ended."

On the ninth day, the whole village sang the thaw-song in the square at dusk, two hundred voices rough and uncertain and gradually finding the melody like a path through fog, and Coral stood among them with salt on her tongue and tears she didn't bother hiding, and felt the mountain lean down close and *listen*, and sigh, and settle.

They left the next morning, eastward along the smuggler's road, toward the villages they hadn't reached yet. Wren counted them off as they walked: Greyford, where the millpond froze in July. Stony Beck, where children were born quiet and stayed quiet. Nine more after that. A season's work, maybe two.

"And then?" Coral asked.

Wren shrugged. "Then we write it all down properly, and find new roads. There are other mountains, you know. Other courts. The world is full of things that trade in souls because nobody ever offered them anything else." He glanced sideways at her, sly and shy at once. "I thought perhaps somebody should start offering."

The road climbed away from Hallowmere through birch woods loud with birdsong, and Coral walked in the middle, between Wren's scribbling and Ulyana's complaining, with the mountain humming under her feet and the spring coming on behind them like a tide.

She had run from an apprenticeship with nothing but a stolen cloak and a talent for hiding, certain she belonged nowhere, on the road but never of it. Now the road itself was hers — every mile of it mapped in her feet, every village a place where someone knew her name and was glad of it, every evening ending at a fire with these two impossible people who had become, without her noticing the exact moment, her home.

Ulyana's hand found hers as the birches opened onto the long eastern view — valley after valley going green, the world washed clean and starting over.

"Well, Salt-Bearer?" Ulyana said. "Where first?"

Coral looked out at all of it — the work, the miles, the years — and found she was humming.

"All of it," she said. "We've got time."

And far below, deep in the patient dark, the mountain — remembered, mourned, loved — hummed back.
