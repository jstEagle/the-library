## 1. The Charm-Seller's Vigil

The rain came to Vask the way debt came to the poor: slowly at first, then all at once, and never quite leaving.

Xanthe Galloway stood beneath the sagging awning of his stall in the Wardmarket and watched it fall in gray sheets across the gray city, and he thought, not for the first time, that Vask had been built by people who had looked at the sky and decided to imitate it. Slate roofs. Smoke-stained stone. A harbor fog that rolled up the streets each morning like a tide of milk gone sour. Ten years ago there had been color here—lantern-glass in amber and violet, dyed banners for the feast days of a dozen gods, the blue-white shimmer of wardlight over every doorway like a blessing made visible. Now the banners were bleached rags and the wardlights were dark, and the only glow came from the tallow lamps of the charm-sellers' row, guttering yellow behind horn panes.

He turned a charm between his gloved fingers. Copper wire twisted around an iron nail, sealed with wax, stamped with a sigil that was almost—*almost*—the mark of Ulyana. He'd learned early that if you got the sigil exactly right, someone would notice. If you got it nearly right, no one did, and the faithful bought them anyway, because faith is a hunger and hunger does not check its food for authenticity.

"Protection from fever," he told the woman haggling across the counter, a fishwife with red knuckles and a voice like a gull. "Hang it over the door. Or wear it. Doesn't matter which."

"Doesn't matter," she repeated, squinting at him. "Then why's this one cost more than the one Marta sells?"

"Because Marta's are strung on hemp and mine are strung on copper," Xanthe said. "And because I don't lie to you about what they do."

The fishwife snorted. But she paid, and took her nail-and-wire amulet, and hung it around her neck with the tenderness people reserve for things they know are empty. That was the part that always caught in Xanthe's throat—not the deception, which was honest enough as deceptions went, but the tenderness. Ten years after the Silence, after the Edict of Ash fell like a guillotine blade across the whole breadth of the realm, the people of Vask still reached for gods. They just had to buy them secondhand now, hollowed out, inert, safe.

Safe. The enforcers' word. *No unlicensed workings. No unsanctioned devotion. The age of miracles is closed.* They said it plainly, the way you'd announce a road closure, and most people had come to accept it the way you accept weather. Magic was outlawed; therefore magic was gone; therefore the ache where it used to be was simply the shape of being modern.

Xanthe flexed his right hand inside its glove.

Under the oiled leather, under the linen wrap he wore even in summer, the covenant-mark sat against his skin like a coal banked in ash. It had been warm for ten years. Not hot—never hot, never burning, never flaring the way it had in the old days when Ulyana spoke through him and the world rang like a struck bell. Just warm. Patient. The way a hearthstone stays warm long after the fire has gone out, holding a memory of heat that has nowhere else to go.

Every healer in Vask would have cut the hand off to study it. Every enforcer would have burned him for it. And every night, when he unwound the linen and looked at it—the seven-pointed sigil inked in light beneath his skin, Ulyana's own name written small upon her servant—he asked it the same question he had asked ten thousand times.

*Are you still there?*

It never answered. It only stayed warm.

* * *

The Wardmarket woke late in the rain and slept early, and between those two mercies Xanthe sold his hollow charms and listened.

Listening was the other half of the trade, though he never charged for it. A charm-seller who listens hears everything: the quarrels, the debts, the sicknesses people won't name to doctors, the prayers people have stopped calling prayers because prayers were outlawed too, technically, though no enforcer had ever worked out how to arrest a whisper. They didn't pray aloud anymore. They murmured. They mouthed words into their collars while walking past the shuttered temples, and if you stood at a stall all day with nothing to do but watch faces, you could read the lips well enough.

*Keep her safe. Keep her safe. Keep her safe.* A dockhand, passing with his collar up, eyes fixed on nothing.

*I'll do better. I swear I'll do better.* A boy of maybe fifteen, hurrying past the pawnshop where he'd clearly just sold something that mattered.

*Let it be nothing. Let it be nothing.* An old man clutching a physician's note folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth.

Xanthe heard them all and kept his face pleasant and blank, and inside his glove his mark stayed warm, and the warmth felt less like a promise every year and more like an accusation.

Because here was the thing he had never told anyone, the thing that made him a fraud twice over: the charms he sold were hollow, but his hands were not. When he pressed a charm into a customer's palm, some traitor instinct in him still reached for the old current, the deep bright river of Ulyana's power that had run through him since he was nineteen years old and kneeling in a shrine of reeds, swearing himself to her service. He could feel the river's absence the way a sailor feels the absence of tide. And sometimes—rarely, shamefully—he thought he could feel something else underneath the absence. A pressure. Like water held back by a dam. Like a voice with its hand over its own mouth.

He told himself it was imagination. He had gotten very good at telling himself things.

The rain thinned toward midafternoon, settling into the fine persistent drizzle that Vask wore like a second skin. Business slowed to nothing. Xanthe was counting his coppers and deciding whether he could afford both bread and candle-tallow this week when he heard the child.

She couldn't have been more than six or seven, standing in the mud of the lane with a basket of wet washing balanced on one hip, too big a burden for too small a body. She wasn't talking to anyone. She was talking up—at the low gray sky, at whatever lived above it now that the gods had been evicted—and her lips moved with the unselfconscious fluency of a child who has not yet learned that prayer is a crime.

Xanthe read the shape of the words before he understood them.

*Mama says you're listening again. Mama says you're coming back. Please come back. Please. She cries at night when she thinks I'm asleep. Please come back and make her stop crying.*

The child waited. Rain dripped off the awning. Somewhere down the row, a tarpaulin snapped in the wind.

Nothing answered.

Of course nothing answered. Nothing had answered for ten years. That was the whole architecture of the world now; Xanthe knew it better than anyone alive, perhaps, because he alone kept feeling for the walls of a house that had been demolished. He watched the little girl's face and saw the exact moment hope failed her—the small collapse around the eyes, the way her shoulders settled under the basket like something being put away. She'd try again tomorrow. Children always tried again tomorrow. That was the cruelest thing about them.

She turned to go.

Xanthe's hand moved before his caution could catch it.

Later—and he would replay this moment many times in the weeks to come, turning it over like a coin worn smooth—he could not say whether it was the child or the mother or himself he meant it for. Perhaps that was a lie he needed. Perhaps it was simply that ten years of silence broke something in a man, some valve, some seal, and everything he'd swallowed came up at once.

He drew the girl's attention with a smile. "You've dropped your ribbon," he said, which was untrue; she wore no ribbon. She frowned, patted herself, found nothing, and came closer to the stall to look, puzzled. Close enough. Close enough that when Xanthe reached out and tucked a strand of wet hair behind her ear—a grandfatherly gesture, harmless, invisible to anyone not watching for it—his bare fingertip, slipped free of its glove in a motion so practiced it was nearly unconscious, touched the skin behind her ear where no one could see.

And he prayed.

Not with words. Words were for the old days, when the line between god and servant ran open and bright. This was older than words, the wordless grammar of the covenant: *here, take this, through me, what I have is yours to spend.* He opened himself the way he had not dared open himself in a decade, the way opening a door in a flooded house opens everything, and he braced for the silence to rush in and fill him—

Warmth moved.

It was not much. It was not a miracle. It was a thread, thin as spider silk, drawn up out of the banked coal beneath his skin, and it passed through his fingertip into the child like breath fogging cold glass. What it carried was small and old and simple: ease. The loosening of grief's knot, not its untying—that was beyond anything now—but a loosening. Enough that tonight, when the mother wept, the daughter might climb into her lap instead of pretending to sleep, and the crying might become holding, and the holding might become the beginning of a thing neither of them could name but both of them needed.

The girl blinked. Her whole face changed—not wonder, exactly; she was too young to know what to wonder at—but recognition, the animal recognition of warmth in a cold room. She smiled at Xanthe with sudden total trust, the way children smile at people who have handed them sweets, and said, "Thank you," though she could not possibly have known what she was thanking him for.

"Mind your mama," Xanthe said, and his voice came out rough.

She went. The drizzle took her, small and quick, basket and all, and the lane closed behind her like water closing over a stone.

Xanthe stood very still beneath his awning. His heart was going hard. His fingertip, where the thread had passed, tingled with fading warmth, and beneath the glove the mark itself was—brighter. Not much. A coal stirred with a poker, glowing a shade deeper red before it settles. He wrapped his hand again, pulled the glove tight, and looked out at the gray street, at the gray city, at the shuttered temple of Ulyana rising three streets away with its windows boarded and its doors chained by order of the Magistracy of Purity, and he felt the two halves of his life grind against each other like millstones.

Ten years. Ten years of selling dead charms and burying living hands. Ten years of *If I do not answer, do not look for me*, her last words, spoken in a voice already thinning at the edges of the world, mid-battle, mid-scream, gone. He had obeyed. Obedience was the whole of the covenant; obedience was what he had sworn among the reeds; and so he had not looked, had not searched, had spent a decade telling himself that silence was a kind of answer, that a god who sealed her own mouth must have had reasons vast and holy beyond the reach of a hired sword with a warm hand.

But the seal had leaked today. One thread. One child. One small forbidden rite performed in broad daylight in the outlawed heart of Vask, and the current had *moved*.

Which meant she was not gone. Which meant the silence was not death. Which meant it was a door held shut from the other side—and doors held shut can be held shut for mercy, or for fear, and Xanthe had just discovered he could no longer bear not knowing which.

"You're a fool," he said quietly, to himself, to the rain. "You're a fool, and she told you not to."

The mark warmed against his palm, faint and steady, like a hand resting in his.

* * *

He closed the stall at dusk, as the law required, though the law required far more than that and everyone on charm-sellers' row broke the rest of it nightly. He packed his hollow charms into their lacquered box—fever-charms, luck-charms, charms against drowning, each one a little lie shaped like hope—and he was latching the box when Odile appeared out of the drizzle with her hood back and her grin undrowned by any weather.

Odile Verrec had a face like a hatchet and the soul of a magpie, and she was the closest thing to a friend the last decade had left him. She dealt in relics—the real ones, pre-Edict, smuggled up the coast in fish barrels—and she fenced Xanthe's supplies and drank his tea and had never once asked him the question everyone else eventually asked: *how does a man keep selling gods after his god stops answering?*

"You look terrible," she announced, dropping onto his counter as if it were a barstool. "Even by your standards. Your standards being 'a man who sleeps in a room with a shrine in it,' which, incidentally, I never mentioned to anyone, ever, because I value our friendship and also my neck."

"I don't have a shrine."

"You have a shelf with a candle on it that you light every tenth-day and stare at. In Vask, that's a shrine." She leaned close, and her voice dropped out of banter into something quieter. "Word from the docks, Xanthe. There's a new inquisitor in the Purity House. Came up from the capital with writs signed by the Magistrate himself. Sweeping warrants. They're saying he means to clean the Wardmarket top to bottom—charmsellers, relic-men, all of it. End of the week, maybe sooner."

Xanthe's hands went still on the latch. "How soon?"

"Soon enough that you should burn that shelf." Odile's eyes flicked to his right hand, then away, quick and deliberate, the way they always did. She had guessed about the glove years ago and had the wisdom never to confirm it. "I mean it. This one's not the usual shake-down. There's something in the air lately. My contacts in the Purity House clerks—they're scared, and clerks are never scared unless the paper's real. And there's—" she hesitated, chewing the inside of her cheek, "—there's talk. Silly talk. Candle-talk. That the wardlights flickered Tuesday night. All of them. All at once. Down by the harbor."

"Candle-talk," Xanthe repeated flatly.

"Mm. Probably nothing." She slid off the counter and pulled her hood up, and for a moment her hatchet-face was grave beneath it. "Go home the long way. Avoid the square. And Xanthe—if they come, don't be brave. You're no use to anyone burned."

She melted into the drizzle. Xanthe stood a moment longer in the failing light, the box of hollow charms heavy under his arm, the mark warm beneath its leather prison, and the child's small voice echoing somewhere behind his ribs. *Mama says you're listening again.*

He walked home the long way, past the chained temple, and did not look up at it, because he had learned that looking was the beginning of hoping and hoping was the beginning of the kind of foolishness he had committed today. He climbed to his rooms above the chandler's shop. He lit no candle on the shelf. He sat in the dark with his gloves off and his bare right hand open in his lap, watching the faint ember-glow of the sigil pulse slow as breathing, and he made himself a promise that felt like breaking another one.

*One more sign,* he told the silence. *One more, and I stop obeying. One more, and I look for you whether you want me to or not.*

Outside, the rain went on falling on the gray city, patient as debt.

Three days later, the enforcers came to the Wardmarket in numbers no shakedown had ever required, and the first thing Xanthe saw, over the heads of the crowd, was Odile running.
