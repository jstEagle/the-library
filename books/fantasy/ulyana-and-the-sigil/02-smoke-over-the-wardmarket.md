## 2. Smoke Over the Wardmarket

The wardmarket woke before dawn, as it always did, in the gray hour when Vask's lamplighters went home and the fog came up off the canal smelling of tallow and river rot. Xanthe had been there since the fourth bell, laying out his wares on a folding table with the practiced care of a man arranging a lie: rows of small charms on knotted cords, each one blessed-looking and each one dead. A knot for safe travel. A knot for easy birth. A knot against fever, against drowning, against the slow grief that settled into widows like damp into stone.

None of them worked. He knew it. The buyers mostly knew it too, or half-knew it, which was enough. In a city where magic had been outlawed for ten years, people didn't buy charms because they believed. They bought them because not believing had become unbearable, and a cord with a knot in it was cheaper than hope and easier to hold.

Xanthe sold them anyway, and hated himself a little more each morning, and kept his glove on.

The mark sat under the leather like a coal banked in ash. It had never gone cold—not once in ten years, not through the purges, not through the night the enforcers burned the last temple of Ulyana and he'd stood in the crowd with his fist clenched around the heat in his palm while everyone around him wept or cheered or simply watched. The covenant-mark of a goddess who no longer spoke should have faded to a scar. His stayed warm. Some mornings he pressed his thumb against it just to feel it answer, the way you touch a sleeping animal to confirm it's still breathing.

"Charm-seller." Odile's voice came over his shoulder, low and amused. "You've got your martyr face on again."

He turned. She was setting up her own stall two spaces down, which was technically illegal twice over—once for trading without a license, once because half of what she sold was relic-adjacent and all of it was stolen. Odile was small and quick and had a face like a closed door, brown skin going gray at the temples, fingers always moving: counting coin, straightening stock, adjusting the knife at her hip the way other women adjusted a necklace. She'd been fencing goods since before the Silence, back when some of those goods still *did* things.

"You're late," Xanthe said.

"I'm worth waiting for." She dropped a crate onto her table and began unpacking candle-stubs, prayer-cards, a chipped icon of some minor hearth-saint whose name even the enforcers had forgotten to fear. "You eat yet?"

"I'm fine."

"You said that yesterday and then fainted at the lockstep. Sit down. I brought bread." She pushed a heel of dark loaf across the gap between their stalls without looking at him, the way she did everything generous—as if it were an accident, as if acknowledging it would cost her something she couldn't spare.

He ate. Around them the market filled: fishwives and rag-pickers, a tanner with stained hands, old Marta who sold blessings-by-the-inch of ribbon, the twins who ran messages for anyone who paid and asked nothing for anyone. The ordinary machinery of a city getting by. Somewhere beyond the rooftops the enforcers' garrison drums marked the changing of the watch, and the whole market flinched by reflex—a thousand small pauses, a breath held and released—and then went on.

That was Vask now. A city that flinched first and lived second.

* * *

It happened at midmorning, when the market was at its fullest and most careless.

Xanthe heard the boots before he saw them—the particular cadence of enforcer patrol, eight men walking in step, iron-shod, unhurried. Unhurried was worse than hurried. Hurried meant a chase, and chases could be dodged. Unhurried meant they'd already decided how this would end.

"Wardmarket," called the sergeant, a broad woman with a voice like a door slamming. "By order of the Magistracy of Public Purity. Stalls down. Everyone stand away from your goods. This is a search, not a seizure—yet."

The market dissolved into motion. Canvas came down, crates were kicked shut, and the crowd compressed itself toward the canal-side exits in that terrible silent way crowds do when they've learned that running is the same as confessing. Xanthe swept his charms into their box with hands that were steadier than he felt. Dead knots. Worthless wood. They couldn't hang him for those, though they might try; a charm-seller was guilty of something by definition, the way a rat-catcher was guilty of rats.

Odile wasn't fast enough.

Later, he would replay it a hundred times and find the moment where it turned—she'd been reaching for the false bottom of her crate, the one with the reliquary locket inside, a silver thing older than the city that she'd been holding for a buyer who never came. Her fingers were still in the crate when the sergeant's shadow fell across her table.

"Well," the sergeant said. "What have we here."

"It's candlestock," Odile said. "Tin. You can see it's tin."

The sergeant reached past her and lifted out the locket, and even from three stalls away Xanthe saw the light catch it—that particular deep gleam silver gets when it has spent decades near an altar, a gleam that no amount of tarnish quite kills. The sergeant turned it over once. Twice. Then she looked up, and her eyes found Odile's face, and whatever she saw there ended the conversation before it started.

"Relic possession," she said, loud enough for the whole square. "Article Nine."

"I want it noted," Odile said, and her voice was level, only a little too careful, "that I was selling it as scrap. As scrap metal. There's no divinity left in it. Ask your own censors—they certified the district inert four years ago."

"They certified it inert," the sergeant agreed, turning the locket in her gloved hand. "So either you're lying about scrap—" she smiled, without any warmth in it at all, "—or it isn't inert anymore. Either way, you come with us."

Two enforcers took Odile's arms. She didn't struggle. That was the worst part, Xanthe thought afterward—she didn't struggle, just went stiff and straight-backed, chin up, a merchant being escorted rather than a woman being taken. But as they turned her toward the wagons her eyes swept the crowd once, quick, professional, the look of a fence taking inventory—and found him.

She didn't call out. Of course she didn't. Calling out would make him a witness, and witnesses got questioned, and questioned people got searched, and searched people with warm marks under their gloves got burned. Ten years of friendship, and what passed between them in that one glance was a whole negotiation conducted in silence: *Don't. Don't be stupid. Don't follow. Live.*

Then the wagon gate banged shut, and she was gone, and the locket went into an evidence box, and the market exhaled and began, shakily, to put itself back together.

Xanthe stood behind his table with his fists at his sides and the guilt sitting on his chest like a stone. Because here was the arithmetic he couldn't stop doing: if he had warned her—if he'd heard the boots sooner, if he'd knocked over his own stall, made a scene, bought her thirty seconds—she'd have gotten the crate shut. If he hadn't been so busy protecting himself, so practiced at invisibility, so *careful*—

Ten years of careful. And it had bought him exactly nothing today except the privilege of watching.

* * *

He was still standing there when the candles lit.

Not his candles—he sold knots, not tallow. Every candle in the wardmarket. Every stub on every stall, every votive in the little tin niches the fishwives kept, every rushlight in the shuttered windows above, every guttering scrap of flame in every lantern along the canal path—all of them flared at once, high and white and steady, as if a great inhale had passed through the district and filled every fire to its brim.

The crowd stopped. All of it, all at once, the way birds go quiet.

Xanthe's first thought was *gas*. The canal gave off marsh-methane sometimes; a pocket of it could make flames leap. But gas made flames gutter and dance and die. This was different. This was every flame standing up straight at attention, burning tall and calm and *attentive*, and the light coming off them wasn't orange but gold-white, the color of noon in a month of fog, and it fell across the ruined market like a blessing falling across a wound.

His mark woke.

Not warmed—woke. Under the glove it flared hot as a brand, and Xanthe bit down on a cry and clamped his hand against his thigh, and through the leather he felt it pulse, once, twice, in a rhythm he knew in his bones because he had felt it ten years ago across a battlefield: the rhythm of Ulyana's heartbeat, transmitted down the covenant like light down a wire.

*No,* he thought. *No, you're dead, you're gone, you went quiet, this is a trick—*

But the candles burned on. And the crowd—gods, the crowd. Old Marta had gone to her knees in the mud with her ribbons spilling everywhere, and she was laughing, actually laughing, tears cutting through the grime on her face. The tanner had snatched off his cap. The twins stood frozen with their message-bags dangling, staring at the flames with identical open mouths. Someone began to pray aloud—the old opening words, the ones that would get a person flogged if an enforcer heard them—and instead of stopping, others joined, haltingly, rustily, voices remembering themselves after a decade of disuse:

*"Light before us. Light behind us. Light that knows our names—"*

"DISPERSE!" the sergeant bellowed, but her voice cracked in the middle of the word, and half her enforcers weren't moving, weren't doing anything except standing in that gold-white light with their faces turned upward like sunflowers. One of them—young, freckled, barely old enough for the uniform—had his hand pressed flat over his chest, over his heart, and his lips were moving.

The flare lasted eleven seconds. Xanthe counted. Eleven seconds of impossible light, and then every flame in the wardmarket sank back to its ordinary size, its ordinary color, its ordinary hunger for fuel, and the fog closed in gray again, and the silence afterward was louder than the praying had been.

Nobody moved for a long moment. Then, all together, as if released from a held breath, the market erupted—into panic, into prayer, into argument, into the frantic business of pretending nothing had happened, which in Vask was the oldest survival skill of all.

The sergeant recovered fastest. "Search is concluded," she snapped. "Market reopens at dawn. Anyone spreading rumors of—of manifestations will answer to the Magistracy." But she said it while staring at the nearest lantern as if it might bite her, and her enforcers withdrew in loose order, glancing backward, and the wagon with Odile in it rolled away through streets where every window held a face.

Xanthe stood alone among the abandoned stalls, cradling his burning hand.

Eleven seconds. He turned the number over the way a miser turns a coin. In ten years of silence, nothing—no flicker, no warmth beyond his own stubborn mark, no answered prayer anywhere in the realm that anyone could prove. And now, within a single week of the first whispered rumors from the provinces, within days of the widow's story he'd dismissed as grief-hallucination, the fires of Vask itself had stood up and *listened*.

The gods are waking, the rumors said. The prayers are being answered again.

And his mark was hot, and his friend was in a wagon headed for the Questioning Rooms, and somewhere under his fear, treacherous and enormous, hope was unfurling like a fist finally opened after ten years.

He wanted to kneel. Right there in the mud between the stalls, glove and all, he wanted to fall to his knees and pour out a decade of words—the accusations, the begging, the love he'd had nowhere to put. Instead he stood very still and let the heat pulse against his leg, and made himself think the ugly thoughts, the necessary ones.

Because something had answered. Something had filled every flame in the district with light. But Ulyana had gone silent deliberately—or so the theologians guessed, before guessing became heresy—and a goddess who chose silence did not break it casually, in a market square, for an audience of fishwives. And the widow's story, the one he'd filed away as wishful thinking—*it healed me, charm-seller, it healed my lungs, but it left a mark*—

A mark. She'd said it left a mark on her skin.

Xanthe looked down at his gloved hand, at the heat still throbbing beneath the leather, and felt the day tilt beneath him. He needed to find that widow. Tonight. Before the enforcers did, before the rumors grew teeth, before whatever had answered the wardmarket's candles answered something else—something bigger, something that couldn't be walked away from.

But first, he thought, with a fresh wave of guilt souring the wonder, first he had to get Odile out. Whatever was waking in the world, it wouldn't mean anything if he abandoned the people who'd kept him alive through the silence.

He packed his dead charms, pulled his glove tighter, and went to find out where they'd taken her—and what, exactly, the locket had done in the evidence box to make the sergeant's hands shake.

Behind him, unnoticed, the last candle stub on his abandoned table guttered, flared once—gold-white, attentive—and went out.
