## 4. The Marked Woman

The mark woke her before the bells did.

It was always like that now—three weeks since the widow's healing, three weeks since the warmth had crawled up out of the crowd and into Sera's wrist like a swallowed coal—and it had opinions about mornings. It pulsed once, twice, patient as a heartbeat that wasn't hers, and then settled into a low simmer beneath the skin of her forearm where she could feel it even through the sleeve of her clerk's uniform.

Sera lay in her narrow bed above the chandler's shop on Veil Street and did not look at it. Looking at it was a concession. She had rules about concessions.

Outside, Vask was assembling itself from fog and lamplight. She could hear the chandler's boy dragging his cart of tallow down the stairs, the groan of shutters, a gull screaming somewhere over the Graywater docks. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of a city that had spent ten years learning to live without miracles and had gotten very good at pretending the loss didn't ache.

Her mark ached anyway.

*You are mine now,* it said, in the wordless way it said everything. *You are known.*

She dressed by feel in the dark: gray wool, gray cloak, the plain collar of the Office of Inquiry. In the washstand's cloudy mirror she pinned her hair back with the efficiency of someone who had stopped expecting to be seen. Twenty-six years old, sharp-faced, forgettable on purpose. A clerk. Clerks were furniture. Furniture heard everything.

On her desk sat the morning's docket, and on top of the docket, weighted under her inkwell, the summons she had been turning over all night like a stone with something alive underneath it.

*By order of Inquisitor Corvane: all persons bearing unlicensed sigils are to be identified, catalogued, and remanded for examination. Priority: witnesses to the Wardmarket manifestation.*

The Wardmarket manifestation. That was what the Office called it—the raid, the arrest of the fence Odile Ferrin, and the moment when every candle in the district had flared at once, high and gold and wrong, like a held breath finally let out. Forty witnesses. Sera had taken eleven depositions herself, sitting behind Corvane's shoulder with her pen scratching, and in nine of them the same figure surfaced like a body in deep water:

*A charm-seller. Gray coat. Gloved hands. He was there when the candles went up. He looked at them like he knew why.*

Sera had written *charm-seller, unknown name* in the margin of four separate statements, and each time her mark had warmed against her cuff, subtle as a cat pressing its flank against an ankle. Approving. Interested.

That was the part she couldn't say aloud, not to Corvane, not to anyone: the mark had been pleased about the charm-seller. Pleased the way a hound is pleased when it scents blood.

She had not told anyone about the mark at all.

---

The Office of Inquiry occupied the old customs house on the riverfront, a building of black basalt and taller windows than any church was allowed these days. Sera climbed the stairs to the third-floor archive room, where the morning light came through leaded glass in gray bars and fell across forty years of confiscated relics, catalogued and boxed and slowly rotting in their wrappings.

Corvane was already there. He was always already there; she suspected he slept standing up in some alcove like a sword racked between wars. He was a long man, dry as August grass, with a voice like paper tearing, and he had been hunting outlawed magic since before Sera could write her own name. He frightened her less than he should have and more than she admitted.

"Vess." He didn't look up from the deposition he was annotating. "The charm-seller."

"Yes, sir."

"Three witnesses place him within ten feet of the flare. One—" he turned the page toward her, and she saw Odile Ferrin's statement in her own neat transcription, "—claims he performed a rite. Unsanctioned. During the raid itself, while my officers were making arrests." His eyes came up, pale and patient. "Ferrin is protecting him. She'll sing differently after sentencing today, but I want him found regardless. If the gods are waking, Vess—and I believe they are—then every marked person in this city is either a door or a kindling. I need to know which."

"And if they're doors?" Sera asked. It was the question she'd been carrying for three weeks, balanced carefully so it sounded like diligence rather than dread.

"Then we learn who's knocking." Corvane smiled without warmth. "The Office has served the law for ten years while heaven kept quiet. We are not going to be caught sentimental now that it's started talking again." He tapped the deposition. "Wardmarket. Today. Watch the sentencing, watch the crowd, find me your charm-seller. Discreetly. If he runs, I want to know his legs before I know his face."

"Yes, sir."

He returned to his annotations. Sera gathered her notebook, her pen, her orders—and as she turned to go, her mark pulsed once, warm and insistent, like a hand closing gently around her wrist from the inside.

*Yes,* it seemed to say. *Go. Find him.*

She walked down the stairs with her heart going hard and uneven, and told herself it was fear of Corvane, because fear of Corvane was simple and permitted, and what she actually felt was not.

---

The Hall of Judgment had been a temple once. Everyone in Vask knew it and no one said it. The goddess's mosaic still glittered under whitewash along the vaulted ceiling—a suggestion of wings, a hint of a woman's face rendered in ten thousand tiles, plastered over a decade ago by men who thought silence could be enforced with lime and brushwork. Now the benches where worshippers had knelt held magistrates and clerks, and the altar dais held the sentencing bench, and the god-shaped absence at the center of the room was filled instead with procedure.

Sera took a place at the back, notebook open, playing her role. Around her the gallery filled: clerks, gawkers, a knot of Wardmarket traders who had come to watch one of their own go down. And near the front, alone on the defendants' bench, sat Odile Ferrin.

Sera had interviewed her for two hours on the night of the raid. Odile was sixty-odd, built like a barrel stave, with a fence's quick dark eyes and a mouth that treated every question as a negotiation. She had confessed to possessing the relic—a cracked votive lamp, pre-Outlawing, worth maybe five crowns and twenty years—with the cheerful defiance of a woman who considered the whole exercise beneath her. But when Corvane asked about the charm-seller, something in her face had closed like a shutter.

*I don't sell names,* she'd said. *I buy and sell objects. Names aren't objects.*

Now she sat listening to the magistrate read the charges, and her eyes moved through the gallery—not searching, exactly. Waiting.

Sera followed the line of her waiting and found him.

He stood at the back of the aisle, half in shadow beside the rain barrels, and she knew him immediately from the witnesses' words: gray coat, travel-worn, a mercenary's shoulders gone lean with years of lighter work. He was perhaps forty, with a beard trimmed short and a face that might have been handsome before something had sanded the softness off it. And his hands—

His right hand was gloved. In a warm courtroom, in autumn, among people who had nothing to hide.

The mark flared.

Not warm this time—*hot*, a wire pulled taut through her arm, and Sera had to set her teeth against the sound that wanted to come out of her throat. Around her, no one noticed. The magistrate droned. The mark sang its silent note of recognition, of appetite, and Sera understood with sick clarity that whatever wore Ulyana's sigil on her skin wanted this man found the way fire wants a wick.

*There,* said the heat. *Him. Ours. Bring him.*

She wrote nothing. Her pen hovered over the page and her hand would not obey, and she watched the charm-seller instead, because watching him was easier than examining what was happening inside her own arm.

He wasn't watching the magistrate. He was watching Odile. And his face—

Sera had spent six years reading faces for the Office. Guilt, fear, calculation, the small corruptions of ordinary people—she could catalogue them in her sleep. What was on the charm-seller's face was none of those things. It was grief, plain and undressed, the grief of a man watching someone suffer for something he believed was his fault. He stood absolutely still, the way soldiers stand when they've learned that movement gets people killed, and his eyes never left Odile Ferrin, and when the magistrate pronounced sentence—two years' labor at the salt works, confiscation of goods—he closed his gloved hand around the rail of the bench behind him and gripped it until Sera, even at thirty feet, could see the tendons stand out like cables.

Odile was led away. She passed within arm's reach of him, and she did not look at him—deliberately, Sera realized, deliberately not looking, protecting him with her blindness—and the charm-seller bowed his head as she went by, a small motion, almost a prayer.

And his glove, Sera saw, had split along the seam where he'd gripped the rail.

Through the split showed skin, and on the skin, faint but unmistakable in the gray light from the high windows: a sigil. A circle broken by a descending line, crossed at the base by two short strokes.

Her own mark answered it.

The heat in her arm didn't flare this time. It *reached*. Sera felt it strain toward him across the room like a plant toward light, felt it press against the boundaries of her body as though it could stretch out through her pores and touch him, claim him, call to him—and underneath the strangeness of it, underneath the violation, she felt something worse. She felt it *recognize* him. Not as prey. As kin.

The sigil on his hand was the same shape as hers. Almost.

Almost. There was a difference, and her eye—which had spent six years cataloguing confiscated relics, which knew the grammar of divine marks better than any clerk in the Office—caught it and held it. Hers was clean-edged, complete, a finished thing stamped on her from outside. His was rougher, older, worn into the skin like a scar rather than printed on it like a seal. Same geometry. Different hand. The way a forged coin can carry every line of the true die and still be wrong in some way the fingers know before the eyes do.

Two marks. Two makers.

One of them was not Ulyana.

---

The gallery emptied. Sera stayed in her seat longer than she needed to, transcribing sentences she barely read, until the charm-seller rose and left through the side door into the colonnade, and she told herself she was following him because Corvane had ordered it, because it was her duty, because a clerk who failed to follow a priority target would answer for it.

All true. None of it the reason.

He walked slowly, like a man whose bones weighed more than they used to. Through the colonnade, past the fish stalls setting up their reeking tables, down toward the Graywater where the fog was burning off in ragged sheets. He stopped at the railing above the mudflats and stood looking out at the water, and Sera drifted to a position behind a stack of crab pots, close enough to hear him breathe.

He took off his glove.

She hadn't expected that. He checked the colonnade first—she flattened herself behind the pots, heart hammering—and then, finding the walkway empty, he peeled the leather back from his hand and held it up to the strengthening light, studying the sigil the way a man studies a wound he cannot remember taking.

And then he spoke. Quietly. To no one.

"I know," he said. His voice was rough, unused to its own volume. "I know you're doing something. I can feel it, these last few days—like a door opening somewhere. Ten years of nothing and now—" He stopped. His jaw worked. "If it's you, I'm here. You know I'm here. I never went anywhere."

Nothing answered. Of course nothing answered. He was talking to the air, to the water, to a goddess ten years dead or fled or silenced, and Sera watched him do it and felt her chest tighten with something she refused to name.

Because she knew that posture. She knew what it was to speak into a silence and get nothing back. She'd done it her whole life—in the foundling house, in the Office, in every gray corridor of a city that had outlawed wonder—and three weeks ago, for the first time, something had finally answered her. Something warm. Something that called itself by the goddess's name and made her wrist glow and told her *you are known, you are mine*, and she had wept the first night, alone in her bed, wept like a child, because being known was the only thing she had ever wanted.

And now she stood behind a stack of crab pots watching a man beg the same silence for the same mercy, and her mark burned on her arm with an eagerness that had nothing holy in it, and the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for three weeks presented itself all at once, complete and undeniable:

Whatever had answered her prayers was hungry. Whatever had healed the widow had left a brand. Whatever wanted the charm-seller found did not want him found out of love.

And this man, this tired gray man grieving over a fence bound for the salt works, had loved the real thing enough to keep faith with a silence for ten years.

The mark pulsed. *Bring him,* it said. *Tell the tall one. Bring him to us.*

Sera looked at her notebook. The page was blank. She had written nothing since the courtroom, and Corvane expected a report by evening bell, and the report would either contain a name and a description and a location—or it would not.

She thought about the widow Marta Ilves, whom she'd deposed on the second day. *It didn't hurt,* Marta had said of the healing, of the sigil burned into her breastbone. *That's the strange part. It should have hurt, and it didn't, and I can't stop being grateful.* Grateful. Branded and grateful. Sera knew the feeling from the inside now, and knowing it from the inside was precisely what made it terrifying.

The charm-seller put his glove back on. He stood a moment longer at the rail, and then he turned, and Sera pressed flat against the crab pots—but he didn't look her way. He walked back up the colonnade with his head down, a gray man in a gray city, and disappeared into the crowd toward the Wardmarket.

Toward home, probably. Toward wherever a charm-seller kept his charms.

She could follow him. She could have his address to Corvane by dusk, and by week's end the Office would kick in his door, and whatever happened after that—examination, interrogation, the things that happened in the basement rooms of the customs house—would happen with her signature at the bottom of the order. And her mark would purr the whole time.

Or she could write *charm-seller, unidentified, trail lost at the Graywater*, and buy him a day, and lie to the only institution she had ever belonged to, on behalf of a stranger, because a thing on her arm wanted him and she was beginning to understand that anything it wanted, she should want to withhold.

Sera opened her notebook. Wrote the date. Wrote: *Subject observed at sentencing. No approach made. Trail lost.*

The lie sat on the page, small and enormous.

Her mark had gone cold. Not dormant—cold, the specific cold of displeasure, of a hand withdrawing. She felt it sulking under her cuff like a wound-up thing denied, and she understood that it knew. However it knew what it knew, it knew she had chosen, just now, in this small way, against it.

Let it sulk, she thought. Let it freeze. She had been cold her whole life before it came along.

She closed the notebook and climbed the colonnade steps toward the customs house, and it was only halfway there, passing the fish stalls and the gulls and the ordinary noise of the waking city, that she admitted what she had really learned at the railing this morning. Not about the charm-seller. About herself.

She had looked at his grief and recognized it, and recognition was the beginning of loyalty, and loyalty to a marked stranger was treason against both her employer and her god.

Both of whom, she was increasingly sure, were not on the same side of anything anymore.

At the top of the steps she paused. Below, far off across the rooftops, the Wardmarket was opening for the day—and she thought of the charm-seller walking toward it, gloved and grieving, walking unknowing into a city that was filling up with things that wore the goddess's face.

*Ten years of nothing,* he had said. *And now—*

She needed to know what the *now* was. Before Corvane found it. Before the thing on her arm did.

Sera turned—not toward the customs house, but toward the archive stair, and the sealed records of the Office, and everything the Office knew about the last days before the Silence.

If she was going to betray someone, she intended to first find out who deserved it.
