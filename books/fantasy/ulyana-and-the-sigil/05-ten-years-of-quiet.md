## 5. Ten Years of Quiet

The rain came down on Vask like a verdict, and Xanthe sat in the back room of Odile's shop with a candle burning low, turning a lead tablet over in his gloved hands.

It was not one of the shards. It was nothing at all—a scrap of roofing lead he'd pried from the chapel ruins on Tanner's Row, blank as an unspoken thought. But it had the weight of the thing he'd touched in the undercroft, or near enough, and holding it let him pretend for a while that his hands were steady.

*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

Ten years. Ten years since those words, and he had obeyed them the way a dog obeys a door left closed—faithfully, miserably, pressing his nose to the wood every night and never once scratching. He had told himself it was devotion. He had told himself it was trust. Tonight, with the widow's burned sigil still ghosting behind his eyes and Sera's warning still ringing in his ears—*the shards are bait, but bait means someone fishing*—he found he could no longer tell the difference between faith and cowardice.

The candle guttered. Outside, the wardmarket bells rang the hour, and somewhere below, Odile moved through her stock with the careful clatter of a woman pretending not to listen.

He had been twenty-six at Redford Bridge. He could still count the years on his fingers if he tried, and some nights he did, the way other men counted sheep.

* * *

They had crossed at dawn, the company of them—forty mercenaries under contract to the Duke of Vask's eastern marches, paid to hold the bridge against the river-clans come down out of the hills. It should have been a small thing. A season's work. Xanthe remembered the mist coming off the water in ropes, and Ulyana's presence in him like a banked fire, warm along the left side of his chest where her mark sat beneath the skin: a circle broken by a descending line, the sigil of the goddess who kept thresholds and heard small prayers.

She had never been a war-god. That was what he'd loved about her, in the beginning, when he'd taken the covenant at nineteen with more desperation than piety—an orphan of the river wars, sold twice before he was twelve, looking for anyone who would claim him. Other gods wanted armies. Ulyana wanted *witnesses*. Her covenant was simple, almost embarrassingly so: *Keep my thresholds. Hear what is small. Speak my name only with your own voice behind it.* In exchange she gave her servants small miracles—the mending of bone, the turning of ill luck, the quieting of fear—and her voice, sometimes, warm at the base of the skull like sunlight through a window.

For seven years she had spoken to him nearly every day. Not grand things. Whether to shelter under the oak or the eave before the storm broke. The name of a fever that was killing a farmer's daughter. Once, memorably, that a man Xanthe was about to shake hands with had stolen from his last employer, and Xanthe had declined the handshake and the theft both.

At Redford Bridge, the mist came up and the clans came down, and the battle went wrong almost immediately. The duke's captain was dead inside the first hour; the line folded like wet paper; Xanthe found himself commanding men twice his age because he was the only one still hearing a god's voice, and her voice was calm, and calm was contagious.

*Left flank. The boy with the red shield is going to break—put Tomas beside him.*

He put Tomas beside him. The line held.

*There is a child under the third cart. Get her out before the horses panic.*

He got her out. The horses panicked anyway, and a clan axe meant for the cart caught him across the shoulder, and he went down into the mud with the battle roaring over him and Ulyana's warmth suddenly—

Gone.

Not faded. Not distant, the way she went when she turned her attention elsewhere. *Gone*, the way a lamp goes when someone carries it out of the room. One heartbeat there was fire along his ribs; the next, cold. He lay in the churned mud of Redford Bridge with blood filling his boot and reached for her the way you reach for a handrail in the dark, and found nothing, and the nothing had a shape to it, a finality, like a door not merely shut but bricked over.

He screamed her name into the mud. He was not the only one. Across the bridge, in the hedge-temples and way-shrines of three kingdoms, every servant of every god was screaming the same scream that day, though none of them knew it yet. The Silence came all at once, everywhere, mid-heartbeat, mid-prayer, mid-battle. Later, the scholars would argue about whether it lasted a moment or an eternity; the priests who survived it said only that the world had gone deaf.

Xanthe crawled off the bridge. He bound his own shoulder with a tourniquet made from a dead man's sash. And because the covenant said *keep my thresholds*, and because there was no one else, he spent the rest of that day dragging the wounded off the crossing one by one, doing with his hands everything she would once have done with a word.

That was the first lesson of the Silence, though he didn't know it yet: the work remained. Only the voice was gone.

* * *

The first year, he waited.

He took a room above a dyer's shop in Vask and told himself she was testing them. Gods tested their faithful; every catechism said so. He prayed at dawn and dusk with a discipline that would have shamed a monastery. He kept her thresholds—the doorways of his building swept clean each morning, the little iron threshold-bells polished, because a threshold untended was a slight to her, and even silent, she might be watching.

He kept the mark hidden. That was law now, within the year—the Outlawing, they called it, the Grand Ordinance of the Assembly, magic proscribed, covenant-marks declared brands of sedition, temples pulled down or converted to granaries and assize courts. The enforcers came through Vask with their null-irons and their ledgers, and men who had healed whole villages were hanged beside coiners and horse-thieves. Xanthe learned to wear gloves. Learned to flinch from no one. Learned that a mercenary's scars excused a great many things about a man's hands.

He did not stop praying. That was the thing Odile had never understood, in all the years of their friendship—that he could have stopped. The mark was inert, as far as any examiner could tell. He could have scraped it off with a null-blade, sold his knowledge of the old rites to the inquisition, lived fat and safe. Plenty had. Half the surviving clergy had recanted within the decade, standing on public scaffolds renouncing gods who had, after all, abandoned *them* first.

But the covenant had never been conditional. She hadn't said *hear what is small, and I will answer*. She had said *hear what is small*. Full stop. The answering had always been grace, not wage. So Xanthe went on doing the work: the mending of bones with splints and salves instead of words, the sitting with the dying so no one crossed alone, the small anonymous kindnesses that had once been miracles and were now merely labor. He became, without meaning to, a charm-seller—because people needed something to hold, and he needed money, and because selling a hollow charm to a grieving woman felt less like fraud than like handing her an empty cup and saying *I know you're thirsty. I am too.*

The second year, he stopped waiting and started keeping.

He began a ledger—not of prayers, but of silences. Every place she had ever spoken to him, he visited once a year on the anniversary. The oak outside Ferrow. The shrine under the aqueduct. The chapel on Tanner's Row where she had first called him by name. He would stand in these places with his bare hand pressed to stone or bark and wait for an hour, feeling nothing, recording nothing, and then walk home in the dark.

Odile caught him at it once, in the fourth year, kneeling in the mud under the aqueduct at midnight with his glove off.

"You look like a man visiting a grave," she said.

"He isn't dead," Xanthe said. "That's the difference."

"Is it?" Odile crouched beside him, a fence's pragmatism softening into something older. "Ten years, Xanthe. If a lover went silent ten years, we'd call you a fool. If a friend did, we'd call you bereaved. What do we call it when a god does?"

"I don't know," he said. "Faithful, maybe. Or stubborn."

"Those are the same word in Vask." She'd put her coat around his shoulders then, because the rain had started, and walked him home, and neither of them spoke of it again for six years.

* * *

But memory is a dishonest ledger, and tonight, in the back room with the blank lead in his hands, Xanthe forced himself to audit it honestly. Because there was one entry he had never written down, one moment he had buried so deep he could almost believe he'd imagined it—and Sera's questions had dug it up like a spade through shallow earth.

The seventh year. The widow Maren, whose son had drowned in the canal. She had come to his stall not for a charm but to rage at him, having guessed somehow what he was—grieving mothers guess well—and she had seized his wrist through the glove and shouted into his face: *Where was she? Where was your goddess when my boy went under? You wear her brand and sell her name and where was she?*

And Xanthe, who had asked himself the same question ten thousand times, had opened his mouth to give the answer he always gave—*I don't know, I don't know*—and instead heard himself say:

"She told me not to look for her."

Maren had gone still. "What?"

"The last thing she ever said to me." His heart was hammering. He had never said it aloud, not to Odile, not to anyone, not even in prayer—because saying it aloud made it real, made it an instruction rather than a wound. "*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*"

"And you obeyed." Maren's voice had dropped to something terrible and quiet. "My son is dead, and you have spent ten years obeying the words that killed him."

She had walked out. He had stood in the market with his ears ringing, and that night he had knelt under the aqueduct until dawn, turning the sentence over like a coin he suspected was clipped.

*Do not look for me.*

He had always heard it as grief. As farewell. As a god's apology delivered in advance. But he had been a soldier at Redford Bridge; he knew how orders worked; and there were other ways to hear five words. *Do not look for me* could mean *I am beyond reach*. It could also mean *I am hiding*. And things that hid—things that *sealed themselves away*—did so for reasons, and the reasons were rarely gentle, and the instruction not to search was exactly what you gave a faithful servant if whatever hunted you must not find him following the trail.

Or it could mean what it plainly said: *leave me alone*. Abandonment dressed as mercy. A god sparing herself the sight of her faithful starving.

Both readings fit. Neither reading fit. For three years he had carried the sentence in both hands like a hot stone, setting it down and picking it up, and finally he had buried it, because a man can serve a silence only if he doesn't examine the silence too closely.

Now the counterfeit had changed the arithmetic. Something was answering prayers in Ulyana's name, wearing a sigil that was hers and not hers, healing widows and leaving brands. Which meant either she had returned and was unrecognizable—or something had found the shape of her absence and poured itself into it. And if the second thing was true, then his obedience for ten years had not been devotion. It had been vacancy. He had guarded an empty house so well that squatters had moved in.

* * *

Below, the floorboards creaked. Odile's voice, pitched low: "You're still awake. I can hear you thinking. It sounds like a mill wheel."

"Come up, then."

She climbed the ladder with two cups and the bottle she saved for bad nights, and sat across from him, and looked at the lead tablet in his hands for a long time.

"Sera came by," she said. "While you were out. Told me what the magistrate's clerk told her—the inquisition has a list. Sigil-bearers. Real ones, the old marks. They're collecting us, Xanthe. Or something is, wearing the inquisition's coat." She poured without asking. "So. Are you going to say it, or shall I?"

"Say what?"

"What you've been circling for three days. I've watched you do it since the undercroft. You hold that lead like it owes you money." She drank. "You're going after her. After *Ulyana*. The shards, the name, all of it. You're going to break the one instruction she ever left you."

Xanthe looked at the candle. The flame stood perfectly straight, as if attentive.

"When I took the covenant," he said slowly, "she told me the mark wasn't a leash. She said a covenant is two voices. She said the day I couldn't speak back to her, the day I couldn't say *no* to her, was the day it stopped being a covenant and became a collar." His thumb moved over the blank lead. "For ten years I've treated her last words as a command. Obeyed them like a soldier. But she never commanded anything in her life, Odile. Not once. She *asked*. And a question deserves an answer, and I have spent ten years giving her silence for silence, like an idiot, like a mourner, when what she left me was a question wearing a soldier's uniform."

"*Do not look for me.*"

"Do not look for me," he agreed. "And I have decided—" His voice caught, steadied. "I have decided that I am done being obedient to a sentence. I loved her. I love her still, God help me, and love that goes silent for a decade either dies or turns into something with teeth. Mine turned into teeth. I'm going to find the shards. I'm going to find her true name. And I'm going to stand wherever she is—at the bottom of whatever grave or fortress or *seal* she hid herself in—and I am going to ask her to her face why. And if the answer is abandonment, then I'll take off this mark myself and burn it. And if the answer is anything else—"

He stopped. Because he couldn't finish it, because finishing it meant saying *then I will bring her home*, and saying that aloud, after ten years of disciplined despair, was more than his throat could bear.

Odile refilled his cup. "And if it's a trap? If the shards are bait, like the girl said?"

"Then I'm the fish," Xanthe said. "But Odile—I've been swimming in circles for ten years. At least this way, I find out what's on the hook."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the fogged window, the city of Vask lay gray and sleeping, its outlawed shrines dark, its new cathedral—still under construction, rising obscene and white over the rooftops where the temple of Ulyana had stood—catching the first pale light of morning.

And on the windowsill, in a draft that had no business reaching that far into the room, the candle flame bent toward the east. Toward the borderlands. Toward the thin places.

Toward the drowned chapel where, Odile's contacts said, a second shard of a dead goddess's name slept under guard.

Xanthe watched the flame lean, and felt—for the first time in ten years—not the ache of absence, but the pull of a road.

He put on his gloves, and began to pack.
