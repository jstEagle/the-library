## 6. The Undercroft of Names

The archive of the Chancellery of Inquiry smelled of dust and vinegar and the particular sourness of old fear. Xanthe had never been inside a government building before—mercenaries and charm-sellers did not, as a rule, stroll through the front doors of the institution that would hang them—but Sera had a clerk's lanyard around her neck and a face that made doors open, and so he walked through them like a man wading into cold water: deliberately, wincing, committed.

"Keep your hands in your pockets," she murmured without turning her head. "You have a thief's hands."

"I have a *mercenary's* hands."

"You have hands that look like they're always about to take something. It's very distracting." She led him past shelves of bound depositions, past a wall of wanted-notices gone gray at the edges, past two junior clerks who looked up, assessed him as furniture, and looked down again. "The theological records are this way. Nobody requests them. The last person to check anything out of the pre-Outlawing collection was nine years ago, and he was purged shortly after for asking inconvenient questions about the nature of divinity."

"What kind of questions?"

"The kind that get answers." She said it flatly, but her fingers brushed her sleeve where, beneath the wool, Xanthe knew now, a sigil sat burned into her skin—a sigil like his own goddess's mark and yet not, the way a portrait is not a man. She had shown him it three nights ago in the back room of a shuttered dye-house, by candlelight, both of them flinching from their own shadows. Two marked people in a city that burned marked people. An alliance built on nothing but mutual incrimination.

It was, Xanthe reflected, the most honest relationship he'd had in ten years.

She stopped before a door of blackened oak, iron-banded, with a lock that had not been oiled since the Outlawing. "The undercroft access ledger says the key was lost in the fire of the old chancellery," she said. "The ledger lies. The key is in Provost Hallow's desk drawer, third one down, beneath a false bottom, because Provost Hallow is a coward who wants deniability about everything, including the fact that he knows exactly what's down there."

"You've been reading his desk."

"I file his correspondence. I read everything I file." She glanced at him, and there was something in her face—not quite guilt, not quite defiance. "I told you. I'm good at my job. That's the problem with me."

"It's the best thing about you," Xanthe said, and meant it, and watched her decide not to be pleased about it.

* * *

Sera had been raised on the catechism of the Chancellery the way other children were raised on bread: daily, unquestioningly, until it became the substance of her body. *Magic is theft. The gods are dead or worse. The Silence was mercy.* She had copied out that catechism in fair hand for eleven years, had stamped it, sealed it, filed it. And then a widow had come to the chancellery with a healing sigil burned into her collarbone that matched nothing in the index of known marks, and Sera had pulled the record because pulling records was what she did, and the record had led her to a charm-seller with grief in his eyes and an old covenant warm under his glove, and since then every certainty she owned had developed hairline fractures that sang when she moved.

Now she stood in Provost Hallow's office while he was at evening prayers—he still attended prayers, though no god answered, which Sera had always found either admirable or obscene—and lifted the false bottom out of the third drawer.

The key was iron, heavy, cold enough to burn. Stamped into its bow was a symbol she recognized from the oldest files: a circle broken by seven strokes, the sigil of the Concordance of Priests, the body that had governed the divine realms' traffic with mortals before the Outlawing made all such traffic contraband. The key had been hidden, not lost. Hidden things were kept by people who intended, someday, to use them.

Hallow knew what was under the chancellery. Hallow had always known. And Hallow let the inquisition above him hunt sigil-bearers through the streets while he kept a key to a vault full of the divine.

She put the key in her pocket and felt it against her hip like a second, colder heart.

* * *

They went down at midnight, when even the night clerks slept, Sera carrying a shuttered lantern and Xanthe carrying the tools of his former trade—picks, wedges, a short pry-bar, and a knife he hoped not to use. The stair behind the blackened door descended farther than any building's foundations should go, spiraling through stone that grew older as they sank, dressed blocks giving way to rough-hewn rock giving way to something older than masonry at all, walls that seemed less carved than grown.

"How deep does it go?" Xanthe asked. His voice came back flattened, swallowed.

"The plans say three levels. The plans also say the third level doesn't exist." Sera held the lantern high. "I've decided to believe the plans about everything except that."

At the bottom of the stair stood a door unlike the others—no wood, no iron, but lead, a whole door cast from lead, dull and lightless, and across its face, in relief, ran words in the old liturgical script. Xanthe couldn't read them. But his hand could. Through the glove, through leather and linen and skin, the covenant mark on his forearm woke like a sleeper hearing its name, and warmed, and *ached* toward the door the way a wound aches toward rain.

"Sera."

"I see your face doing something." She didn't look at him. She was looking at the script. "What is it?"

"My mark. It's—" He pressed his palm flat against the lead. Cold, dead metal, inert as every charm he'd sold for a decade. But under his glove, his skin sang. "It knows this place."

"That's either very good or extremely bad."

"Yes," Xanthe said. "That's been my experience of everything lately."

The lock was a mechanism of seven tumblers arranged in a ring, and the key fit, and turned, and each tumbler fell with a sound like a struck bell heard underwater. The lead door swung inward on hinges that made no sound at all, which was somehow worse than screaming.

Lamplight spilled into darkness, and the darkness gave it back.

Shelves. Ranks upon ranks of them, cut into the living rock, and on every shelf, rows upon rows of tablets—lead, each one the size of a prayer-book, each one stamped with script that glimmered faintly, not with light but with *attention*, the way a cat's eyes hold the lamplight. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. The air itself tasted charged, like the moment before lightning, like the held breath of a congregation.

"Names," Sera breathed. "These are—"

"Fragments of names." Xanthe stepped over the threshold and immediately regretted and rejoiced in it. Every tablet hummed at some frequency below hearing; he felt them in his teeth, in the scar tissue of old battles, in the mark on his arm. "The Concordance didn't trust the gods with their whole names. A name is a handle. A name is *power*. So they broke the great names up, scattered the pieces, kept them in the dark where no one—no priest, no king, no god—could speak any of them whole." He turned slowly, taking it in. "This isn't a treasury. This is a hostage vault."

"They took hostages from heaven," Sera said. There was wonder in it, and horror, and she could not tell which was winning. Eleven years of catechism lay in pieces around her feet. *The gods are dead or worse.* Here was proof they had been neither dead nor worse but *managed*, corralled, their true selves locked in lead while their churchly shadows performed miracles upstairs. No wonder the Chancellery had outlawed magic. No wonder the Silence had been welcomed with such institutional relief. The whole apparatus of the inquisition was built atop this room like a lid on a well.

She should report it. Every instinct of her training screamed it. Instead she lifted her lantern higher and began, methodically, treasonously, to read shelf labels.

* * *

Xanthe walked the aisles like a man walking through a dream of home.

He did not know how he knew what he knew. The knowledge simply arrived, the way the mark's warmth arrived: Ulyana's covenant had always carried a little of her memory in it, and now, in this place saturated with divine fragments, that sediment was stirring. He passed tablets that hummed in registers he could feel but not name—the deep brown note of a harvest god, the thin silver shriek of something that had governed borders and boundaries, a low pulsing thing that made him think of drowned bells and he did not linger near it.

Then he reached the seventh aisle, and the mark on his arm went silent.

Not quiet. *Silent*—the way a room goes silent when someone you love enters it. He stopped. Turned. On the shelf at the height of his heart, set apart from its neighbors by a hand's width of empty space on either side, lay a single tablet smaller than the rest, worn smooth at the edges as if by centuries of handling.

It bore no label. It bore only a single line of script, and the script was not stamped but *cut*, cut deep and desperate, and Xanthe knew the hand. He had seen it a thousand times, inked in the margins of his old covenant-contract, traced in blessing over the heads of soldiers going into battle, written in the frost of a morning window once, playfully, when she had still been playful.

Ulyana's hand.

His fingers shook as he drew off his glove. The mark stood bare in the lamplight—the sunburst-and-crescent of Ulyana's covenant, faded to a ghost-gray after ten years of silence, warm but dim, a hearth banked to embers. He reached for the tablet.

The moment his skin met the lead, the shard *sang*.

Not aloud. In the blood. A single note rose through his arm, through his chest, through the hollow places the Silence had hollowed out of him, and the ghost-gray mark flushed gold at its edges like coals breathed upon, and for one impossible heartbeat Xanthe Galloway stood in a vault under Vask and simultaneously stood somewhere else entirely: a green country under a wide sky, wind moving through wheat, and a voice—

"—*and if I do not answer, do not look for me*—"

The vision snapped shut. Xanthe staggered, caught himself on the shelf, and the tablet came away in his hand, light as a wafer, humming against his palm like a captured bird.

"Xanthe." Sera was beside him, lantern thrust forward, knife drawn—when had she drawn it? Her face was pale and set. "Your arm. Your arm is *glowing*. We need to leave, now, because if I can see it from across the room, then whatever watches for that kind of light can see it from across the city—"

"It's her." His voice came out wrecked. He cleared his throat and tried again. "It's a piece of her name. Her actual name, Sera. Not the title, not the cult-name, not the word we prayed with. The real one. Do you understand? They cut it apart and locked it in the dark, and she *let* them, and this piece has been down here waiting for ten years just like me, and it—" He stopped, because the next word was *missed*, and he could not say it without breaking.

Sera looked at him—at the mercenary who sold hollow charms, who had knelt in a dye-house and sworn to break a goddess's final instruction—and something in her chest, the part the catechism had never successfully colonized, ached in recognition. She knew what it was to serve something that didn't deserve it and love it anyway. She'd done it with the Chancellery for eleven years.

"Then we take it," she said, "and we go, and you fall apart later, on your own time." She sheathed the knife and swept the lantern along the shelves, cataloguing with a clerk's speed. "There are others here that resonate with yours—I can feel it, a pull, like—like harmonics. If one shard of her name was stored here, the Concordance wouldn't have kept the rest in one place. They'd have scattered them. Drowned chapels. Border shrines. Places the inquisition never bothered to inventory because the inventory itself was heresy."

"You've already worked out the pattern."

"I told you. I'm good at my job." She paused at the end of the aisle, looking back at the vast dark ranks of sleeping names, and her voice dropped. "Xanthe. When you touched it. What did you see?"

He closed his hand around the shard. Warmth pulsed against his palm, patient, alive, *hers*.

"Wheat," he said. "Wind. And the last thing she ever said to me." He turned toward the stair, toward the long climb back up into the lying city. "Come on. We have a name to finish reassembling—and I think we just told the whole underworld where we are."

Behind them, as they climbed, the vault of names settled back into its long silence—but one small lead tablet, borne upward in a thief's scarred hand, went on singing, low and gold and unmistakably alive, like a heart remembered beating in a house everyone had mourned as empty.
