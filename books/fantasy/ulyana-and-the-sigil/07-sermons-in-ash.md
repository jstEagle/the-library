## 7. Sermons in Ash

The inquisition's files called it the Ashen Congregation, which was a lie of paperwork the way most things in Vask were lies of paperwork. There was nothing ashen about the gathering that filled the old tannery hall on Corvane Street on the third night of the month. The place had been scrubbed to its bones—Sera could still smell the ghost of lime and cured hide beneath the incense—and every surface had been whitewashed until the brickwork glowed like bone in the light of a hundred candles. Someone had spent money here. Someone with deep pockets and a taste for purity.

Sera stood at the back, hood up, hands folded inside her sleeves the way a clerk stood when she had nowhere better to be. Her inquisitor's badge was left behind in a drawer under three layers of requisition forms; her knife was not. Old habits. The mark on her inner forearm sat quiet beneath her cuff, warm as a stone kept near a fire.

She told herself she was here for the record. That was the phrase she used, even in her own head: *for the record*. Inquisitor Drevna wanted names, numbers, points of entry into the congregation's hierarchy. Sera was good at names and numbers. She had been good at them since she was eleven years old and an inquisitor's clerk pulled her out of a debtors' row because she could read faster than any child he'd ever seen. The inquisition had given her ink, bread, a bed above the archive, and a purpose that fit her like a well-made glove: find what is false, name it, burn it out.

The trouble—the trouble she did not say aloud, not even in her own head, not yet—was the warmth in her arm, and the fact that she had stopped reporting it.

* * *

The preacher came out at the ninth bell, and the crowd's hush came before him, rolling backward through the hall like water parting.

He was younger than Sera expected. She had built him from rumor: fat, gold-ringed, sweating charisma from every pore. Instead he was narrow and gray-eyed, with the calloused hands of a man who had worked rope or rigging, and he wore no vestments at all—just undyed wool, belted with cord. He looked, absurdly, like a fisherman who had wandered in from the docks and found himself a congregation.

"Brothers," he said. "Sisters." His voice carried without effort, without ornament. "You came here carrying things. I can see them from where I stand. Some of you carry grief. Some carry sickness. Some carry the worst one—" he paused, and let the silence do its work, and Sera felt the whole room lean toward him like grass toward sun, "—the ones you carry are questions."

A murmur ran through the crowd, low and involuntary, the sound of people being seen.

"The old gods went silent," the preacher said. "Ten years of silence. And in that silence, the inquisition taught you this: that asking is dangerous. That hoping is contraband. That if you kneel and no one answers, the fault is yours—for asking wrong, or asking at all." He shook his head slowly, and there was real sorrow in it, or something so close to sorrow that Sera could not find the seam. "I am here to tell you the fault was never yours. You were praying into a locked house. Tonight, the door is open."

He raised his hand, palm out, and Sera saw the sigil.

It was inked—or burned, she couldn't tell at distance—into his palm: a circle broken by a descending line, crossed twice by short bars. She knew it intimately now. It lived on her arm. In the archive it was filed under *unclassified devotional marks*, cross-referenced nowhere, because the archivists had never seen it before this season and hated admitting ignorance.

It looked almost like Ulyana's sigil. Almost. The way a forged coin looks almost like currency, if you don't hold it to the light.

"Come," the preacher said simply. "Who will come first?"

* * *

They came first with a boy.

Sera watched from the edge as a woman dragged her son forward—a child of perhaps eight, legs twisted, feet dragging in the manner of the birth-palsy that Vask's charity surgeons charged a season's wages to fail at correcting. The mother's face had the terrible practicality of someone who has exhausted every respectable option and arrived at faith the way a drowning swimmer arrives at driftwood.

The preacher knelt. That was the first thing that snagged in Sera's clerk's mind, the detail she would later turn over at night: he knelt. Not grandly, not as theater. He knelt to be level with the boy, and he spoke to the child first, before the mother, before the crowd.

"What's your name?"

"Tomas," the boy said.

"Tomas. This may feel strange. Like warm water. If it frightens you, squeeze my hand and we stop. Agreed?"

The boy nodded. The preacher took both the small hands in his calloused ones, bowed his head, and said—not a word of liturgy, not a syllable of the old rites Sera had memorized from confiscated texts—simply: *"Come through."*

The warmth hit the hall like an opened oven door. Sera felt it on her face, in her teeth, and felt—she could not deny it, standing there in the press of bodies—the answering heat bloom along her forearm, under the cuff, under the skin. Her mark waking like a held breath released.

And the boy straightened.

Not all at once. Not a miracle in the shape the ballads promised. His spine uncurled by degrees, his ankles rolled, his heels came down flat on the floorboards for what his mother's sobbing made clear they never had. He took a step. Then another. Then he laughed—a child's laugh, startled out of him—and walked the length of the hall while five hundred people wept, and Sera stood among them with wet eyes and a burning arm and the cold little clerk at the back of her skull whispering: *the energy has to come from somewhere. Nothing heals for free. Where is it drawn from? What pays?*

But the whisper was getting quieter every time. That was the thing she could not put in a report. The whisper was getting quieter, and the warmth was getting louder, and somewhere between the two of them was a woman named Sera who was running out of ground to stand on.

* * *

After the healing came the teaching, and after the teaching came the ask.

The preacher did not call it an ask. He called it *coming home*, and he laid it out with the simplicity of a man describing weather. The counterfeit gods—no, he didn't use that word, he used *the hollow ones*, the dead names, the silent houses—had abandoned the world, he said. What remained was what remained tonight: presence, answer, warmth. All it required was everything. Not tithes—he waved that away with genuine contempt, and Sera believed the contempt, which frightened her more than anything else that night. Not obedience to men. Surrender to the voice. Give over your questions. Give over your watching, your weighing, your endless suspicious arithmetic. Be certain, and be done.

"You have spent ten years being careful," he said, and his gray eyes moved across the crowd and Sera would have sworn, would have staked her clerk's salary, that they rested on her for half a heartbeat longer than on anyone else. "Careful is just fear wearing a clerk's coat. Put it down. No one in this room will ever ask you to prove yourself again. You are already known. You are already answered. Isn't that what you knelt in the dark wanting, all these years? To be answered?"

Around her, the crowd breathed as one body. A woman beside Sera whispered *yes* with tears streaming openly down her face. An old dockhand gripped his cap against his chest like a man at a funeral. And Sera—

Sera stood very still and examined herself the way she examined a document suspected of forgery, looking for the tell, the flaw, the place where the ink sat wrong.

Here was the truth she found, and it appalled her: she wanted it.

Not the god. She was too well-trained for the god, too full of Drevna's catechisms about predatory divinity, too aware that whatever wore that almost-sigil had healed a child using power that came from somewhere. But the belonging—that was another matter. Ten years in the archive, ten years of being useful and invisible, ten years of watching inquisitors take credit for her research and superiors take credit for their work, ten years of a purpose that fit like a glove but had never once been asked whether the hand inside it was comfortable. And here was a room where a fisherman-preacher knelt to children, where nobody checked credentials, where the only price was certainty—and certainty sounded, at the ninth bell of a long life spent doubting, like rest.

Her mark warmed again, gently, approvingly, like a hand on the shoulder saying *yes, this one, she understands.*

That was what broke the spell. Not the cold clerk. The warmth itself—the presumption of it. The thing on her arm *approving* of her, the way an owner approves of a dog that sits when told. She had spent her whole life earning her place, and some stitched-together hunger wearing a goddess's stolen face thought it could simply grant her belonging like alms, and have her gratitude forever.

She slipped out during the final hymn.

* * *

The night air off the Graywater was cold enough to hurt, and she was grateful for it. She walked the long way back to the archive, past the shuttered wardmarket with its scorch-marks from the raid, past the magistrate's court where tomorrow Odile the fence would be sentenced—Sera had read the docket; it was her job to read dockets—and past a shuttered charm-stall where, witnesses said, a man sold prayers that didn't work and performed one anyway, once, quietly, for a child.

The charm-seller. The witness descriptions were thin: gloved hands, tired eyes, a voice that stayed low. Drevna wanted him found. Every marked person in Vask was to be found, catalogued, and eventually—Drevna had not said the word aloud, but Sera had read the requisitions for oil and kindling—eventually burned.

Sera flexed her right hand inside her sleeve, where her own mark lay quiet now, sullen at having been denied its congregation.

She should report the sermon. She would report the sermon—the numbers, the layout, the boy's healing, the preacher's face. That much was honest work. But she caught herself, halfway across the Archive Bridge, drafting the sentence in her head: *the congregation numbered approximately four hundred, of whom the following appear to bear the sigil—*

And she realized she had memorized every marked face in that hall. Forty-one of them. Names where she could get them. Mothers, dockhands, a lamplighter, two of the widow-women from Tanner's Row.

Forty-one entries for the purge lists.

She stopped in the middle of the bridge. Below her the Graywater moved black and patient, carrying the city's reflections apart and putting them back together wrong.

If she wrote the list, forty-one people died. If she didn't, Drevna would send someone else, someone who hadn't stood in that crowd and felt the terrible human warmth of it, someone who would write the list with relish. And if she reported her own hesitation—if she so much as hesitated visibly—there were requisitions for oil and kindling with her own name a plausible line-item, because the inquisition had begun, quietly, without announcement, to burn its own clerks when the clerks started asking whether the fire was righteous.

The mark on her arm stirred, faintly, and Sera understood with sudden clarity that it had heard her thoughts—all of them, always, since the day it appeared—and that somewhere across the city, a gray-eyed fisherman-preacher knew exactly what his clerk-spy had decided tonight.

She turned from the rail. She walked on. And she began, deliberately, methodically, the way she did everything—to compose in her head a list of forty-one names that she intended, whatever it cost her, never to deliver.
