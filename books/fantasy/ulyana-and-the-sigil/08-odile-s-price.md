## 8. Odile's Price

The Magistrate's Court of the Third Ward smelled of tallow and wet stone, a smell Xanthe had learned to hate in the way one hates only things that are familiar. Ten years of the Silence had taught Vask to smell like this everywhere—candles burned down to stubs because no one trusted them to mean anything anymore, stone gone damp because the old wards that had kept the cellars dry were chalked over with the inquisition's gray sigils of denial.

He waited on the bench outside Magistrate Corvel's chambers for two hours while a clerk with ink-stained cuffs pretended not to recognize him. That was part of it too. Everything in Vask was theater now: the waiting, the pretending, the careful emptiness of every pocket and sleeve. You performed your harmlessness until they believed you, or until they didn't, and either way the performance cost something you couldn't name.

Odile had been in the Third Ward cells for nine days.

Nine days since the raid on the wardmarket, since the candles had flared all at once like a held breath finally released, since he'd watched her pushed into a black wagon with a reliquary box chained to her wrists—a box she'd been holding for him, because that was what fences did, they held things, and Odile had never once in eleven years of friendship held anything carelessly.

He turned his glove over in his lap. Beneath the kid leather, his covenant mark lay warm against the back of his hand, warmer than it had been in a decade, warm as a coal raked over with ash that refused to die. Since the night the candles flared, it hadn't cooled once. He didn't know what to do with that warmth. It was like carrying someone else's heartbeat under your skin.

"Charm-seller." The clerk's voice was flat. "Corvel will see you now."

* * *

Magistrate Corvel received him in a room designed to make visitors feel like supplicants at an altar—high windows barred with iron filigree, a desk of black oak carved with scales whose pans had been deliberately unbalanced by the carver, so that justice in Vask sat visibly, permanently askew. Corvel himself was a soft-bodied man with a scholar's hands and a moneylender's eyes, and he poured two cups of wine without asking whether Xanthe drank.

"You've come about the fence," Corvel said. "Odile Vasquez. Possession of a class-one relic. You know what that carries."

"I know what it carries."

"Deportation to the salt quarries. Or"—he let the word hang, fat and patient—"we might discuss alternatives."

Xanthe took the wine because refusing it would be a statement, and statements were expensive. "Name your price."

"So direct." Corvel smiled and settled back. "I admire that. Most people who come to this office want to pretend we're doing something other than commerce. But you sell charms, Master Galloway. You understand trade."

*My inert charms,* Xanthe thought. *You understand nothing.* He kept his face still. Ten years of selling hollow glass and tin to desperate people had given him a face like a locked door.

"The relic she carried," Corvel said. "Where did it come from?"

"It came from nowhere. She found it in a gutter."

"She found a pre-Outlawing reliquary bearing the seal of the Order of the Quiet Hand in a gutter." Corvel's smile didn't move but his eyes did, sliding toward the window, toward the city beyond. "You see how that strains belief."

"Vask is a strange city."

"Vask is changing." For the first time something came into the magistrate's voice that wasn't bureaucratic boredom—something quick and hungry, a dog hearing its name. "Have you noticed? People pray now. Not the way they used to, not the empty mouthings the inquisition permits at the state altars. Real prayer. And it gets answered, Galloway. I have seen a child's crooked spine straighten in front of three hundred witnesses. I have seen tumors shrink like snow in spring. Something is listening again."

Xanthe's mouth went dry. He drank wine to cover it. "I sell charms," he said. "I don't follow theology."

"No. But you follow *her*." Corvel said it lightly, almost kindly, and it landed like a knife between ribs. "Everyone in the wardmarket knows the charm-seller who kept Ulyana's covenant after the Silence. Who wouldn't recant when the inquisition offered amnesty. Who wears a glove on his right hand even in summer." He spread his soft hands. "I'm not your enemy. I'm a man who recognizes value. And you, Master Galloway, are sitting on the most valuable thing in this city."

"Which is?"

"Access." Corvel leaned forward. "Whatever is answering prayers—it isn't her. Oh, it wears her colors, it speaks in something like her voice, but I have read the old trial records, the ones from before the Outlawing, and I know what Ulyana's miracles looked like. They were small. A fever broken. A debt forgiven. A door unlocked. This new thing does *spectacles*. Gods who love you don't perform for crowds." His eyes glittered. "But you knew that already. You've seen the marks it leaves."

The room seemed to tilt very slightly. Xanthe set down his cup with exaggerated care. "I don't know what you mean."

"The widow Maren. Third bell district. Healed of a wasting sickness last week. She came to file a complaint yesterday—not about the healing, about the *scar*. A sigil burned into her skin that matches no registry, no order, no god in the sanctioned lists." Corvel studied him. "She said a charm-seller told her it was nothing. She remembered his face."

*I told her it was nothing,* Xanthe thought. *I lied to her because the truth would have gotten her purged.* And now the lie had walked into the Third Ward court and sat down across from him wearing a magistrate's robes.

"What do you want?" he asked again, and this time his voice came out rougher than he intended.

"Two things. First: the source of the relic Odile carried. Second—" Corvel reached beneath his desk and drew out a lead tablet, no longer than a hand, wrapped in oilcloth that had been peeled back at one corner. Even through the wrapping, even across the desk, Xanthe felt it. A hum. A resonance in his own mark, faint as a plucked string heard through a wall.

His blood went cold and hot at once.

"Where did you get that?"

"That is my second question answered by my first condition." Corvel rewrapped it slowly, savoring. "There are more of these in the world, Galloway. Fragments. The inquisition has been confiscating them for years without understanding what they hold—I understood before they did. And whatever is answering prayers wants them. Desperately. Do you know what that means?" He smiled again, and this time there was nothing soft in it at all. "It means I am holding something a god will pay for. And you—you are going to help me negotiate."

* * *

They talked for another hour. It had the texture of every bad bargain Xanthe had ever made: the slow erosion, the concessions dressed as gifts, the sense of walking forward step by step into deeper water while telling yourself you could still touch bottom.

Corvel wanted the shards. All of them, or as many as could be found. He wanted Xanthe's knowledge of the old orders, the sealed places, the priest-craft of the pre-Outlawing years—and in exchange, Odile would walk free, her record cleansed, the reliquary charge dissolved. And the lead tablet on the desk would remain in Corvel's possession, which meant, though neither man said it aloud, that Corvel would eventually offer it to the thing that answered prayers, and would expect to be paid in miracles and rank.

"You serve it already," Xanthe said quietly, near the end. "Don't you. Whatever it is. You've prayed to it."

Corvel didn't deny it. He rolled up his sleeve instead—an act so obscene in its casualness that Xanthe's breath caught—and showed the inside of his forearm, where a sigil sat burned into the flesh in lines of silvered scar. It was shaped like Ulyana's mark the way a mask is shaped like a face. Close enough to deceive. Wrong in every proportion, the curves too eager, the terminals hooked like talons.

"It healed my daughter," Corvel said, and for one moment the moneylender fell away and there was only a father, tired and grateful and lost. "Eleven years sick. Doctors, priests, nothing. Then the sermons started in Ashmarket, and I went because I'd run out of pride, and the preacher touched her forehead and she *stood up*, Galloway. She stood up and asked for supper." He pulled the sleeve down. "You can keep your silent goddess. Silence is just abandonment with better manners."

"She had reasons."

"Then she can explain them to the people who starved waiting." Corvel rang a small bell on his desk. "We're finished. The fence goes free tonight. You have until the next moon to bring me word of the other fragments—or I reopen her case, and this time I add conspiracy. You understand me?"

"I understand you."

"One more thing." Corvel's voice dropped, almost gentle. "When you find them—and you will, because men like you always dig where they shouldn't—remember that everything you uncover, you uncover for me. The thing in the cathedral knows my name. It would be a pity for yours to reach its ears in the wrong context."

Xanthe stood. At the door he paused, hand on the frame, and asked the question that had been sitting in his chest since the sleeve came up. "Does it hurt? The mark."

Corvel looked at him for a long moment. "No," he said. "That's the worst part. It feels like being loved."

* * *

They released Odile at dusk, through the prisoners' gate on Tanner's Row, and she came out blinking in the last light like something exhumed. Nine days in a Third Ward cell had grayed her—she was a woman built of angles and sharp humor, and both had been blunted—but her eyes found Xanthe in the small crowd of gawkers immediately, and what was in them made his chest ache: relief first, and then fury, arriving together like weather.

"You idiot," she said, when they'd put enough street between themselves and the gate. "You absolute, sentimental idiot. What did you pay?"

"Nothing I'll miss."

"Liar." She stopped under a lamp that some brave soul had lit despite the curfew on open flame, and gripped his arm hard enough to bruise. "Corvel doesn't release anyone. Corvel *trades*. What did he take?"

Xanthe hesitated. Eleven years of friendship deserved better than a lie, but the truth was a blade he didn't know how to hand over safely. "He wanted information. About the shards."

Odile went very still. In the lamplight he could see the new lines around her eyes, the ones nine days had carved. "And you told him."

"I told him enough to buy you."

"Enough." She laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a bitter arithmetic. "Xanthe. Listen to me. Listen carefully, because I've spent nine days in a cell with nothing to do but think, and I've thought through everything, and here is what I know." Her grip tightened. "The raid wasn't chance. The inquisition hit the wardmarket on the one night I was holding that reliquary—the one night in three years. Someone sold us. And the person who sold us wanted that box in their hands, and wanted *you* watching it happen."

"Who?"

"I don't know. But think. Everyone who knew what I was carrying for you—everyone—is either dead, arrested, or works for someone new." She released his arm and stepped back, rubbing her wrists where the chains had marked them. "And now Corvel tells you there are more fragments, and sends you out to hunt them, and you're thinking you'll hunt them first and use them for *her*, and he's letting you think it, because—"

"Because the shards are bait," Xanthe said.

The words came out of him flat and cold, and once spoken, they arranged the whole terrible shape of the last nine days into clarity. The widow's scar. Corvel's tablet. The thing in the cathedral that wore Ulyana's sigil and grew fat on answered prayer. If it hungered for the fragments of her true name—if those pieces were the one thing that could complete it or destroy it—then every path leading to a shard was a path someone else had cleared and salted and left open, wide as an invitation.

"They're not just bait," Odile said softly. "They're a trail. You're not hunting them, love. They're hunting *you*. Every shard you touch, you announce yourself—to Corvel, to the inquisition, to whatever sits in that cathedral wearing her face. You're a lantern walking through a dark field, and everything with teeth is following the light."

Xanthe looked down at his gloved hand. Under the leather, the mark pulsed its stubborn, faithful warmth, and for the first time in ten years he wondered whether devotion and foolishness had always been the same gesture, distinguished only by outcome.

"Then I stop being a lantern," he said.

"How?"

He thought of the undercroft beneath the old scriptorium, of the archival clues Sera—the inquisitor's clerk with the frightened, hungry eyes—had helped him find. Of the drowned chapel south of the river. Of the words Ulyana had left him a decade ago, the instruction he had already decided, alone in the dark, to break.

"If I do not answer," she had said, "do not look for me."

"I go looking anyway," he said. "But not where they expect. Not along their road." He met Odile's eyes. "Can you disappear? Properly, this time? No fences, no favors, no one who knows?"

Odile studied him for a long moment, and then something in her sharp face softened—not into kindness exactly, but into the particular tenderness of a woman watching a friend walk toward a cliff and deciding not to waste breath on warnings he'd already heard.

"There's a barge master on the canal," she said. "Owes me more than he'd like anyone to know. I can be smoke by morning." She hesitated. "Xanthe. Whatever you find down there—whatever she did, whatever she didn't do—remember that you kept faith for ten years with no evidence at all. Don't throw that away because the evidence finally arrived and it's ugly."

"And if the evidence says she abandoned us on purpose?"

"Then," Odile said, turning up her collar against the river wind, "you'll finally be free to forgive her or hate her, instead of just missing her. Either one is a kind of rest." She touched his cheek, brief and rough, the closest thing to a blessing she'd ever offered. "Don't die stupid, charm-seller. I've grown accustomed to you."

Then she was gone, into the lamplit maze of Tanner's Row, and Xanthe stood alone with the cold coming off the water and the bait laid out before him like a road.

South of the river, in a chapel the flood had taken, a second fragment waited under guard. He could feel it the way you feel a tooth you haven't touched yet—anticipated, inevitable.

He turned his collar up and began to walk.
