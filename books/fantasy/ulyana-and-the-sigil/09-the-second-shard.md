## 9. The Second Shard

The road to the drowned chapel was not on any map Xanthe had ever bought, stolen, or drawn himself. It existed only in the margins of a dead priest's ledger—a scrap of vellum Odile had pressed into his hands through the bars of a holding cell three nights ago, along with a warning he was still trying to shake loose from his bones.

*The shards are bait,* she'd said. *Whatever left them there wants to be found.*

He went anyway. That was the shape of him now, he supposed: a man who walked toward the thing that wanted him, because the alternative was standing still while something wore his goddess's face and fed on her faithful.

Three days south of Vask, the lowlands began. The land flattened into marsh and memory—drowned orchards with their black branches combing the fog, fence posts standing in water like the ribs of some enormous patient animal. The air smelled of rot and iron and, beneath it all, faintly, impossibly, of incense.

Xanthe followed the smell.

His covenant mark had been warm since morning. It lived under his glove on the back of his left hand, a small silvered scar in the shape of Ulyana's sigil—the crescent cradling a flame—and for ten years it had been nothing but a scar, warm as old ash, a coal that refused to go fully out. Now it pulsed like a second heartbeat, quickening as he waded deeper into the fen, and he found himself pressing it against his thigh through the cloth, half to steady it, half to hide it from whatever might be watching.

*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

Her last words. He'd carried them like a stone in his chest for a decade, turning them over until they were smooth, telling himself they meant *I am gone, grieve and live.* He was beginning to suspect they meant something else entirely. Something she'd been afraid he'd find.

The chapel appeared out of the fog the way drowned things do—gradually, then all at once. First the bell tower's crown, tilted like a drunkard's hat, breaking the gray surface of a wide black mere. Then the roofline, submerged to its eaves. The water was glass-still, and in it the chapel hung upside down, whole and perfect, so that for one vertiginous moment Xanthe saw two chapels—one drowning, one preserved—and could not say which was the true one.

Steps led down into the water at the chapel's western end, worn limestone descending in clean increments, and here was the miracle or the obscenity of the place: the water did not touch them. Where his boots met the first step, the mere parted—not violently, not with any show, but with the quiet courtesy of a crowd making room. A corridor of dry stone descended into the dark, walls of water standing on either side of him, tall as cathedral windows, fish drifting past his shoulder like thoughts he couldn't quite catch.

"Ten years," Xanthe said aloud, because the silence demanded something be said into it. "Ten years you couldn't spare a candle, and you kept *this* dry."

The mark on his hand flared hot enough to sting. He chose to read it as an apology. He was no longer sure he should.

* * *

Inside, the chapel was breathing.

That was the only word for it. The flooded nave rose around him in columns of green-black water, and the whole chamber moved with a slow tidal rhythm—inhalation, exhalation—as though the building itself had learned lungs in its long drowning. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, a pale underwater luminescence that turned every surface the color of old bruises. Silt drifted in slow galaxies. Above the ruined altar, a great rose window stood intact beneath twenty feet of water, its leaded saints staring down through the flood with expressions Xanthe could not decide were serene or horrified.

And on the altar steps sat a priest.

He had been dead for a long time. There was no polite way around it. His vestments had rotted to lace across the shoulders; his skin had gone the gray-brown of bog oak, stretched close over the architecture of his skull. But he sat upright, hands folded in his lap, and when Xanthe's boot touched the last dry flagstone, the revenant lifted his head with a sound like wet rope tightening.

"Herald." The voice came from somewhere below the jaw, resonant and ruined. "You are late."

Xanthe's hand went to the knife at his belt and stopped there, useless. What did one cut, in a place like this? "I'm not—" He swallowed. "I'm no one's herald."

"You wear her mark." The dead man's eyes had gone milky, but they fixed on Xanthe with terrible precision, and Xanthe understood that this creature did not see with light. It saw with something older. "The crescent and the flame. I sealed this door against all comers. I have turned back looters, inquisitors, and worse things wearing better faces. But the mark knows its own." The skull inclined, courteous, awful. "Kneel, herald, and receive your charge."

"I told you. I'm not—"

"Or stand," the revenant said, with the faintest dry rustle that might once have been a chuckle, "and argue theology with a corpse. I have time. It is nearly all I have."

Xanthe looked at the thing on the altar steps—at the devotion still knotted in its ruined body after however many years of sitting in the dark, keeping a door no one had asked it to keep—and felt something in his chest crack sideways. This was what he would become, if he let himself. This was the far shore of faithfulness without answer.

"What's your name?" he asked.

The question seemed to surprise it. The milky eyes blinked, slow as doors closing. "Brother Anselm," it said at last. "Of the Chapel of the Quiet Flame. We were thirty-one, when the Silence came. I stayed. The others went up to argue with heaven." Its head tilted. "They are in the water, if you wish to argue with them instead."

"No," Xanthe said quietly. "I wish to know what you're guarding."

Anselm unfolded from the altar steps. Up close, the stink of him was deep-water stink, cold and mineral, and beneath it the ghost of incense. He moved down one step, then another, joints creaking like a ship in weather, and gestured with a hand that had lost most of its flesh to the water's long patience.

"The flame," he said, "that was never allowed to go out."

* * *

Beneath the altar, behind a slab that took both of them—the living man pushing, the dead one guiding—took an hour to shift, was a crypt no larger than a modest tomb. And in the center of the crypt, floating a hand's breadth above a basin of black stone, hung a shard of lead no bigger than a thumbnail.

It should have been nothing. A scrap of metal, dull and pitted, the kind of thing Odile wouldn't have paid a copper for. But the water-light bent toward it. The silt drifted in slow spirals around it. And the moment Xanthe crossed the threshold, the shard began to hum—a sound felt in the teeth and the breastbone rather than heard, a tuning fork struck against the inside of the world.

"It sang the day the Silence fell," Anselm said from the doorway, not crossing it. His voice had changed; there was reverence in it now, and grief, braided tight. "Every tablet in every undercroft sang at once, and then went quiet, all across the realm. The names went home, we thought. Back to their gods. Except this one." The skull bowed. "This one stayed. We did not know why. We only knew that a piece of Her name, abandoned in the world, must be guarded until She sent someone for it."

"I'm not who you think I am," Xanthe said. It came out hoarse. "She didn't send me. She hasn't spoken in ten years. To anyone."

"She spoke to you." Anselm's milky gaze dropped to Xanthe's gloved hand. "Once. Enough to leave that. The rest is silence, yes. But silence is also a word, herald. In my order we held that it was the longest word in the liturgy. You have simply not yet heard what it says."

Xanthe reached for the shard.

His fingers had barely closed around the lead when the world folded.

* * *

He was on a battlefield, but not as himself—or not only as himself. He was seeing through a vastness that had no proper edges, looking down at a war he half-remembered from the histories and half-remembered from the nightmares: the last day before the Silence, when the gods still walked openly among their faithful and the sky over the borderlands had been full of wings and fire.

Ulyana stood amid the carnage, and she was radiant, and she was *afraid.*

That was the wrongness of it, the thing that made Xanthe's stomach lurch even inside the vision. He had spent ten years remembering her as she'd been in the covenant-dreams of his youth: warm, wry, incandescent with certainty. This Ulyana was none of those things. This Ulyana was a goddess looking at something in the fabric of the world that only she could see, and what she saw had made her go pale as paper.

Because the other gods were feeding.

He saw it as she saw it, in that terrible clear way visions have. He saw Vashka the Stormfather drinking worship like wine, then demanding it like bread, then taking it like a butcher takes a knife to a throat—faithful by the thousands kneeling themselves into husks, emptied, smiling, *grateful*. He saw the Mother-of-Bees hollow out a kingdom in a single season. He saw divinity revealed for what it had always been threatening to become: appetite. The veil between realms had grown thin with use, thin with love, and through that thinness the gods had begun to eat their worshippers alive, and the worshippers had called it grace.

And Ulyana—his Ulyana, the god of the small flame and the kept promise—stood apart from the feast, sickened, and began to work.

He watched her gather the veil in her hands like a woman gathering a torn net. He watched her pull it closed, stitch by stitch, seal upon seal, and felt the cost of it tear through her like a blade: every prayer unanswered, every candle unlit, every voice that cried out to her in the dark met with nothing, forever, because the alternative was to keep the door open, and through the open door something was already coming through.

Something had gotten past her. He saw it—a sliver, a splinter, a thing of stitched-together divinity squeezing through the narrowing gap like smoke through a closing fist—and he heard her cry out, not in pain but in fury, as her seals snapped shut too late to catch all of it.

Then the battlefield again. Her faithful calling to her. Him—younger, bloodied, screaming her name with the rest.

*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

Not a farewell. A plea. A goddess holding a door shut with both hands, begging the man she loved not to come hammering on it from the other side, not to pry at the seams, not to give the thing beyond any crack to pour itself through.

*Do not look for me* had never meant *do not miss me.* It had meant *my silence is the wall between you and the wolf. Let it stand.*

The vision broke.

* * *

Xanthe came back to himself on the crypt floor, cheek against cold stone, the shard clenched in his fist hard enough to print its edges into his palm. He was shaking. Somewhere above him, Anselm's ruined voice was saying his name—no, saying *herald*, over and over, gentle as a lullaby—and the water-light had gone strange and gold, and the mark on Xanthe's hand was burning like a brand.

She'd chosen it.

That was the wound of it, the thing that went in deeper than any blade he'd ever taken. Ten years he had built his life on the story that she'd been taken—that some catastrophe, some enemy, some failure had silenced her, and his faithfulness was a vigil beside an empty bed, waiting for her to wake. Every cold night in Vask, every charm sold hollow, every prayer he'd whispered into a silence that gave back nothing—he had framed them all as loyalty to a victim.

But she wasn't a victim. She was a surgeon. She had cut the realm off from the divine deliberately, knowing the price, accepting the silence, trusting—*trusting*—her faithful to endure without miracles rather than be eaten by gods grown fat and hungry. And he had spent a decade mistaking her sacrifice for abandonment, and now he had broken her instruction, gathered her name out of hiding, and was carrying it straight toward the one thing she had sealed the door to keep out.

The shards were bait. Odile had been right. Not the counterfeit's bait—hers. Left where a faithful fool would find them, humming, singing, irresistible. Or perhaps both. Perhaps everything in this rotten beautiful world was bait now, and the only choice left was whose hook you preferred to hang from.

"Get up, herald." Anselm's hand—cold, light as driftwood—helped him sit. The dead priest studied him with those milk-blind eyes, and something in the ruined face softened. "You saw it. The sealing."

"She told us not to look for her." Xanthe's voice came out scraped raw. "Ten years ago. Last words. And I—" He laughed, and it was an ugly sound in the little crypt. "I've spent ten years being faithful to a woman I never understood, and now I finally understand her, and it's the worst thing I've ever learned."

"Is it?" Anselm cocked the skull, considering. "In my experience, herald, the worst truths are the ones that turn out to have been kindnesses wearing armor." He nodded at the shard in Xanthe's fist. "She hid her name in pieces so that no hungry thing could swallow it whole. She scattered herself across the world and trusted that love would reassemble her. That is not abandonment. That is a woman leaving a map in the only place she knew you'd eventually look."

"The bottom of a drowned chapel guarded by a corpse."

"Where else?" said Brother Anselm, and this time the dry rustle was unmistakably laughter. "Faith has always been poor real estate."

Xanthe climbed to his feet. The shard sat in his palm, humming its low note, and he made himself look at it properly for the first time—made himself see the fine lines etched into the lead, older than any alphabet he knew, a fragment of a name that had once been spoken whole between a god and her people. Two shards now. The undercroft fragment warm against his chest, this one burning in his hand. Somewhere out there, more. And somewhere closer, much closer, a stitched thing wearing her sigil, growing fatter every day on prayers answered in her name.

He thought of the widow in Vask, the sigil burned into her skin that was almost right and wholly wrong. He thought of Odile in her cell, saying *it's bait* with the flat certainty of a woman who had fenced stolen goods long enough to smell a trap. He thought of Ulyana on the battlefield, pulling the veil shut, tears cutting through the radiance of her face.

"If I take this," he said to Anselm, "your vigil ends. Thirty-one years of sitting in the dark, finished because a charm-seller wandered in."

"Not finished." The revenant rose, joints groaning, and pressed both ruined hands briefly over Xanthe's closed fist, a benediction in bog-oak and bone. "Continued. Elsewhere. By you." The milky eyes held his. "Tell Her, when you find the pieces and put them back together—tell Her Brother Anselm kept the flame. Tell Her it never went out."

"I'll tell her," Xanthe said, and found, to his surprise, that he believed he would. That somewhere ahead of him, past the counterfeit and the cathedrals and the thing behind the seal, there was a conversation waiting, and he intended to have it—even if he had to shout every word of it at a locked door.

He climbed the altar steps. Behind him, Anselm settled back onto the stone where he had sat for thirty years, folding his hands, resuming his watch over an empty basin, content.

At the top of the nave, Xanthe paused beneath the rose window, the drowned saints staring down through twenty feet of green water, and the shard in his fist pulsed once—twice—and then, distinctly, in a rhythm he knew better than his own heartbeat, it tapped out a pattern against his palm.

Two short. One long. Pause.

The old field-signal. The one Ulyana's mercenaries had used, a lifetime ago, to mean *come quickly—I've found something.*

Someone else knew where the shards were. Someone else was finding them faster than he was.

Xanthe took the dry stair up out of the chapel two steps at a time, and behind him the waters of the mere closed over the bell tower with a sound almost exactly like a door locking.
