## 10. Heresy of the Heart

The summons came at the hour when the inquisition's tower smelled most of ink and cold stone, and Sera knew before she climbed the stairs that it was over.

She had known for three days, actually, ever since she'd caught Magister Corvane watching her hands during morning devotions. Not her face. Not her ledgers. Her hands—specifically the left one, which she had taken to keeping curled loosely at her side like a woman nursing an old injury. The mark sat beneath her glove now as it had for six weeks, warm as a held coin, and she had told herself a hundred times that no one could see through leather and wool. But Corvane had spent thirty years reading people the way other men read scripture, and he had watched her hands, and he had smiled.

Now she stood in his study while rain wrote its long gray sentences down the window behind him, and he did not ask her to sit.

"Show me," he said.

"Magister?"

"You heard me." He was not unkind. That was the worst part. Corvane was never unkind; cruelty required passion, and he had none left. He was a man who had been hollowed out by the work and filled back up with procedure. "You may pretend for my benefit if it helps you keep your dignity, but I would rather we skip to the part where you hate me honestly."

Sera's heart was a fist against her ribs. She thought about running—not from the room, but from the truth, denying everything, demanding evidence, invoking the protections of rank. She had drafted enough interrogations to know exactly how those words would sound: rehearsed. Guilty. And underneath all of it, quieter than any argument she could make, was the simple fact that she was tired. Six weeks of hiding a warmth in her palm. Six weeks of flinching every time someone brushed her hand. Six weeks of praying to a god she'd been ordered to hunt, and feeling *answered*.

She pulled off the glove.

The sigil lay on her skin like something grown there rather than drawn—a spiral of fine lines with a barbed center, elegant and wrong, the way a beautiful flower is wrong when you find it blooming out of a corpse. It pulsed faintly as Corvane leaned close, and Sera hated that it pulsed. Hated that some part of her wanted him to see it and be impressed.

Corvane studied it for a long moment. Then he sighed, the sigh of a man closing a ledger on a debt he'd always known would come due, and crossed to his cabinet.

"I found the first one in a fishmonger's wife in the Saltrow district," he said, opening a drawer. "Marked on the shoulder. She wept when I asked her about it. Said the pain in her hip was gone for the first time in nine years and she didn't care what put it there." He took out a small iron brazier, no larger than a soup bowl, and set it on the desk between them. "I burned it off myself. Took four passes. She thanked me after."

Sera said nothing. Her mark warmed against the open air, as if listening.

"The second was a choirboy. Fourteen." Corvane produced a stoppered vial of something dark and oily and uncorked it; the smell that rose off it was sharp and mineral, like lightning trapped in oil. "He didn't thank me. He screamed, and then he stopped believing anything I said ever again, and I have thought about his face more often than I would like." He poured the oil into the brazier. "You were the best clerk I ever had, Sera. You are precise where others are thorough, and thorough where others are merely diligent. You notice things. You noticed the charm-seller before anyone else did—you tracked him across half of Vask on witness descriptions alone."

"He hasn't done anything," Sera said. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "The witnesses say he sold charms that never worked. He's a fraud, not a heretic."

"He bears a sigil. Three separate witnesses described a gloved hand and a man who prayed like it cost him something." Corvane struck a match and touched it to the oil. Blue flame stood up in the brazier, silent and hungry. "And so do you. Which brings us to your choice, and I will give it to you plainly because you have earned plainness."

He gestured at the flame.

"Burn it. Here, tonight, with my own hand guiding yours so it takes clean. Then you take the examination for junior magister—I have already written your recommendation—and in a year you'll have a desk with a window and clerks of your own to worry about. Or refuse." He looked up at her, and for one unguarded moment she saw something almost human move behind his eyes, something old and tired and possibly even sorry. "Or refuse, and when we take the congregation on Foundry Street next week, you go into the wagons with them. There is no third door, Sera. I checked. I have been checking for weeks, hoping to be wrong about you."

*There is no third door.* She stared at the blue fire and understood, suddenly and completely, that this was the true test—not the burning, but the framing. Corvane believed what he was saying. He genuinely believed the world contained only two doors, and that offering her either one was mercy.

Her mark pulsed again, warmer, and she realized with a lurch that it wasn't warning her. It was *comforting* her. Whatever answered prayers on Foundry Street, whatever wore Ulyana's stolen sigil, it was in this room with her in the only way it could be, pressing warmth against her fear like a hand on a fevered brow.

*Don't,* she thought at it fiercely. *Don't comfort me. You don't get to make this easier.*

The warmth dimmed—but did not leave. It simply waited, patient as banked coals.

"And if I burn it," Sera said slowly, "the healing stays? The widow in Saltrow—her hip still doesn't hurt?"

"That's irrelevant."

"It's entirely relevant." She heard her own voice change, harden, become the voice she used on reluctant witnesses. "You burned a healing out of a woman who couldn't walk without pain, and she thanked you. You burned a fourteen-year-old boy and he learned that the people who claim to protect souls will hurt bodies to do it. And you're standing there telling me the outcome is *irrelevant*, Magister, and you wonder why the congregations keep growing."

"Sera—"

"They come because someone answers them." The words were coming fast now, ten years of questions finally finding their way out. "Ten years the gods were silent. Ten years the inquisition told everyone that silence was virtue, that unanswered prayer was piety, that wanting to be healed was weakness. And the moment something—*anything*—starts answering, they flood to it in thousands. Doesn't that tell you something? Doesn't that tell you what we've been starving them of?"

"What it tells me," Corvane said quietly, "is that people will follow any voice that speaks their name, and that most of those voices are lies. Which is precisely why the inquisition exists." He nudged the brazier an inch toward her. "Choose."

* * *

She chose nothing. That was her first act of rebellion, though she didn't recognize it as such at the time: she simply stood there, looking at the fire, until Corvane's patience—which had been a professional courtesy rather than a personal one—finally ran its course.

"Take the night," he said at last, turning back to the window. "Report to me at dawn with your answer. I'll have the examination papers ready either way." A pause. "Sera. I mean this as kindness, so hear it as such. Whatever warmth that thing gives you—it is a lure. Anglers use light too. What waits at the end of it is not love. It is a mouth."

"Yes, Magister," she said, and let herself be dismissed.

The corridors of the tower swallowed her whole. She walked them on legs that felt borrowed, past the devotional alcoves where junior clerks murmured the litany of vigilance, past the map room where she'd spent four years of her life cataloguing heresies that turned out, mostly, to be lonely people writing letters to dead gods. Her boots knew the way to her cell without her input. Her hands shook only when she made herself look at them.

In her cell she sat on the narrow cot and held her marked palm up to the light of the single permitted candle.

*It is a mouth.*

Maybe. Probably. She had seen the preacher on Foundry Street from the back of a crowd, seen him heal a man's withered arm with a touch and a smile, and she had felt her own mark sing in response like a tuning fork struck by its twin. She knew, intellectually, that the thing wearing Ulyana's face was a counterfeit—Xanthe Galloway's testimony, buried in her files, had been very clear on that point. Similar, wrong. A spiral with a barbed center instead of the true covenant-mark's clean interlocking lines.

But knowing a thing was false did not stop it from being the only voice in ten years that had spoken to her. That was the trap, and she saw its shape perfectly now: not the mark itself, but the loneliness it fed on. The inquisition had given her purpose without belonging. The cult offered belonging without questions. And she had spent her whole life believing those were the only two options—that you chose certainty or you chose doubt, obedience or exile, and that the space between was just empty air.

*No third door.*

She looked at the candle. Then, deliberately, she closed her hand around the flame.

The pain was bright and instant and hers. She held it for three full seconds—long enough to prove to herself that she could choose her own suffering, that her body belonged to her and not to any god or magister—before she opened her fingers and blew out the wick with a breath that shook only a little.

"No," she said aloud, to the dark, to the mark, to Corvane, to the whole architecture of doors. "That's not how I decide things anymore."

Then she got up, and went to steal a map.

* * *

The archive room on the fourth floor kept the master registry: every confirmed sigil-bearer in Vask and the surrounding provinces, inked onto a single great chart of calfskin that had taken her own team eleven months to compile. Names, districts, dates of marking, observed symptoms. It was, functionally, a kill list with cartography, and she had helped make it, and she had told herself at the time that knowledge was neutral, that a map was just a map.

She let herself in with Corvane's own seal, lifted from his desk an hour earlier while he stood at his window composing his kindnesses. The night clerk was asleep at his post, dreaming whatever clerks dreamed. The chart came off its rollers heavier than she expected—the weight, she supposed, of every name.

She spread it once on the archive table, just to fold it properly, and her eye found the entries she'd written herself. *Maren Ostwick, Saltrow, marked 40 days, cooperative.* The fishmonger's wife whose healing Corvane had burned away. *Tomas Bell, age 14, Foundry Street, marked 22 days, uncooperative.* The choirboy. And near the bottom, in her own careful hand: *Galloway, Xanthe — wardmarket district — charm-seller — subject displays genuine covenant affinity, origin unknown — surveillance recommended.*

She had hunted him for weeks. Witness statements, market schedules, the gray coat, the gloved hand. She had been good at it, and being good at it had felt like being good, and somewhere in those weeks she had started having doubts she couldn't file anywhere.

And she remembered the other thing, the thing that had cracked her certainty in the first place: the day she'd watched him at Odile's sentencing, standing at the back of the courtroom with his bare hand pressed flat against his chest as though holding a wound closed, grief coming off him like heat off a stove. No fraud looked like that. No servant of a hungry counterfeit god stood in a courtroom mourning a fence arrested for possessing a relic, looking like a man attending the funeral of his own faith.

She folded the map along its original creases and slid it inside her coat, against her heart, where it sat like a second, colder mark.

The corridor outside was empty. The tower slept. She walked out through the novice's postern, the one gate the night watch always left unbarred because the steps beyond it led nowhere but the midden and the rain-slick alley below, and she did not run until her feet touched street level, and then she ran.

Vask at midnight was a city of shuttered eyes and smoking chimneys, and Sera ran through it with the map against her ribs and her mark burning steady in her palm—not warning, not comforting now, but *guiding*, tugging faintly southward like a compass needle finding north. She should have distrusted that. Part of her did. Every lesson she'd ever learned said the warmth was a leash and the tug was a collar tightening.

But another part of her—the part that had closed its hand around a candleflame and chosen its own pain—understood that a compass could be seized and still pointed true. Whatever the counterfeit wanted, it wanted the marked ones gathered. And the marked ones included a charm-seller who grieved like a widower and carried, if half the old archives were right, a piece of the real answer.

If the false god meant to herd its flock, then she would walk among the sheep. And she would find the shepherd who wasn't lying—or die learning the difference.

She stopped at last on a bridge over the canal, breath ragged, rain beginning again, the tower a black finger against the clouds behind her. Ten years of service, four years of devotion to procedure, one night of treason. She flexed her bare hand and watched the sigil pulse in the dark like a small stubborn star.

"There's a third door," she told the water. "You just have to build it yourself."

Then she turned south, toward the wardmarket, toward a gray-coated charm-seller who didn't yet know she was coming—and behind her, far up in the inquisition tower, a lamp kindled in Corvane's window, early, the way lamps kindle when a man wakes already certain of what he'll find missing.
