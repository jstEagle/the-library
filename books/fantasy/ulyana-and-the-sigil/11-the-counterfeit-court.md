## 11. The Counterfeit Court

The cathedral had not existed a year ago.

Xanthe stood across the boulevard from it in the blue hour before dawn, hood up, breath fogging, and tried to remember what had occupied the plot before. A granary, he thought. Or a tannery that had gone to rot after the Outlawing gutted trade in anything consecrated. Now there was white stone where rot had been, walls that seemed to have grown rather than been built, and a spire like a needle driven through the city's heart into some vein beneath. The stone was wrong in a way he couldn't name at first, and then could: it was too smooth. No chisel marks, no mortar lines, no weathering. It looked like bone that had never learned it was supposed to be outside a body.

They called it the House of Answered Prayer. Of course they did. Vask had always been good at naming things honestly when the names flattered them.

He had watched the place for six days. He knew the shift changes of the gray-robed wardens who patrolled its approaches, knew which doors took pilgrims and which took priests, knew the rhythm of the bells—no, not bells. He'd never identified the source of the sound that rolled out over the district at matins and vespers, a tone more felt than heard, like a finger drawn around the rim of a very large glass. It made dogs lie down. It made old men weep into their sleeves. On his third day of watching, it had made him take three steps toward the doors before he caught himself, his covenant mark burning cold under his glove like a brand pressed to ice.

That was the thing he kept returning to as he crouched now in the shadow of a grain merchant's awning: the pull of it. Ten years of silence had taught him how to live without the voice of his goddess, the way a man learns to walk on a leg that never healed quite right—but he had never stopped feeling the absence. And this place was built inside that absence. It fit the hollow of him exactly, the way a key fits a lock it was copied from.

*That's how you know it's false,* he told himself. *Ulyana never fit easy. She asked.*

But knowing did not stop the ache.

* * *

He went in through the dead-cellar door on the north side, the one Odile's people had told him about—a delivery entrance for wine and lamp oil, guarded by a boy of perhaps fifteen who spent his watch reading a stolen romance by candlelight. Xanthe gave him a sleep-draught in a cup of posset bought off a night-kitchen girl with a smile and two coppers, and lowered the boy gently onto his own stool so no one would think to look for a struggle. Then he was inside, moving through service corridors that smelled of tallow and incense fighting each other for dominance, following the sound of the congregation gathering above.

He had planned to wait for the evening rite, to lose himself among hundreds of bodies while the counterfeit held court. But the corridors themselves were teaching him things, and he was a poor student of nothing.

The walls were warm.

Not sun-warm. Body-warm. He laid his palm flat against the smooth white stone and felt, unmistakably, a pulse. Slow. Deep. Patient as a sleeping ox. The whole building breathed, and the breath moved through it in waves that he realized, with a lurch, matched the cadence of the hymns drifting down from above—the congregation wasn't singing *to* the god. They were keeping time with its heartbeat. They were part of its circulation.

His hand came off the wall as if scalded.

*Keep going,* he told himself. *You came to see it plainly. So see it.*

* * *

He came up through a stair meant for penitents and emerged behind a pillar in the gallery, half-hidden in the shadow of a hanging banner—and stopped breathing.

The nave was vast, and it was full, though the dawn rite was hours off. Thousands of candles burned in tiers along the walls, and every one of them burned without flickering, flames standing straight as needles, all of them leaning—not toward the altar, but *inward*, toward the center of the air above the congregation, as if drawn by a draft that blew from everywhere at once.

And above the altar hung the sigil.

He knew it the way you know your own signature. Two interlocking crescents around a vertical line, the mark Ulyana's faithful had worn for a thousand years, the mark seared into his own forearm beneath a decade of glove-leather. Except this one was forty feet tall, wrought in something that was not gold and not light but a slow-moving substance between the two, and it *moved*. The crescents turned about the line with the unhurried rotation of millstones. The line pulsed.

Below it stood the preacher Xanthe had seen only in woodcut broadsheets: the Serene Voice, they called him, a man whose face was so smooth and kind it looked sculpted by someone working from a description of a face rather than a face itself. He was healing people. There were dozens of them queued in the transept—the lame, the blistered, a child with a cleft lip, an old woman whose eyes had gone white as boiled eggs—and the Voice moved down the line laying two fingers on each brow, and where he touched, flesh remembered what it was supposed to be.

Xanthe watched the cleft lip close. Watched the blind woman's irises bloom back into color, brown and ordinary and miraculous. He watched her fall to her knees and kiss the floor, and he felt the thing he had felt in the wardmarket when every candle flared: wonder first, huge and helpless, and then dread sliding in underneath it like water under a door.

Because he had sold charms for ten years. Hollow ones, inert ones, honest lies. And in ten years he had never once been able to give a blind woman her eyes. Ulyana herself, in all the years of the covenant, had healed sparingly, reluctantly, as if each miracle cost her something she counted carefully. *Faith is not a purse I dip into,* she had told him once, early, when he was young enough to ask such questions. *It is a fire I feed, and I will not burn my faithful for fuel.*

This thing burned nothing. This thing gave and gave and gave, freely, endlessly, the way a spider gives silk.

The queue moved. The candles leaned. The sigil turned its slow millstone turn, and Xanthe understood, finally, what the stone of the walls reminded him of. Fat. The whole cathedral was fat. Grown sleek and heavy on ten years of unanswered prayer suddenly answered, on the desperate gratitude of a city that had been starving for grace and would eat anything set before it.

* * *

He might have slipped away then. He should have. He had seen what he came to see.

But as he turned, the child in the healing queue—the one with the mended lip, waiting with her mother to be led away—looked up. Not at the Voice. At the gallery. At him.

Her eyes were the flat silver of candle-flame reflected in a mirror, and her mouth, new-made and perfect, opened, and she said in a voice that was not a child's voice, that was not any throat's voice, that came from everywhere in the cathedral at once including the pulse in the walls:

"There you are."

The hymn faltered. A thousand heads began to turn.

Xanthe ran.

* * *

He didn't remember choosing a direction. His feet found the penitent stair, the service corridors, the dark, and he ran through the body of the god with its heartbeat slamming around him faster now, much faster, the patient ox waking, the pulse climbing from slowness into something like excitement. Behind him the congregation was singing again—or chanting, or calling, a single word rolling wave after wave down the corridors after him, and the word was his name. Not Xanthe. His other name. The name Ulyana had given him at the sealing of his covenant, the name no living person knew, spoken in a child's silver mouth and a thousand throats and the groan of warming stone:

*"Herald."*

He burst out the dead-cellar door into gray morning air and kept running until the spire was blocks behind him and his lungs were knives, and then he stopped in the mouth of an alley and put his hands on his knees and shook.

Herald. She had called him that once, the last night, the night of the battle at the veil, the night the world went quiet. *My herald,* she had said, and then she had said the other thing, the words he had carried like a stone in his chest for ten years: *If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

And the thing in the cathedral had known the name. Had *used* it. Which meant—

Which meant it had been listening. To him, or to her, or to the place where she had been. It wore her sigil; perhaps it wore her memories too, the way a thief wears a stolen coat, and somewhere in its stitched and fattened divinity was a pocket where her voice was kept.

The thought arrived whole, unbidden, terrible in its tenderness: *I could hear her again.*

He straightened slowly. The alley smelled of cabbage-water and horse. Somewhere a cart rattled past, ordinary as bread. And Xanthe Galloway, mercenary, charm-seller, last faithful servant of a silent goddess, stood in the filth of Vask and admitted to himself the truth he had been outrunning since the widow's burned skin, since the drowned chapel, since the vision that had cracked him open: that if the thing in the white cathedral offered him Ulyana's voice—her actual voice, warm and wry and stern, saying his name the way she used to say it—he did not know, could not know until it was offered, whether he would refuse.

That was the horror. Not the fat stone, not the silver-eyed children, not the candles leaning like a field of wheat before wind. The horror was that homesickness has no doctrine. It does not check credentials. It only wants home.

* * *

It came for him that night, of course. He had expected nothing less; you do not spit in a god's church and get away with silence.

He was in Odile's back room above the pawnshop, counting the shards wrapped in oilcloth against his chest—one humming leaden fragment from the undercroft, one recovered from the drowned chapel, both of them warm as live coals now, warmer every day, answering each other across the cloth like struck tuning forks—when the candle on the table bent. Not guttered. *Bent*, sideways, its flame stretching into a thin needle pointing at the window, and the room filled with the smell of the cathedral: tallow and incense and underneath it something sweetish and organic, like fruit going soft.

The shadows in the corners deepened and gathered, and in them a shape assembled—not solid, not present, more like a memory of presence, a man-shaped absence wearing the suggestion of robes. Where its face should have been there was a smoothness, and on the smoothness, small as a coin, the sigil turned its slow millstone turn.

"Xanthe Galloway," it said, and oh, oh no, oh *damn* it—it used her voice. Not an imitation. *Hers*, lifted whole out of some moment ten years dead, the exact timbre that had said *my herald* across a burning battlefield, played back with perfect fidelity and perfect emptiness, a bell rung by someone who had never heard it ring.

"I know what you are," Xanthe said. His voice came out steadier than he deserved.

"Do you?" The borrowed mouth of the shadow smiled with her smile. "You know what I am the way a starving man knows bread. Come to my house, herald. I have kept everything. Every word she ever spoke into your ear. Every prayer you ever sent into the dark for ten years—I caught those too, you know. They fell so far. I caught every one." The shadow drifted closer, and the shards against his chest grew hot enough that he flinched. "She is not coming back. But her voice can be yours whenever you want it. Say the word and I will let you hear her say your name tonight. Tomorrow night. Every night for the rest of your life. Is that not worth more than two pieces of scrap lead?"

The candle flame stood straight again. The room was just a room. The shadow was gone between one breath and the next, leaving only the sweetness and the heat of the shards and the ringing hollowness where his certainty used to be.

Xanthe sat for a long time in the dark, his arms wrapped around the fragments of his goddess's true name, listening to the ghost of her voice decay in his memory and wondering, with a grief that had teeth, how long it would be before he could no longer tell the difference between what he remembered and what had been offered to him.

Outside, across the rooftops, the spire of the House of Answered Prayer glowed faintly against the stars, pulsing.

Waiting.

And far to the east, in a borderland where the veil wore thin, a hermit who had once been a high priest was lighting a fire beside a shrine that held the third shard—and, Xanthe would learn too late, a truth about the seal that would change everything he thought he knew about why Ulyana had left him.
