## 12. Do Not Look For Me

The counterfeit's voice followed him out of the cathedral.

Not in his ears—Xanthe had learned, over ten years of silence, the difference between a voice and its echo. This was an echo. It lived behind his eyes now, in the warm hollow where Ulyana's words had once made their home, and it repeated itself with the patience of something that had never once been refused.

*Give them to me, little covenant-man. Give me the shards, and I will give you her back.*

He walked. He did not remember deciding to walk, but his feet carried him down the Processional and away from the white-and-gold monstrosity that called itself a cathedral, past braziers burning with flame that bent against the wind, past converts kneeling on cold stone with their foreheads to the ground. His coat was heavy with the two lead shards sewn into the lining, one over each kidney, like kidneys of a second, truer body. They were quiet. They had been quiet since he'd fled the counterfeit's presence, and their silence felt like abandonment all over again.

*She left you,* said the echo, wearing her voice the way a thief wears a stolen coat. *You heard it yourself. She saw the hunger coming. She saw what the gods had become—what she was becoming—and she chose to close the door with herself on the far side of it. She chose silence. She chose to leave you.*

"I know," Xanthe said aloud.

A convert looked up at him, eyes shining, and smiled as if Xanthe had blessed him. Xanthe kept walking.

That was the wound of it. Not the vision itself—the drowned chapel, the revenant priest kneeling to him, mistaking him for a herald, pressing the second shard into his hands with fingers like riverbed stones. Not even what the shard had shown him: Ulyana standing at the veil with her hands full of light, weaving it shut strand by strand while the other gods howled behind her like wolves denied a kill, while she turned her face away from the world she loved because if she looked at it she would not be able to do what needed doing.

No. The wound was simpler than that. The wound was this: *she meant it.*

*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

Ten years he had carried those words like a coal in his chest, and he had told himself a hundred stories about them. That she had been taken. That she had been weakened. That some enemy had cut her voice out mid-battle and she was somewhere beyond the veil, wounded, waiting, unable to reach him. Every story had ended the same way—with him finding her, restoring her, hearing her say *well done, faithful one.* He had built a decade out of that ending. He had sold hollow charms and hidden a warm mark under a glove and performed small forbidden rites for children's fevers, all of it propped up by the certainty that her silence was a wound, not a choice.

It was a choice.

She had stood at the door between worlds and shut it, and she had known he would come looking anyway, because she knew him, and so she had spent her last breath telling him not to. And then she had let the silence stand for ten years rather than break her own seal, even for him. Even for him.

Xanthe stopped at the edge of the Wardmarket bridge, where the gray water of the Vask moved beneath him like poured pewter, and he gripped the rail, and he understood—with the terrible clarity that comes only at the bottom of a thing—that there was a version of this night where he went back. Where he climbed the cathedral steps and knelt among the converts and opened his coat and gave the counterfeit everything it wanted. Because the counterfeit had offered him the only thing he had ever truly prayed for.

Her voice back.

Recorded. Replayed. A puppet strung on old prayers, saying *well done, faithful one* whenever he pulled the string. But it would have been her timbre, her warmth, the particular way she had always said his name like it was a joke they shared. And after ten years of silence, after learning tonight that the silence was chosen—that she had weighed the whole world against her faithful and chosen the world, or chosen the seal, or chosen whatever cold arithmetic gods do—after learning that, wouldn't a lie that sounded like her be better than a truth that didn't speak at all?

His hands were shaking. The mark under his glove was burning—not warm, not alive, but burning, the way a scar burns before rain.

"Give them to me," the echo whispered, tender as a mother. "You're so tired, Xanthe. Ten years is so long. Let me carry it. Let me sound like her. You'll never know the difference."

"That," said Sera's voice behind him, flat and furious, "is exactly the problem."

* * *

He hadn't heard her come up onto the bridge. Inquisitor-trained, he supposed, even a clerk who had burned her career and her safety in a single night. She stood a few paces off with her hood back and her satchel clutched to her chest like a shield, and her face in the lamplight was pale and set, and she looked at him the way one looks at a man standing too close to a ledge.

"You followed me."

"The whole district followed you," she said. "Half of them were hers—" she caught herself, corrected, "—its. Half of them were *its* people. I was the only one who wasn't praying while I walked." She came closer, slowly, the way you approach a spooked animal. "Your lips were moving, back there on the Processional. Talking to nothing. To it."

"It talks in her voice."

"I know. Mine does too." Something flickered across her face—her own mark had faded when the counterfeit unraveled around its edges, but not entirely; she still bore a ghost of the sigil, a scar of belonging. "Mine says softer things. It says I was right to run. It says the inquisition deserved what's coming. It's very reasonable." She set her satchel down on the bridge rail with a deliberate, careful motion, as if putting down a weapon. "Reasonable is how it gets you. So. Tell me what it offered."

Xanthe laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Everything."

"Specifically."

"Her." He turned to face the water. "It offered me her. Her voice, her warmth, a god-shaped thing that says my name the way she used to. All I have to do is hand over the shards and stop asking questions." His jaw tightened. "And here's the part that should frighten you, clerk: I almost did. I was halfway back up those steps when your voice reached me. If you'd been thirty seconds slower—"

"But I wasn't."

"Luck."

"No." Sera picked up her satchel again, hugged it. "I've been thinking about this since the undercroft. Since the tablets hummed under your hands and you looked like a man seeing the sun for the first time. I've been thinking about why I ran from the inquisition with a map full of death warrants, and I think it comes down to one question." She stepped around to his side, forcing him to either look at her or look away deliberately, and he chose to look at her, because she deserved that much. "What is faith worth if it's never been tested against the truth?"

"That's a clerk's question."

"It's the only question. Everything else is decoration." Her voice stayed level, but her hands didn't; he saw them tremble around the satchel strap. "Listen to me. I grew up in the inquisition. I believed the ledgers were holy. Then I found out the ledgers were being written by the thing we were supposed to be hunting, and my whole life fell apart in an afternoon—and do you know what I wanted? Exactly what you want. I wanted someone to tell me the old story was still true. I wanted to un-know it." She shook her head. "But you can't un-know. There's no rite for it. There's just the truth, sitting there, ugly, and you either build on top of it or you crawl inside a beautiful lie and let it eat you from the inside out."

"She left us." The words tore coming out. "Do you understand? Not captured. Not silenced by an enemy. She *chose* it. She stood at the veil and she closed it and she told me not to follow her, and then she let ten years pass without so much as a whisper, knowing what the silence was doing to us—to Odile, to the widow Maren, to every soul who ever lit a candle to her. She did the arithmetic and we weren't worth breaking the seal." His voice broke on the last word. "I gave that woman ten years. Ten years of selling dead charms and hiding a warm mark like a crime and praying into a well with no bottom. And she counted it all up and decided the price of keeping the door shut was worth more than answering me."

Sera was quiet for a long moment. Below them, the Vask slid past, carrying lamplight in pieces.

"Yes," she said finally. "She did. Probably it cost her more than it cost you—"

"Don't."

"—but yes. She chose it. That's the truth." Sera's eyes were bright and hard. "Now here's the other truth, the one the counterfeit will never offer you, because it can't: she sealed the veil to starve things like *it*. Things that answer prayers by eating the pray-er. Things that wear borrowed names. Everything it offers you—her voice, her comfort, her forgiveness—is stolen goods. It's a fence selling pieces of a murdered god, and you're about to hand it the rest of the body."

"And if the real her never comes back?" Xanthe's voice was low, ragged. "If the reforging fails, if the shards are bait like Odile said, if I burn my mark for nothing and the last ten years end in ash? What's your beautiful truth worth then?"

"Then it's still true." Sera said it simply, without drama, and somehow that was worse and better than any comfort could have been. "That's the whole point. Truth doesn't promise to make things better. It promises to be *true*. The lie promises everything and delivers nothing but itself." She reached out—hesitated—then laid her hand flat on his coat, over the place where the first shard sat against his ribs. Through the wool, faintly, he felt it: a hum. Small. Alive. The first the shards had made since the cathedral. "Feel that?"

He did. Faint as a struck bell heard through a wall, but unmistakably a response—not to the counterfeit's voice, not to his despair, but to her touch, to contact, to something honest.

"They're lead tablets with fragments of a name on them," Sera said. "Dead metal. Except they're not, are they? They answered you in the undercroft and they're answering now. Whatever Ulyana did—whatever she sacrificed—some of her is still in these. Still *here*, on our side of the seal, waiting." She took her hand back. "You can give them to the thing in the cathedral and get a recording. Or you can finish what you started and find out what the real thing sounds like. Those are your choices. I've made mine—I'm standing on a bridge at midnight with a heretic, holding a map that gets me burned. I chose the truth when it cost me everything I had. I'm asking you to choose it when it costs you the thing you love most."

Xanthe looked at the water for a long time.

The echo was still there—he could feel it coiled behind his eyes, patient, sweet, reciting her name like an incantation. *Give them to me. You'll never know the difference.* And the awful thing, the thing he would carry to his grave, was that he might not. That ten years of hunger had worn him smooth enough to fit inside any shape that promised warmth.

But the shards were humming.

And underneath the echo, underneath the grief, there was a smaller, harder thing that had kept him selling charms through a decade of silence: the memory of what her presence had actually felt like. Not comfort. Not warmth. Ulyana had been a storm-calm, a sword-edge, a laughter that dared you. The counterfeit's voice was honey, and honey was wrong. She had never once been honey. She had been the thing that made honey possible—flowers, and work, and stinging.

"Do you know what her last words were?" he said.

Sera waited.

"*If I do not answer, do not look for me.*" He said it slowly, tasting each word like a coin bitten for gold. "For ten years I thought it was a cry for help. A locked door with her banging on the other side. Tonight I learned it was exactly what it sounded like—an instruction. And I've spent all night hating her for it." He straightened. His hand found the rail, and the shaking in it slowed. "But instructions can be obeyed or broken, clerk. That's what makes them instructions. A command from a puppet isn't a command at all—it's theater. Only a real god can be disobeyed." He pushed off from the rail. "So I'm going to keep disobeying her. All the way to the borderlands, all the way to the third shard, all the way to whatever's left of her name. And if she's really on the other side of that seal, really listening—then someday, when the door is open, she can punish me for it personally."

Something moved through Sera's face that might have been relief, or hope, or the beginning of laughter held carefully in check.

"That's the worst theology I've ever heard," she said.

"It's the only kind I've got." He touched his coat, over the humming shards, and for the first time since the drowned chapel the gesture felt like a vow instead of a wound. "Truth over comfort. Even this truth. Even *her* truth." He glanced back once, up the Processional, toward the cathedral's distant glow—white and gold and hungry. "Come on. We leave for the borderlands before dawn, while its people are still sleeping off their conversion. And Sera—"

"Yes?"

"If I start listening to that voice again—if I so much as turn toward those steps—you put a knife in me and take the shards yourself. Swear it."

She looked at him for a long moment. Then she drew a thin blade from her boot, kissed the flat of it like an inquisitor swearing an oath on scripture, and slid it home again.

"Sworn," she said. "Now walk, charm-seller. Dawn's coming, and the road to the borderlands is long, and somewhere out there a hermit is tending a shrine that knows more about your goddess than either of us does."

They left the bridge together, two small figures moving against the current of the sleeping city, and behind them the echo in Xanthe's head went quiet—not gone, never gone, but quieter, like a tide losing its argument with the shore.

And far off, at the edge of hearing, in the space between one heartbeat and the next, Xanthe thought he felt the shards hum again—two notes this time, close together, almost like a word.

Almost like *come.*
