## 13. The Third Shard and the Traitor's Road

The road out of Vask began as a road and ended as a rumor.

Xanthe had walked it once before, ten years ago, in the other direction—walking away from a battlefield where his goddess had stopped speaking mid-word, walking toward a city that was already passing laws against the sound of her name. He remembered the borderlands differently then. Greener. Louder with birdsong. Now the land itself seemed to have been holding its breath for a decade: fields gone to gray grass, hedgerows standing like rows of clenched teeth, farmhouses with their doors nailed shut from the inside, which told you everything about what the people who'd left them had feared.

"Cheerful country," Sera said behind him.

She walked like an inquisitor even out of her clerk's grays—in a borrowed traveling coat two sizes too big, hood up, one hand never far from the knife at her belt. She had stolen more than the map when she ran. She'd stolen the inquisition's route schedules too, and they had spent three days making sure no patrol followed them onto the Traitor's Road, so named because it was the only way into the borderlands that didn't pass through a checkpoint, and everyone who used it was assumed to be fleeing something.

"Most people don't come here to be cheerful," Xanthe said.

"No. They come here to be thin." She caught up beside him, breath fogging. "That's what my superiors called it. The thin places. Where the veil wears through." She glanced sideways at him. "You've felt it, haven't you. Since we crossed the last ridge."

He had. The covenant mark under his glove had been warm for days—not the dull banked warmth of ten silent years, but something closer to the warmth of a hand held near a fire. And twice now, at dusk, he had heard something that might have been wind and might have been breathing, coming from the wrong direction entirely: not from around them, but *through* them.

"It's thinner here," he admitted. "Like walking on ice over deep water."

"And you're still going."

"I'm still going." He looked at her. "You could still turn back. The map's copied. You've done your part."

Sera snorted—a small, sharp sound, the first almost-laugh he'd gotten out of her since the undercroft. "You think I ran from the inquisition so I could stop halfway and be a very well-informed fugitive?" She pulled her hood lower against the wind. "I burned my whole life down in one afternoon, charm-seller. I intend to see what I bought with it."

They walked on. Behind them, Vask was a smudge of smoke on the horizon, half its districts already flying the stitched sigil from their rooftops. Ahead, the land fell away into the borderlands, where the world had always been a little less certain of its own edges.

* * *

They made camp the third night in the shell of a wayshrine—one of the old roadside altars that had dotted the realm before the Outlawing, this one roofless, its dedication stone worn blank by weather or by deliberate chiseling. Someone had built a fire ring in it years ago; the stones were blackened and cold. Xanthe gathered what scrub wood the land offered while Sera saw to the horses, and by full dark they had a small flame going, huddled over it like conspirators, which, Xanthe supposed, they were.

It was Sera who spoke first, in the voice people use when they've been rehearsing a sentence all day.

"When I was a girl," she said, "my mother prayed. Quietly, after the Outlawing—under blankets, into her cupped hands, like she was hiding bread from rats. She prayed to Ulyana for my father to come home from the coast trade." Her eyes stayed on the fire. "He didn't come home. And my mother said, *the goddess is testing us*, and she kept praying, and she got thinner every year, and she died still praying to a god who never answered. And when the new god came—" her jaw tightened— "when the sermons started, when the preacher healed that lame boy right there on the wardmarket steps, I thought: *finally*. Finally someone answers. I hated her a little, you know. Ulyana. For ten years of my mother kneeling in the dark."

Xanthe said nothing. There was nothing yet to say.

"And then I got marked," Sera went on, quieter now. "And I told myself it was the same god, finally awake, working through new channels. And then I saw the widow's sigil, and your grief at Odile's sentencing, and the undercroft, and—" she turned her hands palm-up, helpless— "and now I don't know what I believe. Except that I'm tired of being certain. Certainty is what the preacher sells. It's what the inquisition sold. I want to know what actually happened, Xanthe. Even if it's ugly. *Especially* if it's ugly."

"That," Xanthe said, "is the first prayer I've heard in ten years worth answering."

She looked at him sharply, and he smiled, tired, and threw another stick on the fire, and neither of them said anything else for a long time—but something had shifted between them, some plank laid across a gap, and both of them felt it hold.

* * *

The shrine announced itself before they saw it.

It began with the birds—or rather, with the absence of birds. For a quarter mile around the hollow where the old high temple of Ulyana had once stood, the trees stood empty, and the silence had texture, like held breath. Then the light changed. It came slantwise through the canopy with a gold in it that had nothing to do with the sun, and the air grew warm in defiance of the season, and Xanthe's mark woke beneath his glove like a coal breathed upon.

Sera felt it too—he saw her flinch and press her hand flat against her own chest, where the counterfeit's sigil sat under her shirt like a burr. "Mine's *cold*," she whispered. "It hates this place."

"Good," Xanthe said. "Means we're in the right spot."

The temple was a ruin, but a tended ruin. That was the first strangeness. Ivy had been cut back from the doorway. The fallen columns had been stacked, not scattered. And along the processional path, someone had set candles—hundreds of them, years of them, stubs melted into the stone in layers like geological strata, a decade of small flames burned out one at a time by a single pair of hands.

At the heart of the ruin, in the rotted shadow of the broken apse, knelt an old man.

He was sweeping. That was the second strangeness. He swept the mosaic floor with a broom whose bristles had been replaced so many times the handle was worn to a rope, moving with the slow economy of someone who has performed the same act every day for years beyond counting. He did not look up when they entered. He finished his stroke, and another, and then he said, in a voice like dry leaves:

"You're late."

Xanthe went still. "I beg your pardon?"

"Ten years, four months, eleven days." The old man straightened, joints cracking like green wood, and turned. His face was seamed and brown as an apple left too long in the sun, but his eyes—his eyes were young, and burning, and terrible with patience. "She said her faithful would endure. I told her they would. But I confess I had begun to wonder about you specifically, Xanthe Galloway. You always did take the scenic path to a point."

"You know me?"

"I married you to your covenant, boy. I was Ulyana's high priest when you swore your oath at fourteen, knees knocking, sure you'd be struck dead for stammering." A dry smile. "I am Brother Aldous. Or I was. Now I am the man who sweeps the floor and keeps the candles, because a god asked me to wait, and I am apparently incapable of refusing her anything."

Sera's hand had gone to her knife. Xanthe shook his head, barely. He stepped forward over the candle-strata, and his heart was doing something complicated and painful, because he *did* remember—the great temple, the incense, the enormous calm presence of the priest who had bound his hand to heaven.

"Why are you here?" he asked. "Why stay? The Silence took everything. The order scattered. The inquisition burned the temples. Why keep sweeping a floor no one walks?"

Aldous set the broom aside with ceremony, the way he might set down a relic.

"Because she told me what she was doing," he said. "Before she did it. Come. Sit. Both of you. What I have to say will put you on the ground otherwise—I've seen it do so to better travelers than you."

* * *

They sat among the ruined pews, and Aldous built up the fire from a shrine that had never quite gone cold—it seemed to Xanthe that the coals here answered the old man's hands a little too readily—and he talked.

"She came to me the night before the Silence," he said. "In person. Not a vision. *In person.* She sat where you're sitting, girl, and she looked older than any god should look, and she said: *Aldous, the others have stopped drinking the wine and started eating the vineyard.*"

"The other gods," Xanthe said slowly.

"The other gods." Aldous nodded. "You have to understand what worship became, in those last years. Prayer was meant to be a door. A conversation. Two voices. But gods are what they eat, and mortals had learned to pray like beggars—total surrender, total dependence, *take my will, take my judgment, just fix it.* And the gods drank it down. Every answered prayer made them hungrier. Every miracle demanded a greater one. They stopped being shepherds and started being farmers, and the flock was us." His old hands folded. "Ulyana alone refused. She said love that demands surrender isn't love; it's appetite wearing love's face. So she did the only thing left. She sealed the veil. All of it. Every door between the realms, barred from her side—including her own."

"The Silence," Xanthe whispered. Ten years of ache rose in his throat. "She chose it."

"She chose it," Aldous said simply. "To starve the predators. To give mortals back their own strength. She trusted her faithful to endure without miracles—to learn that the covenant was never meant to be a leash. And she made me promise three things: to keep her name fragmentary, to keep her altar lit, and to tell no one why. Because if the other gods knew a seal existed—if they knew silence was a wall and not an absence—they would spend eternity trying to breach it. Better that the world believed she had abandoned it. Abandonment breaks hearts. Knowledge breaks seals."

Sera had gone very pale. "But something *did* get through. Something is answering prayers in Vask. Something wearing her sigil."

And here Aldous's burning eyes dimmed, and for the first time the old man looked old, and afraid.

"Yes," he said. "That is my failure. Mine. In the last hour before the seal closed—while she poured herself into the working, while every door was swinging shut—something slipped through the gap. Not one of the great ones. Smaller. A leech-thing, a scrap of divine hunger that had been feeding at the edges of her own worship for centuries, wearing scraps of her like a costume. It came through *with her seal*, riding the closing like a rat rides a sinking ship. And it has spent ten years in the dark places of the world, growing fat on the faith of the desperate, learning to wear her face, learning to say *yes* to every prayer she refused to answer." He looked at Xanthe, and his gaze was iron. "Every healing it grants costs the healed something they'll never miss until it's gone. Every church it builds is a mouth. And it cannot make a true covenant—it can only counterfeit yours. Which is why it wants the shards, boy. With her true name assembled, it could finish what it began. It could become her. Permanently. And no one alive would know the difference."

The fire cracked. Somewhere far off, a bird remembered itself and sang, once, uncertainly.

Xanthe found his voice was steady, which surprised him. "Then we get there first."

"You misunderstand." Aldous rose, joints crackling, and crossed to the broken apse, where behind the rotted altar-cloth something glinted—a lead tablet, ancient, set into the stone like a keystone. "You don't *get* there first. You were always meant to come here. She planned for this too—she planned for everything except her own heart breaking." He worked the tablet free with hands that trembled only slightly, and held it out. "The third shard. Kept by her high priest, as commanded. Guarded by a coward who was too frightened of failing her to ever test whether he still could."

Xanthe reached out—and stopped. "Odile warned me the shards were bait. That the thing is herding us."

"It is," Aldous agreed placidly. "It has been herding you since the first candle flared. Every step you've taken toward her name, it has permitted, encouraged, arranged. It *wants* you to gather them." He pressed the tablet into Xanthe's palm, and the lead hummed against Xanthe's skin, and the mark beneath his glove blazed like a brand, and for one vertiginous moment Xanthe heard—faint, distant, muffled by ten years of silence and a sealed door—a voice. Not words. Just a voice. Just the shape of someone he knew.

"But hear me," Aldous said, gripping his wrist, old strength like a manacle. "Bait works both ways. It thinks it is luring a mouse to a trap. It does not understand that it is luring itself to a door. When the name is assembled—truly assembled, spoken jointly, god and faithful together, as covenants were always meant to be spoken—the seal doesn't break, boy. The seal *becomes a gate*. And gates open from both sides." His grip tightened. "Do you understand me? She is not behind the wall waiting to be rescued. She is behind the wall waiting to be *let out*. And the key is not the shards. The key is you."

Sera made a small sound. Xanthe couldn't look away from the old priest.

"Why you?" Sera asked. "Of all the faithful—why him?"

Aldous released Xanthe's wrist and smiled, and it was the saddest smile Xanthe had ever seen.

"Because he kept the covenant for ten years without a single answer," he said. "Because he loved her when loving her cost everything and returned nothing. Because the counterfeit can imitate her power, her sigil, her voice—but it cannot imitate being loved faithfully through silence. That, it has never tasted. That is the one thing in all the realms it cannot digest." He picked up his broom again, already turning back to his endless sweeping. "Now go. Take the western road past the drowned chapel lands—you'll find the fourth piece where you least expect it, and I'll say no more of that, she forbade me. And hurry. The thing in Vask has felt the shard wake. It knows where you are now."

"How long do we have?" Xanthe asked, closing his fist around the humming lead.

Aldous paused, broom mid-stroke, and considered the question with the gravity of a man consulting decades of patience.

"Less than the road requires," he said. "Ride hard. Pray lightly. And boy—" he glanced back, eyes young and burning in the old ruined face— "when you speak her name at the end of all this, and you will—speak it *with* her. Not *for* her. Not *to* her. With. That was always the whole of the law, and we forgot it, and look what forgetting bought us."

They rode out as the false dawn grayed the east. At the edge of the hollow Xanthe looked back once: a small figure in a ruined temple, sweeping a floor no congregation would ever walk, keeping a candle lit for a god behind a wall.

"He'll be there when it's done," Sera said quietly beside him. "Won't he. Sweeping."

"If there's any justice," Xanthe said, "he'll finally get to stop."

They spurred the horses west, toward the drowned chapel, toward the last pieces of a name, and behind them the borderlands held their thin, listening silence—and somewhere far ahead, in a city of smoke and stitched sigils, something hungry felt the third shard move and began, at last, to hurry.
