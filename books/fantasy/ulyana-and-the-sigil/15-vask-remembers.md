## 15. Vask Remembers

The road into Vask had always smelled of tallow and river-mud, of smoke from a thousand cookfires pressed flat beneath the city's gray weight. Xanthe had walked it in every season, in every mood, and he had never once come over the last rise without feeling something loosen in his chest—the particular relief of a man coming home to a place that had never been kind to him but had at least been *his*.

Now he stopped on the rise with Sera beside him and felt nothing loosen at all.

The city was burning candles.

Not one or two—*thousands*. Every windowsill along the river terraces held a flame, every shrine-niche in the old walls, every doorstep where once there had been only the mandated gray lanterns of the inquisition's watch. From this distance they looked like stars that had fallen and gotten stuck to Vask like burrs. The light was wrong for it, though. Too steady. Too uniform. Real candleflame guttered; these burned like they were being fed from somewhere else.

"They've been busy," Sera said quietly.

"Three weeks," Xanthe said. "We were gone three weeks."

He counted the changes the way a soldier counts what's missing from an armory. The inquisition's black-and-silver banners still flew from the Wardgate towers—but beneath them hung new ones, deep blue shot through with silver thread, and stitched across each in thread that caught the light too eagerly: the sigil. Not Ulyana's true mark, which Xanthe carried warm under his glove and had carried for ten years of silence, but its counterfeit twin—the same sweeping curve, the same open circle, rendered just slightly too perfect, too symmetrical, the way a face is too symmetrical when it has been made rather than born.

"The inquisition co-opted," Sera said. She said it flatly, but her hand had gone to her own forearm, where her mark had faded to a pale ghost since the counterfeit began to unravel at the edges of her certainty. "I trained under Inquisitor Halven. He used to say faith was a disease you quarantined." Her mouth twisted. "Now he's carrying its banner."

"People don't change their gods," Xanthe said. "They change their fears. The god comes along inside the fear, like a rat in a grain sack."

He started down toward the gate, and Sera fell in beside him, and neither of them spoke again until the road gave way to cobbles and the cobbles gave way to the press of the city itself.

* * *

Inside the walls, Vask had learned a new posture.

Xanthe had expected conversion to look like joy—he remembered the old festivals of his childhood, before the Outlawing, when the faithful had danced in the wardmarket and the goddess's presence had been a warmth you could lean into. What he saw instead was *compliance*. People moved through the streets quickly, eyes down, and when they passed one of the new blue banners they touched two fingers to their breastbone in a gesture Xanthe didn't recognize. It wasn't worship. It was the motion of a man showing an empty palm to a robber.

At the fish-stalls by the canal, a woman haggled with her left hand and kept her right pressed flat against her apron, and Xanthe saw why when her sleeve shifted: a sigil, burned into the skin, fresh enough that the edges were still pink. She caught him looking and her eyes went wide—not with recognition, not with kinship, but with *fear*, the fear of a marked thing seen by another marked thing in a city where marks were both currency and indictment.

"She thinks you're going to report her," Sera murmured.

"I know." His voice came out rougher than he intended. "That's what we built here. That's what *it* built here."

They crossed the Canalbridge into the wardmarket district as the false dusk gathered, and that was where Xanthe understood how completely the ground had shifted beneath them. The wardmarket—the sprawl of stalls and shuttered booths where he had sold hollow charms for a decade, where Odile had fenced relics and whispered rumors—was gone. Not closed. *Gone.* The stalls had been torn down and replaced with a raised dais of white stone, and on the dais stood a preacher in blue, and around the preacher knelt perhaps four hundred people, swaying together, their voices rising in a cadence that was almost prayer and almost something else—a rhythm, a feeding rhythm, and Xanthe felt his covenant-mark go cold against his wrist like a hand flinching away from a stove.

"—and she who was silent has spoken again," the preacher intoned, and his voice carried wrong, too far, reaching the back rows without effort or echo. "She was silent because you were unworthy. She is silent no longer. Kneel, and be answered. Kneel, and be *filled*."

"Filled," Sera repeated softly. "Not fed. Filled. Like a vessel."

"Like a cup," Xanthe said. "You drink a cup empty."

He turned away from the dais before the preacher's eyes could sweep their way, and led Sera down the alley behind the old chandler's shop, past the door with the iron ring he'd knocked on a thousand times, and knocked now—three slow, then two quick, the old pattern—and waited in the dark with his heart going like a fist against a door.

The grate slid. A single eye. Then Odile's voice, low and incredulous: *"Three-two? Three-two hasn't been safe in a month, you absolute—"*

The door opened, and Odile pulled him inside by the collar hard enough to hurt, and shut it, and threw the bolts, and then simply stood there in the dark of her own hallway looking at him like a woman checking whether a ghost could be punched.

"You're alive," she said.

"Barely. Mostly."

"And you brought the inquisition's clerk." Her eyes went to Sera, narrow and bright in the gloom. "Odile does not approve of clerks. Clerks write things down."

"This clerk stole the inquisition's map of every sigil-bearer in the province," Sera said evenly, "and burned her own career doing it. You can disapprove of me after you hear what I know."

Odile looked at Xanthe. Xanthe shrugged. "She talks me out of bad decisions. I've grown accustomed to it."

Something in Odile's face cracked then—not quite a smile, but the place where a smile would go if the last month hadn't happened. She lit a stub of tallow candle, shielded it behind her hand, and led them down into the cellar.

* * *

The cellar was different.

Xanthe remembered it as a hoard: crates of relics, rolls of outlawed vellum, the comfortable clutter of a fence who'd survived ten years of purges by owning everything and displaying nothing. Now the crates were pushed to the walls, and the center of the room held a long table, and the table held a map of Vask so detailed it must have taken months, and around the map stood nine people, and every one of them turned when the candlelight reached them.

Four bore sigils. Xanthe could see them even in the bad light—the counterfeit mark glowing faintly on wrists, throats, the back of one man's neck, all of them dimmed now, flickering like lamps running dry. The other five were unmarked, and somehow that frightened him more. Unmarked people did not end up in cellars with marked ones unless something had broken the old arithmetic of survival.

"This is everyone?" Xanthe asked.

"This is everyone who matters tonight," Odile said. "There are more. Forty-odd scattered through the Low Terraces and the tanneries. Marked and unmarked both. The marked ones because the thing in the cathedral is starting to *pull* on them—their marks ache when it preaches, did you know that? Like a hook set in the skin. And the unmarked ones because—" she paused, choosing the word with a fence's precision, "—because they watched it take the wardmarket, and the inquisition, and the magistracy, and they noticed that nobody asked any of them first."

"It co-opted Magistrate Corvel," Xanthe said. "I know. I paid him."

"We know you paid him." Odile's voice gentled, which from Odile was more alarming than anger. "Xanthe. Sit down. There's news, and none of it is good, and you look like a man who's been walking three weeks on grief and stubbornness."

He sat. Sera sat. The candle stub threw their shadows huge against the map of the city they were about to talk about saving.

"The ritual needs four shards joined to your mark," Sera said, laying it out for the room in her clerk's crisp cadence. "We have three. The fourth isn't lead—it's living. A woman named Maren Ostrey, a widow on Copperlane, whose body carries a fragment of Ulyana's name from a healing the counterfeit performed badly. The reforging has to happen in the undercroft beneath the old priests' seminary—it's the only place in Vask where the veil is thin enough and the walls thick enough. We move Maren there, we begin the rite, and we hold the line until Xanthe speaks the name."

"Hold the line," Odile repeated. "Against what?"

"Against it," Xanthe said. "It came through the wards at the drowned chapel fast enough to shake stone loose. Here, in its own city, with four hundred throats feeding it every evening? When it feels the shards assembling, it will come like weather."

Silence, in the cellar. The kind of silence that has weight.

"So," Odile said at last, and her voice had gone carefully, terribly reasonable. "We need time. Time means distraction. Distraction means bodies in the streets. Forty people, maybe sixty if we push. Fires in the tannery quarter, crowds at the gates, the inquisition's watch pulled thin—and your ritual happens behind all of it, under cover of our people bleeding for you."

"That's not what I said."

"It's what the plan says." She tapped the map, once, hard. "Look at it honestly, charm-seller. You want to save the world, and the world costs. It has always cost. I have spent twenty years in this city learning exactly what things cost, and I am telling you: without a diversion, the counterfeit reaches that undercroft before you finish the second verse of the name, and everything—Maren, the shards, your goddess, *all of it*—burns. Sixty people against the whole covenant between mortal and divine realms. That is the cheapest price anyone will ever offer you."

And the worst part, the part that sat in Xanthe's chest like swallowed stone, was that she was right. The arithmetic was clean. He had done dirtier sums than this as a mercenary, back when his hands belonged to whoever paid and his conscience was a thing he kept in a box under his bed. Sixty volunteers against a god. Any commander in history would take that trade without losing sleep.

Any commander who had never listened to a child pray to a silent sky.

"No," he said.

Odile blinked. "No?"

"No." He stood, and his legs ached, and his mark pulsed warm under his glove, and he found that the words came easier than he expected—as if they'd been waiting in him for years, since the Silence, since the first unanswered prayer, since he'd decided that devotion meant enduring rather than asking. "You're right about the arithmetic. That's exactly why it's wrong. Because the moment I spend civilians like coin, I'm doing what *it* does. It fills people up and pours them out. It spends four hundred kneeling throats every night to keep itself fat. If I buy my miracle with the same currency, then it doesn't matter whether the true name wins—I'll have already lost the argument. The whole point of the covenant, the *real* covenant, is that it goes both ways. She doesn't spend us. We don't spend each other."

"Pretty," Odile said. "The counterfeit will kill us anyway when it takes the city."

"Then we find another way to be expensive." He leaned over the map, and his finger moved, and the plan began to rearrange itself in his head the way plans do—not cleaner, but truer. "The undercroft has one entrance we know of. But the old seminary was built over the pre-Outlawing pilgrim tunnels, and half of those run under the wardmarket. If the diversion can't be people, let it be *light*. Every candle in the Low Terraces, lit at once, unlit again—no crowd, no blood, just the city itself flickering. The counterfeit reads faith like a predator reads heartbeats. Let's give it ten thousand heartbeats to listen to and nowhere to pick one out."

Sera was already pulling the inquisition's map closer, tracing tunnels with a fingertip. "The Candlewrights' Guild still owes Odile money. Candles can be *placed*, not just lit."

"And Maren?" Odile asked, and her voice had lost its edge, which meant she'd surrendered the argument without admitting it. "The fourth shard is a person, Xanthe. A widow with bad knees and no sword arm. Whatever else we refuse to spend, we can't refuse to spend *her*—she's already the shard."

"No," Xanthe said. "She's already the *vessel*. There's a difference, and tomorrow we're going to explain it to her, and then we're going to ask her. Ask, Odile. Not tell. If she says no, we find another way, even if the other way is worse. That's the whole covenant in one sentence: *nobody gets spent who didn't choose it.*"

For a long moment Odile looked at him across the map of the drowning city, and then she laughed—one short, sharp bark with no humor in it and, underneath the no-humor, something that might have been pride.

"Ten years selling hollow charms," she said, "and it turns out you were stocking the shelves for this. Fine. Fine! But understand what you're buying, charm-seller. Tomorrow night, when those candles go out and the thing in the cathedral notices the dark shaped like a message—we're all in its city, behind its walls, inside its reach. There's no road out of this one."

"I know," Xanthe said.

He looked down at the map, at the small inked square of the undercroft, at the tiny figure of a widow's house on Copperlane, and under his glove his covenant-mark beat warm and patient as a second heart.

Somewhere above them, muffled through floorboards and stone, four hundred voices rose in their feeding rhythm, and the candles of Vask burned steady in their thousands, and the city that had taught him to survive was teaching him, at last, what survival was *for*.

"One more thing," Odile said quietly, as the others bent to their tasks. "The magistrate sent word today. Corvel. He knows you're back in the city. He knows about the shards." She met Xanthe's eyes. "He wants to meet. Tonight. Alone. And Xanthe—he said to tell you that he knows what you paid him, and he knows what it really was." Her voice dropped. "He said: *he knows it was the last true charm in Vask. And he knows who made it.*"

The candle guttered. Outside, the feeding rhythm swelled, and somewhere in the dark above, bells that had not rung in ten years began, very slowly, to toll.
