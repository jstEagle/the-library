## 16. The Fourth Shard Is a Person

Sera had spent six years learning to read a city the way inquisitors read confessions: by what it tried to hide.

Vask at dusk was a confession written in lamplight. The gray districts pulled their shutters early now, not against the cold but against the processions—the white-robed singers of the Counterfeit's cult who walked the avenues with censers burning something sweet and wrong, like honey poured over iron. Whole streets had gone quiet in the way of houses where someone is dying upstairs and the family has agreed not to say so.

But the tenement on Corvane Street did not hide anything. It stood with its windows lit and its door propped open with a brick, and from three floors up came the smell of onions and rosemary, which was the smell of Maren Voss making dinner for whoever needed feeding that night.

Sera climbed the stairs slowly, letting her boots announce her. It was a courtesy she had learned too late to matter—she had once been the kind of visitor who arrived without announcement, and the kind who left whole buildings emptied. She was neither of those things anymore. She was a woman with a stolen map folded against her ribs and an inquisitor's seal broken into pieces in her pocket, and she was climbing toward the person Xanthe Galloway believed might be the last piece of a goddess's name.

The widow opened the door before Sera knocked.

"You're the clerk," Maren Voss said. "The one who came asking questions after my husband's funeral. You've got a face like a closed ledger." She stepped back to let her in. "You look worse now. Come in before the street sees you."

* * *

The room above was small and fiercely clean, though cleanliness here meant something different than it did in the inquisition's halls. There were no icons, no sigils, none of the white cloth the cult pressed on its converts. There was a loom half-threaded in the corner, a pot of soup that would stretch to feed six, and on the mantel a single candle burned in a dish of salt.

"I keep it lit," Maren said, following Sera's eyes. "Since the night I was healed. I don't know why. It feels rude to let it go out."

"May I see your arm?"

Maren pushed back her sleeve without ceremony. The scar ran from wrist to elbow—a burn, old and deep, the kind of wound that took a hand out of a woman's working life forever. Except it hadn't. Sera remembered the file: Maren Voss, widow, weaver, healed in a market square by a preacher's touch while two hundred people sang. The miracle that made the cult's reputation in this district. The healing that had left, beneath the new skin, a mark no one had thought to examine closely.

Sera examined it now, holding the candle close. And there it was—not on the skin, not inked or branded, but *in* it, visible only when the light came at the right angle, the way watermarks show themselves in paper held to the sun. A pattern of lines fine as spider silk, looping through the healed flesh in shapes that hurt Sera's eyes to follow, as if the pattern continued somewhere behind the world and her gaze kept slipping off its edge.

She had seen Ulyana's sigil drawn a thousand times—in inquisition primers, on confiscated charms, and lately, horribly, on the banners of the Counterfeit's cathedral. She had seen Xanthe's covenant mark, the true one, warm under his glove. This was neither and both. It was as if someone had taken the sigil and taught it to breathe.

"It's been itching," Maren said quietly. "For three days. Like the skin remembers being something else." She looked at Sera with the flat courage of a woman who had already buried a husband and a son to the fever years and had nothing left that could be taken cheaply. "The charm-seller sent you. He thinks they'll come for me."

"They'll come tonight," Sera said. "Maybe within the hour."

"And you're going to stop them. Alone." Maren glanced at the window, at the darkening street below. "How many are you?"

"One."

The widow nodded slowly, and then, to Sera's astonishment, began ladling soup into bowls. "Then you'll eat first. I won't have a woman die on an empty stomach in my house; it's bad manners all around."

* * *

They ate at the little table by the window, and Maren talked, because talking was how she held fear at arm's length, the way some people hold a poker at arm's length from a fire.

"My husband prayed for eleven years," she said. "Every morning, every night. When the Silence came he didn't stop. He said faith that needs answering isn't faith, it's commerce. He died of the lung-rot with his prayer book under his pillow, and no god came, and I decided he'd been a fool." She turned her bowl a quarter-turn on the table. "And then a man in white robes touched my arm and the rot-scar that had crippled me for nine years closed like a mouth, and I wept, and I hated myself for weeping, and I went back the next week anyway."

"The pull is real," Sera said. "I know."

"Do you?" Maren looked at her, and Sera realized her sleeve had ridden up, that the false sigil on her forearm was visible in the candlelight—faded now, graying at its edges since the night the counterfeit's hold on her had begun to slip, but still there, still legible. "You wore their mark."

"I still do. Part of it." Sera set down her spoon. "It answered me when nothing else ever had. I was a foundling raised by the inquisition to hunt miracles, and one day a miracle looked at me and said *you belong*, and I—" She stopped. The truth was larger than the sentence. "I understood every convert I'd ever judged. That's the worst thing it did to me. Not the mark. The understanding."

Maren reached across the table and gripped her wrist, over the faded sigil, hard enough to sting. "My husband used to say the gods grew hungry because we fed them fear instead of love. Fear is easy to take. Love has to be given." Her grip tightened. "Whatever comes up those stairs tonight—it will offer to take. Don't let it. Taking is all it knows."

Outside, the bells of the wardmarket rang the hour, and beneath them, faint but unmistakable, came singing.

* * *

They heard the procession before they saw it: the cult's choir, forty voices at least, moving up Corvane Street with the unhurried confidence of people who had never once been told no. The song was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Sera had stood in the cathedral nave and felt that beauty work on her like a current, smoothing away the rough edges of doubt, and she knew that downstairs, behind their doors, the neighbors who had not yet converted were hearing it and feeling their resistance soften like wax.

Through the shutter slats Sera counted them in the street below: eight acolytes in white, two enforcers of the co-opted inquisition in their gray-and-gold, and at the front a deacon she recognized—Brother Halvard, whose sermons she had attended undercover, whose voice could make surrender sound like rest. He carried no weapon. He never needed one. In his hands he carried a covered lantern, and even through the shutter Sera could feel the thing inside it pressing against the wood like a moth against a jar.

Her own mark woke.

It started as warmth, then became a pull, low and insistent, a leash tightening around something in her chest. *Come down,* it said, in no words at all. *Come down and be welcomed. You were always meant to be ours.* The gray edges of the sigil flushed pink again, renewing, and Sera pressed her palm flat against the wall and breathed the way the hermit had taught them on the traitor's road—four counts in, seven counts out—until the pull receded to a dull ache.

"It knows you're here," Maren whispered.

"It always knew. That's why they brought Halvard and not just enforcers." Sera drew the knife at her belt—an ordinary knife, inquisition steel, no enchantment in it, because there was almost no enchantment left to put in anything. She checked the stairwell door: oak, iron-banded, opening inward onto a landing, then twelve steps down to the street door. Defensible, barely. "Listen to me. If they get past me—and they may—you go out the back window, down the drainpipe, and you run for the undercroft. Odile's people will be watching the route. Do you understand? Whatever happens to me, the name goes on."

Maren Voss looked at her for a long moment. Then she crossed the room, took the candle from the mantel, and set it on the table between them, where its flame stood perfectly straight in the drafty room, as if the air itself had agreed to leave it alone.

"No," the widow said.

"Maren—"

"You misunderstand what I am." Her voice didn't rise at all, and that was what made it terrible. "I buried a husband. I buried a boy of nineteen. I worked nine years with a ruined arm because the world had decided gods were contraband and mercy was a crime. And now everyone wants to tell me what to do with the last thing I have. The charm-seller wants to carry me to his ritual like a relic in a box. The white-robes want to carry me to their cathedral like a lamb. And you want to carry me out a window." She touched her healed arm, over the breathing pattern hidden in the skin. "This is mine. My husband's prayers are in it. Eleven years of them, unanswered, and somehow they ended up *here*, in me, growing like wheat. I will decide where it goes."

The singing stopped directly below. Boots on cobbles. Then Brother Halvard's voice, warm as a hearth, pitched to fill a street:

"Sister Voss! We know you're frightened—we know how frightening the unmarked can be, filling your head with doubts. But you were chosen. You were *healed*. Come home, sister, and bring your guest. No harm needs to come to anyone tonight. We only want what was always yours to give."

*What was always yours to give,* Sera thought. Even now, even at the door, the counterfeit's servants spoke the language of consent while meaning plunder. That was the tell she would carry to her grave: the true gods asked. This thing only borrowed the shape of asking.

She blew out the candle.

Darkness dropped over the room, total except for the gray seam of the shutter slats—and in that darkness, Maren's arm glowed. Faintly, softly, the watermark-pattern in her healed flesh lit from within, lines of pale gold tracing themselves through skin, and Sera felt the hair rise on her arms, because she had grown up cataloguing miracles and confiscating them and she had never, not once, seen one that looked like *this*—not power displayed, but presence simply refusing to hide.

"It's never done that," Maren breathed.

"It's frightened," Sera said. "Or it's ready. Possibly those are the same thing." She moved to the stairwell door and set her shoulder against it. "Stay behind me. And Maren—if it offers you anything, anything at all—"

"I know. Taking is all it knows."

Below, the street door groaned. Not forced—*persuaded*, the lock turning with a soft click like a sigh, because locks had opinions now and the counterfeit's voice could reach into small mechanical souls and change them. Lamplight bloomed up the stairwell, and shadows climbed the walls ahead of the men who cast them.

Sera thought, absurdly, of her first day in the inquisition's archive, aged nine, told to memorize the list of forbidden names. She had asked her instructor why the names were forbidden, and he had struck her, and she had memorized them anyway, and for twenty years she had believed that obedience was the same thing as goodness. It had taken a goddess's silence, a false god's flattery, and a widow's stubbornness to teach her the difference.

The door handle began to turn.

Sera threw her weight against the door and shot the bolt she'd wedged into the bracket an hour ago, and the door slammed back against whatever force pushed it, shuddering but holding. Shouts below. Halvard's voice rising, losing its warmth: "Break it, then. Break it. The sister is confused, and confusion is a sickness we cure."

The first blow hit the door like a battering ram wrapped in wool—no shock of impact, just a deep structural groan, the iron bands flexing. Sera braced, knife reversed in her grip, and discovered that her hands were shaking and that it didn't matter, because courage, she understood suddenly, had never been the absence of shaking. Courage was what you did with the shaking. It was not magic. It was not a mark or a sigil or a shard of divine name. It was a woman standing on a staircase with a knife and a decision, and it was the only sacrament the last ten years had left intact.

The door split along its grain.

Through the gap came lamplight and Brother Halvard's face, serene again, reasonable, kind. "Clerk Serafen," he said gently. "We know what you are. We know what you've done. And we forgive it—all of it—because we know what you *want*. You want to belong to something that will never leave you. So does she." His eyes moved past Sera to the glow in the darkness, and for one naked instant the serenity cracked and Sera saw the hunger underneath, vast and patient and bottomless. "Give us the vessel, and you can walk away. That is our final mercy."

"She's not a vessel," Sera said. "She's a person. That's the entire point of everything, and you have never once understood it."

She drove the knife through the gap.

Not at Halvard—at the lantern, at the covered thing that shrieked when steel touched it, a sound like a hymn played backward, and the lamplight guttered brown and the pressure in the stairwell lifted for one precious breath. Sera wrenched the door wide and met the first acolyte on the landing, and it was ugly and close and human, fists and elbows and the acrid smoke of the wounded lantern-thing, and she fought the way the hermit had taught her to pray: without elegance, without hope of glory, simply refusing to stop.

An acolyte's fist caught her temple and the world rang. She went down to one knee, tasted blood, and through the blur saw Maren Voss step forward past her—not fleeing, not hiding—standing over her fallen defender with her glowing arm raised like a lamp, and the acolytes faltered, actually faltered, because whatever lived in that healed flesh looked back at them and it was not afraid.

"Eleven years of prayers," Maren said to them, to all of them, her voice carrying down the stairwell into the street. "Yours too. Your mothers' too. All of it is in here, and it is *not yours to take*."

And the whole tenement seemed to answer. Doors opened above and below—neighbors, unmarked, unarmed, drawn by the light and the voice, filling the stairwell with bodies that had no business being brave and were brave anyway. Odile's quiet resistance, arriving not as soldiers but as a streetful of ordinary people who had finally run out of patience.

In the chaos, in the smoke, Sera got her feet under her and her knife back in her hand, and she laughed—once, breathlessly, at the sheer improbable rightness of it.

Because below, cutting through the crowd, running up Corvane Street with his coat flying and terror and hope fighting openly on his face, came Xanthe Galloway—and behind him, unmistakable even at this distance, even through smoke and lamplight, the air itself was bending toward the tenement like iron filings toward a magnet, as if something vast and silent and *real* had begun, at long last, to answer.
