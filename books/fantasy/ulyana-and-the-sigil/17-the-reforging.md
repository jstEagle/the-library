## 17. The Reforging

The undercroft had not changed since the night they first broke into it, except that now it knew them. That was the only way Xanthe could describe it. The old stones seemed to lean inward as he descended the stair with the lead tablets wrapped in oilcloth against his chest, as if the room remembered his trespass and had decided, after everything, to forgive it.

Odile had done what she promised. The resistance—her word for it, though it was mostly a ragged company of marked and unmarked who had chosen, in one terrible week, to stop pretending—had cleared the approach streets and doused every lamp from the Wardmarket to the river. Vask went dark around the ruined chapel district like a held breath. Somewhere above, Sera walked the perimeter with a knife, no magic at all, just her and her certainty, and Xanthe tried not to think about how thin that line was between them and nothing.

He set the four shards on the altar slab where the pre-Outlawing priests had once spoken names aloud.

They were ugly things, honestly. Lead tablets cracked like river ice, each one crusted with ten centuries of grime, their inscriptions worn to suggestion. Anyone looking at them would have seen junk. But when he unwrapped the oilcloth, the air in the undercroft changed pressure—the way it did before summer storms, before a blade came down—and the grime began to glow faintly from underneath, gold bleeding through gray like dawn through shuttered windows.

The first shard hummed. The second answered it. The third, pried from a drowned chapel while a revenant priest wept over his hands, sang a single low note that Xanthe felt in his back teeth. And the fourth—

The fourth was not lead at all. It was Maren.

She sat on the steps below the altar where they'd brought her, wrapped in Odile's good coat, her healed body luminous in the shard-light. The widow whose prayer he'd failed to answer, whose healing had come instead from the thing wearing Ulyana's face—and who had carried, all unknowing, a living fragment of the true name inside her mended bones. When the counterfeit's cultists had come for her two nights ago, Sera had held the tenement stair alone with a knife and a barricade of furniture and pure furious ordinary courage, long enough for Odile's people to move her through the cellars.

"I still don't understand why me," Maren said. Her voice was steady. She'd been steady ever since the stair; Xanthe suspected she would be steady until she died, whenever that was, and he envied it.

"Because you were healed truly," he said. "It used stolen power, but it used it *right*. That's the crack in its armor. It can't help doing some real work along with the theft." He touched the glove over his covenant mark. "And because Ulyana hid her name where only faith would find it. Not in vaults. In people."

Maren looked at the three lead shards glowing on the altar, then at him. "And you're the fourth piece."

"I'm the fourth piece," Xanthe said, and pulled off his glove.

His covenant mark had never been beautiful—a sigil burned into the meat of his left palm the day he swore himself, ten years and a lifetime ago, back when Ulyana's voice was as constant as weather. Through the Silence it had stayed warm. That warmth had been his whole secret life: proof, on the worst nights, that he hadn't imagined her. Now, in the presence of the shards, it burned like a coal raked from a fire, and the skin around it glowed the same buried gold.

"Xanthe." Odile's voice from the stairhead, low. "Wardline's set. Sera says the streets are empty. You have until it notices, and no one knows how long that is."

"Then we don't wait for it to notice." He climbed to the altar. "Maren—I need you close. Not touching the shards. Just close. Your part is to be found. The rest is mine."

* * *

The ritual was older than the Outlawing, older than Vask, older than the city's whole gray architecture of fear. He had pieced it together from the undercroft archives, from the revenant priest's broken recitations, from the hermit on the traitor's road who had been high priest in the years when gods still walked openly—and from one more source he did not say aloud even to himself: the counterfeit itself, which had shown him, in its cathedral of stitched divinity, exactly how a name could be assembled. Its arrogance had been its confession. It had wanted him to see how it was made, certain he would despair. Instead he had taken notes.

He laid his left hand flat on the altar beside the first shard.

"Ulyana," he said—not the name, not yet, just the handle of it, the word mortals used the way you'd knock on a door rather than walk through it. "I'm going to say your true name now. Or the pieces of it I have. Forgive me the stammering."

The first shard slid toward his hand. Not rolled—*slid*, against the tilt of the stone, and where it met his skin the lead softened like wax and sank into the mark. Pain came a half-second later, white and total, and Xanthe rode it the way he'd learned to ride pain in twenty years of mercenary work: don't fight it, don't flee it, be the ground it falls on.

When he could see again, the sigil on his palm had changed. A stroke of the true name now lived in it, written in light under his skin.

"One," he gasped.

The second shard crossed the altar to him faster, eager, and the pain of its joining was worse because now there were two voices in the name and they harmonized, and harmony, he discovered, hurt more than dissonance. Something about being right. His knees hit the floor. Maren started forward and Odile caught her arm at the base of the steps.

"Let him—" Odile said, and her voice cracked on the rest.

"Two." Blood ran from beneath his fingernails, glowing. "Two."

The third shard took longest. It had spent centuries underwater and it carried the drowned chapel's grief in it, and when it merged with the mark Xanthe saw—not visions, exactly, but *weather*: a storm of memory that belonged to someone else. A battlefield going silent mid-scream. Ten thousand prayers hitting a closed door. Her final words falling through the dark like a coin down a well: *If I do not answer, do not look for me.*

*I'm looking anyway,* he told the memory. *You knew I would. That's why you said it.*

"Three."

The undercroft was full of light now, gold pouring up through the flagstones, and above them—muffled by earth and stone—he heard Vask begin to scream.

* * *

It came early. Of course it came early. Odile had warned him: *the shards are bait, and bait works both ways.* The counterfeit had felt the first shard join and had torn across the city like a storm front with intent.

Through the undercroft's high broken vents, Xanthe heard the wards go. Not break—wards didn't break anymore, not the ones Odile's people had woven—but go *silent*, one ring after another, like bells being smothered by hand. Then the sound of the Wardmarket's clock tower folding. Then a pressure on the world so vast and wrong that the candles guttered flat and Maren cried out and clutched Odile's arm, and even the shards on the altar dimmed as if ducking.

Then the voice, everywhere, coming up through the stones themselves, wearing a warmth it had stolen:

*Xanthe Galloway. My faithful one. You've gathered my name so beautifully. Bring it home.*

"You're not home," Xanthe said through his teeth. Sweat and blood dripped from his left hand onto the altar. "Home is a door you knock on. You're a thief standing in a doorway you cut into the wall."

*I offered you her voice.* The warmth curdled, sweetened again, curdled. *I offer it still. Every prayer she ever heard, recorded, perfect, replayable forever. She abandoned you. I have never once been silent.*

"That's the difference," Xanthe said. "Silence can end."

The ceiling split.

Stone and dust and a hundred years of pigeon nests came down in a slow avalanche that never landed—it hung, suspended, turning gently in the gold light, because the thing descending through the breach had stopped caring about physics the way a fire stops caring about the shape of the candle. It poured into the undercroft like smoke given ambition: a vast stitched silhouette, wings made of borrowed miracle, and across the whole heaving surface of it, repeated a thousand times, ten thousand times, Ulyana's sigil—similar, wrong, drawn by something that had only ever seen the mark from outside.

It reached for the altar. For him. For the three-quarters of a goddess burning in his hand.

And Maren stood up.

She didn't shout. She didn't glow. She simply stepped between the descending dark and the altar, an unmarked woman in a borrowed coat, and said, in the voice of someone who had survived a bad marriage and a plague and an impostor god's healing, "No."

The counterfeit paused. It had eaten faith for a decade and here was faith in its plainest form, unarmed, unreasonable, and it hesitated like a man hesitating at a door he suddenly isn't sure he has the right to open.

One heartbeat. Two.

"Xanthe," Odile shouted from the stairs, "whatever you're going to do—"

He put his bare hand over the place where the fourth shard should be, over his own hammering heart, and spoke the three joined strokes of the true name aloud.

The world answered with a silence deeper than the Silence had ever been—and in that silence, far away and very near, something turned over in its sleep and began, slowly, to wake.

The counterfeit screamed and came down on them all.
