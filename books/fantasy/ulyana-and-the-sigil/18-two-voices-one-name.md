## 18. Two Voices, One Name

The undercroft had not been built for this.

Xanthe felt it in the stones themselves—a groan that lived below hearing, a tremor in the mortar like the shudder of a dog before a storm it cannot name. The lead tablets lay arranged on the altar-slab in the pattern Sera had traced from the hermit's memory: three shards of Ulyana's true name, each humming at a different pitch, and above them his own bare hand, glove discarded, covenant-mark exposed to the dark.

The fourth shard was coming. The widow Marta was coming, escorted by Odile and six of the quiet resistance, moving through streets where every bell in Vask had begun to ring without hands to ring them.

And something else was coming too. Something that did not bother with streets.

Xanthe pressed his palm flat against the first tablet and began the words the hermit had taught him—the old framing of the rite, the covenant spoken as it had been spoken before the Silence, before the Outlawing, back when gods and mortals still negotiated face to face. The syllables tasted of iron and honey. The mark on his hand woke like a hearth relit after a long winter, warmth climbing his wrist, his arm, pooling behind his sternum where ten years of unanswered prayer had left a hollow shaped exactly like her voice.

*Ulyana,* he said—not aloud, not yet, but in the place where prayer lived. *I have your name. Come and take it.*

The candles guttered. Every one of them, all at once, the way they had flared in the wardmarket on the night the gods began answering again—and then they went out entirely, and the darkness that remained was not empty.

It was full of teeth.

"You brought it *here*," Sera breathed beside him. She had her knife out, which was a joke, and they both knew it, and she held it anyway because some part of being human was holding the knife. "Xanthe—you called it here."

"I called *her*," he said. "The other one just follows."

---

It came through the floor.

Not through the door, not down the stair, but up through the stone itself, the way water rises through earth, the way rot rises through timber. The darkness above the altar thickened, gathered, took a shape Xanthe knew now—had seen it plain in the counterfeit's cathedral and would see it until he died. It wore Ulyana's silhouette the way a thief wears a stolen coat: correctly, in every seam, and yet somehow hanging wrong on what filled it. Her height. Her grace. The suggestion of antlers that her icons gave her, branches of the world-tree she had been said to hold. But the face was smooth as an egg, featureless, waiting to be told what to be, and its edges frayed into threads—hundreds of threads, thousands, fine as spider silk, running up through the ceiling, out into the city, into the mouths and hearts of everyone who had prayed to it and been answered.

Stitched divinity. A god made of other gods' scraps, wearing her sigil burned backwards on a breast it had grown fat feeding.

"*Little merchant*," it said, and its voice was Ulyana's voice, and that was the cruelest thing it had ever done to him. Ten years he had ached for that voice. He had dreamed it. He had woken with tears in his mouth from dreams of it. And here it was, perfect, warm, amused in exactly the old way—and it came out of a thing with no face. "*You gathered my name for me. How faithful. How very like you.*"

"It isn't yours," Xanthe said. His voice cracked on the second word and he let it crack. He was done performing steadiness. "You're a leak. You're what slipped through the seal before it closed. She shut the door and you were the draft."

"And yet." The shape drifted closer, and the threads at its edges stirred, and somewhere far above, in the city, Xanthe heard screaming begin—thin, distant, the sound of the marked feeling their false sigils ignite. Sera's hand flew to her own forearm, where the faded burn of her dissolved mark sat, and her face went white. "*I answer,*" the counterfeit said. "*When she was silent, I answered. When her faithful starved, I fed them. Ask the widow whose bones I mended. Ask the preacher whose tongue I loosened. Names are just handles, little merchant. Worship is the only weight a name must bear—and mine has borne ten years of it while hers bore nothing.*"

"That's not—" Sera started.

"*Hold your tongue, clerk.*" It didn't look at her. It never looked at anyone but him. That, too, was theft; that, too, was Ulyana's habit, the old fierce focus of her attention, the sense of being the only person alive in the room. "*You want her back. I can hear it in you, in the hollow you've carried like a stone in your chest. Give me the shards and I will wear her wholly. I will speak with her voice forever. You will have your goddess back, Xanthe Galloway. You will kneel at evening prayers and hear her bless you, and neither of us will ever have to admit she is gone.*"

And there it was. The offer underneath all the offers. Not power. Not salvation.

Comfort. The comfortable lie, warm as a hearth, asking only that he stop asking questions.

His hands were shaking. He looked down at the tablets—at the three shards of her true name, humming against the lead like birds against a window—and he thought: *I could say yes. I could have her back tonight. I would even know, and I could choose not to know, and the knowing would fade, everything fades—*

The undercroft door banged open.

Marta came through it first, which was wrong, because Odile was supposed to be in front—but Odile was bleeding from the scalp and half-carrying the widow, and behind them came the resistance, and behind *them*, filling the stairwell, came light. Not torchlight. Not the counterfeit's greasy glow. This was the light of the first hour of the world, the light the old hymns promised waited behind the veil, and it moved down the stairs like something patient that had been walking a very long way.

The counterfeit turned toward it, and for the first time, the stitched thing sounded afraid.

"*She's not strong enough,*" it hissed. "*Ten years sealed behind her own door, starving on nothing, rationing herself to whispers—she is a candle and I am a bonfire. She cannot take the name from you.*"

"No," said a voice from the light, and Xanthe's knees nearly went. Because it was *her*—not the counterfeit's perfect forgery but the real grain of it, rougher, wearier, threaded through with ten years of silence like a river threaded with ice. "He's right. I can't take it."

Ulyana stepped into the undercroft, and she was smaller than her icons. That was the first thing. Smaller, and gaunter, and dressed in traveling gray like any pilgrim on any road, with dust on her hem and exhaustion carved around her eyes. The antlers were there, but stunted, bare of leaves, winter-brittle. She looked at Xanthe across the altar, across the shards of her own name, and her expression was not triumph.

It was apology.

"But he can give it," she said. "If he chooses."

---

The counterfeit struck first.

There was no other word for it. The stitched shape lunged—not at Ulyana, not at Sera or the widow or the bleeding, stubborn fence clutching her knife, but at Xanthe, at the mark on his hand, and the threads of it whipped out ahead of the body like the tentacles of some deep-sea horror, and they *caught him*. They sank into his skin at wrist and elbow and throat and temple, and the world inverted.

He was standing in the wardmarket again, ten years ago, the day the candles flared. He was kneeling at his mother's grave with Ulyana's last words still ringing: *If I do not answer, do not look for me.* He was in the cathedral, and the counterfeit wore her face now—it had taken it from his memories, it was wearing his *memories*—and it smiled with her smile and said, *come home, come home, you kept the faith, you deserve your reward.*

"No," Xanthe said, through teeth, through the fire of the threads in his flesh.

*You are tired,* it said, in her voice, in her tenderness. *You have been tired for ten years. Let me carry it. Let me be her. No one will ever know. Not even you.*

And beneath him, around him, through him, he felt the other presence arrive—Ulyana, real Ulyana, pressing against the counterfeit's grip like dawn against a shuttered window, and her voice came not into his ears but into the hollow behind his sternum, the hollow shaped like her:

*Xanthe. Listen to me. It holds you because you hold the name alone. That was always the flaw. That was why the old covenant broke.*

*What?* He could barely think. The counterfeit was pouring comfort into him like hot wine, sweet and numbing, and some traitorous part of him wanted to drink.

*The covenant was never meant to be one voice commanding another. That is what the gods became—that is why I sealed the veil. We grew fat on obedience. Prayer became a leash and worship became a chain, and we told ourselves it was love.* Her presence flared, grief-colored. *The covenant was meant to be spoken jointly. God names the faithful; the faithful name the god back. Two voices, one name. Neither complete without the other. I sealed myself away because I could not make them listen—and I left you the words, little merchant, all these years, in the rites you performed for strangers' children. You have been speaking your half of the covenant since the night you blessed that girl in the alley. You simply never knew.*

The counterfeit screamed. It was not a sound any throat should make; it was the sound of a business model failing, of a lie meeting arithmetic. *He is MINE—he prayed to ME—I answered HIM—*

*You answered what you found,* Ulyana said, cold now, cold as the seal, cold as ten years of deliberate silence. *You found a man who loved a god. You mistook the love for a handle. But the love was never yours to pull.*

Xanthe hung in the grip of the threads, and the choice stood before him the way a doorway stands in a burning house.

He could surrender the name. The counterfeit would wear Ulyana forever, and he would have his goddess back, the way a mourner has a portrait back. Warm. Dead. Perfect.

Or—

Or the covenant was a dialogue. Or devotion was not obedience. Or the whole architecture of divine and mortal, the hierarchy the counterfeit had grown fat inside, the hierarchy Ulyana had burned the world's magic to escape—all of it rested on one assumption: that the faithful exist to speak the god's name, and never the reverse.

He opened his eyes.

"Sera," he rasped. His throat was full of thread-fire. "The widow. Get Marta to the altar."

Sera was already moving. She hauled the widow forward—Marta, gray-haired, terrified, healed of a decade of pain by a hungry god and carrying, unknowing, a living fragment of Ulyana's true name in her mended bones—and pressed the woman's hand to the fourth position on the slab. The tablet-space blazed. Four shards, three lead and one living, and between them the empty center where the assembled name wanted to be whole.

"Xanthe," Ulyana said, and her voice shook, and gods should not shake, and he loved her more for it. "It will hurt. It will hurt *you*, because you will be the bridge. Every word passes through the speaker."

"I've been a bridge for ten years," he said. "I sold charms that didn't work and prayed anyway. This is just the same job with better pay."

He put both hands on the assembled name.

---

The counterfeit poured everything it had into him then—every stolen prayer, every borrowed miracle, ten years of harvested faith compressed into a single scream of *mine, mine, mine*—and Xanthe walked through it. He walked through his own exhaustion, his own homesickness, the terrible seductive warmth of the comfortable lie, and he came out the other side into the cold clean air of truth, and truth was this:

He did not miss a goddess. He missed *her*. The specific, particular, argumentative, tender, flawed woman who had gone silent rather than become what the counterfeit already was. And you do not resurrect someone by accepting a puppet in their clothes. You do not honor love by agreeing to be deceived.

"Ulyana," he said.

And then he spoke her name.

Not the fragments on the tablets—those were the scaffolding, the grammar, the bones. He spoke the name the way the covenant demanded: jointly. He took the shards' meaning into his mouth and gave it back to her changed, marked by ten years of his keeping, seasoned with every unanswered prayer and every small forbidden rite and every child in the alleys of Vask he had blessed without hope of answer. *Here is your name as I have held it,* he said. *Broken, and kept anyway. Now take it, and tell me what it sounds like from your side.*

The counterfeit shrieked and *pulled*—and for one endless moment two voices contended through Xanthe's body, through his blood, through the mark on his hand, each claiming the name he held. He felt himself stretched between them like a rope in a tug-of-war between heaven and its forgery. The candles relit and guttered and relit. The lead tablets ran like wax. Marta wept and laughed at once. Somewhere above, the bells of Vask rang so hard that towers began to shed their bells like trees shedding fruit.

And then—

Then Ulyana spoke her own name back to him.

That was the completion. That was the hinge on which the whole old design had turned and been forgotten: not a god naming her servant, not a servant reciting his god, but the name passing between them, each giving it, each receiving it, each *changing* it slightly, the way any word changes slightly in any mouth, until the name belonged to both or neither—to the space between them, to the covenant itself, which had never been a chain at all but a conversation that two parties agreed to keep having.

The counterfeit lost its grip.

Not gradually. All at once, the way a fever breaks. The threads tore out of Xanthe's flesh and he dropped to his knees on the altar-steps, gasping, and heard behind him the stitched god's scream change pitch—from rage to confusion to something horribly like a child's bewilderment—as the name it had claimed, the name it had worn, the name it had *been*, slid out of its possession and into the dialogue it could never join, because it had never once asked anyone anything. It had only ever answered. Answering was all it knew how to do.

"*Wait,*" it said, in Ulyana's voice, and then, trying others, in a hundred voices, in the widow's voice, in Odile's, in Sera's, in *his*— "*wait, wait, I kept them alive, I healed them, I ANSWERED—*"

"Yes," Ulyana said quietly. She stood over the fraying thing with an expression Xanthe would remember the rest of his life: not hatred. Pity, edged with recognition. "So did I, once. Until answering became all I was. That is the hunger, little brother. That is the seal. You are what I would have become if I had stayed."

The counterfeit reached for her with its thousand threads, and the threads passed through her like moonlight through a window, catching nothing, and the great stitched shape began to unravel from the edges inward, thread by thread by thread, each stolen filament drifting loose, seeking—Xanthe watched them go with his heart in his throat—seeking the people they had been taken from. Up through the stone. Out into the city. Going home.

The last thing the counterfeit did, before it was only a scatter of dimming light and a smell like burnt sugar, was look at Xanthe with the smooth egg of its face, and say—in his own voice, the final theft, or perhaps the first honest thing it had ever offered him—

"*You could have had her back.*"

"I know," Xanthe said. "That's why I couldn't."

The light went out. The real light—the gray, ordinary, honest light of pre-dawn Vask—seeped down the stairwell, and the undercroft was only an undercroft again, scorched and cracked and smelling of ozone and lead, with four dead tablets on a broken altar and a goddess standing in the ruins, looking at her last faithful servant with an expression he had never seen on her before.

Hesitation.

"Xanthe," Ulyana said. "We should talk about terms."

Behind him, Sera made a sound that might have been a laugh or a sob. Odile, still bleeding, still upright, said distinctly: "Oh, I'm going to want to hear this."

Xanthe got to his feet. His hands were burned, his mark was smoking faintly, and ten years of ache had just ended in the strangest way possible—not with a restoration, but with a negotiation he hadn't yet begun.

"Yeah," he said. "Yeah, we should."

* * *

They climbed the stairs together into a city holding its breath. Above ground, dawn was breaking over Vask, and everywhere the false marks were fading from flesh like frost off glass, and people were spilling into the streets blinking, touching their own arms, staring at the sky as if expecting it to speak.

In a way, Xanthe thought, it just had.

Ulyana paused at the top of the stair, small and gray-eyed and winter-bare-antlered, and looked out at the waking city—her city, their city, the city that had kept faith with a silence—and when she spoke again, her voice carried the first note of something that was not weariness that he had heard from her in a decade.

"Now," she said. "About this partnership you're going to propose. I have conditions."

Xanthe grinned, despite everything, despite the burns and the exhaustion and the corpse-smell of an unraveling god still clinging to his clothes.

"So do I," he said. "And the first one's going to make you laugh."

It did.
