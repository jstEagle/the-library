## 19. The Unmaking of the Counterfeit

Sera felt it before she saw it—a loosening, like a knot slipping in her chest.

She was on the cathedral steps when the true name finished speaking itself through Xanthe's body, three streets away and yet everywhere at once, because that was the nature of names. The sound had no sound to it. It arrived instead as pressure, as the moment before a sneeze held forever, as the hush in a room when someone important enters. The crowd around her—cultists in their gray-and-gold, inquisitors who had defected or been co-opted or simply stood confused with their warrants still in their hands—all of them went still at once, like birds hearing a hawk.

Then the counterfeit screamed.

It was not a sound she heard so much as one she *was*. Her mark flared white-hot beneath her collarbone, and for one vertiginous instant Sera understood what the thing wearing Ulyana's sigil actually was: a loom of stolen thread, ten years of answered prayers woven into something that could walk and preach and heal, every miracle a strand pulled from the faith of people who had believed they were being loved. She had fed it. She had knelt in its pews. She had felt its warmth spread through her ribs on cold mornings and called it comfort.

The scream went on and on, and the cathedral began to come apart.

Not collapse—*unravel*. That was the only word for it. The great gold-leafed doors, which had drunk candlelight like a living mouth, dulled to ordinary wood. The spires, which Sera had privately thought looked like needles poised over the city's skin, lost their terrible luster and became merely tall. And through every window poured light—but not the honeyed, cloying glow the false god had favored. This was clean and cold and silver, the color of moonlight on snow, the color, she realized with a jolt, of the shard Xanthe had carried into the undercroft. Ulyana's light. The real one.

The crowd did not flee. That was the strangest part. They stood in the unraveling light and wept, or laughed, or clutched strangers' hands, and Sera understood why: they could feel their healing staying. She could see it happening—the lame man from the third pew flexing his mended leg as if he didn't trust it; the fevered child blinking up at her mother with clear eyes; the burn-scarred fishwife turning her smooth hands over and over. Whatever the counterfeit had stolen to build itself, whatever it had taken from them, the healings themselves had been real. Only the claim behind them was false.

*Keep your healing,* said a voice that was not a voice, vast and tired and gentle, and Sera knew—even having never heard her—that this was Ulyana. *Only the debt is forgiven.*

The mark on Sera's chest grew warm. Then warmer. Then—

It did not hurt. That was what undid her. She had braced for pain, for tearing, for the sensation of being flayed of something that had lived under her skin for half a year. Instead the sigil dissolved the way frost dissolves off a window when the sun finds it: quietly, completely, leaving nothing but clarity behind. She felt it go as a fading warmth, a hand lifting from her shoulder, and then there was nothing there at all. No weight. No whisper. No low approving hum when she obeyed, no cold prickle when she doubted.

Nothing there but herself.

Sera put her hand flat against her collarbone and pressed, as if she might find it hiding, might feel the raised ridge of it one last time. Smooth skin. Her own heartbeat, quick and entirely hers.

"Oh," she said aloud, to no one. "Oh, that's what quiet sounds like."

She hadn't known how loud it had been. Six months of the thing's presence, murmuring approval, tugging her toward sermons, warming when she knelt and cooling when she questioned—and beneath all that, the older noise, the inquisition's catechisms drummed into her since girlhood: *the gods are gone, the gods are dangerous, faith is a wound the realm cannot afford.* She had traded one claiming for another and told herself the second was freedom because it asked her to surrender willingly. Now both voices were gone, and in the ringing silence left behind, Sera discovered she had never once, in her entire life, been alone inside her own head.

She should have wept. She thought she might. But what came instead was laughter—small, startled, inappropriate—and then a lightness so profound it frightened her, the way open sky frightens people who have lived their whole lives under a roof. She was not claimed. Not by the counterfeit, not by the inquisition, not by the true goddess whose covenant now rang through the stones of Vask like a struck bell. Ulyana was back, whole and awake, and Sera felt absolutely nothing pulling her toward her. No summons. No warmth seeking purchase in her chest. Just an absence, and in the absence, a strange and total permission to be exactly one ordinary woman standing on cathedral steps in the morning cold.

She found she didn't mourn the mark. She turned the feeling over, examining it for guilt the way she'd once examined documents for forgery, and found none. Grief would have meant the thing had given her something true. It hadn't. Even her belonging in its congregation had been manufactured—a hunger for family, skillfully farmed. What she felt now wasn't loss. It was the lightness of a debt discharged she'd never agreed to owe.

* * *

The cathedral took three days to empty.

Sera stayed, though no one ordered her to and no one paid her to. Old habits: a clerk goes where the records are, and the records here were people. She moved through the gutted nave with a borrowed stylus and loose sheets, taking down names—not for warrants, not for purges, just names, and next to each name what they needed. The widow Maren wanted to know if her healed lungs would last. The boy Tam, who had swept the altar, wanted to know if he was damned. An old chorister sat in the rubble of the choir loft and asked Sera, again and again, whether twenty years of prayers to the wrong god counted for nothing.

"No," Sera told him, and watched his face crumple, and then finished: "They count for everything. You prayed to what you thought was Ulyana. The love was real even if the ear it landed on wasn't." She wrote *chorister, grief, needs work to do* on her sheet, and later found him hauling stone with the repair crews, weeping and singing at the same time.

On the second day, Odile came through with a ledger under her arm and ink-stained fingers, surveying the nave like a fence appraising a warehouse.

"You're the inquisitor's clerk," Odile said. It wasn't quite an accusation. "The one who ran."

"The one who stole the map of every marked person in Vask and gave it to the resistance," Sera said. "Yes."

Odile's grin was quick and crooked. "Then you're the reason half my people are still breathing." She tapped her ledger. "I'm making lists too. Who was fed on, who fed willingly, who needs watching. Difference is mine ends up in a court that might actually be fair, now the magistracy's been cleaned out." She glanced sideways at Sera. "You could work for us. Someone has to write the new laws. Someone who knows exactly how the old ones were used."

"I don't know what I am yet," Sera said. "Give me a few days of not belonging to anything."

"Take a year," Odile said. "Door's open." And she was gone, back into the crowd, a woman already building the future out of salvage.

On the third day, Xanthe came.

He looked hollowed out and lit from within at once, the way Sera imagined a wick looks after the candle's first lighting—consumed and burning both. His right hand was bare. Where his covenant-mark had lived for fifteen years, she could see only pale new skin, tender-looking, like flesh after a scab falls away. He caught her looking and flexed his fingers, almost puzzled by them.

"It doesn't itch anymore," he said, by way of greeting. "Fifteen years, and I'd forgotten what it was like to have a hand that was just a hand."

"Does it feel like losing something?"

He considered this with the seriousness he gave everything. "It feels like setting something down that I was carrying so long I mistook it for my arm." He looked up at the ruined nave, at the shafts of honest daylight falling through the broken roof where the counterfeit's glory had been. "She offered to restore it. The mark, the bond, all of it. I said no."

"So I heard. Partnership." Sera almost smiled. "A mercenary negotiating terms with a goddess."

"She laughed first. That helped." He turned to her then, and his eyes were kind and tired. "And you? How does it feel? Being unmarked?"

Sera opened her mouth to give the clerk's answer, the tidy report-answer, and found she couldn't. What came out instead was quieter and truer.

"Light," she said. "Like I've been carrying a passenger I never invited, and it got off without saying goodbye, and I keep waiting to miss it. And I don't. Is that terrible? Everyone else in this building is grieving something, even if it was a lie. I just feel—" she searched for the word— "*unclaimed.*"

Xanthe shook his head slowly. "That's not terrible. That might be the healthiest thing anyone's said to me in ten years." He hesitated, then reached into his coat and drew out a small object: a charm, plain wooden bead on a leather cord, the kind he'd sold by the thousand in the wardmarket. "I'm giving these away now. They work—small ones, small workings, blessings against fever, luck for travelers. Nothing that replaces a life. That's the rule. Anyone who wants a god instead of a life gets turned away." He held it out. "This one's for clear thinking. Seemed fitting."

Sera took it. It was warm in her palm, faintly, the way a stone is warm that has sat in sunlight—not alive, not speaking, just *blessed*, touched at second hand by something real. She slipped the cord over her head and felt the bead settle against her collarbone, where the sigil had been, and it weighed nothing at all.

"Thank you," she said.

"Don't thank me. Thank her. Or don't—she's not collecting gratitude anymore either, near as I can tell." He smiled, thin and genuine. "New terms. Everything's new terms now."

* * *

She went back to the tenement district on the fourth day, to the widow Maren's street, because the list in her head still had entries and a clerk finishes her work. The city was transformed in ways both large and small. Inquisition banners had come down from the wardhouses; some hung folded in windows as rags, others burned in the gutters. In the market, a herb-woman was openly charging two coppers for a fever-charm, and no enforcers came. Children chalked spirals on doorsteps—the old sign of Ulyana, Sera realized, the true one, the one that predated the counterfeit's stolen sigil—and mothers let them.

Maren met her at the door with bread and strong tea and no ceremony, which was exactly what Sera hadn't known she needed.

"They're saying the widow from the beginning will be the first priestess," Maren said, settling across from her. "Of the new covenant. Can you imagine? Me. I couldn't read until last winter."

"Can you now?"

"Maren's teaching me. The widow." Maren laughed into her tea. "The whole city's gone mad with kindness. It won't last. People are people." She studied Sera over the rim of her cup. "But some of it will stick. You can tell, with buildings and with cities—you can tell what's load-bearing." She nodded at Sera's chest. "And you? Still feeling lighter than air?"

Sera touched the wooden bead through her shirt. "I keep waiting for the other shoe. For something to walk in and claim me again. The inquisition did it for twenty years. The thing in the cathedral did it for six months. I've apparently got a sign over my head that says *vacant, please occupy*."

"And?"

"And nobody's come." Sera looked down at her tea. "There's a goddess awake in the world again, closer than she's been in a decade, and I can't feel her at all. And do you know what I've decided that means?" She looked up. "It means she doesn't want me. Not as property, not as vessel, not as faithful. Maybe that's the whole point of what happened in that undercroft. Maybe the covenant she died ten years to protect was one where nobody gets claimed unless they ask."

Maren was quiet a moment. Then: "My husband used to say the worst prisons are the ones with comfortable cells." She refilled Sera's cup. "You walked out of yours twice. Third time's the charm, as our friend the charm-seller would say—for actual money, once upon a time."

They both laughed, and it was easy, and Sera sat in the warm kitchen of a woman she'd helped save and felt the strangest thing of all: not purpose, not calling, not destiny. Just the ordinary weight of an afternoon, hers alone, with nowhere she was required to be and no voice in her chest telling her otherwise.

She stayed until the light went amber. Walking home through streets that smelled of woodsmoke and frying onions, past doorsteps chalked with true spirals, past the gutted cathedral where repair crews sang work songs under the honest moon, Sera realized she was humming. She couldn't remember the last time she'd hummed.

At her lodging, a note had been slid under the door. Plain paper, no seal, a hand she recognized—Odile's quick slanted script:

*Court convenes Monday to sort out who served freely and who was fed on. Need someone to run the records. Someone the marked will trust. Pay is bad, hours are worse, and you'd belong to something again—but this time you'd get a vote.*

Sera stood holding it a long while in the doorway, the evening cool on her face, the little charm warm at her throat.

Belonging, but with a vote. A claim she could refuse. She thought of the frost dissolving off glass, of the lightness that still hadn't faded, of a goddess who had starved rather than own her faithful—and of a city full of people learning, clumsily, painfully, to choose.

She picked up the stylus lying on her table, tested its weight in her hand like a sword she might finally be glad to carry, and began to write her answer.
