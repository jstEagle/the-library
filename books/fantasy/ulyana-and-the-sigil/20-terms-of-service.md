## 20. Terms of Service

The undercroft was quiet in the way a battlefield is quiet after the last arrow falls—not peace, but the held breath of everything that had survived.

Xanthe sat on the cold lead floor with his back against a pillar that had been old when Vask was a fishing village, and he tried to remember how to be a body. His hands shook. They had been shaking since the counterfeit came apart above him like a tapestry cut from its frame, thread by golden thread, and since Ulyana's name—her *true* name, four shards and one scarred mercenary's mark assembled into something that sang—had gone out through him and into the world like light through water.

His covenant-mark was gone. He kept touching the place on his left forearm where it had lived for fifteen years, first as a brand of binding, then as a warm coal he'd hidden beneath a glove through ten years of silence, and now as nothing at all. Just skin. Just the ordinary, unclaimed flesh of a man who belonged to no god.

It should have felt like freedom. It felt like an amputation.

Above him, through the broken vault where the ceiling had opened during the reforging, dawn was coming down in shafts. Dust turned gold in the light. Somewhere far off, muffled by stone, Vask was waking—and not the way it had woken for a decade, gray and careful and listening for boots. Bells were ringing. Not the inquisition's alarm bells; actual bells, temple bells that hadn't swung in ten years, rung by hands that had no idea yet what they were celebrating but were celebrating anyway, because every candle in every window across the city had lit itself at midnight and stayed lit.

Sera had gone up an hour ago to see to the widow—to Marta, whose healed body had carried the fourth shard all this time without knowing it—and to stand between her and whatever remnants of the cult still had knives and grievances. Odile was somewhere in the streets doing what Odile did, which was turning chaos into organization before the chaos finished happening. Xanthe had been given the only task anyone thought he could manage: sit down, breathe, and don't die of it.

He was failing at the third part when she arrived.

There was no thunder. No descent of light, no choir, none of the theater the counterfeit had loved so much—the stitched god had been all spectacle, because spectacle was how you fed on faith from a distance. What came instead was simpler and infinitely more terrible: the air in the undercroft grew *warm*, the way a room grows warm when someone who loves you enters it, and the dust in the dawn-shadows began to fall upward, gently, like snow remembering the sky.

Then she was standing in the ruined nave, and Xanthe forgot how lungs worked.

She was not what the statues showed. The statues had made her tall and stern and remote, a woman carved by men who feared her. She was of middling height, broad-shouldered, with the weathered look of someone who had walked a great many roads, and her hair was the brown of river water and her eyes were the gray-green of the sea over shallows—the exact color, Xanthe realized with a jolt, of the sigil that had burned on his arm for fifteen years. She wore no regalia. She wore traveling clothes, dusty at the hem, and she looked at him the way you look at a fire you thought had gone out and find one ember still glowing under the ash.

"Hello, Xanthe," said Ulyana.

He meant to say something dignified. He had rehearsed things, in the strange lucid moments after the counterfeit unraveled—rehearsed accusations, rehearsed forgiveness, rehearsed the speech Sera had beaten into him on the traitor's road: *you get to ask questions now, that's the whole point.* What came out instead was:

"You're shorter than I imagined."

Something moved across her face—surprise, and beneath it a grief so old it had become geology—and then, astonishingly, the corner of her mouth lifted. "You imagined me at all?"

"Every day." His voice cracked on the second word. He got up off the floor because kneeling felt wrong and standing felt impossible, so he ended up somewhere in between, swaying, gripping the pillar. "Ten years. Every single day. You want to know what I imagined? I imagined you dead. I imagined you never existed. I imagined—" He stopped. Breathed. "I imagined you'd left us. That we weren't worth answering."

The warmth in the air flickered, like a candle in a draft.

"I know," she said quietly. "I heard all of it. Every prayer. Do you understand what that was like? To hear ten years of prayers and be forbidden to answer?" She took a step toward him, and he saw that her hands were trembling too, which undid something in his chest that had been clenched since chapter one of his life. "You think the silence cost you. It did. But I was on the other side of the wall, Xanthe, pressing my ear against it."

"The seal." The words tasted like ash and revelation both. "You sealed the veil and starved yourself along with the rest of them."

"There was no other way. They had grown—" She searched for the word and hated each candidate. "Hungry is too kind. The gods of that age had stopped being worshipped and started being *fed*. Faith was no longer devotion; it was livestock. And I could feel it happening in myself, that hunger, that pull to take instead of receive. So I built the seal, and I put myself inside it, and I gave my faithful one instruction." Her eyes closed. "If I do not answer, do not look for me."

"And I looked for you anyway."

"Yes." She opened her eyes. "Thank the dark and the deep and every star that doesn't have a name—you looked for me anyway. Because the thing that slipped through before the seal closed, the counterfeit—it needed a key. Four shards of my true name, gathered by a hand that loved me enough to bleed for them. If you had obeyed me, it would have found another way eventually, or worse, it would have worn my face forever and no one would ever have known." She spread her hands. "Your disobedience is the only reason I'm standing here."

Xanthe laughed—a short, disbelieving sound with no humor in it. "Don't say that. Don't make my stubbornness holy. I spent ten years thinking obedience was the last thing I had left of you. You don't get to tell me it was rebellion all along. Let me keep my sins."

That startled her. He watched it land, watched the goddess of the old covenant—who had commanded armies, who had spoken names into being, who had once, mid-battle, gone silent forever—actually stop and reconsider a sentence, the way a person does, the way a *partner* does.

"All right," she said. "Keep your sins. We'll need them. Sinners are the only ones who understand mercy well enough to administer it."

* * *

They walked while they talked, because neither of them could bear stillness. Out of the undercroft, up the broken stair, into the gray-gold morning of a Vask that was busy becoming unrecognizable. People stood in doorways staring at their own lit candles. A woman was scrubbing at a faded sigil on her wrist with lye and a rag, weeping, though whether from loss or relief even she probably couldn't have said. Two children were drawing the true sigil in chalk on a wall—drawing it slightly wrong, from memory of rumor—and where the chalk touched stone, faintly, flowers that shouldn't grow in this district were pushing up through the cracks.

Ulyana walked beside him in her borrowed mortal shape, and people didn't see her—or rather, they saw her and dismissed her, some road-worn traveler, some pilgrim, nothing worth a second glance. Only the flowers noticed. Only the candles leaned toward her as she passed, flames bending like wheat.

At the top of the Wardmarket steps—scorched, half-ruined, the site of Odile's arrest and the night every flame in the district had flared at once—she stopped and turned to him, and Xanthe felt the conversation shift beneath his feet like a deck tilting.

"I owe you a debt no ledger can hold," she said. "So let me offer what I can. Your mark. I can restore it—whole, unbroken, warmer than it ever was. The bond we had before the Silence, before the outlawing, before any of it. My strength in your hand. My voice in your ear. Everything you lost, returned." Her voice gentled. "I know what its absence feels like. I've spent ten years feeling mine."

And there it was. The offer he had dreamed of in the worst years, drunk and alone in his rented room, selling hollow charms to desperate people and hating himself: *give me back the warmth, give me back the voice, give me back the certainty of being held.*

He looked at his bare forearm. Smooth skin. Nothing.

"No," Xanthe said.

The candle flames in the nearest window went very still.

Ulyana didn't flinch, but he saw her brace, the way you brace when a wound reopens. "Say the rest of it," she said. "Whatever it is. Say all of it."

"The mark was a leash." He said it fast, before courage failed, the way you tear a bandage. "Not yours—I know that now, I know you inherited it same as we did, the whole rotten architecture of gods and feeding and faith-as-obedience. But it was a leash, and I wore it gladly, and gladness doesn't make it less of a leash. Ten years ago you told me not to look for you, and I disobeyed, and it saved the world. Think about that. Think about what it means that the most faithful act of my life was disobedience." He turned to face her fully. "I won't go back to being held. I love you. That's not negotiable, and it's not the point. The point is that love isn't servitude, and if we rebuild the covenant the old way—if I take the mark and kneel and belong to you—then we're just planting the seed of the next counterfeit. Hunger grows in gardens like that. It always has."

The morning held its breath. Somewhere below, the bells were still ringing.

"So what do you want?" Ulyana asked. And here was the miracle, here was the thing no statue had ever captured: she asked it as a genuine question. Not a test. Not a trap with a right answer. She wanted to know.

Xanthe had expected to have to argue. He had prepared arguments the way a soldier prepares for a siege. Standing there, facing a goddess who had actually asked, he found most of his ammunition unnecessary, and what remained was simple enough to fit in two hands.

"A new covenant. Written different." He held up one finger. "Gods who answer. Not sometimes, not cryptically, not through prophets with agendas—when your faithful speak to you, you speak back, even if the answer is *no*, even if the answer is *wait*. Silence is how the counterfeit got in. Silence is the wound. We close it." A second finger. "Mortals who may refuse. No compulsion, no marks that bind, no punishment for doubt. A person gets to tell a god *no* and stay whole afterward. You saw what my refusal just did to your candles—they're still burning. Let that be doctrine." A third finger, and here his voice dropped, because this was the part that had come to him on the traitor's road, watching Sera choose exile over comfort. "And partnership. Not worship from below and grace from above. Work. Side by side. You know the roads; we know the mud. You can see the shape of a soul; we can see the price of bread. Neither of us is complete alone—that was always the lie, that divinity didn't need us. The counterfeit proved it. A god wearing stolen faith got fat and stupid and fell apart the moment one man spoke your name *with* you instead of *at* you."

He ran out of fingers and words together, and stood there breathing hard, waiting for the lightning or the silence or the long slow withdrawal of a goddess deciding her last servant wasn't worth the trouble.

Ulyana looked at him for a long moment.

Then she laughed.

It was not a goddess's laugh—there was nothing vast or echoing in it. It was a real laugh, rough-edged and surprised out of her, the laugh of someone who has carried grief for a decade and set it down without noticing, and it rang off the scorched stones of the Wardmarket and startled pigeons out of the eaves and made the chalk-flowers in the distance open another inch. Heads turned below. People would swear afterward that the bells changed key on their own.

"Oh," she said, wiping her eye with the heel of her hand, entirely human in the gesture, "oh, I have missed this. Ten years of hearing prayers and I never once heard anyone *negotiate*. They begged. They bargained, badly. They promised me things they didn't want to give." She stepped closer, and the warmth of her was like standing near a hearth after years in the rain. "Partnership. Gods who answer, mortals who may refuse." She said it slowly, tasting it, testing its weight the way Odile tested coin. "Do you understand what you're proposing? Not just for you and me. For every god that wakes behind this seal as it thins—for the drowned ones, the small ones, the ones the old order ground up for fuel. You're proposing a constitution, Xanthe Galloway. For heaven."

"I'm proposing," he said, "that you hire better help."

She laughed again, shorter this time, and then she did something that no icon, no priest, no scripture had ever recorded a goddess doing. She extended her hand—not palm-down, to be kissed, not raised in blessing, but sideways, level, the way one soldier greets another across a parley-fire.

"Terms accepted," Ulyana said. "With amendments to be argued over wine, because I have a decade of opinions and you're going to hate at least half of them."

He took her hand. Her grip was calloused, warm, strong, and utterly ordinary, and when their skin touched there was no brand, no burning, no sigil seared into his flesh. There was only a feeling like a door opening in a house he'd lived in all his life without knowing the room was there—space, and light, and the sense that something on the other side was listening and would answer when he knocked.

No mark. Just an open line. He could close it whenever he chose. He suspected he almost never would, and that the choosing was the whole point.

"One amendment from me," he said. "The widow. Marta. She carries a piece of your name in her bones whether she wants it or not. Whatever comes next—whatever priesthood, whatever order—we build it around people like her, people who never asked for the sacred and got it anyway. They don't serve us. We serve *them*."

"Agreed," she said, "and you should know the small gods are already arguing about which of them gets to be her patron, and she hasn't consented to any of them, and I find I quite enjoy watching her refuse them."

Below them, the bells changed again—no, not the bells. Voices. A crowd gathering in the Wardmarket square, growing by the minute, drawn by rumor and candlelight and the impossible flowers, and at its center a familiar figure on the courthouse steps with her arms crossed and her voice pitched to carry: Odile, holding court, organizing hope the way she'd once organized contraband. Beside her, Sera stood watch, inquisitor's coat gone, plain clothes underneath, a woman with no mark and no master and, for the first time in her life, no orders—which meant, Xanthe thought, that she was about to start giving some.

"They'll need to know what happened," Ulyana said. "All of it. The seal, the counterfeit, the terms. No more mysteries. Mysteries are where the hungry things live."

"They'll need more than that." Xanthe looked out over the city—half-converted, half-liberated, wholly uncertain, ten thousand people waking to a world where prayer worked and nobody had agreed yet on what it should cost. "They'll need to decide what to believe. And belief built on consent takes longer than belief built on fear. There's going to be resistance. The inquisition's remnant. Cultists who miss being devoured because it felt like purpose. Magistrates with ledgers full of favors owed to a god that doesn't exist anymore."

"Then we'd best begin," said Ulyana, and started down the steps toward the crowd, toward the noise, toward the work—then paused, glancing back at him with a crooked smile that no sculptor would ever manage to capture. "Coming, partner?"

Xanthe Galloway pulled off his remaining glove, folded it, and put it away for good.

"Yeah," he said, and followed his goddess down into the city that might yet learn to believe.
