## 21. A City That May Believe

The law came down from the Magistrate's Conclave on a gray morning in the month of Thaw, read aloud in the Wardmarket square by a clerk whose voice cracked twice from nerves. *The Proscription of Arcanum is hereby dissolved.* Ten years of ash and fear, undone in four hundred words that took eleven minutes to speak.

Xanthe stood at the edge of the crowd with his hood down for the first time in a decade, listening to the words land on the city like rain on ground that had forgotten how to drink it. Around him, people did not cheer. They went very still, the way Vask always went still when something true was said aloud—as if truth were a bird that might startle if they moved too fast.

An old woman near him wept without sound. A man dropped to his knees in the mud, then got up quickly, embarrassed, and brushed at his trousers as though nothing had happened. Somewhere in the middle distance, a bell began to ring—not an alarm, not a summons, just ringing, because someone had decided that this morning deserved bells.

*Decriminalized.* The word sat strangely in Xanthe's mouth when he tried it later, walking home through streets where ward-sigils had been chiseled off doorframes by enforcers' hammers and now, overnight, were being chalked back on again by the same hands, or their children's hands, working from memory and guesswork. Half of them were wrong. Nobody minded. There would be time to learn them properly again. There had never been time before; that had been the whole architecture of the outlawing—not merely to forbid magic but to make forgetting faster than remembering, so that within a generation no one would know what they'd lost.

It hadn't worked. That was the thing the Conclave had finally been forced to admit, in the careful language of decrees: it hadn't worked, because you cannot outlaw what people carry in their bodies. You can only drive it into cellars and back rooms and the space behind a glove, where it grows strange and hungry and sometimes—when something wearing your goddess's face comes calling—it grows teeth.

Xanthe touched his bare left hand as he walked. The covenant mark was gone. He had given it to the reforging, poured it into the joining of the four shards until there was nothing left on his skin but a pale ghost of silver, a scar shaped like a word he could no longer quite read. And yet Ulyana's presence rode beside him still, quiet and warm, not a brand now but a companionship. He kept expecting to miss the mark. He didn't. It turned out that what he'd missed all those years wasn't the burning—it was the being answered.

* * *

Odile opened the charm-house on Copperlane Row three weeks after the decree, and she opened it honestly, which in Odile's case constituted a religious conversion.

For twenty years she had been a fence—a buyer and seller of relics, charms, and things that pretended to be both—and her genius had always been the plausible lie: the provenance forged so beautifully it became its own kind of art. Now she stood behind a counter of pale new wood, in a shop with actual windows, selling charms that worked, each one tagged with its purpose, its limits, and its price, in her own blunt handwriting.

*Warming charm. Good through winter. Will not protect against grief.*

*Finding charm. Locates what is lost within three leagues. Cannot find what does not wish to be found.*

*Sleeping charm. Mild. For nightmares, see the priestess.*

Xanthe came by on the second morning and stood reading the tags while Odile watched him with her arms crossed and an expression she probably thought was stern.

"You've written disclaimers," he said.

"I've written *truths*." She jabbed a finger at the warming charm. "That one fails if the wearer lies down in standing water. I tested it. Twice. In a pond." She said it with the defiance of a woman who had spent two decades selling smoke and had discovered, to her own astonishment, that honesty gave her a headache far less often than fraud. "Do you know how exhausting it was, lying for a living? Don't answer that. You sold hollow charms yourself."

"I sold hope-shaped objects," Xanthe said. "There's a difference."

"There's a margin," Odile said, "and the margin was mine." But she was smiling, and she pushed a wrapped parcel across the counter to him before he asked. Inside was a new glove, fine kid leather, unlined. "For the ghost-mark. Silver shows in cold weather. People will stare."

"People stared anyway."

"Yes," Odile said, softer. "They did. And half of them are outside my door right now wanting to shake your hand, and I'm charging them admission."

He looked past her, through the windows, to the queue forming along Copperlane Row—dockworkers and grandmothers, a pair of former enforcers trying very hard to look casual, a child holding a wooden sword and a question. Not a mob. A line. Vask had spent ten years learning to need quietly, and now the needing had come out into the daylight, blinking, uncertain whether it was allowed.

"Send them to me after," Xanthe said. "I'll take the ones who want small things. Warm hands, found keys, one good night's sleep."

"And the big things?"

"The big things go to Maren." He said the widow's name carefully, the way you set down something heavy. "And the ones who don't want a small thing—who want a god instead of a life—" He shook his head. "Those I turn away."

Odile studied him. "You used to be one of those," she said. "Ten years ago. A man who wanted a god instead of a life."

"I know," Xanthe said. "That's why I know the look."

* * *

Maren held her first open vigil in the tenement room where Sera had once held a stairway alone against a dozen cultists, and she held it with the doors open, which was itself a kind of theology.

She was not a dramatic priestess. She had no cathedral voice, no stitched radiance, none of the counterfeit's gorgeous certainty. She was a widow with burn-scarred forearms and a fragment of Ulyana's living name carried in her healed body, and when people came to her with their dead and their debts and their unsleepable nights, she listened the way stone listens to water: patiently, without flinching, until the shape of the grief was worn smooth enough to hold something else.

"You should know," she told each newcomer, in the flat honest tone of a woman reading terms aloud, "that Ulyana does not fix things. She stands with you while you fix them. If you want fixing done *to* you—if you want to hand it all over and stop carrying it—there was a god who offered that, once. Look what it cost us."

No one ever argued. They had all seen what it cost. Half of Vask still bore the fading scars of the counterfeit's marks, sigils dissolving like frost in sun since the unraveling, leaving behind skin that felt oddly light, oddly unclaimed. Some missed it. Maren let them say so out loud, and did not shame them for it. Belonging that shamed you for your hunger was just the counterfeit wearing better clothes.

On the fourth evening of the vigils, a man came whom Xanthe recognized—the magistrate's under-clerk, the one who had drafted the old confiscation orders, whose neat signature had sent Odile to a cell and a dozen others to worse. He stood at the back with his hat in his hands, and when Maren asked if anyone wished to speak, he said, in a voice like a dry twig:

"I signed the papers. All of them. I told myself it was law, and law was order, and order was mercy of a kind." His hands turned the hat around and around. "I would like to know if there is a rite for that. For having been the instrument."

The room went quiet. Xanthe, sitting near the door, felt Ulyana stir beside him—not speaking, not yet, but attentive, the way she attended to everything now, as if making up for a decade of silence with a decade of listening.

Maren considered the man for a long moment.

"There's no rite," she said. "There's work. Come Thursday. We're rebuilding the shrine wall on Tanner's Lane, the one the enforcers knocked down. Bring gloves."

The man blinked. "That's—that's masonry."

"It's penance that leaves something standing when it's done," Maren said. "Thursday."

And the man—Xanthe watched him do it—nodded, and put his hat back on, and left with his spine straighter than he'd come in, carrying a burden that had suddenly become a task. *That*, Xanthe thought, watching him go. *That's the difference. The counterfeit would have eaten his guilt and called it absolution. She makes him build a wall.*

* * *

Sera came to Copperlane Row at the end of the month, walking with a limp the cold weather sharpened, carrying a satchel full of the inquisition's confiscated archives—which were, as of the decree, evidence of nothing except history.

She had resigned formally, which in the inquisition meant she had walked out and dared them to follow. Most of them hadn't. Her old superior had, and had found, to his evident dismay, that the new magistrates regarded the hunting of sigil-bearers as an embarrassment best forgotten, along with the men who'd done it. Sera's name appeared nowhere in the amnesty rolls. She hadn't asked it to. "I wasn't pardoned," she told Xanthe over Odile's counter, drinking tea that Odile had charged her for, on principle. "I just stopped being worth the paperwork."

"What will you do?"

"Read." She patted the satchel. "Ten years of seizure records. Every relic they burned, every tablet they smashed, every name they tried to erase. Someone should know what we lost. Someone should write it down before the last person who remembers dies." She glanced up, and there was something raw under the dryness. "The hermit in the borderlands says there were other gods under the seal. Not just Ulyana. Whole pantheons, starved quiet. If any of them wake—" She stopped herself. "If. Later. First the record."

"You could rest," Xanthe said. "You held a stair alone. The whole city knows it. You could rest for a year and no one would think less of you."

Sera looked at him with the expression of someone who had spent her entire life being useful to institutions and had only recently discovered that usefulness chosen freely weighed nothing at all.

"Rest is for people who aren't afraid of what's in the boxes," she said, and finished her tea, and paid, and left.

* * *

Spring came up the river in earnest, and with it the customers.

They came to Xanthe's stall in the reopened Wardmarket—his old stall, re-leased to him by a market-master who remembered the raids and seemed to feel that a free charm-seller was a kind of civic apology. The charms were small and they worked. Warming charms that actually warmed. Finding charms that pulled gently toward the lost thing like a finger tugging a sleeve. A sleeping charm he made for a night-watchman who hadn't dreamed in ten years and woke the first morning weeping, saying only, *I dreamed about my mother's kitchen,* as if that were a miracle, which it was.

But it was the others Xanthe watched for, and they came, more every week: the ones with the look.

He knew the look because he had worn it. It was hunger wearing patience. It was the face of a person who wanted not a charm but a surrender—to be taken over, answered, claimed, relieved forever of the terrible weight of their own agency. The counterfeit had fed on that hunger for months, growing fat, and the hunger hadn't died with it. Hunger like that doesn't die. It waits for the next thing willing to wear a holy face.

A young woman came on a bright morning in the month of Seedfall, clutching a letter she couldn't read—her brother was conscripted, or sick, or dead, the story changed with each telling—and she looked at Xanthe's stall and then at his face and said, "You spoke her name back to her. They say you're the bridge. They say if anyone can make her *answer*—"

"She does answer," Xanthe said gently. "Ask her yourself. She listens to everyone now. That's rather the point."

"But I want *you* to ask. I want someone who *knows* her to ask for me. I'll pay anything. I'll serve. I'll—" Her eyes had gone somewhere beyond him, to a place where a power larger than herself would take the letter, and the fear, and the whole unbearable business of being a single small life in an indifferent world, and carry it for her. "I'll give whatever she wants. Just tell me what to give."

And there it was. The look. The old sweet poison, offering itself up again in a new mouth.

Xanthe set down the charm he'd been wrapping and came around the stall, and took the woman's hands—cold fingers, ink-stained, a working woman's hands—and waited until she met his eyes.

"No," he said.

Her face crumpled. "Please—"

"Listen to me. Ulyana's covenant, the real one, the one I nearly died to reforge—it has exactly one term, and it's the term the counterfeit never offered: *you may refuse.* She answers because you ask, not because you belong to her. And the moment you try to hand yourself over—your service, your sacrifice, your self—you're not praying anymore. You're feeding something. Maybe not today. Maybe not anything with her face. But something." He pressed her hands once and let go. "So here is what I'll sell you. A finding charm. It works on letters the way it works on keys. Three leagues, and it pulls toward what's lost. Your brother is farther than three leagues, I'd guess—but the charm will pull toward whoever holds his next letter, and that's someone, and someone is not no one."

The woman stared at him. Something warred in her face—the hunger fighting the human being underneath it—and Xanthe waited, the way Maren listened, the way Ulyana had waited ten silent years for her faithful to endure.

"That's not enough," the woman whispered.

"It's everything there is," Xanthe said. "It's what a life is. Small pulls toward lost things, and the choosing of them." He wrapped the finding charm in brown paper and held it out. "One copper. The wanting-to-be-taken-away is free, and I won't sell it, because it isn't mine to sell. It's yours. Keep it."

She paid the copper. She took the charm. At the edge of the crowd she stopped, and he saw her close her fist around the little paper package, and lift it, briefly, the way you'd touch a talisman—and then she squared her shoulders and walked off toward the river road, small and upright and unbearably mortal, pulling toward what was lost.

Ulyana's presence settled warm against his shoulder, wordless approval, or perhaps laughter—he still couldn't always tell the difference, and suspected she arranged it that way on purpose.

"Don't look smug," he murmured. "You'd have taken her offer, ten years ago. You took mine."

*I did,* she said, in the voice that lived now behind his ribs like a second heartbeat. *And look what it built. A bridge is only useful if it goes somewhere. You went somewhere.*

Behind him, the market roared on—Odile shouting prices across the row, the bells of the Conclave ringing some new decree or none at all, children chalk-drawing ward-sigils that were mostly wrong and entirely beloved. Vask, believing again. Carefully. Imperfectly. On its own terms.

Xanthe turned to his next customer and froze.

At the back of the queue, half-hidden in the shadow of the chandler's awning, stood a figure in traveling gray with a hood drawn low—but not so low that Xanthe missed the glint of silver at the throat, or the way the crowd bent subtly around the stranger, unconsciously, the way wheat bends around a stone.

The figure raised one hand—not a wave, not a summons. An acknowledgment. *I see you. I am not hiding. Yet.*

And beneath Xanthe's ribs, Ulyana went very, very still, and said, in a voice he had never heard her use—not in memory, not in vision, not in all the long years of silence and all the short bright weeks since:

*I know that walk. That is one of mine. That is one I sealed away.*
