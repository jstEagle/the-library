## 4. The Guild's Long Theft

The canal district at dawn was a place the city pretended not to see. Ada had chosen a lockkeeper's cottage with a sagging roof and a door that hadn't opened in years — she'd had to break the seal of dust herself, wincing at every crack of the wood. Now she sat on a stool before a table scarred by generations of knife-work, the stranger's map spread flat beneath her hands, and waited for the dead man to speak.

He did not keep her waiting long.

"You slept," Emeric said, his voice rising out of the ink like breath through water. The lines of the map shimmered faintly where he spoke, roads and rivers rearranging themselves in slow, patient patterns. "Good. What I have to show you will want your eyes fresh."

"Show me, then." Her voice came out harder than she intended. She'd spent the night turning over his words — *covenant*, *keeper*, *your grandmother* — and each one had lodged in her like a splinter. "You died to bring me this map. You said the Spire remembers my name. I want to know why."

"The Spire remembers everyone who has ever touched it," Emeric said. "But it remembers *keepers* differently. And you are her blood."

"My grandmother has been dead for twenty years."

"Yes." A pause, and when he spoke again there was something in it that Ada could only call gentleness — an old man's gentleness, worn smooth by long grief. "And for twenty years before that, she begged the Guild to stop. Come. Let me show you what she saw."

The map *moved*.

Not the way maps moved in a cartographer's imagination — not the trick of perspective, not the illusion of flight. It moved the way a living thing moves. The canals of the city thinned to threads, the threads to nothing, and the parchment opened like a window onto somewhere else.

Ada gasped and gripped the table's edge.

She was looking down at a world.

It unrolled beneath her as if she were a bird riding a thermal: forests whose trees grew in spirals, their leaves the color of struck bells; rivers that ran uphill without effort, singing — actually singing, a sound like glass and honey; mountains that breathed, their stone ribs expanding and contracting in a rhythm too slow to be anything but sleep. Colors existed here that had no names in any language she knew. One of them — a blue so deep it seemed to hold its own weather — made her eyes sting with tears she couldn't explain.

"This is—" She swallowed. "This is impossible. This isn't on any chart. This isn't *anywhere*."

"It is everywhere," Emeric said quietly. "Underneath. Adjacent. The unmapped realm. Your guild calls it the Source. My people called it home."

*My people.* Ada looked closer at the vision, and now she saw them: figures moving through the spiral forests, tall and slight, their skin holding the same bell-colored light as the leaves. They walked among the singing rivers and pressed their palms to the breathing mountains, and everywhere they touched, the world brightened.

"The Vessels," Emeric said. "We kept the covenant. Do you understand what a covenant is, cartographer? Not a contract. Not a trade. A *promise between equals*. Long ago, your world and mine stood at a threshold, and the threshold had to be held from both sides, or both worlds would bleed into each other and drown. So we built the Spire together — your masons and our singers — and we sealed the promise into its stones. And for six hundred years, the door stayed shut, and both worlds stayed whole."

Ada's mouth had gone dry. "And the ledgers?"

The vision shifted. The beautiful world dimmed at its edges, like a candle guttering.

"Now watch," Emeric said, "what your masters do with the door."

* * *

She watched.

She watched a guild surveyor step through a hidden gate in the year 1213, guided by a Vessel who trusted him, who sang open a path through the spiral forest because the covenant promised safe passage to those who came in good faith. She watched the surveyor mark the path on his chart. She watched him return with others.

She watched them cut a branch from a tree whose leaves rang like bells — cut it cleanly, with a blade cold-forged against the realm's own law — and carry it back through the gate. In the human world, the branch became a curiosity. Then a commodity. Bottled starlight, the ledger said. Ada had audited that entry forty times. *Bottled starlight, twelve vials, sold to House Meridian.* She had assumed it was poetry. A code name for something mundane.

It was starlight. It was the light of another world's sky, wrung from a branch still bleeding sap, and it glowed in its vials for a hundred years while the tree it came from stood bare and silent, its leaves no longer ringing.

She watched a river's first word being taken — the actual first word a river speaks when it wakes in spring, a sound the Vessels gathered each year in ceremony, a sound that named the river to itself. The guild's harvesters caught it in a lead-lined box. Sold it to a collector. And the river forgot its own name, and began to dry.

Entry after entry. Ledger after ledger. Two hundred years of them, unfolding across the map in Emeric's low voice, and Ada sat motionless on her stool while her entire profession arranged itself before her eyes into the shape of a crime.

Bottled starlight. A river's first word. The last echo of a mountain's dream. A Vessel's shadow, taken while he slept — *sold to the Guildmaster's private collection*. The memory of a color. The patience of stone. Each one real. Each one carved, living, from the body of a world.

"They didn't even hide it," Ada whispered. Her hands had gone cold on the table's edge. "They wrote it all down. Every theft. In *my ledgers*. I balanced those books. I signed off on the quarterly audits. I—"

"You did your work well," Emeric said. "That is how it survives. Not through villains, mostly. Through careful people doing careful work who never ask what the words mean."

"And the Spire?" She forced the question out. "You said it's failing."

The map showed her the Spire.

It rose from a plain at the border of both worlds — a tower grown rather than built, its stones fused from covenant-law itself, pale and seamless and shot through with veins of light. Or it *had* been. The image Emeric gave her was of the tower as it stood now: cracked from crown to root, the veins flickering like a dying lamp, and from the deepest fissures something wept — not water, but light itself, running down the stones and pooling at the base in dull, fading puddles.

"Every theft weakens it," Emeric said. "The covenant is not a wall, Ada. It is a *body*. The Spire is its spine. When your guild takes a piece of my world through the door, the door strains. Six hundred years of small cuts. And now—" his voice frayed, "now the cracks are spreading faster than the law can heal them. Within a year, perhaps two, the Spire fails. And when it fails, the door doesn't close. It *dissolves*. Both worlds pour into each other. Yours will not survive it. Neither will mine."

Ada thought of the senior cartographers' wing, with its locked cabinets and its hushed meetings. She thought of Corvane, the Guildmaster, with his soft voice and his eyes like ledger-columns — everything accounted for, nothing felt. She thought of the warning old Hessler had given her three days ago, leaning close over the audit desk, his breath smelling of ink and fear: *"Some columns are best left unread, Montrose. Ask fewer questions and you'll die older."*

"They know," she said. "Don't they. Corvane knows exactly what the ledgers mean. He knows the Spire is dying."

"He counts on it," Emeric said, and there was iron under the gentleness now. "A dying world can be strip-mined. A healthy one has laws. Why do you think the harvests have tripled in the last decade? Your Guildmaster is not trying to save the covenant, child. He is trying to take everything of value through the door before it collapses — and then seal himself and his heirs on this side, rich beyond kings, while my world drowns in the dark."

The word landed in Ada's chest like a dropped stone.

*Child.* He had said it the way her grandmother used to say it — and the thought arrived whole, terrible, certain: *Ulyana knew. Ulyana found the ledgers' meaning, just as I have. Ulyana tried to stop it.*

"What happened to her?" Ada asked. Her voice shook. "My grandmother. You said she begged them to stop. What did they do to her?"

For a long moment the map said nothing. The dying Spire hung in the air between them, weeping its slow light.

"She went to the Spire," Emeric said at last. "To reforge the covenant — to renew it, the way it must be renewed every few centuries, with a price paid willingly by the one who performs the rite. She never reached the summit. Someone told the enforcers where she would be. Someone she loved." His voice dropped. "I was there, Ada. I carried her journal for sixty years trying to finish what she started. That is why I came to you. That is why I chose you."

The stool creaked under Ada as she stood, legs unsteady. Betrayal was a strange thing to feel on behalf of the dead — a grief with no proper place to put itself, curdling as it sat. Her grandmother. Her mother's mother, who had taught her to hold a compass before she could write her own name. Who had vanished from family stories like a figure erased from a chart.

Someone she loved.

Ada filed the thought away, sharp-edged, for later.

* * *

She was reaching for the map when Emeric's voice changed.

"Cartographer. Listen."

The lines of the map had gone taut, quivering. Along the canal district's streets — rendered in fine brown ink — six small marks were moving. Converging.

"Enforcers," Emeric said. "Four on foot along Tanners' Row. Two on the water, in a quiet skiff. They've been searching the district since before dawn. They found blood at the Inkwell Door. Yours?"

"A scratch. When I went through the archive window." Ada was already moving, sweeping the map from the table and folding it against her chest — and the map folded *with* her, shrinking obediently small, warm as a live thing. "How do they know I'm here?"

"Hessler," Emeric said simply. "He reported your questions. And the Guild watches the canal district whenever anyone runs, because it is full of old doors. You have perhaps four minutes."

Four minutes. Ada's mind snapped into the cold, precise mode she trusted more than courage: exits, distances, timing. The cottage had one door, boarded. One window facing the canal, shuttered. The roof sagged toward the neighboring tannery — a drop, then a run across wet planking, then—

Then what? She was a woman with a folded map and no allies, hunted by the most efficient enforcement arm in the city.

"The back ways," she said aloud. "You said the stranger — you — knew the guild's back ways. Show me."

"I am showing you." The map warmed against her ribs. "Look down."

She looked. On the flagstones of the cottage floor, faint lines of light had appeared — no, not on the floor; in her *vision*, laid over the world like a transparency. A thread of gold ran from the cottage's broken hearth, down through the chimney's dead flue, into a crawl-space beneath the foundations, along a drainage channel forgotten since the canal was rerouted, surfacing half a mile east behind a drowned chapel.

"That route hasn't existed in eighty years," Ada breathed.

"I mapped it when it did," Emeric said. "Maps remember what cities forget. Go. Now. And Ada—" the ghost-voice softened, "—when they ask you whose side you're on, remember the river that forgot its name. Remember whose books you balanced."

She remembered. She would always remember now; that was the wound and the weapon of it.

Boots sounded on Tanners' Row — heavy, unhurried, professional. A fist tested the cottage door.

Ada went up the chimney flue headfirst, elbows tight, the map clutched to her heart, coughing on eighty years of soot. Below her, the door splintered inward. Above her, a thread of grey daylight. Behind her eyes, the image of a tower weeping light into the dark, and her grandmother's unfinished climb.

She dropped into the crawl-space and ran — through the dark, through the gold-lit thread of a dead man's memory, toward the drowned chapel and whatever lay beyond it.

Because there was a border to cross. There was a Spire to reach. And there was a debt, two hundred years deep, that the Cartographers' Guild had finally, unknowingly, placed in the hands of the one person alive who intended to collect it.
