## 6. Her Grandmother's Hand

The waystation stood where the map said it would, though *stood* was a generous word for it. Ada saw it first as a suggestion against the horizon — a darkening in the already-dim air of the otherworld, like a bruise beneath the skin of the land. As she drew closer, the suggestion became structure: walls of grey stone furred with pale moss, a roof half-collapsed into itself, a doorway that had once been arched and grand and now gaped like a missing tooth.

She had been walking for what felt like a day. Time moved strangely here, pooling and thinning like light through water. The Vessels had left her at the edge of a field of silver grass that whispered without wind, pointing — with hands like folded paper — toward the ruin, and saying only, *What was kept is kept there. What was hidden waits for blood.*

*Blood*, they'd said, and Ada's hand had gone to her palm, to the shallow cut she'd made to seal her promise to them. She hadn't bled since. The grass had drunk it, or the ground had. Everything here drank something.

"You knew this place," she said aloud, to the map.

The lines stirred beneath her fingers. Emeric's voice came faintly, thinner than it had been even that morning, as if he were speaking through several closed doors. *"Every keeper had one. A waystation between worlds, where the covenant could be tended. Your grandmother kept this one."*

"Was she here when—"

*"When they came for her? No."* A pause, long enough that Ada thought the memory might have frayed through. *"When they came for her, she was already running. But she stopped here first. To hide what mattered."*

Ada stood before the doorway and felt the weight of the building looking back at her. It was absurd — stone couldn't look — but the waystation had the quality of held breath, of a room someone had just left. Her grandmother had stood on this threshold. Had touched this doorframe. The thought landed in her chest with a physical weight, and she realized how starved she had been, all her life, for any trace of the woman. Two photographs. One story about a garden. And then silence, and her mother's face going shuttered whenever the name was spoken.

She stepped inside.

Dust hung in the air like weather. The main hall had been a maproom once — she knew it instantly, the way a musician knows a tuning fork struck in another room. There was the great table, its surface scarred and empty. There were the alcoves where instruments would have hung. There was a hearth, cold for decades, over which someone had painted a symbol she recognized from the guild's oldest seals: two interlocking circles, one drawn solid, one drawn broken.

The whole covenant, in two strokes of paint. The world mapped, and the world unmapped, holding each other together.

"It was beautiful," she whispered.

*"It was necessary,"* Emeric corrected gently. *"Beauty came after. Or it should have."*

Ada circled the table twice, then began to search in earnest — the way she searched, methodically, every seam and hollow. She checked under the flagstones near the hearth. She ran her fingers along the mortar lines. She opened a rotted cabinet and startled a moth the color of tarnished copper, which spiraled up past her face and dissolved into dust before it reached the ceiling.

Nothing. The Vessels had promised hidden things, but ruins kept their secrets loosely; scavengers, time, and weather took what wasn't nailed down.

She was turning toward the stairs when Emeric said, *"You're searching like a cartographer."*

"I am a cartographer."

*"Your grandmother wasn't. Not when she hid it. When she hid it, she was a woman who expected thieves."* His voice gathered a little strength, the way a fire does when fed. *"Thieves read rooms. They check the obvious places because they assume everyone thinks like they do. Maren hid it the other way. She put it somewhere no thief would ever think to look — because it looked like nothing at all."*

Ada stopped. Looked around the hall again, slower now. Somewhere that looked like nothing.

Her eyes found the wall beside the door. Bare plaster, water-stained, unremarkable. Except — she crossed to it — except that the stains didn't quite make sense. Water stained downward, pooled, followed gravity. These marks curved. They repeated. With a cartographer's eye she saw it plainly: contour lines. Faint, almost invisible, but deliberate. A map so small and so plain it would never register as a map.

A map of the room itself, drawn by someone standing exactly where Ada stood.

And at the center of the room — the table. She went to it, heart quickening, and knelt, and pressed her cut palm flat against the wood.

The table remembered being touched by blood. Something deep in its grain turned over like a sleeper, and a drawer existed where no drawer had been — sliding silently out of solid oak, revealing a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and tied with cord gone brittle as biscuit.

Ada's hands shook as she untied it.

Inside lay a journal bound in grey leather, its cover tooled with the interlocking circles. Beneath it, wrapped separately, a strip of vellum covered in dense notation — musical staves mixed with surveyor's measurements, angles and intervals, a language halfway between song and geometry. Half a ritual. She knew it instinctively, the way she'd known the map was true: this was half of something, and the other half waited elsewhere.

And beneath both, folded small, a letter addressed in a hand she did not know:

*To whoever finds this — I hope it is no one. I hope the need died with me.*

Ada sat down on the dusty floor with her back against the table, as her legs had quietly decided for her, and opened the journal.

---

The early pages were ordinary — survey notes, supply lists, sketches of the waystation in better days. Then, dated some forty years past, the handwriting changed. Tighter. Faster. Afraid.

*I have confirmed what I feared. The ledgers are not bookkeeping. They are butchery. Every entry we record as "acquisition" is a wound taken from the other side. Bottled starlight — do you know what that is? It is the light of a sky that will never shine again, because we took the sky's own brightness out of it. The river's first word. We stole a river's first word. What kind of creature speaks, and what remains of it after?*

*I went to Corvane. He thanked me for my diligence and doubled the harvest quotas.*

Ada turned pages with increasing dread. Her grandmother — Maren Montrose, keeper of the waystation, sworn to tend the covenant — had spent years documenting, objecting, appealing. Each entry grew more isolated. Each appeal climbed higher and returned lower.

*They do not think of it as theft. That is the horror of it. Corvane believes sincerely that the otherworld is dying anyway, and that harvesting it is mercy — salvage, not slaughter. He has built a cathedral of reasonable men around an unreasonable act.*

Then a new name appeared, and Ada's breath stopped.

*Elara has been asking questions. Good questions. Sharp ones. She has my gift for seeing what maps conceal. I have begun teaching her, though I swore I wouldn't put this on another generation. But if not her, who?*

*Elara.* Her mother. Ada read the name three times, as if it might rearrange itself into someone else.

The entries continued. Maren taught her daughter the border-walks, the old signs, the tending rites. Elara learned quickly. Too quickly, perhaps. Because then:

*Elara asked me today why the covenant must be equal. Why we cannot simply take less rather than give equally. I told her equality is the whole of the law — that the Spire itself is grown from that principle, and to unbalance it is to crack it. She listened. She nodded. I should have seen it. She was not asking to understand. She was asking to find the flaw.*

Ada's throat had gone tight. She read on, faster now, unable to stop.

*I discovered the harvest route tonight — the one that bypasses the Spire entirely, siphoning through the guild's deep vaults. Someone built it. Someone with access to everything I know. There are only two people alive who could have drawn those channels.*

*I confronted Elara at the Inkwell. She did not deny it. She said — God help me, she said — "Grandmother's covenant is starving us. The realms are full of power and she wants to leave it locked away out of sentiment. I'm fixing it. I'm making the guild strong." She believed it. That is the wound I cannot close: she believed it, the way Corvane believes it, the way they all believe, because taking has always been easier than keeping.*

*They know I know. I have one night. I am writing this by candlelight in the waystation while the ink dries on my own death warrant. I cannot reforge the covenant alone — the rite requires two halves, and the second half is kept where only a keeper can reach it, at the Spire itself. If I run, they follow. If I stay, they take the journal and the theft becomes eternal.*

*So I hide it, and I trust it to chance, and to blood. If you are reading this, you are mine — by blood or by oath, it makes no difference. Hear me:*

*The rite can be done. I found it in the first keeper's records. But know its price before you begin, because Corvane will offer you everything else instead, and his offers will be sincere. The covenant is a law of exchange. To reforge it, the one who performs the rite must pay from their own substance — not gold, not blood spilled carelessly, but something woven into the self. The first keepers paid in years of life. One paid in her voice. The law accepts whatever the giver holds dearest, and takes it entire.*

*Whatever you love most of what this work has given you — the covenant will know, and the covenant will take it.*

*I did not attempt the rite because I could not bear the price. I tell myself it was strategy, that I needed the second half of the rite first, that I would have found another way. Perhaps. But I write this knowing the truth underneath: I flinched. I looked at what I loved — this place, this work, the wonder of the unmapped world — and I could not hold it out over the fire.*

*Do not be kinder to yourself than I was. Decide before you climb. Decide in the daylight, while your courage is still yours.*

*The second half of the rite is in the Spire's foundation-stone, set there by the first keepers. Reach it, and choose.*

*Maren Montrose, last keeper of the waystation — until you.*

Ada closed the journal.

For a long time she sat motionless in the dust and the failing light, the grey leather warm in her hands as though the book had been waiting forty years just to be held. Outside, the silver grass whispered its windless whisper. Inside, Emeric said nothing, and his silence was its own kindness.

Her mother. Not a bystander. Not a woman who merely looked away. Elara Montrose had built the siphons herself, had betrayed her own mother to the guild's enforcers, had done it believing she was *fixing* things — and had then come home and raised her daughter among the ledgers, teaching her precision, teaching her to see what maps concealed, and never once saying why.

Every lesson Ada had treasured was a stolen thing. Every skill she owned had been sharpened for the harvest.

"She knew," Ada said finally, and her voice cracked on the second word. "She knew what I'd become."

*"Or she hoped you'd become what Maren hoped,"* Emeric said softly. *"Mothers plant seeds for gardens they won't live to see. Sometimes the seed grows wild. Sometimes—"*

"Don't." Ada pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "Don't make it gentle. She sold her mother to them. She sold *me* to them, just slower."

The journal lay open on her knees. In the margin of the last page, in smaller script, almost an afterthought, Maren had written a single line that Ada read four times before she could breathe past it:

*If my granddaughter reads this — the ache you feel is not weakness. It is the shape of what you'll have to give.*

Ada looked up at the ruined hall, at the painted circles above the dead hearth, at the table that had kept faith for forty years. The otherworld's faded colors pressed against the windows like a tide going out. Somewhere beyond the silver fields, she knew, the guild's enforcers were moving, and behind them came Corvane himself, patient as rot.

She wrapped the journal and the vellum half-rite carefully in the oilcloth and tucked them inside her coat, against her heart.

"Emeric. The Spire. How far?"

His voice, when it came, was barely a thread now, worn thin as the moss on these walls. *"Three days' walk, if the land lets you. Less, if it remembers you kindly."*

"And the second half of the rite?"

*"In the foundation-stone. Where the first keepers set it."* A pause. *"Ada. Your grandmother wrote that you should decide before you climb. Have you decided?"*

Ada rose, brushed the dust from her knees, and walked to the doorway where the faded world waited. Far off, at the edge of sight, the air above the horizon shimmered — a column of pale, wounded light, like heat-haze in winter.

The Spire. Cracked. Weeping. Running out of time.

"Not yet," she said. "But I'm about to."

Behind her, far across the silver fields, something crossed the ridge line — small figures, moving fast, and at their head a shape that cast no shadow at all.
