## 7. The Hunt Through Hollow Light

They came through the border at dawn, or what passed for dawn in a world whose sun had forgotten how to burn.

Ada felt it before she saw it — a wrongness in the light, the way a sailor feels a storm in the set of his teeth. She stood at the edge of the Meadow of Small Hours, where the Vessels kept their last garden, and watched the horizon ripple like disturbed water. Colors drained from the grass in a slow tide, gray spreading outward from a single point, and through that point came men. Six of them first, then more behind them, boots striking ground that flinched beneath their weight.

And at their center, walking unhurried as a man strolling his own orchard, came Corvane.

She had seen him perhaps a dozen times in her years at the guild — always at a distance, always at the head of some procession, a tall figure in charcoal wool with silver at his temples and a voice like a ledger being closed. Up close he was smaller than she'd imagined, and that somehow made him worse. Small men who reached this far did not stop for anything.

"There," one of the enforcers said, pointing. "The cartographer."

"Miss Montrose." Corvane's voice carried across the meadow without effort, gentle as a teacher's. "You've caused a great deal of expense. Paper, ink, men's time. I do hate waste."

*Run,* Emeric's voice breathed from the map against her ribs, thin as smoke through a keyhole. *Ada. Run now.*

She ran.

The Vessels scattered before her like leaves — small beings of woven light and hollow bone, creatures that had once been the covenant's living seals and were now reduced to tending gardens in a starving world. One of them, the elder they called Sennet, pressed something into her hand as she passed: a seed no larger than a grain of salt, warm as a held coin.

"For the Spire," Sennet said. "When you climb. Plant it in the crack that weeps."

Then the gray tide reached the garden, and Ada heard the Vessels cry out — not with voices but with a sound like harp strings snapping, one after another after another.

She did not look back. Looking back was a luxury the dying could not afford, and the map against her chest pulsed once, twice, guiding her feet toward a fold in the landscape that no eye would find unless it knew how to read what wasn't drawn.

* * *

The otherworld was coming apart, and it came apart in ways that made her cartographer's heart break and her survival instinct scream in the same breath.

Rivers ran uphill into skies that forgot to hold them. Mountains softened like wax left in sun, their peaks drooping into valleys that rose to meet them. Whole forests existed only in glimpses — there when looked at sideways, gone when regarded directly. And everywhere the gray followed, the guild's harvest-mark, the color of everything they had ever taken and never returned.

Ada navigated by absence. It was the only craft the otherworld still permitted: reading the gaps between features, the negative space where roads had been, trusting the map's fading voice to name what no longer named itself.

"The Hollow Ford," Emeric whispered. His voice had been thinning for two days, each word costing him something she couldn't measure. "Left bank. The stones remember being a bridge even if the river doesn't. Step where the memory is strongest."

She stepped, and the ford held — a bridge made of nothing but insistence, the ghost of an idea refusing to dissolve while someone still believed in its shape. Halfway across, she felt the far end begin to soften under her, stone-memory giving way to mere water, and she sprinted the last ten strides as the ford unmade itself behind her with a sigh like a closing book.

Behind, on the far bank, enforcers milled at the river's edge. One tried the crossing where the ford had been and sank to his waist in water that hadn't decided whether to be solid. His companions hauled him out, cursing.

Corvane simply stood at the bank's edge and studied the opposite shore the way Ada studied ledgers — patiently, thoroughly, finding the pattern in it.

"He'll read the land," Emeric said. "He's been stealing from this world so long he's learned its grammar. Ada, listen to me." The voice caught, frayed, resumed. "There is a place ahead. The Gallery of Last Light. Your grandmother hid things there. But the way is—"

His voice dissolved into static, into silence, into the faintest whisper of ink settling on paper.

"Emeric?" She pulled the map from her coat and unfolded it with shaking hands. The lines were still there, still beautiful, but fainter — as though the paper itself were forgetting what it had been drawn to say. In the corner where his presence lived, a small constellation of marks that had always glimmered when he spoke now flickered unevenly, a candle drowning in its own wax.

"I'm here," he said at last, so quietly she had to hold the map against her ear like a shell. "I'm here. I don't know for how much longer. Every mile we cross spends me. I was only ever a memory, Ada, and memories are spent by being used."

"Then we'll be careful," she said. "We'll rest."

"We can't rest." A pause, and when he spoke again there was something new in his voice — not fear, but a fierce, exhausted clarity. "Corvane isn't following us, girl. He's *herding* us. Every path I've chosen has bent toward the Spire. I thought it was my own failing, my memory degrading, choosing wrong. But he knows these lands better than I do now. He wants us to run exactly where we're running."

Ada stared at the horizon, where the Spire was visible even at this distance — a needle of pale fire against a bruised sky, cracked and weeping light along its whole length.

"Then let him herd us," she said slowly. "He thinks he's driving us to our ruin. He doesn't know he's driving me to work."

* * *

The Gallery of Last Light had been a waystation once, in the centuries when keepers walked freely between worlds. Now it was a cavern of standing glass — pillars of crystallized dusk, each one holding a sliver of the otherworld's former glory: a sunset that had refused to end, a song frozen mid-phrase, the exact blue of a sky over a country that no longer existed.

Her grandmother had hidden things here. Emeric had told her that much before his voice failed again.

Ada found the hiding place by touch, running her fingers along the base of the tallest pillar until she found a seam that hummed against her skin the way the map did. Inside was a shallow cavity, and in the cavity, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age: half a page, covered in handwriting she recognized without ever having seen it before. Her own hand, older. Her grandmother's hand.

But she had no time to read it.

The gray came through the gallery entrance like floodwater, and with it the enforcers, and with them Corvane, and with them — Ada's stomach turned — three Vessels, bound in chains of cold iron, dragged stumbling across the glass floor. Sennet was among them. The elder's woven-light body had dimmed to the color of old ash.

"You keep making the same mistake," Corvane said, walking the gallery slowly, admiring the pillars. "You think this is about the map. The map is paper, Miss Montrose. Paper burns. What I want is what the map carries — the keeper's line, the covenant-key, the thing your grandmother sealed into her blood and passed down like a curse. You are the last of it. Which means you are the last lock on a door I intend to open permanently."

"The covenant protects both worlds."

"The covenant *protects*," Corvane agreed pleasantly. "That's the problem. Protection is scarcity, Miss Montrose. Boundaries. Rationing. For four hundred years your guild has skimmed the cream off a dying world because the covenant said we mustn't take too much. Well. The covenant is dying with the world it guards. When the Spire falls, there will be no more rules. No more rationing. Everything this realm has left — every wonder, every impossible thing — harvested clean, all at once, before it rots. Do you know what that would make us? Not thieves. *Heirs.*"

"It would make you vultures."

"It would make us rich." He smiled, and it was the smile of a man balancing books. "Your grandmother chose sacrifice. Your mother chose silence. You, I think, will choose sense. Or—" he gestured, almost gently, at Sennet, "—I will spend these creatures finding out how much a Vessel can endure before it unravels. They unravel beautifully, I'm told. Like thread."

Something in Ada went very quiet and very cold.

All her life she had been precise. Careful. The girl who checked sums twice, who never spoke out of turn, who had learned early that precision was armor. She had thought her defiance, when it finally came, would look like courage.

It didn't. It looked like arithmetic.

Three Vessels. Six enforcers. One master. One exit behind them, one passage out of the gallery that only she could read — the seam in the eastern wall, humming faintly, invisible to anyone who hadn't spent a lifetime reading what wasn't drawn.

*Emeric,* she thought at the map, not daring to speak. *The eastern wall. Can you buy me thirty seconds?*

A flicker in the corner of the folded paper. Then, faint as a dying man's pulse, but steady:

*I was a keeper's shadow once. Watch what a shadow can still do.*

Every pillar in the gallery lit at once.

Not brightly — dimly, gutteringly, like lamps at the end of their oil — but enough. Enough to throw shadows, and the shadows moved wrong, sliding across the walls against the direction of the light, pooling around the enforcers' ankles, climbing their legs like cold water. The men shouted. Iron chains clattered. In the confusion, Sennet — ash-dim, chain-bound — lifted his head and met Ada's eyes, and mouthed a single word: *east.*

She went east. Through the seam, into the dark, with half a page of her grandmother's handwriting pressed against her heart beside the fading map.

Behind her, Corvane's voice followed her into the passage, no longer pleasant, no longer patient, stripped down to the bare iron underneath:

"Run, then, little keeper. Run to your Spire. I've already sent men ahead to the summit — and they'll arrive long before you do. Everything you're racing toward, I already own."

* * *

Ada ran through dissolving country for hours, and somewhere in those hours, Emeric began to say goodbye.

He didn't frame it that way. He simply grew quieter, and his directions grew simpler, and once — crossing a plain where the grass sang in chords — he said, unprompted: "I taught your grandmother to read these lands. Did I tell you that? She was hopeless with rivers. Always drew them too straight." A pause. "You draw them honestly. That's rarer."

"Emeric—"

"Don't spend words on me. Spend them on the road." Another pause, longer. "Ada. When you reach the Spire. When you stand where I stood, four hundred years ago, making the bargain that bought this world its borrowed time — remember that we built it wrong from the start. We took the power and called it stewardship. We hoarded and called it duty. Whatever you reforge up there—" his voice thinned to almost nothing, "—reforge it *equal*. Promise me. Promise me the next covenant has no masters."

"I promise."

"Good girl." The faintest ghost of laughter. "Your grandmother hated when I called her that. I expect you do too."

And then, as the Spire rose full before her — vast and broken and beautiful, a tower grown from law itself, its wounds bleeding slow golden light into the darkening sky — his voice faded to a whisper, and the whisper to a shimmer, and the shimmer to the faint warmth of ink on paper, and Ada understood that she was carrying a dead man's last breath toward the place where he had died.

At the Spire's base, the land lay open and empty. Too empty.

She saw them then, high on the tower's flank: torchlight, small and crawling, enforcers already halfway to the summit. And below them, just entering the Spire's great cracked door, a figure in charcoal wool moving with the unhurried certainty of a man walking into his own inheritance.

Corvane had lied about one thing only: he hadn't sent his men ahead.

He had come himself to watch the door close.

Ada put her hand flat against the Spire's wounded stone, felt it shudder beneath her palm like a living thing, and began to climb.
