## 8. The Ascent of the Spire

She saw it first as a wound in the horizon.

The otherworld's sky had been dimming for hours — colors draining out of the clouds like water through a cracked bowl — and where the light failed worst, a shape stood against it, tall and narrow and wrong in the way that only true things are wrong. Not built, Ada understood as she drew closer across the pale grass. Grown. The Spire rose from the earth the way a tooth rises from a jaw, or a verdict from a judge's mouth: inevitable, and terrible, and old.

Up close it was worse. Up close she could see that its stones were not stones at all but clauses — dense, interlocking sentences of light pressed into solidity, each one humming faintly when her shadow crossed it. And every one of them was cracked. Fissures ran up the tower's length like veins of black ice, and from those fissures wept a slow, golden grief that pooled at the base and sank into starving ground.

Emeric's voice came thin from the map folded against her heart. *Careful how you touch it. It remembers hands.*

"Everything here remembers," Ada said. Her own voice sounded small. "That's rather the problem."

*No.* A pause, like a man gathering strength he no longer had. *That's rather the point.*

She set her palm against the lowest stone.

The world folded shut around her like a book.

---

*Firelight. Cold air. A circle of people on a hilltop that would one day be called the Spire's root, though there was no tower yet, only a standing stone and the smell of rain coming. She stood among them — not Ada, not quite; she was wearing Emeric's memory like an ill-fitting coat, seeing through eyes two hundred years dead.*

*A woman stepped into the circle's center. Tall, gray-eyed, with ink stains to the elbow. Ada knew the face before Emeric's memory named it, because she had grown up with a smaller version of it hanging over her grandmother's hearth.*

*Yelena Montrose spoke words in a language Ada did not know and somehow understood anyway: We do not take. We hold. What passes between your world and ours passes by consent, sealed in law, shared in measure. Let any who break this be broken by it.*

*And on the far side of the standing stone, beings of woven light — the Vessels, whole and radiant in their prime — bowed their heads in answer. The covenant poured itself down into the ground like a seed. And the first stone of the Spire rose to hold it.*

---

Ada came back to herself gasping, forehead pressed to the warm stone, tears freezing on her cheeks in the otherworld's thin air.

"That was her," she whispered. "That was really her."

*The making,* Emeric said. His voice was quieter now, frayed at the edges, a thread worn near to snapping. *I stood in that circle too, Ada. I was young then. I believed the words meant what they said.* Another pause, longer. *Climb. You'll want to see the rest before you decide anything.*

"What rest? What decision?"

But the stone beneath her hand had already begun to glow, and stairs were unfolding inside the fissure-light like a spine straightening, and there was nothing left to do but climb.

* * *

The Spire's interior was a throat of light.

Each stair was a clause; each landing, a memory. Ada climbed through the covenant's history the way she'd once climbed through her guild's ledgers — entry by entry, watching the numbers change. At the fortieth step she saw the first trade: a Vessel giving a single thread of its light freely, gladly, so that a drought-stricken village on the human side might have one impossible summer of rain. Consent, freely given. The law working as Yelena had written it.

At the hundredth step, the memories began to sour.

A guild master — not Corvane, someone older, his face smug beneath a hat Ada recognized from portraits in the Guildhall — pressing a Vessel for "just one more thread." The Vessel's light dimming a shade. A ledger entry appearing in the air above the scene, in Ada's own professional hand: *One (1) bottled starlight. Consideration rendered.*

"Consideration," Ada repeated bitterly. "They wrote it down as if it were fair."

*Paper makes thieves honest men,* Emeric murmured. *Keep climbing.*

At the two hundredth step she saw her grandmother again — older now, gray streaking her hair, standing in this same stairwell with her hand flat against the wall, arguing with a room full of guild masters whose faces blurred like bad engravings. *You're killing it,* Yelena's memory-voice rang off the stones. *Every thread you pull is a year off its life. The covenant was meant to be a door between equals, not a tap you leave running—*

And then a younger woman's voice, cold and reasonable, and Ada's stomach dropped through the floor of the memory because she knew that voice too. She'd heard it reading bedtime stories. Signing her first apprenticeship papers.

*Mother.*

*Your mother cast the vote,* Emeric said, very gently. *I'm sorry. I wanted you to see it here, in the law's own house, where it can't be argued away. Yelena tried to reforge the covenant. She got as far as the rite's first half. Then her own daughter signed the order that closed the border behind her, and the guild called it a mercy, and I—*

His voice caught. Frayed. For three full steps there was nothing but the hum of failing stone.

*I ran,* he finished. *I told myself I'd come back with help. I spent two hundred years being a coward with good intentions. Don't be me, Ada. Climb.*

She climbed.

The higher she went, the worse the cracks. Light wept from the walls in runnels now, and through the fissures she could see the otherworld below — the fading colors, the starved fields, the Vessels' dwindling fires — and beyond them, impossibly, a grey shimmer where the human world lay on the other side of everything. Two realms, visible at once through the wounds in the law that held them apart. Both of them bleeding.

Near the top, the last memory took her:

*Yelena, alone on the summit platform, journal open, half a ritual written in her careful cartographer's hand. She looked up — directly up, directly at Ada across two hundred years — and her gray eyes were tired and fierce and unbearably familiar.*

*"It asks a price," she said. "I was ready to pay it. Find out if you are."*

Then the memory released her, and Ada stood alone on the summit of the Spire, wind tearing at her coat, the whole dying otherworld spread out beneath her feet like a map no one had bothered to finish.

* * *

The summit was a broken crown of covenant-stone, wide enough for perhaps twenty people, open to a sky the color of a bruise healing. In the center, a socket of light — the anchor-point itself, guttering like a candle in a draft. Half a ritual circle was still inscribed there, Yelena's work, patient and precise even after two centuries of weather. Waiting.

Ada knelt beside it and unrolled the map. Emeric's lines glowed faintly, and his voice, when it came, was barely a whisper now.

*The second half is in my memory. What's left of it. When I'm gone, Ada, it goes with me — so listen, and remember, because after tonight there will be no one left who—*

"Stop." Her voice cracked. "Don't talk like that."

*Cartographer.* She could hear the smile in it, thin as paper. *Everything ends. That's why we draw maps. So the ones who come after can find their way.*

She was still kneeling there, memorizing the cadence of his fading voice, when the wind changed.

It brought the smell of tallow and hot iron — guild-smell, Guildhall-smell — which had no business existing on this side of the border. Ada was on her feet with the map clutched to her chest before the first boot rang on the summit stairs.

Corvane climbed slowly, unhurried, his gray coat immaculate despite the crossing, six enforcers fanning out behind him with the practiced economy of men who had done terrible things in beautiful places before. The Spire's weeping light gilded them all like saints in a window. None of them deserved it.

"Montrose." He said her name the way he'd said it in the ledger hall, years ago, the day she'd been promoted: warmly, and with ownership. "You've made a remarkable mess of things. Chasing ghosts. Believing maps." He gestured at the guttering anchor. "Do you know what happens if you finish that rite?"

"The covenant reforges. Equal exchange. The theft stops."

"The *trade* stops." His voice stayed pleasant, which was how she knew they'd passed beyond all pleasantness forever. "Do you understand what this tower is, girl? It's not a door. It's a *well*. Two hundred years of accumulated power, sitting right there, and you propose to pour it back into the dirt." He spread his hands. "The guild survives the collapse, Ada. We always survive. We harvest what remains, we rebuild on the human side, and in fifty years no one remembers there was ever another world at all. But you—" He smiled. "You could have something better than survival. Your grandmother founded our greatest age. Your mother nearly matched her. Walk away now, and the Spire falls on its own, blameless, and I make you Master of Archives by winter. Full access. Every sealed vault. Every impossible thing." He let the silence stretch, let the offer glitter. "Your inheritance, Ada. All of it. All you have to do is stop climbing."

Below them, the otherworld flickered — a whole realm guttering like the candle it had become. Somewhere in the fading fields, the Vessels who had died buying her time were already forgotten by everyone but her and a fraying ghost.

Ada thought about the sealed vaults. About the ledgers, complete at last. About never having to wonder again.

Then she thought of her grandmother's face in the firelight-memory, saying *find out if you are*, and of Emeric's voice going quiet in her hands, and of a river somewhere that had once had a first word, and what it must have cost to bottle it.

"You're offering me the family business," she said. "My family's business is robbery."

Corvane's smile didn't move, but something behind his eyes did — the shutter coming down, the warmth draining out like color from the sky. "Last chance, Montrose."

"No," Ada said, and turned to face the anchor, and unfolded the map. "But thank you for showing me exactly what I'm refusing."

Behind her, steel cleared leather, six times in unison.

Above her, the last of Emeric's light gathered in the map's lines like a breath drawn to speak.

"Begin," he whispered.
