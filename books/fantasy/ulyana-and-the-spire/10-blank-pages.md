## 10. Blank Pages

Ada woke with her cheek against the drafting table and the taste of light in her mouth.

That was the only way she could describe it — a taste, faint and mineral, like snowmelt from a mountain she had never climbed. It sat on her tongue as she lifted her head, blinking against the grey morning that seeped through the shutters of her mapping room. Her neck ached. Her hands were stiff, curled as though they had been gripping something all night.

There was nothing in them.

She straightened slowly, and the room resolved around her the way rooms did: the inkwell with its chipped rim, the brass calipers hanging on their hook, the window with its view of the canal district's slate roofs going gold at the edges. Ordinary. All of it ordinary. And yet something was wrong with the ordinariness, some small displacement, like a picture hung a finger's width off true.

The ledgers.

She saw them first because they were always first — forty years of the Cartographers' Guild's accounts stacked in the corner where she'd left them for audit, bound in green leather, their spines cracked from handling. She crossed to them out of habit, out of the auditor's reflex that lived in her bones, and opened the top one to yesterday's page.

Blank.

Not smudged, not water-damaged, not redacted. Blank. The paper was clean and white and slightly warm, as if it had been freshly pressed. She turned the page. Blank. Another. Blank. She went through the stack with increasing speed, pages whispering past her thumb like birds taking flight, and every leaf of every ledger was empty — no bottled starlight, no river's first word, no columns of prices paid for things that could not exist. No record at all of the trade that had fed the guild for generations.

She stood very still in the middle of the room.

*The Spire remembers her name.* Someone had said that to her once. The words surfaced without context, without a face attached, and left an ache behind them — a grief shaped exactly like a person, worn smooth by absence. She pressed her hand to her sternum as though she could hold the shape there, keep it from dissolving.

There had been someone. She was almost sure there had been someone.

* * *

The city outside was brighter than she remembered it being.

She noticed it on the walk to the guildhall — the way the morning light came down between the buildings not in slats but in whole unbroken sheets, the way the canal water held the sky so faithfully that the barges seemed to sail through clouds. Colors were more themselves than colors usually were. A fruit-seller's oranges burned like small suns. A child's red coat made Ada's breath catch for no reason she could name.

And people were looking up. That was the strangest thing. In all her years in this city she had never seen so many faces tilted toward the sky, wearing expressions she could only call homesick — as if everyone in the street had woken from a dream of somewhere beautiful and couldn't quite let it go.

The guildhall doors stood open. Not unlocked — gone. The great iron-studded oak that had sealed the guild from the city for two centuries had been removed entire, leaving a doorway open to the street like a mouth with no teeth. Inside, clerks moved through the halls carrying boxes. Enforcers — she recognized the grey coats, though the men inside them looked smaller than she remembered, deflated, ordinary — were stacking ledgers into crates marked for burning.

No one stopped her. No one even looked at her strangely.

In the master's gallery, where Corvane's portrait had hung — she knew the name the way she knew the ache in her chest, a fact without a story — the wall was bare except for a pale rectangle of unfaded plaster. Beneath it, pinned to the paneling, a single sheet of guild stationery:

*By unanimous resolution of the remaining members, the Cartographers' Guild is dissolved. Its charter is void. Its holdings are released to the city. Its debts are forgiven, for they can no longer be counted.*

Dated yesterday. Signed by names she half-recognized, senior cartographers whose faces floated behind a fog when she tried to summon them.

Ada read it three times. Then she laughed — a short, startled sound that echoed oddly in the stripped hall — because the resolution read like an obituary written by people who had forgotten who had died, or why, or what they had been mourning.

She walked home through streets that smelled, faintly and impossibly, of rain that hadn't fallen yet.

* * *

Her desk had been tidied while she slept. That was the second wrongness, after the ledgers: her instruments squared away, her pens rinsed and capped, her chair pushed in. And in the center of the clean surface lay two things.

A map, folded once. And a note.

She touched the map first. The paper was heavy and cream-colored, good stock, the kind the guild reserved for commissions — but it was empty. Not blank the way the ledgers were blank, scoured and emptied. This was blank the way a field is blank before planting: waiting, fertile, patient. When she ran her fingers across it she felt something underneath the surface, the way you feel water under ice. Not lines. Not yet. But the *possibility* of lines, humming faintly against her skin like a word about to be spoken.

Her eyes stung. She didn't know why. She wiped them impatiently and picked up the note instead.

The hand was old-fashioned, looped, confident — a hand trained in a stricter century. She didn't recognize it. And yet her body recognized it before her mind could object; her throat tightened, her eyes filled again, and she had to sit down before she could read the four words written there:

*Some inheritances are meant to be ended.*

Below it, smaller, almost an afterthought:

*Draw something new, little wren.*

Little wren. The endearment landed in her chest like a key turning in a lock she hadn't known was there. She pressed the note to her lips. She said, aloud, to the empty room, "Grandmother?" — and the word cracked in the middle, and whatever answer might have come, she felt it only as warmth, brief and passing, like sunlight moving off a wall.

She understood then what she had paid. Not the details — those were gone, scoured as clean as the ledgers — but the shape of it. She had traded something enormous. A whole country of memory, sold at the summit of some tower she could not now picture, to seal something she could not now doubt. The evidence was everywhere: the bright air, the open doors, the empty books, the ache. Especially the ache. Grief without content, love without a face, wonder with the wonder itself subtracted, leaving only the hollow where it had been.

She could have raged at that. Part of her wanted to — wanted to shake her fists at whatever law had accepted her payment and given no receipt. But sitting there in the quiet room, holding a stranger's handwriting that was not a stranger's, she found the rage wouldn't take. What remained instead was something quieter and harder to name. Gratitude, maybe. Or the particular peace of a debt honestly paid.

They had taken her memories of the unmapped realms. They had not taken her craft.

* * *

She worked through the afternoon and into the evening, and did not light a lamp until the last blue light failed, because the daylight was too good to waste.

She began, as she always began, with water. Her pen moved over the blank map and the blank map *received* it — that was the only word. The ink sank in and did not sit; the lines seemed to grow rather than be drawn, rising to meet her nib like seedlings toward sun. She drew a coastline she had never surveyed and knew instantly to be true. She drew a river and gave it, on impulse, a bend shaped like a signature, and felt absurdly that the river approved.

Somewhere in the drawing she stopped being an auditor of other people's impossible things and became simply a maker of possible ones. It was the same precision, the same patience, pointed in a new direction. Every line she laid down was a promise: *this exists, this counts, this is worth the ink.* She thought, without knowing why, of a voice thinning into static, of hands letting go, of a door held open at great cost. She thought: *I'll put you in here somehow. I don't remember you, but I'll make room.*

The candle guttered low before she surfaced from the work. The map had grown under her pen until it covered the whole desk — coasts, ranges, a delta braided like hair, and in the center, unfinished, the suggestion of a tower. She had drawn it without deciding to. A tall shape, slender, crowned with light, its stones rendered in fine hatching that made them look almost alive.

She stared at it for a long time. Her chest ached in that familiar hollow way, but the ache had changed key. It sounded less like loss now. More like recognition.

On the lowest line of the map, where the land met the paper's edge, she wrote a small notation in her neatest hand — the kind of marginalia she'd spent her career decoding in other people's charts:

*Here begins the survey. First entry, new covenant. Keeper: A. Montrose.*

Keeper. She tested the word and found it fit, the way a well-made glove fits a hand she didn't know she'd inherited.

Outside, the city glowed softly under a sky full of more stars than any star chart she had ever corrected. Somewhere far off — beyond the hills, beyond the borders, beyond the edge of every map ever drawn — she imagined she heard water moving, and a voice in the water, saying a word she almost caught.

Ada dipped her pen. The blank space at the map's heart waited, patient as morning.

She set the nib down and began.
