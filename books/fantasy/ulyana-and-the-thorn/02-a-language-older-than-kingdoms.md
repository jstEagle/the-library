## 2. A Language Older Than Kingdoms

The child did not sleep so much as fall into it, the way a stone falls into a well — all at once, without negotiation.

Vesper had given her the chair by the stove, wrapped in his own coat because the Guild of Impossible Things kept its receiving room cold on principle; warmth, Corvane liked to say, made people careless with their confessions. She had curled into the wool like a fox into snow, mud still drying in the seams of her dress, and the thorn in her palm had gone on burning its small colorless fire against her skin without scorching either of them.

He should have logged her arrival immediately. That was procedure. Item acquired: one (1) child, age indeterminate, bearing one (1) unclassified artifact, luminous, non-thermal. Provenance unknown. He could already see the entry in his own careful hand, the way he saw every entry before he wrote it, each letter standing at attention like soldiers on review.

Instead he sat across from her and watched her breathe, and felt the particular vertigo of a man whose ledgers have suddenly stopped balancing.

The guild-house creaked around him in its old way — the bottled echoes on the third shelf of the west cabinet murmuring to one another in voices stolen from funerals and festivals, the folded distances in their lacquered boxes ticking softly as they dreamed of being unfolded. Vesper had spent eleven years among these sounds. They were the sounds of order. Nothing in this room had ever frightened him before tonight.

The thorn pulsed.

Not flickered — *pulsed*, a slow contraction like a heartbeat seen through skin, and with each pulse the light in the room changed. It was not that the thorn grew brighter. It was that everything else grew briefly more honest: the grain of the table leapt into sharp relief, the dust motes hung like fixed stars, and for half a heartbeat Vesper could see the shape of the air itself, vast and patient and old.

Then the pulse passed, and the room was only a room again, and the child slept on with her fist curled protectively around the thing that was eating her sleep, or feeding it, he could not tell which.

"Ulyana," he said softly, testing the name she had given him at the door. It was the only word she'd offered that any tongue of his recognized. "Can you hear me?"

She answered in her sleep.

It was not a word. It was a weather system of sound — vowels that opened like doors onto rooms he had never entered, consonants that clicked and hummed in places behind the teeth where no language he knew kept furniture. And yet. And yet something in the middle of it snagged him, caught his ear the way a bramble catches a sleeve, and held.

*Vess-ka-orun,* she breathed. *Vess-ka-orun, i thal.*

He went very still.

He knew those sounds. Not their meaning — never their meaning — but their *shape*. Eleven years ago, a junior cataloguer with ink-stained cuffs and an eagerness he had since learned to ration, Vesper Amberdale had been handed a crate from a confiscation in the eastern marches: maps seized from a cartographer who had drawn places no crown sanctioned. Most had been burned, per guild statute. One fragment had been set aside for study, because it was written in no known script, and the guild collected what it could not read the way misers collect coins they cannot spend.

Vesper had catalogued it. Entry 4,417: *Map fragment, provenance disputed, inscribed in an unidentified script. Linguistic analysis: inconclusive. Note: the script appears older than the parchment, which is itself impossible. Recommend restricted classification.* And beneath, in smaller letters, the note that had earned him a week's reprimand: *Recommend we find someone who can read it aloud.*

Corvane had burned that note out of the ledger with a candle held close enough to scorch the page. *We do not speak what we cannot appraise, Amberdale. Words are inventory. Inventory is power. Power spoken carelessly is loss.*

But Vesper had copied the fragment first. He had been young, and the shapes had been beautiful, and beauty was the one debt he had never learned to stop accruing.

He crossed the receiving room, unlocked the drawer beneath the appraisal desk — three locks, because the drawer held things the guild trusted to no single failure — and drew out a thin folio of tracing-paper rubbings he had told himself for eleven years he kept only for completeness' sake.

The child spoke again in her sleep, and this time he was ready. He laid the rubbings beside her voice as if laying a key beside a lock, matching the sounds to the shapes by feel rather than reason, the way one matches a tune hummed by a stranger to a song half-remembered from childhood.

*I thal,* she murmured. On the rubbing: two characters, the second shaped like a flame bent double, like fire bowing to something.

*Orun.* A character like a door seen edge-on.

*Vess-ka.* His own name, or near enough that his hands trembled — though surely not, surely it was coincidence, surely a dead cartographer's alphabet had no opinion of a guild clerk named after evening starlight —

Except that *vess* meant *evening* in the old trade-tongue, and *ka* meant *keeper*, and the character beside it on the rubbing was the one the confiscated map used to mark, according to the sole annotation anyone had ever deciphered, *the place where the road remembers you.*

The thorn pulsed again, harder, and this time Vesper did not merely see the room grow honest.

He fell into it.

* * *

There was a fire, and it was in a cage, and the cage was the size of a cathedral and the fire was the size of a heart, and both of these facts were true simultaneously in the way that things are true in visions, which is to say completely and uselessly.

He stood — if standing was the word — in a dark that was not empty but *full*, packed with stone that had been stone longer than stone had had a name. Before him, behind bars that were not iron but something older and more patient, burned a light the color of the last coal in a dying grate: deep orange shot through with gold, guttering, magnificent, *afraid*.

He understood, in the wordless grammar of vision, that it had been afraid for a very long time. That it had once been vast enough to shadow valleys. That something had reached into it with hands like winter and torn a piece away, and the piece had run, and run, and become small, and become a girl, and knocked at midnight on the door of a clerk who kept accounts of impossible things.

The fire turned toward him. It had no eyes, but it looked at him the way the sea looks at a shoreline.

*Vess-ka-orun,* it said, in Ulyana's voice, in the child's sleeping breath, in the voice of the map fragment and the voice under all maps. *Evening-keeper-of-doors. I thal. The fire is borrowed. The fire is coming home. Will you open the door?*

"I don't know how," Vesper said, and his voice in the vision was his own, small and precise, a clerk's voice, an accountant's voice, absurd in that cathedral dark. "I keep records. I don't — I have never once in my life opened anything that mattered."

The fire guttered. And in its guttering he saw, brief as a struck match, the shape of the cage from outside: a hall of black marble, a throne, a figure seated upon it wearing a crown that drank the light around its head the way a leech drinks blood, and the figure's shadow moving wrong, moving *first*, moving like something that wore the king as a coat wears a man.

Then the vision snapped shut like a ledger closing, and Vesper was on his knees on the receiving-room floor with his palms flat against cold boards, and dawn was perhaps two hours off, and the child was sitting up in the chair watching him with eyes too old for her face.

"You saw him," she said. Her new-tongue was broken, accented, assembled from stolen pieces — but her meaning came through like light through a cracked shutter. "The hollow one. In the crown."

"The king," Vesper said. His mouth was dry. "You saw the king."

"Not king." She shook her head with a fierce, exhausted patience, the patience of someone who has explained this to burning houses. "Wears king. Like coat." She touched her own chest, then gestured outward, at the walls, at the whole sleeping city. "Under — nothing. Empty. Empty thing wants fire. My fire." Her fist tightened around the thorn. "I bring home."

And there it was, laid out plain as a column of figures: the debt, the debtor, the terms. A child carrying the last ember of the last dragon toward the thing that had stolen the rest of it, walking willingly into the jaws of a wraith that needed exactly what she carried.

Every instinct Vesper had honed in eleven years of service said the same thing: *log it, report it, let the guild decide.* The guild had procedures for artifacts of this magnitude. The guild had vaults and wards and Guild Master Corvane, who had once stared down a living storm and negotiated its rainfall down to a drizzle.

Every instinct, and underneath them all, quieter and older, the thing that had copied the forbidden map, that had written *find someone who can read it aloud* and meant it, that had never once stopped believing that some entries were not possessions but promises.

Boots sounded on the stair. Two sets, heavy, unhurried — the tread of guild wardens, and behind them a third step he knew better than his own heartbeat: Corvane's cane, striking the boards at exact intervals, a metronome for the end of privacy.

Vesper stood up. He moved between the child and the door without deciding to, the way one's hand moves to catch a falling glass, and he heard himself say, in a voice steady enough to surprise him:

"She's asleep. Whatever this is, it will keep until morning."

"It has kept a thousand years, Amberdale." Corvane filled the doorway the way winter fills a valley — not entering so much as arriving. His eyes, pale as valuation marks, went past Vesper to the child, to the fist glowing faintly at her breast, and something moved through his face that Vesper had never seen there before and would spend the rest of his life learning to fear.

It was hunger, wearing the mask of scholarship.

"Wake her," said the Guild Master of Impossible Things, softly. "The appraisal begins now. And Amberdale—" the cane tapped once, twice, a signature at the bottom of a decree, "—from this moment, the child is guild property. See that she is held for examination. See that nothing leaves this house."

*Nothing,* Vesper thought, as the wardens stepped forward and the thorn pulsed once against the dark, like a heart making up its mind.

*Not even the truth.*
