## 3. What the Crown Wears

Ulyana remembers the burning in the shape of a bird.

Not because there was a bird — though there was, once, a wren that nested in the eaves of Mara's bakehouse and sang before dawn like it was owed something — but because fire moves like wings when it is hungry. It lifts. It spreads. It takes the roof of one house and flies to the next without touching the ground between.

She was running when the bakehouse went. She did not look back. Looking back is how they catch you. Her grandmother said this in the old tongue, the tongue with no name that lives under all other tongues like water under ice: *Do not turn your eyes behind you. The past has teeth.*

But she looked. Of course she looked. She was seven years old and her whole village was becoming light.

* * *

Before the burning there was the capital, and before the capital there was the comet, and before the comet there was only Ulyana, who was nobody, who was small, who slept in the loft above her grandmother's loom and dreamed ordinary dreams about bread and rivers.

Then the comet came over the mountains with its tail of green fire, and every child born beneath it woke in the night screaming, or laughing, or speaking words their mothers did not know. Ulyana woke speaking. The words came up out of her like fish rising to feed — old words, deep words, words that made the candle flames stand straight and listen.

Her grandmother went white as flour. She held Ulyana's face in hands that smelled of wool and ash-wood and said, very quietly, in the old tongue: *So it is you. So it was always going to be you.*

Ulyana did not understand then. She understands now. Understanding is a thing that comes to children the way winter comes to fields — slowly, and then all at once, and then it is too late to plant anything.

* * *

They fled to the capital because her grandmother said the capital was large, and large places lose small people. That was the plan: be lost. Be a needle in the city's great grey haystack. Find work. Keep the old tongue locked behind her teeth like a stone in a shoe.

For one season, it worked.

The capital was called Cor Aldren, which in the new-tongue means *the heart of the realm*, but Ulyana had already begun to learn that names lie the way maps lie — smoothly, and for reasons. Cor Aldren was a heart, yes. But hearts can be hollowed. She learned that from watching the king.

You could see him from the lower terraces on feast days, when the palace gates opened and the procession came down the white road. King Aldric. They said he had been a good king once — a hunter, a builder, a man who laughed too loud and paid his debts. But the man on the white horse did not laugh. The man on the white horse smiled, and his smile was like a door painted on a wall. You could see it was a smile. You could not go through it.

And his shadow moved wrong.

This is the thing Ulyana never told anyone until there was no one left to tell. The sun stood east over the palace spires, and every shadow in the square leaned west — the banners, the guards, the beggars, the birds. All except one. The king's shadow leaned north. Always north. Toward the mountains where the last dragon was said to sleep, or sleep no longer, or be only bones and a grief too big for bones to hold.

Shadows are honest things. They go where the light tells them. A shadow that disobeys the light belongs to something else entirely.

She told her grandmother. Her grandmother pulled her into the alcove of a shuttered shrine and pressed one finger hard against her lips.

*Some truths are doors,* the old woman signed in the old tongue, quick and small. *And some doors open onto things that eat.*

* * *

The guards came at the turn of the season, when the first snows grayed the rooftops.

They were looking for children. This was not secret; it was almost polite. A proclamation nailed to every gatepost, written in fine curling script: *By order of the Crown, all children born under the Green Wanderer must be presented for blessing.* Blessing. The word sat wrong in Ulyana's mouth, like meat gone off. In the old tongue there was a word that fit better, a word her grandmother used for traps baited with kindness: *snaresweet.*

The ones who went for blessing did not come back.

Their mothers said they had been taken to the palace school, to be educated, honored. But mothers know the weight of an empty bed, and the women of the lower quarter began to speak in doorways instead of streets, and to hide their comet-children in cellars and chimneys and the false bottoms of grain carts.

Ulyana hid in plainness. She cut her hair short with a fish knife and rubbed ash on her face and walked with a limp she invented herself, one shoulder dropped, one foot dragging. Small places lose small people. Plain places lose plainer ones still.

It might have held. It might have held for years.

But the thorn chose its moment the way fire chooses draft.

* * *

It came to her in a dream first — or what she thought was a dream. She stood in a dark so complete it felt like being unborn, and far away burned a single point of red-gold light, and the light was hurting. Not flickering. Hurting. She knew this the way you know your own hand is broken even in the dark: by the shape of the pain.

A voice came out of the dark, and it was not a voice so much as a warmth, a vast slow breathing that smelled of hot stone and rain-on-cinders.

*Little ember,* it said. Or that is how the words come back to her now, translated into everything she has lost. *Little ember, they have torn out my heart and crowned it in chains. I am guttering. I am guttering, and when I go out, the world goes with me — not all at once, but the way a house goes cold, room by room, and no one notices until the water will not run and the bread will not rise and the green things forget their names.*

*I am small,* Ulyana told it. *I am nothing. I am hiding in a city that eats children.*

*Yes,* said the warmth, and she felt — impossibly, unbearably — that it was smiling. *That is why it must be you. Only the small can pass where the great are watched. Only the nothing can carry the everything.*

When she woke, her palm was bleeding.

In it lay a thorn the length of her smallest finger, black as cooled iron, veined with a red that moved. It did not burn her. It burned *for* her, the way a hearth burns for the house it warms. And it gave no heat — that was the strangeness, the wrongness, the thing that made her grandmother take one look and begin packing.

Heat draws eyes. This drew none. It was fire that had learned silence.

*Take it home,* her grandmother said, and meant the mountain, the vault, the sleeping-or-not-sleeping dark beneath the world. *Take it home before the crown feels it missing. Because it will feel it, child. What wears that crown has been starving a long time, and it knows the taste of what it stole.*

They did not get three days.

* * *

The soldiers came at dusk, and their eyes were the worst part. Not cruel. Not angry. *Empty*, like windows of a house where no one has lived for years. They moved together, turned together, breathed together, and when their captain spoke, his voice had the flatness of a bell with the clapper removed.

*The child,* he said. *Born under the Green Wanderer. Surrender her for blessing.*

Her grandmother put herself in the doorway, small and straight as a spindle, and spoke to them in the new-tongue, telling them there was no such child here, only an old woman and her lame granddaughter. And Ulyana, crouched in the loft with the thorn clenched in her fist, watched through the gap in the floorboards as the captain tilted his head — that smooth, jointless tilt — and said:

*We smell the fire on her.*

Then the torches came out, though the law said no burning inside the walls, though the neighbors shouted, though it made no sense at all unless the point was never to capture her but simply to leave nowhere for her to hide. Fire fills every room. Fire asks no questions.

Her grandmother's last act was to throw her out the back window into the snow and sign one word after her, fast and fierce, the old tongue flashing from her fingers like a bird released:

*Run.*

So Ulyana ran. Through the cabbage gardens, over the frozen ditch, into the pine dark beyond the walls, while behind her the wings of the fire lifted and spread and took the roof of her grandmother's house and flew to the next one without touching the ground between.

She did not stop running for two days. She ate snow. She slept standing up, wedged in the fork of a tree like a dropped egg. And always in her closed fist the thorn pulsed, patient, warm-without-warmth, whispering directions in the language under all languages: *north, little ember, north and west, follow the old roads, find the keepers of impossible things, they owe my kind a debt older than their guild, older than their kingdoms, older than their names.*

* * *

Now she stands in the snow before a crooked door in a crooked town, and the door belongs to a man whose window-light smells of ink and candle-smoke and careful, counted things. She is mud to the knees. Her feet are stones. Her voice, when she uses the new-tongue, comes out bent and broken, a bird with a hurt wing — *please, sir, please, I have — I carrying — fire, no burn, please* — and she hates how small it makes her sound, how lost, when inside her chest the true words sit coiled and waiting, old as mountains, ready.

Let them think her simple. Let them think her a stray. The fox does not correct the hens' opinion of the henhouse cat.

Behind her, far to the south, something wearing a king leans north in its shadow and opens painted-door eyes. It has felt the thorn moving toward home. It is beginning, in its hollow patient way, to smile.

Ulyana raises her fist and knocks.

The thorn beats against her palm like a second heart — *once, twice, again* — and somewhere below the world, in a dark that is learning how to die, the last dragon stirs at the sound and mistakes it, just for a moment, for hope.
