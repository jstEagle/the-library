## 4. The Appraisal

Corvane arrived at dawn, as he always did, before the guild's lamps had finished their long argument with the morning.

Vesper heard him first — the particular cadence of the Master's boots on the stair, unhurried, each step placed like a signature on a document. Then the smell: cold iron and old parchment and something beneath it, faintly sweet, like fruit rotting in a locked room. The Guild of Impossible Things had many smells. Corvane carried all of them at once, the way a ledger carries every debt it has ever recorded.

He entered the counting room without knocking. Masters did not knock. The door swung wide and let in a blade of grey light that fell across Vesper's desk, across the child asleep in the chair beside it, across the small clenched fist resting on her knee where something burned without burning.

Ulyana had not stirred all night. She slept the way the drowned must sleep, Vesper thought — utterly, helplessly, with her whole weight given over to it. Mud still streaked her cheeks where she had not managed to wash it away. Her breathing was slow and even, and with each exhale the thorn in her palm pulsed once, softly, like a heart remembering its work.

"Master Corvane." Vesper rose. His chair scraped. In the silence of the counting room the sound was enormous.

Corvane did not look at him. He was looking at the child, and his face wore the expression Vesper had seen him wear only twice before — once over a bottled echo of the last word ever spoken by a dying god, and once over an invoice for a folded distance that had never been returned. It was the expression of a man doing arithmetic.

"A child," Corvane said. "In my counting room."

"She came to the door at midnight. She was—"

"I know when she came. The door told me." Corvane crossed the room in five steps and stood over Ulyana, his shadow falling across her like a dropped cloth. "The doors tell me everything, Amberdale. That is what doors are *for*."

The thorn pulsed. And this time, in the grey light of dawn, Vesper saw what he had half-seen in candlelight and refused to name: the light inside it was not steady. It guttered. It flared and dimmed and flared again, and each dimming lasted a fraction longer than the one before, the way a fire dims when no one feeds it.

Corvane saw it too. Something moved behind his eyes — hunger, Vesper thought, or its close cousin, fear.

"Wake her," the Master said.

"I don't think—"

"*Wake her.*"

Vesper touched Ulyana's shoulder. Her eyes opened at once, fully awake, no fog of sleep in them at all, and Vesper understood that she had not truly been sleeping — that some part of her had been standing guard all night behind her closed lids, the way a lighthouse keeps its vigil through weather. She looked at Corvane. She looked at the thorn. Slowly, deliberately, she closed her fingers over it, hiding the light.

Corvane smiled. It was not a comforting sight. His smile had the quality of a trap being oiled.

"You have something of ours," he said to her, gently, in the tone men use for children and for animals they intend to keep. "Something very old. Something that belongs in a vault, under glass, catalogued and appraised and *safe*. Do you understand me?"

Ulyana said nothing. But she spoke — Vesper saw her lips move, saw the shape of words in that language older than kingdoms, and though he could not parse them, he knew a refusal when he heard one. He had spent eleven years reading the guild's ledgers. He knew the grammar of *no* in every tongue that had ever tried to say it.

"She doesn't speak the common tongue," Vesper said. "Or anything else we've catalogued. I've tried the translator's lexicons, all nine—"

"Then we will appraise what she carries instead of what she says." Corvane straightened. "Bring it to the examination room. Both of them. If the vessel is attached to the artifact, we inventory the vessel as well."

*The vessel.* Vesper felt the word go into him like a splinter.

"Master," he said, and was surprised by how steady his own voice came out, "she's a child. Not an entry."

Corvane turned and regarded him for a long moment. In that look Vesper saw eleven years of his own careful, invisible, meticulous service weighed and found adequate — adequate the way a lock is adequate, the way a cage is adequate.

"Everything," Corvane said, "is an entry. That is the first lesson of this guild, and I had hoped you had learned it. The second lesson—" he glanced back at the sleeping-waking child, at the fist hiding its small captive sun, "—is that entries which walk in off the street at midnight are either miracles or liabilities, and the guild's entire purpose, Amberdale, is to make certain there is no difference."

* * *

The examination room sat at the heart of the guildhall, windowless, lit by lamps that burned a color lamps should not burn. Its walls were lined with instruments whose purposes Vesper had catalogued but never dared to ask about: scales that weighed intention; a bell that rang only for lies; a great lens of smoked glass through which, they said, one could see the true shape of any object, stripped of its disguises.

They set Ulyana on a stool in the center of the room. She went where they guided her with a terrible docility that frightened Vesper more than struggle would have — the docility of someone conserving strength, biding, watching. Her eyes found his across the room and held them, and in them was a question he could not answer and would not translate even if he could.

Corvane donned gloves of grey leather. He lifted the smoked lens.

"Hold out your hand, child."

She did not move.

"Hold out your hand," Corvane repeated, "or I will hold it out for you, and I promise you will prefer your own way of doing it."

Vesper stepped forward. "Master, perhaps if I—"

"Amberdale." Corvane did not turn. "You are a keeper of records. Keep them. Record."

And because he was what the guild had made him — because obedience had been poured into him year by year like wax into a mold — Vesper stepped back, took up his ledger, and opened it to a fresh page. His pen hovered. His hand, he noticed distantly, was shaking.

Ulyana opened her hand.

The thorn lay on her palm like a splinter pulled from the world's own flesh — black at the base, translucent toward the tip, and along its length ran veins of light that moved, that *circulated*, that lived. Through the smoked lens it must have shown differently. Vesper could not see what the lens showed Corvane. But he saw Corvane's face, and Corvane's face was the face of a man who has just been handed the deed to a kingdom he had never dared covet.

"Oh," the Master breathed. "Oh, you beautiful, impossible thing."

He reached down. Ulyana flinched — a full-body flinch, the flinch of a creature that has been burned before — but Corvane's gloved fingers closed on the thorn and lifted it from her palm, and she made a sound then, small and terrible, the sound of a word in her old language that needed no translation at all.

The moment the thorn left her skin, the light in the room changed. The lamps leaned toward it. The shadows bent. Somewhere in the walls, Vesper would swear afterward, the guildhall itself groaned — the deep structural groan of a building recognizing its master.

"Do you know what this is?" Corvane whispered. He was not asking anyone. He was asking the thorn. "Heart-fire. True heart-fire. The last of it in creation — the last dragon died three hundred years ago, child, and its heart was supposed to have gone to ash with it, and here it is, here it *is*, a fragment torn loose and walking around in the hand of a mud-streaked beggar girl." He laughed, low and delighted. "Do you understand what this is worth? Not coin. Coin is for horses and houses. This is worth more than every ledger in this building combined. More than the building. More than the *guild*. There are kings who would trade their crowns for an ember of this, and wraiths who would trade their souls, and I—" he caught himself, smoothed his face, became again the Master, the accountant, the man of measured appetites, "—I will of course follow proper procedure. Appraisal. Cataloguing. Vault storage. All of it."

He set the thorn in a case of black glass on the instrument table. Inside the case, the light beat against the walls like a bird against a window.

"The appraisal will take until evening," Corvane said briskly, already moving toward the door. "No one touches the case. No one enters. Amberdale — the provisional entry. Now."

Vesper wrote it. *One (1) thorn, origin unknown, luminous, recovered from foundling.* His pen scratched in the quiet. Behind him he heard Ulyana breathing — fast now, shallow, the breathing of a small animal in a snare — and he heard the soft tick of the thorn's light against the black glass, patient as a clock, patient as something that had waited three hundred years and could wait three hundred more.

*Provisional,* he wrote, and hated the word, and kept writing.

* * *

Evening came, and with it Corvane's final instruction: the thorn would be transferred to the deep vault at midnight, under guard, under seal. Until then the examination room stayed locked, and Vesper was to complete the permanent entry in the master ledger — the one written in ink that could never be amended, the one that made a thing forever the guild's.

Vesper sat at his desk with the master ledger open before him and the guild's special inkwell at his elbow, and he did not move for a long time.

Eleven years. Eleven years of flawless entries. He had recorded bottled echoes and folded distances, debts written in candle-smoke and apologies extracted from ghosts. He had never once made an error, because he had never once been asked to choose between the record and the truth. The guild had built him carefully for exactly this life: a man who wrote down what he was told, in ink that could not be undone, and slept well after.

Through the wall, faintly, he could hear the examination room. Or he imagined he could. A small sound, rhythmic, like knuckles against glass. Like knocking.

*It belongs in a vault,* Corvane had said. *Safe.*

But the thorn was guttering. Vesper had seen it — each dimming longer than the last, a fire starving in a glass house. Whatever fed it, whatever it was part of, it was dying by fractions in that black case, and the guild's answer was to seal it deeper, label it, shelve it among the impossible things, and let it go out at leisure. Three hundred years from now some apprentice would open a drawer and find a dead splinter and a card reading *depleted, value nil,* and no one would ever know what had been murdered by good bookkeeping.

And the child. *We inventory the vessel as well.* He had watched Corvane's arithmetic and understood at last what number the child was being assigned. Comet-born children, she had tried to tell them, in her language older than kingdoms — guards with empty eyes, hunts, fires. The guild did not burn villages. The guild did something quieter. The guild put things in vaults and forgot the doors.

Vesper dipped his pen.

His hand was steady now. That was the terrible part — how steady it was, how easily the lie came, as though it had been waiting in him all along, coiled under eleven years of perfect entries, needing only a reason.

He wrote: *One (1) thorn, origin unknown, luminous. Condition upon intake: DEPLETED. Residual luminescence consistent with dying ember; artifact non-viable. Recommend archival storage, low priority. Value: nil.*

The ink sank into the page and set, unamendable, eternal. Somewhere in the guild's long memory, the most valuable thing ever to cross its threshold had just been declared worthless — filed, dismissed, forgotten in advance. Vesper set down the pen. His heart was slamming against his ribs like something trying to get out, and he felt — he searched himself for guilt and found it, yes, thick and real — but braided through the guilt, inseparable from it, was something else. Something that felt terribly like relief. Like a door opening after years of standing in a corridor.

He had falsified the master ledger. There was no coming back from it. Whatever he was now, he had become it in a single line of ink.

"You know," said a voice behind him, dry as old vellum, "in forty years of keeping this guild's books, I have read perhaps ten thousand entries."

Vesper did not turn. He did not need to. He knew the voice the way he knew his own heartbeat — Sable, his mentor, the woman who had taught him that a ledger was a moral document, that every entry was a small act of witness, that the guild's power lived not in its vaults but in its records.

"And in forty years," Sable continued, coming closer, her footsteps soft on the floorboards, "I have seen exactly one false one."

Vesper turned. She stood beside his desk, small and grey and sharp-eyed, looking down at the wet-inked lie with an expression he could not read. Her hands were folded. Her face was perfectly calm. Forty years of calm, worn smooth as river stone.

"Sable," he began. "I can explain—"

"No," she said quietly. "You can't. That's rather the point of a lie."

She looked at him for a long moment. Outside, the midnight bell began to ring, and somewhere below, boots were gathering for the transfer to the deep vault — Corvane's guards, coming up the stairs, coming for the thorn, coming for the thing Vesper had just declared worthless and was already, in the same breathless instant, planning to steal.

Sable leaned in close. When she spoke again, her voice was so low it barely disturbed the air.

"The vault transfer leaves by the east stair at the quarter-bell," she said. "The guards take the west corridor. They always do. Corvane believes in symmetry."

And she straightened, and walked away, and said nothing more — yet.
