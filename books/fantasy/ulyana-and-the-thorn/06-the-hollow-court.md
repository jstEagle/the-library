## 6. The Hollow Court

The capital announced itself three days before we reached it, the way a wound announces itself before you see the flesh.

First came the birds — or rather, their absence. The hedgerows along the king's road went silent, and Ulyana noticed it before I did, her head tilting like a wren's, her small hand rising to press against the thorn beneath her cloak. Then came the light. It was autumn, and autumn in the lowlands is a generous season, all amber and long shadows, but as we crested the last hill and saw Aurelheim spread below us — its white walls, its thousand chimneys, its great palace crowned with spires like needles waiting to sew something shut — the light over the city was wrong. Thin. Stretched. As though someone had taken the sun's warmth and wrung it out like a cloth.

"They drink it," Ulyana said. Her new-tongue had grown steadier on the road; she spent words on me now the way a miser spends gold, one at a time, each one costing her something I could see in the gray that crept into her cheeks afterward. "The crown. It drinks."

I had dressed us both for the part before we entered the fold-road's final stretch: map-sellers, which is to say, the most invisible profession in any kingdom. Nobody looks twice at people who sell maps. Everybody believes they already know where they are. I carried my old guild satchel with its brass calipers and ink-stained corners, and I had drawn a dozen plausible charts of plausible places during our nights by the fire while Ulyana slept and the thorn pulsed beneath her blanket like a second heart. Her hair I had bound up under a seller's cap; her comet-bright eyes I could do nothing about, so I taught her to keep them lowered, to look at everyone's boots, at the ground, at anything but faces.

"Maps," she practiced, in the trade-tongue, flattening the word until it was almost ugly enough to pass. "Maps. Fresh maps."

"Older maps are better," I told her. "People trust what looks like it survived something."

She considered this with the grave attention she gave everything, and then she said, in her own tongue, the word for *survived*, and made me repeat it until I had it right, and the word cost me nothing and cost her everything, and she paid it anyway. That was Ulyana. She paid for every gift she gave as if generosity were a debt she refused to leave outstanding.

* * *

We passed through the Gate of Nine Bells at midmorning, and the guards barely glanced at us — but they glanced at *children*. That was the thing I had not understood until I stood in the shadow of the gatehouse and watched them work. They were not checking papers. They were not checking cargo. A sergeant moved along the line of travelers with a strip of parchment gone brown with age, and whenever a child passed him he held the strip out flat, and if the child was young enough, he held it above their head, and waited.

Ulyana walked beneath it with her eyes down and her breath held, and I confess I held mine too, because I knew what the parchment was. Comet-parchment. Ground from stone that fell in the Year of the Split Sky, it darkens in the presence of children born under comet-light — the guild had three sheets of it in inventory, catalogued under *Instruments of Unjust Finding*, and I had dusted them often enough to know their smell: iron and old rain.

The strip stayed pale above her head. She had folded something around herself — I never learned whether it was craft or instinct or the thorn's own cunning — some quality of *unremarkable*, and the sergeant grunted and moved on, and we were inside.

Inside, the city was beautiful the way a portrait is beautiful. Everything composed. Everything still. The markets were full and the fountains ran and the bells rang the hour, and yet there was a hush underneath it all, a held-breath quality, as if the whole city were a household tiptoeing past a sickroom. People did not look up. In all my years of inventory I had catalogued fear in many forms — bottled echoes of screams, debts written in candle-smoke, a jar of somebody's last courage — but I had never seen an entire city practicing the art of not-looking-up, and it curdled something in me to watch it done so well.

"Vesper." Ulyana tugged my sleeve. "There." 

She pointed with her chin, subtle as a map-seller's wife should be, toward the palace square, where a crier stood on the steps of the Hall of Decrees reading from a scroll. We drifted close, two traders pausing to hear news that might affect commerce.

"—by order of His Majesty, the bounty for children born under the Split Sky is raised to fifty crowns, delivered living to the Palace Undercroft—"

*Living.* The word went through me like a caliper through soft wood. Not alive. *Living*, with all the bureaucratic precision that implied — breathing upon delivery, condition otherwise irrelevant.

Ulyana's hand found mine inside the folds of her cloak. Her fingers were cold. "They take them to the Undercroft," she said, very quietly. "I have seen it. In the fire-dreams. There is a door under the palace older than the palace, and behind the door—" She stopped. Pressed her lips together. Whatever lived behind that door, she had decided I should not carry the knowledge yet, and she guarded it the way she guarded everything, fiercely, at cost to herself.

"The court sits today," I said, to fill the silence. "Public session. If we're selling maps, we sell them where the court can see us. Nothing draws nobility like the promise of somewhere else."

It was good tradecraft. It was also, I would understand later, exactly what she wanted.

* * *

The Hall of Decrees had been built to make human beings feel like punctuation marks — small marks between the large statements of throne and pillar and vaulted stone. We set our little stall of charts near the back, among the merchants and petitioners, and I arranged my forged maps with genuine artistry, and Ulyana knelt beside me with her cap pulled low, and we waited.

King Aldric entered at noon.

I had seen his likeness on coinage all my life, of course — every kingdom puts its king's face where its money goes, so that the people learn to associate their sovereign with the things they lack. But no coin had prepared me for the man himself, and I say *man* with hesitation, because what walked down the aisle of the Hall of Decrees wore Aldric's body the way a well-made coat is worn: beautifully, correctly, and by something else.

He was beautiful. That was the first horror of it. Tall, silver-templed, moving with the unhurried grace of someone who has never once needed to hurry. The court bowed in waves before him like wheat before wind, and he smiled at them, and the smile was perfect, and the smile was empty, the way a painted window is empty — all the colors of glass, none of the sun behind it.

And above his head, the crown drank.

I want to be precise about this, because precision was my trade and it is the only prayer I know. The crown of Aldric was an old thing, black metal worked into shapes like thorned branches, and as the king moved through the light of the high windows, the light *bent toward it*. I watched a shaft of afternoon sun slide across the floor, strike the dais steps, and then lean — actually lean, the way grass leans toward water — up the steps, across the throne, into the crown. And the crown took it. And nothing came back out. Around the king's head there was a region perhaps a hand's width wide where the air itself seemed dimmed, a halo worn inward instead of outward, a corona of subtraction.

Beside me, Ulyana had gone rigid. Beneath her cap, I felt rather than saw her staring, and when I glanced down at her hands, they had curled into fists so tight the knuckles showed white as candle-wax.

"He is still in there," she whispered. Not to me. To herself. Or perhaps to the thorn, which had begun, very faintly, to warm against my hip where the satchel pressed it — warming, I realized with a lurch, in answer to the crown, the way one magnet answers another across a crowded room.

Aldric sat. A chamberlain brought the day's decrees, and the king signed them, one after another, his hand moving in long elegant strokes, and I watched his face while he did it and I will carry what I saw there to whatever ledger receives me when I die. Because once — only once, between the fourth decree and the fifth — something flickered behind those vacant eyes. A contraction. A flinch. The look of a man surfacing from deep water just long enough to see where he is being drowned, before the water closes over him again. His pen stopped. Trembled. A single drop of ink fell onto the parchment, blooming there like a small black wound.

Then the vacancy smoothed back over his face like ice re-forming over a hole, and he signed the fifth decree, and the sixth, and the court murmured its approval at the beauty of his penmanship.

* * *

We sold four maps that afternoon. I remember it because I remember being astonished that my hands could still make change, that my voice could still say *three coppers, m'lord*, while the rest of me stood in the throne-room of my own understanding watching a truth assemble itself piece by piece.

Because I had finally done the arithmetic. It was what I was *for*, arithmetic; I had spent twenty years balancing the Guild of Impossible Things' impossible books, and the equation had been sitting in front of me since the gate, wearing a crown.

The wraith needed the thorn.

Not wanted. Needed. The binding it wore — Aldric's body, Aldric's crown, Aldric's kingdom — was incomplete; that was why the light-drinking, why the hunts, why children born under comet-light gathered *living* into the Undercroft. It was feeding the binding, patching it, holding the seal with spit and stolen fire. And the thorn — the fragment of the last dragon's heart, the one true flame left in the world outside the vault — was the keystone. Press it into the binding and the hollow thing on the throne would be permanent. Eternal. A king who never died because there was nothing left inside him to die.

And the thorn was coming to it. Carried by a mud-streaked child who had fled the capital months ago, crossed half a kingdom, learned a stranger's language word by costly word — and turned, at the end, toward the city, toward the gate, toward the hall, walking straight and steady into the trap with her eyes lowered and her fists clenched.

"You knew," I said that evening, in the rented room above a chandler's shop, with the shutters closed and one candle lit, because even candles seemed owed some discretion here. "From the road. Before the gate. You weren't fleeing *to* safety. You were coming home to be caught."

Ulyana sat cross-legged on the bed with the thorn cupped in her palms, its ember-light painting her face in shifting orange, and she did not deny it. She looked at me with those comet-bright eyes, and what was in them was not fear. It was purpose, worn smooth as riverstone, and it frightened me far more than the crown had.

"The binding breaks from inside or not at all," she said. Each word cost her; I watched the price of it in the tremor of her fingers. "The wraith holds the king through the crown, and the crown holds the binding through hunger. If the thorn stays hidden, it hunts forever. Every child. Every village. Until the fire is found or dies." She closed her hands over the thorn, and the light went out between her fingers, and in the sudden darkness her voice was very small and very steady. "So I bring it to the door it was torn from. And I put it back."

"Into the dragon," I said. "Into the vault. That's suicide, Ulyana. The Undercroft, the guards, the wraith itself—"

"Not the vault." She shook her head slowly. "Closer than the vault. You'll see. You're the cartographer, Vesper. When the time comes, you'll see the way." And she lay down then, curled around the thorn like a key curled in a lock, and within moments she slept, and the thorn began to pulse its slow red pulse, and I sat up alone with the candle and my terror and my ledgers' worth of unanswered questions.

Outside, far off, the palace spires stood black against the thinning sky, needle-sharp, patient. Somewhere beneath them, children born under a split sky waited in the dark for a rescue that had just walked itself through the front gate.

And I understood, sitting there in the chandler's candlelight, that I had not brought Ulyana to the capital at all.

She had brought me.
