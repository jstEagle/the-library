## 8. The Guttering

The stairs go down longer than the palace is tall.

Ulyana counts them the way Vesper taught her to count — not in numbers, because numbers are a young language and hers is older — but in heartbeats. Two hundred heartbeats down. Three hundred. The walls stop being stone somewhere past the fourth hundred and become something else: rib-bone pale, veined with gold that glows when she breathes near it. The thorn in her palm has been warm since they passed the first guard who did not see them, warmer since the second, and now it beats against her skin like a small frightened bird.

*Almost home,* it says, in the way it says things, which is not words at all but pressure and heat and a longing so old Ulyana sometimes forgets it is not her own.

Vesper walks behind her with the folded-distance charm cupped in both hands like water he is afraid to spill. He has been quiet for a long time. In the dark she can hear him breathing the way he breathes when he is adding up a ledger and does not like the sum.

"Ulyana." His voice comes soft, afraid of waking the mountain. "What's down here? Truly. Not what you told Corvane. Not what you told me on the road."

She stops on the stair. Below them the dark opens like a mouth.

"You will be angry," she says. Her new-tongue still sits crooked in her mouth, all edges where there should be curves. "Angry is a word I learned from Sable. She was angry with love in it. Maybe yours will be too."

"I don't think I can be anything but what I am," Vesper says, "until you tell me."

So she tells him.

* * *

"I was not born," she says. "I was *kept.*"

The words come slowly, each one carried up out of her like a stone from a deep well. "In the last days of fire, when the dragon knew its heart would be torn out, it did one thing more. It took its final ember — the smallest one, the one even the wraith's claws could not reach — and it breathed it into the world as a child. Small. Mud-colored. Able to walk through gates of men without burning them." She holds up her hand. In the dark, the thorn's light shows the fine old burn-scar across her palm, the one she has never explained. "The ember learned to be flesh. It learned hunger and cold and the names of village dogs. It learned to love a woman who found me in the ash-row and never asked why my eyes were the color of coals."

"Your mother," Vesper says quietly.

"My mother." The word is warm. She lets it be warm before she goes on. "The comet came, and the crown-thing felt me, the way a spider feels the fly. Guards with empty eyes. My village burned for keeping me, and I ran, and I ran, and I stole the thorn back from the vault beneath the throne room because it *called* me — because half of me had been torn away and the half that walked was dying of the missing." Her voice frays. "I did not come to your door by accident, Vesper Amberdale. I came to your door because the guild keeps maps of every impossible thing, and I needed someone who could read the road home. I used you."

The silence after is very long. Somewhere above them, muffled through a hundred fathoms of bone-stone, the palace makes its small sounds — bells, boots, the creak of a kingdom being eaten from inside.

"Used me," Vesper repeats. Ulyana braces herself. But when he speaks again his voice is strange and steady, the voice he uses for entries in ledgers. "Let me enter this correctly, then. You carried a stolen piece of a dying god across three hundred miles, alone, at eight years old, hunted by an immortal wraith. And the help you needed was a map." A pause. "I have spent eleven years cataloguing miracles, Ulyana. You are the first one to ask permission."

Something in her chest cracks open, and she is crying before she knows it, great silent child-tears that steam faintly in the air because even her grief runs hot. Vesper puts his hand — cool, ink-stained, human — on top of her burning one, over the thorn, and does not pull away even though it must sting him.

"Come," he says. "Finish the errand."

They go down together into the last dark.

* * *

The vault is not a room. It is a wound.

It opens around them vast as a cathedral turned inside out, ceilinged with ribs of black stone that might once have been something living, floored in ash fine as flour. And filling it — filling it the way weather fills a sky — lies the dragon.

Ulyana has seen it in visions all her life, the caged fire behind her eyelids, but no vision was ever this. The dragon is dying the way mountains die: slowly, hugely, in a silence that has weight. Its scales are the gray of old embers, cracked and dull, and between the cracks a light gutters — red, then orange, then almost nothing, then red again, like a coal remembering itself. Its eye, when it opens, is larger than the door they came through, and it is the exact color of her own.

"*Little ember,*" it says. Not aloud. The words arrive inside her bones, in the old language, the one older than kingdoms, the one she has been forgetting word by word with every mile she walked in human skin. "*You grew. I feared the world would snuff you before you grew.*"

"I kept your thorn," she answers in the same tongue, and hears Vesper draw breath beside her, hearing it too now, or feeling it, the meaning arriving in him the way weather arrives. "I kept it safe. I brought it home."

She climbs. It takes a long time, walking up the slope of a foreleg taller than her village's church, over ground that trembles faintly with each labored heartbeat. Where the heart should beat loudest there is instead a hollow — a cavity in the dragon's chest the size of a chapel, its edges blackened and torn, and the ache coming off it is so enormous that Ulyana falls to her knees on the threshold of it, weeping without sound. This is the wound she has felt in herself all her life and never known why. This is the missing half.

"Give it back to me," the dragon says gently, "and I will hold fire in the world a little longer. A year. Ten years, if I am stubborn. Long enough."

"No," Ulyana says.

And she turns the thorn in her hand and shows it, not to the dragon, but to the dark stairway behind them — because she has heard the footsteps. She has been hearing them for a hundred heartbeats. Cold comes down the stairs ahead of the thing that makes them, a cold like the space between stars, and the guttering light of the vault bends toward it as if bowing.

The wraith arrives wearing Aldric's body.

He descends the last steps beautifully. That is the horror of it — how beautiful the king is, tall and silver-crowned, his face calm as a portrait, his robes unmarked by the ash through which he walks without disturbing it. But his shadow moves wrong. It lags a half-heartbeat behind him, and it has too many joints, and where the crown meets his brow the light of the vault simply *ends*, swallowed, drunk. His eyes are the color of a winter sky with nothing behind it.

"There you are," the wraith says, and its voice is the king's voice gone smooth as poured wax. "Little thief. Little candle. Do you know how long I have searched for my own heart?"

"It was never yours," Ulyana says. She stands between the hollow chest and the king's body, eight years old and mud-streaked, holding a fragment of living fire in her fist. "You tore it out. You have been wearing a dead man's face for twenty years and calling it ruling."

"Aldric begged me to take it." The wraith smiles with the king's mouth, and the smile does not fit the face; it sits on it like frost on glass. "He was drowning in grief and I offered him a kingdom. Men trade their hearts for far less, little ember. Your cartographer would know. Ask him what he would trade." The empty eyes slide past her, find Vesper at the foot of the foreleg, and linger. "I know what the guild promised you, Vesper Amberdale. A name in every ledger. Permanence. I can give what they only promise. Kneel, and be remembered forever."

Beside her, she hears Vesper's breath catch — hears the want in it, honest and terrible — and then hears him let it go.

"No," he says. "But thank you for the appraisal."

The wraith's face does not change, but the cold in the room sharpens, and the shadow behind the king unfolds — unfolding and unfolding, joint after joint after joint, rising against the ribbed dark until it blots out the vault's high arches, until it is the largest thing in the largest room Ulyana has ever stood in.

"Then I will take the thorn," it says, "and the child, and the last of the fire, and finish what I began. Hold her still."

The shadow moves. The dragon's remaining eye flares — one last surge of ember-orange, furious and magnificent — and Ulyana feels the thorn leap in her palm like a salmon going home upriver, and she understands, suddenly, completely, what it has been trying to tell her since the stairs:

Not *give it back to me.*

*Give it to the hand that tore it out.*

"Vesper!" she cries, in her broken new-tongue, in her old old tongue, in any voice that will carry. "Fold the space! Fold it *now!*"

Behind her, she hears the whisper of a charm unfolding — and the world begins to crease.
