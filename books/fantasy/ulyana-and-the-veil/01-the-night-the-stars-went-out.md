## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The stars began to die over Thornfell at the hour of the wolf, and Ulyana Sterling was too busy counting crates to notice.

He crouched in the shadow of the old tollhouse, breath fogging in the autumn cold, running his thumb along the wax seal of the third crate. Unbroken. Good. Corvane's buyers in Lowmark paid double for unbroken seals, and Ulyana had built his reputation — such as it was, at twenty-three, with a smuggler's face and a dead mother's name — on never delivering anything less than perfect. Six crates of ward-glass, cut and polished in the guild workshops, bound for a buyer who asked no questions. The last run of the season before the mountain passes closed. After tonight he'd go back to the guildhall, drink bad brandy with the other runners, and let winter swallow the roads.

Routine. That was the whole religion of the guild. Routes measured to the pace of a tired mule, checkpoints timed against the changing of the border watch, hand-signals older than anyone using them. Ulyana had learned the trade the way other children learned prayers: *the north road lies when the fog comes down; trust the river stones, not the river; never be on the Thornfell road past midnight.* He knew every bend of this country the way he knew the scar on his own palm. The world had rules, and the rules held, and that was enough.

Then the star above the eastern ridge went out.

Not dimmed. Not clouded. *Out* — snuffed like a candle pinched between wet fingers, leaving behind a hole in the sky so black it seemed to pull at the eye. Ulyana frowned, tilted his head, waited for cloud. The night was clear as glass; he could see the whole spine of the Milky Way spilled across the heavens like salt from a torn sack.

A second star died. Then a third, lower, near the horizon where the wards ran — the great invisible lattice of old magic that had ringed Thornfell since before the guild had a name, since before his grandfather's grandfather had first carried contraband through the passes. Every child in the province grew up under those wards without ever seeing them. They were simply there, the way the mountains were there. Smugglers joked that the wards were their best business partner: no army could cross them, so all the fighting stayed outside, and all the smuggling stayed inside.

The fourth star went out, and this time Ulyana saw how. It didn't vanish. It *drained* — its light stretching, thinning, drawn sideways toward the ground like water pulled down a drain, until nothing remained but that hungry dark.

His mouth had gone dry. "That's not weather," he whispered to himself, and hated how young his voice sounded in the empty road.

He should have run then. Later, in the long years after, he would think often about that moment — the tollhouse, the crates, the cold — and wonder what would have happened if he'd simply turned his mules around and gone home. But the guild religion has one commandment above all others: *finish the route*. And besides, some stubborn part of him, the part that had survived being an orphan twice over, needed to believe the world still made sense. So he stood in the shadows and watched the sky come apart.

They went out in waves now, dozens at once, each death pulling light toward the earth in long silent threads. The darkness between stars deepened until it felt solid, a ceiling pressing down. Somewhere far off, a dog began to howl, and then another, and then a chorus of them, all along the valley, all singing the same note of animal terror.

And then the wards shattered.

There was no sound at first — only a pressure change, a popping in his ears, the taste of copper flooding his mouth. Then the air above the border ridge lit up. Not firelight, not lightning: a web of pale silver lines suddenly visible, crisscrossing the sky in a geometry that hurt to look at, the wards rendered visible for the first time in living memory because they were *breaking*. They came apart the way a window comes apart when struck just right — silently, all at once, into a thousand falling shards of light that drifted down over the mountains like luminous snow.

Ulyana sank to his knees in the frost.

He didn't decide to. His legs simply stopped being interested in holding him. Because he understood, in the wordless way you understand a scream in the night, that something fundamental had ended. The wards were the walls of the world. He had never seen them, never thought about them, had built his entire life inside their shelter the way a fish builds its life inside water — and now the water was draining away and the fish lay gasping on the stones of a road it no longer recognized.

The light-shards fell around him. One landed on the back of his hand and he felt — warmth? No. A memory of warmth. A touch so faint it might have been his own pulse. Then it faded, and the shard was just frost.

The dogs stopped howling all at once, which was somehow worse.

For a long time Ulyana knelt in the road with six crates of ward-glass beside him and the sky bleeding overhead, and he did the only thing his hands remembered how to do: he counted. Six crates. One mule train. Four miles to the Lowmark drop. Three hours until dawn. Numbers were the scaffolding of sanity, and he climbed them desperately, rung by rung, until his heart slowed enough for thought to return.

Thought returned with a single, practical shape: *get off the open road.*

He was breaking camp in the hollow below the tollhouse when he heard the horse.

It came stumbling down the old drover's path at a pace that had nothing to do with riding and everything to do with dying — lathered, blind, legs shaking. The rider slumped forward over its neck, and when the animal finally collapsed at the edge of the hollow, she rolled off and hit the frozen ground hard and did not get up.

Ulyana swore and moved toward her before his caution could catch up. That was another thing he would think about later: that his feet had decided to help before his head decided anything at all.

She was young, maybe his age, wearing the grey-and-silver livery of the Wardens' Watch — the border keepers, the quiet order who tended the ward-stones the way beekeepers tend hives. Half the province forgot they existed. Her cloak was scorched along one side, and beneath it her left arm hung wrong, and there was blood on her temple that had frozen in her hair. Her eyes opened when his boot crunched the frost near her head, and they fixed on him with a ferocity that had no business living in a body that broken.

"Smuggler," she rasped. She'd read his guild badge by starlight — or by what was left of starlight. "Of course. Of *course* the guild is out tonight."

"You know me?" Ulyana crouched, keeping his hands visible. "I don't know you."

"No one knows the Watch anymore. That's rather the point of us." She tried to push herself up on her good arm, failed, and lay back with a hiss. "Water. Please."

He gave her his flask. She drank like someone who had been rationing hope, then pressed the back of her hand to her mouth and looked up at the ruined sky. Something in her face cracked as she did it — not fear exactly, but grief, raw and personal, the way a person looks at a burned house they helped build.

"How many?" she asked. "How many stars are left?"

Ulyana counted. It took a while. "Eleven," he said at last. "Maybe twelve. In the whole sky."

Her breath came out in a long shudder. "When I rode out of the watch-station there were four hundred." She laughed, a small terrible sound. "We used to count them, you know. Ward-keepers. Each stone anchored to a star. Four hundred and six stones, four hundred and six stars, and as long as both held, the veil between what sleeps and what wakes held too." Her eyes came back to him, sharp again. "You have no idea what I'm saying."

"I have some idea you're hurt and freezing and that your horse is done," Ulyana said. "Come to the fire or freeze here. Your choice."

She stared at him. Then, incredibly, she smiled — a crooked, exhausted thing. "Guildmen. The world ends and you offer tea." She dragged herself upright with his help, biting down on every sound, and let him half-carry her into the hollow.

*

He got a fire going small and shielded, the way the guild taught — low flame, green boughs over it, smoke that vanished into the dark like a secret. He splinted her arm with a tent pole and wrapped her ribs, and she endured it all with her jaw set, watching him work with an expression he couldn't read.

"Sera," she said finally. "Warden-Sera Voss, third circle, if titles matter to a man who steals for a living."

"Ulyana Sterling." He fed the fire a stick. "And stealing implies dishonesty. We prefer *unauthorized logistics*."

Something flickered across her face at his surname — quick, there and gone, like a fish turning under ice. He'd seen people react to the name before; it was an old guild name, his mother's, and old names carried weight he'd never understood. He filed it away, the way he filed away everything, in the ledger behind his eyes.

"The wards," he said. "What happened to them?"

Sera was quiet for so long he thought she wouldn't answer. When she spoke, her voice had changed — flatter, careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water.

"What do you know about why the wards exist?"

"To keep enemies out. Border magic. Old kings' work."

"That's the story for children and tax collectors." She shifted against her pack, wincing. "The wards were never a wall, Sterling. They were a *lid*. There is something underneath this kingdom — under the palace, under the mountain roots, somewhere old and deep — that was bound there a very long time ago. Bound and put to sleep. The wards aren't keeping armies out. They're keeping it *asleep*. Every ward-stone is a stitch in the binding. Every anchored star is the thread." She looked up, at the eleven wounded lights remaining. "Tonight someone cut the threads."

Ulyana sat very still. Firelight moved on her face. "Cut them how? From outside?"

"They can't be cut from outside. That's the entire architecture of the thing." Her voice dropped, and though there was no one within a mile to hear, she leaned closer as if the dark itself might be listening. "The binding is anchored in royal blood. It always has been. Only a king — or the king's true heir, standing at the anchor-point — could release the stitches from within." Her eyes held his. "The king did this, Sterling. Or something wearing the king did. Tonight, from inside the palace, the stitches started coming out one by one. My station felt it like an earthquake. We rode for the capital to raise the alarm, and the Veil—" she stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Something met us on the road. Something that wore the shapes of my fellow keepers and walked wrong. I'm the only one who turned south instead of east."

The fire popped. Above them, through the screen of branches, another star drained out — Ulyana saw its light stretch and thin and die, and watched Sera flinch as if she'd felt it in her own flesh. Maybe she had. Maybe every keeper felt the stones going dark like teeth pulled from her own jaw.

"The king did this," she said again, barely above a whisper, and it wasn't a question anymore. It was a verdict. "Three generations of kings, and the binding has held through all of them, and the year I finally make third circle, the man on the throne unpicks it in a single night. Either Aldric has gone mad, or Aldric hasn't been Aldric for a very long time." She closed her eyes. "Either way, by the next new moon there will be no stars left, and whatever is under the palace will be awake, and nothing will matter. Not borders. Not guilds. Not any of it."

Ulyana looked at his hands. They were steady, which surprised him. Inside, everything was rearranging itself — the reliable world, the world of routes and seals and rules, tipping slowly over like a crate whose straps had been cut.

"My mother," he heard himself say, and didn't know why until the words were already out. "My mother was from the capital. She worked for the crown before she came to Thornfell. Before she died."

Sera opened her eyes and studied him for a long moment, and he had the uncomfortable feeling of being read like a ledger.

"Did she," Sera said softly. "Sterling. What a small world the old families make."

Above the hollow, the twelfth star went out. Eleven remained, and the dark between them seemed to lean closer, listening, patient as winter.

Ulyana fed the fire another stick and understood, with the strange clarity that arrives at the bottom of a fall, that the route he'd finished tonight was the last route of his old life. Whatever came with dawn — the sealed roads, the questions, the thing wearing the crown — there would be no going back to the man who had knelt in the frost counting crates while the sky came apart.

Somewhere far to the east, in the direction of the capital, a light rose over the mountains that was not dawn and not fire and not any color the sky was supposed to make.

Sera saw it too. "New moon's in nine days," she whispered.

The false light pulsed, slow and rhythmic, like something breathing.
