## 2. What the Guild Keeps in Its Cellars

They reached Thornfell two days later, moving by day now because the nights had become something else entirely.

The stars had not come back. Ulyana kept checking — a habit from childhood, when his mother had taught him to read the sky the way other children learned their letters. *The Shepherd's Chain there, see, that's your north when the compass lies. The Pale Sisters rise before dawn in autumn; if you can count all seven, the passes are open.* He had navigated half his life by those lights, and now the sky above the mountains was a smooth black lacquer, unbroken, as if some enormous hand had wiped it clean and forgotten to paint anything back.

The sun still rose. That was the only mercy. But it rose wrong — thin, watery, casting shadows with soft edges, like light seen through dirty glass. Sera said it was because the wards had been woven into everything, sky included, and now the weave was gone. Ulyana didn't know if she was right. He only knew that frost clung to the south-facing stones at midday, and the birds had stopped singing somewhere past the second ridge, and the horses kept their ears pinned back as though listening for something behind them.

Sera rode poorly, one arm bound against her chest where the ward-shatter had thrown her against a wall of her own tower. She refused to talk about it. She talked about almost nothing, in fact, except once, on the morning of the second day, when she reined up on a bluff overlooking the valley and looked down at the guildhall's slate roofs with an expression Ulyana couldn't read.

"That's where you grew up?"

"Born there, more or less." He nudged his horse onto the switchback. "Corvane found my mother dead on the north road when I was three. Took me in. Raised me with the others — the foundlings. It's what the guild does."

"And your mother? What was she? Before."

"A courier, I think. Long-haul. She died on a route." He said it the way he'd always said it, the way Corvane had taught him to say it, flat and finished, a stone dropped down a well with no bottom to listen for. "Why?"

But Sera only shook her head and looked back at the sky.

* * *

The guildhall of Thornfell sat in a fold of the valley like a coin dropped in grass — unremarkable from a distance, which was the point. To the tax assessors and the king's surveyors it was a tannery and wool concern of modest means. In truth, its cellars ran deeper than its roofs were tall, and every barrel of dye and bale of fleece hid a smuggler's artery: tunnels running east toward the capital, west through the mountains into the free ports, south along the old river roads. Sixty years of routes, mapped and memorized, paid for in blood and bribes.

Ulyana felt the change before he saw it. The hall was too quiet. No carts in the yard, no loaders singing the counting songs, no smell of the cookhouse fires. Just men standing at their posts with the particular stillness of people waiting for orders they already know will be bad.

Corvane met him in the yard.

The guildmaster was sixty and built like the doorframe of a fortress church — broad, grey, immovable — with a beard he trimmed himself each morning and hands that had broken fingers belonging to better fighters than Ulyana would ever be. He had raised Ulyana after a fashion: taught him locks, ledgers, the weight of a blade, the arithmetic of loyalty. He was the closest thing to a father the guild's foundlings had ever been given, and he was looking at Ulyana now across the frozen yard with an expression Ulyana had never once seen on his face.

Fear.

"You're alive," Corvane said. It wasn't relief. It was inventory.

"Barely. Roads are—"

"I know what the roads are." He turned and walked, and Ulyana understood he was meant to follow. Behind them, two of the doorwardens fell in — Vess and old Tam, both armed, both watching Ulyana the way they'd watch cargo that might be cursed.

In the map room, the great table stood bare. Every route chart had been stripped away. In their place lay a single sheet of paper bearing Corvane's own seal.

"All routes are sealed," Corvane said. "Effective the night the stars went out. Nothing moves. Not goods, not messages, not people. The cellars are being walled as we speak — brick and mortar, the works. Anyone caught attempting a run is out of the guild and out of my protection, and I'll tell you now that out of my protection is going to mean something very different in the weeks ahead."

"The capital," Ulyana said. "People are saying the king—"

"People are saying a great many things." Corvane poured two cups of small beer without asking whether Ulyana wanted any, which was itself an answer; the guildmaster only performed hospitality when he wanted something or needed to soften a blow. "Here is what I'm saying. No one goes near the capital. Not for gold, not for family, not for revenge. Anyone who asks about the capital gets the same answer: we're wool merchants, and wool merchants know nothing but sheep." He set the cup down in front of Ulyana. "Drink. You look half-dead."

"Corvane." Ulyana didn't touch the cup. "I was under the wards when they came down. I *saw* them come down. That wasn't an attack. There was nothing to fight. They just—" he searched for it, "—*unraveled*. Like someone pulled a thread."

Something moved behind the guildmaster's eyes — quick, buried, gone. "Then someone pulled a thread," he said. "And whoever it was has the kind of power that makes sealed routes and full granaries the difference between my people living and dying. So we seal. We store. We keep our heads below the horizon until the sky decides what it wants to be." He leaned forward, palms flat on the bare table. "You brought the ward-keeper."

"Sera. She's wounded. She needs—"

"She needs to be nowhere, saying nothing. I've put her in the infirmary under Maren's care. She'll be tended. She'll also be watched." Corvane straightened. "You did well, Ulyana. Get food. Sleep. When you wake, we'll talk about what comes next."

There it was again — that softness, that hospitality — and this time Ulyana heard it clearly for what it was. A hand on the shoulder steering him away from something.

He drank the beer anyway. It tasted like obedience.

* * *

He slept four hours and woke in the dark knowing he would not sleep again.

The foundlings' dormitory smelled the same as it always had — tallow, wool, the ghost of a hundred childhoods — but lying in his narrow bed, staring at rafters he'd memorized before he could write, Ulyana felt like a stranger wearing his own name. Every creak of the settling building sounded like thread pulling. Every distant footstep might be a route being bricked shut behind him.

He got up. He told himself it was hunger. It was not hunger.

The guildhall at night was a body with its skin off — corridors he could have walked blindfolded revealed in a new anatomy, the way the doorwardens had doubled at the cellar stairs, the way the archive room now sported a lock he'd never seen before. He drifted past all of it with a candle stub and thirty years of knowing which floorboards sang, down and down, past the wine cellars and the dye stores, into the oldest part of the undercroft where the walls went from dressed stone to raw mountain rock and the air tasted of cold iron.

That was where he found the vault.

It shouldn't have been there. Ulyana knew the undercroft the way he knew his own hands — he'd hidden in these passages from Corvane's drills at age eight, kissed Ilse Brandt behind the salt barrels at fifteen, moved contraband through them since. There had never been a vault here. There had been a blank wall, rough granite, damp and forgettable.

Now there was a door.

Not new — that was the thing that stopped his breath. The door was *old*, older than the hall around it, older than the guild itself by the look of it: black wood banded in verdigrised bronze, set into the rock as if the mountain had grown around it. And carved into the lintel, worn nearly smooth by centuries, was a sigil.

A circle, crossed by a single vertical line, with seven small points set around the rim like stars caught in orbit.

Ulyana knew that mark. He had known it before he could read words. It had been pressed into the leather of the little book he'd kept his childhood drawings in, stamped on the inside cover of his mother's — Corvane had given him her effects when he turned sixteen, a small bundle: a comb, a ring, a book. He had assumed the sigil was a printer's device, a stationer's flourish, nothing.

His mother's mark was on a hidden vault beneath the guildhall.

The candle flame bent sideways, though there was no draft.

He touched the bronze banding and felt it — faint, rhythmic, unmistakable now that he knew what rhythm to listen for. The same pulse he'd felt in the false light over the eastern mountains. Something breathing behind the door. Or something keeping time. He couldn't tell which, and the not-knowing crawled up his arm and settled behind his sternum.

"Ulyana."

He didn't jump. Thirty years of training. But his hand came off the bronze slowly, and when he turned, Corvane stood at the foot of the stairs holding a lamp, and the light threw the guildmaster's shadow huge and crooked up the rock wall.

"You should be asleep," Corvane said.

"There's a door here."

"There's a wall here." Corvane came forward, unhurried, and set his lamp on an iron bracket with the calm of a man arranging his own parlor. "Old foundations. Sealed before my grandfather's time. Nothing in it but rubble and rats."

"Then you won't mind telling me why it bears my mother's sigil."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Ulyana counted them, the way Corvane had taught him to count — evenly, without flinching, because the man who flinches first loses.

"Your mother carried freight for this guild," Corvane said at last. "Freight sometimes bore marks. Coincidence is the mortar of trade, boy. Don't build cathedrals out of it."

"It's not a coincidence." His voice came out low and steady and surprised him. "You've known me since I was three years old. You taught me to read a room, a ledger, a lie. So read my face right now, old man, and tell me what you see."

And Corvane — Corvane who had never once lied to him outright, who dealt in omissions the way other men dealt in coin but kept his word like iron — Corvane looked at him with something that was almost grief, and said:

"I see a foundling the guild fed and clothed and made into the best route-runner I've ever trained. I see a man whose family is behind these walls. And I see that the sky went dark two nights ago, and that everything I have spent my life keeping safe is balanced on a knife's edge, and that the worst thing he could do right now is start pulling threads." He picked up his lamp. "Go to bed, Ulyana. Seal orders stand. And the next time you come down here, bring a reason I can believe."

He climbed the stairs. His footsteps faded. The doorwardens did not follow Ulyana out, which meant they had been posted there before Ulyana ever arrived — which meant Corvane had expected him to come.

Which meant Corvane had wanted him to see the door.

Ulyana stood alone in the cold with his guttering candle, looking at the seven-pointed star circling a line, breathing in time with whatever waited behind the black wood. His whole life, the guild had been bedrock. Corvane's word, the foundlings' code, the routes like veins in his own body — he had never once thought to ask what the heart of it pumped.

*My mother carried freight.*

He thought of the little book with the stamped cover. Of the comb, the ring. Of a woman dead on the north road for twenty-three years, whose mark was set in bronze older than the guildhall above it.

For the first time in his life, Ulyana Sterling stood inside the place that had raised him and felt the floor shift — not the stone, the *story* — and understood that somewhere between the sealed routes and the sealed wall, the family he trusted had begun keeping secrets from him in rooms he'd walked past all his life.

Behind the door, faint as a heartbeat heard through a pillow, something pulsed.

Seven beats. Then silence.

Then seven again.
