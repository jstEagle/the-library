## 3. Sera's Bargain

The fever broke on the third night, and Ulyana knew it the way he knew a lock was about to give — by the small, telling sounds of surrender.

For two days Sera had burned. She lay in the back room of Maren's waystation, an abandoned tollhouse wedged into the rocks above the old salt road, and she burned like wet wood, smoking and hissing, muttering fragments of ward-cant in a voice scraped down to gravel. Maren, the waystation's sole keeper — a stooped woman with ink-stained fingers and no questions in her — had fed her willow bark and honeyed water and said nothing beyond what needed saying, which was nothing at all. That silence was why Ulyana paid her triple. Silence, in Thornfell, was the most expensive commodity on any route.

He sat now in the doorway of the sickroom, sharpening a knife he did not need to sharpen, listening to the fever recede. The fire had been fed. The blankets had been changed twice. And beneath the smell of smoke and sweat and the tallow candle, something else had begun to surface: the cold mineral scent of ward-stone, the smell that clung to people who spent their lives standing between the world and whatever pressed against it from outside.

Outside, the sky was wrong. It had been wrong for three nights. No stars. Not clouded over — *absent*, as if someone had reached up and unpicked them one by one, leaving a black so complete it made the mountains look cut from paper. Ulyana had run the border routes for eleven years and had never once seen a sky without stars. Smugglers navigated by them. Everyone did. Their absence was not darkness; it was a wound.

In the room, Sera stirred.

"Water," she rasped, before her eyes were even open.

He brought it. She drank half the cup in long swallows, then looked at him — really looked, for the first time since he'd carried her bleeding out of the ward-road with shards of light still dying in her hair. Her eyes were grey, the flat grey of river stone, and they took his measure the way a quartermaster takes inventory.

"You're the smuggler," she said. "The one who pulled me out."

"Ulyana."

"Sera Voss. Third Circle, Ward-Keepers' Order." She tried to sit up, failed, and hated it — he could see the hatred, quick and hot across her face, the fury of a body that had betrayed its training. "How long?"

"Three days."

"Three days." She closed her eyes. When she opened them again, they were worse than before, because now they held memory. "Then you saw it. The wards coming down."

"I saw the stars go out," Ulyana said carefully. "And then I saw the wards come down. I'd always understood it was the other way around. Wards first, then dark."

"No." Her hand found his wrist — grip surprisingly strong for a woman three days from death. "Listen to me, because I only have this once in me and I will not say it twice. The stars went out *first*. Do you understand? The Veil-work that holds the wards doesn't draw on the ground or the rivers or the ley-roots under the mountains. It draws on starlight. Old magic. Older than the kingdom. Every ward-stone on the border is a cup, and every cup was filled from the same well." Her breath caught. "Someone emptied the well."

Ulyana set down the knife. "The king did this. You said that, the first night. You said *the king did this*."

"I said what I saw." She pushed herself up against the headboard, and this time she made it, white-knuckled and shaking but upright, and there was a stubbornness in her spine that reminded him uncomfortably of Corvane. "I was stationed at the Cindermark anchor, easternmost of the seven. My captain got a sealed order that morning — royal seal, privy counterseal. We were told to stand down the anchor for 'recalibration.' Recalibration." She laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as ash. "There is no recalibration. There has never been recalibration. An anchor is laid once and dies with its keeper. But the order bore the seal, and we are sworn to the seal, so my captain stood down the anchor, and I stood beside him, and when the light began to drain out of the stones I felt it go through me like—" Her jaw tightened. "Like being unmade a little. Eleven of us at Cindermark. I'm the only one who ran instead of holding position. So I'm either the only coward or the only witness."

"The order came from the palace."

"The order came from the palace." She watched him steadily. "Which means someone inside it wanted the wards down. Wanted them down badly enough to burn their own bloodline's magic to do it — because that's what the anchoring is, smuggler. Royal blood. The binding runs in the king's veins. Nobody outside the palace could have dropped those wards if they lived to be a thousand. Whoever gave that order didn't breach the defenses." Her voice dropped, and the room seemed to lean closer around the words. "They *were* the defenses. They turned them off from inside."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, in the dark of the valley, a wolf began to howl and then stopped mid-note, as if it too had looked up and found nothing there.

Ulyana thought of the guildhall, of Corvane's face going grey when the news came, of the sealed orders and the locked vault with his mother's sigil burned into its door. He thought: *if someone inside the palace dropped the wards, then everything I've ever carried through the night was carried under a lie.* He did not say it aloud. He was a smuggler; he had made a life out of not saying things aloud.

"You're wondering why I'm telling you this," Sera said.

"I'm wondering why you're not dead yet. You should have gone septic."

"Maren knows her craft." A ghost of a smile. "And I'm not done. Dying is easy. Witnessing is hard, and I haven't finished." She studied him for a long moment, and he felt the inventory happening again, item by item. "You carried me four miles through open country with hunters' lights behind us. You splinted my arm with a bow stave. You had every reason to leave me — a wounded ward-keeper is a liability, a corpse waiting to happen — and you didn't. So either you're a fool, or you need something from me."

"Maybe both."

"Good. Fools who admit it are the only ones worth bargaining with." She drew a breath, and he watched her gather herself the way he'd seen cornered people gather themselves a hundred times — not for a fight, but for a sale. "Here is what I know that you don't. The rebels have a cell in the capital. Undercity, dockside quarter. They've been trying to get word to the Order for months about things moving in the palace — servants vanishing, the king not seen, whole wings shuttered. We thought they were fear-mongers. Now?" She flexed her splinted arm, wincing. "Now I owe them an apology and an alliance, and the Order is scattered to the winds, and I am one woman with a broken arm in a smuggler's den at the edge of nowhere."

"And what you want from me is a road."

"What I want from you is *the* road." Her eyes didn't leave his. "Every official route into the capital passes checkpoints, and every checkpoint answers to the crown, and the crown right now is a question mark wearing a throne. I'd be taken inside a day. But you're Ulyana Sterling. Don't look surprised — the Order keeps files on everyone who moves goods along the ward-roads. You've run the deep tunnels under the Graywatch for six years without losing a load. You know ways into that city that aren't on any map the palace owns."

"Corvane has sealed all routes. Guild law. Anyone who runs now is cast out. Branded. Worse."

"Yes," Sera said softly. "I imagine he has. Which tells you something too, doesn't it? A man seals the roads when he's afraid of what might travel them — in either direction." She let that sit. Then, plainly, without ornament: "I can't offer you coin. I can't offer you the Order's protection; the Order may not exist by month's end. What I can offer is this: I know what the wards were, and I know where the anchors were, and I know the names of every keeper who stood them. If you get me to that cell in the capital, that knowledge goes with you. Whatever you find there — whatever your guild has been, whatever your mother was — you'll want someone beside you who understands the shape of the thing. Because you've already guessed, haven't you? That this isn't just about dropped wards. That the stars going out means something is *drinking*."

The word landed in Ulyana's chest like a stone dropped down a well. He thought of the vault door, of the sigil he'd known since childhood scratched into a lock he'd never been allowed to touch. He thought of his mother's hands, remembered dimly, always stained with the same cold mineral smell that rose off Sera's skin now.

"You said safe passage," he said. "That's the bargain. I take you to the capital, you give me what you know."

"That's the bargain."

"And if I refuse?"

Sera leaned back against the headboard. Outside, the starless dark pressed against the window like a palm. "Then I'll walk to the capital myself, on a broken arm, and probably die on the way, and the last person who understood what was done to the sky will die with me. And you'll go back to your guildhall and your sealed roads and your vault with your mother's name on it, and you'll spend the rest of your life wondering what was behind the door." She tilted her head. "You already opened it, didn't you. Or tried to."

Ulyana said nothing. Which was, he realized, an answer.

She nodded slowly, as if he'd confirmed something she'd suspected since the first delirious night. "Then here's the truth of it, smuggler, free of charge: the world you were running goods through stopped existing three days ago. There's no going back to it. There's only forward, and forward runs through the capital, and the capital runs through me." She extended her good hand. "Wary allies. Mutual need. No oaths — oaths are for people who trust each other, and I don't trust you yet, and you'd be a fool to trust me. But I think you need this road as much as I do, and I think you're honest enough to hate that."

He looked at her hand. Small, calloused, scarred along the palm in the pattern ward-keepers took when they learned to handle live stone. He thought of Corvane's face when he'd ordered the routes sealed — not angry, *afraid* — and of the ledger he hadn't yet dared to open, and of the sigil on the vault door catching candlelight like a promise or a warning.

Everything he loved, it seemed, was on the other side of that door. Everything he feared was too.

He took her hand.

"We leave tomorrow, an hour before dawn," he said. "There's a maintenance shaft under the Graywatch cisterns that comes up inside the tannery district. It's cramped, it smells like death, and if the palace has posted watchers on the old ways, we'll hear them breathing before we see them. You keep up, you keep quiet, and you keep that knowledge of yours close until I say otherwise. Agreed?"

"Agreed." Her grip was firm and dry and did not let go immediately. "One more thing, Ulyana Sterling. When we reach the capital — when you see what's sitting on that throne, or wearing it — remember that I told you first. Remember that I didn't flinch."

"Flinching is for people with somewhere else to be," Ulyana said.

For the first time, Sera Voss smiled like a living woman. "Then we're going to get along fine."

* * *

They left Maren's waystation in the blue-black hour before dawn, when the cold was deepest and even the wolves had given up on the sky. Ulyana carried both packs, Sera's strapped across his chest, hers slung loose over her splinted arm. She walked well for a woman who should have been bedridden another week — stubbornness, again, doing the work of sinew — and she asked no questions as he led her off the salt road and onto a track that wasn't a track, a seam in the hillside that only revealed itself to someone who had been shown it by someone who had been shown it in turn, all the way back through generations of people whose trade was not being seen.

At the mouth of the first tunnel, she stopped.

"This is a ward-road," she said quietly. Her fingertips brushed the rock, and where they touched, faint lines of script woke briefly under the stone — old, faded, half-eaten — like embers stirred in a dead fire. "These markings. They're Binder-work. Pre-dynastic, maybe older. Who carved these?"

"My mother," Ulyana said, before he could stop himself.

Sera looked at him. Above them, through a ventilation crack, the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

"Your mother," she repeated, and there was something new in her voice — not suspicion, not quite awe, but the particular attention of a woman recalculating everything she thought she knew. "Then we'd better move fast. Because if Binders built these roads, and Binders bound the wards, and someone just switched the wards off—" She stepped past him into the dark, her voice trailing back like a thread he had no choice but to follow. "—then whoever your mother was, smuggler, the palace has been looking for her bloodline for a very long time. And now it knows exactly where to start."

The tunnel swallowed them both, and somewhere far ahead, in the deep beneath the mountains, water dripped in the dark like a slow clock counting down to the capital.
