## 4. Corvane's Ledger

The guildhall slept the way old men sleep: loudly, and with too many secrets.

Ulyana moved through its corridors in the hour before dawn, when even the night-watch had gone soft-eyed and slow, when the candles guttered low in their sconces and the stone floors gave back only the faintest whisper of his boots. He had spent eleven years learning this building's rhythms — which stairs creaked on the third step, which doors needed oiling, which of the watchmen drank himself past hearing by the second bell. He knew them all. He had thought he knew everything else about the guild too.

Three nights since the stars went out. Three nights since Sera had collapsed against his chest in the smuggler's tunnel beneath the eastern wall, blood on her lips, saying *the king did this* like a woman reciting her own death sentence. Three nights of sealed routes, shuttered windows, and Corvane's voice ringing through the great hall like a hammer on an anvil: *No one approaches the capital. No one speaks to outsiders. The guild's business is the guild's business.*

The guild's business. Ulyana had grown up inside that phrase the way other children grew up inside their mothers' coats. It meant the ledgers were balanced. It meant the routes ran true. It meant that whatever else the world did — wars, famines, kings dying in their beds — Thornfell's guild would still be standing at the end of it, fat and gray and immovable as the mountain itself.

He had believed that. He was beginning to understand how much of his life had been built on believing things.

The archive room sat at the end of the oldest wing, behind a door of blackened oak that most of the younger members assumed held nothing but tax records and route maps going back fifty years. They weren't wrong, exactly. But Ulyana had been running routes since he was nine years old, and he had learned early that the most valuable cargo was always the kind nobody bothered to guard properly.

The lock was old ironwork, older than Corvane, older than Corvane's predecessor. Ulyana knelt before it with two picks and a tension wrench, and his hands — steady enough threading a needle-thin blade between a merchant's guards three days ago — shook now, just slightly, in the cold air of the corridor.

*You're stealing from your own family,* said a voice in his head that sounded uncomfortably like Sera.

*I'm reading my own family's mail,* he answered it. *There's a difference.*

There wasn't, and he knew it. He turned the lock anyway.

Inside, the archive smelled of dust and tallow and something older underneath — vellum, maybe, or the ghost of the leather that had once bound books now crumbled to brown lace. Moonless dark pressed against the single high window; without stars, the night outside was a solid thing, a wall of black. Ulyana lit no lamp. Instead he took out the small shuttered lantern he'd carried up from the cellars, opened its slide a finger's width, and let a knife-line of light fall across the shelves.

The ledgers began where any archive begins: recent years, neat spines, Corvane's own cramped hand on the labels. Ulyana walked backward through time, trailing one finger along the shelf edge, feeling decades flake away under his touch. Twenty years. Thirty. Forty. The handwriting changed. The binding styles changed. Somewhere around year thirty-five the paper quality dropped sharply — lean years, war years, though the histories never mentioned a war in Thornfell — and then climbed again, rich and smooth, as if money had fallen out of the sky.

Or come from somewhere specific.

At the very back of the deepest shelf, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, he found the oldest ledger. Its cover was cracked hide, dyed a color that might once have been blue, and stamped into the leather — faded almost to invisibility, but unmistakable to a man who had worn the same mark branded into his skin at fourteen — was a sigil: a star caught in a ring of thorns.

His mother's mark.

Ulyana stood very still for a long moment. The lantern trembled in his hand, and the line of light shivered across the floor like water.

Maren Sterling had died when he was six. That was the story. Fever, they said, caught on a winter route, buried somewhere in the passes because the ground below the wards wouldn't take her. Corvane had told him that story kneeling, eye-level, a big rough hand on his shoulder, and Ulyana had cried into the man's beard and been given honey-cakes for a week afterward. There was no body. There had never been a body. He had not let himself think about that in twenty years, and now here was her sigil, stamped into the guild's oldest book, and every unasked question he had ever swallowed came up his throat at once like bile.

He set the lantern on the table. He unwrapped the ledger. He opened it.

And there she was.

Not her face — her handwriting. Small, precise, slanted slightly left, each letter formed with the care of someone who had learned to write in secret and never lost the habit of treating it as precious. Maren Sterling had kept the first entries herself. Dates going back sixty years. And beside each date, in columns so regular they might have been printed:

*Received of the Crown of Aldric's line, for maintenance of the quiet ways: forty crowns.*

*Received of the Crown, for silence regarding the northern passages: sixty crowns.*

*Received of the Crown, for the ward-bypass at Greywater Ford: one hundred crowns.*

Page after page after page. Sixty years of payments. Sixty years of the crown paying the guild — paying *his* guild, *his* family — for routes that threaded under and around the border wards. Not smuggling routes, he realized, his stomach turning slowly over. Not at first. The earliest entries called them something else. *Maintenance ways.* *Inspection paths.* *Binder's roads.*

Binder's roads.

The word sat on the page like a coal. Ulyana stared at it until the letters blurred. His mother had written it a hundred times, two hundred, in her careful secret hand, and every entry was countersigned at the bottom of the year by a guildmaster's seal — and every seal, when he traced the lineage back through names he barely recognized, ended in the same place.

Corvane. Who had signed the last twelve years. And before him, Vessik, who had trained Corvane. And before him, a name Ulyana had only ever heard spoken in the cellar songs, half legend: *Old Tam Sterling.*

Sterling. His mother's name. His name. A family of guildmasters stretching back through the ledger like a spine through a body — and nobody had ever told him. Nobody had ever said *your mother kept this book.* Nobody had ever said *the crown pays us, has always paid us, to keep the ways around the wards open and quiet.*

Hush money. The word arrived in his mind fully formed, ugly and exact. Sixty years of hush money. For what? What did the crown need kept quiet about its own border wards?

He copied. He couldn't help himself; his hands moved on their own, transferring dates and sums and those terrible calm phrases into a slim notebook he'd brought for exactly this purpose, his pen scratching in the dark like an insect in a wall. *Received of the Crown.* *For silence.* *Binder's roads.* Each entry he copied felt less like theft and more like surgery — cutting the truth out of the guild's body so he could carry it somewhere it might survive.

He was deep in the middle years of the ledger — the handwriting had changed hands twice, and the payments had doubled abruptly in a single year, the same year the paper got rich again — when the light changed.

Not brighter. Colder. The knife-line from his lantern stayed where it was, but beyond it, in the doorway, a second light had appeared: the gray-white glare of a mage-lamp, the kind the guild used for document work, held at hip height by a man whose shadow filled the doorframe entirely.

"You always did lock-pick pretty," Corvane said. "Wasted talent, in a smuggler."

Ulyana didn't move. His heart slammed once, hard, against his ribs, and then — strangely — went quiet. There was nowhere to run. There was nowhere he wanted to run. He closed the notebook around his pen and looked up at the guildmaster's face.

Corvane was not an old man, but he wore age the way some men wore armor, deliberately, piece by piece. Gray in the beard he kept trimmed short. Lines around eyes that missed nothing, that had watched Ulyana take his first steps across the guildhall floor and his first blade-cut across a rival's palm and had never once, in twenty-six years, looked away from him. Right now those eyes were fixed on the open ledger, and what was in them was not anger.

It was grief. Old, tired, expected grief — the look of a man watching a fire reach a house he'd already decided to let burn.

"How long have you known I'd come down here?" Ulyana asked.

"Since you asked Brannic for lamp oil two nights ago and didn't say what for." Corvane stepped into the room and shut the door behind him with a soft, final click. "You've never once in your life asked for anything you could steal. I told myself I was wrong." He set the mage-lamp on the table. Its light fell across the ledger, across Maren's careful columns, across sixty years of bought silence, and neither man reached to close it. "I wanted to be wrong."

"Then tell me I'm wrong." Ulyana's voice came out lower than he intended, scraped raw. "Tell me there's another word for this. Tell me the crown wasn't paying us to look away from something."

Corvane pulled out the archive's single chair — a scarred thing, older than both of them — and sat down heavily, the way he sat when his knees ached, the way he had sat across from Ulyana at a hundred lessons, a hundred dinners, a hundred quiet evenings when the guildhall felt more like home than any home had a right to feel. That was the cruelty of it. Even now, even with the ledger open between them like a wound, some traitor part of Ulyana wanted to sit across from him and be taught.

"Your mother made me promise," Corvane said, "that if you ever found this book, I wouldn't lie to you. She said you'd know a lie. Said you got it from her." He exhaled through his nose, almost a laugh, entirely without humor. "I've been hoping for twenty years you'd never find it. Hoping you'd stay a smuggler. Smugglers live longer than truth-keepers, Ulyana. I've buried four truth-keepers in my time as master. I dug two of the graves myself."

"The crown," Ulyana said. "What is it paying us to be quiet about?"

Corvane looked at him for a long moment. Then, slowly, he reached across the table and turned the ledger back — back past the middle years, past the lean ones, to the first page, to Maren's very first entry, dated sixty years and one month ago. He put one blunt finger beneath a line near the top.

"*Binder's roads,*" he read aloud. "You saw the words. Do you know what a Binder is?"

"A road-keeper. An inspector of the wards—"

"A Binder is the person who keeps something asleep." Corvane's voice was flat and careful, each word laid down like a man crossing ice. "The border wards of Thornfell aren't walls, boy. They're a lid. Have been since before the guild had a name. And the crown has paid us, every year, every master, since before your grandmother drew breath, to maintain the roads a Binder needs to walk that lid — and to make sure no one else ever learns they exist. No one asks why the wards run where they run. No one asks what's underneath them. We take the crown's coin, we keep the ways open, we keep our mouths shut, and Thornfell stays safe and fat and stupid. That was the bargain. That was always the bargain."

"And my mother?"

Something moved behind Corvane's eyes — the grief again, but sharper now, closer to the surface.

"Your mother was the last Binder," he said. "And when she understood what was coming — when she read the signs the way Binders read them — she chose to disappear rather than do what the crown demanded next." He paused. "She faked her death, Ulyana. To keep you away from all of this. To keep you from being what she was. And I have spent twenty years honoring that choice by keeping you blind, and I would spend twenty more if you'd let me."

The room seemed to tilt. Ulyana gripped the edge of the table. Fever in the passes. Buried where the ground wouldn't take her. Honey-cakes and a big rough hand on his shoulder, and all of it a story, all of it a lid, the same as the wards, the same as everything in his life — a beautiful, careful lid over something that had been paid sixty years of gold to stay asleep.

"Sera," he said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "The ward-keeper. She said the king dropped the wards. Deliberately."

"I know what she said." Corvane rose, and for a moment his shadow fell over the ledger, over Maren's writing, blotting it out. "And I know what happens to guilds that start asking questions the crown has paid us not to ask. I've sealed the routes. I've sealed the mouths. In a month, maybe two, whoever sits that throne next will want the quiet ways reopened, and they'll pay double, and we'll take it, and this will all be a bad season we survived. That's how it's always gone. That's how it goes *now*, if you're smart."

"And if I'm not smart?"

"Then you're cast out." Corvane said it simply, without heat, and somehow that was worse than shouting. "No marks. No routes. No name in the rolls. You walk out of Thornfell with what you can carry and the guild forgets you were ever born under this roof. I've done it before. I'll grieve you the way I grieved your mother — quietly, and with the books balanced." He held out his hand, palm up, across the table. "Give me the notebook, lad. Go back to your bunk. Sleep. In the morning none of this will have happened, and I'll teach you the Greywater run myself, and you'll be the best smuggler this guild has ever produced. That's the choice. It's the only kind one I have."

Ulyana looked at the open hand. Broad, scarred, steady — the hand that had pulled him out of his first rockslide, splinted his first broken finger, gripped his shoulder at his mother's funeral-that-never-was. Eleven years of routes. Fourteen of roof and hearth. Every family he had, offered back to him in exchange for a notebook full of his mother's handwriting.

He thought of Sera's blood on his coat. Of the sky with no stars in it, a sky like a closed eye. Of the word *Binder* sitting in his mother's hand like a coal that had never cooled.

"No," he said.

Corvane's hand didn't waver. "Think—"

"I've thought for twenty years that my mother died of fever in a pass." Ulyana closed the ledger gently, reverently, the way you close a coffin. "I'm done thinking what other people find comfortable." He tucked the notebook inside his coat, over his heart, and met the guildmaster's eyes. "You can cast me out. You can't make me forget. She wouldn't have wanted me to."

For a long moment Corvane said nothing. The mage-lamp hissed softly. Somewhere far above, the guildhall settled against the mountain with a sound like an old man sighing.

Then the guildmaster's hand closed, empty, and withdrew.

"Third bell," he said quietly. "The kitchen door. The watch changes at third bell, and Brannic owes me a debt he'll hate repaying." He turned toward the door, and stopped, and did not look back. "Your mother said you'd know a lie. She was right. But Ulyana—" his voice caught, just once, on the name, "—whatever you think you're walking toward, the crown's hunters are already asking after a Sterling. Get out of Thornfell before they stop asking."

The door clicked shut behind him.

Ulyana stood alone in the archive with his mother's ledger and the knife-line of lamplight, and found that his hands had stopped shaking. Whatever fear lived in him now had gone somewhere deeper than his hands — down into the cold place where resolve is made, where betrayal stops being a wound and starts being a direction.

Sixty years of payments. A mother who faked her death. A king who dropped the wards. And somewhere south, in a city gone dark, a rebel ward-keeper who owed him a debt of blood and had promised him a road to the capital.

Third bell. The kitchen door.

He blew out the lantern and went to pack.
