## 7. A Glimpse Through the Veil

The rehearsal began at midnight, because Dain Ashvale believed that anything worth doing in the dark should be practiced in the dark.

Ulyana stood in the flooded undercroft beneath the old tannery, water to his ankles, watching forty rebels move through the motions of a palace infiltration by the light of three shuttered lanterns. The undercroft had been chosen for its echoes — the vaulted ceiling threw every footfall back doubled, so they learned to move in silence not by discipline but by necessity. Clever. Ulyana would have done the same, once, running routes through Thornfell's cellars.

He was not one of them yet. He suspected he never would be, and that Dain knew it, and that Dain wanted him anyway — wanted him the way a locksmith wants a key, without any particular affection for the metal.

"Again," Dain said from the shadows by the stair. "Third team, you crossed the light-line at the arch. In the palace there are no light-lines, only wards, and the wards do not blink. They do not forgive. Move as if the stone itself were watching you."

*As if the stone itself were watching you.* Ulyana turned the phrase over and found it less metaphor than he'd have liked. Since the night the stars went out, everything in this city felt watched — the gutters, the gargoyles, the long blind windows of the palace rising over the rooftops like a closed eye. The king walked his walls at night; the refugees said so, and the rebels said so, and Ulyana had begun to dream of it himself, a small crowned figure pacing the battlements with its hands behind its back, patient as frost.

"Master Sterling." Sera appeared at his elbow, her ward-keeper's grey cloak dark with spray where she'd waded through the deeper channel. She looked better than she had a week ago — color in her face, the wound in her side bound and healing — but her eyes still carried that bruised vigilance of someone who had watched the sky break open and never quite stopped checking it. "Dain wants you at the far end. There's something he wants you to see."

"He could just ask me."

"Dain doesn't ask." A ghost of a smile. "He arranges."

She led him along the undercroft's edge, past the practicing teams, into a narrower passage where the water ran colder and the lantern-light thinned to nothing. Ulyana's smuggler instincts woke fully here — he counted turns, marked the drip of water, noted the old iron ring set in the wall at shoulder height, rusted but sound. Routes within routes. This cell had been dug out of the city's bones long before the rebellion found it, and whoever had dug it had known what they were doing.

"Where are we going?"

"You'll see." Sera paused at a low archway blocked by a wooden door gone soft with damp. Her hand rested on the latch without lifting it. "Ulyana. Before we go in — I want you to know I argued against this."

"Against what?"

"Against showing you. Dain thinks it will convince you. I think—" She exhaled. "I think some doors, once opened, don't close again. And you've already had enough of those lately."

His mother's sigil flared unbidden behind his eyes: the seven-pointed star worked into the guild vault's lock, the lock Corvane had ordered sealed and then, when Ulyana pressed, had refused to speak of with a flatness that had frightened him more than anger would have. *Forget it or be cast out.* He had copied the ledger instead. He had chosen the truth, and the truth had cost him the only family he'd ever known, and now he was standing in a rebel cellar being offered another door.

"Open it," he said.

* * *

Beyond the door, the passage climbed. Old steps, older stone — pre-wardstone work, Ulyana realized, the kind of masonry that predated Thornfell itself, hauled down from the mountains when the first kings built the capital on the bones of something else. The air changed too. It grew dry, and still, and faintly sweet, like the inside of a sealed jar, and the silence stopped being an absence and became a presence, pressing gently against his ears.

Then the light began.

It came from ahead — a pale, sourceless glow, the exact color of starlight, though no stars had shone in nine days. Ulyana's breath caught. After the black sky, after nights of walking beneath a heaven like a dropped lid, the simple fact of that light hurt somewhere behind his sternum.

The passage opened into a round chamber, and Ulyana understood at once that they had not found this place. It had been *kept*. Someone had swept it, tended it, kept the guttering candles trimmed around the walls. And at its center, half-sunk in the floor as if the room had grown up around it, stood a ward-stone.

It was taller than a man, obelisk-shaped, its surface carved so densely with script that the carvings blurred together at a distance into something like weathered bark. But it was the glow that held him: the stone radiated a faint silver luminescence from within, pulsing very slowly, like a heart beating once a minute. Every other ward-stone Ulyana had ever seen — and a smuggler learns to see them, learns to read their hum the way sailors read wind — had been dead grey rock since the night the wards fell.

This one still lived.

"What is it?" His voice came out rougher than he intended.

"The last anchor-stone in the capital," Sera said quietly. "There were twelve, once. My order kept the maps. When the wards fell, eleven of them went dark — cracked, some of them, physically split, like something reached out of the ground and snapped them. This one didn't. We don't know why." She looked at him sidelong. "Dain thinks you might."

"Why would I—"

"Touch it," said Dain Ashvale, from the doorway behind them.

Ulyana hadn't heard him come in. The man moved like that — quiet as spilled sand, always arriving exactly when he intended to. He stood with his arms folded, firelight from the candles catching the grey at his temples, and his expression was not cruel. That was the worst part. It was patient, and kind, and absolutely certain of what it wanted.

"We've tried everything," Dain said. "Sera's people, my best infiltrators. The stone ignores us. Doesn't warm, doesn't flicker. But it's keyed to royal bloodline magic — Sera told you that much, I think. And your mother—" he let the word sit in the wet air, "—your mother's name is carved into the base. Go look."

Ulyana went. He crouched at the obelisk's foot, and there, among ranks of names worn nearly smooth by centuries, cut deep and fresh by comparison, he read:

**MAERWEN STERLING — BINDER OF THE VEIL — LAST OF THE SEVEN**

His mother. Who had died — who had *been dead*, fourteen years, drowned on a route run gone wrong, mourned in the guildhall with black ribbon on the door — her name cut into a living ward-stone beneath the capital, in a chamber someone still swept.

The pulse of light swelled and dimmed. Swelled and dimmed. Like a heart. Like a held breath.

"She was a Binder," Sera said softly, behind him. "Not a smuggler. Or — both. The guild was never just routes and bribes, Ulyana. Not at the top. Your mother was the last of the line that made the wards, and the wards fell the night the stars went out, and you are her son, and this stone has been waiting nine days for someone with her blood to walk in. Touch it. Please. We need to know what it shows."

"And if I don't want to know?"

Dain's voice, gentle as a hand on the shoulder: "Then the king wins, and everything your mother carved her name into dies with us. I'm sorry. I know how it sounds. But you didn't come all this way to stop at the last door."

Ulyana looked at the stone. At his mother's name. He thought of the vault in the guildhall, and Corvane's face going grey, and the ledger full of crown gold, sixty years of hush money flowing like a vein of corruption through everything he'd trusted. He thought of his mother teaching him cipher letters by candlelight, calling it a game, her fingers steady, her eyes always — he saw it now, always — *watching the window.*

Grief and revelation arrived together, indistinguishable, like two rivers meeting.

He put his palm against the stone.

* * *

The world went white, then went elsewhere.

He stood in a birthing chamber he had never seen and knew instantly: high windows, summer light, the smell of hot stone and lavender and blood. A woman lay in a great bed, grey-lipped, her dark hair plastered to her forehead — a queen, by the circlet askew on the pillow, by the midwives frozen at the walls with their heads bowed as if in church. She was dying. Everyone in the room knew it except perhaps the infant in the cradle beside her, who slept with the absolute, obscene peace of the newborn.

And beside the bed, young — younger than Ulyana was now — stood his mother.

Maerwen Sterling. He had spent fourteen years remembering her as she'd been at the end: tired, laughing, flour on her hands, a woman who smelled of bread and road dust. This Maerwen was another creature entirely. She wore a travelling coat stained to the elbow, and her hair was bound back tight, and her eyes held the terrible calm of someone performing surgery on the world. In her hands she held a length of silver thread that was not thread — it moved like liquid light, and where it passed through the air it left faint lines hanging, geometries Ulyana's eye couldn't follow, as if reality were cloth and she was drawing seams through it.

"Hold the veil open," young Maerwen said, to someone Ulyana couldn't see. "I can't seal it and hold the gate. If it slips while I'm working—"

"It won't slip." A man's voice, low, wrecked with grief. The king? No — younger. A prince, maybe, or a lord, kneeling on the far side of the bed, holding the queen's hand. "Just save her. Whatever it costs, save—"

"I can't save her." No hesitation. No cruelty either. Just the flat mercy of the truth. "The thing took her light to make the crossing. I can only choose where it goes next. Listen to me — it must go into the child. Into the bloodline, where the binding can hold it, where we can watch it, generation to generation. If it goes free tonight it will wear the whole kingdom by winter."

"Into my son." The man's voice broke. "You're asking me to put it into my son."

"I'm asking you to let me cage a wolf in a room instead of the countryside." Maerwen's hands did not shake. Ulyana watched them, his own hands' ghosts, and understood that hers never shook because shaking was a luxury Binders gave up early. "The binding needs a vessel of royal blood and a Binder of Sterling blood, freely given on both sides. That was always the price. You swore the oath, Aldric. Your father swore it. His father before him. Three generations of kings who knew what they carried so the rest of the world could sleep."

Aldric. Younger than Ulyana had ever seen him, and already ruined, already carrying the look of a man agreeing to something that would hollow him out for decades. He bowed his head over the queen's hand. When he spoke again it was barely sound.

"Do it."

What followed, Ulyana watched from a distance that was no distance at all, unable to close his eyes, though these were not properly his eyes. His mother sang — a language with no words he knew, notes that bent the candle flames sideways — and drew the silver thread through the air above the dying queen, and where it passed, something *came out*. Ulyana saw it and his mind flinched away and made him see it anyway: a darkness shaped like absence, like the hole a star leaves, wearing the queen's own face loosely, the way a mask sits on a hook. It screamed without sound. The windows frosted. The midwives wept without knowing why.

And Maerwen gathered it — gathered it like a fisherman hauling a net of living shadow — and turned, and laid it against the sleeping infant's chest, and *sewed*.

The thread sank in. The darkness sank in. The baby did not wake. And across the child's small chest, fading even as it formed, Ulyana saw the mark: a seven-pointed star, his mother's sigil, branded into the skin like a watermark.

"That holds until he's grown," Maerwen whispered. Sweat had broken through her calm at last; her hands, lowering, trembled now that the work was done. "Then it will need rebinding, and each rebinding costs a life, and I am so sorry, Aldric. I am so sorry. I'll spend the rest of mine finding another way. I swear it on my name. I swear it on the stars."

The vision tilted. The chamber dissolved upward like ink in water, and for one last instant Ulyana hung in the white between places, and felt — distinctly, deliberately — a pressure against his palm, as if someone on the other side of a wall had pressed their hand to meet his.

Then he was on his knees on cold stone, and the ward-stone was blazing above him like a captive piece of the lost sky, and Sera was shouting his name.

* * *

They got water into him and sat him against the wall, and the stone's light settled back to its slow heartbeat, and the chamber returned to being a cellar instead of a wound. Ulyana's hands would not entirely stop shaking. He wrapped them around the cup Sera gave him and watched the surface tremble and thought: *she lied to us. She lied for fourteen years, and she sewed a monster into a baby, and she swore on the stars to find another way, and then she drowned on a smuggling route like any common courier, and none of it was true, none of it was the shape of her at all.*

"She was young," Sera said quietly. She had knelt beside him without asking permission, the way she seemed to do everything — present before you noticed, necessary before you agreed. "In what you saw. Younger than either of us."

"She put it in a child." His voice sounded scraped. "She stood there and she *sewed it into a newborn*, and everyone called it mercy, and maybe it was, maybe it was the only—"

"Maybe it was." Sera's voice hardened, just slightly. "I've kept wards for eleven years, Ulyana. I've seen what comes through when they fail. If the choice was the kingdom or one child's freedom, then your mother chose the way ward-keepers choose — badly, and correctly, and alone." She paused. "But that's not really what's breaking you, is it."

No. It wasn't.

"She faked her death." Ulyana stared into the cup. "Fourteen years ago. There was a body, Sera. There was a funeral. Corvane wept — I *watched* him weep. And she wasn't dead, she was down here, or wherever this is, cutting her name into stones and hunting for a binding that doesn't kill, because she promised a dying queen's husband she'd find one. She left me. She left me to grow up in the guild she used as a mask, thinking she was a smuggler, thinking she was *mine*—"

"Or she left you to keep you safe," Sera said. "From the crown. From the thing wearing it. You saw what it does — it wears kings. What do you suppose it would make of the last Binder's heir?"

Ulyana had no answer. The stone pulsed. Somewhere above them, beyond layers of earth and stone and stolen sky, the palace sat on its hill with its lights burning for a king who hadn't truly been awake in months, and inside that king something stirred that his mother had put there with her own two hands, and Ulyana carried her blood the way a fuse carries fire.

Dain crouched down in front of him. Up close, in the stone's silver light, the rebellion's leader looked older and more tired than he ever let himself look in front of his people — and more hungry, too, a hunger he wore like a second cloak.

"Now you understand what you are," Dain said. "You're not a smuggler who stumbled into a war, Ulyana. You're the inheritance. The binding, the bloodline, the whole buried machinery of it — it's yours. Which means the palace isn't just a building we need to infiltrate." His eyes were very steady, and very bright. "It's your mother's unfinished work. And I know where she kept her letters."

Ulyana looked up. The stone pulsed behind him, slow as a sleeping heart, and the light caught the fresh-cut letters of his mother's name, and somewhere in his chest grief and purpose finished their collision and fused into something with edges.

"Her letters," he said.

"Ciphered. Hidden along her old routes — the ones only a Sterling would know to walk." Dain smiled, and it was almost warm. "Your mother left you a trail, Ulyana. All you have to do is follow it home."
