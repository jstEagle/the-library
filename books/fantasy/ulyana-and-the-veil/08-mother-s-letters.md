## 8. Mother's Letters

The first letter was sewn into the lining of a dead man's coat.

Ulyana found it in the waystation at Hollowmere, three days out from Thornfell, in the smuggler cache his mother had taught him to read before she died — before she *died*, he corrected himself now, tasting the word like a coin he suspected was clipped. She had shown him this cache when he was nine years old, crouched beside him in the dark while rain hammered the turf roof, and made him memorize not just where things were hidden but *why* they were hidden there. The coat hung on a peg nearest the door, she'd said, because a fleeing man grabs the coat nearest the door. Everything in her world had a reason. He had thought it was tradecraft.

It was a will. A testament. A map of her heart, folded small and left for him to find only when the stars went out.

He knew it the moment he slid two fingers along the seam of the left sleeve and felt the paper's stiff edge against the oilcloth. His hands shook so badly he had to sit down on the cache crate, in the cold, with the wind moaning through the broken shutter, before he could work the stitches loose without tearing what lay inside.

The letter was written in her cipher — the one she'd called *knotwork*, lines of seemingly innocent trade notation that unraveled under a key he had carried in his head since childhood without knowing it was a key. *Count the goods, love. The numbers are never the numbers.* He read it by candle stub, hunched over his knees, and the first line undid him more thoroughly than any of the rest:

*If you are reading this, the sky has gone dark, and you are alive, and I am sorry.*

*Sorry* was an insufficient word. He read it four times. Outside, the starless night pressed against the shutter cracks like water against a hull, and somewhere far off a wolf sang to nothing — to no moon, no constellations, nothing at all — and Ulyana Sterling, who had crossed the Graywash in fog, who had talked his way past border patrols since he was fifteen, sat in an abandoned waystation and wept for a woman who had been dead for eleven years and was somehow, impossibly, speaking to him in her own careful hand.

*They will tell you I abandoned you,* the letter went on. *I need you to hear me: there is no version of leaving you that did not break me. But the alternative was letting them find you, and they were looking, my darling. From the hour you were born they were looking.*

He read until the candle drowned in its own wax, and then he read by feel, tracing the knotwork with his fingertips like a blind man reading scripture, and when dawn came — a gray, starless, wrong-colored dawn — he copied the letter into his memory the way he memorized routes, every turn and landmark of it, and burned the original in the stove because that was what she would have wanted. The paper curled and flared green at the edges. It smelled, briefly, of juniper. Her ink had always smelled of juniper. He hadn't remembered that until the smoke brought it back, and then he had to grip the edge of the stove until the shaking passed.

* * *

Her old routes ran through the mountains like veins through a body, and he followed them west and south, toward the capital, because the letters told him to. There were three caches between Hollowmere and the pass at Cindervale, she wrote, and each held a letter, and each letter would give him only as much as he could bear. *I know how you take hard news, Uly. You go quiet and you go fast and you do something reckless before the quiet finishes settling. So I have spread the truth out like a long road. Walk it slowly. Please. For once in your life, walk slowly.*

He laughed alone in the dark at that — a short, cracked sound — because walking slowly was the one instruction he had never once managed to follow, and she had known it, and loved him for it, and feared for him because of it, all at once.

The second cache was behind the altar stone of a ruined roadside chapel at Cindervale, wedged in a cavity that generations of smugglers had used and no priest had ever blessed. The chapel had lost its roof a century ago; snow fell through the empty window arches and lay unmelting on the floor, and the silence inside had the particular quality of a place where people had prayed for a long time and then stopped. He knelt at the altar to work the stone loose, and the absurdity of it caught in his throat — Ulyana Sterling, guild thief, kneeling in a house of a god whose name he didn't know, digging for his mother's voice.

This letter was longer. This one had the truth in it, or the shape of the truth, drawn first in outline the way she used to sketch maps for him — borders first, then rivers, then the dangerous places marked in red.

*I was the last Binder,* she wrote. *Not a smuggler who happened to know magic. Not a hedge-witch. A Binder — sworn, trained, the final heir of a line that goes back to the founding of Thornfell, back to the first king who could not sleep safely in his own crown.*

He read the word *Binder* and felt the ground tilt under him. All those years. All the odd disciplines she had dressed up as games — the knotwork cipher, the hours spent teaching him to feel the hum in old ward-stones with his palms flat against them, the way she made him memorize the names of kings not as history but as *lists*, ordered, weighted, certain names spoken softer than others. Aldric's grandfather's name she had said almost in a whisper. He had thought it was reverence. It had been grief.

*You must understand what the Veil is, because the Veil will try to make sure you never understand it. It is not a demon. Demons can be starved, banished, bargained with. It is older and patienter than that. It lives at the edge of our world — in the veil itself, the thin country between what is lit and what is not. And it feeds on starlight the way we feed on bread. When the stars burn, it sleeps, fat and slow. When they gutter, it wakes hungry.*

*And it wears kings.*

She described it the way she'd once described a blizzard coming off the Graywash — plainly, precisely, without decoration, because decoration was for things that weren't going to kill you. *It does not possess them the way stories say. It does not fight the man for his body. It is gentler than that, and crueler. It slips on a king like a mask, and the world sees the mask, and the mask smiles and signs decrees and rides at the head of parades, and underneath, the man is still there — pressed flat, folded small, aware. Always aware. Three generations of kings of Thornfell, Uly. Three men who have sat on that throne and screamed silently for decades while their face gave orders in a voice that was not quite their own.*

*Your father was not one of them. Your father was a ferryman and a fool and the best man I ever knew, and he died honestly, and I envy him sometimes. But the queen — the queen who died birthing Prince Aldric — she summoned me to the palace in her last month because she had discovered what wore her husband, and she was dying anyway, and she wanted one thing done before she went.*

He stopped reading. Snow was coming through the window arches and landing on the page, and he watched a flake touch the ink and melt into a small clear blister, and he understood suddenly why she had written these letters to be found after the stars went out — because the truth required darkness to be legible. In a lit world, none of this would have been believable. In a lit world it would have sounded like madness. The Veil had arranged the sky itself into its own alibi.

*We bound it into the newborn prince,* she wrote. *Me and two others who did not survive the working. The binding is anchored in royal blood — that is why the wards across the whole border sing with palace magic, why they hold so long and fail so completely. We sealed the Veil beneath the surface of the child, layered over layer, a knot with no visible end, and the price of the sealing was paid in full that night, and I swore on my own life that no one would ever learn what I had done or whose son I had saved by doing it.*

*Aldric was nine days old. I held him while the knot closed. He cried the whole time, and I sang to him, and I have wondered for twenty-five years whether some part of him remembers my voice from that night — whether that is why, of all the palaces in the world, the roads I chose to hide us near were the roads that led here.*

Then the letter turned, as her letters always turned, from the wide map to the single marked road — the red line that led to him.

*And then you were born, and everything changed. Do you see it yet, my love? A Binder's power runs in blood. The binding I laid on Aldric recognizes Sterling blood the way a lock recognizes its key. If the crown ever learned that a Binder's child lived — a child who might grow into the strength to undo the knot, or worse, to be used as a lever against it — they would have come for you. Not the Veil. The *men* around the Veil, the chancellors and spymasters who serve it without knowing what they serve. They would have taken you to the palace and put you in a room with the king and let the thing wearing his face study you like a jeweler studies a stone.*

*So I buried myself. I wrote my own death — a fever, a body burned too fast to question, a guild that owed me enough not to look closely. Corvane helped me arrange it; he was young then, and believed in the old purposes, though I think the believing has gone out of him since. And I gave you to the guild as an orphan, because an orphan is invisible, and I stayed near you the only way I could — as a ghost in the route-books, a hand behind the caches, a name in the ledger that no one questioned because the routes kept running true.*

*Every winter I watched you from a distance. Every time you took the Cindervale pass in bad weather I stood at the top of it and counted the lights of your caravan until you were down. You are reading this thinking your mother abandoned you. I stood on a mountain in the snow every winter of your life, my darling, counting your fires.*

He put the letter down on the altar stone.

The chapel was very cold and very quiet and the snow came down through the roofless arches with the patience of something that had all the time in the world, and Ulyana knelt there among other people's forgotten prayers and grieved — not the clean grief of eleven years ago, which had been grief for an absence, but a rawer, stranger grief, grief for a presence he had never known was there. Every winter. Counting his fires. He thought of all the times he had cursed her in his adolescence, quietly, in the way orphans curse — *she could have stayed, she chose not to* — and the curse curdled in his chest now and came back up as something else. She had been thirty feet away in a crowd at the Thornfell harvest fair when he was sixteen. He knew that now with absolute certainty, the way you know a thing you've always half-dreamed. He had walked past her buying roasted chestnuts. She had probably smelled the same woodsmoke he did.

He was mourning a woman he had never met, who had been standing beside him his whole life.

* * *

The third cache was the hardest to reach, because she had meant it to be. It lay in the deep ways beneath the old watchtower at Raven's Stair, a day's climb off her route, down a chimney of rock that required the rope-work only guild-trained hands possessed. *If you cannot reach it,* she had written in the second letter, *then you are not ready for it, and you should carry the first two truths and live a long quiet life.* He reached it in four hours with split palms and a racing heart, and laughed breathlessly at the bottom — at her, at the sheer audacity of a dead woman setting an entrance exam from beyond the grave.

The third letter was different. Shorter. The handwriting had aged in it — he could see the tremor that had come into her hand in her last years, the same tremor he'd seen in the route-books from her final season. She had known she was dying when she wrote it. Fever, the guild record said. He suspected now the record lied even about that.

*By now you know what I was and what I did, and you hate me a little for both, and that is right and proper. Hold onto the anger; it will keep you moving when sorrow would sit you down.*

*Three things remain, and then I will let you go.*

*First: the binding weakens. It was built to last a lifetime and it has lasted nearly two, and knots fray, and the Veil pulls at the threads every night of every year. The stars going out is not the beginning of its freedom, Uly — it is the middle. It has been drinking the sky for months, storing light against the day it tears loose. When the last star dies, the knot breaks, and it will step out of Aldric like a man stepping out of clothes, and it will need a new one.*

*Second: there is another way. I found its beginning and could not finish it — I was too old, and too afraid, and I had you to think of. The notes are in the vault beneath Thornfell, under the old city, in my workroom. A binding that asks no death. A living anchor, freely given. I will not lie to you about what it costs. But it does not cost a life, and that is the whole of the mercy in it.*

*Third, and last, and hardest: trust almost no one. The guild has grown comfortable guarding a secret instead of serving it. The rebels in the capital are brave and angry and half of them want the king dead rather than freed, and a dead king is exactly what the Veil wants, because a corpse is an open door. And the palace — the palace will seem, at moments, like the only honest room in the world, because the man inside it has nothing left to hide. That is the trap of it, my love. The prisoner's cell feels like sanctuary to everyone who visits.*

*Trust the ones who ask nothing. Trust the ones who tell you the cost up front. Trust yourself — you are Sterling, and the knot knows your hands even if you don't.*

*I have loved you every day from the first day, including all the days you did not know I existed. Especially those.*

*Walk slowly.*

*— M.*

He sat a long time in the dark under Raven's Stair, the letter open on his knee, his rope still coiled over one shoulder, his palms stinging. Somewhere above him the starless sky turned its slow wheel over a world gone strange, and somewhere ahead of him the capital waited with its rebel cells and its masked king and its honest rooms, and every word of warning she had written was already arranging itself around people he knew. Sera, who asked things of him constantly, but honestly. Dain Ashvale, whose welcome had fit him like a tailored coat — and he thought now, with a cold slide in his stomach, of his mother's oldest rule: *nothing fits that well unless it was measured for you.*

And Corvane. *He was young then, and believed in the old purposes, though I think the believing has gone out of him.* Ulyana had copied sixty years of crown payments out of the guild ledger with his own hand. He had felt the betrayal of it like a knife between the ribs. Now he felt something more complicated — the ache of understanding a man too late, seeing the arc of him entire: a young believer, handed an impossible secret, choosing comfort year after year until the belief was gone and only the comfort remained, fat and defended and willing to sell anything to keep it.

He climbed back up the chimney as the gray false dawn came, and stood on the watchtower's broken lip looking south toward the capital, and the wind up there had teeth in it. Two days' travel, if the roads held. Less, if he used the deep ways.

He thought about walking slowly. He thought about her voice saying it — he had her voice now, recovered whole from the letters, dry and warm and faintly amused, the voice of a woman who had counted his fires from mountaintops — and he thought about how she would already know he wasn't going to manage it.

Then he saw the smoke.

Far to the south, over the low hills above the capital road, a column of black smoke rose straight in the windless morning — too black for a cookfire, too deliberate for an accident, rising from the exact stretch of the old smuggler road where the rebel cell's outer safehouse stood. And below it, on the road itself, small with distance but unmistakable to a man who had spent his life reading movement at range, a line of riders was moving north. Away from the smoke. Toward him.

They flew no colors. They rode like soldiers pretending not to be soldiers. And one of them, at the head of the line, wore a cloak cut in a style Ulyana had seen exactly once before — hanging on the shoulders of Dain Ashvale's messenger, the night the rebels had first offered him belonging.

His mother had told him to trust almost no one. Standing on Raven's Stair with her last letter against his chest, Ulyana finally understood that she had written that line about this precise moment — about the day the people offering him a home would show him what the home was built on.

He folded the letter into his breast pocket, over his heart, where the knotwork key lived, and started north to meet them.
