## 9. Dain's True Price

The undercity had a smell Ulyana knew from his mother's letters before he ever breathed it: tallow and wet stone, old smoke soaked so deep into the brick that no rain could wash it out. The rebels called this stretch of the capital *the Ribs* — the arched bones of some older city, buried when the palace above decided it wanted more sky. He came down through a coal chute behind a tannery, as Dain Ashvale's note had instructed, and stood in the dark for a long moment letting his eyes adjust, letting his hands remember they were weapons even when they hung empty at his sides.

*Trust almost no one.*

He was tired of the sentence. It sat in him like a splinter he kept probing with his tongue. Sera had earned something like trust — she'd bled on his shoulder through three days of tunnels, cursed him in two dialects, and never once asked what he carried in his breast pocket. But Dain Ashvale was another matter. Dain had sent word that moved like money through the undercity, fast and quiet and expecting nothing back, and nothing in Ulyana's experience that moved like money was free.

A lantern kindled ahead, low and green-shuttered. A woman with a crossbow slung across her back gestured him forward without a word, and he followed her down the Ribs, past doorways bricked shut generations ago, past a shrine to some saint of lost roads whose face had been chiseled off during one of the crown's many purges. The capital above them was starless. Even down here, Ulyana could feel the wrongness of it pressing through forty feet of stone — a weight like held breath, like the whole city leaning toward the palace without knowing why.

They stopped before an iron door painted with a fishmonger's sign gone gray with age. His escort knocked twice, paused, knocked once. The door opened on darkness and the smell of ink.

Inside, the room was long and narrow, lit by a single lamp, its walls papered with maps. Not smuggler's maps — Ulyana knew those at a glance, all trade routes and toll gates and the soft places in a city's skin. These were different. These were the palace itself, drawn over and over in different hands, different decades, each layer annotated in a cramped, patient script. Guard rotations going back years. Water tables. The names of servants, crossed out and rewritten as they died or disappeared or turned. Someone had been building this document the way a siege engineer builds a wall — stone by stone, year by year, waiting for the day it would be needed.

Dain Ashvale stood at the far end with his sleeves rolled and his forearms ink-stained, a man built like a dockworker but moving like a fencing master, economical, nothing wasted. He was perhaps fifty, gray coming into his black beard, and his eyes were the pale gray of a winter river — eyes that took your measure and filed you away somewhere you'd never find again.

"Sterling." He said the name like he was testing the weight of a coin. "You look like your mother."

"You never met my mother."

"No," Dain agreed, and smiled, and the smile was warm the way a hearth is warm when the house around it is burning. "But I've read everything she wrote. Sit. Eat. You've been underground for six days."

There was bread and hard cheese and wine that tasted of cherries and iron. Ulyana ate because refusing food in the undercity was an insult, and insults were currency he couldn't afford to spend. Sera sat beside him, her arm still bound where the ward-shrapnel had caught her, and he noticed she didn't touch the wine.

"You've read the letters," Ulyana said. "Then you know what the king is. What's wearing him."

"I know what the records say." Dain folded his hands. "I know that sixty years ago a Binder named Maren Sterling helped seal something into a newborn prince, and I know that every rebinding since has cost a life, and I know that the stars went out eleven nights ago because whatever sleeps in Aldric Thorne has begun to wake and eat the light that keeps it dreaming." He said it all evenly, without drama, the way a physician recites symptoms. "What I know beyond the records is simpler. The kingdom is dying by inches. Grain rots in the granaries. The wards are down. The guilds are sealing their routes and hoarding like rats. And the man responsible sits in a palace eating off silver while his reflection does his talking."

"So we free him," Ulyana said. "Rebind the Veil. My mother left—"

"Your mother left a rite that requires a Binder of Sterling blood and a willing vessel, and both of those are rare enough that I won't gamble a kingdom on finding them." Dain's voice didn't rise. That was the thing about him Ulyana would remember longest: he never raised his voice, not even later, not even at the end. "I have four hundred people in this city who will starve or hang within the season if nothing changes. I have a court full of parasites — the human kind — who will carve up the succession the moment the throne empties, unless someone is standing there when it does. What I need, Sterling, is not a miracle. It's a door."

"A door."

"Every binding has an anchor. Every anchor is blood-bound to the vessel — that's how your mother's generation built it, so no outsider could tear it loose. Which means the binding can be *un*bound the same way. From inside. By blood." Dain leaned forward, lamplight pooling in the hollows of his face. "You can walk into the palace through ways no guard alive knows. You carry Sterling blood. You can stand in front of Aldric Thorne, put your hand on his chest, and unmake the thing that holds him together — and the Veil with it."

Ulyana set down the bread. His mouth had gone dry. "Unmake it how?"

"Kill him." Dain said it the way other men said *good morning*. "One knife. One moment. The parasite dies with its host — every record agrees on that. The stars come back or they don't, but either way the throne is empty, the curse is ended, and there are four hundred people in this room who get to see next spring."

The silence after was very long. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone, a bell rang the hour, and Ulyana thought about the king weeping — no, he hadn't heard that yet; that was ahead of him — he thought instead about the vision on the ward-stone: his mother, young and terrified, sealing something into a newborn prince while a queen died on the sheets below. She hadn't looked like a woman killing a monster. She'd looked like a woman trying to save a child.

"The records agree," Ulyana said slowly, "because everyone who wrote them only ever saw the binding half. My mother spent twenty years learning the other half. Killing the host doesn't end the Veil, Ashvale. It frees it. A parasite doesn't die with its mask — it finds a new one. You wouldn't be ending the curse. You'd be holding the door open and handing the crown to whatever walks through."

Something flickered across Dain's face — quick, quickly mastered, but Ulyana had spent his life reading faces across smuggling tables where a wrong read meant a shallow grave. It wasn't doubt. It was the look of a man whose prepared answer had been interrupted, and who needed a moment to decide which prepared answer to use.

"Maren Sterling believed the same thing, at the end," Dain said. "And it made her cautious. Cautious women write letters and hide keys and let kings sleepwalk their kingdoms into ruin. I'm not asking you to believe me, Sterling. I'm asking you to open the door. What happens on the other side of it is my burden, not yours."

"That's not how burdens work."

"No," Dain said softly. "But it's how they're sold."

Sera's hand closed on Ulyana's wrist under the table. Not restraining. Warning. *Careful.* But her face, when he turned to her, was not warning at all. It was pleading.

"Dain's people pulled me out of a ward-collapse," she said quietly. "Half-dead. They hid me for a week while Corvane's hunters were sniffing every cellar in Thornfell. They fed me. They got me here." Her thumb pressed harder against his pulse. "These are good people, Ulyana. Whatever you think of the plan—"

"The plan is murder dressed up as mercy."

"The plan is the only one that doesn't require a sacrifice!" Her voice cracked, and she pulled her hand back as if the words had burned it. "Yours, specifically. Have you read your own mother's notes? A living anchor, freely given, bound inside the veil's edge forever. Neither alive nor dead. You want to volunteer for that? Because I've watched you carry that key against your chest for two hundred miles like it was going to hatch, and I know exactly what you're planning, and I am telling you — *telling you* — that a dead king and a live kingdom is a better bargain than a saved king and a friend in a box."

The lamp hissed. On the walls, the layered maps of the palace seemed to lean in, decades of patient observation, all of it converging on this table, on this moment, on him.

"You knew," Ulyana said to her. "About the knife. About what he wants me for. You've known since the tunnels."

"I knew Dain wanted the palace opened," Sera said. "I didn't ask how. There's a difference."

"There isn't. Not anymore." He stood. The chair scraped back, loud in the lamp-lit room, and somewhere behind the fishmonger's door a guard shifted, and Dain raised one hand slightly and the shifting stopped. "My mother faked her death and buried herself alive in ciphered letters for twenty years to keep a binding intact. She gave up her name, her guild, her son — everything — so that no one would ever do what you're proposing. And you want me to be the hand that undoes it because it's faster."

"Faster saves lives," Dain said.

"So did she. Slower." Ulyana looked at the maps one last time — at the patient, terrible architecture of a man who had been planning regicide the way other men planned harvests — and felt something in his chest go cold and clear, like water over stone. "You don't want a Binder, Ashvale. You want a key that doesn't ask questions. And when the Veil pours out of that corpse you make of the king and finds a new mask — and it will — you'll be standing closest to the throne. Was that the plan too? Or just the profit?"

For the first time, Dain Ashvale's smile failed. What replaced it was worse: patience. "Walk out that door, Sterling, and every route out of this city closes behind you. The guild won't take you. The crown wants you. You'll be alone in a starless capital with a dead woman's rite and no army."

"I've been alone before," Ulyana said. "It's the places that feel like home I don't trust."

He walked. Sera didn't follow — he heard her breath catch, heard her start to rise, heard Dain say something low and level that he couldn't make out and didn't stay to decipher. The iron door closed behind him with a sound like a book shutting, and the Ribs swallowed him, dark and dripping, smelling of tallow and old smoke.

He made it three arches before he stopped, pressed his back against cold brick, and took out the knotwork key. It was warm from lying over his heart. In his mother's last letter, beneath the cipher, she had written one line in plain script, the only line she'd ever allowed herself:

*If they offer you belonging, count the price aloud before you accept.*

Above him, through forty feet of stone, the starless sky pressed down like a lid. And somewhere between him and the palace, in the dark of the undercity, footsteps had begun to follow — soft, patient, more than one — keeping pace with the only man alive who could open the king's door.

* * *

They cornered him at the drowned chapel.

Ulyana had led them there deliberately — a smuggler never runs blind, and the chapel of Saint Verene, sunk to its windows in the old cistern, offered three exits and one choke point, and he'd cut purses in worse rooms than this. He stood on the altar rail with black water lapping at the pews below and waited while the lanterns came around the corners: four of them, then five, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who owned the dark.

The first voice was Sera's.

"Don't." She stepped into the lantern light alone, hands empty, and the men behind her stayed back like trained hounds. Her face in the greenish glow was wrecked — sleepless, furious, afraid. "Don't run. Please. Just listen to me for five minutes and then hate me if you still need to."

"You defended him."

"I defended the four hundred people sleeping ten feet from his maps!" Her voice echoed off the flooded nave, off the drowned saints. "Do you know what happens to them if Dain falls? Corvane's hunters, the crown's inquisitors, the Veil's — *things* — picking us off cellar by cellar? I've seen what the court does to rebel cells, Ulyana. I buried friends. So yes, I stood there and I swallowed the taste of it, because someone has to keep those people alive long enough to see a better plan exist." She waded a step closer, water dragging at her boots. "That's not the same as agreeing. That's surviving. You of all people should know the difference."

"And the knife?" Ulyana kept his voice flat. "Did you survive that too?"

"He thinks it will work. He genuinely believes it. That's the horror of Dain — he's not greedy, he's *certain*, and certainty kills more people than greed ever has." Sera stopped at the foot of the altar rail, looking up at him, and the lantern light caught the bandage on her arm, the bruise along her jaw from the tunnel collapse, all the small ledger of what she'd paid to stand here. "But I've been reading your mother's letters since you fell asleep on the road. All of them. And she wrote something in the third one — the one about the second binding. She wrote that the Veil cannot be killed, only held, and that anyone who promises otherwise is either a fool or its creature." Her chin lifted. "I don't think Dain is its creature. I think he's a fool. And fools can be survived. Monsters can't."

Ulyana looked at her for a long time. The water dripped. Somewhere in the dark behind the lanterns, the five men waited with the stillness of people following orders, and he understood suddenly that they weren't there to drag him back to Dain.

They were there because Sera had asked them to help her stop him doing something stupid — or to stop her, if it came to that. Dain's leash ran through everyone.

"Why are you really here?" he asked.

"Because you're going to do it anyway." She said it simply, without accusation. "The rite. The anchor. Yourself. I've watched you walk toward it for two hundred miles and I'm done pretending I can talk you out of it. So here's my price, Sterling, and it's the only one I'll ever ask: when the time comes, you tell me everything. No noble silences. No walking off alone to die politely. If you're going to break my heart, you'll do it to my face, with the lights on."

Below them, the black water shivered — though nothing had touched it. Along the drowned walls, the mosaic saints seemed to shift in the lantern light, their chipped faces turning, and Ulyana felt the old wrongness press down through the stone again, closer now, attentive. The Veil knew where he was. Perhaps it had always known.

"Five minutes ago I walked out on the only people offering me an army," he said. "Now you're offering me a witness."

"I'm offering you the truth," Sera said. "It's worth less and costs more. Take it anyway."

He almost laughed. Instead he crouched on the altar rail and held out his hand, and she took it, and her grip was cold from the cistern air and steady as a lock.

Behind her, at the edge of the lantern light, one of the five men turned his head sharply toward the flooded doorway — listening to something none of the rest of them could hear — and Ulyana saw the man's shadow move a half-second after he did.

Only the shadow. Only a half-second.

But Ulyana had grown up reading the space between things, and he knew a delayed shadow when he saw one, and his blood went cold as cistern water.

"Sera," he said quietly. "How many of your people has Dain lost to the palace in the last month?"

She frowned. "Six. Why?"

"Count again," Ulyana said, watching the man's shadow lag behind him like a thing on a string. "And this time, count the ones who came *back*."
