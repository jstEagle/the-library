## 10. Taken by the Crown

They came for him at the tollhouse of Marrow Bridge, in the hour before dawn when even smugglers sleep with one eye open and one hand on a knife.

Ulyana had chosen the tollhouse deliberately — it was neutral ground, guild-marked, a place where coin bought silence and the wardens of the bridge looked the other way for a fee older than the kingdom. He had slept there a dozen times on routes north, wrapped in his cloak beneath the sagging beams, listening to the river grind its stones below. It was the safest place left to him.

That was how he knew it was betrayal. Nothing else could have found him here.

The door did not burst open. That was the first sign of how wrong everything had become. Royal guards did not burst; they arrived, the way winter arrives, inevitable and already inside your bones. Ulyana woke not to sound but to absence — the river's murmur gone muffled, the draft under the door stilled — and opened his eyes to find the room full of grey cloaks and drawn steel, six of them standing in the dark like they had grown up out of the floorboards.

"Ulyana Sterling." The speaker was a captain, broad and grey-bearded, with the flat voice of a man who had delivered this sentence so many times it had worn smooth as a river stone. "In the name of King Aldric, you are taken."

Ulyana's hand crept toward the knife beneath his cloak, and the captain shook his head, almost gently.

"I'd rather you didn't," he said. "Not because I fear you. Because I've been told to bring you breathing, and I'd hate to spoil my record."

There were too many of them, and they stood wrong — not bunched at the door where a raiding party should crowd, but spread through the room, each one covering another's blind side, trained, patient, unhurried. Ulyana let his hand fall away from the knife. His mind was moving faster than his body, running the arithmetic of it: *they knew the tollhouse, they knew the night, they knew the name I travel under.* He had used three false names since leaving Sera and the rebel cell behind in the capital's undercity. This was the fourth, the oldest, the truest.

Only one living person outside the rebellion knew it.

*Corvane.*

The word landed in him like a stone dropped down a well. He heard it fall and fall and never heard it strike bottom.

"You came quickly," Ulyana said. His voice sounded steadier than he felt. "Marlow's Crossing is four days' hard ride from the capital. Either you've been following me since Thornfell, or someone drew you a map."

The captain's face did not change, but something behind his eyes flickered — the look of a man who has been paid not to answer questions and finds the questions arriving anyway. "We go where we're sent."

"And who sent you?"

"The crown."

"The crown," Ulyana repeated softly. "Or the thing wearing it?"

For a moment — just a moment — the captain's jaw tightened, and his eyes went, involuntarily, to the window, where the sky hung starless and black as spilled ink. Then he mastered himself. "Bind his hands," he said. "Gently. He's worth more whole."

*Worth more.* The phrase followed Ulyana out into the cold, down the tollhouse stairs, past the bridge-wardens who would not meet his eye — paid men, all of them, and their silence had simply been repriced. It followed him as they bound his wrists with silk cord, not rope, because rope chafes and the crown wanted its cargo undamaged. It followed him onto the black coach that waited in the fog with its lanterns hooded, its horses shod in felt so the hooves made no sound on the stones.

Someone wanted him taken quietly. Someone wanted no witnesses, no rumors, no questions.

Someone had sold him, and had done it carefully, and had known exactly what he was worth.

---

The journey south took three days, and Ulyana spent them learning the shape of his cage.

It was a strange cage, soft-walled. They fed him well — hot bread, salted meat, wine that he did not drink because he trusted nothing now, least of all kindness. They did not beat him. They did not question him. When he asked where they were going, they said *the capital*. When he asked what would happen there, they said nothing at all, and the nothing had a practiced quality, like a wall built long ago and well maintained.

On the second day, watching the starless country slide past the coach's shuttered window, Ulyana finally let himself think the thought he had been refusing since Marrow Bridge.

Corvane had traded him.

He turned it over the way he'd turn a suspected counterfeit coin, looking for the flaw, the seam, the place where the lie showed through. But there was no flaw. It fit perfectly, and that was what hurt. Corvane had sealed the routes, forbidden the capital, locked the vault with his mother's sigil on it — and then, when Ulyana slipped away anyway, when the guildmaster's own protégé became the loose thread that could unravel sixty years of careful silence, Corvane had done what he had always done. He had cut losses. He had paid tribute. He had bought the guild's survival with the only currency he had left that the crown might want.

A smuggler who knew too much. A Sterling. The last person alive who had walked the old routes and read the old ledger and touched the vault that bore his mother's mark.

*You were never family,* Ulyana thought, staring at the dark glass. *You were an investment. And investments get liquidated.*

But even as he thought it, he remembered Corvane's hands — scarred, ink-stained, teaching a boy of nine to pick his first lock, guiding his fingers with a patience the man showed no one else. He remembered the ledger's columns, sixty years of crown payments, and understood suddenly that Corvane had not been corrupt so much as *terrified*, all this time, terrified and paying protection money to a monster because the alternative was a war the guild could not survive. Cowardice wore the mask of pragmatism for so long it forgot its own face.

And now, when the choice finally came — the boy or the guild — Corvane had chosen the guild, exactly as Ulyana would have predicted, exactly as Corvane had probably hated himself for doing, if the man still had it in him to hate himself.

"He didn't sell me cheap," Ulyana murmured to the empty coach. "I wonder what he got. Amnesty? Routes? Or just another year of being left alone?"

No one answered. The felt-shod hooves whispered on the road, and the starless sky pressed down like a lid.

---

They brought him into the capital at dusk, through a gate he had never used — not the great southern gate with its customs houses and queues, but a small postern in the western wall, unmarked, unguarded by anything but two bored sentries who waved the coach through without looking inside. A smuggler's entrance. A guild entrance.

*Sixty years of payments,* Ulyana thought, *and this is what they bought. A door.*

The palace rose above the city's rooftops as they wound upward through streets gone quiet and strange. Ulyana had seen the palace before, from a distance, always from a distance — white walls and green roofs, banners hanging limp in air that never seemed to move anymore. Up close it was worse. Up close he could see that the banners were faded, that the green roofs were streaked with grime no one had cleaned, that the windows of the upper floors were dark while the lower ones burned bright with a light that had a color he didn't like — a color like the last breath of a dying ember, reddish and hungry.

The guards marched him across courtyards where servants hurried with their eyes down, past fountains that had run dry, their stone basins cracked and starred with frost. No one looked at him. No one looked at anything. The whole palace moved with the hunched, careful gait of people who had learned that attention was dangerous, that curiosity was punished, that the safest life was the smallest one.

They took him down.

Of course they took him down. Every palace in every story keeps its truths underground, and this one was no different. Stairs spiraled into the earth, torch-lit, the air growing colder and wetter with each turn until the smell of the river reached them — the dungeon had been cut into the rock beside the water, and the water spoke through the walls in a low constant voice, saying nothing, forever.

His cell was clean. That unsettled him more than filth would have. Clean straw, a blanket, a bucket, a candle stub in a niche. Prepared. Expected. He had been awaited.

The door was iron-banded oak, and it closed with a sound like a held breath finally released.

Ulyana sat on the straw and listened to the river and tried to make a plan, and failed, because every plan required knowing what they wanted from him, and he did not know, and the not-knowing sat in his chest like swallowed ice.

*Worth more whole. Worth more whole.* Worth more to whom? To the Veil, which needed Binders dead or bound? To whatever remained of Aldric, lucid enough to send for the son of the woman who had sealed the curse? Or to some third player he hadn't met yet, some chamberlain or spymaster wearing a human face over something else?

The candle burned down. Somewhere above, muffled by stone, the palace settled into its nightly rhythms — doors, footsteps, the distant clatter of a kitchen. Ulyana lay back on the straw and stared at the ceiling, where the torchlight through the door-grille made shapes that moved when he wasn't watching them directly.

That was when he heard the weeping.

It came through the wall to his left — the eastern wall, he noted automatically, the wall that must run deep beneath the palace's foundations, deeper than any cellar he'd mapped in the city above. At first he took it for water, the river finding some new throat to speak through. But water does not catch. Water does not break and gather itself and break again. This was a man sobbing, alone, in the dark, with the raw hopeless rhythm of someone who has been crying for a very long time and has stopped expecting it to help.

Ulyana sat up slowly.

The weeping went on. It was not loud. It was, if anything, terribly quiet — the sound of a man who has learned to weep silently because even his grief is watched. And threaded through it, half-drowned, Ulyana caught words.

"—please— I didn't mean— I never meant—"

A pause. A longer silence, as though the weeper were listening to something. Then, in a whisper scraped thin:

"—she said you'd come. She said her blood would come. Please. Please be her blood."

Ulyana's skin went cold.

*She.* Not a name. A weight, carried carefully, the way you carry something broken. And the plea — *please be her blood* — wrapped around him and squeezed, because there was only one *she* that mattered, only one woman whose blood anyone in this palace could possibly be waiting for.

His mother. The Binder. The woman who had faked her death and hidden her son and left letters in cipher along the old routes, letters Ulyana had spent weeks decoding, letters that ended mid-sentence in a hand gone suddenly shaky.

The king was weeping against his cell wall. The king — the parasite, the veil-born thing, the monster that had eaten three generations of monarchs and snuffed the stars to feed — was begging, in a voice cracked down to the quick, for Ulyana's mother's blood to come and save him.

Unless.

Unless the weeping was the trap. Unless the Veil had learned that monsters catch more flies with grief than with iron, had dressed its hunger in a father's sorrow, had rehearsed this broken voice in the dark for exactly the listener it knew would come. Ulyana pressed his palm flat against the cold stone and thought: *it knows about my mother. It knows I decoded the letters. If it knows that, it knows everything, and everything includes how to make me walk willingly into its mouth.*

The weeping faltered. Stopped. A long silence, and then, so faint he might have imagined it:

"...I can hear you thinking, Sterling boy. Your mother thought loudly too."

Then footsteps — slow, dragging, receding — and silence, and the river saying nothing, forever.

Ulyana did not sleep. He sat with his back against the eastern wall, his knees drawn up, the dead candle guttering beside him, and he listened to the place where the weeping had been, and he hated himself for what he was feeling.

Because it was not fear. It was not despair, though despair was there too, banked coals of it — the guild sold him, the rebels wanted a weapon, the mother he mourned had been a stranger all along, and now he sat in a hole beneath a stolen palace waiting to learn which master claimed him. All of that was true, and heavy, and real.

But underneath it, curling like smoke through the cracks, was something worse.

Curiosity.

*Who are you?* he thought at the wall. *Are you the king, or is the king a coat you wear? Is there a man in there at all, some poor Aldric drowning behind his own eyes, screaming into the dark while the thing smiles with his mouth? Or is the weeping yours, the parasite's, some vast alien loneliness wearing human sorrow like a borrowed cloak?*

*And why do you know my mother's name? Why do you wait for her blood? What did she do to you, in that vault, with that sigil, that you still cry about it decades later?*

The questions would not stop. They bred in the dark like rats. And Ulyana understood, sitting in the straw with the river muttering beyond the stone, that this was perhaps the cruelest part of the cage — not the iron, not the silk cords, not the betrayal that had put him here. The cruelty was that somewhere above him, in a room lit with hungry ember-light, the enemy he had crossed a kingdom to destroy was waiting to be *known*, and some traitorous part of him, the part that had been a lonely boy raised on other people's secrets, wanted nothing so much as to climb those stairs and look the monster in its borrowed face.

The candle died. In the darkness, the river kept its silence, and the wall stayed warm where his palm rested against it — warm, though the stone should have been freezing, warm as a sleeping animal, warm as a hand.

Somewhere far above, a bell tolled once, marking an hour no one counted anymore.

Three nights remained until the new moon.
