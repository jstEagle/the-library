## 11. The King in the Mirror Room

They came for him at the hour when even the rats went quiet.

Ulyana heard them first — boots on stone, too many, moving with the drilled precision of palace guard rather than the loose shuffle of dungeon turnkeys. He had spent eleven days in the black cells beneath the palace, long enough to learn the grammar of its sounds: which drips meant rain above, which groans of settling masonry meant the furnaces below were lit. These footsteps fit none of it. They were deliberate. Ceremonial.

He stood before the door arrived, because a man who could still stand should.

The lock turned without a key he could see — wardwork, then, the kind Sera used to mutter about, the kind that answered to blood and office — and the door swung inward on lamplight so gold it looked like something remembered rather than seen. Four guards in the king's silver-and-blue. A chamberlain whose face was powdered to the color of old tallow. And behind them all, filling the corridor like weather, a woman in grey who did not blink.

"Ulyana Sterling," the chamberlain said, as though reading from a list he'd memorized and resented. "His Majesty will receive you."

"Will he." Ulyana's voice came out rougher than he intended, scraped by disuse and bad water. "Does His Majesty know he's receiving me?"

Something moved behind the chamberlain's eyes — not fear exactly, but the careful blankness of a man standing very close to an edge he has learned not to look down. The woman in grey said nothing at all. She simply watched Ulyana the way a spider watches a wrapped thing.

"You'll come," she said. Her voice was soft and many-voiced, like wind through a reed bed. "You've always been coming here, little smuggler. It only took us this long to send for you."

The guards flanked him. No one bound his hands, which told him more than chains would have: whatever waited upstairs, they did not think his hands mattered.

* * *

The way up out of the dungeons was longer than the way down had any right to be. Ulyana counted turns out of habit — left, left, the long stair, a gallery where portraits hung shrouded in dust-cloths like witnesses with their faces covered. Through narrow windows the sky showed starless, a bruised and lightless dome that pressed on the city below. Eleven days since the wards fell, and the dark had settled over Thornfell like ash over a fire that was not yet out.

He catalogued what he saw, because cataloguing was how he stayed sane. Guard rotations thin on the upper floors. Candles burning low-quality tallow in a palace that should have smelled of beeswax and myrrh. Servants who moved through the corridors with their eyes fixed on the floor, stepping around one another in patterns too practiced to be anything but fear wearing routine's clothing.

And everywhere, mirrors.

That was the thing that began to crawl along his spine as they climbed. Every landing had one. Every gallery. Tall mirrors in gilt frames, small mirrors in alcoves, a great oval of clouded glass at the head of the final stair — and every one of them draped or angled or hooded with black cloth, like eyes being kept shut by force.

"Why cover them?" he asked, mostly to hear a voice that was his own.

No one answered. But the woman in grey smiled, and her smile had too much patience in it.

* * *

The mirror room was at the top of the eastern tower, and it was not what he expected, though he could not have said what he had expected. Not a throne room. Not a place of audience. A single round chamber walled in glass — dozens upon dozens of mirrors, uncovered here, unhooded, catching the lamplight and multiplying it into a cold constellation of reflections. In their center stood a table strewn with maps, half-eaten food, letters sealed and unsealed, a chess set abandoned mid-game. And beside the table, alone, stood a man in a plain grey robe with no crown on his head.

King Aldric Vane was thinner than his coinage. That was Ulyana's first thought, absurd and irreverent, the smuggler's reflex of appraising everything by what it could be sold for. The face on the realm's silver showed a strong jaw, a full beard, eyes level and sure. The man before him had the same jaw gone hollow, the same beard streaked white at the chin, and eyes that were doing something Ulyana had never seen eyes do: looking at him and through him at once, as if Ulyana were a window and something behind the window wanted measuring.

"So," Aldric said. His voice was quiet, cracked, human. "Sterling's son."

"You know me."

"I've known your name since before you could walk." The king turned toward the table, and Ulyana saw his hands — steady, careful, the hands of a man holding himself together by main force. "Your mother wrote to me. Did you know that? Letters, in cipher, twice a year, for years after she died. *After*, I say, because we both knew she wasn't dead. She was hiding you. From me." A pause, and something terrible moved through his expression, grief and shame braided tight. "From it. Wearing me."

The lamps guttered. All of them, at once, though there was no draft in a room walled in glass.

Ulyana felt the temperature fall. Felt, more than saw, the reflections begin to disagree.

It started subtly — the way wrongness always starts, if you're trained to notice. His own image in the nearest mirror blinked a half-beat late. Then the king's reflection in the tall glass behind him did not turn when the king turned; it stayed facing Ulyana, and its posture was different, upright where Aldric stooped, and its mouth was moving.

Sound came from nowhere and everywhere, thin as memory: *he can't hold me long. Boy. Boy, listen—*

"Look away from the glass," Aldric said sharply. "Not at it. Look at *me*."

But Ulyana had spent his life reading rooms, reading faces, reading the space between what people said and what they meant, and he could no more look away from that reflection than he could stop breathing. The reflection wore Aldric's face but none of Aldric's exhaustion. It was beautiful the way frost is beautiful, precise and dead, and its mouth shaped words with a fluency the living king lacked.

*Ask him what happened to the last Binder,* the reflection said, sweetly. *Ask him what your mother really died of. Ask him why the guild was paid in star-silver, boy, ask him what they smelted it from—*

"Enough." Aldric's voice cracked across the room like a whip, and for one instant — one — his shadow stood up straight on the floor, tall and wrong, crowned in darkness, before collapsing back into the shape of a tired man. The king swayed. Caught the table. His knuckles went white against the wood.

"I can't keep it quiet for long," he said, and now he was looking directly at Ulyana, and his eyes were wet, and they were *his*, only his, lucid and desperate and drowning. "When I'm tired, or angry, or afraid — it uses my face. You understand? Twenty-two years I have worn this crown and for twenty-two years it has worn me, and the only hours that are mine are the ones I steal back, and I am spending mine on you, so *listen*."

Ulyana listened.

"The wards on Thornfell were never walls," Aldric said. "Your people built them — the Binders, the Sterlings, the guild before the guild grew fat and forgot. They weren't built to keep enemies out. They were built to keep *me asleep*. To keep it asleep. A cradle of wards anchored in royal blood, and every generation, when the binding thinned, a Binder came and renewed it, and every renewal cost—" His throat worked. "Cost a life. Yours, if the pattern holds. Your mother knew. She found another way, or thought she did, and she ran, and she hid you, and she bought you twenty-five years with her silence."

"And the stars," Ulyana said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "It's eating them."

"To finish unbinding." Aldric nodded, slow, grim. "Starlight is what holds it. Has been since the first binding — the Binders wove the veil's edge out of fallen-star iron and living vow, and the Veil has spent centuries drinking the sky to thin the weave. When the last star goes out—" He gestured, and the gesture took in the shrouded mirrors downstairs, the starless dark beyond the windows, the whole suffocating weight of the night. "There is no weave left to hold. There is only me, and I am *so tired*, Sterling. I am so tired of fighting it in the dark where no one can see."

In the mirror behind him, the reflection laughed without sound, and its laughter bent the lamplight.

* * *

"They tell me you were caught with rebel contacts," Aldric said later. They sat at the cluttered table now, king and smuggler, and the king poured wine with his own hands — two cups, and Ulyana noted distantly that he checked the second cup's rim against the light out of pure habit, and that Aldric saw him do it and almost smiled. "Dain Ashvale's people. The undercity cell."

"I was looking for answers. They were offering some."

"Were they?" The king drank. "What did Ashvale tell you?"

"That the king hadn't been truly awake in months. That the court was rotten. That someone inside the palace dropped the wards deliberately." Ulyana held the older man's gaze. "Was it you?"

For a long moment Aldric said nothing. In the mirrors, a dozen reflected kings said nothing with him, and one of them — just one, in the clouded oval glass by the door — tilted its head, waiting, hungry.

"It was my hand on the mechanism," Aldric said finally. "Mine. Because the alternative was letting it take my daughter."

The word landed in Ulyana's chest like a dropped coin in a well.

"She's nine," the king went on, quieter. "The binding anchors in royal blood. It has always taken the vessel's life — the Binder's craft, the monarch's flesh. My father died renewing it. His father before him. When the wards began to fail this autumn, the rite called for a vessel of the bloodline, and the Veil—" his jaw tightened, "—it *chose*. It reached for her first. Children dream so loudly, Sterling. It hears them easiest." He set down his cup with exaggerated care, the care of a man who wanted very badly to throw it. "So I opened the wards myself, thinking — God help me — thinking if the cradle broke early, while the stars still burned, the Binders' remnants would come running and there would be time. Time to find another way. Time to find *you*."

"And instead the stars went out."

"And instead the stars went out." Aldric laughed, short and bitter and unbearably human. "I traded my kingdom's sky for my daughter's breath, and I would do it again, and it was still a mistake, and now the next new moon is eleven days off and there is no Binder left except the son of the woman who promised me there was another way."

The lamps guttered again. Longer, this time. When the light steadied, Aldric's face had changed — smoothed, composed, the exhaustion wiped away like condensation off glass, and for three heartbeats the man across the table was serene and ageless and smiling, and every instinct Ulyana owned screamed at him that this was not the same person.

Then the king blinked, and the hollowness rushed back in, and he gripped the table edge as if surfacing from deep water.

"What did it say?" Aldric rasped. "While I was under. What did it offer you?"

"Nothing yet."

"It will." The king's eyes were fierce now, wet-bright. "It knows what you are. It's known since you touched that ward-stone in the capital — yes, we know about that, it *screamed*, the whole palace heard it scream through the stones. You saw her, didn't you? Your mother. Sealing the first binding." He leaned forward. "You are the last Sterling, Ulyana. The last Binder's blood. Before the new moon, either you finish what she started, or the Veil finishes what it started, and there is no third road, however many rebels and guildmasters tell you otherwise."

"And if I refuse?"

Aldric held his gaze. Behind him, in every mirror in the room, the reflections leaned closer, a silent congregation.

"Then in eleven days I stop being a man who is sometimes a cage," the king said softly, "and become a cage that is sometimes a man. And it will wear my face into your guildhalls and your rebel cells and your mother's grave, and it will be very, very good at it. It has had three generations of practice loving people. It knows exactly where the seams are."

Somewhere below them, deep in the palace, a bell rang the hour — muffled, wrong-pitched, as if the sound itself were being swallowed. Aldric flinched at it like a blow.

"That's the night watch. I have perhaps minutes before it comes back up through me and takes the wheel." He fumbled at his collar, drew out a chain, and pressed something into Ulyana's palm — warm metal, a signet ring, heavy with old engraving. "My seal. The guards at the postern will let its bearer pass; I've arranged it. Go down into the city. Find what my mother-in-law's letters couldn't tell me and yours could. Find the other way she promised me, Sterling, because I have run out of ways to buy time, and my daughter dreams of a door in the dark, and every night the door is a little more open."

Ulyana closed his fist around the ring. It bit into his palm, real, solid, absurdly ordinary. "Why trust me? You don't know me."

"I know your mother." Aldric's smile was terrible and tender. "She also told me she needed no one's trust. She also lied about everything except the work." He stood, steadier than he had any right to be, and for a moment the ruined king and the smuggler faced each other across a table littered with the wreckage of a hidden war. "Eleven days. Don't be late."

As the guards moved to escort Ulyana out, he passed the great clouded oval mirror by the door, and he could not help himself — he glanced.

His own reflection looked back at him. Just his own. Tired, thin, fists clenched.

But behind his shoulder in the glass, where the king stood in the room, there was nothing at all. Only an empty grey robe, and above it, where a face should be, a slow spreading dark like ink dropped in water — and out of that dark, a voice that came from everywhere and nowhere, gentle as a lullaby:

*Run home, little anchor. We have so much to show you about your mother.*

The lamps went out.
