## 12. Terms of a Dying Man

They did not take him back to the dungeon.

That was the first thing Ulyana noticed — the guards turned the wrong way at the stair, away from the cold stone and dripping dark where he'd spent three nights listening to a king weep through the walls. Instead they climbed. Up and up, through corridors that grew quieter and warmer as they rose, past windows where the starless sky pressed against the glass like a face.

The second thing he noticed was that his hands would not stop shaking.

He had told himself it was fear. It was not entirely fear. Somewhere beneath the terror of what he'd seen in the mirror room — the king's reflection moving without him, pleading; the voice out of the empty robe calling him *little anchor* — there was something worse than fear. Recognition. The Veil knew his mother's name. Which meant everything Sera had whispered in the guildhall's shadows, everything he'd half-believed, was true, and the truth had teeth.

"Here." The guard captain stopped before a door of black oak banded with silver. Not palace work, Ulyana thought. Older. The silver was hammered in patterns like knotted rope, like warding-sigils worn smooth by centuries of hands. "His Majesty asks for you alone."

"Does His Majesty ask," Ulyana said, "or does the thing wearing him ask?"

The captain's jaw tightened. For a moment something flickered behind his eyes — exhaustion, or pity, or the particular blankness of a man who has stopped asking questions to keep himself sane. "You'll know the difference," he said, and opened the door, and pushed Ulyana gently through, and shut it behind him with a sound like a held breath released.

* * *

The observatory was nothing like he'd imagined.

He'd pictured gilded domes, brass orreries, the fat comfort of a court that studied stars it did not need. What he found instead was a room stripped nearly bare. Dust sheets over furniture like shrouded bodies. One great telescope aimed through an open dome at a sky with nothing left to see. And everywhere — everywhere — candles. Hundreds of them, thousands, jammed onto every surface, clustered on the floor in drifts of wax, their flames all bending slightly toward the center of the room as if drawn by a breath.

King Aldric stood among them, small in a plain grey robe, holding a candle of his own. Without the crown he looked like any tired man of fifty. Without the crown, Ulyana could almost believe the weeping he'd heard through the walls.

"You heard that," Aldric said. It wasn't a question. His voice was hoarse, scraped thin. "Nights when I'm myself. I weep. I don't remember doing it, but I know. The walls are thin and my grief is loud."

Ulyana said nothing. He catalogued exits instead — smuggler's habit. Two doors, one spiral stair, the dome overhead too high to reach. One dying king between him and all of them.

"You're wondering if this is me." Aldric raised the candle so its light caught his face: hollow cheeks, eyes red-rimmed but *present*, terribly present, a man looking out from inside his own burning house. "I can't prove it. That's the cruelty of it. The thing that wears me is clever, and it knows your mother's tricks, because it watched her work. So I'll tell you something only a Binder would know how to check."

He set the candle down on a low table and rolled up his sleeve. On the inside of his forearm, old and faded as a scar from childhood, was a mark Ulyana had seen once before — inked into the margin of a ledger page in Corvane's vault, beside a payment dated sixty years back. A circle crossed by seven lines, like a wheel with broken spokes.

"The binding seal," Aldric said. "Your mother put it here herself, the night I was crowned. She told me: *when you doubt what speaks with my voice, look for the wheel.*" He laughed, and it was a terrible sound, dry as paper. "She knew even then that she wouldn't live long enough to explain twice."

*When you doubt what speaks with my voice.* Ulyana's mouth went dry. "My mother worked for the crown."

"Your mother *saved* the crown. Or chained it. The words depend on the year." Aldric sank into a chair by the telescope, and up close Ulyana could see how much the sitting cost him, how each motion seemed negotiated with some other will. "Sit. Please. I've been rehearsing this speech for three nights, and I'm told I may not have many more."

"I'm told the same thing by everyone in this city," Ulyana said, but he sat, on a crate still half-covered in dust sheet, because his legs were no longer asking his opinion.

* * *

"Sixty years ago," Aldric began, "my grandfather wore the crown. Before him, his mother. If you go back far enough — further than any history book goes — you'll find the first one. The king who opened the veil."

The candle flames all leaned together, a field of wheat bowing to wind Ulyana couldn't feel.

"He wanted power over death. Kings usually do. He found a door in the world's skin — the veil, the space between what is lit and what is not — and he pried it open, and something came through. Something that eats light. Starlight, lamplight, the light of a life. It poured itself into him the way water pours into a cup, and it has been pouring itself into every monarch since. It doesn't kill us. Killing the vessel ends the tenancy. It *keeps* us. Wears us. Rules through our mouths and signs with our hands, and every few decades, when the vessel wears out—" Aldric touched the wheel-mark on his arm, "—it must be bound again. Packed down into the deep places of the king's blood and mind, asleep, dreaming, hungry."

"And the Binders do the packing."

"Binders of Sterling blood." Aldric looked at him steadily. "Yes. Your family, Ulyana. Your line made the first chain, and only your line can forge another. Every rebinding since has been done by a Sterling. And every rebinding—"

"Costs a life." He'd pieced it together already, in the dungeon, in the dark. But saying it aloud made it real in a way thinking never could.

"A Binder's life. The rite requires a willing vessel and a living anchor, and the anchor—" Aldric's voice caught. He mastered it. "The anchor steps into the edge of the veil to hold the thing fast from within. Nothing comes back from the veil's edge. Your grandmother went in for my father. Your mother's aunt, for my grandfather. And for me—"

"My mother." The words came out flat, strange, like coins from someone else's purse. "She faked her death. Everyone says she died. There's a grave."

"There's a grave because she needed the crown to believe it. If the Veil had known a Sterling still lived — a child Sterling, untrained, unprotected — it would have hunted him before he could ever become a threat." Aldric leaned forward, and the candlelight moved across his face like water. "It hid you in a smugglers' guild, Ulyana. Do you understand? The guild wasn't hiding stolen goods along those routes. It was hiding *you*. Corvane took coin from the crown for sixty years, and some of that coin bought your anonymity. Your whole life has been a warding-sigil drawn around a single boy."

The room tilted. Ulyana gripped the edge of the crate. All those years — the odd kindnesses, the routes only he was trusted with, the way Corvane had taught him the tunnels like a father teaching a son a trade that was also a shield. He had thought he'd been building a career. He had been being *kept*, like a blade wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for the day someone would need it sharp.

"That's why the wards fell," he said slowly. "The border wards. They weren't keeping enemies out."

"They were the outermost ring of my binding. Your mother wove them into the chain — the land itself holding the Veil asleep. When they shattered, it wasn't an attack. It was the Veil waking, reaching, testing the strength of its cage." Aldric gestured upward, toward the open dome and the dead sky beyond. "And now it's feeding. Come. You should see."

* * *

Aldric led him up the narrow stair inside the dome's ribbing, one hand on the rail, moving like a man climbing toward his own execution. At the top, the eyepiece of the great telescope waited, brass gone green with neglect, and beside it a second instrument Ulyana had no name for — a tube of black glass banded in silver sigils, older than anything else in the room.

"Look," Aldric said. "Not with the telescope. With this. Eyes are honest about distance and liars about everything else."

Ulyana bent to the black-glass eyepiece.

The sky, which from below looked merely empty, merely starless, was not empty.

It was *being eaten*.

Through the strange lens he saw them — shapes vast and slow drifting between the absent stars, or perhaps made of the absence itself, great soft-edged mouths of darkness drinking down the last points of light. Where a star had been, a faint grey scar remained, like a bruise fading. And threading among them, fine as spider silk, filaments of darkness ran downward, converging, funneling toward a single point on the horizon.

Toward Thornfell. Toward the fallen wards. Toward the hole in the world's skin.

"It drinks the stars first because they're far and defenseless," Aldric said quietly above him. "Then it drinks everything else. Lamplight. Firelight. The light in living things. By the next new moon there won't be enough light left in the sky to hold the binding's shape even if we had a Binder to renew it. After that—" he didn't finish. He didn't have to. Ulyana had walked the roads below the wards. He had felt the mountains watching. He had seen what the dark did to people who stayed in it too long.

He straightened. His hands, he noticed distantly, had finally gone still.

"How long," he said, "has it known about me?"

"Since the wards fell. It felt your blood in the world the way a man feels weather in an old wound." Aldric smiled, and it was the saddest thing Ulyana had ever seen on a living face. "It called you *little anchor*. Did it?"

Ulyana went cold. "You heard that?"

"I hear most of what it says, these days, as the binding thins. Like voices through a wall." The king turned to the window, to the starless dark pressing against the glass. "It wants you, Ulyana. Not dead — dead anchors are useless. It wants you *willing*. It will offer you anything. Your mother, alive. Your guild, restored. Belonging. It knows exactly what a lonely boy wants, because it has been reading kings' hearts for six hundred years, and kings are nothing but lonely boys with crowns."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then it kills you, if it can, and waits for the next Sterling, and there are no more Sterlings." Aldric faced him fully, and drew himself up, and for one moment Ulyana saw the ghost of what the man must have been before the wearing — a tall frame, a commander's spine, a king's plain courage. "Or you do what your mother meant you to do. You rebind it. Willingly. Knowing the price."

The candles below them all guttered at once, though there was no draft, and relit, though no hand had touched them.

"There's a third option," Ulyana said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "There has to be. My mother spent twenty years hiding, planning, leaving letters along her old routes — I've read some of them. She wasn't building a trap with only one door."

Something moved behind the king's eyes — hope, quick and painful, like a bird striking glass.

"She spoke of it," Aldric whispered. "Near the end. A better binding. She said the old rite was crude — a life for a cage — and that she'd found references to an anchor freely given that needn't be lost. She never finished it. She ran out of time." He stepped closer, and his hands, when they closed on Ulyana's shoulders, were light as ash. "But her notes, her workbench — if any of it survived, it would be where she worked. Beneath Thornfell. Beneath the first binding."

The deep roads. The vaults under the mountain where the guild's oldest maps simply stopped, marked with a sigil Ulyana had seen his whole life and never questioned: a circle crossed by seven lines.

The wheel. Her mark. Pointing home.

"The new moon is in nine days," Aldric said. "After that, the sky goes out for good, and the Veil walks free of me whether I consent or not. Nine days to get beneath Thornfell, find her work, and finish what three generations of Sterlings started." His grip tightened, and his voice broke at last, the king falling away and only the prisoner remaining, the man who wept through walls. "I am so tired, Ulyana. Sixty years I have fought to stay *me* inside my own skull, and I am so tired. Whatever you decide — run, refuse, let the world go dark — I want you to know that your mother gave everything so you could choose. Don't let anyone take the choosing from you. Not the rebels. Not the guild. Not me."

"Not even the thing wearing you," Ulyana said.

"No," Aldric agreed softly. "Least of all him."

And then the king's head bowed, and when it rose again the eyes were wrong — smooth and patient and amused, a landlord returning to inspect the property — and the voice that came out was warm as honey poured over a blade:

"Well. Now you know the terms, little anchor. Shall we discuss what *I'm* prepared to pay?"

Below them, every candle in the observatory went out at once — all except the one burning in the king's hand, which flared suddenly white, and cast two shadows from his single body: one shaped like a man, and one shaped like nothing that had ever lived.

Ulyana backed toward the stair, and the dark thing in the king's smile let him go, watching, unhurried, certain — the way a spider watches a fly walk willingly toward the web, carrying exactly what the spider has been starving for.

Nine days. His mother's workbench. A door that might not cost a life.

If it existed at all.
