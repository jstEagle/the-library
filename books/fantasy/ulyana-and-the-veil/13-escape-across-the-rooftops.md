## 13. Escape Across the Rooftops

The mirror room's door closed behind Ulyana with a sound like a held breath finally released, and he stood alone in the corridor of the east wing with a dead man's ring in his fist and a map folded against his heart.

The signet ring of House Aldric was heavier than it looked. Gold, obviously — kings did not wear tin — but the weight of it was not in the metal. It was in what it meant. When Aldric had pressed it into his palm an hour ago, his hand had been cold as a cellar wall, and his eyes had been the eyes of a man handing off a lit fuse.

*The vault beneath Thornfell,* he had said. *Where your mother bound it the first time. The ring will open what keys cannot. And Ulyana —* his voice had cracked, a king's voice cracking, which was somehow worse than anything the Veil had shown Ulyana that night — *when it realizes what you carry, it will not send soldiers. Soldiers are honest. It will send things that wear the faces of people you trust.*

Now, alone in the corridor lit by guttering oil lamps, Ulyana turned the ring over once, twice, and made himself stop. There would be time for trembling later. Trembling was a luxury, and he had never once been able to afford luxuries.

He had counted the guard rotations for three days from his cell, the way he'd counted everything his whole life — the rhythm of boot-heels, the creak of the third stair, the exact interval between the changing of the watch on the east stair. The palace thought itself a fortress. The palace was wrong. Every fortress was just a lock, and Ulyana Sterling had never met a lock that didn't want, somewhere deep in its oiled and patient heart, to be opened.

The east wing's servants' stair dropped him into the undercroft of the old chapel, where the crown's masons had bricked up a smuggler's tunnel two hundred years ago and then, in the arrogance of kings, forgotten it. Ulyana had not forgotten it. He had spent one profitable and deeply illegal winter moving crown wine through it when he was nineteen, and he had left the loose brick exactly where he'd found it, because a good smuggler never burns a road he might need to run down weeping someday.

The brick came away like a tooth. The dark beyond it smelled of dust and old wine and, faintly, of cold — the particular cold that had been creeping across the kingdom since the stars went out, a cold with intent behind it.

He went in.

* * *

The rooftops of the capital were a country unto themselves, and Ulyana was its native son.

He came up through a chimney flue in the dye-works on Tanner's Row, emerged gray with soot into a sky that had no stars in it, and stood for a moment letting the wind off the mountains scour him clean. Below, the city lay in its uneasy sleep — shuttered windows, watch-fires burning low because the crown had ordered fuel rationed, streets empty of everything except the patrols. Above, nothing. The absence of stars was a presence all its own now, a black ceiling pressing down, and Ulyana had begun to understand that it was not empty at all. It was *looking*.

He ran.

The rooftops of the capital were his mother tongue. He spoke them without thinking: the slate run between the chandler's and the tannery that sang under a boot but held; the gap over Cooper's Lane that looked impossible and was a stride and a half if you committed; the bell-tower of the Weavers' Chapel whose north face had handholds worn into the stone by three generations of men like him, men whose names were in no ledger because the guild's true ledgers were written in muscle memory and grave-dirt. He moved through his country fast and silent, and for a handful of minutes — the first in days — he felt something that was almost joy.

The ring was against his chest. The map was against the ring. And somewhere beneath the mountains of Thornfell, a vault waited with his mother's work in it, the beginning of the end of the thing that wore his king.

He was halfway across the tanner's long roof, crouched to spring the gap over the rendering-yard, when the hair on his arms stood up.

It was not sound. It was the *lack* of sound — the wind dying mid-breath, the distant watch-dog cutting off mid-bark, the whole city holding itself still the way a field goes still when the hawk's shadow crosses it. Ulyana froze in his crouch and let his eyes do the work, and that was when he saw them.

Three figures on the roof of the granary, two streets back. They had not been there when he'd passed the granary. They stood too still, in a way people did not stand, spaced too evenly, like notes on a staff. And even at this distance, even in the starless dark, Ulyana could see that they did not move to breathe.

*It will not send soldiers,* Aldric had said. *It will send things that wear the faces of people you trust.*

The nearest one turned its head. The motion was smooth as poured oil, too smooth, and in the light of the granary's watch-lamp Ulyana saw the face it wore.

Corvane's face.

His chest went tight and hot. *No.* He knew — he *knew* — the guildmaster was a day's ride south, behind sealed guildhall doors. This was not Corvane. This was the Veil reaching into Ulyana's own skull and pulling out the face that would hurt him most to strike at, the face of the man who had raised him, fed him, taught him the rooftops, lied to him for sixty years of ledgers. The thing wore the guildmaster's gray beard and the guildmaster's patient half-smile, and it raised one hand — Corvane's hand, with Corvane's crooked second finger from the break Ulyana had witnessed at fourteen — and beckoned.

Ulyana sprang the gap.

He hit the rendering-yard roof rolling, came up running, and did not look back, because looking back was a tax and he could not afford it. Behind him he heard — not footsteps. A sound like silk tearing, very slowly, and he understood with a lurch of nausea that the things were *crossing the gaps without jumping*.

* * *

The old aqueduct ran east out of the city on legs of mossy stone, and where it passed over the tanneries its maintenance walk was a smuggler's highway older than the guild itself. Ulyana dropped onto it from the tannery roof, rolled, and came up with his knife already out, because the tearing-silk sound was closer now and he needed to stop being prey.

The first of them dropped onto the aqueduct thirty feet behind him.

It was Sera's face.

That was the moment the exhilaration in his blood curdled into something colder and clearer. Not Corvane — Sera, the ward-keeper whose broken arm he had splinted with a smuggler's sling, who had bargained him across half a kingdom, whose stubborn, starved honesty had been the one thing in this whole rotten affair he had not yet caught in a lie. The thing wore her face and her braid and the scar on her jaw, and it walked toward him with her walk, and it said, in her voice, pitched to carry on the wind:

"Ulyana. Give it back."

His knife-hand did not shake. He was almost proud of that. "You're not her."

"No," the thing agreed, and the agreement in Sera's voice was the worst thing he had heard in his life. "But she is tired, Ulyana. She is so tired of running, and of you, and of a cause that eats its believers. I can show you where she sleeps. I can show you what she dreams." It took another step, and where its feet touched the moss, the moss grayed and died. "You are carrying something that belongs to the crown. To *me*. Bring it home, and I will give you back every face you've lost. Your mother first. She's just behind the veil, little smuggler. She's been waiting for you your whole life."

The words went into him like hooks. He felt them set. And that, more than anything, told him what he needed to know — because his mother, the real one, the woman he was only now meeting through ciphered letters and a dead queen's bedside, would never have waited. She had faked her own death and vanished into the dark to keep one child safe. She had not waited for anything in her life.

"You don't know her at all," Ulyana said. "You just know what she looks like."

And he turned and ran east along the aqueduct with the wind rising again at his back — the wind, he realized, was on *his* side now, the land itself leaning against the thing that starved it — and behind him the silk-tearing sound redoubled, and he did not look back, he did not look back, he looked *forward*, at the mountains.

* * *

Thornfell at night, starless, was a black jaw against the blacker sky.

He came off the aqueduct at the eastern wall where the old culvert drained the tannery yards — a smuggler's door he'd used a dozen times, unwatched, unloved, forgotten by every guard rotation in living memory. But the culvert's mouth had been bricked over, fresh mortar still pale in the dark, and the mason's mark stamped in the new brick was the crown's.

The Veil was sealing the city behind him. It had been sealing it all night, while it wore his dead and walked his rooftops. Every route out that he knew — and he knew forty — would be closing, one by one, in the order he would think of them, because it had been in his head, in Sera's face, in Corvane's beckoning hand. It was not hunting him. It was *herding* him.

Fine. He had grown up herding and been herded by the best. He cut west along the wall's inner face, moving in the dead ground where the watch-lamps didn't reach, and he made himself think not like a runner but like the thing hunting him: *if I were the Veil, where would I leave a door open?*

Nowhere. Unless it needed one open.

Unless it wanted him to run — because every hour he ran was an hour closer to Thornfell, and Thornfell was where the binding was, and the binding was the one place the Veil could end this forever, could catch the cure and the last Binder of Sterling blood in one hand and crush them both.

He stopped in the shadow of a buttress, breathing hard, and made himself face it. The map against his chest was a cure. It was also bait, and he was the fish, and the hook had been in his mouth since the night the stars went out. Maybe since the night his mother died. Maybe since the night he was born.

*Then don't run the route it expects.*

There was one way out of the capital that no one knew — not the guild, not the crown, not the rebels, not the Veil wearing his dead. His mother's way. The ciphered letters had given him more than words; they had given him a route, a smuggler's road marked in her hand along the margins of the third letter, a road she had kept for herself, a road that did not exist on any map because she had built it out of nothing but nerve: the bell-foundry on Candlemaker's Row, the old casting pit, and the fissure beneath it that ran down into the mountain's root like a vein.

The foundry had been cold for sixty years. Cold since the year his mother died.

Ulyana smiled in the dark, and it was not a nice smile. The Veil could wear any face it liked and seal any door it liked. It could not seal a door that existed only in a dead woman's cipher, in the margins of a letter written to a son she'd never met, in a hand he was beginning to love.

He ran west.

* * *

The casting pit of the old bell-foundry was a stone throat thirty feet deep, and Ulyana went down it the way his mother's marginal map told him: handhold, ledge, handhold, the iron rings she'd had set into the wall a lifetime ago still sound under his weight, each one kissed with rust but holding. The Veil's hunters — he heard them now, three streets over, moving with that silk-tearing patience, sealing the east and the south — would find the foundry eventually. But eventually was a currency, and he intended to spend every coin of it.

At the pit's bottom, behind a false wall of slag brick, the fissure breathed cold air up at him, and the cold smelled of stone and deep water and, very faintly, of something older — the same smell as the vault-smell in his visions, the smell of the binding.

He lit his stub of candle and looked back once, up the throat of the pit, at the coin of starless sky.

"Keep her face off your mouth," he told the dark, in Sera's direction, in the Veil's direction, in whatever direction it listened from. "Or I'll make you eat the binding."

The dark did not answer. But far above, at the pit's rim, a figure appeared — just a silhouette against the lightless sky, wearing a braid and a broken arm held in a smuggler's sling, and it stood there a long moment, watching him, and did not speak in Sera's voice or any voice at all.

Then it turned and was gone, and Ulyana told himself the shiver down his spine was the cold.

He turned into the fissure and began to descend, with the ring against his heart and the map against the ring and the mountains closing over him like a hand — carrying, he knew now, both the cure and the bait, and no way left to tell them apart except all the way to the end.

Behind him, far above, the last of the city's watch-fires went out, one by one, like stars.

And somewhere beneath Thornfell, in a vault that had waited sixty years, something that was not quite a door felt the ring of House Aldric coming home — and began, very softly, to unlock itself.
