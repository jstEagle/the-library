## 14. Sera at the Crossroads

The crossroads came to him before he found it.

Ulyana had been walking the old drover's road for the better part of a day — the one that ran beneath the ward-line like a vein beneath skin, walled in by hawthorn gone black with cold and a sky that had forgotten how to hold light. The stars had not come back. He had stopped checking after the second night, because each time he looked up and found nothing there, something in his chest performed a small, quiet amputation. Better not to look. Better to keep his eyes on the road, on the map against the ring against his heart, on the next milestone and the one after that.

But the crossroads announced itself anyway. He smelled it first: woodsmoke, and under the woodsmoke something sweeter and more wrong — burnt sugar, maybe, or the ghost of a festival held too long ago. Then he heard the wind move through the hawthorn in a way wind should not move through hawthorn, all at once, from every direction, as if the trees were turning their heads.

And then he saw her.

Sera stood at the meeting of three roads, where a leaning waystone marked the old boundary between the crown's land and no one's. She was dressed for travel, hood back, sword loose in its scabbard, and she did not look surprised to see him, which meant she had been waiting, which meant she had known which road he would take, which meant —

"You always take the drover's road," she said, before he could speak. "Corvane taught you. You told me once. *The high roads are for people who trust the sky.*"

"I told you that in a cellar in the capital," Ulyana said. "While you were bleeding."

"While I was being stubborn," she corrected. "The bleeding was incidental." But her hand wasn't steady on her sword-belt, and her eyes went past him, up the road behind him, twice, three times, the way a person checks a door they've already locked. "You shouldn't have come this way alone."

"Alone is the only way I come anymore. It seems to be contagious."

He meant it lightly. It didn't land lightly. Her jaw tightened, and for a moment they simply looked at each other across ten paces of frozen mud, two people who had shared a fire and a wound and a secret, now standing on opposite sides of something neither of them wanted to name.

"You know why I'm here," Sera said.

"I can guess. Dain sent you."

"Dain doesn't send me anywhere. Dain has never once in his life sent me anywhere." She laughed, but it came out cracked down the middle. "That's rather the problem, isn't it? He assumes. He assumes I'll follow because following is what I do, because I followed him out of the ash of my village when I was fourteen and I've never stopped, and he builds his plans on top of that assumption the way you'd build a house on a foundation you never bother to inspect." She pulled her hood back further, and in the grey light Ulyana saw how much thinner her face had grown since the undercity, how the shadows under her eyes had gone from bruise-colored to something closer to iron. "You're here because the king gave you a ring and a map and a dying man's plea. And Dain is coming to Thornfell with forty rebels and a headsman's axe, because killing Aldric mid-rite is easier than understanding what the rite is for."

"You knew," Ulyana said. Not a question.

"I suspected. There's a difference, and I clung to it like a drowning woman clings to driftwood." She stepped closer, boots crunching on rime. "I told myself Dain wanted leverage. A hostage king. A negotiated end. I told myself the plan to reach the binding vault before you was about *protecting* the rite." Her voice dropped. "Then I read the supply manifests. Forty men don't need that many blades, Ulyana. They need them for after. For whoever comes out of that vault wearing the crown next."

*Whoever comes out of that vault.* The words moved through him like cold water finding a crack. Because there would be someone. That was the whole architecture of the thing — the Veil wore kings like masks, generation after generation, and if Aldric died unbound, the parasite would simply find the next face, the next throat, the next hungry ambition willing to be worn. Dain's rebellion wouldn't kill the Veil. It would give it a fresher mount.

"And you?" he asked quietly. "What do you need, standing at this crossroads?"

"That's just it." She spread her hands, and he saw they were shaking — Sera, who had held a cauterizing iron to her own arm without flinching, whose hands never shook. "I don't know. I walked out of the undercity three days ago and I still don't know, and every mile I've told myself I'm coming to stop you, and every mile the story gets thinner, and now you're standing here looking at me like I'm the enemy and I *hate* it, because you're the only person in this whole starless country who isn't asking me to be something I'm not."

* * *

They made camp in the lee of the waystone, because the cold had teeth now and arguing was better done beside a fire. Ulyana built it small and shielded, smuggler-fashion, the flames hidden behind a screen of stones so the light showed only upward, a coin of orange glow against a black sky. Neither of them looked at the sky.

Sera talked first. She talked the way people talk when they've been holding a breath for days.

"When the wards fell," she said, "I was on the wall. Third watch, north stretch, the oldest section — the stones that predate the palace, predate everything. We used to joke they were older than sin." She turned her hands toward the hidden fire. "When they shattered, it wasn't like breaking. It was like *waking*. Every ward-keeper felt it go through us — the anchor-magic letting go all at once, sixty years of held breath released into our bones. Three of the women on my watch collapsed. One didn't get up." A pause. "And in that moment, before the pain, before the fear — I felt what the wards had actually been holding. Not armies. Not raiders. Something *asleep*. Something that had been dreaming behind the border for my entire life, and everyone else's, and their mothers'."

"The king's curse," Ulyana said. "Or the thing wearing the king."

"The thing wearing the king," she agreed. "And here's what I couldn't tell Dain, because Dain doesn't want causes, he wants culprits. The wards didn't fail. They were *released*. From inside. Someone with royal blood opened their own cage." She looked at him. "Aldric."

"Aldric, or the thing using his hands. I've seen it slip, Sera. In the mirror room. His reflection moves without him. Pleads without him." Ulyana fed a twig to the fire. "He's lucid in pieces. Minutes at a time. Enough to beg me to finish what my mother started. Not enough to save himself."

"Your mother." Sera said it carefully, the way you handle a blade by the flat. "The Binder."

"The last Binder. Who faked her death and hid me in a smuggling guild so the crown would never find Sterling blood again." He heard his own voice flatten and didn't correct it. "She bound a parasite into a newborn prince and then spent her whole life running from what she'd done. And now the bill's come due, and it's addressed to me."

For a while there was only the fire's small sound, and the wind worrying the hawthorn, and somewhere far off — or perhaps not far off at all; distance had become an unreliable instrument since the stars died — a sound like slow breathing through stone.

"Dain will be at Thornfell in four days," Sera said at last. "Five, if the weather turns. He has the undercity maps, half the cell's loyalty, and a story so clean it polishes itself: the mad king dies, the rebellion rises, the people are free." Her mouth twisted. "It's even mostly true. That's what makes it dangerous. Lies built entirely of lies collapse under their own weight. Lies built of truth stand up and walk around and recruit."

"And you're telling me this because—"

"Because I watched you copy Corvane's ledger by candlelight and say nothing. Because you carried me bleeding through three miles of guild tunnels and asked nothing. Because—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Because when Dain laid out the plan in the map room, everyone nodded, and I nodded, and the whole time some part of me was watching your face, and your face was the only honest thing in that room. And I have been loyal to Dain Ashvale for eleven years, Ulyana, and I am telling you that I would rather betray him than spend one more night not knowing whether the nod I gave was mine."

The words hung in the smoke. Ulyana found, to his surprise, that his eyes were hot, and he looked hard at the fire until they weren't.

"They'll call it betrayal," he said. "Dain. The cell. Everyone we leave behind. There's no version of this where we're the heroes of anyone's story but our own."

"Good," Sera said. "I've been a hero of other people's stories since I was fourteen. It's overrated. The pay is terrible and the ending is always someone else's idea." She reached across the fire, took his wrist — the same grip, he realized, with which she'd once dragged herself upright out of a fever, using him as a handhold, trusting him to bear the weight. "Tell me the plan. All of it. The ring, the map, the vault, the unfinished rite. If I walk away from the only family I've had, I walk away knowing exactly what I'm walking toward."

So he told her. The observatory where the stars were being devoured, the king weeping through dungeon walls, the mirror room and the reflection that begged. The rebinding that demanded a life each time it was done — his mother's predecessors, spent like candles. And then the deep vault beneath Thornfell, the final workbench, the notes in his mother's ciphered hand describing a second binding, a merciful one, one that required no death — only a living anchor, freely given, bound forever at the veil's edge. Neither alive nor dead. Neither here nor there.

Sera listened without interrupting, which was its own kind of discipline. When he finished, she was silent for a long moment.

"The anchor," she said. "You said the rite needs one. Freely given."

"It needs one."

"And you're planning to be it."

"I'm planning to figure that out when I get there," Ulyana said, which was true, and both of them knew it was the kind of true that meant *yes*.

Sera withdrew her hand. She looked into the fire for a long time, and when she spoke again her voice had changed — stripped of argument, stripped of persuasion, down to bare bedrock.

"My village burned when I was thirteen. Plague-cull, the magistrates called it, though there was no plague. I was the only one who got out, because a ward-keeper named Maren broke protocol and carried me through a sealed gate. She died for it. Court-martialed, executed, name struck from the rolls." She looked up. "Everything I've done since — the wall, the watch, Dain's rebellion — I did it trying to be worth what she spent. And I finally understand that I can't be. No one can. You can only spend yourself forward, on something worthy, and hope the arithmetic comes out kind." She stood, and drew her sword an inch from its scabbard, and let it slide home — the sound small and final, a door closing on one life and opening on another. "So. I'm spending myself forward. On the rite. On the truth. On whatever's waiting under Thornfell. And if the arithmetic says the anchor has to be someone, we'll argue about it there, with knives if necessary."

"Sera—"

"Don't." But she smiled, tired and real. "Save it. You'll need your speeches for the thing wearing the crown."

* * *

They walked through the rest of the night and into a dawn that never quite committed to arriving — a grey seeping rather than a rising, the sun a pale stain behind cloud like a bruise deciding whether to bloom. The land changed as they went. The hawthorn gave way to pine, the pine to bare granite shoulders, and the air acquired the mineral smell of deep places, of stone that remembered being underground and resented the sky.

It was Sera who noticed it first.

"Ulyana." She had stopped on the trail, head cocked. "How long since we've heard birds?"

He listened. Wind. Their own breathing. The creak of frost in the rock. Nothing else.

"There were crows yesterday," he said slowly. "On the drover's road. Following us at a distance."

"Crows follow travelers. These weren't following." She pointed back down the trail, to a fence post they'd passed an hour ago, its top whitened with rime. Perched on it, motionless, sat a crow. As they watched, another lifted from a boulder farther off — and it did not fly away. It flew *closer*, settling on a dead branch thirty yards behind them, head tilted, one eye catching the grey light like wet obsidian.

"They're counting us," Sera murmured.

"They're *reporting* us." Ulyana's hand found the ring through his shirt. It was warm. It had been warm for hours, and he had told himself it was his own body heat, and he stopped telling himself that now. "The Veil knows I'm coming. It's known since the palace. Aldric said it would send hunters once I ran — but hunters are people, Sera. People can be outrun, outfought, outbribed." He watched the crow tilt its head, watching them back with an attention no bird should own. "This isn't hunting. This is *herding*. Everything since the crossroads — the open trail, the easy going — we haven't been escaping toward Thornfell. We've been escorted."

"Then we go off-trail."

"Off-trail is three days of cliff and ice instead of one day of road, with the last stars dying overhead and the vault unlocking whether we arrive or not." He weighed it, the smuggler's arithmetic running automatically — risk, time, terrain — and found, for the first time in his life, that the numbers refused to resolve. Because the equation had changed. He was no longer choosing between routes. He was choosing between the trap he could see and the trap he couldn't.

Above them, the clouds tore briefly apart — and in the gap, where stars should have wheeled, there was nothing. Not darkness. Darkness was something. This was absence, a hole in the world shaped like a sky, and Ulyana felt it look down at him with the same patient attention as the crow.

Then the clouds closed, and the moment passed, and the cold rushed back in like water filling a footprint.

"We go on," Sera said. Not a question either. She loosened her sword in its scabbard, rolled her shoulders, and set her face toward the mountains. "Together, then. Since we're apparently doing this."

"Together," Ulyana agreed.

And as they climbed, the crows rose from their perches one by one and drifted after them — not pursuing, not fleeing, simply accompanying, a ragged black escort against the starless sky, growing fewer as the trail steepened, until only one remained, gliding above the pass ahead of them.

Toward Thornfell. Toward the vault. Toward the door that was not quite a door.

Which, when they crested the ridge at dusk and saw the mountain's roots laid bare before them, was already open — and from its depths came a light that had no business existing in a world without stars: soft, silver, and unmistakably, terribly familiar.

The light of starlight, burning underground.

His mother's light.

"She's been down there," Ulyana whispered, "all along."

Beside him, Sera's hand closed on his arm — and did not let go.
