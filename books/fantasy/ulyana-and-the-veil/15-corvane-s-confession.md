## 15. Corvane's Confession

The rain had followed them down from the high passes, and by the time the guildhall's chimneys rose out of the murk like black fingers, Ulyana's boots were full of water and his thoughts were full of ghosts.

Thornfell looked wrong at night without stars. He'd grown up under this sky, learned to read it the way other children learned letters — the Wagon, the Sisters, the pale scatter of the Veilway that smugglers swore by when compasses lied. Now there was only a low, starless ceiling pressing down on the town like a lid on a pot. The mountains had no edges. The river had no shine. Even the lamplight in the windows seemed thinner, as if the dark were drinking it.

Sera moved beside him with her hood up and her hand never far from her knife. She hadn't spoken in an hour.

"You're sure they'll be here," she said finally. It wasn't really a question.

"Corvane doesn't chase. He waits." Ulyana turned off the cart road onto a track he could have walked blindfolded — had walked blindfolded once, on a dare from Rennick, back when the worst thing in the world was a guildmaster's disappointment. "He'll have picked the ground. Somewhere with one way out. He likes to make you feel the walls before he talks."

"And you're walking into that anyway."

"He raised me, Sera." The words came out flatter than he meant them to. "Whatever he is now, whatever he's been all along — I need to hear him say it."

The track bent around a sheepfold and climbed toward the old tollhouse above the mill race. Ulyana saw the lantern before he saw the men: a single yellow light burning in the tollhouse window, steady as a held breath. Around it, shapes stood in the wet grass where shapes shouldn't have been — six of them, maybe seven, spaced with the patient geometry of people who had done this many times. Guild hunters. He knew two of the silhouettes. One of them was Rennick.

His chest did something complicated and painful.

"They're not even hiding," Sera murmured.

"They don't need to." He stopped at the edge of the lantern light, hands loose at his sides, and raised his voice just enough to carry over the mill race. "It's me you want, Corvane. Let her walk."

For a long moment there was nothing but water sound. Then the tollhouse door opened, spilling warmth and lamplight across the mud, and Guildmaster Corvane stepped out into the rain without a hat, without a cloak, without any of the armor of office he wore like a second skin in the hall. In the light from the doorway he looked older than Ulyana had ever let himself see — older than sixty years of ledgers should have made anyone. His grey beard was soaked flat against his jaw. His eyes found Ulyana in the dark the way they always had: first, immediately, before anyone else's.

"You brought a rebel into my town," Corvane said. Not angry. Tired. That was worse.

"I brought the truth into your town. It was already here. You just kept it locked in a cellar."

Something flickered across the old man's face — grief, or its well-practiced counterfeit. "Inside," he said. "Both of you. The rain's long enough tonight without standing in it like fools."

* * *

The tollhouse smelled of tallow and old woodsmoke. Corvane poured three cups of small beer from a jug some hunter had thoughtfully left, and set them on the table between them like a man laying out cards for a game everyone already knew the end of. Sera took hers but didn't drink. She positioned herself by the door, where she could see both the old man and the window, and Ulyana loved her a little for it.

"You copied my ledger," Corvane said.

"Yes."

"Sixty years of crown payments. Routes that ran beneath the wards. Hush money older than me." He sat down heavily, and the chair groaned under him. "You were supposed to burn that page and forget it. I gave you the choice myself. I was gentler than the charter allows, if you want the truth of it."

"The charter." Ulyana laughed, and hated the sound. "What is the charter, Corvane? Really? Because I've been reading things lately. Old things. Things written in my mother's hand. And they say the guild wasn't founded to move cargo at all."

The rain filled the silence. Corvane looked into his cup for a long time.

"No," he said at last. "It wasn't."

Ulyana had braced for denial. For bluster, for threat, for the flat voice that ordered routes sealed and boys cast out. He had not braced for the simple surrender of it, and it went through him like a knife through wet canvas.

"Tell me."

Corvane set the cup down. When he spoke, it was in the voice he used for telling stories to the young ones in the hall on winter nights — except there was no warmth in it now, only the shape of warmth, worn smooth by decades of disuse.

"The guild was founded by Binders. Do you know what that word means yet, boy? Truly means?" He didn't wait for an answer. "There was a king, four generations back, who made a bargain with something that lives behind the sky. Something that eats light and wears men. The Binders caught it, chained it inside the royal bloodline, and built the wards to keep it asleep. And then they asked themselves the question every jailer asks: who watches the watchers? Who keeps the secret when we're dust?"

"So they built us."

"They built *us.*" Corvane's mouth twisted. "Smugglers' roads under the wards, so the court could move without waking what slept. Ciphers in trade ledgers. A sigil here and there — your mother's sigil, boy, the Sterling knot — carved into vault doors from here to the capital. We were wardens dressed as thieves. Every route we ever ran, every toll we ever paid, was a stitch in a seam. And then—" he spread his hands, and they shook, Ulyana noticed, they actually shook— "and then the seams held so long that everyone forgot there was anything underneath. We got fat. We got comfortable. We started believing our own cover story. Generations of wardens who thought they were merchants."

"And when the wards fell—"

"When the wards fell, I sealed the routes and forbade the capital because I was afraid." Corvane said it plainly, without excuse. "Not for the secret. For the guild. For the hall, and the ledgers, and the boys sleeping in the loft. I have spent thirty years keeping a prison quiet, and I discovered, the night the stars went out, that I no longer believed in the prisoner. Only the walls. That's what fear does after enough years, Ulyana. It doesn't make you careful. It makes you *small.*"

Sera shifted by the door. "You knew what the king was," she said. "You knew, and you said nothing while it ate three generations of them alive."

"I knew what the *Binders'* records said." Corvane's eyes came up, hard and old. "I knew a thing needed feeding and a family needed bleeding, and I knew that the last woman who tried to do more than keep the ledger dead was Mara Sterling. And I know what happened to her." He turned back to Ulyana, and his voice dropped. "Or I know the story we told. Faked her death, did she? Ran, and hid, and left her son in my hall like a parcel?"

"She left me somewhere she thought was safe."

"Aye. Well." The old man looked away. "She trusted the wrong wall. We all did."

Ulyana sat with it. Sixty years of payments. Thirty years of this man's voice teaching him knots and ciphers and which guards took bribes and which took offense — every lesson suddenly double-exposed over another life, a hidden one, a purpose Ulyana had been carrying in his bones since before he could walk without knowing it. He had wanted Corvane to be a traitor. Traitors were simple. This — this tired, frightened, faithful coward — was so much harder to hate.

"Why tell me now?" Ulyana asked quietly. "Why not put me in a cellar next to my mother's vault and lose the key?"

"Because I watched you copy that ledger by candlelight, boy, and I saw her hands moving." Corvane's voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "I've spent thirty years telling myself the guild could stay out of it. Stay small. Stay safe. And then the stars went out, and I sat in my hall with all my sealed routes and all my careful ledgers, and I understood that staying small is just dying slowly with better bookkeeping." He pushed back his chair and stood, and straightened, and for one moment Ulyana saw the shadow of whatever he had been at twenty — tall in a doorway, rain running off him, unafraid. "You're going to the binding site. Under Thornfell. That's where she worked; that's where it has to end. Don't insult us both by denying it."

"I won't."

"Then go." Corvane reached into his coat and drew out a heavy iron key, old and black with age, and laid it on the table between the beer cups. "Vault gate, deep roads, north entrance. The hunters will stand aside. Rennick—" his voice caught again, steadied— "Rennick will see you past the mill. After that, I never gave the order and you never asked."

Ulyana stared at the key. "Just like that."

"Just like that." A ghost of the old dry humor moved through the ruin of his face. "Thirty years too late and one lifetime short. Go on, boy. Before I come to my senses, or worse, before I start feeling noble about it."

Sera was watching Ulyana. He realized distantly that he was crying, or close to it, and that he didn't care. He crossed the room and did the thing he had never once done in nineteen years in the guildhall: he embraced the Guildmaster of Thornfell, awkward and brief, smelling rain and tallow and the pipe smoke that had stained every lesson of his childhood.

"You were a good father," Ulyana said into his shoulder. "A bad warden. But a good father."

Corvane held him a moment longer than was comfortable for either of them, then pushed him back to arm's length with rough hands. "Get out of my tollhouse," he said gruffly, "before I remember I'm supposed to be angry."

They went out into the rain. Behind them, the hunters melted from the grass without a word, and Rennick's silhouette lifted two fingers — the old road-sign, *clear path, gods speed* — before turning away.

* * *

They were half a mile down the north road, the tollhouse lamp still a smudge of gold behind them, when Sera stopped walking.

"Ulyana."

He heard it in her voice before he heard it in the night: a wrongness, a pressure, the way the air used to feel before a ward-storm back when there were wards. He turned.

Behind them, where the tollhouse stood, the darkness was *moving.*

Not wind. Not cloud-shadow. The starless night itself seemed to lean toward that little island of lamplight, folding inward like water finding a drain, and the rain stopped falling in a perfect circle a hundred yards wide — hung suspended, glittering frozen in the airless hush, each drop lit from below by a light that was not light.

"No," Ulyana breathed. "No, no—"

He was running before he decided to run, Sera's boots hammering behind him, the road dissolving into mud beneath them. The circle of stopped rain contracted. The lamplight guttered, stretched, went thin as wire.

And through the frozen curtain of rain, Ulyana saw them coming down the slope — figures walking without hurry, without footprints, wearing faces he knew. Hunters. Half the court, Aldric had said. They moved like puppets whose strings had been cut and rehung from somewhere higher, and their eyes, where the strange light touched them, were the color of drowned stars.

The tollhouse door burst open. Corvane stood in it with a hunting crossbow — the old one, the one from above the mantle in the hall, the one he'd taught Ulyana to shoot with — and fired into the dark, once, twice, bolts vanishing into the folded blackness without sound.

"RUN, BOY!" the old man roared, his voice cracking across the valley like the last honest thing in the world. "DON'T YOU DARE LOOK BACK!"

Ulyana looked back. Of course he looked back.

He saw the darkness take the doorway. Saw the crossbow clatter on stone. Saw, for one impossible instant, the old man standing unbowed in the light of his own lamp — small, and afraid, and no longer either of those things — before the night closed over the tollhouse like a mouth, and the lamp went out, and the rain came down all at once everywhere at once, cold and ordinary and merciless.

Sera caught him as his legs gave, dragged him bodily into the treeline, shoved him down behind a fallen trunk while the puppet-things passed on the road below, heads turning in slow unison, listening for heartbeats.

Ulyana pressed his fist against his own mouth and made no sound at all. Grief is a smuggler's trick too, he thought wildly. You seal it in a strongbox and you carry it, and you don't open it until the job is done. He had a vault key in his pocket and a dead man's blessing in his ears and a mountain ahead of him with a door at the bottom of it.

When the last of the hunters had gone by, Sera pulled him up. Her face was pale and streaked with rain that might have been other things.

"He bought us the road," she whispered.

"He bought us everything." Ulyana stood, and wiped his face with a rough sleeve, and looked up at the starless sky that had taken the closest thing to a father he'd ever had. His voice, when it came, was low and level and colder than the rain. "So we're going to spend it. Every mile. Come on — the north gate's two hours' walk, and I intend to be through it before whatever that was finishes counting its change."

Above them, far up the black flank of Thornfell, lightning flickered inside the mountain — silent, sourceless, pulsing like a heartbeat buried in stone.

Like something ancient, turning over in its sleep.

Or waking.
