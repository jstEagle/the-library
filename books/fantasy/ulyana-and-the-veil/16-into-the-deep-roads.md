## 16. Into the Deep Roads

The entrance to the deep roads was a dead smuggler's lie.

Ulyana had learned it from Corvane himself, years ago, in the guildmaster's cups — one of those half-drunk confessions old men make when they believe they'll die before anyone can use the knowledge. *Beneath the tannery at Mill Row, there's a bricked arch. It's nothing. Never mind it.* Which was how Ulyana knew it was everything. Corvane never wasted breath on true nothings.

Now he stood before that arch with Sera at his shoulder and the last star in the sky burning overhead like a nail driven through black cloth. The tannery had been abandoned for a generation; its reek of lime and rot had soaked into the stones so deeply that even the cold couldn't scour it out. The brickwork across the arch was newer than the arch itself, mortared with a haste that told its own story. Someone had sealed this place in fear.

"Your mother's sigil was on the vault door," Sera said quietly. "In the guildhall. And now a sealed arch under Thornfell. She got around."

"She got around," Ulyana agreed. His voice came out rougher than he intended. Three days on the road, hunted, sleepless, carrying a dead king's signet ring against his sternum like a second heart. "She got around, and she left me a trail of locked doors to follow. I'm starting to take it personally."

He set his crowbar into the mortar seam and leaned his weight into it. The first brick came free with a sound like a tooth leaving a jaw.

* * *

Beyond the arch, the stairs went down farther than stairs had any right to go.

They were cut from the mountain's living bone, each step worn concave by feet so ancient that whatever nation had made them had no name left in any book Ulyana had ever stolen or read. The walls sweated. Not water — something thicker, slower, a mineral weep that caught what little light their lanterns gave and threw it back amber and wrong, as though the stone itself were remembering fire.

Sera counted under her breath as they descended. Ward-keepers counted; Ulyana had learned that about her on the road below the wards. Two hundred steps, three hundred, four hundred. At five hundred her counting stopped, and she said instead: "Do you feel that?"

He did. It had been building for a hundred steps without him naming it — a pressure behind the eyes, a fullness in the ears, like standing at the bottom of a well of silence. The mountain wasn't quiet down here. The mountain was *listening*.

"The binding site," he said. "We're close enough that the old magic still has teeth."

"Close enough that it knows we're here." Sera's hand found the ward-stone at her belt — the splinter of the border wards she'd carried since the night the stars went out, since she'd bled on Ulyana's shoulder and whispered *the king did this* into the dark. The stone had been dull for days. Now it glimmered faintly, like a coal deciding whether to live. "It's not hostile. It's... waiting. Like a house where someone left a lamp burning for company."

Ulyana thought of his mother's letters, ciphered along her old routes, each one peeling back another layer of the woman he'd mourned without ever having known. *The last Binder*, she had called herself in the final letter. *If you are reading this, my love, then the veil is thinning, and you are the only thread of Sterling blood left to hold the needle.*

He hadn't let himself think too hard yet about what holding the needle might cost. There would be time for that at the bottom of the stairs. Or there wouldn't, and it wouldn't matter.

The stairway ended at a landing where the passage split three ways. Two of the branches were choked with rubble — recent rubble, Ulyana saw, running his lantern along the collapse. The stone was sharp-edged, dust still sifting from the cracks. The mountain had closed its own veins.

"It's trying to seal itself," Sera said. "Or someone is collapsing it ahead of us."

"The Veil knows Aldric gave me the map." Ulyana touched the signet ring through his shirt. "It's had three days to send hunters. It's had sixty years to learn these roads." He turned to the third branch — narrow, low-ceilinged, breathing a current of cold air that smelled of iron and old candle smoke. "But my mother used this one. Look."

Carved small into the lintel, so slight it might have been a mason's idle scratch, was a mark he'd known all his life without knowing it: a circle crossed by a single vertical line, broken at the crossing. His mother's sigil. The same mark that had been burned into the handle of the hairbrush she'd left behind. The same mark on the guildhall vault.

"She marked her way home," Sera said softly.

"No." Ulyana ran his thumb over the groove. "She marked her way *forward*. Home was never where she was going."

* * *

The passage narrowed until they walked it sideways, shoulders scraping stone, lanterns held high to keep the flames from guttering in the confined air. Twice the floor dropped away into crevasses they bridged with planks Ulyana had hauled from the tannery, and once they waded a stretch of icy water knee-deep that neither of them commented on, because commenting would have meant admitting how much colder it was than any water had a right to be this far from the surface sky.

Above them — far above, beyond a mile of rock — the last star was dying. Ulyana could feel it the way you feel a headache coming: not yet pain, but the certainty of it. Each night on the road the sky had lost another light, and each loss had pressed down on the world like a thumb on a wound. The refugees in the tunnel camps had stopped lighting fires. They said the dark drank them now. They said the king walked the walls at night, talking to no one, and that when he passed, the shadows leaned toward him like wheat toward wind.

And somewhere behind them, gaining or waiting or simply patient, the Veil's hunters moved through the upper roads wearing borrowed faces.

"We should rest," Sera said, when the passage finally opened into a chamber wide enough to stand upright in. "You've been awake—"

"I know how long I've been awake."

"You've been counting too, then." Her mouth curved, tired and wry. In the lantern light she looked hollowed, cheekbones sharp, the healing scar along her hairline silver-pale. But her eyes were steady. They had been steady since the night the stars went out, and Ulyana had come to understand that this steadiness was not calm. It was a decision she made every hour, the way he decided every hour to keep walking.

He sat down against the wall anyway, because his body overruled him, and she settled beside him close enough that their shoulders touched. The contact was practical — shared warmth in the mountain's cold — and it was not practical at all, and neither of them said so.

"The letters," Sera said after a while. "The last one. You read it to me on the road but you skipped the middle part."

"I didn't skip it."

"You skipped it."

Ulyana watched the lantern flame bend and straighten. "She wrote that she was sorry. That she'd spent her whole life choosing between keeping me safe and telling me the truth, and that she'd chosen wrong, and that if I was reading her words then she hoped I'd forgive her for making me finish her work." He paused. "She wrote that the binding doesn't have to cost a life. That she'd found another way. That it was unfinished."

"And you don't believe her."

"I believe she believed it." He turned the signet ring around his finger. Aldric's face swam in the wax-dark of the seal — a tired man's face, a prisoner's face, the face of a king who had wept through dungeon walls and begged a stranger to finish what his mother started. "Every other binding cost a life. A Binder of Sterling blood and a willing vessel. My mother faked her death rather than pay it. Why would the next one be different?"

"Because she was the last Binder," Sera said, "and she had a lifetime to look for a better answer, and she was smarter than everyone who came before her." She said it simply, without flattery, the way she stated facts about wards. "You keep waiting for the trap in the hope. Maybe there isn't one. Maybe she just loved you enough to leave you a door instead of a grave."

Ulyana didn't answer. But he found, in the listening dark of the mountain, that the thought had weight — that it sat in his chest beside the dread and refused to be displaced.

They slept in shifts. When Ulyana woke from his, Sera was sitting watch with the ward-stone cupped in both hands, and its light had strengthened, and she was smiling at it the way people smile at things they're afraid to trust.

"It's brighter," she said. "The closer we get, the more it remembers what it was."

"Then let's go remind it."

* * *

The collapse nearly killed them an hour later.

They heard it before they felt it — a groan rolling up through the rock like the mountain clearing its throat — and then the passage ahead of them shuddered and sloughed, a whole wall of ceiling letting go in a slow, grinding avalanche that filled the corridor from floor to roof. Dust exploded outward. Ulyana shoved Sera back around the last bend and took the spray of grit across his own shoulders, and for a long moment afterward they stood in the ringing aftermath, coughing, blinking, staring at the new wall of stone where the road had been.

"That was no settling," Sera said hoarsely. "That was deliberate. Shaped charges — ward-work. Someone collapsed it *just now*."

"Hunters?" Ulyana's hand went to his knife. "Behind us or ahead?"

"Ahead. Cutting us off." She was already moving to the fresh rubble, pressing her palm flat against it, eyes half-closed. "No — wait. The residue is old ward-craft. Sterling craft. This was laid decades ago, a failsafe, and it just—" Her head came up sharply. "It just answered us. Something we're carrying tripped it."

Slowly, they looked at each other. Then, together, at the signet ring on Ulyana's finger and the ward-stone splinter glowing in Sera's palm.

"It's not sealing us out," Ulyana breathed. "It's testing us. My mother built locks into her own road, and the key is—" He laughed, short and disbelieving. "The key is us. Sterling blood and a piece of the original wards. She built the door for exactly two kinds of people, and we happen to be both."

"Then open it," Sera said.

He pressed his bleeding palm — scraped raw in the collapse — against the rubble, and Sera set the ward-stone beside it, and for three heartbeats nothing happened. Then the stone flared white, the dust lifted off the rubble in a slow spiral like breath drawn inward, and the collapsed stone *unmade itself*, blocks rising and drifting back into their places with a sound like a word being unsaid, until the passage stood clear and cool and open, and the air that rolled out of it smelled of candle smoke and ink and — impossibly, achingly — of lavender.

His mother's scent. The scent of the room he'd grown up in, before she died, before he'd learned she hadn't.

Sera's hand found his. Neither of them spoke. They walked in.

* * *

The vault beneath Thornfell was not a vault. It was a cathedral that had never been blessed.

The passage opened without warning into a cavern so vast their lanterns couldn't find its ceiling, and the floor spread before them in worked stone — concentric rings carved with script older than the kingdom, older than the stars' names, radiating outward from a central dais like ripples frozen mid-fall. The script caught their light and moved with it, letters sliding slowly along their channels, a text reading itself forever to an audience of none.

And against the far wall, tucked into an alcove as deliberately humble as everything else here was vast, stood a workbench.

Ulyana crossed the rings without thinking, without caring whether he should, drawn the way water is drawn. The bench was oak, scarred, cluttered — a human thing in an inhuman place. Jars of ground mineral. Quills worn to stubs. A cold lantern of a design he'd never seen, its glass smoked black. And papers. Dozens of papers, weighted down with river stones, covered in his mother's handwriting — the quick left-slanting script he'd know anywhere, the script from the margins of the one book she'd left him.

On the bench's center, mounted on a stand of black iron, hung a circlet of silver no wider than a crown and no heavier, by the look of it, than a promise.

It was unfinished. Half its filigree was complete; the other half existed only as scored guide-lines in the metal, waiting for a hand. And set into its completed half were seven sockets, six of them empty — and one of them holding a fragment of stone that glowed with a light Ulyana recognized down to his bones.

Starlight. Captured. Kept. The last of it, maybe, outside the sky.

"Oh," Sera whispered behind him. "Oh, Ulyana."

He picked up the topmost paper with hands that shook and did not care that they shook.

*My darling,* his mother had written, in the letter's opening line, as if she'd known he'd read this one first, as if she'd stacked the pages knowing grief would sort them. *If the Veil has driven you this deep, then you already know what it is and what it wears. So I will tell you only what matters: the old binding was a cage, and cages fail, and every cage has cost us a life because we kept trying to imprison something that cannot be imprisoned — only anchored. A cage says stay. An anchor says return. The difference is everything.*

*The circlet will hold the anchor. The anchor must be given freely, by a living soul, at the moment of unbinding — freely, do you hear me, freely — or it becomes a cage again and we begin the whole bloody cycle again. I ran out of time to finish it. I ran out of time for most things.*

*But I did not run out of love, and I did not run out of stubbornness, and I have left you everything you need except the courage, which I could not bottle, though God knows I tried.*

Below the words, in fresher ink — ink that was not his mother's, ink that was careful and precise and unmistakably royal — someone else had written a single line:

*The vessel must be willing also. I am willing. Come quickly. — A.*

Ulyana stood in the cathedral of rings with the paper trembling in his hands, and above him, through a mile of stone, he felt the last star gutter — felt it the way you feel a held breath — and knew they had run out of sky.

"Sera," he said. "Light every lantern we have. We're finishing her work tonight."

From somewhere far above, faint as memory, came the first sounds of pursuit.
