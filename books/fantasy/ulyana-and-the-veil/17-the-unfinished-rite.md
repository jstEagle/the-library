## 17. The Unfinished Rite

The workbench was still warm.

That was the first thing Ulyana noticed, and the thing he could not stop noticing — that after twenty years of darkness, of stone and silence and the slow drip of water through the deep roads beneath Thornfell, his mother's bench should hold any warmth at all. He pressed his palm flat against it and felt something that was not heat exactly but *presence*, a residue of intent soaked so deep into the grain that it had become part of the wood. The way a smuggler's lockpicks wore smooth in the shapes of his fingers. The way a mother's chair remembered her weight.

"She worked here," Sera said softly behind him. "At the end."

"At the end of what?" Ulyana asked, though he already feared the answer. His torchlight moved across the vault in trembling arcs: shelves of star-glass jars gone dark as cinders, coils of silver wire oxidized to black lace, a loom of some pale bone-like material strung with threads that caught the light wrong — bending it, drinking it. And everywhere, everywhere, writing. Chalk on stone. Ink on hide. Scratched into the walls with something desperate and sharp. Ciphered script in his mother's hand, looping and precise, covering every surface like frost climbing a window.

He had spent three days deciphering her letters along the old routes, and those had been hard enough. But this — this was not correspondence. This was a mind laid open. A woman alone in the dark, racing against something she knew would outlast her, writing everything down because there would be no one left to tell.

"Ulyana." Sera's voice again, closer now. "Your hands are shaking."

"I know."

He sat down at the bench. The stool was low, worn concave by years of her sitting, and when he lowered himself onto it his body fit the hollow almost exactly — almost, but not quite, because he was broader than she had been, taller, and the small wrongness of it cracked something open in his chest that he had to breathe around for a long moment before he could move.

The final notebook lay where she had left it, centered on the bench, squared to its edges. Leather cover gone soft as cloth. A silver clasp shaped like an eight-pointed star — the same sigil that had been burned into the guild vault door, the same sigil inked into the margin of every letter she had ever hidden along her routes for him to find, if he ever learned to look.

He opened it.

* * *

The early pages were methodical. Diagrams of the original binding, drawn from memory or from older records now lost: the king's chamber rendered in careful cross-section, the Veil shown as a coiling darkness seated behind the sternum like a second heart, anchored by seven threads of light running up through the crown, down through the spine, out into the world. Starlight, Ulyana realized. The binding had been woven from starlight — drawn down through the observatory's great lens and spun into thread by a Binder's hands. That was why the parasite had killed the stars first. It was eating its own cage.

His mother's handwriting grew tighter as the pages turned. Faster. The margins filled with calculations crossed out and redone, with fragments of prayer in a language he didn't know, with small marginal sketches — a boy's face, over and over, at different ages. Her guesswork at how he would look growing up. He touched one of them, the earliest, a round-cheeked infant with fierce dark eyes, and had to close the book and press both fists against his mouth and simply breathe for a while.

Sera did not touch him. She stood watch at the vault entrance instead, torch high, listening to the deep roads the way she listened to everything — completely, without flinching. He loved her for that. For knowing when silence was the only kindness available.

When he opened the book again, he found the page that changed everything.

*The old rite is murder,* she had written, the letters pressed so hard they'd torn the paper. *Every rebinding since the first has been murder dressed as duty. A Binder of Sterling blood to weave the cage. A willing vessel to hold it. And the vessel dies — always dies — because the cage is built from their life entire. Aldric's grandmother. Aldric's father. Now Aldric, unless I can finish what I started here.*

*I have spent eleven years on a third way. It exists. I have proven it exists. But it is not free.*

Ulyana read the next lines four times. The torch guttered. Somewhere far above, through a hundred fathoms of mountain, the last stars were dying, and he felt each death as a faint cold pressure against his skin, like frost forming on the inside of a window.

*The merciful binding requires no death. It requires an anchor. A living soul, freely given, who stands at the veil's edge and holds the cage shut from within — not consumed, not destroyed, but bound. Forever at the threshold. Neither alive nor dead. Neither in this world nor beyond it. They do not age. They do not sleep. They feel everything the Veil feels, and the Veil feels hunger, endless hunger, and they must hold against it every moment of every year until the end of the world.*

*No one can be forced into it. The anchor's will is the keystone. A coerced soul shatters, and the cage with it. It must be given. Truly given. Which means someone must choose this — must look at the whole shape of it, the centuries of it, the loneliness of it — and say yes with their entire heart.*

*I cannot ask that of anyone. So I am writing it down instead, for whoever comes after me, and I am sorry, whoever you are, that I have left you this choice. If you are reading this, you have Sterling blood, which means you have the weaving, which means the choice is yours whether you want it or not.*

*Forgive me. I chose your life over your freedom, and I would choose it again.*

Below that, one last line, smaller, almost erased:

*If you find a way to spare yourself, take it. Do not let anyone tell you otherwise. Not even me.*

* * *

"No," Ulyana said aloud.

It came out strange in the vault's dead air, flat and small. He said it again, louder, and the ciphered walls seemed to lean in around him.

"No. She's wrong. There has to be another—"

"There isn't." Sera had come back to him. She stood beside the bench now, torchlight catching the healing scar along her jaw where the ward-shatter had cut her, that first night under the dying sky. "You've read it. She spent eleven years. Eleven, Ulyana. If there were another way, she'd have found it."

"Then we don't do it at all. We let the Veil starve. The stars are nearly gone — when the last one dies, it loses its food, doesn't it? It just—"

"It finishes unbinding itself," Sera said quietly. "That's what the letters said. Starlight is the cage, not the food. Or both. When the last star goes out, the binding fails on its own, and the Veil walks free wearing whatever body it likes, and there's no Binder left alive to build anything new." She set the torch down on the bench's edge. "There's no version of this where we walk away. You know that. You've known it since the mirror room."

He had. He had watched King Aldric — gaunt, gray-lipped, lucid for perhaps a quarter hour at a time — beg a stranger to finish his mother's work, and he had seen behind the king's eyes the flicker of the other thing, the vast patient hunger wearing a man like a coat, and he had understood even then that mercy and necessity had collapsed into a single road with no branches.

But he had thought the price would be his own. That had been bearable, in the way terrible things are bearable when they're only yours.

"Sera." He made himself look at her. "Say it plainly. What you're thinking."

"You know what I'm thinking."

"Say it anyway."

She lifted her chin. In the torchlight her eyes were the color of wet slate, steady as a wall in a storm. "I'm thinking that the anchor must be willing, and I am willing. I'm thinking that I've been a ward-keeper my whole life — that I took vows at fifteen to stand between Thornfell and whatever comes, and every vow I ever swore pointed here, to this bench, to this night. I'm thinking that you have the Weaver's blood and I have nothing but a keeper's stubbornness, and between us we might actually manage it." Her voice didn't shake. "And I'm thinking that if you refuse me, you're doing exactly what Corvane did. Deciding for other people what they're allowed to sacrifice."

"That's not—" He stopped. Breathed. "That's not fair."

"No," she agreed. "It isn't. None of this is fair. Fairness died the night the stars went out." She reached across the bench and took his hand — the first time she had touched him since the crossroads, since she had chosen him over Dain and the rebellion and every certainty she'd built her life on. Her fingers were cold. Everything down here was cold. "But listen to me. Listen. If you anchor it, who completes the circuit? The rite needs three. Weaver, vessel, witness. Your mother's notes are clear. If you go into the veil's edge, the binding fails halfway, and the Veil takes us all, and your sacrifice buys nothing but a longer fall."

"The king—"

"Is dying. Lucid for minutes at a time. Can you trust him to hold the circuit while the Veil screams in his skull? While it offers him anything, everything, to break loose?" She shook her head slowly. "You need a witness who can't be tempted. Who has nothing the Veil wants." A ghost of a smile. "I'm nobody, Ulyana. A broken ward-keeper from a border town no one can find on a map. That's worth something tonight."

"You are not nobody." The words came out rougher than he intended, torn rather than spoken. "You sheltered under a shattered ward-line and walked nine days through starless country on a wound that should have killed you. You chose truth over the only family you had left. Don't you dare stand in my mother's vault and call yourself nobody."

For a moment neither of them spoke. The torch flame leaned and steadied. Somewhere in the deep roads above them — or below; distance meant nothing down here — stone shifted with a sound like a slow exhalation, and both of them went still, listening, but it passed.

"Then we have a problem," Sera said at last, gently. "Because I won't step aside. And you can't make me."

"I can bind the rite to myself alone. Refuse the witness. Let it fail."

"And kill us all out of pride."

"Yes!" It burst out of him, ugly and honest, echoing off the ciphered walls. "Yes, if it keeps you out of that — that *threshold*, that forever, that hungry dark — then yes, I'd rather—"

He couldn't finish. Because she was looking at him with such unbearable tenderness that the words died in his throat, and because some traitor part of him heard his own sentence and recognized it for what it was: not nobility. Fear. He could face the Veil, face the hunters, face the long dark of the deep roads. But he could not face a world with her absence in it. He could not become the man who let her go.

His mother's voice rose off the page in his memory: *Do not let anyone tell you otherwise. Not even me.* She had known. Twenty years dead and she had known exactly what he would feel standing at this bench, holding this book, loving someone brave enough to volunteer for eternity.

* * *

They argued until the torch burned down to a stub, and lit another, and argued past that. Sera dismantled every alternative he raised with the patience of a mason taking apart a bad wall — stone by stone, no anger, no haste, until he had nothing left standing. When he tried the rite's mechanics, she quoted his mother's notes back at him more accurately than he could. When he tried guilt, she met it with her vows. When he finally, shamefully, tried ordering her — guild rank, seniority, the brittle authority of a man who had never once outranked her — she laughed, actually laughed, a short bright sound that bounced off the dark jars of dead starlight, and said, "You've never given me an order in your life. Don't start with the last one."

In the end he sat back down on his mother's stool, in his mother's hollow, and put his head in his hands.

"There has to be a third option," he said into his palms. "She always found third options. That was the whole point of her."

Silence. Then the soft scrape of Sera drawing the notebook toward her, turning pages. "Ulyana." Her voice had changed. Tightened. "Come look at this."

He looked. The page showed the rite's full architecture for the first time — not the summary, the whole design, every thread and anchor-point mapped in his mother's smallest, most careful hand. Three figures. Weaver, vessel, witness. But beneath the witness-figure, squeezed into the margin in ink so faint it was nearly invisible, a single addendum:

*Note to self: the circuit holds if the witness stands within the circle — but it holds *truer* if the witness stands at the veil's edge itself, sharing the anchor's burden. Two souls at the threshold halve the weight. Halve the hunger. Halve the forever. I never dared write this down properly. No one should ever have to choose it. But if two ever chose it together — freely, wholly —*

The sentence ended there. No period. Just the trailing pressure of her pen lifting, and the ghost of a decision she had refused to make.

Ulyana stared at it for a long time. Something enormous moved through him — hope and horror in equal measure, tangled so tightly he couldn't tell them apart.

"She never finished it," he whispered. "She couldn't decide whether leaving this was cruelty or mercy."

"Maybe it was both," Sera said. "Maybe that's all any of this is." She looked up at him, slate eyes reflecting the torchflame, and he saw that she had already understood the addendum's shape, had already run its arithmetic, and was waiting — not pushing, just waiting — for him to arrive where she had arrived.

Two at the threshold. Half the forever. Still forever.

Before either of them could speak again, the torches guttered — both at once, though there was no wind — and from far up the deep roads came a sound that did not belong to stone or water.

Footsteps. Many of them. Walking in perfect unison.

And threaded through the uniform tread, thin and sweet as a lullaby, a voice that sounded horribly like his own:

*"Little weaver. I've brought you a gift. Come and see."*
