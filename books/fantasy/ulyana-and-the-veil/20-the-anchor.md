## 20. The Anchor

The Veil wore his mother's face, and it was beautiful.

That was the horror of it — not the rot beneath the courtiers' skin, not the hunters pouring down the deep roads like water finding a crack in a dam, but this: that it knew exactly what to offer him, and it offered it gently. Ulyana stood at the center of the binding vault with his mother's notes spread across her own workbench, and the thing that had worn three generations of kings looked back at him from a face he had spent twenty years trying to remember and only half succeeded.

*Come,* it said, in a voice like starlight falling on snow. *You would be so much more than an anchor. You would be a king of everything.*

Behind him, Sera was screaming his name. Ahead, Dain Ashvale's rebels were fighting their way through a tide of veiled bodies toward the dais where King Aldric lay freed — truly freed, for the first time in thirty years, gasping on cold stone like a man pulled out of deep water. And between all of it, Ulyana felt the pull of the bargain like a hook behind his sternum.

*Your mother, whole and warm. Your guild restored, its ledgers clean, its people safe. A world with no borders, because you will hold them all inside yourself.* The face smiled, and it was her smile, the one from the one memory he trusted, the memory of flour on her hands and a song half-hummed. *You have been outside every door your whole life, Ulyana Sterling. I am offering you every door at once.*

"I know what you are," he said quietly.

*I know what you want. That is better than knowing what I am. Wanting is how I have always gotten in.*

"Yes," he said. "I imagine it is."

He looked down at his mother's final page. The ink had faded to the color of old bruises, but the cipher was his now — he had carried it through tunnels and dungeons and rooftops, had read it by candle stub and stolen light until the letters were carved into him deeper than any scar. *A living anchor, freely given. Not a death. A dwelling. The Binder steps halfway into the veil's edge and holds the door shut from within. Neither alive nor dead. Neither here nor there. Forever.*

Forever was a long word. He turned it over anyway.

*You hesitate,* the Veil said, tender as a lie. *Good. Hesitation means you understand the price. My vessel hesitated too, once, before Aldric's grandmother bound me into him. She chose the quick knife instead. Would you rather I wear someone else? Your ward-keeper, perhaps? She is already reaching for you. It would be so easy to let her—*

"Don't." His voice cracked like ice underfoot. "Don't touch her."

*Then choose quickly, little smuggler. The stars are almost gone.*

And they were. Through the vault's high shaft, the last thread of the night sky showed — a darkness so complete it seemed less like absence than like something drinking. Three stars left. He could feel them going out the way you feel a candle guttering in another room, some animal part of him flinching each time the dark thickened.

Ulyana closed his eyes.

He thought about doors. All his life he had been the man who opened them for other people — the hidden ways, the bribed gates, the routes that existed in no ledger anyone would admit to. He had made a trade of thresholds. He had never once been allowed to stand in the room beyond.

The Veil was offering him every room.

His mother had chosen the threshold instead. Had stepped halfway into the dark and held it, alone, for however long she'd lasted before the guild's cowardice and the crown's hunger had ground her down. She had died — or stopped being anywhere — so that a newborn prince could sleep unhaunted, so that the wards over Thornfell could hum their lullaby for a generation.

She hadn't done it because she wanted nothing. She had done it because she wanted everything, for everyone else.

"No," Ulyana said.

The vault went very still. Even the battle seemed to lean in.

*No?*

"Not your way. Not your gift. You don't get to give me my mother. You don't get to give me anything." He opened his eyes and looked at the thing wearing her face, and he found, to his surprise, that he wasn't afraid anymore. Grief had burned all the way down to something harder. "You're not a king-maker or a god. You're a parasite that learned to flatter. And I've spent my life reading marks, so believe me when I tell you—" he smiled, and watched the borrowed face flicker, "—I know every trick in the book. I wrote half of them."

Then he reached for the binding.

* * *

It began, as his mother's notes promised, with a word spoken into the wound.

The wound was everywhere. The Veil's unbinding had torn the vault open along seams older than the kingdom — hairline fractures in the air itself, glimmering like cracks in black glass, through which poured a cold that was not temperature but *absence*. Ulyana walked toward the largest of them, and the Veil screamed without a mouth, and the hunters redoubled their surge toward him.

Sera met them first.

He heard her before he saw her — the ring of her ward-keeper's blade, the sharp incantations she spat in the old tongue of the border watch, words she'd told him once were just ceremony, just tradition, just things keepers said over dying stones. They weren't ceremony. Light broke from her hands in sheets, and where it touched the veiled bodies they came apart like smoke in wind.

"Ulyana!" She fought her way to him, bleeding from a gash above her eye, her light guttering. "Tell me you're not doing what I think you're doing."

"You told me once the wards needed an anchor," he said. "Someone willing."

"That was theory. That was *my* job, if anyone's—"

"It was never going to be yours." He caught her hands. Her fingers were shaking; his were not, which surprised them both. "Sera. Listen to me. There isn't time to argue and there isn't another Binder. There's just me, and this door, and I'm the only one who knows how to lock it."

"There has to be another way. We found the workbench, we found the second rite, there has to be—"

"There is. This is it." He pressed his forehead to hers, briefly, and the vault's cold rushed around them like a tide around two stones. "When it's done, the circuit needs completing. Me on the inside, Binders' blood on the outside. Aldric carries royal blood. You carry the keeping-words. Between the three of you, the knot ties."

"Between the three of—" She pulled back and stared at him. "*Aldric?*"

Which was when the king stood up.

* * *

Later, Ulyana would try to describe what Aldric looked like in that moment, and fail. Freed of the Veil after thirty years, the man was gaunt as a winter branch, his eyes sunken, his hands trembling — and yet he rose from the stone with a steadiness that had nothing to do with the body. It was the steadiness of a prisoner who has finally seen the sky.

"The last Binder's son," Aldric said. His voice was rust and riverbeds. "Your mother held me once, when I was a day old. I don't remember it. But the part of me she sealed remembers her hands." He looked at the fracture in the air, at the darkness seeping through it, and something in his face broke and reformed. "Thirty years I screamed inside my own skull while it wore me to court functions. Thirty years watching my hands sign things, my mouth say things. Do you know what I prayed for?"

"A way out," Ulyana said.

"I prayed for someone to blame. And then I prayed for forgiveness for wanting one. And then, near the end, I only prayed for *quiet*." The king straightened. "You're offering me quiet. Whatever else happens tonight — whatever the rebels do, whatever the court becomes — I want you to know that I see the price. I'm not letting you pay it alone thinking no one counted the cost."

Dain Ashvale burst through the melee then, sword red, three rebels at his back, and his eyes went from the fracture to the workbench to Ulyana's outstretched hand, and Ulyana watched him do the arithmetic. Watched him realize that if the binding completed, there would be no chaos to inherit. No empty throne. Just a king, alive and lucid and *legitimate*, and a smuggler halfway into legend.

"Stop the rite," Dain said. "Sterling. Stop it, and the rebellion will make you a duke. Lands, titles, a seat—"

"Dain." Sera's voice was low and lethal. "Stand down."

"He's handing Aldric everything—"

"He's handing *us* our sky back, you absolute fool—"

But Ulyana had stopped listening. Because the Veil, denied its flattery, had begun to take.

* * *

It came out of the fracture like floodwater through a broken door.

Not a shape — a pressure. A hunger given weight. The remaining starlight in the shaft above bent toward it, thinning, and every veiled body in the vault dropped at once, emptied, and the darkness gathered itself up out of them and *rose*. The temperature fell so far that breath froze mid-air and drifted down as glittering dust. The walls wept frost. In the black glass of the fracture, Ulyana saw reflections that were not reflections: a thousand faces, a thousand kings and queens and keepers it had worn and discarded across centuries, all of them mouthing the same silent word.

*More. More. More.*

"Now!" he shouted, and stepped into the veil's edge.

Cold took him the way drowning takes — total, immediate, intimate. It was not pain. Pain would have been merciful, would have been *something*. This was subtraction. The warmth of his own blood grew distant, theoretical. The sound of Sera screaming his name arrived stretched thin, like music heard through a wall, through years. He was standing in the vault and he was standing nowhere, both at once, a coin balanced on its edge between worlds.

And the Veil was *there*, all around him, delighted.

*Oh,* it breathed. *Oh, you came willingly. Do you know how long it has been since one of you came willingly? They always fight. Fighting spoils the flavor.*

"I didn't come to be eaten."

*Everything that enters is eaten. Eventually. Slowly. You will forget the sun first. Then names. Then yourself. And I will have a door that walks, a key that thinks, a—*

"You'll have a lock," Ulyana said.

He spoke his mother's word.

It was not a word in any living language — it was older than language, a sound shaped like a knot, and when he spoke it the fracture *heard* him. The darkness convulsed. For one instant the Veil's attention narrowed onto him entirely, all its vast hungry centuries focused on this one stubborn point of refusal, and in that instant —

— Sera spoke the keeping-words.

Her voice cut through the cold like a struck bell. The old tongue of the border watch, the ceremony-words, the dying-stone words, and they were none of those things; they were the outer half of a circuit designed before the kingdom had a name, waiting all these generations in the mouths of keepers who thought they were only preserving tradition. Light poured off her in ropes. She planted her feet on the vault floor, on the sigil his mother had carved there and hidden under decades of dust, and she *held*.

— and Aldric stepped forward, royal blood answering royal seal, and pressed his palm to the stone beside her.

"I am Aldric," the king said, to the dark, to the parasite, to the thing that had worn him like a coat for thirty years. "And you are *leaving*."

The circuit closed.

* * *

What happened next, Ulyana experienced from inside.

The binding was not a cage, he discovered. It was a *knot* — and knots require tension from every side. From within the veil's edge, he pulled. From without, Sera and Aldric pushed. And the Veil, caught between, compressed — its vastness folded down and down and down, its thousand stolen faces collapsing into one, its hunger folding inward like a star collapsing, until all that remained of it was a single dense point of dark held in a lattice of light that was half his will, half Sera's voice, half Aldric's blood. Three halves. His mother's math had never been tidy.

*You cannot hold forever,* the Veil whispered, small now, furious now, a wasp in a jar. *Nothing holds forever.*

"My mother held for twenty years," Ulyana answered. "I'm planning to embarrass her."

He tied the last turn of the knot.

The silence afterward was the loudest thing he had ever heard.

* * *

Above, through the high shaft of the vault, the sky remembered itself.

It began as a single point — a spark in the absolute dark, so sudden and so bright that Sera cried out. Then another. Then ten, a hundred, thousands, the whole drowned firmament igniting in waves like a city lighting district by district, constellations blooming back into their ancient shapes, the Milky Way spilling across the heavens like milk from a tipped jug. Starlight poured down the shaft and into the vault and washed over everything — the fallen, the fighters, the frozen breath-dust turning to drifting silver — and where it touched the veiled bodies, they did not rise again. They simply became bodies. Dead men, finally permitted to be only dead.

Sera fell to her knees in the light. Aldric sat down slowly on the steps of the dais, put his face in his hands, and wept — not the weeping Ulyana had heard through dungeon walls, wrung out of him by something else, but his own. His own grief. His own relief. His own tears, for the first time in thirty years.

And at the center of the vault, at the edge of the veil, Ulyana Sterling stood halfway between worlds and looked at his own hands.

They were translucent. Not gone — not yet, not ever quite — but lit from behind by starlight that passed through them, so that when he raised a palm he could see the faint geometry of the binding shining through his skin like a lantern through paper. Cold lived in him now, permanent and patient. When he breathed, the air frosted. When he moved, he left no footprints in the dust.

"Sera," he said, and his voice came from very far away, from everywhere, from the space between one star and the next.

She looked up. Found him. And her face — gods, her face — went through grief and wonder and fury and love so fast it blurred, and settled, finally, on something fierce.

"You're still there," she said.

"Halfway." He tried to smile. It felt strange on a face that was becoming more suggestion than substance. "Always was, really. Now it's just official."

"Don't." She was on her feet, crossing the vault, reaching for him — and her hands passed through his like light through water, and he watched her feel it, watched the moment land, and hated himself for the sound she made. "Don't joke. Don't you dare stand there being brave and funny, Ulyana, don't you—"

"Sera." He said her name softly, and the vault itself seemed to carry it, the way the old wards used to carry whispers along the border stones. "Look up."

She wouldn't, at first. Then she did.

The stars blazed down through the shaft, whole and burning, and among them — new, impossible — one constellation shone that no star-chart had ever recorded: a figure with one hand extended, holding a door shut.

"That's cheating," she whispered.

"That's the job," he said. "My mother's job. Now mine." He looked at his fading hands, at the beautiful unbearable sky, at her. "I can't leave this threshold. But I'm not gone, Sera. The binding needs an anchor, and an anchor *holds* — it doesn't vanish. On the new moon, when the veil's edge runs thinnest..." He smiled again, and this time it reached whatever was left of his eyes. "I think I can come home. Briefly. Once a month. If someone leaves a door open."

For a long moment she said nothing. Starlight moved on her wet face.

"I'll leave every door in Thornfell open," she said.

Behind them, King Aldric rose, unsteady, unbowed, and looked upon his kingdom's returned sky with the expression of a man reading a pardon. Somewhere in the tunnels above, the survivors of three armies were stumbling out into a night full of stars, shouting. The war was over. The throne was occupied by a free man. And the guild — the fat, frightened, faithful guild — would need rebuilding from its foundations, by someone with a keeper's spine and a smuggler's map.

Ulyana felt all of it at the edge of his awareness, the way one feels sunlight through a curtain. Present. Adjacent. Almost home.

The new moon was twenty-eight days away.

He began, patiently, to count.
