## 21. Halfway Between

The first thing Ulyana learned about the veil's edge was that it had weather.

Not rain, not snow — nothing so honest. But there were tides. The grey country around him breathed in long slow swells, and when it exhaled, the shapes of things grew clearer: the ghost-echo of the binding vault below him, the faint lattice of the old wards stitched across the sky like a half-mended net, and far off, at the horizon where Thornfell's mountains should have been, a thin bright seam — the border between what was real and what had never quite managed to be.

He stood in a place that was neither. The stone under his boots was stone, but when he crouched to touch it, his fingers came away with no dust, no cold, no certainty. His breath made no fog here, though he felt cold all the same — a cold that lived in the marrow rather than the skin.

And through it all, faint as a coin dropped in another room, he could hear the world.

That was the cruelty of it. He could hear Sera shouting his name in the vault below — had heard her scream it for what might have been hours or days; time moved strangely here, pooling and draining like water in cracked stone. He could hear Aldric's voice, hoarse and human, giving orders that no king of the last sixty years would have recognized as orders: *bring water, bring light, count your dead, count them honestly.* He could hear the whole wounded kingdom exhaling.

He could not answer any of them.

When he tried, his voice went out of him like smoke through a keyhole. When he pressed his hand against the seam of the world — and he found the seam quickly, because a smuggler knows a hidden door when he stands in one — the veil pushed back, gentle and absolute, the way deep water pushes back against a diver who has gone too far down.

So he did what Ulyana Sterling had always done when trapped somewhere unwinnable.

He mapped it.

* * *

The edge-country, he discovered, was built out of everything the binding had ever swallowed. There were roads here — smuggler roads, some of them, worn smooth by boots that had crossed into the veil and never come back, though he hadn't known that was where they'd gone. There was a chapel whose bell still swung without sound. There was a farmhouse with a table set for supper, three chairs pushed back mid-meal, forty years gone and the bread on the plates still soft. The Veil had fed on starlight, but it had also fed on *absence*, and it had kept every absence it ever ate, arranged like a miser's treasury.

He walked those roads because walking was better than listening to Sera weep two inches from his ear and being unable to say her name.

On the third day — or what he judged was the third day, by the rhythm of the tide — he found his mother.

Not her. He knew better than to hope for that. But her workbench stood in a hollow of grey stone, transposed here whole, the way a drowned ship keeps its shape on the riverbed. Her tools lay where she'd left them. Her ciphered letters were stacked under a river-stone weight, and when he opened them — they opened for him here, the cipher dissolving like sugar in water, because he was inside her work now, made of it — the last page held only one line, in her quick slanted hand:

*If you are reading this from the other side, then you did what I could not, and I am sorry, and I am proud, and the door opens at the dark of the moon.*

*Count the days. I always did.*

Ulyana sat down on the floor of the grey country and laughed until it turned into something else, and if the veil heard him weeping, it had the decency, for once, to keep quiet about it.

* * *

Twenty-eight days is a long time to watch a kingdom heal through a curtain.

He learned the rhythm of it. The seam thinned at the new moon — not opening, exactly, but going translucent, like ice over a well. For one night, maybe two, he could press close to the world and be *in* it: solid enough to touch, warm enough to feel warmth, present enough that Sera's hand could find his and hold on as though letting go would lose him to a current.

The first time, she nearly struck him before she embraced him.

"You *idiot,*" she said, and her voice broke on the second word, and she hit his shoulder with the flat of her palm hard enough that he felt it — really felt it, pain blooming honest and mortal — and then her arms were around him and her face was buried in his coat and she was saying, muffled and furious, "You counted. You absolute smuggler, you actually *counted.*"

"Twenty-eight days," he said into her hair. "I told you I was good with routes."

Aldric waited until Sera had released him — which took a while, and Ulyana did not hurry it — before stepping forward into the lantern-light of the rebuilt vault antechamber. The king looked older than his years and lighter than his burdens, which Ulyana supposed was what sixty years of stolen sleep coming due looked like. His eyes were his own now. That was the thing Ulyana noticed most. Whatever had worn that face had never once blinked in the way a man blinks.

"Sterling," Aldric said. "Binder."

"Don't." Ulyana shook his head. "Your mother's binder was my mother. I'm just the one holding the tools."

"The tools make the binder." The king studied him with an expression Ulyana was beginning to recognize as guilt wearing gratitude's clothes. "There will be a place for you at court. Whatever form your—"

"I live two inches to the left of your kingdom, Your Majesty. Court would need a chair I can sit in."

"Then we will build one," Aldric said simply, "and you may sit in it twenty-eight nights from now."

It was, Ulyana thought later, the first honest bargain a crown had offered anyone in three generations. He took it.

* * *

The court dissolved the way rot does — slowly, then all at once.

Ulyana watched it from the seam on the following new moons, pressed against the thin place while Aldric worked. The king did not purge; he *audited*. He sat in the throne room with the ledger of sixty years' payments open on his knee — Corvane's ledger, recovered from the guildhall, its columns of hush-money now testimony — and he summoned lords and ministers one at a time and asked them, courteously, terribly, to explain their signatures.

Some wept. Some lied. Two fled north and were brought back by ward-keepers who no longer answered to the palace alone. And the parasite's remaining hands — the hunters, the half-court, the men and women whose reflections had moved wrong for years — Aldric handled differently. He had them gathered in the great hall on a night of full starlight, and he spoke to the *people* inside them, the ones still drowning underneath, and he gave them a choice: the deep cells and a Binder's attention, or exile beyond the wards with their names struck from every roll in the kingdom.

Most chose the cells. A few chose exile. One — the Lord Chamberlain, whose reflection had been the Veil's oldest servant — knelt in the middle of the hall and wept for six hours straight, saying *thank you, thank you* to a king he had spent a decade betraying, and Ulyana, watching from the threshold with his hands pressed flat against the seam, understood that mercy was its own kind of surgery and that Aldric was very good at it, because he had been cut by the same blade.

"They'll call it the Unmasking," Sera told him on his fourth visit, sitting beside him on the vault steps with her shoulder just barely touching his — the most contact the seam allowed for more than a few minutes. "The court, I mean. Half of them are already calling it that. The other half are calling it a massacre of reputations and demanding trials."

"Are they getting trials?"

"Aldric insists. Public ones." She turned a small iron ring over in her fingers — Corvane's guild signet, which she now wore on a cord at her throat. "He says the kingdom can't tell the difference between justice and another mask unless it watches the faces come off."

"And the guild?"

Sera was quiet for a moment. Below them, through the vault door, came the sounds of the new guildhall taking shape — hammering, argument, the scrape of the old smuggling ledgers being burned in a brazier and the ward-keepers' rolls being copied out in their place.

"Corvane left me the seal," she said. "In writing. Dated the night he let you pass. He knew the hunters were coming for him. He signed it anyway." Her thumb moved over the ring. "He wrote that the guild was founded to guard a secret and forgot how to guard anything but itself. He wrote that whoever came after him should decide whether we were smugglers who sometimes kept faith, or keepers who sometimes smuggled." She looked up at him. "I've decided."

"Keepers," Ulyana said.

"Keepers." She almost smiled. "With excellent maps, and a regrettable continued tolerance for moving goods the crown doesn't need to know about. Old habits. But the routes run both ways now, Ulyana. Every tunnel, every hidden door, every road below the wards — they're all being registered, warded, *kept*. The guild isn't a shadow anymore. It's the hinge."

"And me?"

"You're the first entry in the new roll." She said it lightly, but her hand found his through the seam, and her grip said the rest: *the roll is short, and your name is at the top, and we are keeping your place.*

* * *

Between visits, he worked.

His mother's notes were extensive once he stopped grieving them and started reading them. The unfinished second binding — the merciful one, the one that needed no death — had cost her eleven years and, judging by the margins, most of her hope. She had solved the anchor problem twice over: the living vessel freely given, and the circuit completed by blood and will from outside. What she had never solved was *return*. Every model ended the same way, in her tight frustrated script: *the anchor holds, the anchor endures, the anchor does not come back.*

But Ulyana had spent his whole life finding the flaw in other people's routes.

He spread her diagrams across the grey country's version of her workbench and began, patiently, to annotate. The seam thinned at the new moon — that was fact, observed. The seam was thinnest where the original binding had been laid — also fact; he could feel the pull toward the vault like a compass needle. Which meant the door was not a property of the veil. It was a property of the *binding*. And bindings, his mother had written on her very first page, *are arguments, not walls. Every argument can be amended.*

He didn't have an answer yet. But he had something she'd never had: he was on both sides of the lock at once.

He began to draft an amendment.

* * *

The fifth new moon fell on a night of hard frost, and when Ulyana stepped through the seam into the vault antechamber, Sera was waiting with two cups of tea and an expression he had learned to dread.

"Dain's trial starts tomorrow," she said.

Ulyana took the tea. It was hot and real and he wrapped both hands around it like a man warming himself at a fire he wasn't sure he deserved. "How's the cell treating him?"

"He won't eat anything the guards bring. He's representing himself. He asked—" She hesitated, and Ulyana braced himself, because Sera hesitating meant the sentence was bad. "He asked for you. As a witness. He says you're the only person alive who heard what he actually offered, and the court deserves to hear it from someone who isn't a rebel or a royalist."

"He wants me to testify that he planned to murder a king."

"He wants you to testify to *what happened in the vault.* All of it. Including what the Veil offered you." Her eyes came up to his. "Including what you refused. Ulyana — half the kingdom thinks the rebinding was luck or treason or sorcery nobody understands. Aldric wants the truth spoken publicly. Dain wants a stage. And you're the only witness who was standing in the middle when everything converged."

Ulyana drank his tea and looked at the vault door, behind which the guild's new life hammered away, and thought about standing in a courtroom made of his own body's absence, testifying in a voice that faded at the edges of the room.

"If I speak," he said slowly, "the whole kingdom learns what an anchor is. What I am. They'll know I'm halfway to nowhere, Sera. Some of them will start asking why we bother keeping a man who's mostly gone."

"Some of them already ask that." She said it without flinching, which was why he trusted her. "And the answer is that you're not mostly gone. You're mostly *here*, twenty-eight days out of every moon, and the rest of the time you're up there amending your mother's work so that number gets bigger." She set down her cup. "Unless it isn't. Unless the amendment isn't working."

"It's working." He heard how careful his own voice was. "Slowly. The seam is wider than it was. I stayed nine minutes past the tide last month."

Sera went very still. "Nine minutes."

"Her binding was an argument. I'm adding clauses." He smiled, and it cost him more than he let show. "Give me a year. Maybe five. Maybe I walk out of the veil entirely and the first entry in your new roll becomes a man with a pulse."

"And if the amendment fails?"

"Then I'll have spent five years doing the family trade." He shrugged. "Keeping a door open."

She laughed — wet-eyed, reluctant, real — and knocked her cup against his, and outside the vault, above the mountain, the stars wheeled on in their returned courses, bright and unblinking and free.

* * *

He testified at dawn on the sixth new moon.

The great hall had never held so many people; they stood in the aisles and hung from the galleries, and when Ulyana stepped through the seam into the witness light — nine minutes wide now, twelve if he hurried — a murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat, because everyone in Thornfell had heard rumors of the man who lived in the edge of the world, and here he was, translucent at the fingertips, real as bread.

Dain Ashvale stood at the accused's stand thinner than Ulyana remembered, grey at the temples, unbowed. Their eyes met, and Ulyana saw no apology in them and no denial either — only the flat arithmetic of a man who still believed the sum had been right even if the method had been ugly.

"Tell them," Dain said, when his turn came, and his voice carried to the rafters, "what I promised you in the undercity. Exactly."

"You promised me belonging," Ulyana said. "You promised it too easily, and it fit too well, and I should have known then. You told me the king was dead already and killing him was mercy. You didn't tell me you intended to be holding the crown when it stopped being mercy." He paused. "And then you arrived at the binding with armed rebels to kill a man mid-rite, and the Veil nearly won because of it."

"Because of *me*," Dain said, turning to the hall, "or because your precious rite required a sacrifice and you couldn't bear to choose anyone else? Because the truth, Binder, is that your mercy has a price and you paid it with yourself, and this kingdom now keeps its savior in a jar and calls it peace!" His voice cracked upward. "Ask him! Ask him what he is! Ask him whether he sleeps, whether he eats, whether he is *alive* —"

"I'm alive," Ulyana said quietly, and the hall went silent, because the quiet was worse than any shout. "I'm alive twenty-eight nights out of every moon, and less each cycle, and I am working on that. And yes — the price was me. Freely given. That's what makes it binding, and that's what makes it mine." He looked at Dain steadily. "You wanted to save this kingdom by putting a sword through its heart. I put myself through it instead. You can hate the mathematics. So do I. But don't stand there and call it a jar, Dain. It's a door. And doors open."

The silence afterward lasted long enough that Ulyana felt the tide begin to pull at him, the seam tightening around his edges like a closing hand. Aldric rose from the throne — rose *slowly*, an old man's care — and said, in a voice pitched to reach the last gallery:

"This court finds Dain Ashvale guilty of conspiracy against the crown. This court also finds that the crown, for three generations, gave every conspirator in this kingdom their example." He let that land. "Sentence: exile, name struck, pardon available upon ten years' service to the ward-keepers, taken or refused at his own choice. We have had enough of killing in this hall. Let him go and decide what he is."

Dain stared at the king for a long moment. Then he laughed — short, disbelieving, almost broken — and said, "You're as mad as the Veil said you weren't," and walked out of the hall with his chains striking sparks off the stones, and Ulyana, fading at the edges, felt the strangest thing: not satisfaction. *Grief.* For the ally he'd wanted Dain to be. For the belonging that had fit too well because it had been tailored to trap him.

Then the tide closed over his head, and the grey country took him back, and the last thing he heard was Sera's voice rising above the assembly, clear and steady, reading the first entry of the new guild roll aloud:

*"Ulyana Sterling. Keeper. First of his name in a generation. Present."*

*Present,* he thought, drifting through the edge-country toward his mother's workbench, where the amendment lay half-written under its river-stone weight. *Almost. Nearly. Getting closer.*

* * *

Winter deepened. The visits blurred into a rhythm: twenty-eight days of work in the grey country, annotating his mother's argument clause by clause, testing the seam's width with a smuggler's patience; then one night, two, of tea and frost and the hammer-song of the guild rebuilding itself overhead.

The seam widened. Nine minutes became fifteen. Fifteen became a quarter hour, then half. On the ninth new moon, Sera met him at the vault door and pressed a bundle into his arms: his mother's coat, repaired, lined with fresh wool, smelling of cedar and lamp oil.

"You left it here," she said. "The first time. You never took it back."

"I was in a hurry to die," Ulyana said, and put it on, and it fit.

On the eleventh new moon, Aldric came down to the vault himself, old and slow and crowned with plain iron now — he had melted the Veil's regalia, all of it, and struck the metal into ward-stones for the border towns — and he stood before Ulyana in the lantern light and said, "The kingdom owes you a debt it cannot repay to a man it cannot keep. So instead it will wait. As long as it takes. The throne will hold your place, Binder, and the roll will hold your name, and when you walk out of that veil — if you walk out — there will be work here worthy of you."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the work will be worthy of you anyway," Aldric said, "because we will do it the way you taught us. Honestly. With the doors registered and the accounts open and the faces unmasked." The old king's eyes crinkled. "My mother's binder used to say that a binding is an argument. Yours, Sterling, was the best-spoken one this kingdom has ever heard."

After he left, Ulyana stood a long time at the seam, looking out at the world — at Sera crossing the guildhall yard below with a lantern, at the ward-fires burning along the border like a string of faithful beads, at the stars, wheeling, unblinking, *free* — and he felt the tide gather to take him back, and this time he leaned into it the way a swimmer leans into a current he has finally learned to read.

Twenty-eight days. Then home again. Then twenty-eight more.

He was halfway between, and halfway, he had decided, was not a sentence.

It was a route. And Ulyana Sterling had never yet failed to finish a route.

Behind him, in the grey country, on his mother's workbench, the amendment lay open to its final page — and the ink of the last line, which had been blank for eleven months, was drying.
