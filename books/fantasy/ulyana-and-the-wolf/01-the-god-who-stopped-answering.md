## 1. The God Who Stopped Answering

The rain came down the Thornfell passes the way it always did this time of year—not falling so much as *arriving*, sideways and personal, as if the mountain itself had taken a dislike to anyone foolish enough to be walking its roads after dark.

Paloma Okonkwo had been walking them for eleven years. She no longer took it personally.

"Keep your hand off the lantern," she said, without turning around. "You'll make a target of us."

Behind her, the wool merchant's son—Tomas, nineteen, soft-handed, terrified—obediently dimmed his grip until only a coin of light spilled between his fingers. He'd been making noise about bandits since the second switchback above Vell's Crossing, and Paloma had long since stopped reassuring him. Reassurance was a currency, and she didn't spend what she couldn't collect on.

"There's nothing on this stretch," she said instead. "Nothing worth robbing, nothing worth dying over. The wolves keep to the high scree when it rains. The men who'd trouble us are three valleys east, working the salt caravans. You paid me to know that, so let me do the knowing."

"And you're certain?" His voice cracked on the last word, a boy's voice wearing a man's coat.

Paloma stopped. The road here was cut into the mountainside, a shelf of wet granite barely wide enough for two mules abreast, with a drop on the left that went down and down into blackness where the river made its slow argument with the rocks. She turned and looked at him. Rain ran off the brim of her hood in a steady thread. Her face, what he could see of it beneath the oilcloth, was brown and seamed and entirely unimpressed.

"Tomas," she said. "In eleven years on these roads I have been robbed twice, shot once, and married never. Both robberies were my own fault for taking work I shouldn't have. Do you understand what I'm telling you?"

"That... you're good?"

"I'm telling you the odds are in our favor because I've spent a decade making sure they are. Luck is for people who don't prepare." She resumed walking. "Now move. We make the toll-house by midnight or your father's cloth arrives late, and late cloth is cheap cloth, and cheap cloth means he doesn't pay me full rate. And Tomas—" she glanced back, letting him see the flash of her teeth, "—I am very expensive."

He laughed, high and startled, the way people did when fear found nowhere else to go. Good. A laughing client walked faster than a praying one.

* * *

The toll-house was less a building than an argument between stone and weather that the stone had been losing for a century. Its roof sagged; its single window glowed with peat-smoke light; its keeper, a one-eyed woman named Brakka who had once, allegedly, been a pirate on the lowland rivers before the rivers changed course and took the piracy with them, sat inside it wrapped in three blankets and permanent grievance.

"You're late," Brakka said, which was how she said hello.

"The road was wet."

"The road is always wet. That's not a road, girl, that's a river with ambitions." Brakka poured something from a clay jug into two chipped cups and pushed one across the plank that served as her counter. It was not tea. It had never pretended to be tea. Paloma drank it anyway, because it was hot and it burned going down and both qualities were useful tonight.

Tomas huddled near the hearth, wringing out his coat and narrating their journey to Brakka in the breathless way of the young—as if the walk had been an epic rather than a Tuesday. Brakka listened with the expression of a woman watching paint dry, but Paloma noticed her good eye kept flicking to the window, to the dark beyond it, and that her hand rested near the iron poker by the fire rather than the knife at her belt.

Poker meant something bigger than a knife. Brakka knew her business.

"Anything on the roads?" Paloma asked quietly, when Tomas paused for breath.

Brakka's eye came back to her. "Quiet. Too quiet, maybe. No salt caravans through in six days, and the salt men are never quiet." She shrugged, a mountainous movement of blankets. "Could be weather. Could be worse. There's talk down-valley."

"There's always talk down-valley."

"This talk has a shape to it." Brakka lowered her voice, and even the fire seemed to lean in. "Collectors. Not the king's—the other kind. Asking questions at the crossings. About couriers. About maps."

Paloma kept her face still, which was easy, because there was nothing on it to move. "Tithe-collectors don't come down this far. They haven't in years."

"They came down far enough to ask Brakka of the Toll-House, didn't they?" The old woman snorted. "Two nights back. Grey cloaks, faces like unpaid debts. Wanted to know if anything had come through from the high passes. I told them nothing comes through from anywhere, ever, and that looking at my window costs a copper apiece." She grinned with most of her teeth. "They paid."

"And then?"

"And then they went east, toward the Ford Road." Brakka's eye held hers, flat and knowing. "Which is where you're headed tomorrow, if I recall the way of your contracts."

"It's where my contract ends," Paloma said. "After that, the boy goes home to his father and I go wherever pays."

"Mm." Brakka retrieved the jug and refilled both cups unasked. "Eleven years I've known you, Okonkwo. In all that time you've never once asked me what the talk was. Tonight you asked." She drank. "Whatever's coming down out of Thornfell, it hasn't reached you yet. When it does—" she tapped the counter once, twice, "—you remember that the toll-house keeper owes you more than she lets on. There's a cellar under the floor. It fits two, uncomfortable."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. Then she finished her cup, set two coppers on the plank—one for the drink, one for the warning—and said, "There won't be any need."

"No," Brakka agreed comfortably. "There never is. That's why I mention it now, while mentioning is free."

* * *

Tomas slept like the dead, curled against the hearthstone with his arms around his satchel of cloth samples, and Paloma lay awake on the bench by the door listening to the rain and doing the arithmetic she did every night whether she wanted to or not.

Eleven years ago, the god of contracts had spoken to her for the last time.

She remembered it the way you remember a bone that healed wrong—vividly, uselessly, at odd hours. She had been twenty-two, kneeling in a waystation shrine with her palms open on her knees, the way Ilya had taught her, and the presence that lived behind her sternum—that warm, weighty attention, like a hand resting on the back of her neck—had simply *thinned*. Not gone. Thinned. Like a candle behind smoked glass. She had finished the prayer anyway. She had felt nothing answer.

She had gone to Ilya, because Ilya had brokered her contract and Ilya knew everything about everything, and he had frowned in a way she'd never seen before and said, "Say nothing of this. Contracts endure even when the parties are quiet. Keep your vows. Wait."

She had waited ten years.

Ten years of keeping every clause, every condition, every small daily observance written into the contract she'd signed with her own blood at seventeen: *honest weights, honest words, no oath broken, no trust betrayed.* Ten years of praying nightly to a silence. Other mercs had gods who talked—who bargained, joked, occasionally threw lightning. Paloma had a god who had gone quiet the way a house goes quiet when someone dies in a room upstairs and nobody will say so.

And still she prayed. That was the humiliating truth of it. Every night, knees down, palms open, the old words in the old cadence. Not because she believed she was heard. Because the contract said *nightly observance*, and Paloma Okonkwo did not break contracts. It was the only thing left of the arrangement that still worked—her half of it. She kept her half the way a widow keeps a grave.

Some nights she hated herself for it. Most nights she just did it, the way she checked the road behind her, the way she counted her knives. Habit was its own kind of faith.

Tonight, in the toll-house with Brakka's snores rattling the rafters and the boy dreaming of his father's warehouse, Paloma knelt on cold flagstones beside the door, out of the firelight, and opened her hands.

*Contractor to contracted,* she began, in the old tongue that tasted of ink and iron. *Your servant keeps the terms. Honest weights—* she thought of the scale in her pack, calibrated monthly, *—honest words—* she thought of the exact truth she'd given Brakka, *—no oath broken, no trust betrayed.*

The silence answered with silence. It always did. But she waited anyway, the full count of ten heartbeats, because the rite required it and because some stubborn animal part of her had never entirely stopped listening for the hand on the back of her neck.

On the eighth heartbeat, the fire popped. On the ninth, the rain thickened against the roof. On the tenth—

Nothing. As always. As for ten years.

*Your servant keeps the terms,* she finished. *Wherever you are. Whatever happened. I keep them.*

She rose, knees aching, and allowed herself one moment—one—to stand in the dark with her eyes closed and feel the shape of the grief, the way you press a bruise to check it's still there. Then she put it away, in the place she kept it, and went back to the bench, and slept with one knife under her hand and one within reach, because faith was habit but survival was discipline.

* * *

Dawn came grey and rinsed. The rain had moved off east, leaving the road shining and the air scrubbed clean enough to see the whole spine of Thornfell laid out against the sky—white-capped, indifferent, beautiful the way a drawn blade is beautiful.

They made good time. Tomas, rested and fed, had recovered his nerve and most of his chatter, and by midmorning he was telling her about his father's business, his sister's betrothal, his plan to buy a mule of his own by season's end. Paloma gave him the occasional grunt, which he correctly interpreted as encouragement, and otherwise kept her attention on the road: the scree slopes above, the treeline, the places where the switchbacks narrowed and a rockfall could become an ambush with no effort at all.

Nothing ambushed them. Nothing ever did, on the days she chose well.

By late afternoon they had come down below the snowline, into country of pine and grey boulder and the first thin farms clinging to the valleysides, and the Ford Road stretched ahead of them, flat and safe and almost disappointingly ordinary. At a stone marker shaped like a leaning finger, Tomas stopped and dug out his purse.

"My father said the balance on delivery." He counted coins into her palm with the solemnity of a boy performing a sacrament. "And, ah. This besides." One extra coin, worn nearly smooth. "For the rain. He said you'd earned it."

Paloma looked at the coin. Eleven years ago, a smooth-worn coin would have gone into the offering box at the next shrine, per the contract's tithe clause. She hadn't skipped a tithe either. The shrines were empty and cold and she paid them anyway, dropping copper into stone boxes that no one emptied, because somewhere, somehow, the accounting must still hold. Someone had to keep the books, if only one side of them.

"He earned it," she said, and closed her fist. "Tell your father the road east of the ford is watched—grey cloaks asking after couriers. Have him send nothing valuable that way this month."

Tomas's eyes widened. "Is there trouble?"

"There's always trouble. Now it has a schedule." She shouldered her pack. "Go home, Tomas. Marry someone who laughs. Buy the mule."

He hesitated, and then—boyish, irrepressible—he stuck out his hand. Paloma regarded it for a moment. Mercenaries didn't shake hands; hands were for work. But she took it anyway, briefly, his palm damp and young against hers.

"Thank you," he said. "Truly. I was so frightened the whole way and you—you made it feel like it would all be fine."

"It was fine because you did what I told you," she said. "Remember that. Fear obeying sense is worth ten swords."

He laughed, waved, and went down the Ford Road at a jog, growing smaller, until the pines took him. Paloma stood at the marker a while longer, alone on the road with the evening coming up gold behind the peaks.

Alone. The word sat differently than usual tonight. She turned it over, prodded it. Eleven years since the god went quiet, and in that time she had built a life that fit through doorways: one pack, four knives, a network of toll-keepers and smugglers' contacts who trusted her exactly as far as her rates and no further. Brakka owed her a cellar. A fence in Vell's Crossing owed her a favor. A hermit on the high bridge owed her a debt older than either. It was, she supposed, a kind of wealth. It was also, she understood with the clarity that sometimes came at day's end, a kind of poverty. Everyone she knew, she knew in transactions. Even her grief was contractual—a vow kept to a silent partner, terms observed, nothing owed back.

*Ilya would say that's enough,* she thought. *Ilya would say a contract kept is its own reward.*

But Ilya had been dead three years now, buried under a cairn on a pass she still avoided, and his saying-so carried less weight each season. And lately—Ilya's frown, that day at the waystation, *say nothing of this*—lately the memory of that frown had begun to itch in new ways. What had he known? What had he not said?

She shook it off. Speculation was a luxury. Dusk was a fact.

She made camp early, in a hollow below the road where a fallen pine made a windbreak and a spring seeped silver out of the rocks. Fire, small and screened. Food, oats and dried mutton and the last of the journey-bread. She ate methodically, cleaned her blades, checked the scale in her pack out of pure ritual, and set the first watch against a tree with her cloak buttoned and her boots on.

The stars came out hard and cold over Thornfell. Somewhere upslope, a wolf spoke to another wolf, two long notes and a pause, and Paloma found herself listening to it longer than she needed to. Wolves kept their contracts, at least. The pack was the pack. Nobody in a wolf-pack woke to find the sky had stopped answering.

She was drifting—not sleeping, drifting, the mercenary's half-sleep that kept one ear on the dark—when the sound came.

Footsteps. On the road above. Wrong rhythm: fast, stumbling, the gait of someone running on legs that had stopped agreeing to the job. Then a cry, short and bitten off, and the heavy sliding crash of a body coming down the embankment through the brush.

Paloma was on her feet with a knife in her hand before the echo settled, pressed into the shadow of the fallen pine, reading the darkness above the road. For a long moment: nothing. Rain-smell. Pine-smell. The spring whispering.

Then, from the tangle of brush at the road's edge, a voice—cracked, wet, human:

"Okonkwo." A cough, terrible and deep. "Paloma Okonkwo. I was told—find the mercenary—find her at the ford—" Another cough. "Please. Before they come. *Before they come.*"

Paloma stood motionless in the dark, knife loose in her grip, and felt ten years of careful, transactional, survivable life tilt beneath her feet like a road giving way.

Nobody knew her name out here. Nobody was supposed to.
