## 2. Blood on the Switchback

The rain had been falling for three days, and Paloma Okonkwo had stopped minding it somewhere around the second.

Rain was good for her trade. Rain washed away tracks, drowned scent, turned the smuggler's roads of Thornfell into ribbons of mud that no honest traveler would touch and no dishonest one could follow. She moved through it the way she'd learned to move through everything—quietly, efficiently, without complaint—and by dusk on the third day she had delivered her cargo: six crates of unmarked steel, three letters in cipher, and one nervous salt merchant who paid double for discretion and got triple his money's worth.

Now she was making camp on a switchback ledge above the Vell gorge, where the mountain dropped away into blackness and the wind came up cold off the water. A small fire, screened by a wall of split stone she'd built herself years ago and rebuilt every season since. Dried venison. A skin of wine cut with melted snow. The ordinary rituals of an invisible life.

She ate with her back to the rock and her eyes on the road below, because that was the first rule Ilya had ever taught her: *eat like a woman being watched, sleep like a woman being hunted, and you'll die old in your bed instead of young in a ditch.*

Ilya. Ten years dead, and still the voice in her head that corrected her posture.

She finished the venison, banked the fire low, and did what she did every night, in every weather, in every corner of the mountains where no one could see her.

She knelt.

Not to anything visible. There was no shrine here, no carved stone, no offering bowl. Just the wet dark and the wind and the small stubborn heat of the fire. Paloma pressed her bare knuckles to the ground—a habit from her novitiate days, when the god of contracts had still been a presence rather than an absence—and spoke the words she'd spoken every night for ten years without answer.

*Witness me. I kept my word today. I took only what was agreed. I broke nothing I swore to hold.*

Silence.

It wasn't the silence of an empty sky. Empty skies were easy; empty skies were just the world being the world. This silence had weight. It pressed against her like a hand over her mouth. Somewhere beneath Thornfell—or beyond it, or inside the marrow of it—her god existed, bound to her by a contract signed in her nineteenth year, and that god had simply... stopped answering. No punishment. No release. No severance, which would at least have been something, would have been a door closing instead of a door left open onto darkness.

Ten years of prayers dropped down a well with no bottom.

*Witness me,* she said again, out of stubbornness more than faith. *And if you won't—if you've gone wherever gods go when they stop caring—then witness yourself, because someone has to keep accounts in this world, and it's clearly not going to be you.*

The wind answered. The rain answered. The gorge breathed its long cold breath.

Paloma rose, wiped the mud from her knuckles, and told herself—as she told herself every night—that tomorrow she wouldn't kneel. That she'd finally let the contract lapse into whatever limbo held dead vows and dead mentors and the girl she had been before both of them failed her.

She never meant it. That was the worst part. Some traitorous chamber of her heart still believed the god was listening, and hated itself for believing, and believed anyway.

She was pulling her cloak around her shoulders when she heard the footsteps.

* * *

They came up the switchback fast and wrong—too heavy on the outside foot, dragging on the turns, the gait of someone running on the last fumes of their body. Paloma killed the fire with a sweep of her boot and slid behind the stone screen, knife already loose in her hand, and watched the road.

A figure staggered into view through the rain. Young. A courier's oilskin, courier's satchel, courier's desperate pace—but no lantern, no escort badge, no colors. An off-the-books runner, then, same as her. He made it three more steps toward her campsite—he couldn't have known it was there; he was following the smell of dead coals, or the instinct of a dying animal seeking any warmth—and then his legs simply quit.

He went down hard on the wet stone, and Paloma heard the sound his knee made and winced.

She gave it ten heartbeats. It might be bait. It had been bait twice before in her career, once with bandits and once with something worse that wore a tax collector's face. But the man wasn't moving anymore except to breathe, and his breathing had the wet rattle in it that Paloma had heard from enough gutted men to recognize across a distance.

She checked the road above and below. Nothing. She checked the treeline. Rain, black pines, rain.

She went to him.

Up close he was even younger than she'd thought—nineteen, twenty, a farm boy's jaw under the hood, chapped lips cracked open around each rattling breath. And he was bleeding. Not from a wound she could see at first; the oilskin hid it until she peeled it back, and then she wished she hadn't. Someone had opened him from hip to ribs with a single stroke, deep and surgical, the kind of cut that took time to kill and was meant to. He'd been running with that in him. Hours, maybe. In this rain. Up these roads.

"Who did this?" she asked, though she was already pressing cloth into the wound, already doing the arithmetic that said the cloth didn't matter.

His eyes found her face. They were gray and enormous and they focused on her with the terrible clarity of the nearly gone.

"You're—" He coughed. Blood came with it, thin and bright. "You're the Okonkwo woman. The road-witch. They said—I was told to find—the one who knows the ways."

"Who told you?"

"Ilya's ghost," he whispered, and laughed, and the laugh cost him so much blood that Paloma felt it leave him under her hands. "No. His notes. His notes said—if anyone comes asking—find the woman who prays to the quiet god."

Every muscle in Paloma's body went still. The rain filled the pause with its indifferent static.

"He knew about my—" She stopped. Swallowed it. "What notes? Whose notes?"

But the boy was past answering questions. His hand crawled out from under the oilskin, shaking, fingers black with cold, and clawed at the satchel strap across his chest. Paloma undid the buckle for him. Inside: a packet wrapped in oilcloth, tied with waxed cord, sealed with a blob of dark red wax stamped with a sigil she didn't recognize—a bell, inverted, with a crack down its crown.

He pushed it into her hands with both of his, as if it weighed as much as a coffin lid.

"The tithe comes due," he said.

"What tithe? Boy—what tithe, whose due—"

"The hundredth year." His teeth were chattering now, or maybe that was death settling in. "They're coming down early this time. Collecting ahead of the feast. Everything owed. Everyone owed. He wrote it all down—" another cough, another bright thread of blood at the corner of his mouth "—he wrote it all down and they burned the house for it, and I ran, and I ran, but they don't stop, they never stop, they're not—"

He looked past her shoulder. At the empty air behind her. Whatever he saw there drained the last color from his face, and Paloma had seen men look at death before, but never quite like that—never with that specific flavor of recognition, as if greeting an acquaintance.

"They're not people," he finished, very quietly.

Then he died. It wasn't dramatic. The rattle slowed, spaced itself out like a failing heartbeat, and stopped between one breath and the next, and the rain went on hitting his open eyes, and Paloma closed them for him with two fingers because no one else was going to.

She sat back on her heels in the mud, the oilcloth packet heavy in her lap, and let herself feel exactly four seconds of grief for a stranger whose name she'd never learn. Then she heard the bells.

* * *

They came down the switchback without sound.

That was the wrong part. That was the part that made the animal brain behind Paloma's eyes scream at her to run before her conscious mind had even identified the threat. Men with bells should make noise. These bells hung from the collectors' belts and staffs and swung with every step, and they made no sound at all—muted bells, or bells that swallowed their own ringing, and somehow that was infinitely worse than any alarm.

There were five of them. They came around the upper turn in single file, tall and narrow, draped in gray oilskins that shed the rain in perfect sheets, hoods drawn so deep that where faces should have been there was only shadow with two points of pale light in it. Not eyes. Paloma refused to call them eyes. Reflections, maybe. Coins at the bottom of a well.

Her body made the decision before she did. She rolled sideways off the road into the rocks, taking the packet with her, dragging the boy's body by the collar so it wouldn't sit alone as evidence of company. Behind the stone screen, in the black wet dark, she became furniture. Became stone. Became nothing.

The collectors reached the campfire's ashes.

One of them crouched—unfolded, really, folding down like a carpenter's rule, joints bending in directions joints shouldn't go—and extended a long gray finger toward the dead coals. Still warm. Paloma watched the pale points of light swing from the coals to the boy's body, and she understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, that they had been following the boy. That the wound in his side had been theirs. That they had let him run because running things led them to other things.

The light-points swung upward. Swept the ledge. Passed across the stone screen where Paloma crouched with her knife against her chest and her heart hammering so loud she was certain it would give her away, certain, certain—

—and stopped.

The lead collector tilted its head. The motion was slow, curious, wrong, like an owl considering a mouse hole. The bells on its belt stirred in the wind and made no sound, and Paloma realized she had stopped breathing entirely, and that her lungs were beginning to burn, and that if she gasped she died, and that she could not hold her breath forever—

The collector rose. Turned. And the five of them continued down the switchback, past her hiding place close enough that she could have touched the nearest hem, close enough that she smelled them—not rot, not corruption, but *cold*, the mineral cold of deep caves, of places sunlight had never taxed—and descended into the gorge and were gone.

Paloma breathed. It came out as a shudder she couldn't control. Her hands were shaking. She looked at them shaking and felt a fury at herself so hot it burned the fear down to embers.

*Ilya.* The name surfaced through the terror like a corpse through floodwater. *Whatever this is, whatever that boy carried—it's yours. Your notes. Your ghost. Your mess.*

She waited a full hour. She was good at waiting; it was most of her profession. Then she built the fire back up, because the boy deserved better than rain on his face, and while the flames caught she unwrapped the oilcloth packet with hands that had almost stopped trembling.

Inside was a map.

Not a map of anywhere Paloma knew, and she knew every fold of Thornfell the way other women knew their mothers' faces. This was drawn on hide, cured thin as paper, inked in a brown that might have been rust and might not. It showed the mountain from the inside—a descending labyrinth of galleries and stairways and flooded halls, marked in a spidery hand, with routes threaded through it in red. And in the margins, in annotations cramped and slantwise and utterly, devastatingly familiar—

Paloma's breath stopped again, and this time it had nothing to do with collectors.

She knew that handwriting. She'd copied it out a thousand times as a girl, practicing her letters under a mentor's critical eye. She'd read it in contracts and margins and letters left tucked under her pillow on the mornings after he'd slipped away before dawn. *Check the knot twice. Trust the river, not the bridge. Eat like a woman being watched.*

Ilya's handwriting. On a map of a place beneath the mountain that Paloma had never believed existed outside of drunk miners' stories. Marked with routes. With *routes*—which meant visits, which meant familiarity, which meant that the man who had taught her every hidden way through Thornfell had known a second country underneath it, and had walked its halls often enough to annotate the roads, and had never, in fifteen years, said one word.

In the lower corner, beside a chamber marked with the inverted-bell sigil from the wax seal, Ilya had written a single line:

*Owed: one soul, held in surety. Do not let them collect.*

Paloma sat by the fire with the map in her hands and the dead boy at her back and the rain coming down like the sky trying to wash the whole mountain clean, and she understood that her life had ended sometime in the last quarter hour. The invisible life—the quiet life, the escorted-cargo-and-melted-snow life, the life where the worst thing that happened in a week was a client who haggled—that life was over. The collectors had seen warm coals. They had seen a body. They would work backward from the body to the roads, and from the roads to the woman who knew them all, and the bells would come for her eventually, silent as they came for everyone.

She should burn the map. That was the smart move. That was the move Ilya himself would have counseled, probably, in one of his infuriating aphorisms—*the wise courier eats the letter*—and she held the edge of the hide over the flames and watched the firelight shine through it, through the red-threaded routes, through the cramped familiar handwriting, through the words *do not let them collect.*

*Do not let them collect.*

Whose soul? Owed by whom? What had you done down there, old man, in the country under the country, and why did a stranger die on my doorstep whispering your name?

The map curled slightly in the heat. Paloma pulled it back.

"No," she said aloud, to the fire, to the boy, to the ten years of silence kneeling inside her. "I'm done eating letters."

She doused the fire properly this time, kicked the ashes apart, and stood over the boy's body a moment longer. "I'll come back for you when it's safe," she lied gently, because the safe time was never coming and they both would have known it. "Rest."

Then Paloma Okonkwo took the first road south—the narrow one, the goat-track one, the one that existed in no ledger and no memory but hers—and ran.

Behind her, far below in the gorge, faint as a thought, the bells began to ring.
