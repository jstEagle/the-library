## 3. What the Map Shows

The shrine had burned a long time ago.

Paloma could tell by the way the black had weathered off the stones, by the saplings shouldering up through the cracked flagstones, by the fact that the god's statue had no face left at all—just a smooth oval of scorched granite where features used to be. Whoever the shrine had honored, the fire had taken his name along with his face, and the forest had moved in afterward and made itself at home among the bones of him.

It was as good a place to hide as any. Better than most, because nobody prayed at dead shrines, and nobody hunted in them either.

She'd run through the dark for three hours after the switchback, off every trail she knew, doubling back twice through a streambed so cold it ached in her boot seams, until the collectors' lantern-songs faded behind her into the ordinary noises of Thornfell at night—wind in the pines, something small dying somewhere, rain beginning again with that thin, patient insistence the mountain rain had, like it was being paid by the hour.

Now she crouched inside the gutted nave with a shuttered candle stub burning low between her knees, and the courier's map lay open on a flat stone before her like a thing dissected.

She hadn't dared look at it on the road. On the road you looked at nothing; on the road you were ears and feet and the space between heartbeats. But here, behind walls that had already survived their worst day, with rain drumming the broken roof and washing out every sound she might have made, she allowed herself to look.

The map was good work. That was the first thing she noticed, and the first thing that frightened her. It wasn't a smuggler's scrawl, all crooked lines and remembered landmarks. It was surveyed. The passes were drawn in elevation hatching, the rivers gauged, the distances marked in hours rather than miles—which was the only honest way to measure mountain country. Someone had walked every road on this vellum with a chain and a compass and a great deal of patience, and then someone else had hidden it inside a courier's satchel and gotten a man killed for carrying it.

*The tithe comes due.*

Paloma turned the map toward the candlelight and began, methodically, to read it the way she read everything: edges first, then structure, then the details that didn't want to be found.

The edges told her the scope. Thornfell entire, from the salt caves below Greywater to the high pass they called the Widow's Teeth, and past them—this was the part that made her go still—the interior. No smuggler's map showed the interior of Thornfell. The mountains' heart was a blank on every chart she'd ever stolen or bought or memorized, a white silence where cartographers wrote *here be old debts* if they wrote anything at all. This map filled the silence in. Roads ran down into it like roots. Switchbacks descended past the tree line into country that had no name, spiraling inward and *down*, and at the center, drawn in ink so fine it was almost a whisper, was a gate.

Not a door. A gate—a formal thing, rendered with care, flanked by two pillars and a set of stairs that went down farther than the map's border allowed the artist to show. Below the stairs, the artist had simply given up on architecture and written, in a cramped hand:

*the court beneath. do not enter in debt.*

Paloma read it four times. Her breath fogged in the cold air of the shrine, and each time it cleared, the words were still there.

*Do not enter in debt.*

She knew about debts. She carried one the size of a god's silence, ten years deep and interest accruing. She knew what it was to owe something that couldn't be weighed in coin. But this was the first time she'd seen those words written as a warning—as if debt were a toll, a condition of entry, a currency.

As if the court beneath the mountain did its accounting in souls.

She sat back on her heels. Rain ticked against the roof beams overhead, finding the gaps the fire had left. Somewhere outside, an owl asked its one question and got no answer, which felt appropriate.

"Right," she said quietly, to the faceless god, because talking to herself was a habit she'd stopped fighting years ago. "Let's see who drew you."

She worked inward from the edges. The main routes were marked in brown ink—trade roads, tithe-roads, the veins along which the mountain's commerce flowed, legal and otherwise. She recognized half of them from her own years of running cargo. There was the Salt Road, there the ford at Vetch Crossing, there the long climb to the Widow's Teeth where the wind cut like a debtor's knife. All familiar. All safe to ignore.

Then there were the annotations.

They were in a different hand entirely—sharper, slanted forward as if the writer were always leaning into the next word, written in a rust-brown ink that some part of Paloma's mind identified as blood-and-iron-gall before the rest of her was ready to admit why it looked familiar. The annotations crawled over the map like ivy over a corpse. Marginal notes. Corrections. Little arrows redirecting routes around landslides that must have fallen decades ago. And beside the gate beneath the mountain, in that same sharp slanted hand, a passage in cipher—not the merchants' cipher, not the guild cipher, but a private one, idiosyncratic, built out of symbols Paloma had seen exactly one other place in her life.

On the flyleaf of Ilya Vosk's personal ledger, which he'd let her copy characters from when she was nine years old, telling her it was a game, telling her *a contract you can't read is a cage with the door painted on*, laughing his quiet laugh while she scratched the letters into frost on the window of their rented room in Kesselmark.

Paloma did not move for a long time.

The candle guttered. Rain came harder, sheeting off the broken roof, and still she knelt there with her hands flat on the stone on either side of the map, the way you'd pin down a snake, and stared at handwriting she would have known blindfolded, drunk, dead.

Ilya had been gone eleven years. She'd watched the fever take him, had held his hand through the last of it, had heard his final words—which were, in full, *check the south cache, girl, and don't trust anyone who smiles first*. She'd buried him under a cairn above Greywater and spent a year afterward waiting for grief to make sense, and it never had, and eventually she'd decided that was just what grief was: a contract with terms nobody explained.

And now he was writing to her from under a cairn and eleven years of silence, in cipher, on a map that had gotten a courier murdered.

"You absolute bastard," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "You absolute, careful, *careful* bastard."

Because that was the thing—the annotations weren't panicked. They were meticulous. This was Ilya at his best, the man who'd taught her that paranoia was just planning with better posture. He'd known these roads. He'd walked them. He'd corrected the surveyor's errors in places no surveyor could have survived to err in, which meant he'd been *down there*, past the tree line, past the gate, into the white silence at the heart of every honest map.

He'd been into the court beneath the mountain. And he'd come back out. And he'd never said a word.

Paloma began breaking the cipher.

It took her two hours, and she was rusty, and twice she had to stop and press the heels of her hands against her eyes because the shape of a particular character was so exactly his—the little hook he put on the symbol for *debt*, like a fisherman's curve, like something caught—that grief ambushed her sideways when she least wanted it. But she'd been copying his letters since she was nine. The door he'd painted opened onto a room she still knew.

The first decoded line read:

*Entry permitted only to the owed or the owing. They weigh you at the gate. Bring nothing you cannot account for.*

The second:

*The Lord Beneath keeps what is pledged. There is no statute of limitation below. There is no appeal. There is only the ledger.*

The third made her stop breathing for a moment:

*I have settled my account. What remains outstanding is not mine. If you are reading this, little bird, then it has come to you, and I am sorry, and I was always going to be sorry, and I did it anyway. Forgive me the way the river forgives the ford: by carrying it.*

Paloma read it five times. Then six. Then she got up, walked to the far wall of the ruined shrine, and put her fist through what remained of a stained-glass window, and stood there with her bleeding knuckles hanging at her side, watching rain run red-diluted off her fingers onto the faceless god's cracked altar.

*What remains outstanding is not mine.*

Not his. Hers. Or someone's. A debt he'd known about, accounted for, and left standing—left *transferring*, the way debts did, the way contracts did, flowing downhill to whoever inherited the ledger, the roads, the name. To her. He'd spent her whole childhood teaching her to read contracts, and the last contract he ever wrote had made her the collateral.

She thought of the courier dying against her at the campfire, pressing the map into her hands with the last of his strength, whispering *the tithe comes due* like a man passing along a bill he'd been handed too late to dispute. The collectors coming up the switchback minutes later, patient as rain, killing without hurry because hurry was for people who feared consequences. Ten years of a silent god and a life lived in the cracks of the mountain, and all of it—all of it—had been the long shadow of a signature Ilya had put down in a court she'd never heard of, for a price she couldn't yet see.

"What did you sell?" she asked the empty shrine. Her voice cracked on the last word, and she hated it. "What did you *sell*, old man?"

The faceless god said nothing. Gods were good at that. Hers had had a decade of practice.

* * *

She worked the rest of the night, because work was the only prayer she had left.

By candle stub and then by the grey pre-dawn leaking through the broken roof, she decoded the remaining annotations and committed them to memory, then burned her scratch-notes in the altar brazier one strip at a time, feeding them to the flame like offerings. The map itself she studied until she could redraw it from memory, and then folded along its original creases and hid it in the false seam of her left boot, against the ankle, where she'd carried important things since she was fifteen.

The picture that emerged was worse than she'd hoped and clearer than she'd feared.

The court beneath the mountain was real, and old, and hungry in a very specific way. It did not take coin—Ilya's notes were dismissive on the subject, *they mint their own, it spends like snow*. It took pledges. Souls, memories, names, years—the annotations used all the words, sometimes interchangeably, sometimes with distinctions that made Paloma's skin crawl. Everything was collateral. Everything was entered in the ledger. And once a decade—she found the notation, a small neat mark repeated at regular intervals down the margin like stitches in a wound—the tithe came due, and the collectors came up out of the ground, and the mountain's people paid what they owed or the mountain collected it themselves.

The courier hadn't been carrying a map. He'd been carrying evidence. Routes in, the gate's conditions, the cipher that proved a human being had entered the court and returned—and somewhere in Ilya's careful, slanted hand, the location of whatever remained of his own account. His cache. His confession.

*Check the south cache, girl.*

South was the direction she'd been running anyway. South was the ford at Vetch Crossing, and beyond it the salt caves, and beyond those the foothills where the mountain's grip loosened and a person could almost believe the world was bigger than Thornfell.

South was also, according to the map's margins, in Ilya's hand, in characters she now understood: *If they send the wolf, do not run. Wolves remember roads. Run from anything else.*

Paloma frowned at that one. She'd decoded it twice to be sure. *If they send the wolf.* As if the court kept wolves. As if Ilya had known, eleven years ago, exactly what would be sent after this map, and had left instructions for it the way he'd left instructions for everything—calm, precise, leaning into the next word.

She looked at the faceless god.

"I'm going to find out what he did," she told it. Not a vow. She didn't make vows anymore; vows were contracts, and contracts needed two living parties. Just a statement of intent, spoken aloud in a dead shrine to a deaf idol, which was the only kind of honesty that came free in Thornfell.

"And then I'm going to decide whether to forgive him."

Outside, the rain had stopped. The sky over the eastern ridgeline was going the color of old pewter, and the birds were starting up in the burned trees around the shrine, tentative, testing whether the world was safe to sing in. Paloma snuffed the candle, rolled her shoulders until they cracked, checked her knives out of habit, and stepped out through the gap where the door had been.

The road south ran down through the wet pines toward Vetch Crossing, eight hours' walk if the mud was kind, and the mud was never kind. Somewhere down there was a ford, and a cache, and answers with teeth. Somewhere behind her, she had no doubt, the collectors were regrouping and counting their dead and revising the price on her head upward.

She went south.

Halfway down the first switchback, she paused—some instinct older than training, the animal at the base of her brain that had kept her alive through ten silent years—and looked back the way she'd come, at the grey light and the dripping trees and the ruin of the shrine disappearing into the mist.

Far off, at the edge of hearing, down in the valley she'd have to cross, something howled.

Long. Low. Not quite like a wolf.

Like something remembering how.
