## 4. The Wolf at the Ford

The river came out of the mountains the way all Thornfell water did—cold enough to hurt, grey-green with glacier silt, and loud as a congregation of angry priests. Paloma crouched at the treeline above the ford and watched it for a long time before she moved.

Three days since the courier died against her shoulder with her blood on his lips and his blood on her hands. Three days since she'd killed two men in the dark and run east with a dead man's map burning a hole against her ribs like a coal sewn into her coat. In three days she had slept perhaps four hours total, and every one of those hours had been punctuated by dreams of wax seals and Ilya's handwriting crawling across the page like insects.

She was being followed. She'd known it since yesterday.

Not by the tithe-collectors—they were behind her, back in the low country, licking their wounds or reporting to whatever passed for their master. This was something else. Something that kept pace with her without ever quite showing itself. Twice she'd found tracks: broad paw-prints in soft ground, too large for any wolf native to these slopes, spaced in a gait that suggested an animal moving at an easy lope for hours without tiring. Once, on the second night, she'd heard a howl that raised the hair on her arms—not because it was close, but because it sounded *wrong*. Too structured. Too much like language.

So now she sat above the ford and did what smugglers did best: nothing, patiently, until the world made a mistake.

The ford itself was a wide shelf of submerged stone where the river spread shallow over half a hundred paces. On the far side, the trail climbed toward the salt caves and the high passes beyond—the only route to the court beneath the mountain that didn't go through territory held by the tithe-collectors. The map said so. Ilya's annotations said so, in that careful clerk's hand she'd have known anywhere, the hand that had taught her to write her own name.

*You sold something,* she thought at the map, at him, at the grave she'd never been able to afford to visit. *You sold something down there, and now men are dying in the rain because of it.*

The rain had stopped an hour ago. Mist rose off the water in slow ribbons. Somewhere upstream, a dipper worked the shallows, its song thin and bright as wire.

Paloma checked her crossbow, checked the knife at her hip and the one in her boot, and started down toward the water.

She was halfway across when the wolf hit her.

There was no warning growl, no circling, none of the theater wolves were supposed to perform. One moment she was picking her footing across the wet stone, the next the air behind her tore open with a sound like a banner snapping in a gale, and eighty kilos of grey muscle came down on her back.

She went under. The river took her the way rivers do—completely, instantly, filling her nose and ears and dragging her sideways with a strength no animal could match. Her crossbow was gone. Her knife-hand was pinned somewhere beneath her. Above her, distorted through churning green water, the shape of the wolf was a vast dark shadow, jaws closing on the strap of her pack—

And then the shadow *came apart*.

It happened between one heartbeat and the next. Bone lengthened and shortened, fur poured away like smoke off a doused fire, limbs stretched and thinned and reshaped themselves into arms and legs. What surfaced beside her, coughing and flailing in the shallows, was not a wolf at all.

It was a young woman. Naked, soaked, maybe twenty years old, with pale hair plastered flat to her skull and eyes so light a grey they were nearly white. She was shaking—not from cold, though the river would freeze anyone to the bone inside a minute—and she was looking at Paloma with an expression that had no violence left in it at all. Only terror.

"Give it back," the woman gasped. Water streamed from her mouth. "Please. Please give it back."

Paloma came up out of the water with her boot knife already drawn, because she was not a woman who drowned politely. She put the riverbank at her back and the stranger in front of her and leveled the blade, and only then did she let herself register what she'd just seen.

A shifter. A full-blooded one, not one of the part-blood hedge-witches who could manage a dog's shape on a good night. Thornfell had legends about them—whole packs that had gone into the deep mountains a century ago and never come out. She'd assumed they were stories, the kind old women told to keep children from wandering.

"You were going to kill me," Paloma said. Her voice came out flat and steady, which surprised her, given that her heart was trying to break through her ribs.

"No." The woman's teeth were chattering. "No—I wasn't—I don't—" She pressed both hands over her face. "I don't know."

"You don't know whether you were trying to kill me."

"I don't know why I *did* anything!" It came out cracked, almost a scream, swallowed immediately by the roar of the river. The woman lowered her hands. Up close, in the flat grey light, there was something wrong with her eyes beyond their color—a hollowness, like rooms in a house from which the furniture had been sold. "I followed you for two days. I don't remember deciding to. I saw the map when you read it at your fire, the night the men came, and something in me just—*knew* it. Knew it the way you know your own name." She swallowed. "Do you know your own name? I'm not certain I know mine."

Paloma didn't lower the knife. But she didn't use it either, which by her own accounting was practically a embrace.

"The courier," she said. "The man who died at my fire. He carried this map. You knew him?"

"I don't know anyone." The words came fast now, tumbling. "That's the whole—that's what I've been trying to tell the trees and the rocks and the river for however long it's been. I woke up in the high forest with snow in my hair and nothing in my head. Nothing. Just pieces. A sister laughing. A den burning. A table where someone signed something, and ink, so much ink, and a voice saying *the court accepts the terms.*" Her breath hitched. "I sold something. I don't remember selling it, but I know I sold it, and I know the map leads to where they keep what I sold, and you have it, and I need it back."

The river roared. The dipper sang somewhere, indifferent. Paloma stood in water to her knees with a knife in her hand and considered, with the cold clarity that ten years of trusting no one had honed in her, exactly how many ways this could be a trap.

It could be a trap. The tithe-collectors had beasts; everyone knew that much. They had things that wore shapes and things that wore faces and things that wore neither. A naked girl who shifted from a wolf mid-leap and then wept in a freezing river could be bait wrapped around a hook, and Paloma would deserve what she got for biting.

But.

But the eyes. You could fake a lot of things—you could fake tears, fake fear, fake a whole history—but Paloma had spent a decade reading people whose lives depended on deception, and she had never once seen that particular hollowness faked. That wasn't performance. That was a person looking out through the windows of a house that had been stripped bare, seeing their own reflection in the emptiness and not recognizing it.

And underneath the hollow, when the woman spoke of the map, there was something else. Recognition. Not of the paper—of what it pointed to. A compass needle finding north.

"What's your name?" Paloma asked.

"I told you—"

"You said you weren't certain. Try anyway."

The woman closed her eyes. Her brow furrowed, and Paloma watched her reach into that stripped-bare house and grope along the walls. When she opened her eyes again, they were wet, and not from the river.

"Ulyana," she said slowly, tasting it. "My mother used to shout it up the valley when it got dark. Ulyana. That's—it's mine. I'm sure it's mine." A ghost of something crossed her face—not a smile, but the place where a smile used to live. "It's the only thing I'm sure of."

*Ulyana.* Paloma turned the name over once and set it down carefully, the way you'd handle a coin you suspected was cursed.

"Here's what happens now," she said. She waded to the bank, keeping the knife visible, and pulled herself up onto the stones. "You're going to stay on the far side of the ford. You're going to shift back into whatever keeps you warm, because hypothermia kills faster than I do. And I'm going to walk up that trail alone, and if you follow me, I will put a bolt in you at forty paces, and I won't feel bad about it."

The woman—Ulyana—stared at her from the water. Then, absurdly, she laughed. It was a small, broken sound, one note, quickly smothered, but it was real.

"You're lying," Ulyana said.

"I beg your pardon?"

"You're lying. About not feeling bad about it." She hauled herself out of the river a few paces downstream, water sheeting off her, and stood shivering on the stones with her arms wrapped around herself, utterly unashamed and utterly vulnerable, which was somehow more disarming than either. "You watched me shift and you didn't run. You had every chance to put that knife in me while I was coughing up half the river, and you didn't. You're angry about it. I can see you being angry about it. But you're not going to shoot me."

"You know nothing about me."

"I know you talk to your god at night." Ulyana's voice went quiet. "I heard you, the first night. Before the men came. You prayed out loud, even though he doesn't answer. I could hear it from the ridge." She tilted her head, and for a moment the hollow eyes had something in them—pity, maybe, or recognition. "You pray like someone keeping a promise to a person who's already dead."

The words landed harder than the wolf had. Paloma felt them go in under the armor, past the leather and the scar tissue and the decade of practiced indifference, and find the soft thing underneath that she had named, privately, only ever at night: *hope.*

"That," she said, "was the last time you spy on me."

"I wasn't spying. Wolves hear everything. It's not optional." Ulyana hugged herself tighter. "Please. I'll follow you anyway. I want you to understand that—I'll follow you whether you let me or not, because I have literally nowhere else in the world to go, and that map is the only thread connecting me to anything I've lost. But I'd rather you let me. I'd rather—" Her voice caught. She steadied it with visible effort. "I'd rather not be alone. I've been alone in my own head for longer than I can measure, and it turns out the head is the worst place to be alone."

Paloma looked at her.

She looked at the goosefleshed shoulders and the mud on the woman's knees and the way her weight kept shifting like she couldn't remember how stillness worked in this body. She looked at the empty eyes and the single recovered name, held like a candle flame cupped in two hands. And she thought about Ilya, who had taught her that mercy was a currency you couldn't afford, and about the courier dying with *the tithe comes due* on his lips, and about the ledger she hadn't found yet but could already feel waiting for her beneath the mountain, full of names and prices and her mentor's careful hand.

*Ilya brokered deals with that court. This woman sold something to that court. Those facts are going to meet each other eventually, and I am going to be standing between them.*

"Get dressed," Paloma said.

Ulyana blinked. "What?"

"You're freezing, and I'm not explaining to whoever finds your body why the mercenary let a naked woman die of cold out of spite." She shrugged off her pack, dug past the oilcloth and the rations, and threw a bundle at the other woman: spare shirt, wool stockings, her oldest cloak, the one with the burned hem. "They'll be enormous on you. Complain quietly."

Ulyana caught the bundle against her chest and stared at it as if it might be another trick of the shifting blood—as if it might dissolve back into fur and teeth. Then she looked up at Paloma, and the expression on her face was so raw, so nakedly grateful, that Paloma had to turn away and busy herself with wringing out her coat.

"Why?" Ulyana asked, into her back. "Why help me?"

"I'm not helping you. I'm acquiring a liability I can't shake." Paloma twisted the water out of the wool and didn't turn around. "You'll slow me down. You'll get us both killed. You'll probably eat me in my sleep at some point. And if the tithe-collectors catch us, they'll take the map and give us both to whatever lives under that mountain, and I will spend my last breath telling you I told you so."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer you're getting." She shouldered the ruined pack. The map pressed against her ribs, warm as a living thing. "We move in five minutes. There's a salt cave two miles up the trail—we sleep there tonight, and tomorrow I teach you how to walk without leaving a sign, how to listen without being heard, and how to lie, because you are catastrophically bad at it. If you can't learn, I leave you at the hermit's bridge and that's the end of it."

Behind her, cloth rustled. When Paloma finally turned, Ulyana stood swamped in the old cloak, hood up, grey eyes watching her from its shadow—and for one strange moment the image doubled in Paloma's mind, wolf and woman laid over each other, the same watchful stillness in both.

"One more question," Ulyana said.

"Make it fast."

"The men who came to your fire. The ones you killed." No accusation in it—only curiosity, and something colder underneath. "They were collecting. For the court. Weren't they?"

"They were."

"Then we're hunting the same debt." Ulyana stepped up onto the trail beside her, close enough that Paloma could smell river-water and wet wool and, faintly, underneath it, something wild—pine sap and cold stone, the smell of the high forest. "Whatever I sold, and whatever your dead friend sold, it's all in the same vault. So you may as well stop pretending you're dragging me along as ballast." The ghost of the smile lived again, briefly, at the corner of her mouth. "Partners. Or at least, fellow thieves."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment. Then she snorted—an actual, involuntary sound, the first one she'd made in days that wasn't pain or prayer—and started up the trail.

"Keep up, wolf," she said. "And if you hear bells in the night, wake me before you panic. Bells mean collectors."

Far above them, at the lip of the pass, the mist parted for a moment—and through it, briefly, Paloma saw the mountain's shoulder, and carved into its black rock, a door.

It was open.

Someone had gone in ahead of them.
