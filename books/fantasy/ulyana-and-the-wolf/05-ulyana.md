## 5. Ulyana

Ulyana remembered fire.

Not the fire itself—she had tried, a hundred times in the dark of the last three years, to summon it whole. What she kept was only the *shape* of it: orange light crawling up a wooden wall, the smell of pine pitch and scorched fur, someone screaming her name in a voice she could no longer put a face to. The memory was like a coat with the lining torn out. She could hold it, wear it, feel its weight—but everything warm had been picked away.

She sat across the dead campfire from the mercenary and watched the woman sleep sitting up, which Ulyana had learned was something soldiers did, and which she thought must be very lonely.

The mercenary's name was Paloma Okonkwo. Ulyana knew this because she'd said it aloud at the ford, half-begging, half-threatening, and the woman had answered with a knife at her throat and her name given like a coin tossed to a beggar: *here, now leave me alone.* It hadn't worked. Nothing the mercenary did seemed designed to make people leave her alone; it seemed designed to make sure that if they stayed, they would regret it.

Ulyana had decided, somewhere in the last two days of walking behind her, that she didn't care.

She pulled her knees up under the borrowed wool blanket—the mercenary had grudgingly surrendered it when Ulyana started shivering so hard her teeth chattered into bird-sounds—and looked east, where Thornfell's peaks cut black notches out of the stars. Somewhere under all that stone was a door. Somewhere under all that stone was the rest of her life.

*The tithe comes due.* The courier had died saying it, and Paloma had gone white as bone-meal, and Ulyana had understood then that the words meant something even to a woman who sold her sword instead of her soul.

*You don't know what you sold,* the mercenary had told her on the second morning, not unkindly, which for Paloma Okonkwo was practically a lullaby. *That's what frightens me.*

But that wasn't true. That was the cruel joke of it, the thing nobody who still owned themselves could understand. Ulyana knew exactly what she'd sold. She just couldn't remember it.

* * *

Three winters ago there had been a den in the high country above the Vell, forty wolves and as many shifters, her mother's mother's pack, older than any border drawn on any map. And there had been a winter that came down like a judgment—snow to the eaves, game gone silent or gone stiff, the little ones crying themselves too weak to cry.

And there had been a man who came through the snow without leaving footprints.

Ulyana remembered him perfectly, because he was one of the few things the court had let her keep. They always left you the transaction. That was the price of the price: you had to remember agreeing, or the contract wouldn't hold. She remembered his grey gloves. She remembered the way the firelight bent around him wrong, the way her grandmother went flat and quiet when he spoke, the way the pack's oldest hunters stood between him and the children with their ears back and their hands already shaking.

*One soul,* he had said. *Yours. Not your life—we have no use for your body, girl, and your wolf is more trouble than she's worth. Your memories. The ones that make you you. We'll take them gently, and your pack eats until spring.*

Her grandmother had wept. Ulyana remembered that too—the old woman's tears freezing in the creases of her face while she argued law with a thing in grey gloves, citing compacts older than the mountain, and the thing listening politely and answering, every time, the same way:

*The debt stands. The tithe comes due. One soul, freely pledged, or forty graves by thaw.*

Freely. There was the word they hid behind. Freely, when the alternative was watching her baby cousins turn blue in the night. Freely, when her father had already frozen on a hunt and her mother followed him a week later, and the pack looked at Ulyana—seventeen, strong, the fastest shifter in three generations—with eyes full of terrible arithmetic.

She had knelt in the snow and said yes.

She remembered the yes. She would always remember the yes. It was carved into her somewhere deeper than memory, a groove worn into the bedrock of her, and everything that had been *above* that groove—her mother's lullabies, the name of the boy she'd been going to mate in spring, the taste of her grandmother's smoked fish, the sound of her own laughter—had been lifted out of her like marrow sucked from a bone.

They hadn't taken her sister.

That was the other thing. The thing she circled every night like a moth against glass. The grey-gloved man had looked at the small dark-haired girl hiding behind their grandmother's legs and said, *one soul per household, by the old terms,* and Ulyana had thrown herself between them and said *take mine, take all of it, take everything,* and he had smiled—she remembered the smile, she hated that she remembered the smile—and said, *already done.*

And her sister's name was—

It was—

There. Every time. A blank wall where a name should be, smooth and white and utterly featureless, and behind it, faint as heat-shimmer, a laugh. High and bright and slightly off-key, the kind of laugh that made other people laugh whether they wanted to or not. Her sister laughed like that. Ulyana knew it the way you know a scar is yours. She couldn't hear it anymore. She could only remember that she used to be able to.

* * *

The mercenary stirred. Ulyana went still, the way she did around anything that might bite.

Paloma Okonkwo slept like a cat on a fence—eyes shut, but the whole architecture of her awake underneath. Even now, even genuinely asleep, her hand lay six inches from the knife on her thigh. Ulyana had watched her pray the first night, kneeling in the mud with her head bowed over a worn iron sigil, lips moving around words that got no answer. She prayed like someone knocking on a door they knew was empty. She prayed anyway.

*Who did you lose?* Ulyana wanted to ask her. *What did they take from you, that you talk to a god who doesn't talk back?*

But asking questions like that of Paloma Okonkwo was like knocking on a fortress gate. You'd get a crossbow bolt and a bill for the arrow.

So instead, when the mercenary's eyes opened—grey, quick, already cataloguing the treeline before they found Ulyana's face—Ulyana said, "Tell me about the map again."

"No."

"You've told me nothing about the map."

"Exactly." Paloma sat up in one motion, no wasted movement, and began kicking dirt over the coals. "Eat. We move in ten minutes. There's a stream bed that runs blind past the ridge scouts, and I want to be in it before light."

"I'm not going anywhere until—" Ulyana heard her own voice crack and hated it, hated how young it made her sound, and pushed on anyway because the wall inside her chest was right there, right now, and she was tired of walking around it. "I need to know what Ilya wrote about me. On the map. In the margins. You read it at the shrine. I saw your face."

Paloma's hands stopped moving over the dead fire.

"You saw my face," the mercenary repeated, flat.

"You looked like someone had walked over your grave. So either my soul is worth less than I thought, or—" Ulyana swallowed. "Or it's worth exactly what I thought, and knowing that is worse than not knowing."

For a long moment the only sound was wind moving through the black pines and the tick of cooling embers. Then Paloma did something Ulyana had never seen her do: she hesitated. Visibly. Like a woman standing at the edge of ice she didn't trust.

"Ilya Vetrov was my mentor," she said finally. "He taught me roads, contracts, how to stay alive and invisible. He's been dead four years. His handwriting is all over that map—not just directions. Annotations. Prices." Her jaw tightened. "He brokered for the under-court. Souls. Yours may have been one of his sales."

The words landed in Ulyana's chest like stones dropped down a well. She counted the seconds until they hit bottom.

"He *sold* me."

"He *recorded* you. Whether he sold you or just kept the books—" Paloma shrugged, and the shrug was a lie, and both of them knew it. "Either way, he knew your name. He knew what you paid and what they gave. It'll be in the margins."

Ulyana stood up. The blanket slid off her shoulders and she didn't notice. Something was happening in her body that she recognized distantly, from the outside, the way you recognize weather coming—the pressure drop, the tightness in the jaw, the wolf rising under her skin like water coming to a boil. Her hands had become paws without her deciding it. Claws scored little crescents in her own palms.

"Hey." Paloma was on her feet too, and her voice had changed—not softer, never softer, but level, the voice of someone talking down a spooked horse. "Hey. Wolf-girl. Look at me. Shifting here, now, with collectors on our trail, gets us both killed. Breathe."

"They took my *sister's name*," Ulyana said, and was horrified to find she was crying, hot and furious, the tears steaming faintly in the cold. "Do you understand? Not my sister. Her *name*. I can see her face if I try—I can almost see her face—it's like looking at her through river ice. And some dead man wrote down what she cost in a *margin*, next to the price of a smuggler's pass, and you've been carrying it for two days and reading it by firelight and you didn't think I deserved—"

"I didn't think you deserved to learn it from me at midnight on a mountainside," Paloma snapped, and for the first time since the ford, something raw showed through the armor. "You want to know what I deserve? I deserve a god who answers. Ten years I've prayed into a hole in the world. Ten years. So don't stand there and tell me what people deserve, girl, because I am the last person alive qualified to say."

The words hung in the cold air between them.

Ulyana breathed. In, out. The claws receded. The wolf settled back beneath her ribs, growling low, unwilling but obedient to the shape of the woman who carried her.

"I'm sorry," she said.

"Don't be sorry. Be quiet, and walk, and don't die." Paloma turned away, shouldering her pack, and Ulyana thought that was the end of it—that the fortress gate had slammed, conversation over, toll collected.

Then, without turning around, the mercenary said: "The margin note says *female shifter, approx. seventeen, den above the Vell. Pledged freely. Price: one winter's provision, forty souls' weight in grain and meat. Quality of goods: exceptional.*"

Ulyana's breath stopped.

"*Exceptional,*" Paloma repeated quietly. "Whatever you gave them, they considered it the best thing they'd bought in a century. That's why the courier was running. That's why the tithe-collectors haven't stopped hunting since the ford. They're not chasing a map, Ulyana. They're chasing *you*. The court doesn't let go of goods like that."

The stars wheeled slowly overhead. Somewhere far below them, in the black folds of the foothills, a dog barked twice and went silent—the kind of silence that meant someone had hushed it.

Ulyana wrapped the blanket back around her shoulders. Her hands were steady now. Steadier than they'd been in three years, actually, because for the first time since the grey gloves and the snow, someone had told her the truth, ugly and complete, and she was still standing.

"Then we'd better get there before they do," she said.

Paloma glanced back at her, and in the starlight Ulyana caught something flicker across the mercenary's face—too fast to name, gone before it landed. Almost like respect. Almost like fear.

"Stream bed," Paloma said. "Stay behind me. Step where I step."

They went down into the dark together, and behind them, on the ridgeline they'd left an hour ago, three lanterns lit in perfect silence, spaced evenly apart, like bells waiting to be rung.
