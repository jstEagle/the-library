## 6. Tithe-Collectors

The town was called Vetch's Crossing, and it smelled like every border town Paloma had ever bled in: wet wool, tallow smoke, and the sour-sweet rot of fruit going over in the barrels no one could afford to buy. Rain had followed them down out of the high passes for two days, and by the time they came through the palisade gate under a sky the color of dirty pewter, Ulyana was shivering so hard her teeth clicked.

"We eat," Paloma said. "We resupply. We're gone before dark."

Ulyana looked at her with that particular expression she'd perfected in four days of travel—the one that said *I hear you* and *I intend to do something else entirely*. "And if we see a bathhouse?"

"You'll want to stay in it until your skin wrinkles off."

"And you'll want to keep walking." The girl smiled. Rain dripped from the ends of her hair, and she pushed it back with both hands like a child shaking off a river. "You know what I remember about baths? Nothing. I remember nothing about baths. Which means I've never had one worth remembering, and I refuse to die without correcting that."

Paloma didn't answer. She was watching the gate guards.

They weren't watching the road behind them, which is what gate guards were supposed to do when collectors might be following. They were watching *her*. Both of them. One of them turned away too quickly when her eyes found his, and the other made a small motion with his hand—two fingers flicking toward the tavern row—that might have been nothing at all.

It wasn't nothing. Paloma had spent fifteen years reading men's hands the way other people read weather.

"Change of plan," she said quietly. "No taverns. We take the alley behind the chandler's, we buy bread and salt pork from the woman who sells it out her window, and we cross the ford north of town where there's no toll and no eyes."

"The bathhouse—"

"Forget the bathhouse."

Ulyana's smile faded. She'd learned, in four days, to read Paloma's silences almost as well as Paloma read hands. "They're here already."

"They're here already."

The chandler's alley stank of rendered fat and old wax, and the rain came down harder, drumming on the lean-to roofs and turning the packed mud to soup. The bread-woman took Paloma's coin through her shutters without looking at her face, which was exactly what Paloma paid for. Ulyana crouched under the eave, tearing the loaf, and for a moment—one stupid, soft moment—Paloma let herself watch the girl eat like eating was a pleasure instead of a necessity, crumbs on her chin, steam rising off her shoulders, alive in a way that made the whole gray street seem briefly lit from inside.

Then the bells started.

Not the chapel bell. Vetch's Crossing had a second bell, up in the toll-tower above the palisade, rung only for fire or flood or the arrival of someone the town was not permitted to refuse. It rang three times, paused, rang three times again. Paloma had heard it once before, nine years ago, in a different town, on a night she still dreamed about.

"That's not for us," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question, but her voice shook anyway.

"It's for us."

Down at the mouth of the alley, where it opened onto the market square, the crowd was parting. Not fleeing—parting, the way water parts around a keel, everyone suddenly finding urgent business elsewhere, stalls closing, shutters coming down with a clatter like a flock of startled birds. And through the gap walked three figures in oilcloth cloaks the color of iron slag, hoods back, faces bare, because tithe-collectors never hid their faces. Hiding implied shame, and shame implied law above them.

Paloma knew the one in front. Not his name—collectors traded their names away as part of their office—but his face. She'd seen it across a smuggler's campfire two nights after the courier died, lit orange by a burning wagon, right before she put a knife into the throat of the man beside him. This one had watched her do it and hadn't moved. Hadn't blinked. He'd simply turned and walked back into the dark, and Paloma had told herself that meant he was afraid.

He did not look afraid now.

"Sef," Ulyana breathed beside her.

Paloma's head snapped around. "You know him?"

"I—" Ulyana pressed a hand to her temple, hard, the way she did when she was trying to squeeze a memory through whatever wall the under-court had built inside her skull. "I know the name. I don't know why I know the name. He's—he was at the door. When I sold. He was the one who counted out what I got. He smiled at me like a priest." Her hand dropped. Her eyes were wide and wet. "Paloma, he smiled at me like a *priest*."

"Get behind me."

"No. No, listen—" Ulyana grabbed her sleeve, and her grip was stronger than her frame suggested, wolf-strong even half-lost. "If he knows my name, then this isn't a hunt. Hunts are loud. This is an *offer*. I can smell it on him from here. They always come with an offer first."

"How would you know that?"

"Because I'm the thing people get offered." Ulyana's jaw tightened. "I just don't remember being it."

The collectors stopped twenty paces down the alley. Sef stood slightly ahead of the other two, and now that Paloma got a clean look at him, she understood why he led. He was unremarkable in the way a well-made lock is unremarkable—medium height, medium build, a face you'd forget in an hour except that you wouldn't, because of the eyes. They were pale gray, almost colorless, and they held the patient emptiness of deep water. His hands stayed visible at his sides, palms half-open. Everything about him was designed to say: *I am not a threat. Yet.*

"Paloma Okonkwo," he said. His voice carried down the alley without effort, pitched under the rain but reaching them perfectly, the way sound carries in a chapel. "You killed two of my colleagues on the Thornfell switchbacks. Ossory and Vane. Vane owed me eleven crowns, incidentally, and I mourn him mostly for that."

Paloma said nothing. She shifted her weight onto her back foot, measured the distance to the alley's far end—a gap between a cooper's shop and a stable wall, maybe four feet wide, enough for her, enough for Ulyana—and put the bread-woman's shutter between herself and Sef's line of sight.

Sef watched her do all of it, and smiled faintly, and waited.

"I'm told you were a contract-woman," he went on. "Sworn to the Quiet One. The god of terms and seals. A respectable trade, once. Your word was iron, your knife was honest, and your clients slept well." He tilted his head. "And then your god went silent, and the contracts kept working anyway, which must have felt like a miracle. Or a trap. I imagine it depends on the decade."

"Say what you came to say," Paloma said.

"There she is." Sef spread his hands, delighted, a showman finding his audience. "Here is what I came to say. The court beneath Thornfell extends its protection to you, Paloma Okonkwo. Full amnesty. Every debt of blood accrued on those switchbacks—forgiven. The bounty on your head—withdrawn. You walk out of Vetch's Crossing a free woman, and no collector, mine or any other, touches you for the rest of your natural life. That is the offer."

"And in exchange."

"In exchange," Sef said, "you give me the map, and you give me the girl."

Beside her, Ulyana had gone very still. Not frightened-still. Wolf-still. Paloma could feel it coming off her like heat off a stone—the coiled readiness, the hackles-up tension of an animal deciding whether the thing in front of it was predator or prey. Four days ago that stillness would have terrified her. Now it made her want to step sideways, to put her body more fully between the girl and the alley mouth, and she noted that impulse with a kind of grim, distant surprise, the way you note a crack in a foundation you'd sworn was solid.

"She's not mine to give," Paloma said.

"No," Sef agreed pleasantly. "She isn't. That's rather the point. She belongs to the court. She signed. She received payment—payment, I'll remind you, that saved her entire pack from a winter that would have eaten them to the last cub. The court honored its side of the bargain to the letter. All it asks is that she honor hers." He took a single step forward, boots silent in the mud. "You, though. You're the interesting one. You owe the court nothing. You killed its servants, you carry its stolen property, and you have no contract, no god, no obligation to anyone. So I find myself wondering—" the pale eyes glittered— "*why?*"

"Why what?"

"Why throw your life away for a stranger who can't even tell you what she lost?" Sef's voice softened, and that was worse than the showman's ring; the softness was the real instrument, the blade under the velvet. "I've read your file, Okonkwo. Ilya Vaschenko's last student. The man who taught you that a vow is a vow is a vow, that the word given is the self spent. He also taught you that sentiment is how clever people get robbed. So look at her. Truly look. She remembers nothing—not her name's history, not her family, not what she was before. She is a beautiful empty vessel following you through the rain because you're the first person who didn't try to cage her. That's not loyalty. That's a stray dog imprinting on the first warm hand." He shrugged, elegant, reasonable. "In a year she'll be dead or worse, and you'll have died getting her there. Whereas today, right now, you can be free. Genuinely free. No god, no debt, no dead mentor's sins dragging at your heels. Just the road, and your knife, and ten good years ahead of you." He smiled his priest's smile. "I am authorized to make this offer exactly once."

The rain filled the silence. Somewhere in the square, a shutter banged. Ulyana hadn't moved, hadn't breathed, and Paloma realized the girl was waiting—waiting to hear what she was, what she was worth, whether the warm hand would close or open.

Paloma thought about Ilya. She thought about him often lately, more than in the previous ten years combined, and each memory now arrived pre-poisoned, laced with the knowledge of what he'd done, what he'd brokered, what ledger-entries might bear her own name. But this memory was older than the poison. Ilya, sitting across a winter fire, teaching her the first law of contracts: *Never sign anything you haven't read to the end.* And beneath it, quieter, the thing he'd actually believed, the thing under all his iron rules: *A person is the one contract you can't put a price on. The moment you price them, you've already broken.*

She'd thought that was wisdom. Now she wondered if it had been confession.

"Here's my counter-offer," Paloma said.

Sef raised his eyebrows, amused. "I'm listening."

"You take your amnesty, and you shove it up the mountain sideways. You tell your masters the map burns tonight. You tell them the girl is dead in a ravine, if that helps them sleep. And you tell them—" she stepped out from behind the shutter, into the open, into the rain, letting him see all of her, the knives and the scars and the flat gray certainty in her face— "that the next collector who comes for her doesn't get a warning shot. I killed two of yours half-asleep in the dark. Come at me rested and I'll make it expensive."

Something moved behind Sef's eyes. Not anger. Something colder and more interested, the way a moneylender looks at a debtor who has just done something mathematically insane.

"You understand," he said, "what you're refusing. Not the amnesty. The *terms*. The court does not make second offers, and it does not forgive twice. From this moment, you are not a fugitive, Okonkwo. Fugitives are business. You are a *problem*, and problems get solved with fire."

"I understand fine."

"Do you?" Sef glanced past her, at Ulyana, and his voice dropped into something almost gentle. "Ask her, girl. Ask her if she wants to spend for you. Because that's what she's doing—spending. Years of her life, spent like coin, for someone who can't even remember her sister's face." He looked back at Paloma. "When she remembers—if she ever gets it back—do you think she'll thank you? Or do you think she'll look at everything you burned and wonder what gave you the right?"

It was a good question. It was a knife slipped between ribs, and Paloma felt it turn, because she'd asked herself versions of it every night since the ford. *What gives you the right?* Not justice—she didn't know yet if it was justice. Not pity—pity was cheap and Ulyana deserved better. She'd been carrying the girl through the mountains on nothing but a dying stranger's map and a stubbornness she couldn't explain, and Sef had just named the shape of that stubbornness out loud in the rain, and it sounded, from outside, like madness.

But then Ulyana moved.

She came forward, past Paloma's shoulder, into the open, and she stood there dripping and thin and half-starved with her chin up and her eyes blazing, and she looked at Sef the way wolves look at things that have entered the den.

"She doesn't need the right," Ulyana said. "That's the difference between you and her. You need *right*—contracts, signatures, authorization, permission. She just decided. I watched her decide at the ford, when she could have walked away and kept everything. And I don't remember my sister's face, you smug little clerk, but I remember—" her voice cracked, and she steadied it— "I remember that someone once chose me when choosing me cost something. I don't know who. But I know what it felt like. And it feels like *this*." She turned to Paloma, and her eyes were wet and furious and certain. "So tell him. Tell him what you told me at the ford."

Paloma hadn't told her anything at the ford. She'd dragged her out of the river, wrapped her in a blanket, and grumbled about wet wool. But she understood what the girl needed, and understanding that felt like another crack in the foundation, widening.

"She's under my protection," Paloma said. "That's the whole contract. No terms. No expiry. Take it to your masters verbatim."

For a long moment Sef simply looked at her, and the rain ran down his colorless face, and the market square behind him stayed empty and silent, every soul in Vetch's Crossing pretending very hard not to exist.

"Well," he said at last, softly. "There it is. The bridge, burning." He reached into his cloak, and both of Paloma's hands went to her knives, but what he drew out was only a small brass token, stamped with a spiral sigil—the mark of the court beneath the mountain. He held it up between two fingers, letting the rain strike it. "Do you know what this is? It's a receipt. For debts not yet collected. There are forty-one towns between here and Thornfell, Okonkwo, and every one of them has a toll-tower, and every toll-tower has a bell, and every bell-ringer holds one of these. From tonight, your face is on the other side of it." He tucked the token away. "You will not sleep indoors again. You will not buy bread from a warm window. Every road you know, I will know you're on it. You've chosen the girl over the world, and the world keeps excellent records."

"I know," Paloma said. "I used to keep them myself."

"Yes," Sef said. "That's what makes this so disappointing." He turned, and the other two collectors turned with him, and for a heartbeat Paloma considered putting a knife in his back and ending it here—

—and didn't, because Ulyana's hand closed on her wrist, light as a bird, and because some animal instinct older than strategy whispered that killing Sef in front of forty witnesses would buy them an hour and cost them the town, and possibly the county.

Sef walked to the end of the alley and stopped. He didn't look back. He spoke to the rain.

"One courtesy, since you refused mine. The pass you're planning—the Salt Road, through the hermit's bridge. It's a good choice. Clever. Almost impossible to follow." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "Which is why we lit the beacon at Crow's Rest an hour ago. Dragon-fire, Okonkwo. It burns for six days, and it can be seen from the gates of the under-court itself. Whatever route you take, whatever clever smuggler's path you crawl along—" he finally turned his head, just enough to show the pale edge of his profile, the ghost of the priest's smile— "the mountain already knows you're coming. Run fast. Make it sporting."

Then he was gone, into the square, into the rain, and the crowd closed behind him like water, and the bell in the toll-tower rang three times more and fell silent.

Paloma stood in the alley with the rain hammering her shoulders and the taste of ash in her mouth, and beside her Ulyana was shaking—not with cold now—with something else.

"He knew about the Salt Road," Ulyana whispered. "Paloma. How did he know about the Salt Road? You only told me yesterday. You said nobody knew that road. You said—"

"I know what I said."

"Then someone told him. Someone who knows the roads. Someone who—" Ulyana stopped. Paloma watched the thought arrive in her face like a slow dawn, terrible and bright.

Paloma was already thinking it. Someone who knew the roads. Someone who knew her habits, her routes, her mind. Someone whose handwriting annotated a map leading into the court beneath the mountain. Someone dead—or supposed to be.

"Pick up the bread," Paloma said. Her voice came out flat and cold and steady, and she was grateful for that, because underneath it everything was moving. "We leave through the north ford. Now. Before the beacon-light reaches the pass and every eye in these hills turns our way."

"And then where?"

Paloma looked north, where the rain was thickening into the gray smear of the high country, where a burned-out shrine waited with a dead man's secrets folded inside it, and where a question she'd been refusing to ask had just grown teeth.

"Then," she said, "we find out whether Ilya Vaschenko is actually in his grave."

* * *

They crossed the ford at dusk, the water thigh-deep and vicious with snowmelt, Ulyana's hand locked in hers—not for balance, Paloma told herself, though the girl's grip said otherwise—and on the far bank, as they wrung out their cloaks in the last failing light, Paloma saw it.

Far to the northeast, above the black teeth of the high pass, the sky glowed a deep, wrong red. Not sunset. Sunset didn't pulse.

Dragon-fire, burning on Crow's Rest, lighting the road ahead of them like a wound in the world.

Ulyana stared at it, and her ears—Paloma noticed, and filed it away with a chill—had gone subtly, unmistakably pointed.

"Paloma," she said. "I think I remember the way to the shrine."
