## 7. Roads Only Smugglers Know

The pass behind them was called the Shepherd's Throat, and it had earned the name honestly: three men had died in it last winter, swallowed by a slide that came down without warning, without sound, like a god clearing his throat. Paloma knew this because she'd buried two of them. The third had been a stranger whose name nobody ever learned.

She did not tell Ulyana any of this.

"Stay off the snowfield," she said instead, pointing with two fingers at the white scar curving along the mountainside above them. "Walk the rock. Rock doesn't move."

Ulyana looked up at the snowfield, then at the rock, then back, her nose wrinkling in that way she had when she was deciding whether to argue. "The rock is steeper."

"The rock is honest."

"You say things like that," Ulyana said, "like they're supposed to mean something."

"They do."

"To you, maybe." But she climbed onto the rock anyway, barefoot because Paloma had taken her boots to re-stitch the soles by firelight two nights past and hadn't given them back yet, and she moved like the mountain was something she was reading rather than climbing—head up, weight low, always knowing where her hands would go before they went there.

Paloma watched her for exactly as long as it took to admit she was watching, then turned and led on.

* * *

They made the salt caves by late afternoon, which was good, because the light was going thin and gold and the wind had picked up the smell of weather coming from the west. The caves were an old smuggler's cache, one of seven Paloma knew between Thornfell's spine and the low country, hollowed out of a salt seam by men who'd needed somewhere to store brandy and gunpowder where no excise man would think to look. The salt glittered on the walls when you held a flame near it, catching light like frost, like sugar, like something precious that wasn't.

Ulyana licked the wall.

"What are you doing."

"Tasting it." She licked it again, thoughtfully. "It's sweet. Sort of."

"It's salt."

"It's *sweet* salt." She said it with such conviction that Paloma almost laughed, and the almost itself was alarming enough that she turned away and busied herself with the fire.

That was the thing about Ulyana she was still learning to survive: not the wolf, not the shifting, not even the way the girl could go silent and deadly as a drawn blade when something moved wrong in the dark. It was this. The tasting of walls. The naming of stars with names that were obviously invented. The way she'd sat up half the night before, keeping watch she didn't need to keep because Paloma had already taken first watch, and when Paloma asked why, Ulyana had shrugged and said, "You sleep like someone who expects to be woken badly. I wanted you to be wrong."

Nobody had watched over Paloma's sleep since Ilya died.

She got the fire going small and smokeless, the way you did in country where smoke could be read like a letter, and dug the last of the smoked goat out of her pack. Ulyana produced, from somewhere, a handful of wild onions and a fistful of some herb Paloma didn't recognize, and looked at her expectantly.

"You're going to put those in my dinner."

"I'm going to make your dinner edible," Ulyana said. "There's a difference. You cook like someone being punished."

"I cook like someone who doesn't want to die of smoke inhalation."

"You cook like someone who's forgotten food can be good." Ulyana took the knife out of Paloma's hand—not grabbing, just taking, the way water takes a path—and set to work slicing the onions thin. "My mother used to say you can tell everything about a person by how they feed people. She'd have taken one look at you and—" She stopped.

The knife stopped too.

Paloma kept her eyes on the fire. She knew the shape of these silences now, had learned their borders the way she learned the borders of passes: where they started, how long they ran, what waited on the other side. Ulyana's memory came back in pieces, mostly wrong-shaped—a sister's laugh with no face attached, a den burning, hands pulling her through snow—and each piece arrived and then slid away again like a fish under ice. You couldn't help her hold them. Paloma had tried, early on, asking questions, and had watched Ulyana's whole face go grey with the effort of reaching for something that reached back empty.

So she didn't ask. She took the knife back gently when Ulyana offered it, finished the onions, and put the herbs in the stew because the girl was right—it was better, damn her.

"Eat," Paloma said. "Then sleep. We cross the hermit's bridge tomorrow, and I want us fresh."

* * *

The hermit's bridge was real, though the hermit was long dead. His name had been Anselm, or so the story went; he'd built the span across the Greywater gorge single-handedly over nineteen years, rope and timber and iron scavenged from wrecks, and he'd charged no toll except that every traveler had to tell him one true thing before crossing. He'd been dead forty years. The bridge remained, swaying gently in the gorge wind, and Paloma had crossed it perhaps thirty times and never once told it anything true.

Today she stood at the anchor post while Ulyana stepped out onto the planks, and heard herself say, to nobody, to the dead hermit, to the gorge:

"She eats walls."

It wasn't true, exactly. It wasn't false either. It was the closest thing to a joke she'd made aloud in ten years, and it hung in the air over the drop like a bird that didn't know where to land.

Ulyana, halfway across, turned around—turned around on a swaying bridge over a hundred feet of nothing, because of course she did, because she was a wolf and wolves don't understand vertigo—and grinned at her. "I heard that."

"Heard what."

"*Heard what,*" Ulyana mimicked, and kept walking, laughing, the bridge bouncing under her like a living thing.

Paloma followed. The planks creaked. The gorge breathed cold air up at her. And somewhere below, the Greywater ran silver and fast over stones that had been round since before there were roads, and Paloma found, to her private horror, that she was humming. Two notes. Barely a tune. She stopped immediately.

But Ulyana's ears—wolf ears, sharp as knives—twitched, and she didn't turn around, and she didn't say anything, and she smiled the rest of the way across.

* * *

On the fourth day it rained, and on the fifth day it rained harder, and on the sixth day they sheltered under an overhang of granite while the world outside turned to grey roaring water, and Ulyana taught Paloma a game.

"It's called truths," she said. "Wolves play it. You say a true thing about yourself. If it's boring, you lose."

"That's not a game, that's an interrogation."

"Wolves don't interrogate. We sniff. This is sniffing, but with words." She pulled her knees up under her chin. Her hair had gone curly in the wet, dark ringlets she clearly fought constantly and lost to daily. "Me first. I'm afraid of deep water. Not rivers—rivers are fine, you can feel the bottom. Deep water. Lakes. Where you can't touch."

Paloma said nothing. The rain hammered the granite.

"Your turn. One true thing. Boring loses."

"No."

"You're losing."

"Yes."

Ulyana sighed theatrically and lay back against the stone, arms behind her head, entirely unbothered, and Paloma understood suddenly that the game had never required her participation. That Ulyana had simply wanted her company in the telling. That this, too, was a kind of feeding—the girl offering up pieces of herself the way you'd offer food, saying *here, see, it doesn't kill you, try*.

"My brother used to bite his nails," Ulyana said to the rain. "Even after he shifted the first time. Even after his hands grew claws. He'd sit by the fire and bite them down to nothing and my mother would smack him and he'd do it again. I can't remember his face but I remember the biting. Isn't that strange? The useless things stay."

"The useless things stay," Paloma agreed, before she could stop herself.

Ulyana rolled her head sideways and looked at her, and there was something in the look that was careful and warm and patient all at once, and Paloma felt it land under her ribs like a hook set gently into flesh.

"One true thing," Ulyana said quietly. "Not for the game. Just—one. Whenever you want. I'll wait. Wolves are good at waiting."

Paloma looked out at the rain.

"When I was nine," she said, "I stole a wheel of cheese from a caravan and ate the whole thing and was sick for two days, and Ilya laughed until he cried, and then he taught me how to steal so I'd never be sick again."

Silence. Rain.

"That's two true things," Ulyana said softly. "You're getting good at this."

* * *

They traveled the hidden ways after that, and the hidden ways changed them both, though neither said so.

Paloma showed her the fold in the cliffside north of the Greywater where a whole valley hid behind six feet of rock, invisible until you were inside it—meadow, stream, old corrals, a smuggler's paradise that three generations of men had used and none had named. She showed her how to read scree for recent passage, how to cache supplies against need, how to walk a ridge at dawn when the frost held and left no print. Ulyana absorbed it all with that quick, hungry attention, filing it away, and in return she taught Paloma things Paloma hadn't known she lacked: which birdsong meant empty forest and which meant occupied; how to find water by listening for midges; how to sleep in shifts so deeply that eight hours became four.

At night they cooked together. It had become, without discussion, a shared labor—Ulyana gathering, Paloma butchering, both of them seasoning, arguing mildly about salt. Once, Ulyana fell asleep mid-sentence with her head on Paloma's shoulder, and Paloma sat perfectly still for three hours rather than wake her, and told herself it was because moving would disturb the watch rotation.

Once, crossing a scree field, Paloma slipped, and Ulyana caught her wrist without looking, without breaking stride, and hauled her up, and said, "Got you," in a voice so easy and certain that Paloma thought about it for the next two days.

Once—only once—Ulyana woke screaming, and Paloma held her while she shook, and neither of them mentioned it after, and somehow that was its own kind of intimacy, the kind that lives in what two people agree not to say.

* * *

On the ninth day, coming down out of the high passes toward the shrine road, Ulyana stopped so abruptly that Paloma walked two steps past her before noticing.

"What?"

Ulyana was staring east, where the mountains folded down toward the lowlands, toward market towns and roads and people. Her nostrils flared. When she spoke, her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice she used when the wolf was close to the surface.

"Smoke," she said. "Woodsmoke. A lot of it. And under it—" she frowned, hunting the scent, "—something else. Something old. Like a cellar full of coins."

Paloma felt the hair rise on her arms. She knew that smell. Every smuggler knew it, the way sheep know the smell of the slaughter track without ever having walked it.

Tithe-collectors. Many of them. Ahead of them, between them and the low country, camped on the only road down.

She looked at Ulyana. Ulyana looked back, and there was no fear in her face—only a question, offered plainly, the way she offered everything: *what do we do?*

And Paloma, who had spent ten years trusting no one, who had planned every step of every road alone, heard herself say:

"There's another way down. Nobody uses it. It's ugly, and it's long, and halfway along it there's a chapel to a god who doesn't answer anymore." She shouldered her pack. "We'll need shelter tonight anyway."

Ulyana fell into step beside her. "Your god?"

"My god."

"Maybe he'll answer *me*," Ulyana said, and grinned, and started downhill, and after a moment—hating herself, unable to stop it—Paloma smiled too.
