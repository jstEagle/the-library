## 8. The Heretic's Chapel

The storm found them an hour past dusk, and the chapel found them an hour after that.

Ulyana smelled it before she saw it—old stone and older incense, the ghost-sweetness of myrrh soaked so deep into walls that no rain could ever wash it out. She had been walking in Paloma's wake for three days now, learning the rhythm of the woman's shoulders, the way she tilted her head at a fork in the road like a hound tasting wind. Paloma never hesitated. That was the thing Ulyana kept noticing. Every other person she could remember—and her memories were a torn tapestry, threads knotted where whole scenes should be—every other person hesitated.

Paloma just turned left, always left, as if she'd already walked every road in Thornfell twice.

"Here," Paloma said now, and stopped so suddenly Ulyana nearly walked into her back. "We shelter here tonight."

Ulyana squinted through the sheeting rain. The chapel crouched in a fold of the hillside like something trying to hide from its own god: low-walled, slate-roofed, its bell tower snapped off at the shoulder the way a man breaks a finger. No light in the windows. No sigil above the door—only a square of paler stone where one had been chiseled away.

"This is a shrine," Ulyana said. "Someone's shrine."

"It's mine." Paloma pushed the door open on hinges that groaned like dying men. "Or it was meant to be."

* * *

Inside, the dark smelled of dust and candle-tallow gone rancid with age. Paloma struck flint to a lantern, and light climbed the walls in slow stages, revealing pews furred grey with mold, an altar draped in cloth that might once have been white, and—everywhere, on every surface—paper.

Ulyana had never seen so much paper outside a magistrate's office. Ledgers stacked on the altar. Ledgers filling the pew-benches. Ledgers heaped in corners like snowdrifts, their spines cracked, their pages swollen with damp. And pinned to the walls, hundreds of small squares of parchment, each bearing a name written in careful ink, each name struck through with a single line.

"What is this place?" she breathed.

"A chapel of the Quiet Accord." Paloma set the lantern on the altar and stood looking up at the empty niche behind it, where a statue should have been. There was only a scar of bare stone, and beneath it, carved deep into the wall, words Ulyana couldn't read—the letters were worn smooth, or perhaps they'd been deliberately defaced. "My god's house. One of the last ones. The Accord held that contracts were sacred things—a word given was a word kept, no matter what it cost you. Merchants swore by him. Mercenaries. Anyone whose life depended on someone else keeping faith." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "He stopped answering ten years ago. The priests said it was a test of devotion. The congregations dwindled. The chapels burned or rotted." She touched the edge of the altar cloth. "This one, I think, someone simply walked away from."

"And the papers?"

Paloma didn't answer right away. She was looking at the ledgers the way Ulyana imagined a soldier looked at a battlefield after the fighting—cataloguing the dead.

"I don't know," she said finally. "I've never been inside one of his chapels. We should search it. Storm won't break till morning; we may as well learn what's worth learning." She pulled off her wet gloves finger by finger. "Take the east wall. Don't touch anything with wax seals intact. Some contracts bind the reader."

That last part was probably a joke. Probably. Ulyana went to the east wall anyway and began, carefully, to read.

* * *

The names came first, and the names were the worst of it.

They were written on the little pinned squares in a clerk's neat hand—*Marta of the Vale, aged 34. Tomas Bellweather, aged 19. Ivetta, daughter of Radoslav, aged six.*

Six.

Ulyana's fingers stopped on that square. The ink had faded to brown, but the line struck through the name was still sharp, still deliberate, drawn by someone who wanted it clear that this account was closed. Below the name, in smaller script: *Received in full. One soul, unblemished. Pledged against debt of the father, 400 crowns, interest compounded.*

She read it again. Then again, as if a third reading might change the number of years, might make the child older, might make any of it make sense.

"Paloma." Her voice came out strange, flattened. "What is a soul worth here? In numbers."

Paloma looked up from a ledger across the room. Whatever she saw in Ulyana's face made her cross the chapel in four strides. She took the square from the wall, read it, and went very still.

"That's not possible," she said quietly. "The Accord doesn't—" She stopped herself. Ulyana watched her do it, watched the sentence die in her mouth, watched the mercenary's discipline close over whatever had been about to come out. "The Accord doesn't deal in souls," she finished, differently.

"Then who wrote this?"

Paloma didn't answer. She was scanning the wall now, really seeing it for the first time—hundreds of squares, hundreds of names, and not all of them struck through. A cluster near the window still hung unmarked, waiting, their debts presumably unpaid, their souls presumably still... wherever souls went when they were held as surety.

Ulyana knew where they went. She felt it like a cold hand between her shoulder blades. They went below. Into the court under the mountain, into the glass reliquaries, into the long dark where her own name was filed away among strangers' treasures.

She had sold hers—or someone had sold it for her—to save her pack. She remembered that much: firelight, snarling, a sister's hand slipping out of hers, and then the terrible arithmetic of desperation. But standing in this dead god's chapel, looking at a child's name priced at four hundred crowns, the shape of her own bargain curdled into something new. She hadn't known, then, what a soul was worth. She hadn't known there was a market at all.

"There are children here," she said. Her voice shook and she let it. "Dozens. Look—" She moved down the wall, touching each square lightly, a benediction or an accusation. "Aged nine. Aged seven. Aged four, Paloma. Four years old. What debt does a four-year-old owe that costs a soul?"

"None." Paloma had gone to the ledgers now, flipping pages with a speed that looked calm and wasn't. "None of them owe anything. That's not how it works. A soul can only be pledged by its owner, or—" She stopped again. This time the silence stretched.

"Or?" Ulyana pressed.

"Or by someone with authority over them." Paloma's jaw was tight. "A parent. A guardian. A lord." She set the ledger down on the altar with exaggerated care, the way people handle things when they want very badly to throw them. "Debts pass down bloodlines, in the old law. A father's default becomes the son's collateral. It's supposed to be forbidden. The Accord forbade it—that was half of what he *was*. Free consent, freely given, or the contract is void. That was the first law. The first law." She laughed, one dry note with nothing in it. "And someone built an entire industry on breaking it."

* * *

It was near midnight when Ulyana found the signature.

She had worked her way through three ledgers by lantern-light while Paloma slept sitting up against the altar steps, sword across her knees, because that was how Paloma slept—like a knife in a drawer, ready to be drawn. The ledgers were all the same in structure: names, dates, sums, terms. But the third one was different in one detail. Where the other books bore a variety of marks—clumsy crosses, merchant glyphs, the occasional true signature—the broker's line in this one carried the same hand every time, page after page, year after year.

*I. Vasilov.*

Ulyana didn't know the name. But she knew handwriting—wolves read sign, and a hand leaves sign the same way a paw does—and she knew this particular hand, because she had spent three days watching Paloma mark their route on her map, and the map's annotations were written in exactly these letters. The same sharp downstrokes. The same way the tail of an *l* hooked backward, like something flinching.

"Paloma." She said it softly, and when that didn't work, again, harder. "Paloma. Wake up. Who is Ilya Vasilov?"

The change that came over Paloma was instant and total—one breath she was asleep, the next she was on her feet with the lantern raised, eyes finding Ulyana, the door, the windows, the dark, in that order. Then the words landed, and Ulyana watched the readiness drain out of her and leave something worse behind.

"Where did you see that name?"

Ulyana held out the ledger, open to the page. "Everywhere. This whole book. He brokered all of it." She turned pages back, back, back—years of them. "And look. The map you carry. Your map. It's the same hand."

Paloma took the ledger the way you take news of a death: carefully, with both hands, as if it might be heavier than it looks. She read. Ulyana watched her lips move slightly over the entries—*one soul, pledged against debt of the widow Anka, term ninety-nine years*—and watched the color leave her face in stages, like a tide going out.

"He taught me everything," Paloma said at last. Her voice was level in the way ice is level—flat, and hiding depth. "Ilya Vasilov. My mentor. He found me starving in a border town when I was thirteen and taught me roads, contracts, survival. He taught me that a given word was the only thing a person truly owned." She turned another page. Another. "He used to say the god of contracts had two commandments: keep your word, and know whose word you're keeping. He made me swear both before he'd teach me the mountain passes."

"And he was selling children to the under-court."

"He was *brokering*—" Paloma began, and then heard herself, and stopped. The distinction collapsed even as she reached for it. She sat down slowly on the altar step, the ledger open in her lap, and for a long moment she just breathed. "Yes," she said. "He was selling them. Or holding the pen while someone else sold them. I don't know which is worse, and I don't think it matters."

Ulyana came and sat beside her. Not too close—she had learned that much about Paloma, that closeness had to be offered in inches—but close enough that their shoulders nearly touched in the lantern-light.

"The map," Ulyana said gently. "The routes into the court. His writing. He knew the way in."

"He didn't just know the way in." Paloma's thumb traced one entry, then another. "Look at the dates. These span twenty years. Twenty years of this, and he was visiting the court the whole time—he must have been, to broker in person, to collect payment. The routes on that map aren't smuggler's knowledge. They're a supplier's knowledge." She closed the ledger. Opened it again, as if hoping the words would rearrange themselves. "Everything I am, I built on what he taught me. Every road I trust, every rule I live by. *Keep your word.*" Her voice cracked on it, just barely, and she pressed her lips together until it passed. "And the whole time, he was the reason there were names on those walls."

* * *

The storm eased toward dawn, the thunder retreating down the valleys like an army withdrawing. Neither of them slept again.

Ulyana spent the dark hours doing what wolves do with grief: she worked. She took down every unstruck square from the east wall—the living debts, the souls still held—and laid them in a row on the altar, forty-one names, smoothing each one flat. Paloma watched her do it for a while without comment, and then got up and helped, and neither of them spoke, and the work itself became a kind of prayer, though Ulyana couldn't have said to whom.

When the last square was laid, Paloma stood looking down at them, and something in her face had changed. Ulyana had seen her afraid on the switchbacks, seen her ruthless with the tithe-collectors, seen her wary as a wild thing around every kindness. This was none of those. This was the face of a woman arriving somewhere she couldn't walk away from.

"When we get your soul back," Paloma said, "we're coming back for these."

Ulyana blinked. "Back? Through the gate? After?"

"After. Before. However it has to be." Paloma picked up the square with the six-year-old's name and held it in the lantern-light. "Ilya taught me that a contract freely given is sacred. Fine. None of these were freely given. Which means, by the first law of my own god, every one of them is void—it's just that nobody with the power to void them has bothered to try." She set the square down with the others, aligning its edges precisely. "My god is silent. My mentor is dead and damned. The law that's left is the one underneath the mountain, and the law underneath the mountain only understands payment." Her jaw set. "Then we'll speak its language. But we're not leaving those souls below just because escaping with one is easier than justice for forty-one."

Ulyana stared at her. Something hot and unfamiliar was rising in her chest—not the wolf-heat of anger, but something steadier, something with roots. For three days she had thought of Paloma as armor: hard, closed, self-contained, a woman carrying her silences like stones. Now, in the grey pre-dawn light of a dead god's chapel, surrounded by the names of stolen children, Ulyana saw what the armor was for. It was for exactly this. It was so that when the moment came, the woman inside it would not flinch.

"My sister," Ulyana said quietly. "In the fragments I have left—I remember her laugh, and I remember our den burning, and I remember letting go of her hand. If she's alive, she's either below or she lost everything the night I did. Either way—" She looked down at the row of names. "Either way, I'm not the only one who was robbed. I just might be the only one who can walk in and ask for it back."

"Not the only one." Paloma tapped the ledger in her lap, Ilya's ledger, the broker's book. "His accounts will show where the payments went. Who collected. What the court owes and to whom. Debts cut both ways, Ulyana. He taught me that too." A ghost of the not-quite-smile. "Turns out it's the one lesson worth keeping."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the broken window, the eastern sky was going the color of hammered tin, and the mountains stood black against it, and somewhere inside one of them—under all that stone—forty-one souls waited in glass, and a wolf's name waited among them, and a sister who might still be alive.

Ulyana gathered the squares together and bound them with a strip of linen from the altar cloth, forty-one names in a bundle she tucked inside her shirt, against her skin, where she would feel them with every breath.

"We'll need more than a map," she said, "to get forty-two souls out of that mountain."

Paloma was already repacking her gear, quick and precise, but she paused at those words—*forty-two*, Ulyana had counted herself in, and Paloma had noticed—and something flickered across her face that Ulyana was beginning to recognize as the closest thing she had to warmth.

"No," Paloma agreed. "We'll need a miracle. Or failing that—" she slung her pack over her shoulder and pushed open the chapel door onto the washed silver morning, "—a better broker than Ilya ever was."

She stepped out into the light. Ulyana followed, the names warm against her heart, and behind them the silent god's empty niche watched them go with its worn-away face, saying nothing, the way it had said nothing for ten years.

But as Ulyana crossed the threshold, she could have sworn—for just a heartbeat, in the space between one breath and the next—that the air in the chapel stirred, like a held breath finally released.

Like something ancient, waking up, and beginning, very slowly, to listen.
