## 9. Sef's Price

The trap closed on them at the salt caves, three days out of the market town, and Paloma knew it before she saw a single soul—knew it in the way the birds had gone quiet over the pass, in the way the wind carried no smell of rain when the clouds promised it. She had spent twenty years learning to read the mountains the way priests read scripture, and every line of them now said *ambush*.

She said nothing to Ulyana. Not because she didn't trust her—though she didn't, not entirely, not yet—but because there was nothing left to do about it. The caves were the only shelter within a day's walk, and the snow above the treeline had turned mean overnight, the kind of wet heavy fall that killed the unprepared and buried the unburied. They went in because they had to go in.

The salt walls glowed faintly amber where their lantern touched them, all that ancient light trapped in crystal, and for a hundred paces the only sound was their boots crunching drifts of glittering powder. Ulyana walked close behind her, close enough that Paloma could hear her breathing, quick and shallow. The girl had been quiet since the pass. Since the beacon. Since she'd looked at Paloma with those flat grey eyes and asked, politely, *what is your name again?*

Paloma hadn't answered then. She wasn't sure she could answer now without her voice breaking, and so she kept walking, one hand on her knife, counting exits.

There were three ways into the deep salt galleries. She'd used two of them herself, years ago, running contraband tin past the Thornfell tolls. The third was a legend Ilya had told her once, drunk on plum brandy, laughing: *the smuggler's door, little bird, the one even the mountain doesn't remember.*

Sef was sitting at the junction of all three.

He had a brazier going, which meant he'd been waiting long enough to bother with comfort, and he had tea steeping in a dented copper pot, which meant he'd been waiting long enough to be certain. He sat on a crate of what smelled like raw wool with his ankles crossed and his hands folded on his knee, and he looked up at them with the mild, patient expression of a man watching bread rise.

"Paloma Okonkwo," he said. "You look terrible."

Behind her, Ulyana made a sound low in her throat—not quite human, not quite wolf—and Paloma put a hand back without looking, palm flat, the old signal: *stay. wait.* She felt the tremor run through the girl's arm and held it there a moment longer than she needed to.

"Sef." She stepped forward into the lantern-light, letting him see her hands empty, her knife still sheathed. "You're a long way from your office."

"My office travels." He gestured at the second crate, the one across from his. "Sit. The tea's decent. It's the last decent thing that's going to happen today, so I'd take advantage."

"I'll stand."

"You always did like making things difficult." He poured anyway, two cups, steam curling up through the amber glow. "I want you to understand something before we start. I'm not here for you. Honestly, I never was. You were a name on a ledger, a loose thread, an embarrassment to people who prefer their embarrassments dead. But then you killed two of the Lord Beneath's collectors on a switchback, and embarrassments became debts, and debts—" he spread his hands, "—well. Debts are my whole profession."

"Your profession is selling people," Paloma said.

"My profession is *pricing* people. Selling is just the arithmetic afterward." He sipped his tea. "And you've become very expensive."

* * *

They came out of the dark while Sef talked—six of them, maybe seven, moving with the soft deliberate tread of professionals, cutting off the gallery mouths one by one. Paloma marked each one. Two crossbows. A woman with a hooked pole, the kind used for pulling wolves out of fights. A big man by the smuggler's door who moved like he'd been born in it. They didn't threaten. They didn't need to. That was the thing about Sef's people: they arrived after the threat had already been made, somewhere else, by someone else, and their job was only the paperwork.

Ulyana's hand found the back of Paloma's belt and gripped it. Not pulling. Just holding. Like a child in a crowd.

"It's all right," Sef said pleasantly, to her, as if she were the one being negotiated with. "Nobody here wants to hurt you. Quite the opposite. There are people below who value you enormously."

"She doesn't know what that means," Paloma said.

"No," Sef agreed. "That's rather the tragedy of it, isn't it?" He set down his cup and leaned forward, elbows on knees, and for the first time his pleasantness dropped away like a coat taken off indoors, and underneath it was something older and colder and much more honest. "Let me tell you how the under-court pays its bills, Paloma, since you've been carrying that map around for weeks without understanding what it's a map *of*. It isn't a treasure map. It's an invoice."

"Ilya's handwriting," she said. "I know."

"You know routes. You don't know rates." He ticked them off on his fingers, a broker reciting terms. "A memory of a face: one year. A first love: five. A mother's voice: three, unless sung to, in which case seven. An entire soul—" he paused, savoring it, "—well. An entire soul is priced by weight. And hers—" he nodded at Ulyana, who had gone very still against Paloma's back, "—hers weighed more than most. Wolf-shifters carry double. Everything they are, they are twice: once in the body, once in the blood. When she sold herself to save her pack, the court took the whole account. Every year of her. Every memory. Everything except what couldn't be lifted out without killing the animal, which is why she still shifts, why she still dreams in shapes she can't name." He smiled thinly. "She's been living on credit ever since. And credit, Paloma, always comes due."

Paloma heard her own heartbeat in her ears. "You're telling me this because you want something."

"I'm telling you this because you're a professional, and professionals deserve honest terms." Sef reached into his coat and produced a folded paper, sealed with grey wax, and laid it on the crate between them. "Here are mine."

* * *

She read it twice. She made herself read it twice, because the first time her eyes had slid off the middle lines the way eyes slide off a lie.

*The bearer, Paloma Okonkwo, having no standing in the matter of the soul called Ulyana, may yet purchase her release by service rendered: delivery of the map of the under-court, alone and unaccompanied, to the gate beneath Thornfell. Upon such delivery, the debt of the soul called Ulyana shall be discharged in full, and she shall go free, remembering nothing, owing nothing.*

*In exchange, the bearer surrenders all claim to the estate and obligations of Ilya Vosk, deceased, and shall be permitted to depart the Thornfell jurisdiction unmolested.*

*This instrument void if the bearer attempts entry, or if any companion accompanies her beyond the high pass.*

Alone. Unaccompanied. *Void if any companion accompanies her.*

"They'll wipe her anyway," Paloma said quietly. "Even freed. 'Remembering nothing.' That's not freedom. That's a clean animal turned loose in the woods."

"That's mercy, by the court's arithmetic." Sef shrugged. "You've seen what remembering costs down there. Besides—" and here his voice softened into something almost gentle, which from Sef was worse than cruelty, "—what does she have to remember? You met her three weeks ago. She forgot your name in a snowstorm. You'd be freeing a stranger, Paloma. A stranger who owes you nothing, who can't even thank you. Whereas you—" he tapped the paper, "—you get to walk away. Alive. Unmolested. With your god's silence still intact and your mentor's sins finally, finally not your problem."

He let that sit. He was good at letting things sit; it was half his trade.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we take you both now. The collectors outside have orders. The girl goes below tonight, and you—" he considered, the way a man considers cuts of meat, "—you go below too, eventually, but slower, and through worse rooms. Or." He lifted one shoulder. "You sign, you deliver, and in ten years' time some other courier dies on some other road, and none of it was ever yours to carry."

Paloma looked at the paper. The grey wax caught the lantern-light and threw it back dull, like a coin from a country that no longer existed.

She thought about Ilya. She thought about him often lately—more than she had in years, more than was comfortable—and always the same moment: his hand on her head when she was nineteen and newly sworn, saying *the contract protects you, little bird, that's what contracts are for*, and her believing him, because she'd had no reason not to, because she'd been young, because he'd been the only warm thing in a cold decade. She thought about the ledger she hadn't found yet, though some part of her had started to suspect it existed, some locked box with her name in it written in ink she hadn't seen dried yet.

She thought about Ulyana's hand on her belt. About the weight of it. About how, three weeks ago, she would have cut off her own arm before letting anyone hold onto her like that.

"Leave us," she said.

Sef raised his eyebrows.

"A professional deserves honest terms," Paloma said. "So give me an honest minute to consider them. Alone. If your people are going to stand there breathing down my neck while I decide, I'll decide spiteful, and spiteful decisions cost everyone money. Isn't that how you'd put it?"

Something flickered behind Sef's eyes—annoyance, maybe, or respect, and with Sef it had always been hard to tell the difference. He rose, brushed wool-dust from his coat, and inclined his head with theatrical courtesy. "Five minutes. Dmitri, with me." The big man by the smuggler's door peeled away from it, and the two of them withdrew up the gallery, boots crunching, until the amber glow swallowed them.

The crossbow-people stayed. Of course they stayed. But they moved back to the gallery mouths, and for a little while there was only the hiss of the brazier and the slow drip of meltwater somewhere in the salt dark.

* * *

Ulyana came around to face her. Up close, in the lantern-light, she looked hollowed out—the last weeks carved into her face, the missing memories showing as gaps the way missing teeth show when someone smiles. She looked at the contract on the crate, and Paloma watched her read it, watched her lips move slightly over the words, and saw the exact moment she understood.

"You'd leave me," Ulyana said. Not accusing. Worse than accusing—*checking*. Testing the floor before she put her weight on it.

"The paper says alone."

"I know what it says." Her jaw tightened. "I'm asking what *you* say."

Paloma picked up the contract. Turned it over. Set it down again. Bought herself the seconds the way Sef bought silence, with small motions and a steady face, and hated how easily the trick came to her, how natural it felt to wear a lie like a well-fitted coat.

Because here was the truth she couldn't say out loud, the truth that sat in her chest like swallowed river-stone: *she might mean it.* Some cold practical corner of her mind—the corner that had kept her alive through ten silent years, through winters and betrayals and the slow erosion of everything soft—was looking at this paper and whispering *this is a good price. This is the best price you will ever be offered. Take the girl's freedom, take your life, walk away, and grieve later, the way you always grieve: efficiently, privately, on a road where nobody can see.*

That corner of her had kept her alive. That corner of her had also, she was beginning to understand, been built by Ilya, brick by brick, for purposes she was only now starting to guess at. A mercenary who trusts no one. A woman whose god has stopped answering. A weapon with no attachments, perfect for delivering maps to doors beneath mountains.

*Ilya,* she thought, *what did you make me for?*

"Paloma." Ulyana's voice cracked on the name—on having it, on using it, on the effort of holding onto something so new and so fragile. "Look at me."

She looked. Grey eyes, steady despite everything, despite the gaps, despite the animal fear rolling off her in waves she couldn't fully hide. A girl who had sold everything she was to save people she loved, and who stood here now with nothing left to sell except the asking.

"If you take that bargain," Ulyana said, "I won't blame you. I want you to know that. Whatever happens—I won't blame you. I sold my own sister's laughter to buy my pack one winter. I know what prices do to people." Her breath shuddered out of her. "But I'd rather die in these caves as myself—one week of myself, one day of myself—than live free down there as nothing. So if you sign that paper, don't sign it for me. Sign it for you. Because I will fight them. All of them. And I will lose. And it will be for nothing."

The brazier popped. Somewhere far above, muffled through a hundred feet of salt and stone, thunder rolled over the pass.

Paloma looked at the contract a long moment. Then she picked it up, and she signed it.

She wrote her name in the space provided—*Paloma Okonkwo*, in the careful clerk's hand Ilya had drilled into her, the hand that had signed forty contracts and broken exactly one—and she blew on the ink, and she handed the paper back to Sef's waiting man, and she watched the grey wax take her thumbprint beside her signature, binding, official, done.

Sef reappeared as if summoned by the sound of it, smiling his bread-rising smile. "Wise. I confess I hoped, but I wouldn't have bet on it."

"Don't spend the goodwill," Paloma said. "One condition. Not in the paper—a rider. Verbal, witnessed by your crossbows." She jerked her chin toward Ulyana. "She walks free until the gate. Fed, unhurt, unbound. If any of your people touch her between here and the high pass, the contract's void and I'll collect on it personally, starting with you."

Sef considered. "Granted. It costs me nothing—you're to deliver her to the pass regardless. Sentiment dressed as leverage." He extended his hand.

She shook it. His palm was dry and cool, like paper.

"We move at dawn," he said. "The weather above is filthy, but filthy weather makes filthy roads, and filthy roads are yours, aren't they? Roads only smugglers know." He gathered his coat, his cups, his little portable empire of crates and copper, and paused at the edge of the lantern-light. "For what it's worth, Paloma—I've brokered four hundred souls, and I've never once seen a seller come back up. Whatever you think you're doing, whatever clever thing you're planning in that stubborn head of yours—the mountain has heard it all. The mountain was hearing it before your mentor was born."

"I know," Paloma said.

"Do you?" He smiled, and for one unguarded instant there was something almost like pity in it. "Then sleep well. Dawn comes early."

* * *

They gave them a corner of the gallery, a blanket, and the illusion of privacy. The crossbows settled at the gallery mouths. The brazier burned low. Somewhere in the dark, Ulyana lay awake, and Paloma lay awake, and neither of them slept, and neither of them spoke, because there were ears everywhere and words were now the most dangerous cargo either of them carried.

But near midnight, when the watch shifted and the crunch of boots covered all other sound, Ulyana's hand found hers in the dark. Cold fingers. The calluses of someone who had worked, once, at something—gripping, hauling, holding. She didn't say anything. She just held on, the way she'd held onto Paloma's belt, testing the floor, putting her weight on it.

Paloma let her.

And in the black behind her eyes, she turned the contract over and over, reading not the words but the shape of the trap inside them. *Delivery of the map, alone and unaccompanied. Void if any companion accompanies her beyond the high pass.* Sef thought he'd bought her obedience. Sef thought the signature meant surrender.

But Paloma Okonkwo had spent twenty years learning roads that weren't on any map, and one of them ran along the spine of the high pass itself—a knife-edge trail, half legend, that no collector, no broker, no army had ever managed to guard, because no one believed anything with a heartbeat could walk it.

The contract bound her to deliver the map. It said nothing about what else she might deliver alongside it.

Dawn came early, grey and merciless, and they broke camp in silence, and began to climb.
