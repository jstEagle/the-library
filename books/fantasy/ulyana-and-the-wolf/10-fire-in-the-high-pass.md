## 10. Fire in the High Pass

Ulyana smelled the lie on Sef before he ever spoke it.

It was a wolf-thing, older than her stolen memories—a scent-sense that lived in her spine even when the rest of her was full of holes. Sef smelled of tallow and old paper and, underneath it all, of cold iron, like a man who kept his promises in a strongbox and only opened it for himself. He had walked with them for two days through the salt caves, talking pleasantly about amnesty and safe roads, and every hour the iron smell got stronger.

Paloma didn't seem to notice. Or she noticed and said nothing, which Ulyana was learning was the same thing with her. Paloma carried suspicion the way other people carried knives—always, everywhere, even sleeping—but she never shared it. She just tightened her grip on the map-roll in her coat and walked a little faster.

The high pass began where the salt caves ended, at a mouth of grey stone that breathed cold air down at them like something sleeping. Above, the sky had gone the color of old pewter, and the wind came off the peaks carrying a smell Ulyana knew from somewhere she couldn't name: sulfur and hot metal and something sweetly rotten underneath.

*Dragon-fire,* some buried part of her whispered. *You've smelled that before.*

She didn't ask Paloma about it. There were so many things she didn't ask anymore. She had learned to live inside her own gaps the way a cripple learns to live inside a narrow room—carefully, turning sideways, not looking too hard at what was missing. She knew she'd had a sister. She could almost hear the laugh, high and bright as a struck bell. She knew there had been fire. The rest was white space, and the white space was growing.

That was the thing nobody warned you about, selling pieces of yourself. The edges didn't stay neat. Every time she reached for what was gone, the hole got wider, like a wound that fed on being touched.

"Camp here," Sef said, gesturing at a shelf of rock sheltered by an overhang. "We cross the saddle at first light. The collectors don't fly at night."

"The collectors," Paloma said flatly, "don't fly at all."

"They borrow wings." Sef smiled his paper smile. "Everything below borrows, mistress Okonkwo. That's rather the whole business model."

Ulyana watched Paloma's jaw work. Watched her decide—visibly, the way she decided everything—to let it go because they needed sleep more than they needed truth. It was one of the things Ulyana loved about her, though she hadn't found a way to say it and probably never would: Paloma made her compromises honestly, out loud to herself, and then stood by them like a soldier stands by a bad position.

They made camp. Sef took first watch, which should have been the first warning, because a broker never gives away anything free—not even labor.

* * *

Ulyana woke to the smell of burning pitch.

For a heartbeat she didn't know where she was—the overhang, the cold, the dark all wrong, all strange—and then the wolf in her solved it faster than the woman could. *High pass. Fire. Up.*

She scrambled up the scree in her human shape, barefoot, breath tearing, and crested the shelf of stone in time to see Sef standing at the lip of the pass with a brazier-iron in his hands, tipping it against a cairn of oil-soaked wood that had clearly been built long before any of them arrived. Waiting for him. Built for him.

Flame went up with a sound like a gasp.

But it wasn't ordinary flame. It burned green-gold at the heart, and it cast light that moved wrong, crawling along the rock like something alive, and when the wind caught it the smoke didn't rise—it *poured*, sliding downhill toward the mountain's roots in a slow black river. A signal. A beacon lit with dragon-fire, the oldest currency of the under-court, and everyone within fifty miles would read it the way you read a scream.

*Sef.* He was already turning, already reaching into his coat, and Ulyana's body understood before her mind did.

"Sef!" Her voice cracked across the pass.

He looked at her without surprise. That was the worst part. No guilt, no flinch. Just the mild disappointment of a merchant whose goods had turned out faulty. "I'm sorry about this," he said, and he sounded like he meant it, which somehow made it worse. "Truly. But the court pays in years, girl. Years. You'll understand when you're older—or you won't, because you won't get older. Either way."

He threw something. Not at her—at Paloma's bedroll below, a small glass vial that burst in a bloom of violet smoke, and from the smoke came shapes. Collectors. Three of them, unfolding out of the vapor like wet paper unfolding into men, except no man ever folded that way, joints bending backward, faces smooth as eggs with eye-holes punched in them. They moved toward the camp with terrible patience, the way water moves toward a drain.

Paloma was already up. Of course she was; Paloma slept like a cat with a knife, and the sound of the beacon had brought her out of her roll with blades in both hands before Ulyana's shout finished echoing. Ulyana saw her take in the scene in one sweep—Sef above, the collectors closing, the map still strapped against her ribs—and saw her do the arithmetic she always did, fast and cold and fair.

Three collectors. Two of us. One road down, blocked.

One road *up*, toward Sef, toward the beacon, toward the thing that had to be stopped or every tithe-collector in Thornfell would come running to a lit dinner bell.

"Ulyana." Paloma's voice, sharp as a whip-crack. "The beacon. I'll hold these."

"You can't hold three—"

"I wasn't asking."

And that was the last clear thought Ulyana had for a while, because the collectors reached Paloma, and steel rang, and something in Ulyana's chest tore open.

* * *

Here is what the wolf knows that the woman forgets: the shift is not a door you open. It is a door that opens *you*.

Ulyana had shifted before, since the under-court, since the selling. Small shifts. Ears, eyes, the lean grey shape for running. Each time, she had paid for it. She'd learned that much by now, the hard tuition of finding words missing, faces missing, whole seasons of herself gone soft at the edges. The soul was a candle, and shifting in the wind spent wax, and hers was already half-burned down.

She knew all of that. She stepped off the ledge anyway.

The fall was short. The change happened in the air, mid-leap, the way it had the first time Paloma ever saw her at the ford—except this time she didn't fight it, didn't try to keep a handhold on the woman-shape. She let the wolf come up through her like floodwater through a broken dam, gave it everything, held nothing back, because Paloma was below her with three impossible things closing in and because some debts are paid in coin and some are paid in self.

The world exploded into scent and motion.

Green-gold firelight. Blood—Paloma's, a thin ribbon of it already, which sent a howl up Ulyana's throat that she felt in her teeth. The collectors turned toward her, egg-faces tilting, and she hit the first one like a landslide. It came apart in her jaws with a taste like burnt hair and cold candlewax, and the second one raked claws across her shoulder that burned worse than steel should, and she barely felt it, because the third had gotten past her, had gotten its backwards hands on Paloma, was dragging her toward the edge of the drop—

Ulyana took it from behind and worried it like a terrier with a rat until it stopped being a shape at all.

Then it was quiet, except for the beacon roaring, except for the wind, except for Paloma breathing hard against the rocks with blood on her cheek and her knives still up, watching Ulyana with an expression Ulyana had never seen on her before.

Fear.

Not of her. Never of her—she understood that even through the wolf-haze, the way you understand things in dreams. Fear *for* her. Because Ulyana had given the shift everything, and everything was more than she had, and she could feel the cost arriving even now, rolling in like weather.

Memories going. She grabbed for them and they slid through her hands like fish.

A kitchen—whose kitchen? Gone. A name she'd been called as a child—gone. The color of her sister's eyes—gone, gone, the bell-bright laugh fading even as she clawed at it, and she whined, deep and helpless, the sound a wolf makes when the trap is on the leg and pulling only makes it worse.

*Hold on to something,* she told herself. *Hold on to one thing.*

There was Paloma. Paloma was real, Paloma was here, Paloma was kneeling in front of her now saying her name—"Ulyana. Ulyana, look at me, you come back now, you hear me?"—and Ulyana held onto that voice the way a drowning woman holds a rope. She let the wolf recede. She let the bones lengthen and the fur fall away and the woman come back up, shaking, naked in the freezing wind, bleeding from three long lines across her shoulder.

She looked up at Paloma. Paloma's face. She knew that face. She trusted that face. That face had pulled her out of a river and taught her which mushrooms lied and slept between her and the cold for twenty nights and never once asked for anything back.

That face had a name. She knew it had a name. She had said it a hundred times, had shouted it across valleys, had whispered it into bedrolls in the dark like a prayer to a god who actually answered.

The name was gone.

It had been standing right next to the color of her sister's eyes, and it had gone with them, spent like a coin in the dark, and Ulyana sat in the snow and the ash and the green-gold dying light of the beacon and wept—not for the pain in her shoulder, not for the cold, but for the small white space where a word used to be.

"I'm sorry," she said. Her voice came out wrecked. "I'm sorry—I know your face, I know—I know you, I do, but I can't—" Her hands pressed flat against her own temples as if she could hold the rest of herself in by force. "What's your name? Please. Tell me your name before I lose that too."

Paloma went very still.

Ulyana had seen her take wounds without blinking. Had seen her stand over a dead friend's grave—no, wait, whose grave? *gone, another one gone*—without a tear. But now something moved across her face like cloud-shadow over a field, quick and devastating, and when she spoke her voice was rough as gravel.

"Paloma," she said. "My name is Paloma Okonkwo. Say it back."

"Paloma." The word landed in Ulyana like a lantern lowered into a well, lighting nothing but showing her exactly how deep the dark went. "Paloma. I'll remember. I'll write it on my arm if I have to, I'll—"

"No." Paloma shrugged off her coat and wrapped it around Ulyana's shoulders, rough-handed, gentle-eyed, the contradiction that was entirely her. "You won't have to. We're getting your soul back. All of it. Every piece." She stood, and turned, and looked up at the beacon.

The green-gold fire was guttering low, its message already sent, the black river of smoke still sliding down into the mountain's throat. And far below—so far it might have been imagination—lights were answering. Tiny, patient, wrong-colored lights, kindling in the passes beneath them like eyes opening one by one in a dark room.

Sef was gone. Of course he was. Only his footprints remained, leading not down the pass but sideways, along a goat-track Ulyana's nose told her climbed toward a hidden trail she'd never find in the dark.

"They're coming," Ulyana said. It wasn't a question. The lights answered it anyway, multiplying.

"Yes." Paloma pulled her up by the arm, steadied her, checked the map-roll against her ribs with two fingers the way another person might check a heartbeat. "Then we don't take the roads they know. There's a cache near here. Ilya's. I hoped I'd never need it." She paused, and Ulyana watched her swallow whatever came next. "He left instructions for the last stretch. If we're going into the mountain, we go in knowing what he did. All of it."

Ulyana pulled the coat tighter around herself and looked back once at the beacon, at the answering lights, at the place where her memories had burned like offerings on an altar.

"Paloma," she said, testing the name, nailing it down. "Which way?"

Paloma looked at her—that long, level look, cataloguing damage, making plans—and pointed up, into the teeth of the pass, toward the hidden cache and the mountain beyond it.

"Away from the lights," she said. "And straight toward the dark."
