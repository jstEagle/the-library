## 11. Ilya's Ledger

The cache was where it had always been, which was the cruelest part.

Paloma had walked past the split oak a hundred times in the years since Ilya died—past the lightning scar that ran down its trunk like a seam of black glass, past the hollow at its base where a badger family came and went with the seasons. She'd never once thought to look inside. Why would she? The tree stood on the old boundary between Thornfell and the lowland pastures, on a stretch of road Ilya had made her memorize when she was fourteen and still small enough to be carried over his shoulder when her feet gave out. *If you're ever lost,* he'd told her, tapping the bark with the flat of his knife, *come back to this tree. Everything you need to find your way is here.*

She'd assumed he meant the road itself. The landmarks. The way the mountain's shadow fell across the ford at dusk so that even a child could read it like a clock.

He had meant something else entirely.

It was Ulyana who found it—or rather, it was Ulyana's restlessness that found it. They had been hiding for two days in the ruins of a shepherd's bothy above the treeline, waiting out a patrol of tithe-collectors that moved through the valley below like a slow black comb drawn through wool. Paloma could wait. Waiting was most of what survival was, and she had made an art of it: the stillness, the rationed water, the mind turned inward and quiet as a closed hand. But Ulyana was young, and wolf-blooded, and patience sat on her like a coat two sizes too small. By the second evening she was pacing the bothy's broken walls, and by the third morning she announced—cheerfully, infuriatingly—that she was going to look around.

"Stay within sight," Paloma said.

"I'm always within sight." Ulyana grinned, and her grin did something to the air between them that Paloma refused to examine. "You just don't look."

Paloma looked. That was the problem. She looked too often, tracked the girl's grey-eyed, quick-moving shape through the pines with more attention than any job required, and told herself it was because losing her would mean losing the map's only other reader—and because the collectors wanted the girl alive, and a living captive was a complication Paloma did not have the resources to manage. All true. None of it the whole truth.

Ulyana came back before noon, breathless, muddy to the knee, holding a scrap of birchbark like it might bite her.

"There's writing on the tree," she said. "Down low, under the moss. It's old. It's—I think it's yours."

*Yours.* Not Ilya's. Ulyana had never met Ilya; she knew him only as the dead mentor whose handwriting crawled across the map's margins, whose name made something behind Paloma's ribs go tight and silent every time it was spoken. But she had watched Paloma touch that handwriting once, in the burned shrine, and she had understood without being told that some names are carried in the body.

They went down together through the cold bright morning, and Paloma knelt at the base of the split oak, and scraped away twenty years of moss with her thumbnail, and there it was: a single line cut into the bark in Ilya's hand, shallow, deliberate, weathered almost smooth.

*What I keep is not mine to keep.*

Below it, half-hidden by root-shadow, a keyhole no larger than a coin, ringed in iron gone green with age.

Paloma sat back on her heels and did not move for a long time.

She had keys. She had carried Ilya's keyring for eleven years, worn smooth against her hip, and she had never known what half of them opened—the little brass one, the flat iron one shaped like a fish, the one with teeth filed into it like a row of mountains. She had kept them out of grief and habit, the way one keeps a dead man's boots. Now she drew them out with fingers that would not quite steady, and tried the flat iron one first, because Ilya had loved riddles and hated obviousness, and a fish-shaped key seemed like the kind of joke he would play from beyond the grave.

It turned. The sound it made was very small—a click, a sigh of old wood—and yet it went through Paloma like a bell struck inside her chest.

The hollow behind the keyhole was deeper than the tree. Deeper than the tree should allow. She reached in past her wrist, past her elbow, feeling cold dry air move against her skin like breath, and her fingers closed on oilcloth, and leather, and the unmistakable weight of paper bound in string.

Ilya had been many things—smuggler, fence, occasional liar, the best teacher Paloma ever had—but he had never been careless. Whatever he had hidden here, he had hidden it to last. She hauled the bundle out into the light: a packet wrapped in waxed canvas, tied with cord gone stiff as wire, and beneath it a smaller thing wrapped separately, soft, the size of a prayer book.

Ulyana crouched beside her, close enough that their shoulders touched, and said nothing. Paloma was distantly grateful for that. There were silences that pressed down on a person and silences that held them up, and Ulyana, who remembered almost nothing of her own life, had a gift for the second kind.

Paloma untied the cord.

Inside the large bundle: maps, dozens of them, hand-drawn, annotated in Ilya's cramped leftward slanting script—routes through Thornfell she had never seen, passes she had believed were legend, and one map of the under-court itself, far more detailed than the courier's, marked with rooms and guard-rotations and a route out that began and ended in the same place, a loop, a trap, or a promise. Inside the small bundle:

A ledger.

Bound in grey leather gone soft as skin, its corners rounded by handling, its spine cracked from being opened and opened again. Ilya's ledger. She knew it on sight; she had seen him write in its twin for years, recording debts and favors and the small brutal arithmetic of their trade. But this one was different. This one was older. And the first page bore a date thirty-one years past, ink faded to the brown of dried blood.

Paloma opened it anyway. Some doors, once found, cannot be left shut—not honestly, not while tithe-collectors ride the valleys below and a girl sits beside you who sold her soul to save people she can no longer remember.

She read.

And the mountain, which had felt like an enemy all this while, became something worse: a mirror.

* * *

The early entries were ordinary. Debts of grain, debts of silver, a debt of a horse owed to a woman named Marta Voss who had, according to Ilya's marginal note, "died owing me nothing, which makes her the richest person I know." Page after page of the smuggler's economy: favors logged, interest calculated in weeks rather than coin, each entry signed with Ilya's mark—a small hooked glyph like a fishhook, like a question, like a man perpetually asking the world what it intended to do about him.

Then, forty pages in, the entries changed character.

There was a section set apart, ruled off with double lines, titled in letters larger than any others in the book: *THE UNDER-COURT ACCOUNTS.* And beneath it, entry after entry, year after year, in Ilya's hand but written with a care that bordered on ritual—each date recorded, each name, each price.

Names. Dozens of them. Souls sold to the court beneath the mountain, brokered by Ilya Okonkwo's own hand. A cooper from the valley towns who traded his memory of his mother's face for his son's fever broken. A girl of nineteen who sold her grief—her actual grief, weighed and priced—for passage out of Thornfell during the plague years. A shepherd who sold his courage and spent the rest of his life afraid of his own shadow, alive, fed, hollow. Each entry noted the terms, the payment rendered, the court's seal-stamp pressed in wax at the margin. And beside nearly every name, in smaller script, a note that curdled Paloma's stomach as she read: *the client does not understand what they are selling. No one does, until it is gone.*

Ilya had brokered souls. The man who taught her that a contract was sacred, that a word given was a wall built, had spent decades selling people pieces of themselves to a court that wore borrowed faces and ate memories by candlelight.

She had known, in the abstract. The map's annotations had told her he had dealings below. Knowing and reading were different animals. Reading was standing in the room while it happened, watching his pen move, watching him write *she wept when it was done, and thanked me* beside the name of a girl who had sold her grief and would now spend sixty years wondering why the world felt like a corridor with all the paintings removed.

Paloma's hands were shaking. She set the ledger on her knees and breathed, and Ulyana's hand came to rest on her forearm—light, warm, wordless—and stayed there.

"Keep going," Ulyana said softly. "Whatever it is. You'll hate yourself more if you stop."

So she kept going.

Near the end of the under-court accounts, the entries thinned. And then, on a page dated the winter Paloma was nine years old, a single entry, set alone, ruled about with lines like a cell:

*I, Ilya Okonkwo, broker, do record the following transaction, executed this day beneath Thornfell, in the presence of the Lord Beneath and His officers of the quiet bells.*

*Item pledged: my soul entire, held in perpetuity, redeemable by none.*

*Consideration received: the guarantee, binding upon the court and its law, that the soul of Paloma Okonkwo, my ward, shall never be taken, tithed, claimed, or collected by the under-court, its agents, successors, or assigns, for so long as she lives.*

*Term: until my death. Upon my death, the guarantee lapses, and all obligations herein transfer to the said Paloma Okonkwo, who shall stand as debtor in my place.*

*Signed and sealed.*

His mark. The hooked glyph. Pressed deep enough into the paper to emboss the page behind it.

Paloma read it three times. Her eyes moved over the words and the words refused to become meaning, the way a sentence in a dream repeats itself without arriving anywhere. Then meaning arrived all at once, the way cold water arrives, and she made a sound she did not recognize as coming from herself—something between a laugh and a wound.

All those years. All those years she had prayed to her contract-god and received silence, and grieved the silence as abandonment, as punishment, as the slow death of the only covenant that had ever held her. Ten years of kneeling on wet stone with her palms open, telling herself the god had turned away, that she had failed some test, that faith was a muscle she had let die.

Her god had not gone silent. Her god had been *outbid.*

Or—no. Worse. Truer. The god had not stopped answering because it had abandoned her. It had stopped answering because there was nothing left to administer. Ilya had gone beneath the mountain when she was nine years old and purchased, with the whole currency of his existence, a wall around her soul so absolute that even heaven could find no claim on her. The contract-god of her childhood, the one who sealed oaths in fire and counted every vow like coin, had simply... stepped back. There was nothing to enforce. The greatest contract of her life had been signed without her knowledge, in her name, by a man who loved her, and it had made her the one person in Thornfell whom the gods themselves could not touch.

She had spent ten years mourning a silence that was, in fact, the deepest protection anyone had ever bought her.

And now—

Now Ilya was eleven years dead. The term had run. The guarantee had lapsed on the day they put him in the ground, and everything he had pledged had transferred, exactly as written, to the said Paloma Okonkwo, who shall stand as debtor in my place.

She was the collateral. She had always been the collateral. Every safe road she had walked, every collector she had slipped, every night she had slept unclaimed in the high passes—all of it had rested on a dead man's soul sitting in a reliquary beneath the mountain, paying her rent. And the moment he died, the debt had come due, quietly, invisibly, accruing interest for eleven years while she prayed to a silence she mistook for absence.

The tithe-collectors hadn't been hunting the courier's map at all. Or not only. They had been hunting *her.* The court had merely been waiting for someone to carry the debt's location back into its reach. The dying courier, the map, the whisper of *the tithe comes due*—it had all been a summons dressed as a secret. She had fled from the collectors for days, and the whole time she had been running toward the thing that owned her.

"Paloma." Ulyana's voice, careful. "You've gone white as the snowline. What is it?"

Paloma couldn't answer at first. She looked at the girl beside her—at the wolf-shifter who had sold her memories to save her pack, who remembered fragments, who clung to Paloma's map because it was the only thread leading back to what she'd lost—and saw, with a clarity that felt like falling, the shape of the symmetry. Two souls entangled with the same court. One sold knowingly by a desperate girl. One sold unknowingly by a loving man. Both held below. Both marked.

Both, if the ledger's final page was anything to go by, redeemable.

Because there was a final page. Of course there was. Ilya had never in his life left a document unfinished. Paloma turned to it with hands that had gone numb past feeling, and found a single paragraph, written later than the rest—the ink darker, the hand less steady, dated eight days before his death:

*To whoever finds this, and if the world is kind, it will be her:*

*You will hate me. Good. Hate is honest, and honesty is the only inheritance worth leaving a smuggler's daughter. Know this much and let it be enough: I never sold anything that wasn't mine to sell. Everything else in these accounts belongs to my shame. This last page belongs to my love, and I make no apology for it.*

*The guarantee lapses when I die. I could not stop that. Death honors no contracts, not even mine. But a debt transfers, child—it does not vanish. Which means the court holds a claim on you that is real, recorded, and lawful. And a lawful claim can be traded. Remember that. In the under-court, law is the only god, and law can be beaten with its own instruments.*

*Do not come for me. My soul is forfeit and I regret nothing. Come for the ledger. Come for the accounts. Every soul in this book was taken lawfully, and what is taken lawfully can be returned lawfully, if someone is willing to pay the price the law demands. I was willing. I suspect you will be more so, though God forgive me, I pray every day that you won't have to be.*

*The fire below is dying. I saw it when I signed. Ask the dragon.*

*—I.*

Paloma closed the ledger. Held it against her chest. Sat beneath the split oak with the snowmelt dripping from its scarred branches and let the grief come, all of it at once, ten years of misread silence and eleven years of misread death crashing down in a single wave. Ilya had not betrayed her. He had done something so far past betrayal that betrayal would have been simpler—cleaner—easier to survive. He had dismantled her entire understanding of her own life in one paragraph of fading ink. Her god's silence had been a gift. Her isolation had been engineered. Her safety had been purchased at a price she was now, by the pitiless mechanics of contract-law, required to pay forward.

And the last line. *Ask the dragon.*

She had thought it was a figure of speech. A smuggler's flourish. But chapter by chapter, mile by mile, the pieces had been assembling themselves into a picture she didn't want to see: the guttering brazier at the heart of the under-court, the nobles wearing borrowed faces, the souls in glass feeding something. Feeding *someone.*

Feeding a chained dragon whose dying fire kept the whole rotten court alive.

Ulyana knelt in front of her and took her face in both hands—gently, firmly, the way one handles something that might shatter—and looked at her with those grey eyes that had seen too much and remembered too little.

"Tell me," she said.

So Paloma told her. All of it: the ledger, the accounts, the names, the sale, the transfer, the collateral. She told it plainly, because plainness was the only armor she had left, and Ulyana listened the way she seemed to listen to everything—completely, without flinching, as if another person's pain were a country she was willing to walk into barefoot.

When it was done, Ulyana was quiet for a moment. Then she said the thing that broke Paloma open a second time, wider than the first:

"So you've been free this whole time. Really free. Untouchable. And you're grieving it." She shook her head slowly. "That's not ingratitude, Paloma. That's just what it costs, finding out the walls around you were made of someone's love. You don't miss the cage. You miss the person who built it."

Paloma looked at her—at this girl who had lost everything and still knew, instinctively, precisely, what to say to a woman made of locked doors—and felt something shift in her chest that she would not name, not yet, maybe not ever.

"We go down," Paloma said finally. Her voice came out rough, scraped clean. "Not for escape. For the accounts. Every soul in this ledger gets a chance at the door. That's the job now. That's the contract."

She paused. Looked at the last page again, at the words she had read four times already and would read a hundred more.

"And then we ask the dragon."

Above them, far up the white shoulder of Thornfell, thunder rolled—though the sky was clear, and the sound came from underground, from the deep places, like something vast turning over in its sleep. Or waking.

Or counting the days until the fire went out.
