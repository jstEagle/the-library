## 12. The Gate Beneath

They climbed for two days through country that did not want them.

The passes above the tree line were old smuggler roads, and Paloma knew them the way she knew her own scars—which ones still ached, which had gone quiet, which would kill her if she forgot them. She led Ulyana up goat tracks no wider than a boot, across scree fields that shifted like living things, along ledges where the wind came off the icefields hard enough to peel breath from lungs. Twice they sheltered in salt caves where the walls glittered in the dark like frozen stars, and Ulyana pressed her palm to the crystals and said, *it's warm*, and Paloma said nothing, because it wasn't, and because she was beginning to understand that warmth was something Ulyana carried with her rather than found.

The thunder under the mountain followed them. It came at intervals now—every few hours instead of every few days—a deep rolling groan from the roots of Thornfell, as if the mountain were clearing its throat before speaking.

"The dragon-fire," Ulyana said on the second night, huddled against Paloma's shoulder for heat neither of them acknowledged needing. "It's getting worse."

"It's dying." Paloma fed another stick to their small fire. "Everything down there runs on it. When it goes out—" She didn't finish. She'd read Ilya's ledger enough times to know how the sentence ended. Every soul sold feeds it. When the fire gutters out, all stolen souls go free—but the mountain falls.

"Then we're racing it," Ulyana said.

"We're racing it."

Ulyana was quiet a moment. Then, in the tone of someone asking about weather: "What will you say? At the door. When it asks you what you want."

Paloma looked into the fire and felt the question slide between her ribs like a thin blade finding the gap in armor.

"I don't know," she said, which was the truth, and the truth was the only coin the door would take.

* * *

They found the gate on the third morning, in a valley that should not have existed.

The maps called it nothing. The smugglers' songs called it nothing. Paloma had crossed Thornfell eleven times in her life, and she had never seen this place, and she understood immediately that this was not because the place was hidden but because the place hid itself—it showed itself only to those already walking toward it, already lost, already owing.

The valley was a bowl of grey stone ringed by cliffs, floored with grass gone silver with frost though it was high summer everywhere else on the mountain. In its center stood a wall.

Not built. Grown—or exhaled, or wept into being. A single sweep of black stone, smooth as cooled glass, forty feet high, curving away into the cliff faces on either side until rock and wall became indistinguishable. No battlements. No towers. No gatehouse. Just one door, set in the middle of all that dark, tall as three men and narrow as a coffin stood on end, carved from wood so old it had gone the color of dried blood.

No hinges. No handle. No keyhole.

Only words, cut deep into the lintel in a script older than any alphabet Paloma knew—and yet she could read them, the way you can understand a language in a dream:

*WHAT DO YOU WANT MOST?*

"Charming," Paloma said, because her voice worked better than her courage did.

Ulyana said nothing. She had stopped three paces back, and when Paloma glanced at her, she saw the wolf-shifter had gone very still—the particular stillness of an animal standing at the edge of a scent it knows means death. Her eyes had gone amber. Her hands had half-shifted without her noticing, claws sliding out where fingernails should be.

"Ulyana."

"I know this door." Her voice came out low, roughened. "I've dreamed it. Since I sold—I've dreamed this door, and a voice behind it, and my own voice answering, and I can never hear what I answer." She swallowed. "It's the last thing I remember of the selling. Standing here. Answering."

Paloma's hand found hers without her deciding it. Cold claws. Warmer palm. She held on.

"Then we answer better this time," she said.

* * *

The door did not open when approached. It opened when answered—and it asked each traveler alone.

They learned this because when they walked up together, the door simply waited, patient as stone, and after a long moment a pressure settled over them like deep water—not hostile, not cruel, just absolute. A voice that was not a voice spoke inside their skulls, or perhaps inside the mountain, or perhaps inside the space between them where two people's silences touch:

*ONE AT A TIME.*

Paloma stepped back. It seemed important, suddenly, not to hear whatever Ulyana said—not to hold that over her, not to know her that deeply without permission. Some doors you could only go through naked.

Ulyana walked to the threshold alone. Stood before the blood-dark wood. The frost-grass around her feet stirred in a wind that touched nothing else.

And the door asked her.

Paloma couldn't hear the question. But she watched Ulyana's face while it was asked, and what moved across it was not fear. It was grief—old grief, worn smooth by ten years of carrying, surfacing all at once like a body rising from a lake. Ulyana's mouth trembled. Her claws retracted. She pressed both hands flat against the door as if bracing against a gale, and her shoulders shook once, twice, and then she said, clearly, in a voice cracked down the middle:

"My sister. I want my sister. I want to hear her laugh again and I want to tell her I'm sorry and I want—" Her voice broke entirely. She steadied it the way Paloma had taught her to steady a blade grip: deliberately, brutally, by force. "I want her back. That's what I sold everything for. That's what I want most."

Silence.

Then the door sighed—a sound like a great lung emptying after a century of holding breath—and swung inward on darkness, revealing a stair descending into the mountain's throat, steps worn concave by feet older than kingdoms.

Ulyana turned. Her face was wet. She didn't wipe it.

"It let me in," she said, wondering. "It heard me."

"It heard you," Paloma agreed.

"Your turn."

And there it was. The thing she had been walking toward for three days and, if she was honest, for ten years.

Paloma approached the door. Up close it smelled of cold iron and old incense and, faintly, impossibly, of Ilya's pipe smoke—tobacco and juniper, the smell of the man who had raised her, betrayed her, and died owing her everything. Her chest tightened.

The pressure descended. The voice that was not a voice filled her skull.

*WHAT DO YOU WANT MOST?*

She opened her mouth.

Nothing came.

She tried again. Reached for the obvious answers the way a drowning woman reaches for driftwood. *I want justice.* True, but not truest; justice was a job, and jobs ended. *I want Ilya's debt paid.* True, but that was the ledger talking, not her. *I want my god back.* And there her mind flinched away like a hand from a hot stove, because that was close, that was very close, and underneath it lay the thing she had not said aloud in a decade:

*I want to know why he left me.*

*I want to know if I was worth keeping.*

*I want—*

The door waited. The silence behind the question was not impatient. It was worse than impatient. It was compassionate. It was the silence of something that had heard ten thousand liars and could afford to be kind about one more.

*WHAT DO YOU WANT MOST?*

"I—" Paloma's voice failed. Her heart slammed against her ribs. Behind her, Ulyana stood at the edge of hearing, not watching, giving her the same privacy Paloma had given her, and somehow that made it worse, because there was no audience to perform strength for, and without an audience Paloma had no idea who she was.

That was the problem. That was the whole rotten problem, laid bare at forty years old on a mountainside. Ulyana had an answer because Ulyana had lost something and knew its shape. Paloma had lost everything gradually, by inches, by choice, until there was nothing left whose absence she could name. A god who wouldn't speak. A mentor who'd sold her. A profession that required her to be no one. She had spent twenty years building a person out of competence and contraband routes and the discipline of wanting nothing, because wanting nothing meant having nothing to take.

The door wanted her truest want. And Paloma Okonkwo, mercenary, survivor, keeper of every smuggler's road through Thornfell, stood before it and discovered that she had amputated the part of herself that wanted things, and the wound had healed over so cleanly even she couldn't find the scar.

"No," she said aloud. Not to the door—to herself. To the girl she'd been at seventeen, kneeling in the rain, praying to a god who had already begun to go quiet. "No. You don't get to do this. You don't get to make me say it."

The door said nothing. The mountain said nothing. Somewhere far below, the thunder rolled again, closer now, and the frost-grass shivered.

Paloma drew her knife.

"Paloma—" Ulyana's voice, sharp with alarm.

"There are other tolls." She'd heard it in the oldest songs, the ones the smugglers sang only when drunk enough to forget they were singing heresy: *blood answers when the tongue won't.* "Old law. Older than the door. Blood freely given opens what questions cannot."

"That's not— you don't have to—"

"I know what I have to do." She turned her left hand over. The palm, scarred from twenty years of rope and steel. She set the blade against it, just below the lifeline, where the blood ran closest to the surface. "Door," she said formally, the way she'd been taught to address gods and magistrates and men with armies. "I cannot answer your question honestly, because I unmade the part of me that answers it. That is my confession. Take my blood in place of my truth. Freely given. Witnessed."

"Witnessed," Ulyana said instantly, stepping forward. "I witness it."

The door considered.

Paloma cut.

The pain was clean, bright, almost a relief—pain she had chosen, pain with terms. Blood welled black in the grey morning light and dripped from her fist onto the threshold stone, and where it fell, the stone drank it. Actually drank it, the drops vanishing as if into sand, and a hairline seam of red light traced itself across the door's ancient grain, spreading like frost in reverse, like a vein remembering its purpose.

The voice came one last time, softer now, almost gentle:

*A TRUTH DEFERRED IS STILL A DEBT. THE MOUNTAIN KEEPS ACCOUNTS.*

"I know," Paloma said. "I'm counting on it."

The door opened.

* * *

The stair went down farther than stairs should.

They descended for hours, torches lit from Paloma's kit, the walls sweating mineral light—veins of quartz that glowed faint blue-green, so that even without flame they would not have been blind. The air grew warmer as they went, which was wrong, and sweeter, which was worse: honey and woodsmoke and roses, the smell of a feast somewhere below, a feast that had been waiting a very long time.

Ulyana walked close behind her. Closer than the narrow passage required.

"You didn't answer," she said finally. Not accusing. Just saying it, laying it down between them like a card face-up.

"No."

"Because you don't know what you want?"

"Because I know exactly what I want," Paloma said, "and I'll be damned before I say it to a door."

Ulyana was quiet for three steps. Four. Then, softly, with the terrible directness she wielded like everyone else wielded knives: "You could say it to me."

Paloma's boot caught a rut in the stair. She recovered. Kept walking. "Focus on the descent."

"That's not a no."

"It's not anything. Keep moving."

But Ulyana smiled—she heard it in the dark, the small exhale that was half laugh—and when Paloma's hand throbbed where she'd cut it, she reached back without looking and Ulyana took it, careful of the wound, cradling it like something borrowed and breakable, and they went down together through the glowing dark, hand in hand, toward the sound of distant bells.

The bells began an hour later. Thin, silver, wrong—each note slightly too long, ringing past the point where bells should stop, as if the sound itself had been kept alive past its season. Like everything else down here.

The stair ended at an archway hung with curtains of woven silver thread, and beyond it Paloma could see firelight, gold and flickering, and shadows moving in patterns too graceful for people.

Ulyana's grip tightened on her hand.

"Whatever's in there," Paloma murmured, "remember: we're not guests. We're auditors."

"And if they ask us to sit at the table?"

Paloma looked at the curtain, at the light bleeding through its threads, and thought of Ilya's ledger, and the dragon counting days beneath her feet, and the debt written in her own name.

"Then we sit," she said. "And we watch what they eat."

She pushed through the curtain—and the Court of Quiet Bells opened before them, beautiful and waiting, and every face at the frozen feast turned at once to look.
