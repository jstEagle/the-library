## 13. Court of Quiet Bells

The gate let them through, and the world changed.

Ulyana had expected darkness. What she got instead was light—soft, sourceless, the color of candleflame seen through milk. It came from everywhere and nowhere, pooling on stone steps that spiraled down and down beneath the mountain's roots. The air was warm. That was the first wrong thing, though she didn't know it yet. They had walked for days through passes where the wind cut like a flensing knife, and now the air wrapped around her like a mother's arms.

"Stay close," Paloma murmured behind her. The mercenary's voice was flat as slate, but Ulyana had learned to read her—the tightness at the jaw, the way her thumb kept finding the hilt of the knife she'd named after a river she'd never explained. "Don't eat anything. Don't drink anything. Don't tell anyone your name."

"You've said."

"I'll say it again until we're out."

Ulyana touched the map folded against her heart, Ilya's spidery annotations ghosting under the leather of her coat. *Third landing, mind the bells.* She could feel them before she heard them—a pressure in her back teeth, a hum in the marrow of her ribs. Then, as they rounded the spiral's third turning, the sound arrived: bells, hundreds of them, ringing somewhere far below in a key that made her think of funerals she'd attended as a cub, of snow falling on open graves.

Except the bells weren't sad. That was the second wrong thing. They were *welcoming*.

"They know we're here," she said.

"The bells always ring," Paloma said. "That's what Ilya's notes say. They rang when he came, too." A pause, long enough for Ulyana to hear the mercenary swallow. "He wrote that he found it beautiful."

"And you?"

Paloma didn't answer. Her silence was its own kind of bell.

* * *

The stairs ended at an archway carved from a single piece of black stone, and beyond it opened the court itself—and Ulyana forgot, for one full breath, every warning Paloma had ever given her.

It was a feast hall built for giants. Vaulted ceilings soared up into shadow, hung with banners of cloth that moved like water though there was no wind. Long tables of pale wood stretched away into the distance, laden with food that gleamed like jewels: roast birds with skin of beaten gold, bread that glowed faintly from within, fruits the color of bruises and dawn. And people—hundreds of them—seated along the tables in garments of silver and deep green, their faces lit from below by candles that never guttered.

Music played. She couldn't see the musicians. The melody wound through the hall like smoke, sweet and slow, and it tugged at something in her chest, some memory-shaped hollow that ached to be filled. *This is what home sounds like,* the music whispered. *This is what you lost. Come sit. Come eat. Come remember.*

Beside her, Paloma made a low sound in her throat, like a dog deciding whether to growl.

"It's beautiful," Ulyana breathed.

"It's bait," Paloma said. "Look closer. Look at the *people*."

Ulyana looked. And the wonder began, slowly, to curdle.

The feasters were not eating. Their hands held cups and cutlery in perfect attitudes of enjoyment, but nothing moved—not a bite taken, not a sip drunk, not a laugh finished. A woman in green sat with her head tipped back, mouth open on a joke she would never complete. A man beside her raised his glass in a toast frozen mid-gesture, wine trembling at the lip but never spilling. Their chests did not rise. Their eyes, when Ulyana crept close enough to see, were open and fixed on the middle distance, and in them was an expression she recognized with a lurch: the look of someone watching something wonderful just over the horizon of the world.

"They're alive," she whispered. "Paloma—they're *alive*. I can smell it. Blood, breath—"

"Frozen," Paloma said. "Mid-feast. Look at the food—it hasn't rotted. Nothing here rots. Nothing here *ends*." Her voice dropped lower. "Ilya's notes called it the centennial tithe. Every hundred years, the court wakes for a feast. Between feasts—" she gestured at the still figures, the impossible abundance, the music playing to no one— "*between* feasts, this is what they are. Paused. Preserved. Waiting for the fire to be fed so the clock can start again."

"The fire?"

Paloma pointed, and Ulyana followed her finger down the length of the great hall to its heart.

There stood a brazier the size of a wagon, wrought iron shaped into climbing roses and strangling vines, and within it burned a flame unlike any she had ever seen. It was red—but not fire-red. It was the red of the inside of a living body, of blood seen through closed eyelids, and it moved wrong, swelling and shrinking like the breathing of some vast sleeping thing. Its light reached every corner of the hall, and yet it cast no shadows. As Ulyana watched, the flame stuttered—flickered down to almost nothing, then swelled again, laboring, like a heart missing beats.

Even from across the hall, she felt it. A pull. A hunger answered by hunger. Something in her soul leaned toward that fire like a plant toward sun.

"That's dragon-fire," Paloma said quietly. "And it's dying."

* * *

They moved along the edges of the hall, two shadows among many, and Ulyana saw more of what wonder had hidden from her.

The nobles wore borrowed faces.

She understood it gradually, in pieces, each one worse than the last. First it was small things: a lord whose eyes were amber and slit-pupiled, set in a face otherwise soft and human; a lady whose hands, as she lifted her frozen cup, showed the fine grey scales of a serpent at the wrist. Then it was larger: a countess whose beauty was *arranged*, somehow, features assembled like a bouquet rather than grown—and beneath the arrangement, glimpsed when the light shifted, something older and hungrier looking out through the borrowed skin with an expression of profound boredom.

One figure near the high table turned its head—slowly, impossibly, while all around it stayed frozen—to watch them pass. Its face was a young man's face, handsome and mild, but the eyes were ancient, and the mouth smiled a fraction wider than a mouth should.

Ulyana's hackles rose. The wolf under her skin pressed close to the surface, growling without sound.

"Don't," Paloma breathed. "Don't shift. Don't meet its eyes. Keep walking."

The head turned back. The smile remained, aimed now at the middle distance, patient as stone.

"They're wearing us," Ulyana said. Her voice shook. "Those faces—some of them are human faces. Taken faces."

"Soul-tithe buys more than souls," Paloma said. "It buys shape. It buys years. It buys everything a person is, portioned out and served at table." She was quiet a moment. "Ilya brokered deals like this. He told himself he was saving lives—the desperate ones who came to him would have died anyway. He told himself the court only took what was freely sold." Her jaw tightened. "I believed him. Gods help me, I believed him for twenty years."

Ulyana looked at the frozen feast—at the golden birds and the glowing bread and the beautiful, vacant faces—and thought about a burning den, a sister's laugh, a price paid in the dark to save what could be saved. Had her own sale looked like this from the other side? Had someone stood over her sleeping form and told themselves a comforting story?

She didn't remember. That was the whole horror of it. She had sold the memory of selling, and so she could not even properly grieve.

"Paloma," she said. "My soul. Is it here? Can you feel it?"

Paloma stopped walking. For a moment Ulyana thought she wouldn't answer. Then the mercenary nodded once, toward the far end of the hall, past the high table, where a doorway glowed with soft blue light.

"The reliquary vaults," she said. "Ilya's map marks them. If your soul is anywhere, it's there."

* * *

The reliquary antechamber was smaller than the feast hall, round, walled floor to ceiling in glass.

Not windows—*shelves*. Thousands upon thousands of glass vessels lined the curved walls, rising beyond sight into the dark above, each one glowing with its own faint inner light. Some shone white-gold. Some pulsed slow and red. Some flickered weakly, guttering like the great flame in the brazier, and some were dark—empty glass, polished clean, waiting.

Ulyana stepped inside and the humming started.

It began in her teeth, spread to her skull, filled her whole body with a resonance like a struck tuning fork. The lights on the shelves *answered* her—rippling, brightening, a wave of recognition rolling outward from wherever she stood, thousands of souls stirring in their glass like moths in a jar sensing an open window.

"They know me," she whispered. "Paloma, they—"

"Find yours," Paloma said. "Quickly. Before whatever runs this place notices us noticing."

Ulyana moved down the rows, searching, drawn by instinct deeper than memory. Each vessel bore a small plate etched with characters she couldn't read, but she didn't need to read them. She was hunting her own scent across a hundred miles of forest, and the trail was hot, and getting hotter, and then—

There.

On a shelf at the height of her heart, in a niche worn smooth by centuries of attention, sat a reliquary of smoked glass, and inside it burned a light she knew the way she knew her own heartbeat.

It was *her*. Not a picture of her—an essence. Warm gold shot through with threads of silver, and moving within it, shapes: the suggestion of running legs, of pointed ears, of a den and a fire and small bodies pressed together for warmth. Her pack. Her sister. Everything she'd traded away, burning gently in its prison, and as she drew close the light surged against the glass like a wave against a seawall, pressing, straining, reaching for her with something that felt unbearably like *recognition*.

Ulyana put her hand against the glass.

The warmth bled through into her palm. Somewhere very far away, or very deep inside, she heard—or remembered hearing—a girl laughing, the specific bright laugh of a sister, and the sound cracked something open in her chest that she hadn't known was sealed.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, there you are. There you *are*."

Her vision blurred. She was distantly aware of Paloma at the chamber's entrance, keeping watch, knife drawn, coiled and silent. She was aware of nothing else but the light, the warmth, the way her own soul pressed itself against the barrier between them like a child at a window watching a parent walk away.

"We're going to get you out," she heard herself say. Her voice came out ragged. "I don't know how yet. But I found you. Do you understand? I *found* you."

The light flared—bright, brighter, blinding—and for one instant the glass went clear as water, and Ulyana saw reflected in it not her own face but a feast hall, whole and awake, crowded with laughing people, and herself among them, whole, unafraid, a hand warm in hers—

Then the vision snapped out. The light sank back to its gentle prisoner's glow. And in the sudden dimness, Ulyana became aware of a new sound in the antechamber: footsteps. Unhurried. Soft on stone. Coming from a part of the vault where there had been no door.

A figure resolved out of the dark between the shelves. Tall. Robed in the same deep green as the frozen court. Wearing a young man's handsome, mild face—and smiling that fraction too wide.

Behind it, faint and far off across the great hall, the dragon-fire gave a long, shuddering stutter, and every soul in every vessel on every shelf flickered in answer, like ten thousand candles bowing to the same wind.

The Lord Beneath had come to see who was visiting his collection.

"Ah," it said, in a voice like the bells, welcoming and terrible. "The wolf returns. Tell me, little thief—did you miss what's mine?"
