## 14. The Bargain Broker's Debt

The bells woke her.

Not the quiet bells of the court—those had been ringing since they came through the gate, a sound like teeth clicking in a dead man's jaw—but something sharper, closer, coming up the corridor of the guest wing where the Lord Beneath's servants had lodged them. Paloma was on her feet with a knife in her hand before her eyes finished opening.

Ulyana was already awake. She sat upright on the narrow bed across the room, wrapped in the grey wool blanket the servants had left, and her eyes were fixed on the door the way a deer fixes on a treeline.

"That's not the court," Ulyana said. "That's outside."

Paloma listened. Under the bell-sound, faint as a heartbeat through stone, came another rhythm: footsteps. Many of them. And beneath that, a smell threading under the door that did not belong to this place of cold candlelight and frozen feasts—the smell of the surface. Wet pine. Woodsmoke. Oiled leather.

And under all of it, sweet as rot: dragon-fire.

"Sef," Paloma said.

* * *

They had been in the under-court for two days. Two days of being fed by silent servants whose faces were almost right but never quite—eyes set a half-finger too wide apart, smiles that arrived a beat after the words that required them. Two days of walking halls where the feast tables stood laden with food no one ate, where nobles in brocade drifted past wearing faces Paloma could have sworn she'd seen on corpses along the smuggler roads, on missing-person notices nailed to shrine doors, on the wanted bills Sef's people posted in every market town from Thornfell's spine to the southern passes.

Two days of Ulyana pressing her palm against glass cases in the reliquary corridors, searching for the shape of herself among ten thousand small lights, and finding nothing yet—and worse, finding other things. A light the size of a child's fist that flickered when she passed. Another that guttered entirely, as if it had heard her coming and flinched.

Paloma had spent those two days doing what she did best: learning the exits. There were three she'd found so far, and all three were wrong—doors that opened onto stone, doors that opened onto the feast hall again no matter how many turns she took, one door that opened onto a corridor lined with bells, each one humming a name when she walked past. Hers had hummed. She hadn't stopped walking.

Now the bells were ringing for someone else.

She dressed quickly, strapped her knives where the court's servants could see them and two where they couldn't, and put her hand on the door.

"Paloma." Ulyana's voice behind her. "If it's him—if he's come to trade—you don't have to—"

"I know what I have to do."

"You don't." Ulyana crossed the room, bare feet silent on cold flagstone, and caught Paloma's wrist. Her grip was careful, always careful, even now—even with the wolf so close under her skin that Paloma could see the amber ring around her irises. "You've had that look since the gate. Since you bled on the threshold rather than answer its question. You've been carrying something, and you think if you set it down hard enough it'll break before it breaks you."

Paloma looked at her hand where it lay in Ulyana's grip. Ten years ago, Ilya had held her hand the same way, the night he made her swear the vow: *Never sell what cannot be bought back. Never stand surety for another soul. The god of contracts is silent, little wren, and silence is the only mercy he has left us.*

"Let go," Paloma said quietly.

Ulyana let go. That was the thing about her—that was the thing that had cracked Paloma open over these weeks on the road, more than any argument or plea. She asked. She reached. And when the answer was no, she stepped back, and she stayed anyway.

"The bells are for Sef," Paloma said. "He's come to collect. Which means the court summoned him, which means—" She flexed her fingers. "Which means they already know why we're here. And there's only one law in any court, above or below, that matters when a debt comes due."

"What law?"

"*Payment.*" Paloma opened the door. "Stay close. And Ulyana—whatever happens in that hall, whatever you hear me say—don't interrupt. A contract spoken over is a contract voided, and I can't afford voids today."

Ulyana's face went pale. But she nodded, and pulled the blanket around her shoulders like a mantle, and followed.

* * *

The feast hall had changed overnight.

The frozen banquet was still there—roasts glazed and gleaming, wine standing mid-pour from tilted ewers, a lord's laugh hanging in the air like smoke—but the court had arranged itself around the central brazier in concentric rings, nobles kneeling on cushions of black velvet, and the dragon-fire in the brazier had burned lower. Paloma noticed it immediately, the way a sailor notices a change in the wind. Two days ago it had roared. Now it breathed. Long slow exhalations of red-gold light, and between them, moments where the whole hall dimmed and every borrowed face in the crowd looked briefly like what it was: wax. Clay. Hunger wearing a mask.

At the far end of the hall, on a throne of fused glass that must have been poured, not built, sat the Lord Beneath. He had not aged since their arrival—or perhaps he had; his face kept shifting at the edges, a handsome man in his prime, then an old soldier, then something with too many teeth, depending on the angle and the light. His eyes found Paloma the moment she entered, and he smiled with genuine warmth, which was the most frightening thing she had seen below the mountain.

"Wren," he said, using Ilya's old name for her, and her blood went cold. "Come down. Your broker is here, and he brings papers."

The crowd parted. And there, walking up the center aisle with the unhurried gait of a man who has never once been late for an appointment, came Sef.

He looked exactly as he had in the market town at Coldharbour Row: neat beard, merchant's rings, a ledger chained to his belt, ink-stained fingers. Only his eyes were different. In the market town they had been calculating. Now they were satisfied, and satisfaction on Sef's face was worse than any rage.

"Mercenary," he said pleasantly, stopping six paces away. Close enough to talk. Far enough that her knives would have to travel. He'd learned that much from Coldharbour Row, where she'd put a blade through the shoulder of his lieutenant. "You cost me two collectors and a season's reputation. Do you know what that does to a man's standing among brokers?"

"It improves it," Paloma said. "Everyone wants to deal with the man who survived me."

A ripple of laughter went through the kneeling court—a sound like dry leaves—and Sef's smile thinned. He unclasped the ledger from his belt and held it up.

"I've come to collect, mercenary. Not the map—I have the map. I've had it since the pass." He watched her absorb that. Watched her realize that the beacon-fire on the high pass, the night Ulyana shifted and forgot her own name screaming into the storm, had never been about signaling the court at all. It had been about getting *inside*. "I mean the debt. The old one. The one your mentor signed in blood and ink thirty years ago, and which transferred to you upon his death, per clause nine, which you never read because you never knew it existed."

"Ilya's debt is settled," Paloma said. "Whatever he sold, he paid."

"Oh, he paid." Sef flipped the ledger open with one finger, and the pages turned themselves, whispering. "He paid everything. But debts below don't end, Wren. They *transfer*. That's the beauty of the system. A soul pledged here isn't consumed—it's held. As collateral. Against future payment." He looked up, and his eyes glittered. "Your mentor pledged himself to guarantee that *your* soul would never be taken. Noble. Foolish. Because when he died, the guarantee died with him, and the protection lapsed, and the court's claim reverted to the next of kin. To the heir of the contract."

The hall was very quiet. Even the bells had stopped.

"To me," Paloma said. Not a question. She had known since Ilya's cache, since the ledger with her name written in the margins in his cramped hand—*she was always the collateral*—but hearing it aloud, in this place, before this court, made it real in a way paper never could. Somewhere in her chest, the old wound ached: the silent god, the broken contract, ten years of prayers into nothing. All of it suddenly legible. *This* was why the god had gone quiet. Not abandoned. *Foreclosed.*

"To you," Sef agreed warmly. "Which brings us to the matter at hand. The wolf-girl." He gestured, and two of his remaining collectors—new men, hard-faced, scarred where the old ones had died—brought forward a litter, and on the litter, under silk, lay something that made Ulyana make a sound Paloma had never heard from her: a small, wounded, human noise.

On the litter, in a case of smoked glass, burned a light. Amber-gold, restless, throwing shadows like running paws against the walls. Ulyana's soul. Pulled from the reliquary vaults and carried here like merchandise to a market stall.

"The court is prepared to be generous," Sef said. "The centennial tithe-feast approaches. The Lord Beneath dislikes unpleasantness during the feast. So: terms. The mercenary surrenders all claim—hers, her mentor's, any claim arising from any contract, past or present—to the soul of Ulyana Varga. In exchange, the court releases her from Ilya Okonkwo's transferred debt. She walks out free. Clear. Unencumbered." He snapped the ledger shut. "The girl stays. The soul stays. Everyone leaves happy except one person, and she's a wolf, and who counts wolves?"

"No," Ulyana said.

It was the first time she had spoken since entering the hall, and her voice carried in a way that made several of the kneeling nobles flinch. She stepped forward, out from behind Paloma's shoulder, blanket still around her shoulders, chin up.

"She doesn't speak for me. I sold myself. My choice, my price, my pack saved. And I'll tell you what I told the thing that bought me: I'll walk out of here with my soul or I'll walk out of here without anything, but *she* walks free either way. That was the bargain when we came down the mountain. That's the bargain now."

"Ulyana." Paloma didn't turn her head. "I told you not to interrupt."

"And I told you on the ford that you couldn't abandon me, and you didn't listen either." Her hand found Paloma's sleeve—not gripping, just touching. Anchoring. "We're both bad at taking good advice. It's the only thing we have in common besides terrible cooking."

Despite everything—despite the glass case and the guttering fire and the smiling lord on his glass throne—something in Paloma's chest turned over. Warm. Fatal.

Sef watched them both with the expression of a man watching interest accrue.

"There is," he said softly, "one alternative. Though I hesitate to mention it, as it rarely ends well for the party proposing it."

"Say it anyway," said the Lord Beneath from his throne, and his voice filled the hall like water filling a cellar. "The feast loves drama. Give it some."

Sef bowed toward the throne, then turned back to Paloma, and now his voice dropped into the register he used for closing deals, smooth as river stone: "The law of the under-court, older than the Lord Beneath, older than the fire itself: *one soul may be redeemed only by an equal pledge.* Not equal in size. Equal in weight. Equal in what it costs the giver." He tapped the ledger. "The girl's soul was given to save others. Forfeit freely, for love, under duress of circumstance. To redeem it requires a pledge of the same character. A soul given freely, for love, knowing the full price."

The hall waited. The dragon-fire breathed. In the silence Paloma could hear the smallest sounds: the tick of cooling glass somewhere in the walls. Ulyana's breath, quick and shallow beside her. Her own heart, steady, steady, the way it always was when everything was ending.

"You're asking me to pledge my soul," Paloma said.

"I'm asking nothing. I'm *describing* the law." Sef spread his hands, all innocence, all ink. "What you do with the description is your affair. Though I'll note—for the record, for the ledger, for posterity—that yours is a soul already encumbered. Transferred debt. Foreclosed contract. A god's lien, ten years outstanding." His smile sharpened. "A pledged soul pledging again. The court's lawyers would call it *poetry*. They would also call it *binding*, because the last vow you swore still holds, doesn't it, Wren? Never stand surety for another soul. Your mentor's dying command. Break it, and everything he bought you with—every year of freedom, every road unwatched, every prayer he thought he was answering—breaks with it."

There it was. The trap inside the trap. Not the pledge itself—Sef didn't care whether she pledged; the court would win either way, feeding on her soul or Ulyana's, keeping its fire lit another century. The trap was the *vow*. The last thread tying her to Ilya, to the life she'd built on the ruins of his betrayal, to the identity she'd worn like armor for ten years: *the woman who keeps her word. The woman who trusts no one and owes no one.*

Break the vow, and she was nothing. Keep it, and Ulyana burned in glass forever, and Paloma walked out alone into a spring she'd no longer have anyone to share with, and that, she understood suddenly, was the true price. Not her soul. Her solitude. The court wasn't offering to take her aloneness. It was offering to *preserve* it. Forever.

Paloma looked at Ulyana.

Ulyana was looking at the floor. She had gone very still, the stillness of the wolf before the leap or the retreat, and her knuckles were white where they gripped the blanket, and she was not going to argue. She had said her piece. She had offered herself first, as she always did, as she had on the pass when she shifted and lost her own memories to save a woman she couldn't remember the name of. That was the arithmetic of her. She would spend herself down to nothing and call it fair.

*Ilya,* Paloma thought. *You bastard. You beautiful, cowardly bastard. You tried to buy my safety with your soul, and you never once asked what I'd want to buy with yours.*

She stepped forward.

"Then witness," she said, and her voice rang off the glass walls, off the frozen feast, off the ten thousand borrowed faces, "and record, and let every bell in this mountain bear me out."

"Paloma—" Ulyana's hand closed on her sleeve, really closed now, and Paloma covered it with her own. Held it. Did not look away from Sef.

"I, Paloma Okonkwo, daughter of no house, heir of Ilya Okonkwo, do hereby enter pledge." Each word came out steady, and each word cost her like a rib drawn one by one. "My soul, whole and entire, freely given—not traded, not sold, *given*—in exchange for the soul of Ulyana Varga, held in trust by this court. I know the price. I pay it with open eyes. And I break—" her throat closed, and she forced it open, "—I break the vow I swore to my mentor, the last vow, the vow of never. Let it be broken. Let him forgive me or let him burn with the rest of this place. I choose her."

The silence afterward was absolute.

Then the dragon-fire in the brazier *roared*—a great convulsive flare, red-gold light slamming across the hall, shadows leaping—and every noble in the court turned to look at it, and Paloma saw their faces in that light and understood: the fire had felt the pledge. The fire fed on souls, and a soul freely given was a meal such as it had not tasted in a hundred years, and it was *hungry*.

Sef was writing. The ledger's pages turned themselves under his pen, ink flowing without touch, recording, sealing. "Witnessed," he murmured, almost reverent. "Recorded. Binding upon—" He stopped. Frowned. Looked down at the page.

Paloma saw it too. Saw the ink stutter. Saw a line of text rewrite itself, letters crawling like ants: *binding upon the pledgor... save where the pledge is superseded by prior gift...*

"What," Sef said slowly, "is *that*?"

The Lord Beneath rose from his throne. And for the first time since the gate, since the frozen feast, since the beginning of everything, the Lord Beneath looked afraid.

"Someone," he said, and his voice shook the glass in the walls, "has been giving gifts in my mountain."

Behind Paloma, Ulyana's grip tightened—and Paloma felt it then, faint as a second pulse, warm as a coal cupped in a palm: something moving along the bond the pledge had just carved through her chest. Something that had been there since the ford, maybe. Since the campfire. Since the first night a terrified naked girl had begged her for a map and Paloma, who trusted no one, had handed it over.

Something already given. Already hers. Already freely paid.

The bells began to ring—all of them at once, every corridor, every hall, a sound like a mountain laughing—and the dragon-fire guttered low, low, lower—

And the Lord Beneath screamed: "*Find out what she gave!*"
