## 15. A Sister in Glass

The reliquary vaults lay beneath the frozen feast-hall, down a stair that had not been built so much as grown—each step a pale rib of stone, slick as ice, curving into the dark. Ulyana counted them out of habit, the way she'd once counted the turns of a deer trail before dawn, and lost her count somewhere past two hundred when the walls began to change.

The stone here was veined with glass.

It caught what little light Paloma's stolen lantern gave and bent it into colors that didn't belong underground: green like spring water, gold like honey held up to sun, and once—a color Ulyana had no name for, something between grief and memory that made her chest ache until she looked away.

"Stay close," Paloma murmured behind her. The mercenary moved like smoke, blade drawn even though they'd seen no one since slipping from the feast-hall's edge. "The court sleeps shallow here. Everything below is listening."

*Everything below is listening.* Ulyana was beginning to understand that this was not a figure of speech. The vaults breathed. She could feel it against her skin, a slow tidal pull, as if the whole mountain inhaled through these corridors and exhaled somewhere she couldn't name. And threaded through that breath, faint as a hair across her palm, ran something she knew.

Her own scent.

Not her body-scent—the wolf-smell of pine and blood and river mud that clung to her always—but the other thing, the thing the court had taken. Her soul left a trail the way a wounded animal leaves blood on snow, and Ulyana followed it with her nose because her nose had never lied to her, not once, not even now with half of who she'd been scraped out and sold.

"It's this way." She turned left where the corridor forked, and felt rather than saw Paloma's surprise. "Don't ask how I know."

"I wasn't going to." A pause. "Your nose?"

"My nose."

Paloma said nothing else, but Ulyana heard the ghost of something in her breathing that might have been, in another woman, a laugh.

* * *

The first reliquary room stopped them both at the threshold.

Shelves rose floor to ceiling, carved from the same ribbed stone, and on every shelf stood vessels of glass—some no larger than an egg, some tall as a wine jug, some shaped like hearts or hands or, horribly, like small sleeping birds. Each one held light. Not firelight, not moonlight, but a light that moved on its own, slow and searching, pressing against the inside of its prison the way a moth presses against a window.

Ulyana had imagined finding her soul would feel like coming home. She had imagined it might sing to her, or warm her hands, or at the very least *recognize* her.

What she felt instead was the sound of the nearest vessel—a little egg-shaped thing on the lowest shelf—and what it made was not song. It was weeping. Thin, continuous, patient weeping, the kind a child does after everyone has stopped coming to check on it.

She put her hand over her mouth.

"Don't touch them," Paloma said quietly. She'd gone very still, the way she went still before violence, and her eyes were moving shelf by shelf with the flat horror of someone reading a battlefield. "Some of these are old. Centuries old. Look at the dust lines—some haven't been disturbed since before Ilya was born."

"They're alive in there." Ulyana's voice came out wrong, scraped. "They're all *alive* in there."

"That's what they're for." Paloma crouched beside the lowest shelf, studying the weeping vessel without touching it. Her face did the thing it always did when she was keeping herself together by taking things apart—cataloguing, measuring, turning feeling into information. "A soul in glass keeps. Like meat in salt. The court doesn't eat them all at once. It rations."

"Rations." The word sat in Ulyana's stomach like a swallowed stone. "Rations *what?*"

"The dragon-fire. You saw it guttering in the brazier upstairs. Something has to feed it." Paloma stood, and for a moment her composure slipped—not much, just enough that Ulyana could see the girl underneath the mercenary, the one who had prayed every night for ten years to a god that never answered. "We knew this. We came anyway. Keep walking."

But Ulyana lingered one heartbeat longer, looking down at the weeping little vessel, and made it a promise the way wolves make promises—with her whole body, silently, irrevocably. *I'm coming back for you. All of you.*

Then she turned and followed her nose deeper into the mountain.

* * *

The second chamber was hers.

She knew it before she crossed the threshold, the way you know your own den. The air tasted of her childhood—of woodsmoke and wet fur and the particular green smell of the forest after the first thaw—and that was impossible, that was *wrong*, because the court had taken her memories and yet here they were, soaked into the walls like rain into earth.

Glass cases lined this room too, but fewer of them, and set apart with more care, the way a collector sets apart his favorites. In the center of the room, on a pedestal of black stone, stood a vessel taller than the rest, shaped like a teardrop, and inside it burned a light the color of Ulyana's own eyes.

Gold. Amber. Wolf-gold.

"There," she whispered, and her voice cracked down the middle. "That's—that's me. That's—"

She was moving before she finished the sentence. Her fingers closed around the glass and it was warm, it was *warm*, it pulsed under her palms like a second heartbeat, and for one blinding moment she felt the edges of herself begin to knit—

And then the memories hit her.

They came through the glass like water through a cracked dam: a sister's laugh, high and bright, ringing off the pines. Small hands braiding flowers into Ulyana's hair while their mother watched from the den-mouth. Fire. Fire everywhere, red-orange-black, the den collapsing, screaming—her own scream, and another voice screaming *run, Ulyana, run*—and then cold, and then a table of black stone, and then a voice like dry silk saying *the debt is accepted, the tithe is sealed,* and then nothing, nothing, nothing, ten years of nothing where a life should have been.

Ulyana came back to herself on her knees, forehead pressed to the warm glass, tears freezing on her cheeks in the vault's deep cold.

"Ulyana." Paloma's hand hovered over her shoulder, not quite touching. Even now, even here, the woman asked permission for contact as if touch were a contract that required consent. "Breathe. What did you see?"

"A sister." Ulyana pushed herself up. Her legs shook. "I have a sister. I *had*—" She stopped. The memory had shown her the sister laughing, the sister braiding flowers, the sister screaming *run*—but never the sister's fate. The fire had eaten everything after. "I don't know if she lived. I don't know if she's—"

She turned back to the teardrop vessel, and this time she looked past it. Past the pedestal. Past the shelves of her own scattered years.

To the far wall, where a narrow door of grey wood stood slightly ajar, and through the gap came a smell that stopped Ulyana's heart.

Woodsmoke. Wet fur. Pine.

Home.

* * *

Later, Ulyana would try to remember whether she opened that door or whether it opened for her, and she would never be able to say. She only remembered the hinge's silence—oiled, maintained, *used*—and then the room beyond, and then the girl.

The room was a servant's cell, small and clean, with a narrow bed, a basin, a single candle burning low. And sitting on the edge of the bed, brushing out her hair with long slow strokes, was a young woman of perhaps nineteen summers.

She had Ulyana's eyes.

Not similar. *The same.* Gold-amber, wolf-gold, catching the candlelight exactly the way Ulyana's own reflection did. The same dark hair, worn longer. The same strong jaw, softened by youth. When the girl looked up at the door's opening, her face arranged itself into polite blankness—the smooth, empty courtesy of the court's servants—and she said:

"Welcome, guest. Will you take wine? The feast is served above; I am to pour for the high table tonight."

Her voice was Ulyana's mother's voice. Younger. But the same.

The brush kept moving through her hair. Stroke. Stroke. Stroke. Her eyes were clear and calm and utterly vacant, and Ulyana understood, in a single sickening lurch, what she was seeing. Memory-wiped. Hollowed. Kept. Not dead—never dead, the court didn't waste what it could use—just *emptied*, poured out and shelved somewhere in the rooms behind them, wearing a body that still rose each day and poured wine and smiled its smooth smile and never once wondered why the mountains smelled like home.

"No," Ulyana heard herself say. Her voice sounded like it came from the bottom of a well. "No wine."

"As you wish, guest." The girl returned to her brushing. Stroke. Stroke. Then, without any change in tone, without any flicker in those gold-amber eyes: "You have my face."

Ulyana's knees nearly went.

"I—" She took a step forward, and the girl didn't flinch, didn't react at all, just kept brushing. "What's your name?"

"I am called Cupbearer." Stroke. "Names are given by the Lord Beneath. I have not earned one."

"You had a name." Ulyana's voice was shaking now, and she didn't care. "You had a name and a mother and a den in the pinewood and a sister who loved you more than her own—"

"Guest." The girl set down the brush with perfect gentleness and looked up, and her vacant eyes were suddenly, terribly kind. "You're distressed. Shall I fetch the steward? Distressed guests are given poppy-wine and rest."

"*Your name.*"

A pause. Somewhere far above, muffled through leagues of stone, a bell tolled—one of the quiet bells of the court, marking some hour that meant nothing to the living. The girl tilted her head, listening to it, and for just a moment—half a heartbeat, less—something moved behind her eyes. A shadow of a shadow. A fish glimpsed under winter ice.

Then it was gone, and she smiled her smooth smile, and said:

"I had a sister once. I don't know why I said that. Isn't that strange?"

* * *

Ulyana did not remember crossing the room. One moment she was standing; the next she had the girl in her arms, holding her the way you hold something already broken, careful and desperate all at once, and the girl went stiff with confusion and then—slowly, helplessly, like a body remembering an old dance—melted into the embrace.

"Oh," the girl said softly, into Ulyana's shoulder. "Oh, that's... that's familiar. Why is that familiar?"

"Because I'm your sister." Ulyana was crying openly now, great silent heaves that shook them both. "Because your name is Vesna, and I used to braid flowers into your hair, and when the den burned you told me to run, and I ran, and I have been running for ten years without knowing what I was running toward—"

"Vesna." The girl tried the syllables like a foreign word. "Vesna. It tastes like... like spring. Why does it taste like spring?"

"Because Mama named you for the thaw. Because you were born in the melting snows and you laughed the first time you ever touched green grass, and I remember, Vesna, I *remember*—"

"But I don't." Vesna pulled back, and her smooth courtesy had fractured, and underneath it was fear—the animal fear of a creature whose cage has been named. "I don't remember anything. There's only the pouring and the bells and the long stairs, and sometimes a dream about fire, and—" Her hand rose to her own chest, pressing flat, as if checking that her heart still beat. "There's a hole where I should be. I've always known there was a hole. I thought everyone had one."

Ulyana caught that hand and pressed it against the teardrop vessel's warmth through her own shirt, against her own hammering heart.

"Not everyone," she said. "Just us. Just the ones they robbed."

* * *

Paloma stood in the doorway, and Ulyana saw her clearly for the first time in minutes—saw the mercenary's face stripped of all its armor, saw her staring at Vesna with an expression Ulyana had no name for. Grief, maybe. Or recognition. Or the terrible arithmetic of a woman who had spent her life among contracts, watching a debt compound in real time.

"How many?" Paloma asked, and her voice was rough. "How many do they keep like this? Servants. Cupbearers. How many hollowed people pour wine up there?"

Vesna blinked at her, courteous again, the moment of breaking already smoothed over. "All the household serves the high table. There are forty-one of us. We are rotated seasonally, so the guests do not grow attached."

"Forty-one." Paloma closed her eyes briefly. When she opened them, the flat battlefield look was back, but hotter now, banked fire instead of cold steel. "Ulyana. Your soul is on that pedestal. Hers—" she nodded at Vesna, "—is on some shelf in the last room, or the next one, or a hundred rooms we haven't found. And there are forty more bodies upstairs pouring wine for nobles wearing borrowed faces."

"I know."

"This stopped being about you the moment we walked down those stairs. You understand that. If we break the reliquaries—if we free what's in the glass—we're not freeing one wolf-girl's memories. We're declaring war on the entire under-court, and every soul in every vessel becomes either a refugee we shepherd out or a hostage they execute. There's no middle road. There's no smuggling our way out of this one."

Ulyana looked at her sister—at Vesna, who was watching them both with the polite blankness cracking wider now, curiosity leaking through like light under a door, because whatever else they'd taken from her, they hadn't taken the wolf's instinct to know her own pack.

"I spent ten years not knowing what I lost," Ulyana said. "I'm not spending one more hour pretending I can walk away from *this*." She gestured—at Vesna, at the doorway, at the whole buried mountain full of weeping glass. "Every one of them. The souls, the servants, all of it. We burn the tithe-ledgers, we shatter the vessels, we carry out whoever can walk. And if the mountain comes down on our heads—"

She stopped. Because Vesna had risen from the bed, and taken Ulyana's hand, and was looking at her with those identical gold-amber eyes in which—for the first time—something was waking. Not memory. Not yet. But the space where memory would go, cleared and waiting, like ground after snowmelt.

"Sister," Vesna said slowly, tasting the word. "If I help you... will I get the hole filled?"

Ulyana pulled her close, forehead to forehead, wolf to wolf, and answered the only way that mattered.

"Yes. I swear it on my own soul—which is right there on that pedestal, which means I've never sworn on anything worth more."

From the doorway, Paloma made a sound that was almost a laugh and entirely despair. "You realize what you've just done. You've recruited the court's own cupbearer. Do you know what happens to us if we're caught now? It won't be exile. It won't be the tithe. They'll seal us in glass ourselves and pour us out a century from now to serve wine at their centennial feast."

"Then we'd better not be caught," Ulyana said.

And that was when the bells changed.

Above them—far above, through leagues of ribbed stone—the quiet bells of the court began to toll in a new pattern, faster, urgent, a rhythm Ulyana had never heard but whose meaning needed no translation. Somewhere in the frozen feast-hall, something had gone wrong. Someone had noticed an empty chair, a missing cupbearer, a door ajar in the vaults below.

Vesna went rigid in her arms. "The summons bell," she whispered. "When it rings, all household must attend the high table. If I'm absent—"

"How long?" Paloma was already moving, snuffing the lantern to a coal-dim glow, her mind visibly racing ahead through corridors and contingencies. "Before they send someone down."

"Minutes. The stewards are fast when the bell—"

"Then we move *now*. Ulyana—your soul, can you carry it? The vessel?"

Ulyana turned to the pedestal. The teardrop of glass pulsed its wolf-gold light, warm under her hands, *hers*, so close she could taste the pine woods and the laughter and the fire. Every instinct screamed to seize it, to run, to clutch her stolen self to her chest and never let go.

But the vessel was large, and fragile, and the corridors were dark, and Vesna's hand was trembling in hers, and forty-one hollowed servants were even now rising from their duties to answer a bell.

"Not yet," she said, and the words tore coming out. "Not yet. First we get her out. First we get *everyone* out. Then I come back for me."

Paloma looked at her for a long moment—looked at her the way you look at something you hadn't expected to find in the world, something rare and possibly fatal—and then nodded once, sharp as a blade leaving a sheath.

"Together, then. Sister and smuggler and cupbearer." She pulled the door wide and glanced up the dark stair. "Try to look like a guest being escorted, Vesna. Smile your court smile. And pray to whatever's left of your gods—"

The bells tolled again, closer now, or maybe just louder, and down the corridor beyond the reliquary rooms came a sound that froze all three of them where they stood.

Footsteps. Measured. Unhurried. The footsteps of someone who owned every stone they walked on, descending.

"—because something's already on its way down," Paloma finished grimly, and drew her blade.
