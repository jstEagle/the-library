## 16. The Last Dragon-Fire

The dragon was not what Paloma expected.

She had imagined the thing that fed the under-court as vast and terrible, a mountain of scales and malice chained at the world's root. What she found instead, three levels below the reliquary vaults and behind a door Sef's stolen key opened with a sigh of cold air, was an old creature curled small in a cavern of black glass, its chains so thick with verdigris they looked like garden vines.

It was still enormous. That was the trouble with dragons; even dying, even diminished, even folded up like a moth in winter, it filled half the cavern, and one amber eye the size of a cart wheel turned slowly to fix on them when their footsteps rang against the glass floor.

"Visitors," it said. Its voice was like a hearth collapsing. "It has been some years."

Paloma put herself between the eye and Ulyana without thinking about it. Her hand went to her knife out of habit, which was absurd—she might as well have drawn it against a landslide—and she made herself lower it.

"We're looking for answers," she said. "Not trouble."

"No one looks for trouble here." The dragon's nostrils flared, exhaling air that smelled faintly of woodsmoke and old snow. "Trouble finds everyone who comes beneath Thornfell. You smell of contracts, little mercenary. Old ones. Broken ones." The great head shifted, chains dragging across the glass with a sound like church bells drowning. "And you—" the eye moved to Ulyana, and something in it softened, or seemed to, "—you smell of home. A den. Pine needles. Someone's hands in your fur. You have been sold, wolf-child, but you have not yet been spent."

Ulyana's breath caught. Paloma felt her shift beside her, felt the tremor run through the girl's shoulders the way it always did when someone spoke to the wound she couldn't remember receiving.

"You know what happened to her," Paloma said.

"I know everything that happens under this mountain." The dragon settled its chin back onto its foreclaws, wearily, the way an old dog lies down. "I am the furnace it is built around. Ask your questions. I have perhaps a season of fire left in me, and I would spend some of it talking. It has been long since anyone asked me anything but *burn*."

* * *

They had come down from the vaults running. The sabotage had gone wrong—or rather, it had gone right, and then kept going, the freed souls from the first reliquary spilling through the frozen feast-hall like smoke given legs, and the court's beautiful wrong-faced nobles rising from their century-old banquet with expressions that did not belong on borrowed faces. In the chaos, in the ringing dark, Paloma had grabbed Ulyana's wrist and followed the one thread she trusted: Ilya's handwriting, copied into her memory over twenty years of reading his maps. *Below the vaults there is a door that is not locked because no one dares.* He had written that once, drunk, on the back of a salt-route chart, and laughed until he cried, and never explained it.

No one dared. But Paloma Okonkwo had spent ten years learning not to dare, and had recently discovered she was better at the opposite.

So now: black glass, green chains, a dying god-furnace with amber eyes, and questions she had carried since a burned-out shrine in the high country.

"The court," she said. "What is it? Really. Not the feast, not the faces. What keeps it standing?"

The dragon laughed. It was a horrible sound, like a forest fire remembering joy.

"What keeps any lie standing? Fuel." One claw lifted, gesturing vaguely upward, toward the vaults, the feast-hall, the whole rotting splendor of the Court of Quiet Bells. "You have seen the brazier in the great hall. The guttering flame the Lord Beneath tends like a housewife trimming a lamp. Did you wonder why a court of immortals bothers with a fire?"

"It's the last dragon-fire," Paloma said slowly. "That's what the ledger said. Ilya wrote it in the margin. *The last dragon-fire.*"

"I am the last dragon-fire." The amber eye blinked, slow as a season turning. "I was young when the first kings of the surface were young. I flew before your mountains had names. And when I grew old—when my fire began to gutter, as all fires do—the ones who became this court came to me with a bargain dressed as mercy. They could not let me die, you see. A dragon's death-fire would crack this mountain to its roots and bury every soul within a hundred miles. So they chained me. And they learned something in the chaining." The head turned, and the light of the eye fell full on Paloma, warm and terrible. "A soul burns longer than wood. Longer than coal. Longer than years. Every soul sold beneath Thornfell comes down to me, eventually, and I burn it, and the burning feeds the fire, and the fire holds up the mountain, and the mountain holds up the court. That is the arithmetic. That is the whole of it. Your mentor's ledger, the tithe-collectors, the broker's bargains, the girl's sister pouring wine with empty eyes—all of it, all of it, is fuel for one old flame that should have been allowed to go out four thousand years ago."

The silence after that was very large.

Paloma became aware of her own heartbeat, slow and stubborn, and of Ulyana's hand finding hers—not grabbing, just resting there, palm to palm, the way she'd learned to do it. Asking, not taking.

"The children," Ulyana said quietly. "In the chapel records. The ones sold sixty, eighty years ago. They're—"

"Some burned fast. Children's souls are bright and brief, like kindling." The dragon said it without cruelty, which somehow made it worse—the flatness of a fact stated by something too old for cruelty. "Some are still burning. Slowly. A soul does not die in my fire, wolf-child. It is *spent*. Used up the way a candle uses wax. What remains afterward goes... where all spent things go. I do not know. Even I do not know that."

"And if the fire goes out?" Paloma asked. She already knew. She had known since the ledger, maybe since the map, maybe since the moment a dying courier pressed wax-sealed vellum into her hands and whispered about the tithe coming due. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud in a voice like a collapsing hearth were different animals.

The dragon regarded her for a long time.

"If the fire goes out," it said, "every soul still held—still burning, still stored in your glass reliquaries—is released. All at once. Free. That is the nature of the fuel; it cannot burn unheld. The court's law binds them to the flame, and the flame is me, and when I end, the binding ends." A pause. Chains settled. Somewhere far above, muffled through miles of stone, Paloma thought she heard bells beginning to ring—the centennial feast assembling itself, course by frozen course. "And the mountain falls."

"All of it?" Ulyana's voice was thin.

"All of it. The courts, the vaults, the tunnels, the gate. Everything beneath the tree line, and much above it. The passes will close. The valleys will shake. Anyone still inside when the last ember dies will be buried in the oldest grave in the world." The eye dimmed slightly, guttered, brightened again—a lamp running low. "You see the shape of it now, mercenary. This is why no one has ever done it. This is why the court has stood ten thousand years while kingdoms rose and drowned around it. To free the stolen, you must kill the fire. To kill the fire, you must bring down the mountain. And the mountain is full—full—of souls who did nothing wrong, who will be freed only to be crushed, unless someone leads them out first, through tunnels that will be collapsing, past a Lord Beneath who will do anything, *anything*, to keep his lamp lit."

* * *

Paloma walked to the edge of the black glass floor and stood there with her arms crossed, looking at nothing, doing the arithmetic.

It was the same arithmetic she'd done her whole life, really. Every job, every escort, every smuggler's road: weigh the cargo against the risk, the coin against the blood, decide what a life was worth and get paid accordingly. She had thought she was good at it because she was cold. Now she understood she was good at it because she was grieving, and grief makes excellent bookkeepers.

But this ledger didn't balance.

On one side: every soul the court had taken in ten thousand years. Ulyana's among them—her laughter, her sister's name, whatever else she'd sold to save her pack. The cupbearer girl with the wiped eyes. The children from the chapel records, whose names Ilya had copied out in his careful merchant's hand and then, apparently, done nothing about except feel bad and drink.

On the other side: the mountain. The refugees in the foothills. The villages that clung to Thornfell's knees. The roads she knew better than any living person, and the people who traveled them. And the practical, ugly truth that you could not evacuate a population that didn't know it needed evacuating, not in a season, not in a month, not with two women and a handful of half-remembered ghosts.

"There's another way," she said finally, not turning around. "There's always another way. Smugglers survive by finding the other way."

"There is not," the dragon said gently. "I have had four thousand years to look. There is only the fire, and the falling, and how many you can carry out between the one and the other."

"How long?" Paloma turned. "When the fire dies. How long before the mountain comes down?"

The amber eye considered her.

"Hours," it said. "Perhaps a day, if I fight the collapse, and I will fight it—I can hold the deep ways open longest, the old tunnels, the ones your kind used before the court sealed them. But hours, mercenary. Hours to move hundreds of souls through miles of stone."

"Then we don't move hundreds." Paloma heard her own voice go flat and certain, the voice she used for prices. "We move whoever's left in the vaults—you said the spent ones are already gone, so it's the stored ones, the reliquaries, the living captives like the cupbearer. How many?"

"Fewer than a hundred, now. The court burns most of its stock at each tithe-feast." The dragon's lip curled off teeth like plowshares. "Tonight is a tithe-feast."

"Fewer than a hundred." Paloma worked it. The old tunnels—Ilya's maps, the salt caves, the hermit's bridge, the ways only smugglers knew, the ways that ran shallow and quick to the surface. If the dragon held the deep ways open. If the freed souls could walk. If the Lord Beneath was busy with his feast. If, if, if. It was the worst contract she'd ever priced, and she'd once escorted a cargo of live scorpions through a blizzard.

"And you," she said to the dragon. "When it ends. When the last of you burns. What happens to you?"

The great head lowered. For a moment the creature looked, impossibly, like something tired and old and ready—which was, Paloma supposed, exactly what it was.

"I sleep," it said. "Or I end. At my age the words mean nearly the same thing. Do not grieve for me, contract-keeper. I have been dead for four thousand years. This is only the funeral arriving late." The eye flicked to Ulyana, then back. "But understand what you are choosing. The court will not sit quietly while you rob its larder. The Lord Beneath has one law older than all his others, and it is this: *a debt must be paid*. If you break his reliquaries and empty his vaults, he will look for payment. And he will find it in whoever stands closest to the scale."

Paloma thought of Ilya's ledger. *Sold his own soul to guarantee hers would never be taken; the debt transfers on death.* She was the collateral. She had always been the collateral. The Lord Beneath wouldn't need to look far at all.

"Good," she said.

Ulyana's hand closed hard around hers. "Paloma—"

"Don't." She said it without heat. "Don't argue yet. Let me finish pricing it."

* * *

They stayed a while longer. It was Ulyana who asked the question Paloma hadn't thought to ask—Ulyana, who remembered nothing but fragments, who had crossed half of Thornfell chasing a map and a sister's laugh.

"What was it like?" the girl asked the dragon, kneeling at the edge of the glass floor, close enough that the heat of its breath stirred her hair. "Before. Flying."

The amber eye went distant, and the firelight in it changed color, and for a moment the whole cavern smelled of open sky.

"Warm," the dragon said. "Everything was warm, and I was the warmest thing in it."

Later—they would be leaving soon, back up through the vaults, into the noise and the bells and the work of breaking a ten-thousand-year court before dawn—later, Paloma stood apart, sharpening a knife she didn't need to sharpen, and let herself feel the size of what she'd decided.

Every soul sold feeds the fire. Kill the fire, free the souls, drop the mountain. Carry out whoever you can in the hours between. Pay whatever the Lord Beneath demands from whoever stands nearest the scale.

Her god had gone silent ten years ago, and she had thought that was the worst thing silence could hold.

She was wrong. The worst thing silence could hold was arithmetic like this—and the discovery, arriving cold and clear as spring water, that she already knew the answer, and had known it since a river ford, since a naked terrified girl had begged her for a map and Paloma had looked at her and been unable, simply unable, to walk away.

Above them, faint through the stone, the centennial bells began to ring in earnest.

The dragon raised its head, and its chains sang, and its fire stood up tall in its throat for the first time in centuries.

"So," it said. "Shall we begin?"
