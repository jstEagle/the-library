## 18. The Feast Interrupted

The bells began at moonrise.

They were not loud. That was the worst of it—the court's bells rang soft as water dripping in a deep cave, and every noble in the Hall of Quiet Bells turned toward the sound with the same slow, rapturous tilt of the head, like flowers following a sun that had died centuries ago. Ulyana stood among the serving captives with a tray of candied rowanberries she did not dare eat, and watched three hundred stolen faces smile at nothing.

*One hundred years,* the cupbearer had said—no. Not the cupbearer. *Sorcha.* Her sister's name was Sorcha. Ulyana held it behind her teeth all day like a hot coal, saying it only in her head, because if she said it aloud she would weep into the wine and ruin everything.

One hundred years since the last tithe-feast. One hundred years since the under-court had opened its vaults and paraded its reliquaries through the hall so the nobles could admire what they had bought. Tonight the glass souls would come out again, ranked on silver stands along the eastern wall, each one glowing faintly with the color of the life it had been peeled from. Gold for a merchant's ambition. Blue for a fisherman's grief. And somewhere among them, small and pale as a winter moon—

Hers.

"Stop staring," breathed Vess, the tanner's boy beside her, one of the first they'd freed from the low cells. His hands shook around his own tray. "You'll crack the porcelain."

"I'm not wearing porcelain." The court had dressed its servants in masks of glazed clay, smooth and featureless except for painted smiles. Every captive wore one. It was meant to be merciful, the steward said. It was meant to make them easier to look at.

"You know what I mean." Vess nodded, barely, toward the high table where Paloma sat.

Paloma looked wrong in court clothes. She looked like a knife wrapped in silk, which perhaps was the point—she'd insisted on being presented as Sef's captured prize, delivered to the Lord Beneath as proof of the broker's good faith, and now she sat at the dais with her wrists bound in ribbon instead of rope, eating nothing, drinking nothing, watching everything. Her eyes found Ulyana across the hall. Held for one heartbeat. Moved on.

*The reliquaries when the bells stop,* she'd told them in the dark of the cell corridor, drawing the plan on the floor with charcoal. *The nobles will be called to the toast. They always toast the Lord Beneath first—that's the law of the court, even the law obeys it. When their backs are turned, you break the glass. All of it. Don't be gentle. Gentle is how they catch you.*

"And you?" Sorcha had asked—not knowing she asked, not knowing why her own voice made Ulyana's chest ache. "What will you do?"

Paloma had looked at Ulyana then, and something passed between them that needed no words: *I bought your soul with mine. Now I go to collect.*

The bells stopped.

The silence afterward was enormous, a held breath three hundred years long, and then the Lord Beneath rose from his throne of black root-wood and the whole court rose with him, a rustling tide of borrowed faces, and every eye in the hall turned to the dais.

Ulyana moved.

She had learned, in the weeks below the mountain, how the servants walked: heel-first, silent, eyes down, as though the floor might report them. She walked that way now along the eastern wall, past the silver stands where the reliquaries gleamed in their ranks, and set down her tray of berries with hands that had gone very steady. Steady was a thing she'd learned too. You found it underneath the fear, down where the wolf lived, and you let it hold you up.

The nearest reliquary held a soul the color of honey and smoke. A farmer's, maybe. Someone who had loved early mornings. The glass was etched with a name she couldn't read—the court wrote its contracts in a script that squirmed if you looked too long—and sealed with wax stamped by the sigil of quiet bells.

Vess came up on her left. Sorcha on her right, carrying a ewer of wine she would never pour. Behind them, scattered through the crowd of bowing nobles, eleven others: the ones they'd sprung from the cells at dusk, the ones who still could walk, who still remembered enough of themselves to want it back.

The Lord Beneath lifted his cup. His voice, when it came, was beautiful the way a landslide is beautiful—all gravity, no mercy.

"A century," he said, "since the last great tithe. A century of plenty beneath the stone. Drink, my court, to the fire that keeps us—"

"To the fire," murmured three hundred throats.

Ulyana took the stand in both hands and tipped it over.

Glass burst against the flagstones like a struck bell, and the honey-smoke soul rose out of the wreckage in a column of light that made the nearest nobles scream and flinch away, their borrowed faces sliding half off, revealing the grey, starved things beneath. She didn't watch it go. She was already moving to the next stand, and the next, and Vess was swinging a candlestick like a flail and Sorcha—Sorcha was hurling the ewer itself, wine arcing red through the air, shattering two reliquaries at once, and the hall came apart around them into noise and light and the smell of scorched sugar.

A soul the color of riverwater rose past her shoulder. A soul like green shoots after rain. Each one that escaped left a hole in the room, some pressure lifting, and the nobles staggered as though the mountain itself had exhaled.

Then the doors at the far end banged open, and the tithe-collectors came in.

There were more than Ulyana had ever seen together—a dozen at least, tall and jointed wrong, their faces smooth as eggshell, carrying hooks of black iron that hummed with contract-law. They moved through the panicking court without touching a single noble. They came for the servants. For the freed. For the broken glass.

"Forget the collectors!" Ulyana shouted, wrenching a stand down with her boot. "Break everything! Break it until there's nothing left to break!"

Something huge hit the wall near her—a noble, or what had been a noble, hurled aside by a grey blur that resolved into Vess mid-shift, his half-formed wolf-shape tearing the throat out of the first collector before its iron hook could fall. Good. Good boy. She grabbed Sorcha's wrist—"with me"—and hauled her sister down the line of stands, smashing as they went, glass crunching underfoot, freed souls streaming upward through the vaulted dark like reversed rain.

And then a hook caught the hem of her sleeve.

She spun. The collector holding her was taller than the others, and where its face should have been there was a seal of wax, unbroken, and when it spoke its voice was the voice of every contract she had ever signed and forgotten.

*"Ulyana of the Thornfell pack. Your debt remains outstanding. Your vessel remains forfeit."*

"My name," she snarled, shifting her hand into claws, "is not on any paper you get to keep."

She raked the seal-face open. The collector made no sound as it came apart, but the wax screamed—high and thin, a dying note that every remaining collector turned toward at once, and Ulyana understood, with cold clarity, that she had just become the loudest thing in the room.

Fine. Let them look at her. Every moment they spent on the wolf was a moment Vess and the others spent on the glass.

She ran. Not away—*through*, weaving between tables laid with a century of frozen feast, ham and honeybread and peacocks glazed gold, all of it untouched since before her grandmother's grandmother, all of it rotting sweetly under preservation-spells. Collectors converged. She vaulted a table, dropped, rolled, came up with a carving knife in hand and put it through the knee-joint of the nearest one, and then she was moving again, laughing breathlessly, wildly, because this—this was the part the wolf knew how to do. This was the part ten years of sold memory had never managed to take.

Across the hall, above the chaos, she saw Paloma rise from the dais.

* * *

Later, Ulyana would try to piece together what happened next, and the pieces would never quite fit, because everything happened at once.

Paloma snapped the ribbon-bonds on her wrists like thread and stepped directly into the path of the Lord Beneath, who had descended from his throne for the first time in living memory—or in whatever passed, below the stone, for memory. He was taller than he'd seemed seated. His shadow went the wrong way, pooling upward along the pillars like smoke that had changed its mind about falling.

"The broker Sef promised me a prize," the Lord Beneath said, and his voice reached every corner of the hall without effort, cutting through the screams. "Instead she brings me a thief with a dead man's ledger. Give me one reason, little mercenary, why I should not take your soul tonight and call the debt settled."

"Because it isn't yours to take yet." Paloma's voice carried too. Smaller, but it carried—the way a nail carries when you drive it true. "Because I read the old law. All of it. The parts your stewards don't recite. An equal pledge redeems an equal pledge, and I have come to pay."

"You have nothing."

"I have Ilya Okonkwo's debt. Transferred to me at his death, witnessed by the ledger, binding under the same law that built this court." She drew the ledger from her coat—when had she kept it on her person all this time? Of course she had; Paloma trusted paper more than people—and held it up. "He pledged himself to guarantee another's safety. He died owing. I inherit. And I am here to spend it."

"On the wolf?" Something like amusement moved through the court, a ripple of grey laughter. "Souls are not traded for affection, mercenary."

"No," Paloma said. "They're traded for debts. And hers was taken unlawfully—taken as collateral against a man who was already dead when the contract was signed. Check your own books. I'll wait."

The hall went very quiet. Even the collectors paused, hooks lowered, heads tilted in that listening way, and Ulyana realized with a lurch that the court's own law was being invoked against it, publicly, in front of three hundred witnesses who could not afford to let it be seen bending.

But the Lord Beneath only smiled, and his smile was the beginning of the end of everything.

"Check the books," he agreed softly. "By all means. But the centennial feast has laws of its own, and the first of them is this: *nothing leaves the table before the toast is done.*"

He raised his cup.

Every cup in the hall rose with it—every noble, every collector, every servant-mask, and Ulyana felt her own arm begin to lift, the wine-cup she'd caught up without thinking dragging her hand upward against her will, contract-law crawling up her muscles like frost. Around her, Vess froze mid-stride. Sorcha's face went slack and obedient. The freed souls still rising through the air stuttered, hung, wavered.

"To the fire," the Lord Beneath said, "that keeps us. To the fire, that feeds us. To the fire—"

"—that is going out," said a voice like grinding continents.

The chained dragon had spoken.

Ulyana had almost forgotten it was there, coiled in the great brazier-pit at the hall's heart, its chains running down into the mountain's roots, its scales dull as old coals. But now it raised its head, and its eyes opened, and they were the color of the last ember of a fire that had burned for a thousand years and was finally, finally allowed to die.

"The girl is right," the dragon said, and its voice cracked a pillar. "I am nearly empty. Every soul you stole fed me a little longer, and I am ashamed of every mouthful. The fire ends tonight, Lord Beneath. With the toast or without it."

"You are *bound*—"

"I am *tired*," said the dragon, and pulled.

The chains did not break. Chains forged by gods do not break. But the dragon was older than the forge, and it simply *stopped holding itself up*, and its vast weight came down across the brazier-pit, and the guttering dragon-fire within was smothered like a candle under a palm.

Darkness fell on the court of quiet bells.

Not total darkness—the reliquaries still glowed, dozens of them, the ones they hadn't reached, casting frantic colored light across the panic—but the warmth went out of the world. The frozen feast began, at last, to rot. The nobles' borrowed faces slid from them entirely, and what remained were thin grey creatures clutching at each other and keening, and the beautiful music of the court curdled into a sound like ice cracking on a lake.

In the colored dark, Ulyana found Sorcha's hand and gripped it.

"Sorcha," she said. "My name is Ulyana. I'm your sister. I need you to run with me now, and later—I promise—later I'll tell you everything."

Her sister's masked face turned toward her. For one terrible moment nothing happened. Then Sorcha's fingers tightened back, hard enough to hurt, and she said, in a voice scraped raw from a century of pouring wine:

"I don't know why I believe you. But I do. Run."

They ran. Through the collapsing hall, over glass and rotting gold, past collectors who no longer moved with purpose—who drifted, directionless, their iron hooks dimming as the contracts that powered them lost their anchor. Vess appeared at Ulyana's flank, human-shaped again, bleeding from the ear, grinning like a madman. Behind them the last reliquaries were being smashed by freed captives who no longer cared about stealth, and the souls rose in a single bright column through the shattered vault-ceiling, up and up, toward stone that groaned and split above them.

The mountain was coming down. Paloma had warned them: *when the fire dies, the court dies with it, and the mountain falls.* Ulyana had thought she understood. She hadn't. The whole world was groaning now, dust sifting down through colored light, pillars folding like knees.

And Paloma—

Ulyana skidded to a stop on the dais steps, scanning the chaos, and found her at the center of it, standing over the fallen Lord Beneath with the ledger open in her hands and blood running freely from her nose, her ears, her eyes—blood-price, the toll of speaking law above her station, or of the pledge already taking hold. The Lord Beneath lay pinned beneath the dragon's tail, screaming in a language that predated speech, and around him the freed souls had gathered—not rising anymore, not fleeing, but *descending*, hundreds of them, circling him like bright wolves around a wounded stag.

"Paloma!" Ulyana screamed.

Paloma looked up. Even from across the ruined hall, even through the dust and the dark, Ulyana saw it: her eyes were going pale at the edges, frost creeping inward, the color draining out of them the way color drains from a soul in glass.

"It's done," Paloma called back, and she was smiling, actually smiling, bloody and blazing. "The trade is sealed! Get them out, Ulyana! Get everyone out!"

"Not without you!"

"That wasn't the bargain!"

The ceiling chose that moment to come apart. A slab the size of a barn door sheared loose overhead, and the world became falling stone and screaming and Sorcha hauling Ulyana backward by both arms, and when the dust cleared there was a wall of rubble where the dais had been, and Paloma was on the other side of it.

"No," Ulyana said. "No, no, *no*—"

She hit the rubble with her shoulders, clawing at stone, shifting halfway without deciding to, fur rippling down her arms, strength flooding into her back and shoulders as the wolf took the burden her human body couldn't. Slabs that would crush five men moved under her hands. Behind her, Vess shouted for the others to help; Sorcha pressed something into her palm—a shard of reliquary glass, still warm, still glowing faintly blue—and Ulyana didn't ask, just held it, just dug.

The mountain screamed around them. Somewhere far above, impossibly, daylight showed through a crack in the world.

And beneath her hands, through a gap in the stone no wider than a coffin, she heard Paloma Okafor laugh—low, ragged, alive—and say:

"Well. Don't just stand there, wolf. I've got a debt to default on, and you're going to help me."
