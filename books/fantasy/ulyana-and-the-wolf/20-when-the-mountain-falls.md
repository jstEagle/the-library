## 20. When the Mountain Falls

The first bell cracked at the moment the dragon-fire died.

Ulyana felt it in her teeth before she heard it—a high, splintering note that ran through the stone like a crack through ice. Then the great bronze bell above the feast hall split down its middle, and half of it fell, and the sound it made when it struck the marble was not a sound at all but a silence so complete that for one heartbeat the whole under-court seemed to hold its breath.

Then everything screamed.

Not the people—the walls. The mountain itself groaned, a deep animal moan that Ulyana knew in her bones, because she had heard dying things make that sound before. The frozen feast shattered all at once, a hundred goblets and platters bursting like frost on a window. The nobles in their borrowed faces came apart mid-scream, their stolen features sliding off like wax in a fire, revealing beneath them nothing but pale, blinking shapes—souls wearing bodies that had never belonged to them.

"Run," Paloma had said.

Ulyana ran.

* * *

The corridors of the under-court were built to confuse, and now they were built to kill. Chandeliers of green glass swung from ceilings that rippled like water. Staircases she had descended an hour ago were gone, replaced by raw rock still warm from the mountain's shifting. Dust fell in curtains. Somewhere far below, water was finding paths it had never been given before, and the sound of it was like a thousand small voices whispering hurry, hurry, hurry.

Ulyana carried two people.

The first was her sister.

Mira weighed almost nothing. That was the worst part—not the weight of her but the absence of it, the way her body had been pared down by years of the court's keeping until she felt like a bundle of sticks wrapped in a dress. Her eyes were open. They had been open since Ulyana smashed the cupbearer's collar and dragged her out of the wine cellar, open and empty as winter pools. Whatever the court had poured into her to keep her docile was draining out slowly, and what remained behind did not yet know its own name.

"Mira," Ulyana said again, as she ran. "Your name is Mira. You laughed like this—" and she made the sound, breathless and silly, the laugh she had carried ten years in fragments, "—like that. You're my sister. I sold myself to save you and I would do it again."

Mira's fingers twitched against her shoulder. It was not much. Ulyana held onto it like a rope.

The second person she carried was Paloma.

Paloma walked. That was the terrible thing about it—she walked, steady as ever, one hand trailing the wall, her face gray as ash and her steps growing shorter with every corridor. The pledge was taking her. Ulyana could see it happening the way you can see frost creeping across glass: Paloma's edges going faint, her outline less certain, as if the mountain were drinking her a sip at a time. When Ulyana grabbed her arm to pull her along, her fingers sank in slightly, like gripping smoke that hadn't yet decided to be flesh.

"I'm fine," Paloma said. She said it every few minutes, in exactly the same flat tone, and each time it was less true.

"You're lying," Ulyana said.

"I'm a mercenary. Lying is a professional skill." But there was no edge left in the joke. Paloma stumbled, caught herself on the wall, and where her palm touched the stone it left no print at all.

Behind them, deeper in the mountain, something vast shifted its weight. The freed dragon. Ulyana had seen it only once, in the last moments before the brazier died—a creature old beyond reckoning, chained so long its shackles had grown into its bones, its scales the color of banked coals. When the fire went out, the chains had not broken. They had simply become meaningless, the way a lock becomes meaningless when the door it holds has rotted away. The dragon had risen, and the mountain had begun to fall, and the dragon had opened its throat and roared a word in a language older than the court's law:

*Out.*

* * *

They found the others at the crossing of the Sunken Road, where the court's grand processional avenue met the smugglers' tunnels—and Ulyana's heart lurched, because Paloma had mapped these passages for them in the hours before the feast, scratching routes into memory with a courier's precision, saying *if the east gallery floods, take the salt vein; if the salt vein collapses, follow the warm air, warm air means sky.* Now those instructions were the only scripture any of them had.

There were perhaps forty souls gathered at the crossing. Freed captives, mostly—men and women who had been sold below across a century, some of them blinking in the torchlight like newborns, some weeping, some simply standing and touching their own faces over and over as if confirming they still had faces to touch. A handful of the faceless court-shapes huddled apart, terrified, holding onto each other. Nobody hurt them. There wasn't time, and besides, they looked pitiful enough already.

"The tunnels are failing," said a broad-shouldered man Ulyana recognized from the reliquary vaults—one of the first to wake when his soul returned to him. His name was Davor. He had spent thirty years below and he spoke like a man remembering how. "Two already gone. The whole western spur just... folded."

"Then we don't go west." Paloma's voice came out thin, scraped. She leaned against a fallen pillar as if it were an old friend. "Warm air. All of you. Feel for warm air moving toward you, and follow it backward. That's the way out. That's always the way out."

"And you?" Davor asked.

Paloma didn't answer. Ulyana answered for her.

"She comes with us."

Paloma turned her head, slow as a door on rusted hinges, and looked at Ulyana with an expression Ulyana had never seen on her before. Not defiance. Something softer and much more frightening: apology.

"Ulyana—"

"No." Ulyana shifted Mira higher on her hip. "No, whatever speech you've been composing in that stubborn skull, you can save it. You don't get to buy a girl's soul back and then evaporate in a tunnel like morning fog. That's not how this ends. I forbid it."

"You forbid it." Almost, almost, a smile touched Paloma's fading mouth. "You know, when I met you, you were naked in a river trying to rob me."

"You were robbing me back."

"I was retrieving stolen property."

"It was *my* map."

Their eyes held. Around them the mountain groaned and shed its ceilings, and forty frightened people waited on the words of two women arguing like sisters over the last piece of bread. And in Paloma's eyes Ulyana saw the truth she had been refusing to see since the brazier died: the pledge was not waiting. It was eating. Every minute they stood here arguing, the mountain took another bite of her.

"Go," Paloma said quietly. "Take your sister. Take them all. You found what you came for—that's more than I ever managed in ten years of running these roads. Let me finish my part of the ledger."

"Your part of the ledger is *living*," Ulyana snarled. "Ilya wrote you into that debt without asking. You've spent your whole life paying other people's prices—your god, your mentor, every contract anyone ever put in front of you. Well, I'm putting a new one in front of you, and here are the terms: you walk out of this mountain on your own feet, or I will shift into a wolf twice your size and carry you out between my teeth, and I promise you, Paloma Okonkwo, I have *very* sharp teeth and *no* patience left."

For a moment—just a moment—Paloma looked at her the way people look at a fire in winter. Then the pillar she leaned against crumbled to gravel, and she went down, and Ulyana caught her, and Paloma weighed almost nothing too.

That was how Ulyana stopped arguing and started moving.

* * *

The salt caves came first, and the salt saved them.

Paloma, half-carried with her arm across Ulyana's shoulders, directed them by voice alone now—her body too light, her footsteps printing nothing—but her mind was still iron. "Left at the white seam. The floor will look solid past the third rib of crystal—it isn't. Stay on the crystal. Salt remembers its shape even when the mountain forgets its own."

She was right. Twice the tunnel floor behind them sagged and slumped into darkness, and twice the company stood safe on shelves of ancient pink crystal while the world rearranged itself around them. A child cried. An old woman sang to her, low and tuneless, some lullaby from a country none of them could name anymore, and the singing kept them all walking.

Mira walked on her own now. Somewhere in the last mile, the fog in her eyes had thinned, and she had looked up at Ulyana and frowned and said, in a voice rusty as a gate hinge: "You laugh different than you used to."

Ulyana had nearly dropped to her knees right there in the salt.

"Not yet," she'd told her sister instead. "Laugh with me later. Walk now."

And Mira had walked, and Ulyana had burned the moment like a coal in her chest to warm her hands by, because ahead of her Paloma was fading faster, and she needed every scrap of hope she could carry.

By the hermit's bridge, Paloma could not speak above a whisper.

The bridge was a smuggler's relic—a rope-and-plank span across a chasm that had once, according to Paloma's stories, been crossed by mule trains loaded with untaxed brandy. Now the chasm breathed. Cold air rose from it in long exhalations, and the bridge swayed, and half its planks were already gone into the dark.

"One at a time," Davor said, taking command of the crossing with the calm of a man who had clearly organized difficult things before his soul was taken. "Children first. Then the old ones. Then the rest."

Ulyana got Mira across. She got the crying child across, and the singing woman, and a dozen others, hauling the rope, steadying the sway, her arms burning. Each time she returned to the near side, Paloma was smaller. By the fourth trip, Ulyana could see the far wall of the chasm through her shoulder.

"No," Ulyana said, crouching in front of her. "Look at me. Look at me, Paloma. You do not get to go translucent on me. You are the most infuriating, competent, closed-off person I have ever met, and I refuse—I *refuse*—to learn to live in a world with one fewer of you in it."

Paloma lifted her head. Through the fading of her face, her eyes were still dark and clear and entirely her own.

"Ulyana," she whispered. "When this is done—if I'm—there's a cache. Under the shrine. Burned-out shrine, north road. My maps. Everything I know. Give it to someone who needs roads."

"I'm not taking your maps. I'm taking *you*. Maps are useless without the miserable woman who memorized them."

"That's..." A ghost of the dry smile. "Actually true. I'm very hard to replace."

"Then stop trying to be replaced!"

The bridge shuddered. From deep in the chasm came a rushing sound, like the mountain clearing its throat, and everyone still on the near side—Ulyana, Paloma, and six others—looked at the fraying ropes and understood the arithmetic at the same moment.

"Go," Paloma said. "Get the others across. I'll follow."

"You can't even stand."

"I'll follow," Paloma repeated, and there was the old steel in it, the voice that had killed two tithe-collectors on a rain-slicked road and never lost a night's sleep over it, and Ulyana hated her for it and loved her for it and could not tell, anymore, which feeling was heavier.

She got the six others across. It took everything. The bridge lost three more planks and one entire rope-strand, and when the last of them—the singing woman—stepped onto solid rock, the span hung by threads.

Ulyana turned back.

Paloma stood at the near end of the bridge, swaying, her outline flickering like a candle in wind. Behind her, the tunnel they had come through folded inward with a sound like a giant closing a book. There was no way back. There was barely a way forward. And Paloma Okonkwo, who had survived ten years of silent gods and broken vows and roads no honest person knew, took one step onto the bridge—

—and the remaining rope parted.

* * *

Ulyana did not remember deciding to shift.

Later, she would piece it together: the rope snapping, the bridge dropping, Paloma falling with it, and then the world going gold-edged and huge as her body remembered what it was. The wolf came out of her the way a river comes out of a broken dam—all the grief and rage and love of ten lost years and one impossible month pouring into muscle and fang. She leapt the gap in a single bound that no natural wolf could have made, caught Paloma's falling body in her jaws by the back of the coat, and twisted mid-air, and hit the far ledge rolling, and came up on four legs with the most precious thing in the collapsing world held gently, so gently, in her mouth.

Paloma weighed nothing. Less than nothing. The wolf could feel her slipping through her teeth like snow.

*Shift back,* Ulyana wanted to scream. *Fight it. Hold on.*

But the wolf could not speak, and Paloma's eyes were closing, and around them the cavern was coming down in sheets, and from somewhere above—above, real open-air above—a shadow swept across the falling dust.

The dragon.

It came down the chasm like a storm deciding to be gentle, wings that had not beaten in a thousand years filling the dark, and it landed on the broken bridge-ends as if they were perches built for it, and it looked at the wolf with one eye the size of a cartwheel, ancient and ember-dim and unbearably sad.

*Little sister,* it said, and its voice was not sound but heat, settling into her bones. *You carry a soul being unmade.*

*Then help me,* Ulyana thought back, wildly, fur bristling. *You owe this mountain nothing. Help me carry her out.*

*The pledge is not a chain I can break,* the dragon said. *It is a debt, and debts are paid in full or not at all. But—* the great head lowered, and its breath washed over Paloma's fading body like a hearth-wind— *I was chained by contracts for a thousand years, wolf. I learned every clause. And there is one thing the old law fears above all others, one thing it has no answer for, because no one in the history of courts and tithes and pledges has ever been fool enough to try it.*

The dragon's eye fixed on Ulyana's, and in its depths she saw reflected a truth so simple it seemed impossible no one had spoken it aloud.

*A soul taken can be redeemed by a pledge. But a soul* given*—freely, wholly, asking nothing back—cancels what it touches. The law cannot weigh a gift. It has no scale for it.*

Ulyana's heart slammed against her ribs. She looked down at Paloma—gray, weightless, going—and understood.

*Will it work?*

*It has never been tried,* said the dragon. *Neither has love surviving this mountain. Do both.*

Above them, the last of the ceiling began to fall, and the dragon spread its wings, and Ulyana shifted back into her own skin kneeling beside the woman who had taught her every road, and took Paloma's fading hand in both of hers, and bent close, and began to speak the words that would break a thousand years of law—

"—listen to me, Paloma, listen, I have something of mine I want you to have—"

—and the mountain came down around them like a held breath finally let go.
