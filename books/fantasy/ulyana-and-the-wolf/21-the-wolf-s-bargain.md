## 21. The Wolf's Bargain

The mountain was still screaming.

Ulyana felt it through her knees, through the frozen mud of the foothills, a groan so deep it wasn't sound anymore but weather, the whole mass of Thornfell shrugging off the court that had parasitized its heart for a thousand years. Behind her, the pass they'd crawled out of was vomiting smoke and light. Chunks of the mountainside sheared away and fell, slow as snow, into the valley dark. The freed dragon wheeled above it all, wings like storm-fronts, catching falling people out of the air two and three at a time.

Ulyana didn't watch any of it.

She was on her knees in the mud with Paloma's head cradled against her chest, and Paloma was fading.

Not dying—dying would have been simpler, would have been something Ulyana knew how to fight. This was worse. The mercenary lay in her arms like a coat someone had stepped out of, like a shape the world was already forgetting how to hold. Her skin had gone the grey of old ash. Her breath came so rarely that between one exhale and the next, Ulyana caught herself leaning down to check, again, whether there would be another.

The pledge was taking her. She could feel it happening, could feel the edges of Paloma going soft and porous, the way a soul poured out of a body leaves the body an empty cup. Somewhere beneath the collapsing mountain, in whatever ledger-room of the old law still stood while its walls fell, a transaction was completing itself. *One soul, pledged against one soul.* Paloma Okonkwo, who had traded everything—the god who never answered, the vow that had held her together, ten years of careful silence—for a wolf-girl who couldn't even remember her own sister's face until three days ago.

"Hey." Ulyana's voice cracked. She bent close, her hair falling around them both like a curtain against the burning sky. "Hey, you stubborn, impossible woman. You don't get to do this. You hear me? You don't *get* to."

Paloma's eyes opened. Just barely—a sliver of brown, unfocused, finding nothing. But her mouth moved.

"...loud," she whispered.

"Yes!" Ulyana laughed, and it came out half sob. "Yes, I'm loud, I'm a wolf, we're loud, and you're going to be loud too for another fifty years, do you understand me—"

"Ulyana."

The name stopped her cold. Not because Paloma said it—but because of what it meant that she could. In the high pass, when Ulyana had shifted fully to save her, she had lost more than memories. She had lost *this*. She had looked at Paloma across the fire and seen a stranger, a kind stranger, a stranger whose name she had to ask. And now, with most of herself gone into the pledge, with only scraps left rattling in her skull, Paloma was spending her last strength to say Ulyana's name like it mattered. Like it was a thing worth carrying out of a falling mountain.

"I'm here," Ulyana said. "I'm here, I've got you."

"Ledger," Paloma breathed. "In my pack. Ilya's... you have to..."

"I know about the ledger. I don't care about the ledger."

"You have to—" A cough, wet and terrible. "Someone has to remember what it cost."

Ulyana pressed her forehead to Paloma's cold one. "Then stay," she whispered, "and remember it yourself."

Above them the dragon screamed—not rage, Ulyana realized, but warning—and a section of the pass mouth collapsed inward with a boom she felt in her teeth. Refugees were streaming down the switchbacks now, dozens of them, the souls the dying fire had loosed from their reliquaries: pale, blinking people who hadn't walked under open sky in years, some in decades, some—she thought of the records in the heretic's chapel—in longer than that. They flowed past like a river parting around two stones. One of them, an old man with a glass-blower's burn-scarred hands, paused. Looked at Paloma. Looked at Ulyana.

"The broker's law," he said quietly. "It took her?"

"It took her."

He crouched, joints cracking, and studied Paloma's grey face with the professional interest of a man who had spent a century watching souls move. "Pledged, then. Not sold. Pledged souls can't be pulled back out—that's the whole point of a pledge. It has to run its course." He shook his head. "I'm sorry, wolf-girl. There's no way under the law."

"No," Ulyana said.

And then, somewhere in the hollowed-out place where her soul used to be whole, something turned over. Something small and bright and furious, the last ember of the thing she'd gotten back only hours ago when the reliquaries broke—her soul, restored, entire, humming in her chest like a second heartbeat.

*There's no way under the law.*

She thought of Sef, with his silk voice and his ledgers full of other people's years. She thought of the Lord Beneath, dragged down at the last by the very souls he'd bottled, torn apart by a thousand hands because no single bargain could touch him. She thought of the door beneath the mountain that asked each entrant their truest want, and how it had no answer for Paloma, and how Paloma had bled instead—had paid in the only coin she trusted, because asking for what she wanted was the one thing she'd never learned to do.

The old law knew pledges. It knew sales, trades, tithes, debts, inheritance, theft. It had a clause for everything a person could do to another person.

But Ulyana had spent the last week learning Paloma Okonkwo, and if she had learned anything, it was this: Paloma had spent her whole life outside the law. Smuggler's roads. Contracts with a god who'd gone quiet. Justice that fit through gaps the law didn't cover.

So Ulyana did the one thing the law had no clause for.

She reached into her own chest.

Not flesh—she didn't bleed, though her breath went ragged and her vision swam. She reached into the deeper place, the soul-place, and she took hold of what was hers the way she'd once taken hold of a burning beam to drag her sister from their den. And she *split* it.

It hurt the way grief hurts. It hurt the way love hurts. It was not a wound; it was a choosing, made permanent, made flesh. Half of her soul came away in her hands like a handful of warm light, like a coal carried carefully from a dying fire, and she could already feel the absence of it—the memories it held dimming, her sister's laugh going distant again, the smell of the den-smoke thinning. She would lose things. She was losing them even now, watching them go, and she let them go anyway.

Because a soul freely given cancels a soul forcibly pledged. Not bought. Not traded. Not owed. *Given*—and the old law, which had an answer for every cruelty ever devised, had never once been asked to account for kindness.

"Old man," she said, and her voice came out strange, resonant, doubled. "Is there a law against gifts?"

He stared at her. At the light in her hands. His scarred fingers trembled. "No," he said slowly. "There's no law against gifts. There's never been a need for one. No one gives what the courts can take."

"Then watch," Ulyana said, "closely. Because I'm about to break your whole profession."

She cupped the half of her soul in both hands, leaned over Paloma Okonkwo, and spoke—not to the dying woman, not to the dragon wheeling overhead, not to the silent god whose chapel they'd sheltered in, but to the law itself, to the deep old ledger-thing that lived under mountains and behind doors and in every contract ever signed in blood:

"Hear me. I am Ulyana, daughter of the western packs, and this is mine to give. No one bought it. No one holds debt against it. No one is owed. I give my soul's half to Paloma Okonkwo freely, wholly, without condition or expectation, to cancel the pledge taken from her. Witness me."

The mountain stopped screaming.

For one heartbeat, everything held—the smoke, the falling rock, the refugees frozen mid-stride, the dragon hanging on the wind—and Ulyana felt the old law turn its attention on her like a vast, blind eye. She felt it search its ledgers. Sales: no. Trades: no. Debts: no. Theft: no. Tithe: no. It searched for the clause, the hook, the price, and found nothing, because there was nothing to find, because she had built her gift in the one shape the law had never been forced to hold.

*A gift.*

The eye closed.

The light in Ulyana's hands sank into Paloma's chest like rain into drought-cracked earth.

* * *

For a moment, nothing.

Then Paloma gasped—a huge, tearing breath, the kind a drowning woman takes when she breaks the surface—and her back arched off the mud, and color came flooding up her throat and into her face like dawn coming up over the foothills. Grey flushed to brown. Ash became skin. Her hands, which had lain open and empty at her sides, closed into fists, and then opened again, flexing, testing, *remembering themselves*.

Her eyes opened all the way.

They found Ulyana immediately, and they were sharp and clear and absolutely, ferociously awake.

"What," Paloma rasped, "did you do."

Ulyana laughed. It came out wrecked and watery and she didn't care. "Something illegal."

"That's not—" Paloma pushed herself up on her elbows, wincing, alive, *gloriously* alive, and grabbed Ulyana's wrist hard enough to bruise. "That's not possible. Pledged souls don't—there's no mechanism—"

"There's no mechanism for gifts either. That's rather the point." Ulyana covered Paloma's hand with her own. "You taught me that. All those roads only smugglers know. You spend your whole life walking where the law doesn't look. So I gave you something where the law can't follow."

Paloma stared at her. And Ulyana watched the arithmetic happen behind her eyes—the same terrible arithmetic she'd watched Paloma do a hundred times on the road, weighing costs, counting exits—and then watched it fail, completely, for the first time, because there was no cost column here that made sense. Someone had paid for her life and the payment was *love*, freely given, and there was no line in any ledger for that.

"You split your soul," Paloma said finally. Her voice was very quiet. "You'll lose things. Memories. You told me—you told me what it was like in the pass, forgetting my name. Why would you—"

"Because I remembered it again," Ulyana said simply. "You came back. You always come back. And I figured—" her throat tightened, "—I figured a soul isn't meant to be kept whole anyway. It's meant to be shared. With a pack. With—" she shrugged, suddenly shy, suddenly the terrified naked girl at the ford again, "—with family."

Paloma made a sound Ulyana had never heard her make before. It might have been a laugh. It might have been something older and sadder breaking cleanly in half. She sat up all the way, mud-streaked and smoking-haired and impossibly alive, and she pulled Ulyana into her arms, and she held on the way people hold on to things they once believed they'd have to live without.

"You absolute disaster," she muttered into Ulyana's hair. "You beautiful, reckless, law-breaking disaster."

"Wolf," Ulyana corrected, muffled against her shoulder. "Law-breaking wolf."

Around them, the world resumed. The dragon landed in the valley below with a ground-shaking thud, folding its ancient wings, and began—carefully, gently, with a delicacy that seemed almost comical in something so vast—to help the last refugees down the final stretch of scree. The old man with the burned hands squeezed Ulyana's shoulder as he passed. "First gift in a thousand years," he said. "The brokers are going to be apoplectic." He smiled, and it transformed his whole ruined face. "Good."

Behind them, Thornfell settled into its long collapse—not destruction now, but exhalation, the mountain breathing out a thousand years of held breath. Snow slid from the high peaks in long white ribbons. Somewhere deep inside, Ulyana knew, the great halls were filling with rubble, the frozen feast burying itself at last, the reliquary vaults crumbling open to the sky. The court of quiet bells was quiet forever now.

Paloma got to her feet. Slowly, testing each leg like a soldier after a long illness, but steadily. She looked up at the burning pass, then down at the valley full of stumbling, blinking, free people, then at Ulyana—at the faint new luminescence that hung around her, the mark of a soul given away, which was somehow brighter than a soul hoarded.

"You'll feel the gaps," Paloma said. "When they come. I'll tell you what's missing. Everything I know about you, everything from the road—I'll keep it for you until it grows back. That's a promise."

Ulyana tilted her head. "Was that a vow? To your silent god?"

Paloma considered. Then she shook her head, and something eased in her face that Ulyana had never seen ease before—not in the shrine, not at the ford, not once in all their weeks of roads.

"No," she said. "That one's just for you."

* * *

They walked down into the valley as the sun came up.

It was a strange procession. The freed souls moved slowly, blinking at grass as if they'd forgotten it, weeping at ordinary wind. The dragon ranged along the ridgeline above them, a dark sentinel against the paling stars. And in the middle of it all walked a mercenary with no god and a wolf-girl with half a soul, supporting each other, arguing softly about whose fault it was that neither of them had died.

"Yours," Ulyana said. "Entirely yours. You pledged your soul without telling anyone."

"You stole my map at a river crossing."

"You *gave* me the map at a river crossing, eventually, after I nearly drowned us both."

"After *you* nearly drowned *us* both, yes, I remember, I was there—"

"See, this is why I gave you half my soul. Someone has to remember things around here."

Paloma stopped walking. Ulyana took two more steps before she noticed, and turned, and found the mercenary standing very still in the grey-gold light, looking at her with an expression Ulyana couldn't read.

"What?" she said. "What is it?"

"Nothing," Paloma said. But her voice was rough. "It's just—Ilya told me once, near the end, that a soul was the only thing worth trading, and the worst thing to trade. I've spent ten years thinking he was right." She looked east, where the sun was clearing the last ridge, laying gold across the whole broken, beautiful valley. "He was half right. It's the only thing worth *giving*."

Ahead of them, down in the green foothills where spring was already pushing up through the thaw, Ulyana saw figures waiting on the road—refugees gathered around cookfires, and among them, unmistakable even at this distance, a young woman with Ulyana's own dark hair standing on a cart, scanning the climbing crowd with desperate, hungry eyes.

Ulyana's heart lurched. The memory was still there—her sister, rescued from the vaults, memory-wiped, carried out on the dragon's back. Her sister, who didn't know her. Her sister, who was looking for someone she couldn't name, the way Ulyana once looked at Paloma across a ford.

"She's looking for you," Paloma said quietly.

"I know." Ulyana's throat ached. "And she won't know me. And I'm half a soul short of the memories that would make it easier." She wiped her face with the back of her hand, and squared her shoulders, and felt the gap inside her where the given half had been—and found, strangely, that it didn't ache. It rang. Like a bell. Like a room made bigger by an open door.

"But I remember the important thing," she said. "I remember that she's mine, and I'm hers. The rest we can grow back together."

She started down the hill. After a moment, Paloma fell into step beside her—close, solid, alive, her shoulder brushing Ulyana's, the way family walks.

Below them, the sister on the cart went suddenly still. Turned. Found Ulyana's face across the distance.

And even with half a soul, even with the gaps, even across a hundred yards of thawing spring mud—Ulyana saw recognition kindle in her sister's eyes like the first flame of a fire that nothing, no court, no law, no mountain, would ever put out again.
