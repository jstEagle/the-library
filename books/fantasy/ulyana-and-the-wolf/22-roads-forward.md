## 22. Roads Forward

Spring came to the foothills the way forgiveness came—slowly, and all at once.

Paloma noticed it first in the mud. All winter the ground beneath the refugee camp had been frozen iron, hard enough to bruise a knee, indifferent as any god. Then one morning in late Thawmonth she stepped out of her tent and sank to her ankle in soft brown earth that smelled of worms and rain and things beginning again. She stood there for a long moment, one boot sunk, the other dry, and felt something in her chest loosen that she hadn't known was clenched.

The camp had grown since the mountain fell. What began as forty souls stumbling out of the disintegrating tunnels with soot in their lungs and nothing but what they carried had become a settlement of nearly three hundred. They came down from Thornfell in ones and twos at first—the freed captives who'd wandered lost for years, some of them decades, blinking in daylight like creatures pulled from deep water. Then came their kin, walking in from villages across three valleys when word spread that the tithe-collectors were no more, that the under-court was rubble and its Lord Beneath dragged down by the very souls he'd stolen. They came with carts and goats and children, with hammers and seeds and grief, and they built.

Paloma watched it happen the way she watched everything: from the edge, with a hand near her knife.

She told herself it was habit. A mercenary's instinct, honed on twenty years of smuggler's roads where the friendly face was the dangerous one. But Ulyana saw through it, because Ulyana saw through everything now—they shared half a soul between them, and apparently that came with certain disadvantages, privacy chief among them.

"You're doing it again," Ulyana said, appearing at her elbow with two bowls of barley porridge and handing her one without asking. "Standing at the perimeter like you're expecting Sef to crawl out of a ditch."

"Sef's dead."

"Sef's *kind* isn't." Ulyana shrugged, steam curling off her bowl. Her hair had grown back in over the winter, thick and dark, and she wore it braided now in the style of her pack—a style she'd relearned from her sister, which meant she'd had to teach it to her sister first, which meant they'd spent an entire evening laughing and failing and starting over until both of them could do it blind. Paloma had watched from the firelight and pretended not to be moved. "Bargain brokers don't die. They just find new mountains."

"Then I'll watch for new mountains."

"You'll eat your porridge before it goes cold," Ulyana said, "and then you're coming with me. Mira wants to show you something."

Mira. Ulyana's sister, restored whole from the court's cellars along with every other soul in the reliquary vaults when the dragon-fire died and the pledges dissolved. She'd been the court's cupbearer for eleven years, memory-wiped, pouring wine for nobles wearing borrowed faces, while her own sister sold everything she was to buy the pack's survival and couldn't even remember why the girl's face made her ache. Now Mira ran the camp's infirmary, because it turned out that eleven years of serving poison-laced feasts taught you exactly which herbs countered which toxins, and she had steady hands and a fury she'd learned to aim at suffering instead of people.

Paloma ate her porridge. It was, annoyingly, good.

* * *

Mira's surprise was a garden.

It sat in the sheltered fold of a hillside above the camp, terraced into the slope with timber palisades against the deer, and it was already green: rows of feverfew and comfrey, yarrow and valerian, woundwort and saintsbell, each bed marked with wooden tags burned in Mira's careful hand. Paloma stood at the gate and stared, because she recognized the layout. She'd have recognized it anywhere.

"That's Ilya's," she said. Her voice came out strange. "That's the physic garden behind his house in Vhalen Row. The one he kept when everyone said a contract-broker had no business growing healing herbs."

"He wrote it down," Mira said. "In the ledger. There was a page near the end—not debts, just... notes. Things he loved. The garden was on it. So was a recipe for cough syrup that actually works, which the children of this camp can attest to." She smiled, and it was Ulyana's smile, and it still caught Paloma off guard sometimes, seeing the sister in the face. "I thought you should see it before the first harvest."

Paloma walked the rows slowly. The soil was black and rich. Somewhere below, a hammer rang on anvil, and children shouted over a game whose rules seemed to involve a great deal of screaming, and smoke rose from thirty cookfires into a sky washed clean by last night's rain. None of it required her. None of it needed guarding, or brokering, or a knife.

Ilya had loved this garden. She'd forgotten that—or rather, she'd filed it away under things too soft to carry on the road. He'd grown saintsbell for the fever cases nobody else would treat, back when treating them broke quarantine law. He'd brokered contracts all day and knelt in the dirt all evening, and he'd never once explained himself, and she'd never once asked, because asking would have meant admitting she wanted to understand him, and wanting things got people killed.

He'd sold his soul to guarantee hers. He'd written the debt into his ledger knowing it would transfer to her on his death, and she'd spent a year believing that was betrayal before learning it was the opposite—the most enormous, most Ilya-like act of love imaginable, done in the only language he spoke fluently: binding terms, sealed clauses, collateral. And then she'd traded it anyway, pledged herself in his place, and the pledge had taken hold, and she had begun to fade—

—and Ulyana had torn her own soul in half and given half away freely, because the old law had no answer for a gift.

Paloma crouched beside the saintsbell and pressed one finger to a leaf. It was real. All of it was real. The mountain had fallen and the world had not ended; it had, impossibly, continued, and continued *better*, and here she was kneeling in dirt in the spring with half of someone else's soul warm in her chest like a second heartbeat.

"Ulyana says you haven't prayed since," Mira said quietly, behind her. "Since the god answered."

"I wouldn't call it answering."

"The silence released you. That's more than my gods ever did, and I worshipped four of them."

Paloma didn't answer. But she stayed in the garden long after Mira left, until the light went gold and long, and the smell of thyme rose up around her like a memory she'd finally earned the right to keep.

* * *

The dragon ranged free now, and the foothills had learned to live with it.

They saw it perhaps once a week: a vast shape crossing the high passes, wings catching the sun like hammered bronze, and the first few times the whole camp froze until someone—usually a child—pointed and shouted its name. Because it had a name now. The freed captives had argued about it for days, and settled, in the end, on Ember-Who-Waits-No-More, which was too long for anyone to actually use, so the children called it Embers, and Embers it became.

It had carried stragglers out of the collapsing mountain on its back, dozens of them, refusing to stop until the last soul stood on open ground. Afterward it had simply... stayed. It nested in the caldera of a dead volcano two valleys north and hunted in the high country and occasionally descended to let the children gawk at it from a respectful distance. Once, in midwinter, it had landed at the camp's edge during a wolf-cull gone wrong—raiders, not wolves—and the raiders had reconsidered their profession with remarkable speed.

"They remember," Ulyana had said, watching it go. "The old fires. Whatever chained it down there, whatever fed it souls—it remembers being free, and it chose us."

"It chose the open sky," Paloma said.

"Same thing, maybe."

Today, as Paloma walked back down from the garden, Embers crossed the sky directly overhead, low enough that its shadow swept the camp like a benediction, and three hundred people looked up without fear, and Paloma found she was looking up too, and smiling, and that no one was watching her do it except the wolf-girl leaning against the fence with her arms crossed and an expression of insufferable satisfaction.

"Don't," Paloma warned.

"I didn't say anything."

"You were about to."

"I was going to say," Ulyana said, pushing off the fence and falling into step beside her, shoulder brushing shoulder, "that the caravan leaves tomorrow. The one going south. Kess is leading it—he's got family in Marrowdale—and there's room if we want it. Or there's the eastern road, toward the coast. Or we stay." She paused. "Or you stay and I stay, which is the same thing, but I like saying it."

Paloma looked at the camp. At the new roofs going on the longhouse, at the smoke, at Mira's green hillside, at children who had never known the tithe and would grow up thinking dragons were neighbors and monsters were stories. Justice carried. Not perfectly—Sef's kind always found new mountains, and somewhere in the world other courts were surely counting other tithes—but carried, here, in this valley, by people who would not forget.

"I've been thinking," Paloma said slowly, "about prayer."

Ulyana went very still beside her. Of everyone in the world, she alone knew what prayer had cost Paloma Okonkwo—ten years of speaking into silence, ten years of keeping vows to a god who would not say yea or nay, and then the final night beneath the falling mountain when she had tried to void the contract and the silence had finally, finally answered—not with words, but with acceptance, with release, with something that had felt like a hand letting go and had left her hollowed and grieving and free.

"You don't have to," Ulyana said.

"I know." Paloma stopped walking. The evening light lay across the valley like spilled honey. "That's the thing. For ten years I prayed because the contract required it. Before that, I prayed because Ilya taught me the words and I didn't know there was a choice. I have never once in my life prayed because I *wanted* to say something." She turned to look at Ulyana, at this woman who had been a wolf at a ford and a stranger and a burden and a partner and half of her own soul, literally, in ways the theologians would need decades to untangle. "I want to try. Tonight. Will you—" Her voice caught, and she hated it, and let it. "Will you come with me? Not to pray. Just to be there. In case it's quiet."

Ulyana took her hand. "It won't be quiet," she said. "You'll be talking."

* * *

They went up at moonrise, past the garden, past the palisade, to the bare crown of the hill where the wind came clean off the snowmelt and the whole valley lay below them, firelit and breathing. Paloma had brought nothing. No offerings, no incense, none of the ritual apparatus of a decade of obligatory devotion. Just herself, kneeling in the spring grass, and Ulyana sitting close enough that their shoulders touched.

She folded her hands the old way, out of habit, and then deliberately unfolded them, palms open on her knees, empty.

"To no one," she said aloud, testing the words. "Or to everyone. I haven't decided."

The wind moved through the grass. Below, a dog barked twice and fell silent. Somewhere far north, wings beat against the stars.

"I used to think the silence was punishment," she went on. "Ten years, and I thought: I've failed some term, some clause, some fine print I never read, and this is the penalty—no guidance, no answer, just me and the road. I built my whole life inside that silence. Trusted no one. Slept armed. Kept moving." She laughed softly, without much humor. "Turns out the silence wasn't a wall. It was a door, held open, waiting for me to walk through it on my own feet. You can't guide someone who won't choose a direction. I never chose anything until her."

She glanced sideways. Ulyana's eyes were bright in the moonlight, and she was not pretending not to listen.

"So this is the prayer. No requests. No terms. No contract." Paloma breathed in the cold sweet air, the mud and the thyme and the woodsmoke of three hundred hearths. "Thank you. To whoever kept the ledger open long enough for a gift to count. To whatever made the old law blind to love. To the dragon, and the dead mentor who grew herbs, and the sister who pours wine no longer, and the wolf who split her soul in half because the math demanded it and she refused the math." Her voice steadied. "Thank you. That's all. That's the whole of it."

She bowed her head. Held it there. Listened.

And the silence—she knew this the way she knew a road, the way she knew a loaded crossbow, the way she knew the difference between a friend and a trap—it was not empty. It was full. Full of wind and dogs and distant wings and a heartbeat that was half hers and the small sounds of a valley full of people building a future out of mud and stubbornness. If a god lived anywhere, it lived in that. If it didn't, then the fullness was enough, and she had decided, kneeling there with her palms open, that she could live with enough.

Paloma Okonkwo got up off her knees smiling.

"All right?" Ulyana asked.

"All right." She looked south, where the road unspooled toward Marrowdale and the coast and every place she'd never let herself want to see. Then east, toward the passes. Then at the camp below, home, if she wanted it, for as long as she wanted it—which might be forever, or might be until the road called, and either way the choosing was hers. "Kess's caravan leaves at dawn?"

"Dawn."

"We'll walk with them as far as the ford," Paloma said. "After that—" she smiled again, easier this time, the smile of a woman with no contract left to break and no vow left to keep except the ones she'd chosen— "after that, the road's open. Every road. We can take our time deciding."

Ulyana laced her fingers through hers. "We're good at taking our time. We took a whole mountain apart one careful lie at a time."

"One honest choice at a time," Paloma corrected. "The lies were mostly other people's."

They started down the hill together, two shadows in the moonlight, and behind them the valley glowed, and ahead of them the dark road ran on toward morning, and neither of them hurried, because for the first time in either of their lives, everything worth reaching would still be there whenever they arrived.

At the bottom of the hill, Ulyana stopped suddenly, head tilting, ears pricking at something on the wind.

"What is it?" Paloma asked, hand drifting toward her knife out of twenty years of habit—and then, deliberately, away again.

Ulyana grinned, wild and bright as the dawn coming. "Riders on the south road. A lot of them. And they're carrying something that smells like—" she breathed in, and her grin widened— "like sealed wax, and old paper, and trouble."

Paloma looked at the dark horizon, where torchlight flickered like a scatter of falling stars, and felt the old familiar pull in her chest—not fear, not anymore, but the road itself, calling.

"Well," she said, and her smile was all teeth and welcome. "Trouble always did know where to find us. Let's go introduce ourselves properly."

Hand in hand, the wolf and the woman walked down to meet whatever came next—and the road, open as a promise, ran on ahead of them both.
