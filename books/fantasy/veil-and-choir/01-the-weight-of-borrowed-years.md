## 1. The Weight of Borrowed Years

The fog came down off the ridge at dusk the way it always did, slow and deliberate, like a landlord collecting rent. Paloma Brightwater watched it swallow the last of the market stalls from the window of their shop — *Brightwater & Sons, Memory Brokerage*, though there had never been any sons, and Paloma had painted the sign themselves at nineteen with a confidence they no longer possessed — and thought, not for the first time, that Grayhollow was a town built for forgetting.

That was the business, after all. The Veil had no magic in it; everyone said so, everyone agreed, the way people agree the sky is blue. Whatever power moved the world beyond the valley's borders simply stopped at the fog line, and travelers who crossed into the Veil carried their memories like glass bottles on a rocky shore. Something about the passage cracked them loose. Grief went soft. Faces blurred. And what slipped free drifted, faint and luminous as moth-wing, until someone with the knack — someone like Paloma — could gather it up, bottle it, and sell it back.

It was honest work, more or less. Honest the way pickpocketing is honest if you leave a receipt.

The traveler was staying at the Gilded Wren, which was the finest lie Grayhollow told about itself: three stories of peeling plaster and a lobby that smelled of tallow and old ambition. He was a wool merchant, he claimed, from somewhere east, but his boots were too good for wool, his hands too soft, and his purse too heavy by half. Paloma had watched him at dinner, watched him drink two cups of the Wren's watered wine and complain loudly about the fog, and watched him retire to his room with the particular swagger of a man who believed himself the cleverest thing in any room he entered.

Paloma knocked at his door an hour past midnight, dressed in the gray apron of a housemaid, carrying a basin of water nobody had asked for.

"Housekeeping," they said, in the flat accent of the low town.

The man opened the door already smiling at his own good fortune, and that smile was the last thing he gave away freely. Paloma set the basin down, met his eyes, and *pulled*.

The knack felt different every time, but tonight it felt like drawing a thread from a spool — one smooth, silver length of him unspooling into Paloma's cupped hands, invisible to anyone without the gift. The merchant swayed, blinked, and found nothing missing, because that was the art of it: you never took the whole memory, only its brightest thread. He would remember being robbed someday, vaguely, the way you remember a dream. He would blame the wine.

Paloma murmured an apology, closed the door, and sat down on the stairs to look at what they'd stolen.

And the world fell open.

The thread bloomed in the mind's eye the way all lifted memories did, but this one — this one was *vast*. Paloma had taken memories before from travelers who'd come from beyond the Veil, little scraps mostly: a market square with a fountain, a mother's voice singing, the taste of some fruit that didn't grow in the valley. Souvenirs. Postcards. This was a cathedral.

He had been to the capital. He had stood in the Amphitheater of Voices on the day of the Great Choir, when ten thousand singers in white robes filled a bowl of stone the size of a town, and when they sang, the air itself had *bent* — Paloma felt it even secondhand, felt the memory's owner weep without shame as bridges of light arched over the crowd, as the river rose from its banks and hung there, glittering, obedient, held in the shape of the song. Magic was real. Not rumor, not fireside tale. Real, and enormous, and *out there*.

The memory kept going. Cities stacked like wedding cakes, lit through the night. Roads carved through mountains. And then — the thread darkened here, the way threads did when they touched grief — war. A border burning. Soldiers marching under banners Paloma didn't recognize, and above them, always, the white-robed cantors, singing walls into being, singing arrows out of the sky, singing and singing while the world below them burned anyway.

The merchant had turned away from the front. That was the shame coiled at the thread's heart. He had made money on the war and traveled far to make money away from it, and the Veil — magicless, forgotten, *safe* — had seemed to him exactly the kind of place a coward could prosper.

Paloma came back to themselves on the stairs of the Gilded Wren with cold hands and a racing heart.

There was a whole world. There were cities where the air itself sang, and wars fought with voices instead of swords, and ten thousand robed figures who could lift a river with a note. And here was Grayhollow, huddled in its fog, trading in other people's leavings, telling itself the fog was simply weather.

They walked home through streets gone blue with moonlight, and the fog pressed close around them, and for the first time in twenty-six years it did not feel like a blanket. It felt like a wall. It felt like something with a purpose.

Paloma tried to shake the feeling. They failed.

* * *

The shop smelled of cedar and lamp oil, shelves lined with stoppered vials that caught the lamplight like a congregation of tiny moons. Each one held a memory — sold back to their owners for a fee, or bought outright from those who wanted to be rid of them, which paid better and hurt less. Paloma lit the lamp behind the counter, set the merchant's bright new thread into a vial, and labeled it in their careful hand: *Amphitheater. Do not dilute.*

Then they sat with it a long time, turning the vial in their fingers, wanting it and fearing it in equal measure. To drink a memory that deep, that foreign — travelers who bought back such things sometimes wept for days. Sometimes they didn't come back for more. But Paloma had spent their whole life nibbling at the edges of other lives, and tonight the edges weren't enough. Tonight they wanted the whole meal.

A knock at the door stopped them — or rather, the lack of a knock. The sound was a body sagging against wood. Once. Twice.

Paloma blew out the lamp on instinct and crossed the dark shop. Through the frosted pane: a shape, swaying, one hand braced against the doorframe.

"We're closed," Paloma said.

"Please." A man's voice, scraped down to gravel. "I was told — Brightwater. You buy memories. You *keep* them."

"I sell them back."

"No." A wet cough. "You keep them. That's what matters. Please."

Every sensible instinct Paloma owned said *bar the door*. Instead — because curiosity had been the first knife ever stuck in them, and it had never come out — they unbolted the lock.

The stranger collapsed across the threshold into their arms. He was young, maybe younger than Paloma, dressed in road-leathers gone black with blood from a wound high in his side. His eyes were fever-bright and fixed on Paloma's face with terrible urgency, as if Paloma were a door and he needed only to get through.

"They're coming," he whispered. "Cantors. Two days behind me, maybe less. You have to take it."

"Take what? Who's coming? Sit down, you're—"

His hand shot out and seized Paloma's wrist with shocking strength, and something was pressed into their palm: a roll of oilskin, stiff and heavy, stained dark along one edge. Blood. Old blood and new.

"The map," he breathed. "To the heart. Under the mountain. The seal is thinning — do you understand? The *seal is thinning*. They'll want it destroyed or they'll want it used, and either way they'll kill whoever holds it, so hold it anyway. Hold it and *look*."

"Look at what?" Paloma's voice sounded very small in the dark shop. "What seal? What are you talking about?"

But the stranger's eyes had gone past them, past the shelves of bottled memories, fixed on something far away. When he spoke again it was barely a breath.

"The fog isn't weather," he said. "It never was. Ask the fog what it's *for*."

And then the strength went out of him all at once, like a snuffed candle, and his weight became dead weight, and Paloma Brightwater knelt on the floor of their own shop holding a dead man and a bloodstained map, listening to the fog press against the windows like something patient, something waiting, something that had been waiting a very long time.

Outside, far off toward the valley's edge, a bell began to ring.

No one rang bells in Grayhollow at night. No one had ever needed to.

Paloma looked down at the oilskin in their hands, and unwrapped it just enough to see the first inked line — a mountain, drawn in careful strokes, with a spiral descending beneath it into darkness — and understood, with a dread that settled into the bones like cold water, that the world they had glimpsed tonight in a stolen thread had been looking for them all along.
