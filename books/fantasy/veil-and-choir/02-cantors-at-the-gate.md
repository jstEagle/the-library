## 2. Cantors at the Gate

The bells woke Paloma before the light did.

Not the market bells, which rang in thirds and meant nothing. These came from the gate tower — six strokes, then silence, then six again. The alarm of authority. In nineteen years of living above their shop on Tallow Row, Paloma had heard it exactly twice: once for a tax assessor nobody liked, and once for a fire that had eaten half the tannery.

They dressed in the dark and went to the window.

Fog lay over Grayhollow as it always did, thick as spilled milk, but beneath it moved shapes that had no business moving. Twelve riders in grey-and-white livery, their cloaks stitched with a device Paloma knew only from stolen memories: a circle bisected by a single vertical line. The mark of the Choir. Their horses made no sound on the cobblestones. That was the detail that turned Paloma's stomach cold. Hooves should ring. These horses crossed the square like thoughts crossing a mind.

*The seal is thinning.* The courier's whisper still lived behind Paloma's teeth, three days old now, worn smooth by repetition. They had hidden the map inside the false bottom of their memory-chest, wrapped in oilcloth beside the journal, and told themselves every night that no one could possibly know. Thieves were good at lying to themselves. It was practically the trade.

Down in the square, Magistrate Ondrell Voss emerged from his fine house in his fine coat, bowing before he had even reached the riders. Paloma watched him gesture — toward the market, toward the tannery row, and then, with a small apologetic flourish that made them feel sick, toward Tallow Row.

Toward the shop.

Paloma was already moving. Not out — they'd learned long ago that doors were where pursuers expected you — but down, through the trapdoor beneath the memory-chest, into the cellar where they kept the goods that couldn't be sold. Jars of borrowed grief. A soldier's last morning. A bride's whole wedding day, paid for by a groom who wanted to forget it. They took none of it. They took the map, the journal, a knife, and the coat with the good pockets, and they climbed out through the coal chute into the alley behind the chandler's, just as boots began hammering on their front door.

From the mouth of the alley, they watched.

Cantor Serah Vane stood in the middle of Paloma's shop like a woman visiting a grave she had dug herself. She was younger than Paloma had imagined from the memories they'd tasted — Choir cantors featured in travelers' recollections the way storms feature in sailors' stories — with close-cropped hair and a voice that seemed to arrive slightly before her lips moved. She touched nothing. She looked at everything.

"The chest," she said, and one of her grey-cloaked attendants knelt and opened it, and Paloma watched the man's hands find the false bottom within ten heartbeats because of course he would; it was a Choir trick, and Paloma had stolen it *from* the Choir without knowing.

The attendant lifted out the oilcloth, unwrapped it, and went very still.

"Empty," Serah said.

"Magistrate." Her voice carried into the street without effort, the way sound carries underwater. "You were certain?"

Ondrell Voss spread his hands, sweating even in the fog's chill. "I saw it myself. The courier came to this shop bleeding. He died here. Whatever he carried, she has it."

"She?" Serah turned slowly, and though the fog swallowed most details, Paloma felt her gaze pass across the alley mouth like a lantern beam. "You sold me a certainty, magistrate. I paid you in a year without your debts. Certainties are worth more than years."

"I—" Voss's throat worked. "She steals memories. Everyone knows. She would have taken whatever he—"

"Yes," Serah said softly. "She takes what others lose. And so we will take what she loves until she brings it back." A pause. "Burn the shop."

Paloma didn't wait for the first flame. They ran.

* * *

Serah Vane stood in the ashes an hour later and felt the old sickness rise in her, the one she had been trained to call *certainty* and had privately begun to call *doubt*.

Twelve cantors and forty blades, and the thief had slipped through fog thicker than any ward the Choir maintained beyond the valley — which was itself the wrongness she kept circling. She had read the expedition records. She knew why they had come: the seal at the valley's heart was failing, the song fraying note by note, and the map drawn by the last Keeper showed the way down to mend or unmake it. She knew what her superiors intended, too, though they dressed it in liturgy. Not mending. *Reclaiming.* If the source of all magic woke, the Choir meant to be standing closest when it opened its eyes.

But the thief had run not outward, toward the passes and escape, but *inward*. Deeper into the valley. Toward the heart.

That was not the behavior of someone carrying stolen goods. That was the behavior of someone who had read the journal.

"Track her," Serah told her lieutenants. Then, quieter, to herself, watching the smoke thread up through the fog: "*Him.* Or neither. We don't actually know, do we?"

No one answered. No one ever did. She adjusted the silver band at her throat — the one that let her sing the Choir's commands instead of speaking them — and thought, not for the first time, that the order had sent her to hunt a thief, and she was beginning to suspect she'd been sent to hunt a witness.

* * *

By dusk Paloma stood at the valley's edge for the first time in their life.

Everyone in Grayhollow knew the Veil's border. You walked far enough and the fog simply became *more*, a white wall you could press your hand into, and beyond it the world happened to other people. Children dared each other to touch it. Old men swore the fog had been there since the flood, since the gods, since always. Natural. Eternal. As much a part of the world as rain.

Paloma pushed through it now, map clutched against their ribs, journal stuffed inside their coat, lungs burning, and stopped.

Because there were seams.

Great curved lines in the fog itself, faint as scars on glass, running up into heights the eye couldn't follow and down into depths the feet refused. Where the fog met the seams, it *bent*. Fog did not bend. Weather did not bend. Paloma pressed a palm flat against the nearest seam and felt — not cold, not damp, but a vibration, low and patient, like a held breath. Like a note being sustained by someone with extraordinary discipline and no intention of ever stopping.

"No," Paloma said aloud, to no one, to the fog. "No, it's weather. It's just weather."

But they had spent their life handling other people's certainties, and they knew the texture of a lie when they finally held their own. This was not weather. This was *masonry*. Someone had built this wall, note by note, song by song, and sealed a valley inside it — and everyone born within it, every friend and neighbor and customer who had ever sold Paloma a memory or bought one back, had lived their whole lives inside a cage and called the bars *horizon*.

The journal was heavy in their coat. By the light of a shuttered lantern, huddled in the seam of the world, Paloma opened it again to the page they'd read a dozen times:

*The Veil is not a curse upon the land. It is a lock upon a door. What the Choir buried sings still, and the song is wearing out.*

And beneath it, in a different, hurried hand — the courier's dying addition:

*If the seal thins, the fog thins with it. Find the Keeper's road. Do not let them open it. Do not let them keep it shut forever either. There is a third thing. Ask the thief. Only a thief can hold what others lose.*

Behind, far off through the fog, Paloma heard the bells of Grayhollow ringing six and six again — the Choir, sweeping the town, getting closer. Ahead, the seams in the wall of the world glowed faintly where the lantern touched them, and the map in their hands showed a road that began exactly here and went *down*.

Home was burning behind them. Home had been a cell all along. There was nothing left to do that was honest except walk forward.

Paloma snuffed the lantern, found the first marker stone of the Keeper's road by touch, and stepped off the edge of everything they had ever believed.
