## 3. What the Song Unmade

The Keeper's road did not feel like a road. It felt like being swallowed slowly and politely.

Paloma walked for what their body insisted was a day and their mind refused to confirm, down a stair of marker stones that appeared out of the fog only when their boot was already reaching for it — as though the road existed only for the person standing on it, and slept otherwise. The lantern stayed unlit. Light, the journal had warned in the dead courier's cramped hand, *draws the eye of everything that still has one*. So Paloma went by touch and by the faint seam-glow of the ward itself, that hairline brightness running along the walls of the world like a scar that hadn't finished deciding whether to heal.

They talked to themselves to stay sane. They told themselves stories — fragments lifted from travelers over the years, a fisherman's wedding, a queen's coronation, a child's first snow — stitching them together aloud as they descended, because silence down here had weight, and weight pressed.

It was on the third telling of the fisherman's wedding that Paloma realized someone else was breathing in the dark.

* * *

They woke bound, which was embarrassing, because they didn't remember falling asleep. The fog had thickened into something almost solid, and in it stood a camp that shouldn't have existed: iron braziers burning without smoke, tents of grey canvas, and soldiers whose armor was dull as riverstone, made not to shine but to be forgotten. At the center, warming his hands at a brazier as if he'd been waiting all night for company, stood a broad man in a coat stitched from what looked like dozens of smaller coats, none of them matching.

"Memory-thief," he said, without turning. "You walk loud for someone who steals for a living."

"You're outside the Veil," Paloma said. Their voice came out steadier than they felt. "Magic doesn't work here."

The man turned. He had a face like a cliff that had been argued with — handsome once, then weathered by ambition into something harder. His eyes were the problem. They were too bright, lit from behind, like windows in a house where someone was burning the furniture.

"I'm Kestrel Marr," he said, "and I've been *inside* longer than your valley has been a valley." He crouched, bringing his burning eyes level with theirs. "Where is my map?"

"It isn't yours."

"No." He smiled, and it was almost warm. "It's mine now, because you're going to give it to me, and because you're going to do it gladly, once I tell you what your Choir has been hiding from you." He reached out and, with two fingers, touched Paloma's temple — and the fog around them *moved*, rippling outward like the surface of a struck drum.

Paloma felt it like a hook behind the eyes. Not magic — there was no magic here — but something older wearing magic's clothes. Marr's voice dropped into them without passing through their ears.

"The Choir told you they sealed away a monster. A devourer. A god of hunger." He laughed softly. "They sealed away the *well*. The source. Every spell ever sung, every miracle, every curse — it all came up from this place, through the roots of the mountain, and the Choir got greedy and capped the well and called the cap a mercy. Your fog isn't a blessing, thief. It's a lid. And I—" he spread his hands, "—am going to take the lid off."

"And rule whatever wakes," Paloma said. "I've met men like you. You all have the same speech."

Marr's smile thinned. "I'll rule what wakes because nothing else will be left standing to argue. But you — you're interesting. A thief of memory, walking the Keeper's road alone. Do you know why the seal is thinning? It isn't age. It's *hunger*. The well wants its song back, and it's been eating the edges of the binding for a century." He leaned close. "And you, little thief, are full to the brim with other people's songs."

He took the map from inside Paloma's coat himself, gently, the way one takes a candle from a sleeping child.

* * *

Beneath the roots of the mountain, the world ended in a door.

That was the only word for it. The tunnel opened into a cavern vast enough to have weather, and at its center stood a door of white stone carved with ten thousand names in relief — names worn shallow, as if the stone had been trying to forget them. Through the cracks around its frame seeped light that wasn't light: a pressure, a hum, a note held so long it had become the shape of the silence around it.

Paloma's stolen memories screamed. All of them at once. The fisherman's wedding, the queen's crown, the child's snow — every borrowed thing in their head pressed against the inside of their skull like people at a window watching a fire.

"Hear that?" Marr said reverently. He had chained Paloma to a pillar of the same white stone, close enough to watch. His soldiers knelt in a ring, faces blank, and Paloma understood with a lurch that these were not volunteers. These were men whose fear had been taken out of them, the way Paloma took memories out of travelers. "That's the first note. The note before all others. The Choir stole it and built a graveyard over it and called the graveyard home." He unrolled the map across a slab of stone before the door. It was bloodstained, and the bloodstains moved — slow constellations crawling across the parchment, rearranging themselves into instructions no living hand had drawn.

"You'll die too," Paloma said. "When it opens. Whatever comes out won't care about your speech."

"Oh, I don't intend to open it." Marr drew a knife of black glass. "I intend to make *you* open it. The map needs a keeper's hand — that's what the courier was, that's what the road chose. And you need motivation." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing warm left in it at all. "So let's discuss what happens to the town of Grayhollow if you refuse."

He set the knife-tip against the map's center seam.

And the dark answered him.

Serah Vane came out of the fog like a verdict. Paloma never saw her arrive — only heard the wet sound of her blade leaving the throat of the nearest soldier, and then she was there, white cantor's robes gone grey with tunnel dust, her voice raised in something that was absolutely not a hymn. The Choir's song, but broken, inverted, sung *against* itself, and where it touched Marr's soldiers they crumpled like puppets with cut strings.

"*Traitor!*" Marr roared, wheeling.

"*Student,*" Serah said, and struck at him.

What followed was not a duel; it was a demolition. Marr fought with the well's pressure behind him, each blow landing with the weight of a collapsing roof, and Serah fought with everything the Choir had taught her and hated herself for using. Paloma strained against the chains, useless, watching the two of them carve trenches in the stone, watching Marr back her step by step toward the map —

— watching his black knife fall toward the seam.

Paloma did the only thing they knew how to do.

They *took*.

Not with hands. With the thief's gift, the thing they'd done ten thousand times in Grayhollow's market fog — but aimed inward, at the map, at the bloodstained constellation crawling across it. They seized the memory written in the courier's blood, the knowledge of the opening, the sequence, the *song* of the lock — and pulled it out of the parchment and into their own skull, where it burned like swallowed lightning.

The knife came down on a map that was suddenly, beautifully, only paper. Blank. The bloodstains were gone. They lived behind Paloma's eyes now.

Marr stared at the empty parchment, and for one heartbeat his burning eyes went wide with something very much like fear. "What did you—"

Serah's blade took him through the chest. He fell against the door of white stone, and the ten thousand carved names flared once, briefly, as if tasting him — and then he was ash on the floor, and the pressure in the cavern eased by a fraction, and by no more than a fraction.

Paloma slid down the pillar, shaking. Serah crossed the stone to them, bleeding from a dozen places, and looked at them with an expression Paloma had never seen on a cantor's face: awe, and terror, in equal measure.

"You hold it now," she whispered. "The opening. The whole song of the lock. If Marr's people find you—"

"They can't take it out of me," Paloma said. Then, understanding arriving cold and complete, like water down the back of the collar: "*But I can't put it back.*"

The door hummed. Somewhere in its depths, patient as geology, the first note waited — thinner than it had been a century ago, thinner than it had been yesterday, hungry, and listening.

And Paloma Brightwater, thief of things that could not be carried, realized the seal had never needed a warrior or a king or a choir of ten thousand voices.

It needed a keeper who could hold what others lose.

The names on the door began, very softly, to sing — and every one of them sounded like Paloma's own name, which they were beginning to suspect they would not keep much longer.
