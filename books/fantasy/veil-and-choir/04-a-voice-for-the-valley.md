## 4. A Voice for the Valley

The mountain's roots were quiet now. Kestrel Marr lay where the collapsing ward-stone had crushed him, his war-axe half-buried in rubble, his face — even in death — still hungry, still reaching toward the light that leaked from the fissure like water through a cracked dam. Serah Vane knelt a few paces away, her cantor's robes torn to ribbons, one hand pressed hard against her side where something had opened her skin. She was breathing. That was enough.

Paloma stood before the seal.

It was not what they had imagined. No great door, no altar, no chains of gold and iron. It was a *song* — or had been, once. Paloma could see its shape now with the strange new sight the map had burned into them: a lattice of woven sound, ancient beyond reckoning, stretched across a wound in the world. And through the gaps in that lattice, magic itself bled out into the valley above, thinning year by year, decade by decade, until the fog at the borders grew heavy and dead and no spell could live there.

That was the truth of Grayhollow. Not a curse. A cage — and the cage was failing because the singers who built it were dust, and their song had been dying for three hundred years without a voice to hold it up.

"The binding needs a keeper," Serah said hoarsely. "Not just a singer. The Choir sang it in relays, generation after generation. There is no relay anymore." Her laugh was bitter and small. "There is only you."

Paloma looked down at their hands. A thief's hands, calloused and quick. For twenty years those hands had lifted memories from travelers — first loves, dead mothers, the taste of foreign rain — and sold them back at a fair price, or an unfair one when rent was due. They had told themselves it was honest work. Nothing in the Veil was stolen outright; everything was borrowed, held, returned.

But some things they had never returned. Some things had stayed curled inside them like coins in a hidden purse: the memory of a wedding on a hillside in a country whose name Paloma had never learned. A child's funeral, sung in a language of bells. The sight of the sea. Dozens of small eternities, kept because keeping them was the only wealth a thief could carry that couldn't be taken.

And beneath all of those, older than any theft: their own life. Their mother's kitchen smelling of burnt honey. The first time they'd slipped a stranger's memory loose and felt it settle into them like a swallowed stone. Their name — *Paloma Brightwater*, given by a father whose face they suddenly realized they could not picture, because he had left before the remembering started, and the mother was gone too, gone eleven years now, and all that remained of her lived in Paloma's skull alone.

"If I sing it," Paloma said slowly, "what happens to what I pour in?"

Serah was silent a long moment. "Gone," she said finally. "Woven into the ward. Held forever, but not by you. You'll wake with holes where those years were." Her eyes, gray as the fog, did not flinch. "I won't lie to you, thief. I've watched cantors give pieces of themselves to lesser wards. What remains afterward is... thinner. But it remains."

Paloma thought about the valley. About old Marta at the bakery who hummed off-key and gave away the burnt loaves. About the children who ran the fog-line on dares, believing the mist was the edge of the world. About every traveler who had ever walked into their shop frightened and hollow and walked out whole again, weeping over a returned memory of someone long dead.

They had spent their whole life holding what others lost. Perhaps that was never thievery at all. Perhaps it had been training.

"Then I'll need you to remember me," Paloma said. "After. Someone has to."

"I will," Serah said. "On my order's grave, I will."

And Paloma began to sing.

They had no training. They had no cantor's discipline, no choir's harmony. What they had was every stolen thing — and when the first note left their throat, all of it rose at once, eager, like birds released. The lattice heard them. The lattice *answered*. Paloma felt the seal reach into them not as violence but as invitation, the way a river accepts rain, and they gave, and gave, and gave.

The wedding on the hillside went first — the bride's laughter, the smell of rosemary, the groom crying without shame. Then the sea, vast and blue and terrible. The funeral of bells. The taste of a peach eaten in summer by someone who had died two winters later, and who had loved that peach so much that the memory of it had been worth more to him than his house. Each memory unspooled from Paloma and became thread, and the threads wove themselves into the failing lattice, brightening it, thickening it, singing in voices that were not Paloma's but belonged to the seal now, forever.

And then the deeper vaults opened. Their mother's kitchen. Burnt honey. Her hands, flour-dusted, shaping dough while she sang nonsense songs. Her face — oh, her face, Paloma clung to it, tried to sing around it, tried to keep it — but the seal was gentle and it was not negotiable, and the last thing Paloma knew of her mother was gratitude, and then that too flowed out of them and into the ward, and became part of the wall that would keep the world's magic caged and the valley's people safe for another age.

Their name went last. *Paloma Brightwater.* They sang it themselves, deliberately, offering it like a coin placed flat on a counter. It left them the way a breath leaves in winter — visible, warm, and then gone.

The lattice closed. The fissure sealed. In the sudden silence, the mountain hummed like a struck bell fading, and somewhere far above, Paloma would have sworn the fog itself sighed.

Then the dark took them.

* * *

They woke to sunlight through a dusty window and the smell of bread.

Grayhollow. Their own narrow bed above the shop, their own crooked ceiling, the water stain shaped like a leaping fish that they had — that someone had — once told customers was good luck. Paloma lay still and took inventory the way they took inventory after any job. Hands: present. Body: bruised, exhausted, alive. Mind—

Mind full of holes.

Not painful ones. Strange ones. They reached for their childhood and found a smooth blank wall, beautiful and featureless, like fog. They reached for their name and found — nothing. A shape where a name should be, worn smooth as a river stone. They reached further back and found other absences, dozens of them, each one aching faintly with the ghost-shape of something wonderful that had been given away.

They sat up too fast and the room tilted. A cup of water was pressed into their hands before they could spill anything.

"Slowly." Serah Vane sat in the chair by the door, still in her ruined robes, a fresh bandage at her ribs, a book open facedown on her knee. She looked as though she hadn't slept in days. Probably she hadn't. "You've been out for four days. The town thinks you fell down your own stairs. I saw no reason to correct them."

"Who am I?" Paloma asked. It came out steadier than they expected. "Do you know?"

"You're the keeper of the seal," Serah said. "And you're the person who saved everyone in this valley, most of whom will never know it. As for a name—" she hesitated, and something almost soft moved through her cantor's composure, "—you get to choose one. That's rarer than you think. Most people are named by people who don't ask."

Paloma drank the water. Outside, Grayhollow went about its morning: cart wheels, gulls, Marta's off-key humming drifting up from the bakery, muffled by distance. The fog stood at the borders, patient and deep, and behind it — though no one in town would ever feel it — the valley hummed faintly, held, safe, sustained by weddings and peaches and burnt honey and a name freely given.

"Thieves keep ledgers," Paloma said at last. "Every item taken, every item returned. Mine burned with the shop next door when Marr's men came through." They looked at their hands, empty now in a way hands had never been empty before. "So I'll start a new ledger. Everything I don't remember, written down as I find the edges of it. And everything I choose going forward, written down so it can't be taken twice."

Serah raised an eyebrow. "A memoir, then."

"A map," Paloma said. "Of a country with one citizen." They swung their legs off the bed, tested their weight, found it held. "Starting today. First entry: woke up. Sunlight. Bread. A woman with gray eyes who kept her promise."

Below, the shop bell rang — a customer, early, probably a traveler with something lost and something to trade. Paloma Brightwater — no. Paloma *nothing-yet*, Paloma *blank page*, went to find clothes that fit the person they were becoming.

At the top of the stairs they paused, one hand on the rail, and listened. Beneath the ordinary noise of the town, so faint it might have been imagination, the valley was singing.

They smiled, and went down to answer the bell.
