## 1. The Taster's Tongue

The pears were wrong.

Jonah knew it before the spoon touched her tongue — knew it the way a musician knows a string gone flat, from the smell alone. The honey had been warmed twice, which was against kitchen custom but not against any law, and beneath the sweetness of it there sat something else, something thin and green and patient, like a snake coiled in the bottom of a well.

She stood at the sideboard in the king's breakfast room with the silver tasting spoon in her hand and the morning light coming gray through leaded glass, and she let herself have three full seconds of being ordinary. Three seconds in which she was only a tired woman in an apron, hungry for her own breakfast, resentful of the cold stone under her feet. Then she put the spoon in her mouth.

Honey. Pear. The ghost of clove from the syrup they steeped them in. And there — at the very back of the tongue, so faint that anyone else would have called it imagination — bitter almond.

Her body did what her body always did, which was nothing. No tightening of the throat, no prickle of warning, no heat. The poison went into her the way rain goes into a river, and the river did not care. That was the gift, though no one in the Veiled Valley would ever have used that word aloud. *Gift.* In the valley you said *a good constitution* or *strong blood* or, if you were old Wenna and it was very late and the fire was low, you said nothing at all and looked at you too long.

Jonah set down the spoon with a small deliberate click.

"Steward Aldric," she said, and her voice came out level, which surprised her, because underneath the levelness her heart had begun to run like a rabbit. "Don't serve the pears."

Aldric Vane was arranging the king's napkin with the concentration of a man defusing something. He was forty, maybe forty-five, with the kind of face that made people trust him without knowing why — open, weathered, honest as a fencepost. He had been steward to the royal household at Caelmere for eleven years, back when there had still been a royal household worth the name, and he had kept the post through the dissolution the way a man keeps a lamp lit in a house everyone else has abandoned: out of habit, out of stubbornness, out of love for a thing that no longer officially existed.

He looked up. "The pears are His Majesty's favorite."

"The pears are poisoned."

The word dropped between them like a coin into deep water. Jonah watched it fall, watched his face go through the stages — disbelief, then the quick arithmetic of a man deciding whether the kitchen girl is mad or lying, then, because Aldric had survived eleven years of court by believing his own eyes over his own comfort, belief.

"You're certain." Not a question. A request for permission to act.

"I'm certain." She turned the sideboard toward him so he could see the dish. "Bitter almond. It's masked under the honey — someone who knew the syrup would know where to hide it. If His Majesty eats two bites he'll be ill. If he eats the whole dish—" She didn't finish. Finishing things gave them weight.

Aldric moved fast after that, and Jonah watched him move with the particular admiration she reserved for competent people. He swept the dish away under a cloth, sent a boy running for the physician with instructions vague enough to be deniable, ordered the entire breakfast service remade from stores that had never left the locked pantry, and did all of it without raising his voice, because a raised voice in a palace travels faster than poison.

By the time the king came down — not the king, Jonah corrected herself, the *old* king, the last king, the man the new crown in the east still called a rebel when it bothered to call him anything — the table bore fresh bread, butter, cold ham, a new dish of pears tasted twice by Jonah and found clean, and tea brewed from leaves she had personally smelled and stirred and sipped.

King Edmun the Third — though the charters in the eastern capital listed no such person — was seventy-one years old and ate like a man who expected every meal to be his last and intended to enjoy the expectation. He ate the ham. He ate the bread. He ate the pears with evident pleasure, and Jonah stood against the wall with her hands folded and felt the strange double pulse of her life beat on: pride in one ear, terror in the other.

Because here was the thing about the Veiled Valley, the thing every child learned before they learned their letters: magic could not follow you in. The boundary stones ringed the valley like a closed fist, and whatever ran wild and bright through the rest of old Cael — the hedge-craft, the hearth-spells, the gift of tongues and the gift of finding and the gift, rarest and most cursed, of a tongue that knew what it tasted — all of it went quiet at the stones. Inside the veil, a gifted woman was only a woman. Her gift slept like a limb gone numb, heavy and distant and hers only in memory.

Which meant that if anyone ever asked how the taster had known about the almonds, she had exactly one answer available, and it was a lie, and she was going to have to be very good at it.

* * *

The king finished his tea and said, as he said every morning, "Still alive, Thornbury?" and Jonah said, as she said every morning, "Seems so, Your Majesty," and the old man laughed his dry leaf-rattle laugh and shuffled off to his study to read dispatches from a country that insisted he was dead.

She washed dishes. She scoured the copper. She took stock of the spice cabinet and noted that the cinnamon was running low and that someone — Marta, almost certainly, Marta with her sweet tooth and her total absence of shame — had been into the candied ginger again. Ordinary work. Ordinary hands. She watched her own hands move and thought about how strange it was that they looked the same as everyone else's.

It was nearly noon when Aldric found her.

He came into the kitchen the way he always did, sidelong, apologetic, as though his own authority embarrassed him. But today he didn't drift past toward the wine cellar. Today he stopped beside the sink where she stood, picked up a drying cloth, and began drying dishes next to her, which he had never once done in four years.

"Physician says it was amygdalin," he said quietly. "Enough in the dish to stop an ox."

"Mm."

"Guards are questioning the fruit merchants. The gate logs are being pulled. Whoever did it bought the almonds somewhere, carried them through some gate, and someone will remember." He turned a plate over in his big careful hands. "You saved the king's life this morning, Jonah."

"I did my job."

"You did more than your job. You caught what the physician himself said he'd never have caught — not in the dish, not under honey, not before it was too late." He set the plate down. He wasn't looking at the plate anymore. He was looking at her, and his honest fencepost face had something new built into it, something she liked very much less than trust. "How did you know?"

The water ran hot over Jonah's wrists. She kept her eyes on the pot she was scrubbing and gave herself the luxury of one slow breath.

There were answers she could not give. *I am gifted. My tongue knows poison the way your tongue knows salt. I am immune to everything that has ever been brewed against me, and I only survive because the valley's stones smother what I am, and if the wrong person hears the right words, the stones will not save me from men.* None of that could be said. Some of it could barely be thought.

But there were other answers. There were always other answers; her mentor Maren had taught her that before she'd taught her anything else. *A lie isn't a wall, girl, it's a door painted on a wall. Make it a door someone wants to walk through.*

"I've been tasting for the crown since I was nine years old," Jonah said. She let a little weariness into it, the weariness of a woman stating a plain fact. "Twenty years. You drink enough bad wine and smell enough spoiled meat, you learn what *wrong* smells like. This morning the pears smelled wrong. That's all."

"That's all."

"That's all."

Aldric dried another plate. Set it down. Picked up a bowl. The kitchen sounds filled the silence around them — the boy chopping onions, Marta singing badly at the far end, the kettle beginning its low complaint.

"My grandmother," Aldric said at last, "could tell when a storm was coming three days out. Said she felt it in her teeth. Everyone in her village said the same about her, and everyone smiled when they said it, that particular smile." He looked at her sideways. "That particular smile, Jonah, is the smile I'm trying very hard not to make right now."

Jonah's stomach went cold and stayed cold. But her hands kept moving, because her hands had been trained by a dead woman to keep moving no matter what, and she heard herself say, lightly, "Then don't make it. Make yourself useful instead — that stack won't dry itself."

And Aldric laughed — a short, startled laugh, half against his will — and for a moment the thing between them loosened. He finished the stack. He set the cloth down neatly, squared its corners the way he squared everything. At the door he paused.

"Whatever it is," he said, not turning around, "whatever the truth of this morning is — I owe you my king, and kings are scarce these days. So I'll ask once, and then I'll never ask again. Are you safe? Are *we* safe, with you in this kitchen?"

The question hung in the steam and the onion-smell and the ordinary noise of an ordinary room, and Jonah understood with terrible clarity that her whole life now balanced on the answer — that Aldric was offering her a door, and that behind the door was either friendship or a report, and that she could not see which, and that he probably could not either.

"I'm just a woman with a good nose," she said, "who loves this valley and wants to grow old in it."

Aldric nodded slowly. "Good," he said. "That's what I'll write in the log, then. Nothing. Because there's nothing to write." And he left, and his footsteps faded down the corridor, and Jonah stood with her hands in the cooling water and discovered that she was shaking.

* * *

She walked home along the millstream as the light went long and gold, past the bakehouse and the cooper's yard and the shrine where somebody had left winter apples for a saint whose name the valley had also forgotten. The Veiled Valley in autumn was beautiful the way a held breath is beautiful. Smoke rising straight from chimneys. Geese going over. The boundary stones somewhere north, gray and patient, doing their gray patient work.

*The valley forgets you if you let it,* her mother used to say, back when her mother was alive and saying things that sounded like nonsense and later turned out to be maps. *That's its mercy and its trap both, little wren. It forgets what you are. But if you're not careful, it forgets you entirely.*

Jonah had spent twenty years being carefully forgotten. She had a small house with a leaky roof she kept meaning to fix. She had a garden. She had a neighbor who brought her eggs and expected nothing but gossip in return. She had a routine worn smooth as river stone, and inside the smoothness she had hidden the one irregular thing about herself so deeply that some mornings even she couldn't find it.

This morning she had found it. This morning it had saved a king's life, and the saving had a sound like a crack, hairline, running through the smooth stone of her days.

At her gate she stopped. Old Wenna was sitting on her wall, which was a thing Wenna did sometimes, uninvited, the way cats do, and Wenna's face in the slanting light was doing something Jonah had never seen it do.

Wenna looked afraid.

"There's criers come to market," the old woman said, before Jonah could even ask. "Rode in at midday under the new crown's seal. They'll read it proper tomorrow, but I heard it early, and I thought—" She stopped. Started again. "I thought you'd want to hear it from a friend first, girl. They've passed a law. About folk like—"

She caught herself. Looked around at the empty lane, the golden air, the smoke rising straight.

"About folk," she said, "with gifts."

Far off, beyond the northern ridge, the boundary stones stood in their ancient circle, keeping the magic out, keeping Jonah safe, keeping her — as her mother might have said — in a cage built exactly to the size of her secret.

And tomorrow, Jonah understood, the cage was going to start feeling very small indeed.
