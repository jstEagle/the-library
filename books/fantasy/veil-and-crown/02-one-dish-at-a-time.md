## 2. One Dish at a Time

The pears had been the first thing she'd ever learned to fear, and now they were the thing she loved best.

Jonah stood at the basin in the palace undercroft, rinsing her hands in water that smelled of iron and old stone, and let herself remember — because remembering was a kind of prayer, and because Master Corvin had told her once that a taster who forgot how she learned would forget why she lived.

*You were nine,* he had said. *You'll remember it as kindness. That's all right. It was.*

* * *

The kitchen of the Veiled Valley's royal residence was not like other kitchens. Jonah had understood that even at nine, though she hadn't understood why. Other children spoke of kitchens with smoke and shouting and the sweet violence of bread; hers was quiet as a chapel, its floors scrubbed pale, its copper pots hung in ranks like soldiers at rest. The cooks moved without hurry. The knives were sharpened on a schedule. And in the corner nearest the cold hearth stood a small table with one chair, and at that table sat an old man with white brows and hands steady as poured lead, tasting everything twice.

His name was Corvin, and he was the only person in the valley who held his post for life, because no one else could hold it at all.

Her mother brought her there on a morning in late autumn, when the fog off the valley walls lay so thick it seemed the mountains had been erased. Jonah remembered the walk — remembered clutching her mother's hand and counting her own breaths, because her mother had taught her that counting was a way to be brave. She remembered the smell of the kitchen: rosemary, rendered fat, something bitter underneath it all that she could not name and would spend years learning to name.

She remembered her mother crouching down to her eye level, smoothing back her hair with both hands, and saying the words that would follow Jonah for the rest of her life like a second shadow.

"The valley forgets you if you let it," her mother said. "So let it, little bird. Let it forget your name, your face, the color of your eyes. Let it forget everything about you except what you can do. What you can do is safe. Everything else is a door left open."

"I don't want to be forgotten," Jonah had said, because she was nine and honest.

"No," her mother agreed, and her voice did something complicated — grief folded inside love folded inside a decision already made. "But being forgotten is a kind of armor. Wear it anyway."

Then she kissed Jonah's forehead, stood, spoke briefly and quietly to the old man at the small table, and walked out through the fog. Jonah watched her go until the gray took her. Her mother came to visit — of course she came, every market day at first, then less, then only at festivals, then never — but in memory that first departure was the true one, the one that mattered, the moment when Jonah Thornbury stopped belonging to anyone and began belonging to a chair in a corner of a kitchen.

Corvin gave her a spoon.

It was plain tin, dented at the bowl, worn smooth at the handle where forty years of fingers had polished it. "This is yours now," he said. "Not mine. Yours. A taster owns three things: her spoon, her tongue, and her silence. We'll work on them in order."

* * *

The dosing began in the second month.

She understood later — much later, when she was old enough to understand anything — that this was the whole architecture of her apprenticeship: not recipes, not etiquette, not the elaborate choreography of the king's table, but poison, administered in increments so small they were almost courtesy. Bitter almond hidden in honeyed wine. Foxglove steeped until it was barely a rumor in the broth. Hemlock in quantities that would have felled a grown man, diluted into a child's cup of milk.

They called it building tolerance. Corvin called it nothing at all. He simply set the spoon before her each morning and watched her eat, and his face never changed, and afterward he sat with her while her stomach cramped or her vision swam or her tongue went numb, holding a bucket she rarely needed and telling her stories about the valley's history in a low, unhurried voice, as if the stories themselves were medicine.

"Tell me again about the boundary stones," she would say, sweating through a foxglove morning, and he would tell her. The stones had been raised after the Dragon War, by survivors who wanted one place in Cael where magic could not reach. The veil ran from stone to stone around the whole valley floor, a ring of quiet. Inside it, the gifted felt nothing. Their gifts went silent as a bell wrapped in wool. Outside it—

"Outside is outside," Corvin said, and would say no more.

"Why does my mouth know things?" she asked him once, age eleven, after she had tasted arsenic in a soup meant for the chamberlain and saved the man's life with a shriek that got half the kitchen scalded with thrown soup. "Why do I taste what isn't there?"

"You taste what *is* there," Corvin said. "That's the trouble. Most people taste dinner. You taste truth." He looked at her for a long moment over the rim of his cup. "And here's the second lesson, girl, and mind it well: a taster who asks questions becomes a question herself. The crown keeps us because we're useful and quiet. Be useful. Be quiet. The day you ask the wrong question out loud is the day you stop being a tool and start being a threat, and the crown has a great many tools for dealing with threats."

"What tools?"

He almost smiled. "There. You just asked one."

* * *

She learned the silences the way other children learned their letters.

Silence when a lord's dish arrived already smelling faintly wrong, and she tasted it, and said nothing, because the wrongness was deliberate — a rival's message, a warning, a thing between great houses that had nothing to do with a girl at a tin spoon. Silence when Corvin himself winced at a vintage and waved the cup away with a critic's frown rather than a taster's alarm, because some poisons came from friends and were meant to be survived. Silence, above all, about herself. About the fact that her tolerance was not entirely built. About the mornings, rare and terrible, when she tasted something in her own skin — a brightness at the edge of her tongue, a sense that the world had flavors it wasn't showing anyone else.

Once, aged thirteen, she caught herself knowing the exact moment the milk had turned, three days before it turned. Once, aged fifteen, she had stood over a pot of stew and known, without reason, without evidence, that the cook had wept into it, and had tasted salt that wasn't there, and had said nothing, and had carried that salt on her tongue for a week.

She never told Corvin about those moments. But she thought, sometimes, watching him watch her, that he knew anyway. That he had always known. That the whole slow ritual of the spoon — the doses, the stories, the patience — had been built not only to protect the king from poison but to protect a gifted child from a kingdom that burned such children, and that Corvin had spent forty years standing between one small girl and the fire, armed with nothing but a dented spoon and a talent for silence.

The last time she saw him alive, he was teaching her the pears.

"Honeyed pears for the king's breakfast," he said. "Simple dish. Dangerous dish. Everyone thinks poison comes in the exotic things — the sauces, the wines, the spices from beyond the veil. Amateurs hide poison in complexity. Professionals hide it in simplicity, because simple dishes get tasted carelessly. A pear is trusted. Trust is the best vehicle ever devised for bitter almond." He cut a slice and offered it to her on the flat of the knife, an old habit, intimate as communion. "Taste."

She tasted. Pear, honey, a thread of lemon, the ghost of the orchard's late summer.

"And?"

"And nothing," she said. "It's clean."

"Good." He ate the slice himself, which he almost never did — the master taster eating from the apprentice's confirmed-safe plate, a gesture of trust so old neither of them had ever remarked on it. "Tomorrow you taste alone. I'm old, Jonah. My tongue is going soft. It's time."

"It's not time," she said, and heard the childishness in her own voice, and hated it.

"Everything is time," Corvin said. "Time is the one poison nobody's built tolerance to." He looked at her then with an expression she had never learned to read and had never stopped trying to. "You were the best thing I never meant to happen. Do you understand? I took an apprentice because the crown required it. I kept you because—"

He didn't finish. He never finished. He died that winter, quietly, in his sleep, in the small room above the kitchen, and the cooks found him with his hands folded and his face composed, a man who had done his work and put his tools away clean.

Jonah tasted the funeral meal. Of course she did. Someone had to. She stood at the small table in the corner with her dented spoon and confirmed the bread, the broth, the wine, and pronounced them all safe, and her voice did not shake, because she was a professional, and professionals hide their grief in simplicity.

* * *

Now, four years later, she dried her hands and climbed the stairs toward the morning's service, and the grief sat in her chest the way it always sat — unnamed, unopened, a door she kept locked on principle. Corvin had taught her never to ask questions. He had neglected to teach her how to stop asking the biggest one, which was: *what am I, and what happens to me when the valley stops forgetting?*

The kitchen was already loud with morning. Cooks shouted, pots clashed, the day's first bread came out of the ovens in a tide of warmth. Jonah took up her station at the small table in the corner — her table now, her chair, her spoon — and began.

Bread: clean. Butter: clean. Smoked trout: clean, though the brine was heavy-handed and she made a mental note to speak to the fishmonger. Honeyed pears, fresh from the pan, glistening:

She paused.

There it was. Faint as a whisper under a hymn. Bitter almond, threaded through the honey, invisible to any tongue in the kingdom but hers.

Four years of training held her still. She finished the bite. She set down the spoon. She thought of Corvin, and of her mother in the fog, and of the boundary stones she had never seen, and of the word *outside*, which Corvin had never finished defining.

Then she stood, and carried the dish toward the king's table, and began composing in her head the lie that would save a steward's life without spending her own — because somewhere below the dread, calm as deep water, the oldest lesson of all was already speaking:

*A taster owns three things. Her spoon, her tongue, and her silence.*

Today, silence would have to wait its turn.
