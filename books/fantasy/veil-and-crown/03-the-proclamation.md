## 3. The Proclamation

The morning the criers came, Jonah was salting a pot of stock and thinking about nothing at all, which was her favorite way to think.

She had learned early that the mind was like a kitchen garden: leave it untended and it grew whatever it pleased — weeds of worry, thorns of memory. So she kept hers pruned. Salt in the pot. Fire under the pot. The smell of marrow bones and leek tops rising through the palace kitchens like a benediction. There was a whole liturgy to a working morning, and if you performed it faithfully, the day had no room left for anything else.

"Thornbury." The head cook, Marta, jerked her chin toward the window that faced the market square. "They're calling something. Go see what the crown wants from us now."

Jonah wiped her hands on her apron and went, because it was easier than arguing, and because some part of her — the part she kept locked in the cellar with the other dangerous things — had already begun to listen.

* * *

The Veiled Valley woke slowly on market days, the way old people wake: joints cracking, lamps guttering out one by one as the sun took over their work. By midmorning the square was full — farmers' wives haggling over eggs, the fishmonger shouting down the price of river trout because the catch had been too good, children weaving between cart wheels with the shameless speed of small animals. A man sold roasted chestnuts near the well. Two women argued cheerfully about whether the new baker's loaves were honest bread or clever fraud.

Jonah stood at the edge of it all with a basket on her arm she'd filled mostly for appearances, and felt the ordinary morning the way you feel a coat: warm, worn soft at the cuffs, yours.

Then the bells began.

Not the chapel bells, which rang the hours in a pattern every soul in the valley could recite in their sleep. These came from the gatehouse — the crown's bells, installed two winters past when the new administration decided even a valley that magic could not enter deserved the crown's punctuality. They rang a sequence Jonah didn't know, and neither did anyone else, and that was the first wrongness: the sound itself was foreign, an accent from another country speaking into theirs.

The square did not go quiet all at once. It went quiet the way frost comes across a pond — from the edges inward, silence spreading along the ground until it reached the center and found nowhere left to go. The fishmonger stopped mid-shout. The chestnut seller set down his pan. The arguing women fell silent without resolving anything, which Jonah would remember later as the truest sign of how deep the wrongness went: the valley's arguments never died unfinished.

Three riders in crown livery — grey and gold, the colors of the Regency Council — walked their horses to the steps of the old tollhouse. Behind them, a clerk unrolled a scroll with the ceremony of a man who had done this forty times and been ignored forty times, except that this time nobody looked away.

"Citizens of the Veiled Valley," the clerk read, in the flat carrying voice of officialdom, "by decree of the Regency Council, acting in the name of the Crown of Cael—"

*Cael,* Jonah thought. *There hasn't been a Cael in twenty years.* But the words went on, gathering weight.

"—the Gift Registry Act is hereby proclaimed throughout all territories and holdings of the former kingdom. All persons possessed of the gift — being any unnatural sensitivity or power of body, sense, or spirit — shall present themselves before the appointed officers within thirty days, to be entered upon the rolls of the realm, examined, and assessed."

A pause, professionally measured. The clerk let the next sentence stand alone.

"Those who fail to present themselves shall be deemed concealed gifted persons, and hunted accordingly."

Jonah heard the words. She understood each one individually; she had read enough proclamations nailed to the tollhouse door to parse the grammar of power. But the sentence refused to assemble into meaning, the way a familiar room refuses to assemble when you wake in the dark. *Present themselves. Examined. Assessed.* Words for livestock. Words for plague.

Beside her, a farmer's wife said, very quietly, "Assessed for what?"

Nobody answered. Nobody asked again. That was the second wrongness: the valley always asked. The valley's whole character was the question — *how much, why, says who?* — shouted cheerfully across market stalls for generations. Now the questions stayed in people's mouths like stones they were afraid to swallow or spit out.

The clerk read on. Registration would be conducted at the gatehouse. Officers of the Inquisition would assist local authorities in identifying the gifted. Concealment was a crime against the realm. Harboring was a crime against the realm. The penalties section was read in the same voice as everything else, which somehow made it worse — *seizure of property, imprisonment, and such further measures as the Council shall determine* — a sentence with a hole in the middle where the burning went.

When it was done, the riders turned their horses and clattered out through the gate, and the clerk rolled up his scroll, and the square stayed silent a moment longer — one breath, two — and then the noise came back, but wrong. Too loud. Too bright. Laughter with cracks in it. The fishmonger shouting his price again but forgetting, halfway through, what the price was.

Jonah stood among them holding her basket of apples she had no intention of eating, and understood, with the particular clarity that dread provides, that the weather had changed.

Dread arrived in her the way real weather does — not as a thought but as a pressure, a heaviness behind the eyes, a taste at the back of the throat. Copper, she thought. Old blood. Her gift, waking in the suppressed hush of the valley just enough to tell her what her mind already knew: *something in the air has turned.*

* * *

She made herself walk home the long way, past the boundary stone.

It sat at the crook of the mill road, a grey finger of granite older than any kingdom, carved with symbols so worn they might have been plow marks or might have been prayers. Every child in the valley learned its name before they learned their own: the Veilstone. Beyond it, the world. Within it, safety — of a kind. Magic could not follow a person inside the valley's bounds. Whatever gift burned in your blood went quiet here, banked like a fire under ash. Jonah had lived her whole life inside that hush. It was the reason she was alive. It was the reason she had survived nine years of royal kitchens, tasting dishes dosed by men who wanted kings dead, immune to poisons that killed three tasters before her — immune not by luck, never say luck, but by a gift the valley smothered so thoroughly that even she could almost pretend it wasn't there.

Almost.

The protection ran only to the stone, Wenna had told her once, years ago, in the matter-of-fact voice she used for delivering babies and bad news alike. *The veil doesn't love you, girl. It loves the ground. Stand inside it and it holds you. Step past it and you're on your own.*

Registration meant leaving. That was the arithmetic, simple and brutal. Present yourself at the gatehouse, be entered on the rolls, be *examined* — and examination meant testing, and testing meant the crown's officers would want to see the gift work, and the gift did not work inside the valley, and a taster who couldn't demonstrate what made her gifted was either lying about having it or hiding it, and both roads led to the same place. Or don't present yourself, and become one of the *concealed*, and wait for the knock at the door.

Either way, the valley stopped being a home and became a cage whose bars she could finally see.

She pressed her palm flat against the Veilstone. Cold granite, cold as ever, indifferent as ever. She had touched it a thousand times without thinking, the way you touch a doorframe going past. Today it felt like the flank of a great animal sleeping — vast, protective, and utterly without opinion about what happened to the things sheltering against it.

"You knew," she said aloud, to the stone, to the valley, to whoever kept such accounts. "You always knew this day would come."

The stone said nothing. The valley said nothing. Somewhere behind her, faintly, the market noise resumed its broken rhythm, and Jonah Thornbury stood at the border of the only safe country she had ever known and felt, for the first time in her life, how narrow it was.

* * *

That evening the kitchens were quiet in the way of rooms where everyone is listening.

Marta salted nothing. She stood at the big table with her arms folded, watching the apprentices chop vegetables with unnecessary force, and when Jonah came in she jerked her head toward the back step, and they went out together into the yard where the slop buckets stood and the eaves kept the wind off.

"Well," Marta said.

"Well," Jonah agreed.

Marta was sixty and built like the door of a strongroom, and she had run the palace kitchens through two changes of administration without losing a single member of her staff to the purges that had come and gone in the lowlands. She had done it by knowing exactly which questions not to ask. Jonah had watched her do it for eleven years with the admiration of a student who understands she is studying survival itself.

"They'll come here eventually," Marta said. "Clerks first. Then the ones with the grey sashes. They always send clerks first — soften the ground." She picked a bit of straw off her sleeve. "Kitchen staff get interviewed. Names, families, habits. Who drinks. Who keeps odd hours. Who's *different*."

"I'm different," Jonah said. "I'm the taster."

"You're the best taster I've ever had, and I've had four." Marta's eyes were steady and unreadable. "Best I've ever heard of, truth told. And I've wondered, these eleven years, the way you wonder about a thing you've decided not to wonder about."

The yard was very still. Somewhere beyond the wall, the town murmured — doors closing, voices low, the sound of a community pulling its shutters in.

"Marta—"

"Don't." The cook held up one broad hand. "I don't want to know. Understand me, girl? The day I know, I'm a witness, and witnesses get questioned, and questioned people answer. As long as I don't know, you're just my taster, and my taster saved Aldric's life last spring, and there's not a soul in this household who'd say otherwise." She said it fast, in the tone of a woman nailing a door shut. "But hear me. If they take you, I never saw anything. If you run, I'll grieve you proper and mean it. And if you stay—" she paused, and for one moment the strongroom door opened and Jonah saw the fear underneath, old fear, fear that had been waiting thirty years for this weather, "—if you stay, you'd best be certain the valley can keep hiding what it's always hidden. Because it didn't hide the others. Back in my grandmother's day. Ask old Wenna why her family's small."

Jonah's mouth went dry. "Wenna's sister—"

"Was taken. Before your mother brought you through the stones. Before my time, but the story keeps." Marta straightened, and the door closed again, and she was only a cook giving orders. "Stock needs skimming. Go on."

Jonah skimmed the stock. Her hands knew the work and did it while the rest of her stood at the Veilstone in her mind, palm against cold granite, feeling the shape of the cage.

*Thirty days.*

* * *

She dreamed of her mother that night, which hadn't happened in months, and in the dream her mother was standing at the boundary stones with the dawn behind her, saying the thing she had actually said, on the actual morning, eleven years ago:

*"The valley forgets you if you let it, little wren. That's its mercy and its danger both. Forget hard enough and even the poison can't find you. But—" and here in the dream, as in life, her mother had gripped her shoulders hard enough to bruise, "—don't forget what you are. Promise me. Forget it out loud all you like. Just never lose the knowing of it, because the day you need that knowing will come, and it will come fast, and it will not wait for you to remember."*

Jonah woke before dawn with her heart hammering and lay in the dark of her attic room listening to the valley breathe.

Below her window, the town was stirring — earlier than usual, she noticed. Doors opening and closing with purpose. Voices in the street, low and urgent. And threaded through it all, a sound she had never heard in the Veiled Valley in all her born days: the creak and jingle of harness, many horses, moving up the mill road toward the gatehouse.

She went to the window. Grey light. Frost on the rooftops. And below, walking through the streets in twos and threes with lanterns still lit, the first of the valley's people heading not to market, not to the fields, but to the gatehouse — where, she could see even from this distance, a table had already been set up, and a clerk was already unrolling fresh paper, and an officer in a grey sash stood watching the road with the patience of a man who has all the time in the world and intends to use it.

Some of the people walking toward that table walked with their heads high. Some walked like the condemned. One old man carried a birdcage with nothing in it, and Jonah would think about that birdcage for the rest of her life, though she never learned why he carried it or what became of him.

She dressed quickly. She packed nothing — packing was evidence — but she moved through her morning rituals with a new attention, the way a woman handles her own belongings when she has begun, secretly, to say goodbye to them. The iron key Wenna would give her was still three days away from existing in her life. The road east was still only a rumor. Everything that would save her was still invisible, still unmade, waiting in the future like weather on the far horizon.

All she had, this morning, was thirty days minus one, a gift she must never show, and a valley that loved her conditionally, the way a border loves.

Downstairs, the palace was already awake. Marta stood at the head of the kitchen looking grayer than the dawn, and when Jonah appeared in the doorway, the cook said only:

"They're taking names at the gatehouse. Started before light." A pause. "And there's a rider come from the capital. Says the Inquisition's sending someone to interview the palace staff personally." Marta's jaw worked. "Someone with a reputation."

"When?" Jonah asked. Her voice came out steady. She was almost proud of it.

"Tomorrow," said Marta. "Day after, latest."

Outside, the frost was melting off the roofs in the strengthening sun, and the whole valley glittered like something beautiful and doomed, and Jonah tied on her apron and went to work, because the stock would not skim itself, and because a woman who intended to survive had best practice, starting now, looking like a woman with nothing in the world to fear.
