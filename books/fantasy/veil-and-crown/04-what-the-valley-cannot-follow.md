## 4. What the Valley Cannot Follow

The boundary stones did not look like much.

That was the first thing Jonah thought, standing in the wet grass of the eastern march with her skirts soaked to the knee and the morning fog still unraveling off the river. She had lived in the Veiled Valley all twenty-three years of her life and had never once come to see its edges. You did not, any more than you went looking at your own skin. The valley was simply the world, the way bread was simply food, and its borders were the kind of thing you trusted without examining, like a mother's promise.

But here they were: seven gray stones in a crooked line along the riverbank, each no taller than her hip, furred with moss, one of them chipped as if a cart had kissed it carelessly a century ago. They looked like nothing. They looked like the sort of rocks a farmer would curse at when his plow struck them.

"Don't be disappointed," said Wenna, coming up behind her with the particular rolling gait of a woman whose hips had opinions of their own. "Power that shows off gets noticed. Power that hides gets to keep living. There's a lesson in that for people too, though most never learn it."

Wenna was seventy, or thereabouts — she claimed different ages depending on who was asking and what she wanted from them — and she was the closest thing the valley had to a healer who wasn't merely a barber with clean hands. She smelled always of comfrey and woodsmoke and something underneath both, something mineral and old, like the air inside a cave. Jonah had known her since childhood the way everyone knew her: as the woman you were sent to when a fever wouldn't break, when a birth went wrong, when a cow moped by the fence and refused its feed. Wenna fixed things. No one asked how. In the Veiled Valley, not asking how was practically a civic virtue.

Today, though, Wenna had asked *her* to come. Had appeared at the kitchen door two days after the criers, after the square had gone silent as a held breath, and had said only: "Bring boots you don't mind ruining. We're going to walk the edge."

Jonah hadn't needed to ask which edge. The one that mattered.

Now she crouched beside the nearest stone and put her palm flat against it, half expecting something — warmth, a hum, a shiver up the arm. Nothing. Cold granite, damp moss, a beetle making its patient way through the lichen. She felt foolish.

"It's asleep," Wenna said, lowering herself onto a flat rock with the groans of a woman who considered every sitting an achievement. "Or sleeping's the wrong word. It's *listening*. Put your hand there and it listens to you. That's how it knows who belongs."

"Belongs?"

"The veil doesn't keep magic out, girl. Everyone says it does, and everyone's wrong, the way everyone's wrong about most things they say confidently." Wenna pulled a twist of dried apple from her pocket and worried at it with teeth gone brown at the edges. "Magic comes through fine. Magic doesn't know the difference between inside and outside — it's weather, it falls on everything. What the stones do is simpler and crueler. They make the gift *quiet*. A gifted child born inside these lines is born into a house where their own blood has been gagged. The power's there. It just can't speak. Can't reach out, can't flare, can't do anything but sit in you like a swallowed stone." She chewed. "That's why you've never known what you are. That's why none of us ever know, until we leave."

The words landed in Jonah's chest with a weight she hadn't expected. She had known, of course — had known since the steward, since the pears, since the bitter almond blooming on her tongue like a warning written in a language only she could read. But knowing a thing and hearing it said aloud in the open air, with the river muttering past and the fog lifting, were different animals entirely.

"And outside?" she asked. Her voice came out smaller than she wanted.

"Outside, it wakes up." Wenna said it the way you'd say *outside, it rains*. "First hour, maybe less. Colors go loud. Sounds sharpen. And the gift — whatever shape yours takes — starts stretching like a leg that's been folded under you all night. Pins and needles of the soul. Some people scream. Some people laugh and can't stop. One man I heard of, years back, walked out to fetch a stray lamb and came back able to hear what his wife was thinking, and neither of them ever forgave him for it." She snorted. "He moved beyond the stones eventually. Said he'd rather be alone and whole than married and deaf to himself."

Jonah looked down at her hand on the stone. Beneath her palm, she imagined — or almost imagined — a vast patience. Something holding its breath on her behalf.

"How far does it go? The listening."

"That's why we're walking, isn't it." Wenna heaved herself upright. "Come on. Twelve miles today if the mud permits. I want you to know this border better than your own kitchen, because the day's coming when knowing it will matter more than anything else you know."

* * *

They walked the eastern arc through the morning, and Wenna talked, and Jonah learned that the veil was not a wall but a sentence, endlessly repeated.

Every few hundred paces another stone rose from the grass or the heather or the shallow water of a stream — some squat and obvious, some so sunk into the earth that only a crown of granite showed, some disguised deliberately, stacked into field walls or half-buried beneath the roots of an alder. Wenna named them as they passed, the way a shepherd names sheep: the Widow, the Twins, Old Grumble, the Liar (because it had been moved once, in some forgotten dispute, and the veil had bent to follow it rather than leave a gap). There were more than three hundred stones, Wenna said. Maybe more than four hundred. No one had counted in living memory because counting them felt like asking them to justify themselves.

"They were set before the Dragon War," Wenna said, picking her way along a sheep track. "Before Cael was Cael, some say. The first gifted who understood what being gifted meant in a frightened world — they came here and they made a bargain with the land itself. Not with gods. With *stone* and *water* and the slow old minds of hills. Give us a place where our children can grow up unremarkable, they said, and we will tend you, and remember you, and pass the tending down." She shrugged. "So we have. Every hedge-healer in every generation learns the stones, walks the lines, sets right what frost and flood knock over. My grandmother did it. Her grandmother. When my hands give out, someone younger will do it, and if the young ones stop doing it—" she let the sentence hang, unfinished, the way she let many sentences hang, trusting the silence to finish for her.

"What happens then?"

"Then the veil thins. Then a child in Millbrook flares bright enough to scorch her cradle, and word travels, and men with ledgers come." Wenna glanced sideways at her. "You'll notice I say *when*, not *if*. Everything thins, given time. Even mercy."

By midday they had reached the southern march, where the valley floor climbed into low wooded hills and the boundary ran along a ridge with a view outward — and Jonah stopped, because the view was the flaw made visible.

From the ridge she could see the country beyond: a scatter of rooftops, a road, a plume of smoke from some ordinary chimney, fields stitched in green and brown. Ordinary. Reachable. A woman could walk from where she stood to that chimney smoke in under an hour, and nothing in the world would stop her except the stones at her back going quiet behind her.

"Do you see it now?" Wenna said quietly. "The flaw."

"The veil protects what's inside."

"It protects what's inside *and stays* inside." Wenna sat down on a boulder with the finality of a queen taking a throne. "It is not a cage, whatever the frightened call it. You can walk out any hour of any day. Nobody stops you. But the moment you cross, the moment those stones are behind you instead of around you — the quiet ends. Your gift wakes. And everything you are becomes visible to anyone with the wit to look." She tapped her own temple. "And lately, girl, the men with ledgers have very good wits."

Jonah stood at the ridge's lip and felt the truth of it settle into her bones like cold rain. All her life she had thought of the valley as safety, and it was, but it was a specific and conditional safety, the safety of a house whose walls loved you only while you remained within them. Step outside and the walls didn't follow. They couldn't. Their love ended precisely at the last stone, as exact as a knife cut.

A border that loves you conditionally. She turned the phrase over in her mind and found she could not decide whether it was a comfort or an insult. Perhaps both. Perhaps that was what all love was, in the end — a warm room with a door, and the door worked from the inside.

"My mother used to say something like that," Jonah heard herself say. She hadn't planned to. The words came up the way water finds a crack. "*The valley forgets you if you let it.*"

Wenna was quiet a long moment. Somewhere below, a wood pigeon called its idiot four-note song.

"She wasn't wrong," the old woman said at last. "Forgetting is the valley's second gift, after the quiet. It forgets your gift, and if you're lucky it forgets your griefs too, and your enemies, and the face of everyone you've lost. Some people hate it. Some people pray for it." She looked at Jonah with eyes the color of pond water, seeing far too much. "Your mother prayed against it, I think. She wanted to be remembered even here. Especially here. That's a hard prayer, in this place."

"You knew her."

"I've known every woman in this valley who carried something she couldn't name." Wenna said it plainly, without apology, and Jonah understood suddenly that this walk had never been about geography at all. "Sit down, girl. Eat something. We've got the western line yet, and the worst mud, and I've got things to tell you that need a full belly to hear properly."

* * *

They ate bread and hard cheese and the last of the dried apples, sitting shoulder to shoulder on the boulder like a pair of crows, and Wenna told her the rest.

She told her how the veil chose its depth — how the quiet sank strongest near the stones and softened toward the valley's heart, so that a gifted person living in the deep middle might feel, on certain strange nights, something stirring in them like a fish turning under ice. She told her that the suppression was not perfect and never had been: that gifts leaked, in small ways, in ways the untrained eye misread. A cook who never once burned her hand. A midwife whose babies never failed to breathe. A taster whose tongue could find poison in a spoonful of honeyed pears.

"I'm not accusing you," Wenna said, watching the western hills. "I'm telling you what I've seen across seventy years. The valley muffles us. It does not erase us. Whatever you are, you were it before you crossed your first threshold, and you'll be it after, and the stones only decide whether the world gets to notice."

Jonah hugged her knees. Below them the afternoon light lay long and gold across the valley floor — the market roofs, the palace's modest gray tower, the threads of chimney smoke rising straight up in the windless air. It looked, from up here, exactly like what it pretended to be: a small, dull, safe place where nothing ever happened and nothing ever would.

"Why are you telling me this?" she asked. "Why me? Half the valley eats food. Why walk *me* along the stones?"

Wenna took her time answering. She brushed crumbs from her lap with great ceremony. She watched a hawk — or something shaped like a hawk — circle the far ridge.

"Because of the square," she said finally. "Two days ago. When the crier read the Act." She turned and looked at Jonah full-on, and there was nothing vague or dotty in her gaze at all; it was sharp as a lancet. "I was watching faces, girl. It's a habit of old women and hunters. And every face in that square went white or went blank or went busy pretending to think of something else. Except yours. Yours went *still*. Still like a hare that's caught the fox's scent and is deciding, right that instant, which way to run." She held up a hand before Jonah could speak. "Don't deny it. Denying wastes both our time. I've buried sisters of mine under stones quieter than these, and I know the look of a woman doing sums she hoped she'd never have to do."

The wind moved through the heather with a sound like whispered counting. Jonah found she could not look away from the old woman, and could not, either, produce a single lie. It was a strange sensation — she was, by trade and by nature, an excellent liar — but here on the boundary, with the veil humming its endless patient sentence around them, lying felt somehow beside the point. Like locking a door in a house that was already burning.

"If they come with their registry," Jonah said slowly, "and they test people—"

"They'll test people. They'll bring their little vials and their clever questions, and they'll stand right here inside the veil where nobody's gift can answer for itself, and they'll draw conclusions from leaks and habits and tongues too accurate for comfort." Wenna's voice stayed level, but her hands, Jonah noticed, had closed around her walking stick until the knuckles whitened. "The veil protects us from our own power. It does nothing at all to protect us from their suspicion. That's the flaw, girl. That's the whole flaw in one sentence. The border keeps the magic in — and it keeps the proof in with it. Inside these stones, a gifted woman and a guilty woman look exactly alike, and only one of them gets a trial."

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "What do I do?"

"Now that," said Wenna, "is the wrong question, and I'll tell you why. *What do I do* assumes there's a thing to do, some clever move, some trick that lets you stay. There isn't. There's only *where do I stand* — inside the line or outside it — and how long you can bear the waiting before the choice makes itself for you." She pushed herself to her feet, joints cracking like green firewood, and nodded westward. "Come. Last stretch. There's a stone I want you to touch before dark, and it's the most important one on the whole line, though it looks like all the others, because that's the entire point of everything I've taught you today."

* * *

The stone stood alone in a fold of the western hills where two streams met, and it did look like all the others — gray, moss-furred, patient, unremarkable. Wenna stopped three paces short of it and would come no closer, and Jonah, not understanding, stepped forward and laid her hand on the cold granite.

Nothing happened. Of course nothing happened. And yet —

She could not have said what changed. Only that standing there, palm against the stone, with the two streams whispering together at her feet and the whole valley spread out behind her in the failing light, she felt the border the way you feel your own heartbeat: not as information but as fact. This side. That side. Here, the quiet. There, the waking. And between them, this single gray tooth of granite, holding the line the way a held breath holds a secret.

"This one's called the Threshold," Wenna said from behind her, oddly formal, as if reciting. "Every healer learns it last, because you don't teach a child the door until you're sure the child understands the house." A pause. "You understand the house now, Jonah Thornbury. You understand what it costs and what it shelters and exactly where its love runs out. So I'll say the rest plain, and then we'll go home and eat something hot and never speak of this walk again unless the day comes when speaking saves your life."

Jonah kept her hand on the stone.

"The day may come — soon, girl, sooner than either of us likes — when staying means dying slowly and leaving means being seen. If that day comes, you come here. Dawn, before the light's fully up, when the mist sits in the hollows and no shepherd's abroad. You put your hand where it is now, and you step over, and you do not look back, because looking back is how people turn around, and turning around halfway is how people die in the shallows between." Wenna's voice dropped, roughened. "Beyond the line your gift will wake and the world will get loud and dangerous and true, and you will be alone with all of it. But you will be *alive*, and alive is the first requirement of everything else."

The light was going amber and then copper, and the streams ran louder in the hush, and Jonah stood with her hand on the stone that was the edge of everything safe and felt — strangely, terribly — that she was saying goodbye to a version of herself that had until this moment believed she would never leave.

"Wenna," she said. "Why me? Truly. You've watched this valley seventy years. There must be others who—"

"There aren't." Flat. Final. "There's you, and there's me, and I'm too old to run and too stubborn to hide, which means my work is to be the one who stays and opens doors." The old woman's pond-water eyes glinted in the dusk. "Besides. Your mother asked me, once, a long time ago, to watch for the day her daughter's sums came due. I promised her I would. I keep my promises past the grave, girl. It's one of the few powers I've got left."

They walked home in the dark, following the stones' invisible sentence back toward the lamplight of town, and neither spoke again. At the lane above the market square they parted, Wenna limping off toward her hut with a raised hand, Jonah descending toward the kitchens and the smell of tomorrow's bread already proofing in the dark.

The square was empty. But pinned to the notice board by the well, fresh paper gleaming pale in the night, was a new bill beside the faded text of the Act — a summons, printed neat and official, announcing that an officer of the Crown's Inquisition would arrive within the fortnight to conduct interviews of all household staff in the valley's principal residences, beginning with the palace.

Beginning, the paper said, with the kitchens.

Jonah stood in the empty square a long time, the summons' ink drying somewhere in the pit of her, and above the roofline the first stars came out — stars she had looked at all her life from the safe side of the line, and which suddenly looked, for the first time, like the far shore of a crossing she had not yet chosen to make.
