## 5. The Steward's Debt

The candle had burned down to its last finger's width when Steward Aldric finally took out the paper.

He had been avoiding it for three days. He knew the shape of his avoidance the way he knew the shape of every duty in Thornbury House — by its weight, by the particular drag it put on his steps as he walked the corridors he had walked for twenty-two years. A steward who could not name his own hesitations was a steward who would soon be surprised by them, and Aldric Fenwick had not been surprised by anything in this house since the winter of the bad fever, when he had learned that grief and arithmetic could occupy the same ledger page.

But the Act did not care about ledgers.

*An Act for the Registry of Gifted Persons,* the criers had called it, in their flat official voices, three days ago in the market square, while the whole of the Veiled Valley went quiet around them like a held breath. *All persons possessing the gift, or suspected of possessing the gift, are required to present themselves for registration within thirty days. Masters and stewards of households employing such persons bear equal obligation to report.*

*Equal obligation.* As though it were a debt like any other. As though it were grain owed at harvest, or wages owed at quarter-day.

Aldric set the paper on his desk and looked at it without touching it. It was a printed form, one of hundreds that had come up from the provincial office in a government cart, crisp and smelling faintly of ink and authority. *Report of Suspected Gifted Staff.* There were columns. There was always a column for everything now. Name. Position. Duration of service. Nature of suspicion. Evidence thereof.

Evidence thereof.

He rubbed his eyes with thumb and forefinger until he saw orange ghosts against the dark of his study. Outside, rain had begun again — the soft, patient rain of the valley, the rain that fell on a country that officially no longer existed, though no one had told the rain.

There was only one name that belonged in those columns, and they both knew it.

* * *

He had been thinking about the pears all week. That was the trouble. A man could survive a great deal if he could keep events separate — file them away in different drawers of the mind, the way he filed invoices. But the pears refused to stay filed.

It had been eleven months ago. The old king was still alive then, still resident at the summer lodge upriver, and the house had been in one of its seasons of preparation — the king's household sending ahead requisitions, the kitchens working from dawn until the tallow gave out. Jonah Thornbury had been tasting the morning service as she tasted every morning: standing at the sideboard in her plain grey dress, unhurried, methodical, a girl of perhaps nineteen going about her work with the gravity of a woman twice her age.

The honeyed pears had come up from the preserve cellar. Aldric remembered that much with terrible clarity — remembered because he had been standing right there, checking the inventory of silver against the day's requirements, close enough to see everything.

She had lifted the spoon. She had paused.

It was nothing, that pause. Less than nothing. A half-second, the kind of hesitation any taster might have over an unfamiliar batch. But Aldric had watched servants for two decades, and he knew the difference between a pause that was thinking and a pause that was knowing, and this one —

This one had been knowing.

Then she had set the spoon down, very carefully, and said, in her level way, "These aren't right. The batch is off." And she had carried the dish herself to the scullery and dumped it in the swill bucket, all four servings, before anyone could argue, and when the under-cook had protested — a full tray of preserves wasted, on whose authority — Jonah had said only, "Smell it yourself," and the under-cook had smelled it and gone pale and said nothing more.

They never did determine what was wrong with the pears. The preserve cellar was searched; the jars were examined; nothing was found but honey and fruit and the ordinary chemistry of keeping. The incident was written down as spoilage. The under-cook took a whipping for careless storage, which Aldric had signed off on with a hand that had not quite wanted to hold the pen.

And Steward Aldric Fenwick had stood in his counting room that evening and told himself a story. It was a good story, well-constructed, load-bearing. *The girl has a remarkable nose. Some people do. Her mother had it too, they say. She smelled something off — fermentation, perhaps, or a whiff of the copper pans — and acted quickly, and saved lives, possibly including mine, because I take my breakfast from that kitchen same as everyone.*

He had believed the story the way one believes a roof: not by examining it, but by living under it.

Then the proclamation had come, and the roof had begun, quietly, to leak.

Because there was a word for what Jonah Thornbury had done at that sideboard, and the word was not *nose*. The word was in the Act itself, printed in black letters on government paper: *the gift.* The ability to know what should not be knowable — poison in a dish, sickness in a body, a lie in a mouth. The old kingdom had a hundred names for it across its hundred years, and the new crown had settled on the simplest one, and the simplest ones were always the cruelest.

Aldric picked up the pen. Put it down. Picked it up again.

*Name:* he wrote. Then stopped.

* * *

Here was the thing that Aldric could not stop turning over, the stone in his shoe that grew larger with every mile: the girl had saved his life. Or very nearly his life — he could not prove the pears were meant for him, could not prove anything except that four servings of poisoned preserve had gone into a swill bucket instead of into four bellies, and one of those four bellies, on most mornings, was his.

Twenty-two years in this house. He had served the old king's factor, and the factor's widow, and the widow's nephew who held the valley estates now in trust for a crown that pretended the valley was simply another province. He had buried a wife here. He had raised, in whatever imperfect way a childless man raises things, a standard of order that he had once believed was a form of goodness. Every door in Thornbury House opened to his key. Every servant's wage passed through his hand. He knew their debts, their illnesses, their small dishonesties — and he had always told himself that knowing these things made him their shield rather than their jailer.

Now the crown had handed him a form, and the form asked him to be neither shield nor jailer but something else entirely, something with a column labeled *evidence thereof*, and Aldric Fenwick sat alone in his study discovering that he did not know what he was anymore.

If he reported her, she would be taken. He understood what *taken* meant now; everyone understood, after Harrowmere. The news traveled even into the valley, smuggled in with the wool carts despite the censors — a town purged, chalk marks on doors, ash in the gutters, and afterward a silence where a market used to be. Thirty days to present herself. If she ran, she would be hunted. If she stayed, she would be registered, and registration, everyone whispered, was only the first letter of a longer word.

If he did not report her, and she was discovered anyway — and she would be, because the clerks were coming, the proclamation said so, interviews with all household staff, all records reviewed — then it would be Aldric who was charged. Concealment. The penalties were printed on the back of the form in smaller type, the way the worst things always were.

So it was not mercy versus duty. He had spent three nights telling himself it was mercy versus duty, and on the third night he had finally admitted the truth: it was cowardice versus cowardice. Report her and betray the one person in this house who had ever, knowingly or not, stood between him and death. Shield her and gamble not only his own neck but hers — because a steward's clumsy concealment might draw the inquisitors' attention precisely where a clean record might not.

Both roads were paved with his own skin. That was the discovery that made him sick. Whatever he chose, he would be choosing it partly to save himself, and he would spend the rest of his life unable to say how large the part was.

* * *

On the fourth day, the clerk came early.

Not the inquisitor himself — the proclamation had promised inquisitors, but the provinces sent clerks first, the way armies send surveyors. This one arrived on a wet grey mule at half past seven, while the kitchen was still loud with breakfast, and Aldric met him at the front door with the smile he kept for tax assessors.

"Master Quillon," the clerk said, "for the Office of Registry. I'm to interview your staff. All of them." He was young — younger than Aldric had expected, with the smooth cheeks and earnest eyes of a man who still believed his paperwork described the world rather than remade it. Rain dripped off his oilskin cloak onto Aldric's clean floor, and Aldric found himself hating him for it with a passion entirely disproportionate to puddles.

"Of course," Aldric said. "We're loyal subjects of the crown. You'll find everything in order."

"I'm sure we will." Master Quillon smiled back, and the smile was pleasant and empty as a fresh ledger page. "I'll begin with the kitchens, if you don't mind. Food service is where the gift most often hides. Poison-tasting, you understand. The crown takes a particular interest."

"The crown may take whatever interest it likes," Aldric heard himself say, "in this house."

He led the clerk through his own corridors like a man leading a stranger through his own chest. Here is the hall where I have walked for twenty-two years. Here is the counting room where I balance what cannot be balanced. Here is the kitchen door, and behind it, the girl whose name I have not yet written down, and God forgive me, I do not know yet which sin I am committing, only that I am committing one.

Jonah was at the bread table when they entered. She looked up — calm, incurious, her hands dusted white with flour — and Aldric watched her face do nothing at all, and marveled at it, and feared it. A guilty woman would have flinched. An innocent woman would have asked questions. Jonah Thornbury did neither; she simply returned to her dough as though the crown of Cael were a delivery of salt fish, expected and uninteresting.

Master Quillon interviewed them one by one in the pantry: the under-cook, the scullery girls, the baker's boy. Aldric stood by the door through each interview, listening, cataloguing, doing the arithmetic of ruin. The questions were gentle enough on their surface — *how long in service, any family outside the valley, ever been ill in an unusual way, ever known something you couldn't explain how you knew it?*

That last question. Aldric felt it go through the room like a fishhook through water.

And then it was Jonah's turn, and she sat down across from the clerk with her flour-white hands folded, and lied so fluently, so boringly, so perfectly, that Aldric almost wept with relief — and then the clerk asked about the wine, and Aldric saw the hook set.

* * *

They were gone by noon. Master Quillon mounted his wet mule and rode south toward the next household, promising to return "when the registry review is complete," and Aldric stood at the gate watching him shrink down the road, and felt no triumph and no relief, only the peculiar hollowness of a man who has survived an inspection he knows will come again.

That night he built the fire in his study higher than he needed to.

He told himself it was cold. It was not particularly cold. He told himself the damp got into an old man's bones. He was fifty-one, and his bones were fine.

What he burned, first, was nothing. That was the ritual of it — he fed the fire blank paper, sheets of his own rough accounting notes, drafts of inventories, anything with no name on it, warming his hands over the flames as though the fire were merely a fire. And while it burned he let himself think the thoughts he had been refusing for four days, letting them come up one at a time like men out of a cellar.

*I owe her my life.* True, and insufficient. Debts between master and servant were not paid in treason.

*She is guilty of nothing but being born.* Also true, and also insufficient. The Act did not recognize innocence of that kind. Under the law, the gift was the crime, and the crime was in the blood, and no amount of good service could launder it.

*If I write her name, I kill her. If I don't, someone else will, and I will have died for nothing.* There it was. The true ledger entry. The one with both columns filled in honest figures. He hated it. He hated that it was true. He hated most of all that some cold clerk-part of himself, the part that had kept this house solvent through famine years and fee years alike, looked at that entry and pronounced it *balanced*.

Balanced. As though a girl's life were a figure that could be carried forward.

Aldric knelt at the hearth. His knees cracked, and he barely heard them. From the drawer of his desk he took the papers he had prepared in the small hours — not the registry form, never the registry form, that would be filled out in due course with careful omissions, *no suspicions, staff long-serving and loyal* — but other papers. The incident record of the spoiled pears, in his own hand, eleven months old. The inventory note showing which servings had been destined for which tables. The whipping warrant for the under-cook, which Aldric had signed and which now read, in the firelight, like a confession of his own blindness. A page of his private observations, begun the night after the proclamation and abandoned halfway down, in which he had tried to write *what I know concerning J. T., taster* and had managed only three lines before his pen had stopped as though the ink had frozen.

He fed them all to the fire, one at a time, and watched the curl and blacken of them, and told himself this was prudence. Destruction of compromising material. A steward protecting his household. Duty.

The fire ate the words *remarkable nose* and left the words *knowing* visible for one last instant before the flame took them too.

When it was done he sat back on his heels in the heat and understood, with a clarity that felt like falling, exactly what he had done. He had not destroyed evidence against Jonah Thornbury. He had destroyed evidence against himself — the record of his own knowledge, his own long complicity, the eleven months during which he had known and said nothing and slept soundly. The fire would not save her. The fire would only make sure that when the inquisitors came — and they would come, the clerk's empty smile had promised as much — there would be nothing to show that Aldric Fenwick had ever seen the truth and chosen, night after night, to look elsewhere.

He had burned his doubts. That was all. The doubts had weighed on him, and now they were smoke, and the smoke went up the chimney into the rainy dark over the Veiled Valley, and somewhere above the valley's boundary stones it dispersed into air that did not belong to any kingdom, existing or dissolved.

He was crying. He noticed it the way one notices weather in another country — distantly, irrelevantly. He did not wipe his face. There was no one to see, and no one left, now, who could testify that he had.

*No suspicions,* he would write on the form. *Staff long-serving and loyal. The household of Thornbury submits itself wholly to the crown's review.*

Every word would be a lie, and every lie would be legible, and the difference between him and the corrupt factors he had despised all his career was a difference he could no longer locate in the dark.

* * *

In the morning, the summons came up from the provincial office, forwarded through the valley magistrate with the usual rubber-stamp indifference:

*RE: Household of Thornbury Estate. Be advised that an Inquisitor of the Second Chair has been assigned to the valley circuit, one Serane Voss, with authority to re-interview all staff and review all prior testimony. Inquisitor Voss is noted for thoroughness. Households are advised to ensure records are complete and consistent.*

*Complete and consistent.*

Aldric read it three times at his desk, in the grey light, with the hearth swept clean and no ash anywhere to tell what had burned. Complete and consistent. They wanted a story with no seams. Very well. He had spent his whole life making accounts balance; he would make this one balance too.

Down in the kitchen, he could hear the morning sounds beginning — the pump handle, the clatter of the griddle, the low voices of the staff. And beneath them, unmistakable to a man who had listened to this house for twenty-two years, the particular silence of one person working very carefully and saying nothing at all.

Aldric dipped his pen and began, in his best clerk's hand, to compose the household's answers for Inquisitor Voss — and it was not until he had written the date at the top of the page that his hand stopped, because the date was wrong, or rather the season was wrong, because today was the day the wine had been delivered, and he remembered suddenly, with a steward's infallible memory for provisions, that the new cask had come up from the cellars yesterday, and that tonight, as on every night, a glass of it would be poured for the household officers at supper.

Poured for everyone.

Poured, as it happened, for the taster too — and Jonah Thornbury, he realized with a cold that started in his chest and worked outward, had never once, in all the years he had watched her, touched the wine.
