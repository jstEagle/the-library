## 6. Knock at the Kitchen Door

The knock came at half past nine in the morning, which was wrong on two counts.

First, no one knocked at the kitchen door of the Steward's House. Cooks came and went through it like weather; delivery boys pounded on it with the flat of their hands; Aldric himself had been known to shoulder it open with a basket of ledger-books balanced on one hip. But no one *knocked* — three measured raps, patient, official — because knocking meant you expected to be let in rather than merely tolerated, and nobody official had ever wanted into a kitchen before.

Second, half past nine was the dead hour between breakfast service and dinner prep, when the kitchen pretended to rest and actually schemed. Whoever chose that hour knew kitchens. That frightened Jonah more than the knock itself.

She was elbow-deep in a basin of soaking beans when the sound came, and she felt her whole body go still in a way she hadn't permitted it to go still since the criers came through the market square with the Gift Registry Act rolled up in their fists like a scroll of plague notices. Stillness was a tell. She'd learned that at nine years old, kneeling on a cold flagstone while Master Corvin dosed her tea and watched her face for the flicker that would betray what she tasted. *A taster's face is a locked room,* he used to say. *You may live in it. No one else may.*

So she did not go still. She went *slow*. She lifted her hands from the water, let them drip, wiped them on her apron with the exaggerated patience of a woman mildly annoyed at being interrupted, and said, "It's open," in a voice pitched somewhere between boredom and hospitality.

The man who entered was not an inquisitor. She knew this immediately, the way she knew bitterness under honey — by the small honest details. Inquisitors wore grey and moved like they owned the floor beneath them. This man wore brown, good brown but second-hand at the elbows, and he stood just inside the doorway as though aware that kitchens belonged to other people. He had a clerk's satchel across his chest and a clerk's ink-stained fingers and a clerk's expression of polite, exhausted diligence. He was perhaps thirty, with the kind of face that made you want to offer him something warm to drink and then feel foolish for wanting to.

"Good morning." He produced a folded paper — not brandished, merely *presented*, the way a man shows a pass at a gatehouse. "Merric Hale, Office of Inquiry, provincial circuit. I'm conducting interviews of household staff under the Registry Act. Your steward has been notified."

"He has." Aldric had told them at dawn, his voice carefully level, his eyes finding Jonah's for exactly half a heartbeat too long. *He knows,* that look had said. *He can't say he knows.* "We're honored," Jonah added, and heard how the word landed — flat as a dropped spoon — and didn't try to fix it. Fixing things was for people with something to hide.

Which, of course, she was.

"May I?" He gestured at the long scrubbed table where the scullery girls took their bread and onions at midday.

"It's your kingdom now," Jonah said, and turned back to her beans, giving him her back. It was a gamble. A nervous woman kept her eyes on the danger; a bored woman kept her eyes on her work. She counted her own heartbeats while behind her he pulled out a bench, set down his satchel, uncapped an inkhorn. The sounds were very ordinary. Ordinary sounds had never seemed so loud.

"I'll begin with names and positions, if I may. For the record."

"Jonah Thornbury. Under-taster."

"In the king's— forgive me, in the *late* king's household, I understand. Before the dissolution."

"Before everything," she said, rinsing beans with more attention than beans required. "I've been here eleven years. Tasted for the steward since old Master Corvin died. Nothing poisoned yet but my own opinions."

That got a breath from him that might have been a laugh. Good. Laughter loosened people; laughter also, she hoped, filed her away as unremarkable. She kept her hands moving. Bean, bean, bean. The water was cold enough to ache.

"You'll have heard the Act read," Hale said. His voice had shifted into something recited, worn smooth by repetition down a whole circuit of kitchens and stables and laundries. "All persons possessed of the gift — any sensitivity beyond natural constitution, any immunity to substances injurious to common persons, any knowing or unknowing commerce with the unseen arts — are required to present themselves for registration within thirty days of proclamation. Failure to register is itself an offense. Registration is not accusation." He paused. "I'm required to say that last part. It's even true, mostly."

"Mostly," Jonah echoed.

"Registration protects the gifted as much as anyone," he went on, and here his voice lost its recitation and gained something almost apologetic. "There are allowances. Exemptions for those whose gifts are trivial. Provisional certificates for those whose work depends on unusual constitution — midwives, certain healers, tasters." He said the last word lightly, a stone skipped across still water. "A registered taster is a protected taster. The crown values the profession."

"The crown valued the profession right up until it needed someone to blame," Jonah said, and let a beat of silence follow it, the silence of a woman who has said worse over worse bread. "Go on, then. Ask me what you're going to ask everyone."

* * *

He asked it all, and she lied through all of it, fluently, the way she had learned to lie before she had learned to write her own name.

Had she ever noticed anything unusual about her own senses? *I notice when Cook stretches the pepper with ash. Does that count?* Had she ever consumed anything harmful without effect? *Once ate a bad oyster in front of the whole hall and was the only one who enjoyed it.* Had she ever known, before tasting, that a thing was spoiled? Here was the knife-edge question, the one Master Corvin had drilled her for across a hundred imagined interrogations, and she gave it the answer drilled into her: *Spoiled food announces itself, sir, if you've the nose for it. That's not gift, that's nose. My mother could smell rain two days off. Nobody called her a witch for it.*

"Your mother," Hale said, and she heard the nib scratch. "Her name?"

"Rutha Thornbury. Dead eight years. Buried in the valley churchyard, third row from the yew, if you want to dig her up and ask her yourself."

"I don't," he said quietly. "I'm sorry for your loss."

And there it was again, that terrible humanity in him, the thing that made him dangerous in a way no true believer ever was. A fanatic you could predict. A tired, decent clerk doing a rotten job with his conscience strapped to his back like a second satchel — him you had to watch, because he might decide, halfway through, to actually *see* you.

So she gave him nothing to see. She gave him the kitchen: the smell of onions and lye, the drip of the eaves, the scullery girl Betta clattering pots with the studied innocence of someone eavesdropping. She gave him her hands in cold water and her voice pitched at the exact frequency of a woman who found all this tedious. Terror performed as boredom. It was the finest performance of her life and no one would ever applaud it, and she thought, wildly, that Master Corvin would have wept with pride.

* * *

It was near the end that he asked for the wine.

"Not for me," he added quickly, as she reached for the jug. "It's part of the protocol. Each staff member is offered a cup. It's—" He hesitated, and she watched him decide to tell the truth, watched it cost him something. "It's a test, Mistress Thornbury. The wine is dosed. Faintly. With a compound that most people taste as nothing at all, but that registers — differently — on certain constitutions. If you drink it and remark on nothing, that is noted. If you drink it and grimace, that is noted. If you refuse—"

"That's noted loudest of all," Jonah finished.

He had the grace to look at the table. "Yes."

She looked at the cup he'd poured. Dark red, ordinary, sitting on the scarred oak like a small dark pond. Somewhere in it floated whatever the Office of Inquiry brewed up in its grey rooms — something subtle, something designed to catch exactly the tongue she'd spent her whole life disguising.

She could refuse. Refusal was suspicious but survivable; plenty of folk didn't drink on duty, didn't drink at all, had vows or sour stomachs or simple stubbornness. But refusal written in a clerk's neat hand traveled. Refusal followed a name down a road. And thirty days was already bleeding away.

She could drink it and perform ignorance — swallow, shrug, ask if there was any more. If the compound truly registered on nothing in a common mouth, she was safe. If it registered on *her*—

But that was the thing, wasn't it. That was the trap inside the trap. She had never once, in twenty-two years, tasted poison without knowing it. Her tongue was a bell that could not be unrung. Whatever slept in that wine, she would hear it the moment it crossed her lips, and her face — her poor loyal, honest face, the one instrument of her trade — would know before she could command it otherwise.

Unless.

Unless she had already tasted it.

"Cook," she called, not turning, voice easy as Sunday. "This clerk's brought his own wine. Fetch the cups off the high shelf, will you? The ones without chips."

Betta went, puzzled, and Jonah rose, unhurried, and took the clerk's bottle in both hands as if admiring it. "May I? Force of habit. Everything that crosses my table gets tasted — it's how I keep the household alive." She poured a thimbleful into a saucer, raised it, and drank it down in front of him, meeting his eyes.

The compound bloomed on her tongue like frost: bitter underneath a sweetness too smooth, a mineral ghost, something engineered. *There you are,* she thought. *Hello, little lie.*

Her face did nothing. That was the craft of it — not blankness, which was its own confession, but the mild, faintly pleased neutrality of a woman tasting a decent table wine. She swayed the saucer, sniffed it like a connoisseur, and set it down.

"Plum-dark, bit of smoke, well-made," she pronounced. "Whoever supplies the Office of Inquiry eats better than the Office of Inquiry deserves." Then she filled the proper cup and pushed it across the table to him. "Now. Protocol says I drink it?"

"Protocol says—" He stopped. She saw him arrive at the problem a half-second after she'd built it for him: the sample was tasted, the ritual was performed, the record could say what the record needed to say. Or the record could say she'd pre-tasted it, which was only what a taster *did*, only sense, only nose, only the job.

"Yes," he said finally, hoarsely. "Protocol says you drink it."

She drank it slowly, letting it sit on her tongue like a secret she was choosing not to keep, and swallowed, and sighed, and said, "Bit heavy for morning," and went back to her beans.

In the satchel, the nib scratched. *No reaction observed,* it wrote, or words to that effect. She listened to each stroke of the pen the way other people listen to footsteps on stairs.

* * *

He gathered his things at last, and paused at the door with his hand on the latch, and asked the question she'd been waiting for the way you wait for thunder after lightning.

"One last thing. Your wine glass." He nodded at the table, where her own cup sat — the cup she'd poured herself at the start of the interview, out of pure theater, so that her hands would have something honest to do. Full when he arrived. Full now. Untouched.

"What of it?"

"You haven't drunk it."

"I'm working." She held up her dripping hands, let the absurdity speak for itself. "You want me to stop mid-basin to sip wine with a stranger who's writing down whether my eyebrows move? I'll drink it with my dinner, and I'll enjoy it more."

It was a good answer. It was a *true* answer, which was the best kind of lie, and she delivered it with the exact blend of weariness and courtesy that a thousand kitchen women had offered a thousand officials since officials were invented. He looked at her a moment longer — a long moment, a moment with weight in it — and then he nodded, and smiled a small tired smile, and said, "Of course. Thank you for your time, Mistress Thornbury. You've been very helpful."

"No I haven't," she said. "Nobody ever is. That's why you keep coming back."

The door closed behind him. She listened to his boots cross the yard, fade on the lane, gone.

Then she picked up the untouched glass and carried it, very steadily, to the window, and poured it out into the herb bed where the thyme grew, and watched the dark stain soak into the earth, and discovered that her hands had begun, at last and all at once, to shake.

* * *

They shook all through the dinner service. They shook while she tasted the stew — clean, safe, nothing but mutton and time — and while Aldric passed behind her and murmured, without looking at her, "Well?" and she answered, without looking at him, "Clean bill. He wrote it down himself." They shook while she banked the fire and sent Betta home and stood alone in the great empty kitchen with the embers ticking like slow clocks.

Because she had won today. She knew she had won today. And winning, she understood with a clarity that arrived like cold water, had bought her nothing but a better view of the next round.

Hale would file his report. *No reaction observed* would travel up whatever grey chain led to the Office of Inquiry, and somewhere in that chain sat a woman named Voss — the name had come through the market gossip like a chill through a floorboard, the new inquisitor, the thorough one, the one who read files the way priests read scripture. And a report of *no reaction observed*, attached to a taster, attached to a valley, attached to a name already flagged in some ledger she'd never see — that report wouldn't close a file. It would open one. Careful hunters didn't pounce on the first anomaly. They circled. They waited. They sent clerks back a second time, and a third, and each time the questions would be a little finer, the wine a little stronger, until the day her face failed her, because faces always fail eventually; Master Corvin had said that too, in the last year of his life, holding her hand with his poison-raddled grip: *One day the mask won't fit, child. Make sure you're somewhere else when it slips.*

Thirty days. The Act had given the gifted thirty days, and the criers had made their announcement six days ago, and every hour since had been a coin dropping into a purse that would run dry.

Jonah stood at the kitchen window in the dark, looking out past the herb bed, past the wall, toward the western hills where the boundary stones stood in their ancient patient line, marking the edge of the only place in the world where her blood was safe. Wenna would know about the stones. Wenna knew everything about the veil; the hedge-healer talked to it like an old rival. Wenna, whose sister had been taken in the last purge, and who had looked at Jonah across the market square this morning with an expression Jonah had pretended not to understand.

Tomorrow, she promised the dark. Tomorrow I'll go to Wenna, and I'll stop pretending.

Behind her, in the cooling oven, the bread she'd forgotten sat burning — a small black smell filling the kitchen, ordinary as sin, and she let it burn, because for the first time in her life she understood that some things deserved to be ruined, and that tomorrow was going to require all her courage, and tonight she intended to stand very still and be afraid, honestly, for as long as the fear lasted.

It lasted until dawn.
