## 7. Wenna's Warning

The feverfew had gone over in the night, and Wenna Maren stood in her garden at first light with a knife in one hand and grief in the other, cutting down what frost had already claimed.

She was seventy-one years old and had buried more people than she could count — not with shovels, most of them, but with words. Names spoken into the ground. That was the hedge-healer's second trade, the one no one paid for: remembering. The valley forgot, if you let it. That was its mercy and its cruelty, wound together like the twin threads of a bandage. It forgot your gift, and then it forgot you, and then it forgot itself, and everyone went on boiling their simples and salting their pork as though nothing under the sun had ever needed hiding.

Wenna had never let it.

She worked the dead stems loose with her thumb, and she thought about her sister, because the frost always made her think of Sera. Sera, who could make a candle flame lean toward her like a dog hearing its name. Sera, who was taken on a morning exactly this cold, thirty-nine years ago, when the last purge came through the valley's mouth like a cold wind and left behind a silence that had still not entirely lifted. They had not burned Sera. That was the thing no one said aloud. Burning was for witches, and the crown was careful to insist the gifted were not witches, only *sick*, only a contagion to be catalogued and contained. They took her in a cart with grey curtains drawn, and there was no fire, no grave, no body. There was a receipt. Wenna's father had kept it folded in his prayer book until the day he died, as though paper were a kind of bone.

*One (1) female, gifted, age nineteen. Removed pursuant to statute.*

Her sister had been reduced to an inventory line, and the inventory line had outlived her.

Wenna straightened, pressed a hand to the small of her back, and looked out across the low stone wall toward the town, where the market square would be filling now. She could not see it from here, but she did not need to. She had lived through a proclamation before. She knew how it moved through a place — not like news, which travels mouth to mouth and fades by evening, but like weather. Like a pressure drop. Dogs go quiet. Old women close their shutters. And somewhere in the crowd, always, there are four or five people who go very still, who hear their own names in a stranger's bellowed voice without their names being spoken at all.

She had seen that stillness once before, on a girl of nine, standing in her mother's shadow while a crier read out the old Act's grandfather law. She had seen it again yesterday, in the same face grown into a woman's, standing among the onions and the salt fish while the new crier read the Gift Registry Act, and the whole square had gone silent all at once, the way a flock goes silent when the hawk's shadow crosses it.

Jonah Thornbury. Taster's girl. Dead mother, dead mentor, quick hands, quicker tongue. A girl who had come to Wenna's door twice a year since childhood with honey or a cut finger or a lie about needing willow bark, and every visit had been a small performance, and Wenna had watched the performance with the patience of a woman who had spent a lifetime watching for exactly this.

Because you did not survive thirty-nine years in the Veiled Valley without learning to see the veil's seams. And Jonah Thornbury was a seam walking around in human shape.

* * *

The girl came at dusk, as Wenna had known she would. They always came at dusk, when the light made it harder to be seen and easier to pretend you hadn't been.

Wenna heard her on the path before the knock — heard the particular care of her footsteps, the way she placed each boot like a woman crossing a frozen pond. Guilty feet. She'd have heard them at twenty paces even without the trick her own grandmother had taught her, the listening-through-the-floor that was the smallest and safest of the hedge-gifts, so small the valley barely noticed it, so small Wenna sometimes wondered whether it counted at all. Whether she herself was on any list, in any ledger, in some archive beneath the capital where careful men wrote careful lines about girls in carts with grey curtains.

"Come in before you're seen," she called through the door, and heard the guilty feet stop, and start again, and the latch lift.

Jonah slipped inside carrying the smell of the palace kitchens — tallow and onion and, beneath it, something faintly bitter that Wenna chose not to identify. The girl stood just past the threshold with her hood still up, dripping meltwater, looking around the single room as though she might be searched later and wanted to know what she'd touched.

"You knew," Jonah said. No greeting. "Yesterday. In the square. You looked at me."

"I looked at a lot of people."

"You looked at me *like* something." The hood came back. Underneath, the girl's face was doing what it always did — holding steady, holding pleasant, holding nothing — but her eyes were working hard underneath, and Wenna remembered being young enough to believe that a still face was a safe face. "You've been looking at me like something for years, Mistress Maren. Every time I bring you honey. I want to know what I am to you."

"A risk," Wenna said. "Sit down."

"I'd rather—"

"*Sit.*"

The girl sat. That was the thing about them, Wenna thought, watching her fold onto the bench by the cold hearth with her spine straight and her hands quiet in her lap. They were always braced. Always waiting for the blow to fall from some direction they hadn't yet mapped. You could see it in the way they entered rooms — last, fastest, nearest the door.

Wenna put the kettle on the hook. It was a small domesticity and she performed it deliberately, because fear fed on hurry, and one of them, tonight, was going to have to stay calm enough for both.

"You want to know what you are," she said. "I'll tell you what you are. You're the reason I keep feverfew growing in a garden nobody visits. You're the reason I know the weight of a grey curtain. You are—" she paused, choosing, "—my sister's debt, come due."

"I don't understand."

"No," Wenna agreed. "You don't. Because your mother taught you the first lesson and died before she could teach you the rest. *The valley forgets you if you let it.* Wasn't that it?"

He saw it land — the flinch, quickly mastered, the girl's whole body going still the way prey goes still. "My mother talked too much at the end," Jonah said carefully. "Sick women say strange things."

"Sick women say true things," Wenna said. "And you've spent your whole life pretending not to hear them." She poured the water over the dried nettle and yarrow, watching the steam rise between them like a curtain of her own. "I'm going to tell you a story, girl, and you're going to sit there and let me, because I've earned it. Thirty-nine years is what I've earned. Do you know what happened thirty-nine years ago?"

"The last purge," Jonah said quietly. "Everyone knows."

"Everyone knows *of* it. Nobody knows it. There's a difference, and the difference is names." Wenna sat down across from her, joints complaining, and laced her fingers over her knee. "There were eleven gifted souls in this valley the year the criers came. Eleven. I can give you every name, in the order they were taken, because my father wrote them on the inside of his prayer book where he thought God would keep them safe, and God, whatever else He is, is a better archivist than the crown. Tam Broadhurst, who could find water under stone. The Ellery twins, who spoke to bees. Marta Cade, who was ninety and bedridden and they carried her out anyway, wrapped in blankets, because the Act does not recognize dying. And Sera Maren. My sister. Nineteen years old. Could make a candle lean."

"Candle flames," Jonah repeated. Something moved behind her eyes — recognition, maybe, or envy, or the particular loneliness of a person discovering their secret has siblings.

"She used to do it for the little ones," Wenna said. "Make the flame bow to them like a courtier. They'd laugh and laugh. And then one day the criers came, and the laughing stopped, and a man with very clean fingernails asked my father, very politely, whether we had any sick persons in the household. Sick persons. As though she had a cough." Her voice stayed level. She had practiced level for thirty-nine years; it was the one craft she'd perfected beyond healing. "They gave us a receipt, girl. Paper. *One female, gifted, age nineteen.* Like a sack of meal. And my father kept that paper until it wore soft as cloth, and I burned it on his grave, and I have regretted burning it ever since, because you should keep the evidence of what they do. Evidence is the only inheritance they can't confiscate."

The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the light was going blue and then dark, and somewhere down the lane a dog began to bark and then thought better of it.

"Why are you telling me this?" Jonah asked. Her voice had changed — lower, stripped of the kitchen-girl pleasantness. This was the real one underneath, Wenna thought. Thin and hard, like wire. "Why now? Why me?"

"Because yesterday I watched a hawk's shadow cross a market square, and one face in forty went still, and it was yours." Wenna leaned forward. "And because the new Act isn't the old Act, child. Hear me. The last purge, they came for the loud ones — the ones whose gifts showed, the candle-bowers and bee-speakers. The quiet ones survived by being quiet. But this registry — this is different. This wants *everyone*. Written down. Numbered. Filed. And once you're filed, girl, you're filed forever. Registries don't die with the kings who order them. They sleep. And they wake up whenever someone new needs a list of the guilty." She tapped the bench with one knuckle, once, hard. "Your immunity saved a steward's life two months ago. I've heard the story — the whole valley's heard the story, dressed up as luck. But luck gets retold, and retold, and eventually it reaches ears attached to clean fingernails. It may have reached them already."

"They have nothing on me."

"They have a pattern. Patterns are worse than proof. Proof you can argue with. A pattern just sits there, being a shape." Wenna rose, joints cracking like green wood, and went to the hearth. Third stone from the left, set a hand's width proud of its neighbors — she worked it loose with the poker, the way she had done once a year for thirty-nine years, oiling the cavity against damp, checking, waiting, telling herself she was a foolish old woman keeping a foolish old ritual. "My father hid Sera's things here. What was left. A ribbon. A letter she never sent. And this."

She brought it to the table and set it down between them.

It was a key. Iron, long-shanked, heavy as a sin, black with age except where recent handling had worn bright paths along its teeth. Its bit was a confusion of cuts and notches that matched no lock Wenna had ever seen, and she had looked — oh, she had looked, in her forties, traveling on pretext of gathering herbs, trying every church door and cellar gate from here to the river towns. Nothing fit. Nothing had ever fit.

"What is it?" Jonah whispered. She hadn't touched it yet. She was staring at it the way people stare at snakes — fascination and revulsion in equal measure.

"I don't know," Wenna said. "That's the truth, so hold it close. My father didn't know either. He got it from *his* mother, who got it from hers, and the oldest story says it was cut during the Dragon War, before the kingdom dissolved — cut for certain people, certain families, by men who knew what was coming. Keys were made for those meant to survive. That's the story. It may be a story. But my family kept it through three purges, and keeping it cost us nothing, and not keeping it—" her voice caught, just slightly, the hairline crack in the level, "—not keeping *other* things is what cost us Sera."

Jonah reached out at last. Her fingers closed around the iron and Wenna watched the girl's face and saw it happen — the small involuntary shiver, the widening of the eyes. Cold. The key was always cold. Iron held the chill of the earth itself, her grandmother used to say, and never explained further, and Wenna had stopped asking questions at nine years old, the same age they'd started dosing a certain kitchen girl to build her tolerance, and wasn't that a thought — the whole valley full of children being trained for survival without ever being told what they were surviving.

"It's freezing," Jonah said.

"It's been waiting," Wenna said. "There's a difference."

The girl turned the key over in her palm, and her thumb found the worn bright paths on the teeth — paths made by other thumbs, generations of them, Wenna's whole vanished family handling this iron in the dark — and Wenna saw the moment the inheritance landed. Saw it pass, hand to hand, the way it had passed to her: not power, not knowledge, just *weight*. The weight of being the one still alive to carry it.

"I can't take this."

"You can, and you will, and you'll take it tonight, because tomorrow the clerk comes."

Jonah's head snapped up. "What clerk?"

"Inquisitor's office. A man named Dree, with a ledger and a list of questions. He'll be at the palace by midmorning and in the kitchens by noon, and he'll ask every soul where they were born and whether anyone in their family ever did anything *unusual*, and he'll write down the answers, and the answers will be cross-checked against answers given by other people about other people, and somewhere in that web of careful writing, a shape will form." Wenna held the girl's gaze. "A shape with your name in the middle of it. You have one night, Jonah Thornbury. Maybe two. After that, the pattern closes."

"And go where?" The wire-voice cracked now, finally, and underneath it Wenna heard the nine-year-old, the one who'd stood in her mother's shadow. "There's nowhere. The valley protects—"

"The valley protects those *inside* it," Wenna said. "That's all it's ever done. It doesn't love you. It doesn't know you. It's a wall, not a mother. And a wall that keeps the magic out also keeps *you* in, and the crown's men can walk through that gate any time they please, because they aren't magic, they're just men with lists." She stood, and took the girl's free hand in both of hers — the hand holding the key, so that the iron was caught between their palms, cold against warm, old against young. "Beyond the boundary stones there's a country the maps have stopped admitting to. Cael proper. Roads that go to towns that officially don't exist. If the stories are true, there's a door in the Ashreach mountains, east, where the land goes to ash and the last dragon is said to be dying. A door out of magic's reach entirely. Out of *everything's* reach."

"That's a fireside tale."

"So was a girl who couldn't be poisoned," Wenna said. "Until two months ago."

Silence filled the room, thick as the steam had been. Jonah looked down at their joined hands, at the key, and Wenna watched her do the arithmetic every hunted person must do — the weight of staying against the weight of going, the known cage against the unknown country — and saw the moment the sum came out wrong either way, and the girl understood that too.

"If I run," Jonah said slowly, "they'll know. Running is confessing."

"Staying is confessing, slower." Wenna squeezed the hand. "Listen to me, child, because this is the last thing and then I'll let you hate me in peace. My sister waited to be sure. She waited for a safer season, a better plan, a sign. She was nineteen and she was patient and she was *sure*, and the cart came on a Tuesday." Her voice did not crack this time. She had used up all its cracks decades ago, and what remained was dry and hard as old bread. "Do not wait to be sure. Sure is a luxury for people who aren't on lists. Go ugly, go early, go unready. Alive is the whole of the strategy."

For a long moment the girl didn't move. Then her fingers tightened around Wenna's — around the iron between them — and she nodded, once, the smallest nod Wenna had ever been given, and it was worth more than every thank-you in thirty-nine years.

"There's a smuggler caravan works the river road," Wenna said, releasing her. "Odile's people. Red lantern at the third mile marker past the west stones, dawn after next. Tell them Wenna Maren owes you, and mind, they'll test that claim, so look insulted when they do." She almost smiled. "Now eat the bread on that shelf, take the cloak off the peg — the grey one, grey hides best at dawn — and get out of my house before I start crying, because I'm too old for it and it frightens the cats."

Jonah stood. At the door, hood up again, key hidden somewhere in her clothing, she paused with her hand on the latch.

"Mistress Maren. Your sister. Sera." The girl didn't turn around. "If I find out what happened to her — the ones they took — I'll come back and tell you. Even if it's bad. Especially if it's bad."

Wenna looked at the girl's back, at the thin shoulders carrying thirty-nine years of someone else's waiting, and felt the strangest sensation — not hope, exactly. Hope was a young woman's currency and she'd spent hers. Something older. The feeling of a debt changing hands.

"Mind the boundary stones at dawn," was all she said. "The veil bites when you leave it. Everything you've been missing will hit you at once, and it will feel like drowning in color. Let it. Drown, and then swim."

The door closed. The guilty feet went away down the path, lighter than they'd come, or perhaps only faster.

Wenna banked the fire, fed the cats, and sat down at her table in the dark without lighting a lamp. On the bare wood where the key had lain, the faintest ring of cold lingered — she could feel it with her palm, a ghost of iron, already fading.

Three days, she thought. Perhaps four. Then the clerk's careful pen would finish its web, and men with clean fingernails would come asking after a kitchen girl, and they would come to her door too, eventually, because old women who grew feverfew nobody visited were always on somebody's list.

She found she didn't mind. She had spent her whole life keeping names, and it seemed fitting, somehow, that she should end by becoming one.

Down in the valley, the lamps of the palace were lighting one by one, and somewhere among them a clerk named Dree was sharpening a pen.

* * *

Dawn came grey and slow, and Wenna was in her garden when the first riders passed on the far road — not the clerk, not yet, just carter's traffic, ordinary wheels on ordinary mud. She cut the frost-killed stems anyway, though the work was done, because her hands needed something to hold that wasn't shaking.

By noon, the town knew that the taster's girl had not reported for her shift. By evening, the story had grown a dozen heads, none of them accurate, all of them useful. And Wenna Maren sat by her window with her father's old prayer book open on her knees, reading the eleven names written on the inside cover in faded brown ink, adding nothing, subtracting nothing, keeping watch.

Somewhere east, past the boundary stones, past the drowned-color country, a girl was running with an heirloom in her pocket and a hawk's shadow on her heels.

"Hold it well," Wenna said to the empty room, to the dead, to the cold iron miles away and warming slowly against a living skin. "Hold it well, and don't look back."

But of course the girl would look back. They always did.

And far to the east, in mountains that no longer appeared on any map worth trusting, something ancient stirred in its sleep of ash, and dreamed, faintly, of a bell being struck.
