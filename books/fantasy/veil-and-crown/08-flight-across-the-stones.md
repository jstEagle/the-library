## 8. Flight Across the Stones

She left before the birds did.

That was the trick of it — Wenna had told her that much, pressing the cold iron key into her palm two nights ago with fingers that smelled of yarrow and smoke: *Go in the dark hour, when even the rooks are still dreaming. The valley sleeps deepest just before it wakes.* Jonah had not asked how a hedge-healer came to know the habits of boundary stones. There was no time for questions anymore, and besides, she had spent her whole life learning not to ask them.

The kitchen was dark behind her. She had banked the fire herself, wiped down the boards, set the morning's bread to prove under its cloth so that whoever found the kitchen empty would find it *ordered*, would think for one confused moment that she had only stepped out to the well. It was a small cruelty and a small kindness at once, and she did not examine either. She had learned long ago that you could survive almost anything if you refused to look directly at it.

Her bundle weighed nothing and everything: a change of clothes, a heel of bread wrapped in linen, a knife, Wenna's key on its cord around her neck where it lay against her sternum like a second heartbeat, colder than her own. And beneath her tongue, folded small as a seed, the slip of paper with the survey fragments copied out in Wenna's crabbed hand — *Ashreach foothills, the old war-road, follow the water until the water forgets its name.*

She went out through the scullery door, and the valley let her go.

* * *

The Veiled Valley at night was a held breath. Jonah moved through it knowing every stone by feel — the cow-track behind the tannery, the ford where the water ran shallow over white pebbles, the sheep path that climbed through gorse toward the high pasture where the land began to remember it was mountains. The moon was a fingernail paring, low in the west, giving light like an apology. Frost furred the grass and took the shape of her boots and kept it, crisp and accusing, and she thought absurdly of Aldric reading her footprints like a ledger, line by line, and made herself stop thinking of Aldric.

The steward had burned something in his brazier last week. She had seen the ash on his cuffs, gray and soft, the ash of paper, and he had looked at her across the morning room with an expression she still could not name — gratitude wearing duty's uniform, or duty wearing gratitude's face. He had not reported her. Yet. But the Act sat over the valley now like weather coming, and weather always arrived.

The high pasture gave way to scree. Here the valley narrowed to a throat between two shoulders of hill, and the boundary stones stood in their ancient procession up the throat: waist-high, lichen-crusted, unremarkable to any eye that did not know what they were. Wenna had walked her along this line once, in another life, pointing with a stick.

*They're not wards, girl. They're a wound that healed wrong. Everything inside is inside. Everything outside is outside. The stones are just the scar.*

Jonah stood before the first stone and felt nothing, which was the whole point of the valley, which was the whole tragedy of her life. Inside the Veiled Valley, magic could not follow — could not live, could not breathe, could not so much as whisper. Her gift had been a drowned thing here, sunk so deep she sometimes managed to pretend it wasn't hers. Twenty years of tasting the king's breakfast with a tongue that knew bitter almond from honey not because of any art but because of what she was, and the valley had swallowed that knowledge every time, pressed it flat and silent as flowers in a book.

She put her hand on the stone. Cold. Lichen. Nothing else.

*Once you cross,* Wenna had said, *you'll feel it come back. Like a limb gone numb, the blood returning. It hurts. Don't fight it, whatever you do. Fighting it is how they find you.*

"They" being everyone. "They" being the criers with their proclamations, the clerks with their lists, the inquisitor whose name she refused to learn because names were invitations. "They" being, possibly, the whole world outside the scar.

Jonah Thornbury stood at the edge of the only country that had ever protected her, and discovered that the hardest part was not fear. The hardest part was that she loved it.

Not the crown, never the crown — but the valley itself, the smell of the bakery at dawn, the specific gray of the river in November, the way the market square went quiet all together when bad news came, a thousand people holding one breath. Her mother was buried here. Her mentor was buried here. Every version of herself she had ever been had been possible only inside these hills, where the thing in her blood was smothered into harmlessness and called safety.

And the valley's love was conditional, and the condition was written on a registry now, and staying meant walking to the clerk's table with her hands open like a good citizen, and leaving meant —

Leaving meant this. One foot past the stone.

She stepped over.

* * *

Nothing happened, at first. That was the cruelest joke of it. She stood with both feet on the far side of the boundary, on ground that belonged to a kingdom officially dissolved, and felt exactly the same: cold, frightened, ordinary. The frost crunched. Her breath hung. Somewhere below, the valley slept on, keeping its secret, forgetting her already — *the valley forgets you if you let it*, her mother had said, and Jonah had spent twenty years letting it, and now she was standing outside its forgiveness and it had not even noticed.

Then the blood came back.

It started in her teeth. A prickling, a fizzing, as though her jaw were full of snowmelt — and then down her spine like someone drawing a hot wire through her vertebrae one by one, and then everywhere, everywhere at once, and Jonah dropped to her knees on the frozen grass because her legs had simply resigned.

The world turned on.

Not brightened — *turned on*, the way a lamp turns on, the way a struck bell doesn't get louder but becomes more itself. The frost under her hands blazed silver, each crystal distinct, each one singing a note too high for ears. The pre-dawn dark peeled back in layers she hadn't known were layers: blue under black under a green so young it hurt. The gorse on the hillside was not merely yellow-flowered; it was *burning* with yellow, each blossom a small declaration. She could smell the mountain's bones. She could taste the air — actually taste it, mineral and pine and somewhere far off woodsmoke, and beneath it all a faint copper thread that she recognized with a lurch as *herself*, her own gift rising through her blood like a fish surfacing, shaking off twenty years of deep water.

"Oh," she said aloud, and her voice sounded strange, too large, as though the world outside the valley heard everything twice.

It hurt. Wenna had promised it would hurt and it did, magnificently. It was like the first bite after a fever, like standing up too fast, like weeping — pain and relief indistinguishable. Her tongue pressed against the roof of her mouth and *knew things*: that the water in the stream fifty feet below ran clean for three miles and then picked up iron; that the bread in her bundle had begun, very slightly, to turn; that the frost itself carried a trace of something sweetish and wrong, some residue of old magic soaked into the land beyond the stones, the way a wall keeps the shape of what once leaned against it.

Her gift woke like a limb gone numb, and the pins and needles were agony, and she laughed — actually laughed, kneeling in the frost with tears freezing on her lashes — because it was *hers*. All those years of pretending to be only quick, only careful, only lucky. She was not lucky. She had never been lucky. She was gifted, and the word that had been a death sentence on a crier's lips was, in the privacy of her own body, simply true.

Then the vertigo hit, and the laugh died in her throat.

Because the world was too much. That was the truth of it, the thing no one warned you about the outside: it was *too much*. Inside the valley everything came to her muffled, wrapped in wool, a life lived through a curtain. Out here every input arrived naked. The wind had opinions. The darkness had texture. Her own heartbeat was a drum in a small room, and her gift rode on top of it all like a rider on a restive horse, tasting, cataloguing, *wanting* — and Jonah understood suddenly why the gifted who fled the purges so often got caught. Not because they were careless. Because they were drunk. Because the world outside the veil was wine to anyone raised on water, and no one makes good decisions drunk.

She knelt there and breathed, deliberately, the way she'd been taught to breathe between dishes at a state banquet. In for four. Hold. Out for six. The vertigo didn't leave but it stopped spinning her, settled into a hum, became a thing she carried instead of a thing that carried her.

*One dish at a time,* her mentor used to say, sliding the next covered plate across the tasting table. *You don't eat the banquet, girl. You eat the plate in front of you.*

All right. The plate in front of her was a hillside, a dawn, a road that didn't exist.

She ate it.

* * *

By full light she had climbed above the valley's throat and could look back, and she made herself do it, made herself stand on the ridge and see what she was leaving, because she owed it that much.

The Veiled Valley lay below in its cup of hills, silvered with frost, smoke already rising from a dozen chimneys — the bakery first, of course, the bakery always first. From up here it looked like what it pretended to be: a place where nothing had ever happened. A pocket of ordinary stitched into the mountains' hem. She searched for the market square, the clerk's table that would be set up there today or tomorrow or next week, the queue of the frightened and the righteous. Too far to see. Good. Let it stay abstract a while longer.

Behind her, the valley closed.

There was no other way to describe it. As she watched — as the sun cleared the eastern shoulder of the world and poured gold down the slopes — the valley seemed to draw itself in, the mist thickening along its rim, the details softening, the whole basin settling back into its long sleep of not-noticing. She felt it happen in her newly woken skin: a sensation like a door shutting in a house she'd just left, like a hand gently but firmly removing itself from hers. The suppression that had smothered her gift for twenty years was still down there, working, dutiful, indifferent. It had never been protecting *her*, she understood now. It protected anyone inside it and no one in particular. She had mistaken shelter for love.

*The strange comfort of a border that loves you conditionally* — Wenna had said something like that once, though Jonah couldn't have repeated it verbatim if her life depended on it, and maybe her life did depend on it, and maybe that was the whole lesson of the morning: nothing verbatim survives. You carry the shape of things, not the words.

She touched the iron key at her throat. Still cold. Colder than the morning justified, cold as if it had been sitting in a stream, and she wondered — not for the first time — what lock had been cut away from it, and why Wenna's sister had died holding its twin.

"Don't," she said to herself, aloud, and the wind took the word and shredded it. Grieving was a luxury for later, for rooms with doors, for years with enough of themselves left. Right now there was a road.

She turned east, toward the Ashreach, toward the water that would forget its name, and began to walk.

* * *

The old kingdom announced itself gradually, the way a stranger's mood announces itself before the stranger speaks.

First it was small things. A milestone, half-swallowed by turf, bearing a heraldry she knew only from the archive plates in her mentor's few books: the crowned stag of the old royal line, antlers worn smooth by centuries of weather and thumbs. Then a shrine at a crossroads, its niche empty, its offering-bowl still holding rainwater and one green coin that no one had dared remove. Then, as the sun climbed and the frost burned off and her gift settled from riot into something like attention, the roads themselves — broader than any valley track, engineered, cambered, running straight toward distances that maps no longer admitted to.

Cael proper. The dissolved kingdom. The country that officially did not exist, and yet had paved its existence into the earth so thoroughly that dissolution looked like a bureaucratic rumor.

Jonah walked it feeling like a ghost attending her own funeral — except that ghosts, she suspected, felt less. Every mile the world grew louder in her blood. By midday she could taste the next village before she saw it: woodsmoke, tallow, pig, and underneath, sour and unmistakable, *fear* — the same flavor she'd caught on the breath of the market square the day the proclamation was read, a whole community marinating in it. She kept to the hedgerows. She pulled her hood up. She practiced, as she walked, the flattening of herself, the taster's oldest skill: becoming furniture, becoming background, becoming a woman of no particular gift going about no particular business.

But her gift would not flatten. That was the trouble. It rose in her like yeast, tasting everything, naming everything, and once — passing a field where a boy was mending a wall — she caught his sweat on the wind and read it whole: oats and honest labor and a flicker of something medicinal, willow-bark, a headache, poor lad — and she had to stop behind a hawthorn and press her palms against her eyes until the world shrank back to human size.

*This is how they catch us,* she thought. *Not with hounds. With ourselves.*

She thought of the inquisitor then — the one whose name she wouldn't learn — and imagined her for the first time not as a black coat on a scaffold but as a woman, perhaps clever, perhaps tired, walking these same roads with a list in her saddlebag. The thought arrived uninvited and unwelcome, the way empathy always does when you can least afford it, and Jonah put it down the way you put down a dish you've tasted and condemned: firmly, without lingering, before it can poison you.

Toward late afternoon the land began to lift. The Ashreach showed itself in the northeast — not mountains so much as a suggestion of mountains, blue-gray, hooded in cloud, the kind of range that appears on old maps with warnings printed in the margins. Between her and them lay a river valley, and the river ran silver in the low sun, and following the water was the first instruction in Wenna's crabbed hand.

*Follow the water until the water forgets its name.*

She drank from it at a bend, cupping her hands, and her gift read the water as it passed her lips the way a clerk reads a ledger — clean, snow-fed, a whisper of limestone, nothing harmful, nothing hidden — and she drank deeply, the first water in her life she'd tasted freely rather than forensically, and nearly wept at the extravagance of it.

It was dusk when she felt the bell.

There was no bell. That was the only way her mind could render it afterward: a single note, vast and low, struck somewhere in the direction of the Ashreach — not a sound in her ears at all but a sound in her *gift*, a vibration through the risen part of her, the drowned thing now swimming, and it rang through her ribs and settled in her teeth and would not fade.

She stood in the failing light with her hand pressed flat over the iron key, listening to a note that no one else on the road could hear, and understood two things with perfect clarity.

The first was that something in the mountains had felt her cross the stones. Had felt her gift ignite like a struck match in a dark country, and had turned toward her, the way you turn toward a voice calling your name across a crowded field.

The second was that she was not afraid.

That frightened her more than anything else had, all week, all month, all her careful hidden life. She should be afraid. Everything in her training said run, hide, flatten, become furniture. But the note rang on, ancient and enormous and — there was no other word — *lonely*, and it rang against the loneliness she had carried since her mother's grave, since her mentor's, since the first lie she'd ever told about how she knew, and the two notes harmonized.

Far off in the blue dusk, the mountains waited. Somewhere among them, a door that wanted fire. Somewhere among them, whatever had struck the bell.

Jonah pulled her cloak tight, checked the sky for the road's sake, and walked north along the river — toward the water that would forget its name, toward the thing that already knew hers.
