## 9. A Country That No Longer Exists

The road out of the valley was signed for a town that did not exist.

Jonah stopped at the first waymarker she came to, a leaning post of grey stone half-swallowed by bramble, and read the letters twice to be sure her eyes weren't still adjusting to the world beyond the boundary stones. *Caelmark — 6 leagues. Harrowmere — 11. The Ashreach Road.* She knew none of these names. She had grown up in the Veiled Valley, and the valley's maps — the few, the faded, the forbidden-to-ask-about — showed nothing but the valley itself, ringed by mountains, and beyond them a blankness that schoolmistresses called *the Crown Lands* and never described.

But Caelmark was not a blankness. Caelmark was carved in old, deep lettering, older than the proclamation nailed to the market square, older than the crown that had issued it. And beneath the town's name, worn nearly smooth by centuries of weather, ran a heraldic device: a crowned stag beneath seven stars.

Jonah stood in the road with her bundle on her shoulder and felt something move through her that she had no word for. It was not fear, though fear was there, humming under everything like a struck string that would not stop. It was not grief, though grief had been her constant companion since Wenna pressed the cold iron key into her palm and said *run*. It was closer to what she imagined an orphan might feel, standing at last before the house where her parents had died — a homesickness for a home she had never been allowed to enter.

She touched the stone. It was warm from the morning sun, ordinary, real. Whatever country this marker belonged to, it had been here a long time, waiting for someone to come looking.

She walked on.

* * *

The land itself gave nothing away at first. Rolling pasture, hedgerows heavy with late summer, wheat going gold in fields that stretched toward hills she did not know the names of. Sheep watched her pass with the flat indifference of sheep everywhere. If a kingdom had been dissolved here, the grass had not been informed.

It was the third waymarker that unsettled her properly. This one stood at a crossroads, taller than the first, its carving sharp enough to suggest it had been renewed within living memory — and yet it bore the same crowned stag, the same seven stars, and directions to four towns, every one of which Jonah had never heard of. She crouched and examined the base of the stone, because nine years of kitchen service had taught her that the truth of any household lived at floor level, in the things people stopped seeing.

Someone had chiseled out a fifth name. She could just make it: *Vellengard*, the letters gouged away with violence, the stone beneath scarred white. Below the wound, in smaller, newer script, someone had added: *no such place*.

Jonah sat back on her heels. No such place. But the road — she looked up — the road still ran in Vellengard's direction, wide and well-kept, rutted by cart wheels, lined for its first mile with the stumps of what had once been signposts. Roads did not keep themselves for towns that were no such places.

A cart came rattling up behind her then, and she stepped aside, and the driver — a broad woman with flour dusted into the creases of her knuckles — reined in and looked down at Jonah with the frank appraisal of someone sizing up either a customer or a corpse.

"You're standing like you've never seen a crossroads," the woman said.

"I'm from the valley," Jonah said, and let the words do their work. They always did. People outside spoke of the Veiled Valley the way people spoke of weather far off over the horizon — something known about, never questioned.

"Valley." The woman said it the way one says *the moon*: a thing that exists, that one does not visit. Her eyes went to the mutilated waymarker, then back to Jonah, and something in her face closed a little, the way faces close in kitchens when the steward walks in. "Bound for Caelmark?"

"I don't know where I'm bound."

"Honest answer." The woman chewed on something — Jonah's tongue, unbidden and useless here, catalogued it as dried plum and cheap tobacco. "Caelmark's three hours if your legs are young. There's bread and cheese in the box behind me if you'll walk alongside and tell me what the valley's like. I've wondered my whole life."

So Jonah walked, and talked, and lied only a little — she said the valley was green and quiet and that nobody left, which was true, and she said she didn't know why she'd left, which was not. In exchange she learned more in three hours than she had learned in nineteen years inside the stones.

She learned that the woman's name was Marta, that she drove flour to Caelmark twice a week, and that the kingdom they were driving through was called Cael and had ceased to exist forty years ago.

"Dissolved," Marta said, spitting the word like a cherry stone. "That's what the new crown's writs say. After the Dragon War, when the old line burned out — literally, some say, the last king and his whole court, ash and bone, though others say it was plague, and my grandmother swore it was the gifted what did it, curse her gossiping heart — after all that, the victors drew new borders and wrote new laws, and Cael was struck from the rolls. Doesn't exist. Never did, officially."

"But we're driving through it."

"We're driving through *provisional territory*, love. That's the joke." Marta laughed, and it was not a kind laugh. "The roads are Cael's roads. The bridges are Cael's bridges. The mills pay their tithes to Cael's old charters because the new crown never got round to writing its own — too busy hunting dragons and then hunting the folk who might be like them. Every deed, every birth record, every grave in this ground is written in a dead kingdom's hand. We live in a country that's been dissolved the way you dissolve salt in water. You can't see it anymore, but taste anything around here and it's there."

Jonah's tongue ached at the word *taste*, some old reflex, and she kept her face still.

"And the towns?" she asked. "Like—" she nodded back toward the crossroads, toward the gouged name.

Marta was quiet for a long moment. The cart wheels spoke for both of them, a language of axle-grease and dust.

"There were towns," she said finally. "Before the War, and during it. Some burned. Some emptied. And some —" she flicked the reins idly, not needing to, the horse knew the road — "some were *struck*. You understand? Not destroyed. Erased. A town could be on the wrong side of some decree, or shelter the wrong family, or simply stand where the new surveyors wanted a garrison, and one morning the clerks would strike its name from the rolls, and that was that. No soldiers came. No fire. Just — paperwork. The name gone. The taxes rerouted. And folk are strange creatures; take a place's name away and folk start to forget it's there at all. They drift off. Roofs fall in. Within a generation there's nothing but a waymarker pointing at a field, and children ask why the road goes somewhere that isn't anywhere, and their parents say *no such place*, and that's the end of it."

"But the roads stay."

"The roads stay." Marta glanced at her sidelong. "Roads remember longer than people do. That's what my grandmother used to say, and she said a lot of foolish things, but I think that one was true."

* * *

They parted at the edge of Caelmark, Marta pressing half a loaf and a wedge of hard cheese into Jonah's hands and refusing payment with a wave that brooked no argument. "For the valley talk," she said. "Cheap at the price." Then she was gone in a rattle of wheels, and Jonah stood alone at the gates of the first town she had ever seen outside the valley, holding bread like an offering.

Caelmark was not large. A market square, a church whose bell tower leaned slightly eastward as if listening, two streets of shops and one of houses, a mill on a brown stream. But to Jonah it was overwhelming in a way she could not have explained without confessing everything. The valley had one market day, one square, one baker, and everyone's bread tasted like everyone else's bread. Here there were three bakers, and she could smell the difference between them from thirty paces — sourdough against milk-loaf against a dark rye scented with caraway — and her gift, waking properly now for the first time in her life, unrolled itself through the town like a tongue across a table.

It was too much. She had spent her whole life tasting in whispers, in the suppressed half-light of the valley where magic slept and her gift slept with it. Now every smell arrived with its full biography attached. The stream told her where the mill upstream dumped its waste. The air above the tannery row carried tannin and lime and, faintly, underneath, something metallic and wrong that made her want to warn everyone within a mile. The crowd in the square was a symphony of sweat and onion and soap and illness, each person legible to her whether she wished it or not.

She ate her bread sitting on the church steps, deliberately, slowly, forcing herself to taste only the bread. Rye. Caraway. A whisper of ash in the crust — the oven had been fired with wood from somewhere recently burned, and her gift, treacherous, generous, offered her the ghost of that smoke: pine resin, and under the pine, something older and bitterer, like burnt hair.

She put the bread down.

Across the square, on the wall beside the mill office, a notice board held the usual rural clutter — a lost dog, a wool price, a funeral announcement. And in the center, fresh-pasted, corners still stiff, a printed sheet she recognized with a lurch: the Gift Registry Act, the same proclamation that had silenced the valley's market square, reproduced here in a provincial hand. All gifted persons must present themselves for registration. Sheltering the unregistered is itself a crime. The hunt is a duty of every loyal subject.

*Loyal subject,* Jonah thought, *of a kingdom that doesn't exist.*

That was the thing she kept circling, the thing that would not resolve. The crown that issued this proclamation claimed Cael was dissolved — Marta had said so, and the waymarkers agreed, and the gouged name of Vellengard agreed. And yet the Act invoked the crown's law as though the law had never changed hands, as though the dissolution had dissolved nothing that mattered. The roads were Cael's. The records were Cael's. The hunts, she was beginning to suspect, were Cael's too — the machinery running on and on, powered by a dead kingdom's momentum, grinding through provinces that officially were nowhere at all.

You could erase a town with paperwork. Apparently you could erase a kingdom and keep its teeth.

An old man sat down beside her on the steps, uninvited, easing his knees with a grunt. He smelled of pipe smoke and liniment and, beneath that, to her unwanted gift, of something she had smelled only once before — in the valley, on Wenna, faint as memory: herbs worked into tinctures, hedge-craft, the green bitter signature of someone who dealt in remedies that were not entirely remedies.

He said nothing for a while. Then, nodding at the proclamation: "New paper, old ink."

"Sir?"

"That Act." He tapped his pipe against his boot. "Folk think it's new because the printing's new. It's not. There've been lists before. My grandmother's grandmother saw the last purge — before the War, when Cael was still Cael and proud of it. Same words, near enough. *Present yourselves. Sheltering is a crime.* Kingdoms die and come back wearing different coats, but the lists —" he shook his head — "the lists go on. Like the roads."

Jonah's hand had gone, without her deciding it, to the pocket where Wenna's iron key lay wrapped in cloth. The old man's eyes followed the movement, and he smiled, and it was a sad smile, and he stood up, knees cracking, having apparently gotten whatever he came for.

"There's a room above the cooper's, if you need a bed," he said. "Tell them Tam sent you. And girl —" he paused at the bottom of the steps, not turning around — "whatever you're carrying in that pocket, don't show it to anyone who asks to see papers. Papers are how they caught them, last time. Not hounds. Not fire. Paper."

He walked away into the evening, small and bent, and Jonah sat on the church steps of a dissolved kingdom, in a town that existed only by courtesy of its own erasure, and felt the uncanny ache again — homesickness for a home she'd never seen. Because that was the truth she kept refusing: Cael was not foreign to her. The heraldry on the waymarkers stirred something. The dead kingdom's laws had shaped the valley that shaped her; the purges that made her mother flee into the stones had been Cael's purges, run on Cael's lists. She had been born in a hiding place built by this country's terror, and now she walked its roads like a daughter walking a father's lands, recognizing nothing and belonging to all of it.

The light was failing. Somewhere a bell rang the hour in a tower that leaned to listen. And down the Caelmark road, from the direction she had come — from the valley, from the boundary stones, from the life she had cut loose at dawn — a rider appeared at the far bend, moving fast, and behind him, faint on the wind, her gift caught a scent that did not belong to any kitchen or any road:

Ink. Fresh ink, and oiled leather, and the cold mineral tang of a man who handled other people's names all day and washed his hands after.

A clerk. An inquisitor's clerk, riding the roads into a country that officially was not there, following a trail that officially could not exist.

Jonah rose, took up her bundle, and slipped into the shadow of the leaning bell tower as the hoofbeats grew louder — already learning the first law of the erased places: the best way to survive in a country that doesn't exist is to become someone who was never there.
