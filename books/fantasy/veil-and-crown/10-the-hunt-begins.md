## 10. The Hunt Begins

The plague ledgers were the first thing Serane Voss read every morning, and she read them the way a physician reads a pulse.

She sat in her office above the Hall of Inquiry — a narrow room with one high window that admitted light the color of weak tea — and turned the pages with hands she kept deliberately still. The ledgers recorded the progress of the Affliction: villages where children had been born breathing frost in midsummer; a miller's son in Corrow who had set his father's barn alight by dreaming; a woman in the salt marshes whose touch had cured fevers right up until it stopped curing them and started something else. Forty-one incidents this season. Nine deaths attributable directly to uncontrolled manifestation. Three whole households gone.

To Serane, these were not atrocities to be mourned so much as symptoms to be tracked. She had trained as a physician before the crown had found better use for her, and she had never entirely abandoned the physician's habit of mind: disease was not evil. Disease did not hate you. Disease simply grew where conditions allowed it to grow, and the only moral failure lay in refusing to see it clearly.

The gifted were a condition. The kingdom was a body. And a body that ignored an infection deserved what came to it.

She had said as much once, years ago, to a seminar of new clerks — young men and women with ink-stained fingers and heads full of ballads about persecuted witches. *You are not hunters,* she had told them. *You are quarantiners. The difference matters. A hunter wants his quarry to run; it makes the sport longer. A quarantiner wants the sickness contained before anyone else must suffer.* Some of them had looked at her as though she were monstrous. Most of them had come around within a year. It was remarkable, she thought, how quickly people accepted necessity once they were permitted to stop pretending it was cruelty.

A knock at the door. Her junior clerk, Pellam, entered carrying a leather folio the way one carries a sleeping animal — carefully, and with a certain dread.

"The valley file, Inquisitor. From the provincial archive at Dunmarrow." He set it on her desk and did not immediately let go of it. "It's... older than we thought. There are annotations going back three generations of registrars."

"Then three generations of registrars have been negligent," Serane said, "and I will be reading every word."

Pellam withdrew. She broke the seal on the folio and drew out the contents: a thick sheaf of reports bound in faded cord, census extracts, testimony transcripts, and at the top, a summary sheet in the current crown hand — the new crown, the young king's chancery, all sharp angles and no flourishes.

*Veiled Valley. Population 4,112. Anomalous immunity cluster. Gift incidence reported at zero for ninety consecutive years.*

Ninety years. Serane smiled without warmth. In her experience, there were two explanations for a district reporting zero gift incidence across nine decades. Either the place was a miracle, or it was a hiding place. Miracles, she had found, could be audited.

She began at the beginning, because she always began at the beginning.

* * *

The Veiled Valley had entered the historical record the way a stone enters a shoe: as a persistent irritation. Old Cael, before the Dragon War, had counted the valley among its richest agricultural districts — orchards, barley, river loam black as coffee. Then the war, then the dissolution, then the reorganization, and somewhere in the shuffling of borders the valley acquired its name and its strangeness together. Travelers wrote of it in the way travelers write of anything mildly uncanny: the mist that clung to the boundary stones and would not disperse; the sense, crossing in or out, of passing through cold water; the odd muteness of the place in the crown's own records, as though the clerks' pens had grown tired whenever they approached it.

And then there was the matter of the tasters.

Serane spread the earliest report flat and weighted its corners with her inkwell, her paper knife, her cup, and a smooth river stone she kept for exactly this purpose. The report dated from sixty years back, written by a regional assessor named Halloway whose prose style she found grating but whose diligence she could not fault. He had been investigating a poisoning at a border garrison — arsenic in a stew pot — and had traced the cook's flight toward the valley, expecting to find him sheltering among friends.

He had found instead a community that did not fear poison.

Not a community immune to it — Halloway had been precise about this, and Serane underlined the precision twice. They suffered poisons like anyone else. What they did not have was *the gift*. In every other district of old Cael, the assay was simple: administer a mild toxicant under supervision, observe whether the subject's body neutralized it. The gifted metabolized what would kill an ordinary person. It was the clearest marker known, more reliable than any birthmark or family history, and it was why the crown's screening protocol centered on it.

In the valley, the assay failed. Not refused — *failed*. Subjects tested inside the boundary showed nothing. The same subjects, Halloway noted in a postscript that had evidently cost him some courage, showed everything when tested a mile beyond the stones.

He had written: *It is as if the valley itself drinks the gift out of them. I cannot say whether this is mercy or concealment. I recommend further study.*

Further study had not occurred. Sixty years of files followed, and Serane read them all — the annual censuses with their suspiciously round numbers, the reports of boundary stones "maintained" by valley families in unbroken succession, the single interview with a valley elder who had answered every question with a proverb and been dismissed as a simpleton. She read until the tea-colored light through her window went gray, then lit her lamp and read on.

What emerged from the pages was not a conspiracy, exactly. Conspiracies required coordination, and coordination left fingerprints. This was softer than that. This was a community that had learned, over generations, a single overriding lesson — *do not be interesting* — and had executed it with the discipline of a monastic order. No gifted persons registered. No unusual healings reported. No prodigies, no anomalies, no complaints that might draw an inquisitor's eye. Just barley, orchards, and silence, harvested annually.

Most administrators would have closed the file with a shrug. Whatever the valley was doing, it wasn't hurting anyone.

But Serane Voss had spent eleven years studying how plagues begin, and she knew that the most dangerous reservoirs were always the quiet ones. A fever that burned hot announced itself and was doused. A fever that smoldered — low, controlled, generation after generation — built up a population of carriers who didn't know they carried. And when the smolder finally met dry ground...

She thought of Corrow. The miller's son who burned down his father's barn had been twelve years old, unregistered, untrained, his gift undiagnosed until the night it expressed itself through his dreams. If one competent hedge-witch had identified the boy at six, if he had been brought into training, taught containment, given the structured life the crown offered its registered gifted — the barn would still stand. Perhaps the boy would too. That was the part the balladeers never understood, the part that made her jaw tighten when she heard the word *purge* spoken in taverns with relish. Registration was not punishment. Registration was diagnosis. Diagnosis was the beginning of treatment, and treatment saved lives — the gifted's own lives most of all. Uncontrolled manifestation killed more of the gifted than the crown ever had. She had the numbers. She kept the numbers.

The valley, then, was not a refuge. It was an untreated wound ninety years deep.

She pulled the current census extract toward her and began cross-referencing occupations against the old Halloway protocol. If the valley suppressed the gift, then the only reliable way to find carriers was occupational: which professions, historically, attracted the gifted or required their talents? Poison-tasting, obviously — the crown's own screening had grown out of the observation that royal food-tasters skewed gifted at rates no chance distribution could explain. Healing, midwifery, weather-sense in farmers, fire-watching. She built her list the way she'd once built differential diagnoses: by asking what presentation would look like, in a population doing its best to look like nothing at all.

The valley's roster of licensed food-tasters ran to four names. Three were elderly, long-tenured, thoroughly documented — their assay results from decades past were attached, all negative, all performed *inside* the boundary, and therefore, Serane now understood, worthless.

The fourth name was newer. Younger. A woman, twenty-three years of age, appointed five years ago upon the death of her mentor.

*Jonah Thornbury.*

Serane read the file twice. Apprentice taster from age nine. Mentor: Master Corvin Hale, deceased. Mother: listed in the prior census as *Thornbury, Maren*, occupation taster, deceased. No assay on record for either — or rather, assays existed, both negative, both performed in-valley, both therefore meaningless. The daughter had tasted for the lord's household since her appointment without incident. No anomalies. No reports. Nothing.

Nothing, except that her mother had been a taster before her, and her grandmother — Serane traced the lineage back through the census rolls with a growing, cold satisfaction — had been a taster before *her*. Three generations of poison-tasters in a valley where the gift supposedly did not exist. Three generations of a profession that, throughout the rest of old Cael, ran in the blood of the gifted like a family trade, because it was one: only the gifted survived the apprenticeship, those deliberate micro-doses that trained a normal palate and killed a normal child.

Serane sat back. The lamp hissed softly. Somewhere below, in the Hall, a clerk was stamping documents with the patient rhythm of a heartbeat.

She made herself argue the other side, because she always made herself argue the other side; certainty unexamined was just another kind of infection. Perhaps it was coincidence. Tasting ran in families in ordinary districts too — trades passed parent to child, nothing sinister in it. Perhaps the Thornbury women were simply skilled, careful, lucky.

Three generations of lucky.

She thought of the boy at Corrow again. Twelve years old, and everyone around him — parents, priest, schoolmaster — had looked at the small strangenesses and chosen not to see them, because seeing meant paperwork, and paperwork meant the crown, and the crown meant fear. Fear was the true vector of the disease. Fear made families hide their gifted children until the children's gifts hid themselves no longer. Every tragedy in her ledgers had fear somewhere in its pedigree.

She would not be afraid. That was her whole method, distilled: go in without fear, without hatred, without the theatrical cruelty that made the crown's work hated. Find the truth of the valley gently. Test fairly. Register honestly. If Jonah Thornbury was gifted, she would be offered training, protection, a place in the ordered world — and the valley's ninety-year silence would end not with fire but with light. That was the difference between an inquisitor and a butcher, and Serane Voss intended to be remembered as the former.

She took up her pen and wrote the order in her own hand, because some orders should carry the weight of a signature rather than a stamp:

*To the Provincial Office, Dunmarrow. Re: Veiled Valley compliance review under the Gift Registry Act. Full household interviews to commence with kitchen staff of the lord's residence, priority on food-service personnel. Subject of particular interest: J. Thornbury, taster. Conduct assays beyond the boundary stones only. — S. Voss, Inquisitor.*

She hesitated over the last line before signing — not the words, but the memory behind them. Years ago, her own sister's fever had broken overnight after a visit from a traveling wise-woman whom the local registry later took away. Serane had been fourteen. She had hated the inquisitors then, with the clean, total hatred of a child. She had applied to their academy two years later anyway, because hatred was a luxury for people who could afford to lose someone else next time, and she could not. The wise-woman, she'd learned much later, had lived out her days in comfortable training quarters in the capital, teaching others to control what had nearly killed a village. Saved, not destroyed. That was the story Serane carried like a stone in her boot: it could be done rightly. It *had* been done rightly, once, for someone she loved.

It would be done rightly again.

She signed, sanded the page, and rang for Pellam.

"Dispatch this tonight," she said. "Fast rider. And Pellam—" he paused at the door, folio in arms— "when the interviews begin, I want the taster's wine service observed. Every glass, every pour. Note especially anything she declines to drink herself."

Pellam frowned. "If she's poisoned something, Inquisitor?"

"If she's *gifted*," Serane said, "she'll never once have needed to test her own food. Ninety years of tasters in that valley, and I promise you not one of them has ever flinched at a bitter cup. Immunity leaves a habit of carelessness, Pellam. People forget to pretend."

He nodded, wrote it down, and left. Serane remained at her desk a while longer, the valley file open before her, her lamp burning down. Outside, the city moved through its evening — carts, cries, the smell of bread and horse — ten thousand ordinary lives, none of them knowing how thin the membrane was between order and outbreak.

She touched the page bearing the name *Thornbury*, once, lightly, the way a physician touches a swelling.

"Hold still," she murmured, to the girl in the file, to the valley, to the whole quiet country of hidden things. "This will only hurt if you make it necessary."

Then she closed the folio, trimmed her lamp, and began drafting the questions she would ask herself, in person, when she rode west — because a name flagged in a file was a hypothesis, and Serane Voss had never yet lost an argument with a hypothesis.

She did not know, yet, that she had just begun the longest argument of her life.
