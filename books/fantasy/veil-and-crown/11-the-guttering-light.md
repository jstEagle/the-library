## 11. The Guttering Light

The mountain had a name once. Ember-that-Remains had known it in the days when names were given by fire and answered to fire, when the Ashreach rang with the voices of his kin and every peak had a keeper and every keeper had a purpose written into the stone like veins of ore. He had forgotten the mountain's name. That was the first theft of dying — not the body, which went slowly and could be endured, but the small things first, the way frost takes the tips of branches before it takes the tree.

He had not forgotten his own name. He guarded that the way a miser guards a single coin.

*Ember-that-Remains.*

Not Ember-that-Blazes. Not Ember-that-Ends. His mother had named him with terrible precision, in the last spring of the Dragon War, when the sky over the eastern valleys was black with ash that was not cloud, and she had looked at the single egg left in her clutch and understood exactly what she was naming: what would be left. A remainder. A sum after subtraction.

He had been subtracting from himself for two hundred years now.

* * *

He lay in the caldera of his own making — for dragons do not choose their mountains so much as hollow them, the way a heart hollows a chest — with his chin on his forelegs and his wings folded like a funeral shroud he had been sewing for himself one scale at a time. The fire in him had gone from white to gold to copper to a dull red the color of old brick, and some mornings — if the long dark between his wakings could be called morning — he woke and checked it the way an old man checks whether his hands still close.

They closed. The fire answered. But each answer came slower, and each time it came back smaller, and he had begun, without admitting it, to keep count.

Two hundred years is nothing to a dragon. Two hundred years alone is everything.

He had tried, in the first decades, to fill the silence. He had spoken to the wind, which at least moved. He had spoken to the ravens, who were flattered and stupid and told their cousins, and for a season the peaks around him had been loud with birds come to see the monster, until he realized they came the way people come to look at ruins, and he ate three of them out of spite and felt worse afterward, and the rest learned to give his caldera a wide and reverent berth.

He had spoken to the dead. That was worst of all, because the dead answered in his own voice.

So he stopped speaking. He let the mountain speak instead — the tick and groan of cooling rock, the whisper of snow sliding off the rim, the deep patient respiration of stone under pressure. Stone did not flatter him. Stone did not pity him. Stone simply continued, indifferent, and there was a kind of company in indifference that grief could not offer.

He slept. He woke. He counted his fire. He slept again.

And then, on a night in late autumn, with the stars sharp as nailheads and the snow on the caldera's rim glowing faintly rose-colored from the banked heat of his breath, something struck him across two hundred miles of sleeping country like a bell being rung inside his skull.

* * *

It was not sound. Dragons hear in ranges that make human hearing seem a rumor, and this was below all of it, beneath the mountain itself, a vibration in the deep fabric of things — the same register in which the door-seal hummed its endless note, the register where magic lived when it thought no one was listening.

Ember's eyes opened. Both of them. For the first time in years, both at once, fully, the slit pupils narrowing to blades.

There it was again. Faint. Far south and west, past the drowned forests and the dissolved towns, past the border of the valley that had been veiled before even his mother's mother drew breath — a flicker. A tongue of gift, tasting air it should never have tasted, waking in a body that had spent its whole life holding it under.

He knew what it was because he knew what it smelled like. Every dragon knew. It was the smell of the door-seal — that impossible alloy of iron and intention the old kingdom's masons had folded into the gate in his mountain's flank, the seal that drank magic rather than blocked it, that turned every spell cast at it into fuel for its own keeping. The seal had a scent, and living gifts had a scent, and the two scents were siblings. Cousins. Made by the same hands, in the same war, for the same desperate purpose.

Someone out there had just brushed against the edge of the veil and felt their gift wake like a limb gone numb.

Ember lay very still and listened with the whole cathedral of his body.

The flicker came again — closer now, or stronger, or both — and this time it resolved into something with shape. Not the wild flare of a battle-gift, not the cold architecture of a weather-worker or a binder. This was small. Precise. A taste-shape, a knowing-on-the-tongue, the gift of someone who survived by noticing what was wrong in a cup of wine. A taster's gift. A poison-sense.

He almost laughed, and the laugh became smoke that curled from his nostrils and hung in the freezing air above the caldera like a question mark.

*A taster.* Of all the ten thousand shapes a gift could take, the world's last waking gift was the humblest one. The one that existed only to keep other bodies alive. The one that asked nothing, built nothing, ruled nothing — that stood in kitchens behind kings and swallowed death so that someone else might live another day.

His mother would have said the world still had a sense of humor. She had believed that right up until the day the lances took her, laughing, they said, though Ember had never decided whether to believe the ones who reported it.

The flicker faded. The sleeper — whoever she was; he was certain of *she* the way he was certain of north — had gone back under, back behind the veil, back into the numbness she had probably mistaken for safety her whole life. The bell-note dimmed to a thread, then to a memory of a thread.

But Ember did not sleep again that night.

* * *

In the morning — the gray non-morning of high altitude, where dawn was more a rumor than an event — he hauled himself up the caldera's inner slope, joints cracking like green wood in a fire, and made his way to the place he went perhaps twice a year: the gate.

It sat in the northern flank of his mountain, half-swallowed by the rock that had grown over it in two centuries of patient forgetting, and it was the most beautiful thing Ember had ever seen, which was saying something, because he had seen the hanging gardens of Cael before they burned and the great library at Vael Sarran before the water took it, and he had wept — actually wept, steam and salt — at the vaulting of the Sunspire, which humans had raised in a single generation with no magic at all, only arithmetic and arrogance and rope.

The gate was none of those things. The gate was ugly. It was a slab of black alloy taller than three men and wider than a wagon-road, set into the living rock with no frame, no arch, no ornament, only a keyhole in its center shaped like nothing so much as a mouth about to speak. The masons of the old kingdom had made it in the last year of the Dragon War, when everyone — dragons included — understood that the war was lost and the only question left was who would be allowed to run.

It had been built to let the guilty flee. That was its shame and its genius. No army could use it, because armies move in numbers and the gate opened for one. No king could command it, because it answered to fire — true fire, dragon-fire, the kind that spends life along with light — and no king commanded dragons anymore. There were no dragons to command.

There was one dragon. And he was spending his last coin simply staying alive.

Ember lowered his head until his brow nearly touched the cold metal, and breathed on it, gently, the way one breathes on a dying coal. The gate did not respond. It never responded. It only waited, drinking the faint heat, humming its subterranean note, keeping its counsel the way it had kept it since before he hatched.

"You and I," he rumbled, and his voice came out rusted, "are the last two things in this country that remember what it was."

The gate said nothing. Gates are worse conversationalists than stone.

But today — for the first time in longer than he cared to measure — the gate was not the only thing in the world that shared his register. Somewhere south and west, under a veil that had been drawn to hide a whole valley from a whole kingdom, a girl with a taster's tongue had brushed her gift against the edge of the world, and the world had rung, and he had heard it.

* * *

He spent the following weeks — he was no longer sure they were weeks; the seasons had gone soft-edged on him — in a state he could only have described, if he had still had anyone to describe things to, as *attentive*. He slept less. He ate less, which cost him, because eating was fuel and fuel was fire and fire was the shrinking inheritance he was trying to stretch across whatever remained. He climbed to the caldera's rim each dusk and turned his head south and west and *listened*, the way a deaf man watches a doorway for the face he knows will never come through it.

Most nights: nothing. The veil held. The girl slept inside her numbness, and the kingdom outside her valley went on dissolving and pretending it hadn't, and the crown's new machinery — he could smell that too, on the wind, the particular char-smell of lists being compiled, of names being gathered like dry wood — went on grinding.

Some nights: a flicker. Smaller than the first. Then, once, unmistakably, larger — a night when the note rang clear enough that he caught its color, and the color was fear. She had been frightened awake. Something had come near her, something with authority in it, and her gift had flared like a candle cupped in a sudden draft.

He had roared that night. Actually roared, the first time in forty years, a sound that brought down a cornice of snow three valleys away and sent every living thing within earshot fleeing, and when it was done he stood trembling in the echo of his own voice and understood that he was not roaring at anything in particular.

He was roaring at the loneliness. At two hundred years of it. At the fact that somewhere under the veil was the first creature in two centuries whose blood sang in a register his bones recognized, and she was afraid, and he was a ruin on a mountain, and neither of those facts could be changed by roaring.

After that he was quiet for a long time.

* * *

Winter came down the Ashreach like a verdict. Snow filled the caldera's ledges. His fire sank another shade — copper to rust, rust to ember-red, the red of a hearth at 3 a.m. when everyone has gone to bed and the house is only pretending to be warm. He began to dream, which he had not done in decades, and the dreams were all the same dream: the war's last day, the sky full of falling brothers and sisters, his mother's voice saying *remain* — not as a name, as an instruction — and himself, small and new-hatched in the smoking ruin of the eyrie, doing exactly that. Remaining. For two hundred years. With no one remaining *with*.

He woke from the dream one night to find the bell ringing again.

South and west. Stronger than ever before — strong enough that he could feel its direction the way a compass feels north, strong enough that the snow on the caldera floor around him had melted in a rough circle, though whether from his agitation or the resonance he could not say. And this time the note did not fade. It held. It moved — slowly, laboriously, the way a wounded thing moves — northward. Toward the mountains.

Toward *him*.

Ember stood very still in the falling snow, steam rising off his shoulders in slow columns, and let himself consider, for the first time, the possibility he had forbidden himself for two centuries.

She was coming. Or she might come. A girl with a kitchen-gift and a frightened heart, moving through a dissolved kingdom with the crown's hunters fanning out behind her like a net of knives — and ahead of her, if the old survey fragments still existed anywhere, if any archive had survived the purges, if she was clever enough to read what the masons had left — the gate. The door. The one way out of Cael that did not require passing through Cael.

And the gate wanted fire. True fire. Fire that spends.

He looked down at his own chest, at the dull red glow showing through the gaps between his scales like light through shuttered windows, and did the arithmetic he had been avoiding since the night the bell first rang. There was not much left. Enough to live out his span here, alone, guttering by degrees — a decade, perhaps two, of silence and snow and counting. Or enough, spent all at once, given wholly, to open a door that had never once opened.

One flame. One choice. One remainder, finally deciding what remained *for*.

"Mother," he said to the snow, to the dark, to the two-hundred-year silence, "you named me honestly. Let me spend the name well."

The bell-note in the south pulsed once, faint and stubborn, like a heartbeat heard through a wall. Closer tonight than it had been yesterday. Weary, but unbroken. Still walking.

Ember-that-Remains settled his chin onto his forelegs, faced south and west, and began — for the first time in two hundred years — to wait for someone.

Far below, at the foot of his mountain, the snow lay unbroken across the old war-roads. But roads, he knew, had a way of remembering feet. And somewhere down in the dark of the world, a girl with poison on her tongue and a dead woman's iron key in her pocket was already climbing toward him, though she did not yet know his name, or the gate's price, or what he intended to ask of her before he gave her anything at all.
