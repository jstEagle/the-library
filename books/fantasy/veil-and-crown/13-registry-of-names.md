## 13. Registry of Names

The valley's records arrived in three crates, tied with the flat gray cord of the Office of Inquiry, and Serane Voss opened them the way she did everything: alone, at a clean table, with her sleeves rolled to the elbow.

She had requisitioned them from the Veiled Valley's administrative hall — such as it was. The valley kept its archives in a converted granary that smelled, according to the clerk who'd fetched them, of rye and mouse droppings and something older underneath, something like stone that had been wept on for generations. Voss had not gone herself. She did not go places unless she intended to change them, and the valley was not yet hers to change.

She worked by lamplight in the inquiry house in Marrowgate, the nearest garrison town, where the walls were thick enough that her voice never carried and no one else's ever reached her. The first crate held tax rolls. The second held baptismal registers, marriage lines, burial lists — the ordinary sediment of a small place trying to account for its dead. The third crate she saved for last, because it was labeled in a hand that made her pause: *Kitchen and Household Staff, Current and Former.*

It was the third crate that mattered. She knew it before she cut the cord.

Voss had been an inquisitor for eleven years, and in eleven years she had learned one thing above all others: institutions lie with their mouths but tell the truth with their filing systems. A witness would swear to you the sun rose in the west if you frightened him enough. But a ledger — a ledger only wanted to be accurate. It had no fear in it. It had no hope either, which was better. Hope made people creative. Accuracy made them useful.

So she read the way other people prayed: completely, and without expectation of mercy.

* * *

The kitchen staff rolls went back sixty years, and they were meticulous in the way of small offices run by frightened people. Every taster listed. Every under-cook. Every scullery hand promoted or dismissed or died. And beside certain names, in a faded brown ink that predated the current administration, a small notation: a circle with a line through it. Not a symbol Voss recognized from any official cipher. She copied it into her notebook anyway, because she had learned that the things institutions thought were meaningless were usually the things they'd stopped understanding.

Fourteen names bore the mark across six decades. Fourteen tasters, over sixty years, all marked with the same sign, all — she checked the burial lists against the staff rolls — dying in service, none younger than fifty, none ever dismissed.

That was wrong. Everything about it was wrong. Kitchens chewed through staff the way mills chewed grain; cooks burned out, moved on, married, fled, thieved, drank. Sixty years of turnover in every position except this one, where fourteen people had apparently served until death with the loyalty of monks and the mobility of furniture.

Unless they couldn't leave.

Unless leaving meant something worse than staying.

Voss sat back and let the thought turn over. This was the part of the work she loved — she admitted it to herself now, in the privacy of her own skull, the way a miser admits he counts his coins for pleasure and not prudence. The moment when a document stopped being paper and became a door. Most of her colleagues found the archives dreary. They wanted confessions, drama, the theater of the interrogation chamber. Voss wanted the ledger. The ledger never performed for you. It simply waited, patient as a grave, until you asked it the right question.

The right question here was: *who marked these names, and what did the mark mean?*

She found the answer's shadow two hours past midnight, in a deposition filed nineteen years ago and never once reviewed since. It had been misfiled — deliberately, she suspected — tucked inside a dispute about mill rights, wrapped in the bureaucratic equivalent of darkness. Someone had wanted it lost. Which meant someone had thought it worth hiding.

The deposition was a testimony, sworn before a valley magistrate long dead, by a woman named Wenna of the Hedgerow — no surname recorded, though the record noted she practiced "the gathering arts," which was the valley's gentle phrase for hedge-healing, for herb-craft, for the small illegal magics that flourished anywhere law was too far away to notice.

Wenna's testimony concerned a death. A taster named Maren Cotch, who had served thirty-one years and died, according to the burial list, of "a stoppage of the heart." Wenna disagreed. Wenna, called to lay out the body — healers did that work in poor places, the washing and the closing — testified that Maren Cotch's tongue was black, her throat scarred "like a road map of old burns," and her stomach, when Wenna opened it, full of things no breakfast contained.

*I have laid out four tasters now,* the testimony read, in a hand that pressed hard enough to emboss the next page. *I know what I am looking at. They are fed poison their whole lives, in doses, so the body learns to refuse it. That is not a trade secret. That is a slow murder dressed as an apprenticeship, and every one of them knows it and stays anyway, because outside the valley their gift would wake and the crown would take them. The valley does not protect us from poison, magistrate. It protects the crown from us. We are the proof of our own imprisonment, and we die of it politely.*

The magistrate's marginal note, in ink of a different age: *Struck as speculation. Witness warned.*

Voss read it three times.

Here was the thing about Serane Voss that her superiors had never quite understood, the thing that made them promote her and distrust her in equal measure: she did not hate the gifted. She had never hated them. Hatred required the belief that your enemy was other than you, and Voss had examined herself with the same ruthless honesty she brought to ledgers, and concluded that she was not different from the people she hunted — only luckier in her blood, or unluckier, depending on how you counted. What she believed — what she believed with the whole flat calm of her being — was that the gift was a plague. That it spread along bloodlines and through proximity, that it warped whatever it touched, that unregistered gift-bearing persons were not criminals but *vectors*, and that the registry, the hunt, the purges — all of it was medicine. Brutal medicine. The kind you held a person down for, because you loved them and loved everyone they might infect.

She had watched a gifted man burn a village. She had been nine years old. She remembered the color of the fire, which had been wrong — green at the edges, hungry in a way fire should not be — and she remembered that afterward, the survivors' eyes had the look of people who had seen the bottom of the world. Order was the only wall between that and everywhere. She had built her life into that wall, brick by brick, and she was not ashamed of it.

But she was honest. That was her flaw, in the eyes of her superiors, and her virtue in her own: she followed the evidence even when it walked somewhere uncomfortable. And the evidence in front of her was saying something she did not want to hear.

*They are fed poison their whole lives.* Not as punishment. As *containment*. The valley suppressed magic — that much was doctrine, established fact, the reason the valley had been permitted to exist after the Dragon War while the rest of Cael was dissolved. Inside the boundary stones, the gift slept. Outside, it woke. Which meant a gifted person born in the valley could live and die without ever knowing what they were — unless something woke the gift despite the veil.

Or unless the gift was strong enough to function anyway.

Like an immunity to poison.

Voss pulled the staff roll back toward her and looked at the circled names again. Tasters. Of course tasters. The one kitchen post where the gift's particular shape — a body that refused harm, a tongue that tasted truth in substances — would express itself as competence rather than miracle. The valley hadn't been employing tasters for sixty years. It had been *hiding* them, in plain sight, in the most dangerous job in the palace kitchens, where their survival was itself the disguise.

And one of those circled names — the most recent, the ink barely dry — was *J. Thornbury.*

* * *

She worked until the lamp gutted, then lit another and worked more. Sleep was a tool like any other and she used it sparingly. Toward dawn she found the second thread, and it was finer than the first, and it nearly slipped through her fingers entirely.

A requisition slip. Dated forty-three years ago. Requested: one iron key, cold-forged, no maker's mark. Issued to: the office of the valley steward. Purpose, in the cramped hand of some clerk now dust: *"per standing arrangement."*

No further explanation anywhere in sixty years of records. One key. Standing arrangement. And beneath the requisition, folded so many times it had gone soft as cloth, a scrap of paper that was not part of any file — someone's private note, hidden inside the archive the way a locket holds hair:

*Keys were cut during the War. Nine of them, for the nine doors, though only one door was ever finished. They gave them to the ones meant to live. My grandmother carried hers out of Harrowmere the night it burned. Iron, because iron remembers nothing — no magic will find it, no spell will open it. The door doesn't want power. It wants absence. It wants someone with nothing left to lose.*

There was no signature. There was a small drawn symbol at the bottom, and Voss felt the hair rise on her arms before her mind caught up: a circle with a line through it. The same mark that stood beside fourteen tasters' names across sixty years.

The mark wasn't a dismissal code. It wasn't an administrative notation at all. It was a *flag* — a quiet one, passed hand to hand among people who could not write down what they were. The circle with the line through it said, to anyone who knew how to read it: *this one is ours. This one is hidden. This one must be kept inside the stones.*

Which meant the valley's own records — the crown's own records, gathered by the crown's own clerks — had been carrying a resistance network's membership list for sixty years, invisible to everyone who didn't know the cipher. Invisible to everyone except a woman who read ledgers the way priests read scripture.

Voss set down the page very carefully, as if it might startle.

She was aware, distantly, of the light coming up gray behind the shutters, of the garrison waking below her window, of the ordinary world resuming its noise. None of it touched her. She was deep in the puzzle now, in the place she privately thought of as the clear country — the cold bright landscape at the center of an investigation, where everything connected to everything and the mind moved through it like weather. Her colleagues called her brilliant. Her superiors called her relentless. Neither word was right. What she was, was *in love* — with the architecture of secrets, with the way a truth assembled itself out of paper the way a body assembled itself out of food, cell by patient cell. Other inquisitors broke people. Voss broke *files*, and the files confessed everything, because files had no courage.

Nine keys. One finished door. *They gave them to the ones meant to live.*

Given to whom? Cut during the Dragon War — which meant cut by the old kingdom, dissolved Cael, the country that officially did not exist and whose laws, inconveniently, still did. The old kingdom had known it was losing. Had known the gifted would be hunted after the war, had perhaps *foreseen* the registry, the purges, the machinery she herself now operated. And had prepared a lifeboat. A door out of magic's reach entirely — beyond the veil, beyond the crown, beyond the reach of any registry — and nine cold iron keys to open it, distributed to nine people chosen to survive.

One of those keys, by the testimony of a dead woman's hidden note, had come to the Veiled Valley. Forty-three years ago. Into the keeping of the steward's office.

And the steward's office kept the kitchen staff.

Voss turned back to the staff rolls one final time, and this time she did not look at the circled names. She looked at the margins, the flyleaves, the backs of pages, running her thumb along the bindings the way a locksmith runs a pick. In the back of the third crate's oldest ledger, sewn into the spine itself, she found a pocket of heavy paper, and inside the pocket, a rubbing — charcoal on vellum, the kind taken of an engraving — of a key. Long-shanked, cold-forged, bit cut in a pattern of notches that spelled nothing in any cipher she knew, because it was not meant to be read. It was meant to be *matched*. Somewhere there was a lock shaped exactly like this key's absence, and the two of them together made a sentence that no magic in the world could parse.

On the back of the rubbing, in pencil, in a hand she was beginning to recognize — the same hard-pressing hand as Wenna's testimony, nineteen years older and shakier — a single line:

*If you found this, the girl has it already, or she soon will. Follow the key, not the girl. The girl is only the hand. The key is the inheritance.*

Voss sat for a long time in the gray morning light with the rubbing flat on the table before her, and she did not move, because moving would mean deciding, and deciding would mean choosing between the woman she had been when she opened the crates and the woman she was now.

Because here was the trouble, and she looked at it squarely, as she looked at everything: the puzzle had stopped being abstract. Somewhere in the Veiled Valley there was a girl — a kitchen girl, a taster, marked with a circle-and-line, flagged in her own file just days ago by a clerk who didn't know what he was flagging — carrying, or about to carry, an iron key cut before her grandmother was born, an heirloom of a dead kingdom, a ticket on a lifeboat built for the condemned. And Serane Voss was going to have to find her. That was the work. That was the wall. Every brick of her life said *find her*.

But the hunter in her — the true hunter, the one who lived in the clear country, who loved the architecture more than the arrest — the hunter in her had already begun, treacherously, wonderfully, to admire the design. The hidden cipher. The misfiled testimony. The key sewn into a spine. Sixty years of quiet hands passing silence down like an heirloom, building a fortress out of filing errors. It was beautiful. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever read, and she had read everything.

*Follow the key, not the girl.*

She would do both. She was honest, and honesty meant admitting she would do both.

Voss rolled down her sleeves, buttoned them at the wrist, and rang for the duty clerk. When he came, blinking, she handed him a requisition order already written in her own precise hand: the valley steward's correspondence, forty years complete, and the personnel file of one J. Thornbury, taster, flagged for interview.

"Priority seal," she said. "And clerk — find out where the Thornbury woman is right now. This hour."

The clerk hurried out. Voss stood at the window and watched the sun come up over Marrowgate, and in her breast pocket, over her heart, the charcoal rubbing of the key lay flat and cold as a coin from a country that no longer existed.

Somewhere north, in a valley behind its loving stones, a kitchen girl was about to learn that the net had begun to close — and Voss found, examining herself honestly one more time, that she could not decide whether she hoped the girl would run.

She suspected she already knew the answer. She suspected the girl would run beautifully.

And she suspected, with the cold bright certainty of the clear country, that she would enjoy the chase more than she had ever enjoyed anything in her life — right up until the day it stopped being a chase, and became something else entirely.
