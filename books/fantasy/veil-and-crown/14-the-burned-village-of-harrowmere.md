## 14. The Burned Village of Harrowmere

The road told her before the sign did.

Jonah had learned to read roads the way she had once learned to read kitchens — by smell first, by the small betrayals of texture and temperature second, by the things that were missing last. For two days the road east of the caravan's turning had smelled wrong: not of woodsmoke, which was ordinary and honest, but of ash that had been rained on and dried and rained on again, a sour mineral ghost that clung to the back of the throat like a secret swallowed. Not fresh grief. Old grief, weathered, gone gray.

Then the waymarker appeared at the crossroads, and it was wrong too.

She had been following dead heraldry for a week now — the stag-and-crown of the old royal line carved into stones that no map acknowledged, marking roads that led to towns that no longer existed on paper. But this marker had been defaced. Not broken — defaced. Someone had chiseled out the stag with patient, official strokes, leaving only the crown floating above an empty shield, and beneath it, in fresh paint the color of arterial blood, a single word:

**CLEANSED.**

Jonah stood in the middle of the crossroads for a long time, her pack straps cutting into her shoulders, and made herself say the word silently, once, the way you touch a bruise to learn its shape.

Cleansed. As if the town had been a wound. As if the people in it had been infection.

Harrowmere lay half a mile down the lane, where the land dipped toward what had been a mere before the drainage works of some forgotten duke, and Jonah could have gone around. The caravan master's route bent north at this junction; Odile had said so plainly, the night before, watching her over the fire with those flat merchant's eyes that missed nothing. *There's nothing in Harrowmere, girl. Nothing but what the crown left. You don't want to see what the crown leaves.*

But Jonah had spent her whole life being protected from knowing things. First by the valley, which folded its silence around her like a mother's arm. Then by her own discipline, which had taught her to taste poison without flinching and call it professionalism. She had crossed the boundary stones. She had let color flood back into the world and felt her gift wake in her like a drowned woman surfacing. And she had done all of it on rumor and hope, without once letting herself look directly at the thing she was running from.

She turned down the lane toward Harrowmere.

* * *

The first thing she understood was that the fire had not been an accident of war or weather. Accidental fires are chaotic. They leap where the wind sends them, they spare what chance spares, they leave a ragged edge. This fire had been drawn like a diagram.

Every building on the village green was burned to its foundation stones — every one, precisely, so that the char stopped in clean lines where property boundaries had run. The mill was ash. The chapel was ash. The long low inn with its stable yard was ash. But the granary at the lane's end still stood, untouched, its doors barred with new iron, because grain was taxable and tax did not burn. A crown surveyor's placard hung on the granary door, listing the seizure date, the assessed tonnage, the name of the collecting officer.

The officer's name was Voss. Serane Voss, Inquisitor Second Rank.

Jonah read it twice and found she had memorized it against her will, the way she memorized flavors. Somewhere behind her ribs, a cold little seed took root and began, very quietly, to grow.

She walked into the village the way she imagined you would walk into the inside of a bell that had been struck so hard it broke. Silence here was not absence. It was pressure. The green was soft underfoot with a year's worth of grass already growing through the ash — no, not a year, she corrected herself, looking at the growth, a season, a month and a half — and the grass itself was wrong, too green, too eager, feeding on something in the soil that soil should not have. Things had been burned into that ground that would feed the grass for years.

Chalk marks were everywhere.

She had heard of them — Wenna's voice, low and urgent across a kitchen table, *they mark the doors, girl, chalk and sigil, so everyone knows what happened in the house even after the house is gone* — but hearing and seeing were different countries. Here the houses were gone and the marks remained anyway, painted instead on standing foundation stones, on gateposts, on the lone well housing at the green's center. The sigil was a circle bisected by a vertical line, and beneath each one, in the same red paint as the crossroads, a number.

Not a house number. She understood that when she saw the smallest mark, on a stone barely knee-high, worn smooth by generations of children sitting on it to dangle their feet in the horse trough that no longer existed. Beneath the circle-line sigil, someone had written: **2**.

Two. Two gifted persons registered at this dwelling. Two names on a list. Two lines in a ledger somewhere in a vault beneath a capital she had never seen, and because of those two lines, this stone was all that remained of a home.

Jonah crouched beside it. Her hands, she noticed distantly, were shaking, and she watched them shake with the strange detachment of the taster's training — observe, catalog, do not react until the reaction can be afforded. Her fingers hovered above the chalk-white circle. The chalk itself had been renewed recently, she realized. Rain should have taken it weeks ago. Someone came back. Someone walked through this graveyard of foundations and refreshed the marks, kept the ledger's verdict legible, maintained the sentence after the sentence had been carried out.

That was worse than the burning. Fire, however deliberate, is at least over when it is over. This — this was a country that intended to remember. That had built remembering into its machinery, that employed men to walk the ash and repaint the arithmetic of the dead.

She stood, and her boot crunched, and she looked down.

A child's shoe.

It lay at the edge of the green where the lane met the grass, half-sunk in ash-mud, sole upturned. Small. Leather, good quality, stitched twice at the toe by someone who meant it to be handed down. One shoe. Its pair was nowhere, and Jonah caught herself scanning the ground for it with a desperation that shamed her — as if finding the other shoe would mean something, would balance some equation, would make this one shoe a lost object instead of evidence.

She knew how the shoe had come to be there. That was the horror of a taster's mind, the curse of a profession built on inference: she could not stop herself reconstructing it. A child running. Children ran, in villages, on evenings, toward friends or away from chores, and shoes came off, and mothers scolded. Except this child had run on the night the riders came, or been carried, or been dragged, and the shoe had come off in the mud of the lane, and no one had stopped to pick it up because the people who would have picked it up were the people the fire was for.

The gift woke in her then, unbidden, the way a scar aches before rain. It was not magic in any way the registry would have cared about — it was only her tongue, her nose, her trained and cursed attention — but it rose in her like a tide, tasting the air of Harrowmere whether she willed it or not. Char. Wet stone. Old blood soaked so deep into the chapel foundation that the ground itself remembered it. And underneath, faint as bitter almond in honeyed pears, the residue of something that did not belong to any fire: a mineral tang, cold and precise, that she recognized from Wenna's stories and had never hoped to smell.

Quench-powder. The crown used it to keep purges contained — dusted on rooftops and boundary fences so that no gifted flame, no hedge-healing, no desperate working could spread past the chosen kill-zone. They had not merely burned these people. They had made certain the burning could not be interrupted by anything the victims might carry in their blood.

They had known, Jonah thought, standing very still in the ruined green with the wind moving the too-green grass. They had known some of them would try.

* * *

She found the well because she needed water, and she found the truth of Harrowmere because she needed water and was too honest with herself to pretend otherwise.

The well housing was the only structure on the green still standing whole, and its chalk mark bore the largest number she had yet seen: **11**. Eleven souls marked at a well that served forty households. The arithmetic of it sat in her stomach like a swallowed stone. Eleven in one place meant the list had not been random. It meant someone in Harrowmere had kept count of their neighbors' gifts — or guessed, or informed, or simply been believed when they accused — and the crown's clerks had transcribed suspicion into scripture, and scripture into ash.

The bucket rope was burned through just below the beam, but the chain remained, and Jonah hauled with hands that wanted to tremble and did not quite, because hauling gave them work. The bucket came up dark and sweet-smelling, deep-water cold, and she drank from her cupped palm before she let herself think about it, because thirst is older than fear.

Then she looked down the shaft, and the light fell past the water.

At the bottom, wedged between the wet stones where the waterline met the wall, something pale. Cloth. She told herself it was laundry. She told herself a great many things while her body, with the independent wisdom of a body that had survived twenty years of court kitchens, was already backing away from the wellhead, already turning her face from the shaft, already refusing.

She did not look again. Some knowledge is a door you can choose not to open, and she chose, with the last of her composure, to leave it shut. But she knew. The way she had known about the almonds. The way she always knew. Eleven at the well, and cloth at the waterline, and a village that had been cleansed to its foundations while its granary stood full and taxed.

They had not all died in their beds. Some of them had run to the one place quench-powder couldn't reach and fire hadn't thought to look, and the ones who came after had simply waited, because a well holds water longer than a held breath lasts.

Jonah walked to the far side of the green and was sick into the grass, quietly, thoroughly, with the efficiency of someone who had learned long ago that illness must never interrupt service. When she finished she knelt on the wet ground and stayed there, forehead nearly touching the earth, breathing.

And in that kneeling darkness, something in her changed gear.

It was not rage, though rage was there, banked and enormous, a furnace-room heat she would carry for the rest of her life. It was not even grief, though grief arrived too, sudden and specific — for the child whose shoe lay in the lane, for the eleven at the well, for Wenna back in the valley growing old inside a cage that loved her conditionally, for her mother whose name she had never been allowed to speak aloud in the place where her mother was buried. All of it was present. But beneath it, steadier than any of it, was something colder and more durable, the thing that had let a nine-year-old girl swallow poison on command and smile afterward: understanding.

She understood now what capture meant. Not the scaffold, not the pyre — those were theater, the public grammar of it. Capture meant this: that your neighbors' suspicions became a clerk's ink, and the ink became a number on your door, and the number became powder on the rooftops and riders in the lane, and the whole apparatus ran on rails laid centuries before you were born by men who believed lists were mercy because a listed man at least knows he is hunted. Capture meant that the most dangerous thing about you was a line in a ledger, written in a hand you'd never see, in a room you'd never find, argued by no one, witnessed by no one, true because the crown said it was.

And if that was what capture meant — then the ledger was not a record. The ledger was the weapon. Burn the village and you kill eleven. Burn the ledger and you unmake the machine that kills everywhere, forever, in every village whose numbers have not yet been painted.

The thought arrived whole, the way her detections always did — not reasoned-to but simply *known*, bitter-almond certainty — and Jonah knelt in the ash of Harrowmere and let it become hers.

She was not going to reach the door in the mountains and vanish beyond magic's reach. She had told herself that story for a hundred miles because it was bearable, because survival was a shape she could hold. But survival, she saw now, looking at the child's shoe, was just another word for being the one who lived. The valley had taught her that lesson gently. Harrowmere taught it in ash.

Somewhere south and west, beneath a capital built on top of its own guilt, there was a vault full of names. Hers among them now — she was sure of it; the moment she had crossed the boundary stones and her gift had flared, some clerk's pen had moved. Her mother's name above hers, maybe, in an older hand. Wenna's name coming soon, unless Wenna ran, and Wenna would not run; Wenna would stay in the valley tending her herbs and her sister's memory until the riders came, because that was what Wenna was made of.

You cannot outrun a list, Jonah thought. You can only outlive it, or end it.

* * *

She buried the shoe.

It seemed a small and insufficient thing, and she did it anyway, because no one else was going to. She dug with a shard of roof tile at the base of the defaced waymarker where the lane left the green, in the strip of ground the fire had skipped, and she laid the small stitched leather in the earth with both hands, the way you handle something that might still be warm. She had no words that fit. She offered the only prayer her life had taught her, the taster's benediction, murmured to the dirt:

*I tasted it first. I would have caught it. If I had been here, I would have caught it.*

It was not true — her tongue could not have saved a village from its own government — but lies have uses, and this one held her upright.

Then she went back to the well, because she had decided something else too, and decisions are cheap until they cost water. She cut a length of rope from her pack, weighted it with a stone, and fished the pale cloth up from the waterline rather than leave it in the dark. It was a shift, homespun, small — a woman's or a grown girl's, rotted at the hem, and she wrapped it in her oilcloth without examining it further and carried it to the chapel foundation, where the largest chalk mark stood, and buried it there with a handful of ash from the altar-stone laid on top.

Eleven, the well had said. She could not give them back their names. She did not know their names. But she stood at the chapel stone in the failing light and she spoke aloud, her voice shocking her own ears with its rustiness, the only naming she could manage:

"I'm going to remember this. Not the number. You. Whatever your names were, I'll carry the fact of you, and I'll spend it. I don't know how yet. But I'll spend it."

The wind moved the grass. Nothing answered, and the silence of Harrowmere received her vow the way ash receives rain — absorbing everything, holding nothing, promising nothing. That was enough. Vows made to the dead are not contracts; they are compasses.

* * *

She walked out of Harrowmere in the last light, and at the crossroads she stopped before the defaced waymarker and did something she had been rehearsing in her head since the green.

She took out the piece of charcoal she carried for marking trails, and beneath the red word CLEANSSED — beneath the crown floating over its empty shield — she wrote, in small, level letters that no clerk would think to renew:

**NOT FORGOTTEN.**

It was childish. It was graffiti. It would be scrubbed away within the month by whatever functionary maintained the arithmetic of the dead. She wrote it anyway, because the functionary would have to stand where she stood, read what she read, and know that someone had come after, someone had seen, someone was counting the counters. Fear travels both directions down a road. Let it begin learning that.

Then she shouldered her pack and turned north, toward the Ashreach foothills, where the smugglers' fires would be burning and Odile would be watching the lane with those flat eyes, and beyond the smugglers' camps, higher than any road went, a sealed gate in the bones of the mountains waited for a key that was not iron.

She had come to Harrowmere believing the door was her answer. She left knowing it was only a question — and that somewhere below the capital, in a vault full of ledgers, the real answer was waiting, shelved and indexed, bound in leather, written in ink.

She only had to find a way in.

Behind her, the first stars came out over the ash of Harrowmere, and ahead, far off in the blackening east, the mountains stood up against the sky like a closed hand — and Jonah Thornbury, food-taster, fugitive, witness, walked toward them with a dead child's grave at her back and a living purpose in her mouth, sharp as almond, impossible to spit out.
