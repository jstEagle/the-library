## 15. Odile's Price

The checkpoint appeared the way bad weather does in the Ashreach foothills: first a smudge, then a certainty, then something you had to live inside.

Odile Marchand saw it from the driver's bench before any of her people did, because seeing things first was the whole of her trade and she had been practicing it for forty years. A line of grey tents strung across the river road like beads on a wire, pennants snapping, horses picketed in rows. Crown livery. Not the faded blue of some provincial garrison but the hard new black-and-gold of the Gift Registry Act, the colors that had only existed for a season and already looked like they had always been there.

She did not sigh. Sighing was for women who could afford to feel things at inconvenient moments.

"Roust," she said quietly, to no one, and the word went down the caravan the way water goes down a dry channel — wagon to wagon, hand to hand, until eleven wagons and thirty-one souls had rearranged themselves into their traveling shapes. The bolts of Mirefen wool that were actually bundled letters went under the floorboards of the third wagon. The unlicensed tinctures in the fourth became, by the simple magic of a dropped canvas, part of a shipment of dried apples. And the girl — the kitchen girl, Jonah Thornbury, who had been riding in the grain wagon with her hood up and her hands quiet in her lap for six days — Odile found a place for among the apple barrels, where a small woman with flour on her sleeves would draw no more eyes than flour itself.

It was not kindness. Odile was careful with herself about that. Kindness was expensive and she had never once in her life paid full price for anything.

It was arithmetic. The girl had come to them at the ford below Harrowmere with burned soles and a face like a closed door, and Odile had fed her, because you feed people; that was not negotiable, that was the law beneath all her other laws. But feeding was one thing and sheltering another, and sheltering a marked fugitive through a crown checkpoint was a debt with interest accruing by the hour.

*You'll owe me,* she'd told her, the second night, over the cookfire.

*Name it,* the girl had said, and hadn't flinched, and Odile had thought: *there it is, there's the spine under the flour.*

*I don't name debts in advance,* Odile had said. *That's how you get cheated.*

Now the debt had come due, and it wore black-and-gold, and it was standing in the middle of her road.

* * *

The man who came out to meet the lead wagon was young, which was worse than old. Old men in checkpoints wanted bribes and could be read like large print. Young men wanted to be right.

"Caravan mistress." He had a ledger under his arm and ink on his fingers and the particular hungry neatness of someone recently promoted. "Odile Marchand? Of the Marchand road-train?"

"Depends who's asking, sweetling."

"Sergeant Pell. By authority of the Office of Inquisition, I'm conducting manifest inspections on this stretch." He smiled the way a lock smiles. "New procedures. You'll have heard."

"I've heard the roads are dangerous," Odile said, spreading her hands, letting her rings catch the light — garnets, good ones, chosen this morning for exactly this. "Dangerous roads, dangerous times. What can an old woman carry that's worth a sergeant's trouble?"

"That's what we're going to find out." He flipped his ledger open. "Manifests first. Then names. The Act requires a roster of every soul in your train."

"There are thirty-one of us and half of them can't write their own names."

"Then you'll write for them." He said it pleasantly. He was enjoying this. That was the thing about the new procedures, Odile thought; they had been designed by people who enjoyed them. "And mistress — you should know there's a standing notice. A fugitive, gifted, fled the Veiled Valley some weeks back. Kitchen worker, royal household. There's a reward."

He let it sit there between them, the way you set a coin on a table and watch what the other person's eyes do.

Odile's eyes did nothing. Forty years of practice. She laughed instead, short and rich, and patted the bench beside her as if inviting him to share a joke. "A kitchen girl. Sweetling, look at my train. I've got two cooks, a farrier, three brothers who fight about everything except cards, and a woman who swears she was married to a duke. If I turned away every runaway girl with quick hands, I'd be hauling empty wagons."

"So you're saying you haven't seen her."

"I'm saying I see a hundred faces a week and remember the ones worth remembering."

Sergeant Pell wrote something down. Odile watched him do it and felt the old cold move through her gut, the cold that had nothing to do with the weather off the Ashreach. Because here was the arithmetic again, laid out plain: he would search the wagons. He might be lazy or he might be thorough, and she could not buy thoroughness with garnets alone, and somewhere behind her, under a canvas, among the apple barrels, a girl with a taster's tongue was breathing very slowly and counting her own heartbeats.

There were three ways out of a checkpoint. Bribe, bluff, or bleed. She had used up most of her bluff in the last minute. Her strongbox could maybe buy the sergeant and not his sergeant. Bleed meant blood, and blood meant the whole train marked, and thirty-one souls against however many tents that grey line held.

Which left the fourth way, the way nobody put in the songs because it didn't make a good verse: give them something true.

* * *

They searched for two hours. Odile stood in the cold and supervised the losing of her own property with the serenity of a woman watching rain fall on someone else's field. They took the Mirefen wool — contraband, unstamped, gone, and she mourned it exactly as much as she showed, which was not at all. They took the tinctures after a corporal with clever fingers found the false bottom in the fourth wagon, and that stung, because that had been Wren's work, three weeks of Wren's work, and Wren was going to be insufferable about it for a month.

They did not take the apples apart. The corporal stuck his knife into two barrels, pulled out fruit, bit one, made a face at the tartness, and moved on. Odile watched the kitchen girl's hiding place survive on the strength of a sour apple and thought: *that's the whole world, that is. Everything hanging on whether some fool likes the taste.*

Then Sergeant Pell came back to her bench with his ledger and his ink-stained fingers and his hungry neatness, and he sat down across from her like a man settling in for the real business.

"The wool and the tonics are fines," he said. "You'll pay at the toll table. But the notice stands, mistress. The fugitive. My captain has orders from Inquisitor Voss herself on this one. It's a career-making capture." He leaned forward. "And between us — a woman your age, running goods through a war country, you must know how these things go. Information is lighter than contraband. It doesn't get fined. It gets *paid* for."

There it was. He'd walked himself right up to her door and knocked.

Odile looked at him for a long moment. She let her face do what her face was famous for doing along three hundred miles of river road: soften. Grow tired. Grow honest. Every smuggler learns to lie with the body before the mouth, and Odile Marchand had been lying with her body since before this sergeant was weaned, but what she felt underneath the performance was not performance, and that was the strange part, the part she would examine later, at night, with a cup in her hand and no one to perform for.

Because she was going to sell the girl. That decision had already been made, somewhere below thought, in the place where mothers keep the instinct that outranks love. Thirty-one souls against one. A train full of families against a stranger with a marked tongue. Any matriarch worth her salt made that trade with both hands and slept after.

So she sighed, and lowered her voice, and gave Sergeant Pell what he wanted.

"She boarded us below Harrowmere," Odile said. "Burned feet. Wouldn't say her name, but I've hauled enough runaways to know the type when they sit down and don't get up. Kept to herself. Never touched wine — never once, six days on the road, and a girl who won't touch wine is a girl who knows what's in it, if you follow me." She watched him write, watched the hook go in clean. "Paid her passage with work. Kitchen work. She's good at it. Too good."

Pell's pen had stopped. He was looking at her with the naked shine of a man being handed his future. "Where?"

"Now that," Odile said, "is the part that costs."

* * *

She named a price that made him blanch and then haggle, and they settled where she had known they would settle, because she had picked the number while he was still walking toward her wagon. Half in coin, half in a stamped pass — a genuine one, wax and ribbon and all, good for the whole train on this stretch of road and the next. With the pass, the wool she could replace. Without it, the train crawled from checkpoint to checkpoint like a wounded thing, bleeding fines at every grey tent between here and the coast.

The coin went into her strongbox. The pass went into her bodice, against her heart, where the important papers lived.

And then Odile Marchand told Sergeant Pell, in a voice pitched low and urgent with the shame of a woman betraying a guest, exactly where the kitchen girl had gone.

"She jumped ship at the split in the river road," she said. "Two nights back. We woke and she was gone, and I'll tell you why, sergeant, because you seem a straight sort and I don't sell liars. She heard my outriders talking. About the notice. About the reward. She knew I'd have to make a choice, and she spared me making it. Walked off into the hills with nothing but what she carried." Odile shook her head, and the grief in it was real, which was the trick of it, the terrible efficient trick of it: every word true except the direction. "South fork. Toward the ferry at Coldwell. If I were hunting her, that's where I'd stand. A girl with burned feet and no horse needs a ferry."

Every word true except the direction. The girl had gone nowhere near the south fork. The girl was asleep under a canvas twenty feet away, among the apples, and would ride out of this checkpoint inside the hour wearing a pass bought with her own description.

Odile had done this kind of thing before — sold truth bent just enough to spend like coin — and she had always told herself it was the same as honesty, near enough, honest-adjacent, the way tin is the same as silver if you never bite it. But sitting there watching Pell signal to his riders, watching four of them wheel and trot south toward Coldwell and a ferry where no one waited, she felt the difference in her chest like a cracked rib. Tin bends. Silver sings. And somewhere in her, faint and unmistakable, something was singing.

Because here was the thing she would not say aloud, not to Wren, not to the dark, not to the cup in her hand at midnight: she had liked the girl. Six days, and the girl had worked without complaint, had fixed the porridge — *fixed the porridge*, which no one had managed in nine years, some trick with salt and timing — and had sat with little Marta in the evenings teaching her letters in the dirt with a stick, patient as rain. And Odile had caught herself, twice, three times, thinking in the shape of a future that included her. *When we reach the coast, the girl could—* and then stopped herself, because futures with strangers in them are how matriarchs die.

She had fed her. That was the law beneath the laws. And now she had sold her — sold her *true*, sold her with such convincing detail that Pell would report it up the chain, and the report would go to whoever reported to Inquisitor Voss, and somewhere a file would grow thicker by one page, and the page would say *south fork, Coldwell ferry*, and the page would be wrong, and the wrongness would keep a girl alive.

Guilt worn like a coat. That was the phrase that came to her, sitting there in the cold with the pass warm against her heart. Not guilt carried, not guilt borne — guilt *worn*, put on in the morning with everything else, buttoned to the throat, taken out into the weather. A coat keeps you alive too. She refused to take it off. Taking it off would mean admitting she needed it, and needing it would mean admitting what she'd done required forgiveness, and Odile Marchand had never in her life paid full price for anything, and forgiveness was the one commodity she suspected cost everything.

* * *

The train rolled out an hour past noon, the pass working like a charm, the grey tents sliding past on the left, a bored sentry waving them through with barely a glance at the wax seal. Odile rode the lead bench and did not look back at the checkpoint, and did not look back at the covered wagon where a girl among the apple barrels was surely listening to every hoofbeat and doing her own arithmetic about what had just been purchased above her head.

Wren drew level with the bench a mile on, walking beside the wheels, rubbing his chapped hands. "Coldwell," he said, too low for the others. "You sent them to Coldwell."

"Ferry's slow this time of year," Odile said. "Ice on the landings. They'll stand around being important for three days."

"And when they come back empty?"

"Then they come back empty, and they blame the ice, and we're eighty miles gone." She glanced at him. "Say what you're going to say, Wren."

He was quiet for a few strides. "Marta asked me this morning if the kitchen lady was coming to the coast with us." His voice did something complicated. "I didn't know what to tell her."

"Tell her the truth when she's older and a pretty lie until then. That's parenting." But her voice came out flatter than she intended, and Wren heard it, because Wren always heard everything, curse him, and he squeezed her shoulder once and dropped back to his wagon and left her alone with the reins and the road.

The road ran north and east, toward the high passes, toward the Ashreach, toward whatever it was the girl wasn't telling them she was looking for. Because Odile had known that too, of course — known it the way she knew everything, from small things added patiently together. The girl studied maps by firelight when she thought everyone slept. She asked questions about the mountains in a voice carefully sanded free of hunger. She had looked at the Ashreach the way Odile had once looked at the sea, before she'd learned that wanting things was a tax you paid forever.

Let her look. Let her want. Odile would carry her as far as the foothills, because a debt unnamed was still a debt, and because the coat was warm, and because — she admitted it now, in the privacy of her own skull, where she kept the only honest ledger she owned — because the porridge had been very good, and because a girl who teaches letters in the dirt to a child who isn't hers is not the plague the criers promised. She was just somebody's daughter, running.

But the foothills were the end of it. Odile had decided that too, in the same secret ledger, in the same ink. At the last camp before the high country she would wake the girl in the dark, press bread and cheese and a skin of water into her hands, point at the mountains, and say the only benediction her trade allowed:

*From here, sweetling, you're nobody's cargo. God walk with you, because I can't.*

And if the crown's riders took her in the passes, well. Odile would wear that too. She had the shoulders for it.

Behind them, far to the south, four riders splashed across a ferry landing and found nothing but ice and gulls — and the report they carried back would travel, as reports do, upward, page by page, toward a desk where a woman with careful eyes kept files on everyone, including, soon enough, a certain caravan mistress whose story had been almost perfect.

Almost.
