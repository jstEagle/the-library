## 16. The Door in the Mountain

The fragments said *three days past the last waymarker*, and the last waymarker had been lying on its side in the heather for so long that lichen had eaten the heraldry whole. Jonah had spent an hour kneeling over it anyway, tracing the worn grooves with her fingertips like a blind woman reading a face, because it was the first thing in weeks that had pointed anywhere at all.

*Three days past.* She had been walking five.

The Ashreach foothills did not look like a place where doors lived. They looked like a place where doors went to be forgotten — grey slopes stitched with scree, wind coming off the high snowfields with a smell of nothing at all, not even stone. No birds this far up. No smoke from any chimney. The road she'd followed out of the drowned valley country had thinned to a track, then to a suggestion, then to nothing but her own stubbornness and the oilskin packet of papers Brother Cassian had pressed into her hands at the monastery gate.

She had read them so many times the creases had gone soft as cloth.

*Survey fragment, 3rd Regiment of Sappers, year 812 of the old reckoning.* The handwriting was a soldier's — fast, square, indifferent to beauty. *Gate confirmed at the head of the Greywater cut. Masonry intact. Lock mechanism unlike any in the crown's inventory; sapper Vell requests permission to attempt breach. Permission denied by order of —* and there the paper was burned away, a neat scorch the width of a thumb, as if someone long dead had decided exactly how much history the future deserved.

Jonah had learned to love these papers the way you love a person who tells you only half the truth: completely, and with a low constant anger.

She climbed.

By midday the wind had found the gap between her collar and her neck and made a home there. By late afternoon her boots had begun to speak to her — a wet complaint in the left one, a loosening heel in the right — and she let them, because listening to small miseries was easier than listening to the large one, which was this: that she did not know what she would do if the door opened, and did not know what she would do if it didn't, and both of those not-knowings were starting to feel like the same shape.

Behind her, somewhere south and east and days away, Serane Voss was reading her name. She could feel it sometimes, that reading — not magic, never magic, the valley had wrung that superstition out of her early — just imagination, working its old cruelty. A woman with neat handwriting drawing a line through a map toward a kitchen girl who had run. Jonah walked faster when she felt it. The wind took her breath and gave back cold.

It was near dusk, with the light going copper on the snowline, that she came around the shoulder of the mountain and saw the cut.

Cassian's map had called it the Greywater cut, and there was indeed water — a thin stream threading down from the heights through a gash in the rock — but it wasn't grey, it was the color of old glass, and it ran so quietly it seemed embarrassed to exist. The walls of the cut rose sheer on either side, close enough in places that she could have touched both with outstretched arms, and the floor was smooth-worn stone, polished by ten thousand years of patient water.

And at the head of it, where the stream spilled out from under the mountain itself, stood the door.

Jonah stopped walking. Her body simply declined to continue, some animal committee in her legs voting unanimously for stillness, and for a long moment she stood in the failing light and looked at the thing she had crossed a kingdom of ash and checkpoints and burned villages to find.

It was smaller than she had imagined. That was the first absurdity. In her head the door out of Cael had been vast — a gate for armies, for exoduses, for whole peoples carrying their gods on their backs. This was a door for one person. Maybe two, if they were friends. It was set directly into the living rock beneath the stream's source, black stone shot through with veins of something that caught the last light and held it — not gold, not silver, something warmer, ember-colored, as if the mountain had a hearth behind its ribs. The frame was carved with writing in no alphabet she knew, characters like flames frozen mid-flicker, running up one side and down the other. And in the center, where a handle should have been, was a recess shaped like nothing she had ever seen: a shallow bowl of dark metal, wide as her two hands cupped together, empty.

No keyhole. No lock. No hinges she could find, though the door sat flush in its frame with a precision that made her mason's-eye ache.

"Well," she said aloud, because the silence was enormous and her voice was the only tool she had left for measuring it. "You're smaller than they said."

The door said nothing. Doors, in her experience, rarely opened with pleasantries, but she had been alone long enough that courtesy to objects had begun to seem reasonable.

She made camp in the lee of the cut's western wall, out of the worst of the wind, and allowed herself the extravagance of a fire — small, shielded, fed with the twisted dwarf-pine she'd gathered on the climb. While the water boiled she laid out Cassian's papers again, all of them, weighting the corners with stones, and read them by firelight as if a new reading might yield new truths.

The second fragment was older than the first, the ink browned to sepia. *Petition of the Guild of Doorwrights, year 791.* Most of it was guild politics — fees, apprenticeships, a bitter dispute over who owned the secret of *fire-tempered bronze* — but one line stopped her the way it always stopped her, though she had read it a dozen times:

*Let it be recorded that the Gate of Going-Forth is keyed to flame alone, as was agreed at the Founding, iron being the metal of men and flame being the metal of the world.*

Keyed to flame alone.

She had known that. She had known it since the monastery, since Cassian's shaking hands and his archive full of careful, damning lists. She had known it and she had walked here anyway, because knowing a thing and believing it are different countries, and hope issues passports freely across the border.

But she took out Wenna's key anyway.

It lay in the inner pocket where it had ridden against her heart since the night Wenna pressed it into her palm — a bar of cold iron, longer than her hand, unadorned except for a single groove worn into one edge, as if a thousand thumbs had worried it in the dark. *There's no lock for it,* Wenna had said. *Not anymore. But the ones who carried them lived, girl. Remember that. The ones who carried them lived.*

Jonah knelt before the door and fit the key into the bowl-shaped recess.

It didn't fit. Of course it didn't fit — the recess was a shallow dish, not a socket — but she turned it anyway, pressed it, scraped its edges against the dark metal, half-expecting, half-dreading, some click of ancient machinery waking. Nothing. The iron sat in the bowl like a bone in an empty socket, wrong and mute. When she pulled it free, the metal of the recess was warm. Not sun-warm. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, from the inside, and she snatched her hand back and then, furious at herself for flinching, put it flat against the door itself.

The door was warm too. All of it. The whole black slab breathed a faint heat into her palm, steady as a pulse, and beneath her fingertips — or perhaps only beneath her imagination, she could no longer tell — something almost like rhythm.

"You want fire," she said quietly. "I know. Everyone's records agree. You want fire."

The wind came down the cut and guttered her little campfire and she had to go and coax it back to life, crouching over the embers with her cupped hands, and while she crouched she thought about fire, and about what she had, and about the arithmetic of the thing, which was brutal and simple and had been waiting for her at the end of every mile.

She had no fire. Not the kind the door wanted. She had a tinderbox and a cook-fire and a taster's trained tongue, and none of those opened mountains. What she had instead was a rumor she had been carrying since the smugglers' wagons, since Odile's firelit table, since before that maybe, since the monastery: that in the high caldera above the Greywater's source, in the last of the Ashreach peaks, something old still lived. Something that was mostly fire, and mostly dying.

Ember-that-Remains, the caravan folk called it, when they called it anything at all, in the lowered voices people use for things they don't believe in but aren't willing to bet against.

Jonah sat back on her heels and looked at the door, and the door glowed faintly in the last light, ember-veined, patient, and she understood — with the clean, sick clarity of a taster identifying poison in the first spoonful — that the door was not locked against her. It was waiting for her. It had been waiting, perhaps, for a very long time, the way a trap waits, the way a debt waits. *Hope arriving and immediately demanding more than she has.* She had thought that phrase in the foothills without knowing she was composing her own epitaph.

Because here was the arithmetic. There was fire in the caldera. There was a dragon up there, if the rumors were true, and a dragon's flame could open any door ever built by doorwrights — that much the archives agreed on, in their scorched, partial way. And dragons, the same archives whispered, in margins and marginal languages, do not give their fire. Fire is life to them, is literally life, each flame a season of their burning hearts spent once and never recovered. To ask a dragon for fire was to ask it to die a little, or wholly, for you.

To ask a stranger to die for you.

"I haven't asked yet," she told the fire. It crackled, noncommittal. "I've only climbed."

But she knew herself well enough by now to know how the asking would go. She had spent her whole life being the person who noticed the bitter almond and said nothing, who swallowed the poison and smiled, who kept other people alive at cost to herself and called it a job. People like her did not ask dragons to die. People like her died quietly in kitchens so that kings could eat pears in peace. If she climbed that mountain, she would stand in front of whatever waited there, and it would look at her with whatever eyes it had, and she would open her mouth, and what came out would be *no, forgive me, I'll find another way*, and she would mean it, and she would walk back down the mountain, and the door would stay shut, and Serane Voss would find her eventually in some valley or some kitchen, because there was no other way. There had never been another way. That was what the door was. It was the absence of other ways, rendered in black stone and warm metal.

Unless.

She banked the fire and lay down in her blanket with her boots on, the way she'd learned to sleep on the road — ready to run, never fully off duty — and stared up at the stars, which this close to the snowline were viciously clear, cold points in a sky that had never heard of kingdoms.

Her mother's voice arrived, as it did on nights like this, worn soft by years of repetition: *the valley forgets you if you let it.* And underneath it, newer, Wenna's voice: *the ones who carried them lived.* And underneath that, oldest of all, a voice she had never actually heard, her grandmother's, reaching her only through the dragon-rumors and the archive pages and Wenna's stories — a woman whose name Ember-that-Remains was said to remember, though how a rumor could know such a thing Jonah had never asked.

Three generations of women telling her, in three different grammars, the same sentence: *survive, and carry something forward.*

She slept badly, and dreamed of ledgers burning, which was strange, because she had never seen a ledger burn. She dreamed pages of names curling at the edges, going brown, going black, lifting off the shelves like startled birds, and in the dream she was holding a torch she did not remember lighting, and her hands did not shake, and someone enormous watched her from the smoke with eyes like the last coals of a fire that had once warmed a whole world.

*

Dawn came up the colour of pewter, and with it a decision she had made sometime in the night without consulting herself.

She packed slowly, deliberately, making each motion a small ceremony: the oilskin papers wrapped and stowed, the tinderbox checked, the last of the smoked fish from Odile's stores divided into tomorrow's portion and today's. She filled her waterskin from the glass-clear stream and drank, and the water tasted of snowmelt and stone and, very faintly, of warmth — the door's warmth, leached into the current over centuries, the mountain breathing through its water. Her tongue, her treacherous gifted tongue, catalogued it all without being asked: *clean, mineral, safe.* Some habits were not burdens. Some were the only inheritance she liked.

Then she stood before the door one last time.

She put her palm flat against the black stone. It pulsed its slow interior heat against her skin, patient as ever, and she leaned close, close enough that her breath fogged the warm surface, and spoke to it the way she had once spoken to poisoned dishes and locked pantry doors and the whole machinery of a court that wanted her silent: clearly, and without hope of answer, and meaning every word.

"I'm going to find your fire," she said. "I don't know what it will cost, and I don't know who pays. But I'm done standing outside doors."

The vein-work in the stone seemed, in that moment, in that light, to brighten — one thread of ember-colour kindling along the doorframe like a fuse considering its options — and then it faded, and she told herself it had been the sunrise finding the cut, and she picked up her pack.

The path to the caldera went up. Of course it went up. Everything in her life, lately, went up: out of valleys, out of cellars, out of the low green country of safety into the thin cold air where the truth lived. The stream showed her the way, climbing beside it as it shrank from river to rill to trickle to a seep in the rocks, and the air grew colder and thinner and began, very gradually, to change.

She smelled it before she understood it. Faint, at first — a thread of sulphur braided into the snow-smell, and under the sulphur something else, something older, char and hot iron and a sweetness like resin burning in a distant room. Her tongue woke fully for the first time in days, tasting the air in long involuntary pulls, and what it told her made the fine hairs rise along her arms.

*Fire,* it said. *Old fire. Living fire. Fire that has been burning in one place for longer than your kingdom has existed.*

The trail steepened. The dwarf-pine gave out. She climbed through fields of black glassy rock that clicked and rang under her boots like broken bells, and the sulphur thickened, and steam began to rise from vents in the ground, white plumes that streamed sideways in the wind like banners fleeing a battle. Twice the ground trembled under her, a long subterranean sigh, and twice she stopped and waited it out with her hand flat on the warm stone, the way you'd calm a horse.

Near midday she crested a ridge of slag and looked down into the caldera, and forgot, entirely, how to breathe.

It was a bowl of ash perhaps half a mile across, ringed with black crags, and the floor of it was veined with slow-moving rivers of dull red light, like the door's ember-threads writ large — the whole basin glowing faintly, breathing heat, and at its center, coiled among the cooling flows like a ship wrecked in a harbour of fire, lay the dragon.

He was vast and he was ruined. His scales, which must once have been the colour of the door's warm veins — of sunset, of forge-light, of every fire she had ever cooked over — were grey now, ashed-over, cracked in places to show a dull glow beneath like coals under cinders. One wing lay folded at an angle that spoke of old breaks badly healed. His sides moved with a slowness that frightened her more than size or teeth would have: the breathing of a thing conserving everything, spending nothing, a miser of breath at the end of an empty treasury.

And as she stood on the ridge, pack straps cutting her shoulders, wind pulling at her hair, the dragon's head lifted.

It took a long time. The neck unfolded joint by joint, carefully, the way an old man rises from a chair he's sat in too long, and the head swung toward her across fifty yards of smoking ash, and the eyes that found her were deep-set and lidless and lit from within, banked coals, the last light of a fire the world had forgotten to tend.

The great nostrils flared. Drew in her scent. Held it.

And then the dragon spoke, and his voice was the sound a hearth makes when it settles, deep and cracking and impossibly gentle, and the first words he said to Jonah Thornbury, taster, fugitive, carrier of a key with no lock, were not a challenge or a warning at all.

They were a name.

"Thornbury," rumbled Ember-that-Remains, in a voice like embers remembering a blaze. "I know that name. I knew it before you were born."
