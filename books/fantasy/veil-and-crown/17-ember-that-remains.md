## 17. Ember-that-Remains

The name landed on Jonah like a hand laid flat between her shoulder blades — not striking her, but holding her still.

"You knew it," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended, scraped thin by the climb and the sulfur air and the sheer impossible fact of standing before a living dragon in the caldera of the Ashreach mountains. "How could you know it? I'm nobody. I'm a kitchen girl from a valley that isn't even on the maps anymore."

The dragon's head was enormous — larger than the taster's cottage she'd grown up in, larger than the whole kitchen of the steward's residence where she had spent nine years pretending to be only what she appeared to be. Scales the color of banked coals covered him, dull and cracked in places, and through those cracks glowed a faint light, like the last heat of a fire everyone else had given up on. He was dying. She had known it before she saw him; the mountain itself had told her, the way a house tells you when someone inside has stopped moving. The Ashreach were warm the way a body is warm after the fever breaks and the warmth is only what's left behind.

"I knew a Thornbury," the dragon said. Each word arrived slowly, the way stones settle into a riverbed. "A woman with quick hands and a quicker tongue. She came to me when the war was ending, when the sky over Cael was full of smoke and the ground was full of men with nets and iron. She was not afraid of me." A sound moved through his vast chest, and it took Jonah a long moment to understand it was laughter — or something like laughter, dry as paper catching. "Almost no one who came to me then was not afraid. But she climbed up my foreleg like a girl climbing an apple tree and looked into this eye" — the left eye, vast and amber, its pupil a vertical seam of black — "and she said, *You're smaller than the stories say.*"

Jonah's breath went out of her.

The wind at the caldera's rim pulled at her hair, carried the smell of hot stone and old ash and something underneath it all, faint but unmistakable: bitter almond. Not poison here, not exactly. The mountain breathed it the way other places breathed pine. Her gift, awake now for weeks outside the valley's veil, read the air the way her tongue read a dish — automatically, helplessly, everything labeled and sorted whether she wanted it or not. And beneath the sulfur and the stone, beneath the dragon's own smolder, she tasted something that made her knees soften.

She tasted kinship. Old blood. The same faint mineral sweetness she had once, as a child, caught on her grandmother's hands after the old woman came down from the hills with her baskets of herbs no one else dared gather.

"She was my grandmother," Jonah whispered.

"I know," said Ember-that-Remains.

* * *

She did not remember sitting down. But she found herself on a shelf of black rock at the caldera's edge, her legs folded under her, her pack still strapped to her back because some stubborn part of her refused to set it down even now, even here. Habit of the hunted. You never put down what you might need to run with.

The dragon lowered his head until it hung above her, close enough that the heat of him pressed against her face like an open oven door. This near, she could see how bad it was. The glow beneath his scales flickered unevenly, guttering in places, flaring weakly in others, and the great nostrils whistled faintly on each inhale, as though something inside him had partially collapsed. His eyes were the worst part. They were not dim. They were terribly, heartbreakingly clear — the eyes of a mind still whole watching its own house burn down around it room by room.

"You came here to die," he observed. It was not an accusation. If anything, there was a terrible gentleness in it, the gentleness of one dying creature recognizing another's despair.

"No," Jonah started to say, and then stopped, because it would have been a lie, and lying to this creature felt like lying to a hearth. "I don't know. Maybe. I came here because there was nowhere else." She looked out across the caldera, at the far wall rising in terraces of black glass, at the thin plume of steam rising from a vent like a soul deciding whether to leave. "There's a door. In the foothills below. The archives say it leads out of Cael — out of everything, past the reach of any crown or any registry. It wouldn't open for my key. The archivist thought—" She swallowed. "He thought it needed fire. Dragon-fire. Your fire."

"And so you climbed to ask a dying man for his last breath."

"Yes," she said. And then, because the altitude of honesty up here seemed to demand it: "And part of me thought if you refused, or if the climb killed me first, that would be an answer too. An easier one."

The dragon was quiet for a long time. The wind moved. Somewhere below them, deep in the mountain's throat, something shifted with a groan of settling stone.

"When your grandmother stood where you are standing," he said at last, "she did not ask me for anything. She brought me a gift. A jar of honey, of all things. She said the bees in the low country made it from clover, and that a creature who lived on ash should taste something sweet before the world finished forgetting us." His eye closed, opened. "I have not forgotten the honey. Everything else from that year is going, piece by piece, the way a shore gives way. Names go first. Then faces. Then the reasons. But the honey stays. Isn't that a strange thing? That a mind holds hardest to the smallest kindnesses."

"What was her name?" Jonah asked. "Her true name. My mother never spoke it. She said names were dangerous after the war, that our family learned to wear them lightly."

"Maren," said the dragon, and the name came out of him like a coal rolled gently from a fire — glowing, careful, set down where it could be seen. "Maren Thornbury. Hedge-witch, herb-cunning, unregistered by any crown because she was too clever to be counted and too kind to run farther than she had to." A pause. "She died in the second purge. I felt it. Do you know what that is, little taster — to feel a small light go out across a whole kingdom, one among hundreds, and to know that yours will be the only grief it receives?"

Jonah's eyes burned. She thought of Wenna reciting names over graves. She thought of her mother's hands kneading bread, always flour-dusted, always moving, as though stillness were a luxury their bloodline couldn't afford. She thought of the chalk marks on the doors of Harrowmere, and the child's shoe in the ash, and the registry ledgers waiting beneath the capital with her mother's name written somewhere in a fair clerk's hand, filed like a debt.

"I'm starting to," she said.

* * *

He told her stories while the light changed overhead, the caldera's rim cutting the sky into a shrinking circle of late-afternoon gold. He spoke the way old men speak when they have finally found someone willing to listen — without order, without hurry, following the shape of memory rather than the line of events.

He told her about the Dragon War, which was not, he insisted, called that by dragons. "We called it the War of Lists," he said, and Jonah's spine went cold. "Your kind fought us with swords at first, and we barely noticed. Swords we understood. It was the lists that killed us. Men with careful handwriting, going village to village, writing down who fed us, who healed us, who sheltered our eggs, who simply spoke kindly of us. The lists grew for years before the first soldier marched. By the time the burning started, every name had already been entered, weighed, and sentenced. The killing was just clerical work by then."

"The registry," Jonah whispered. "It didn't start with the gifted. It started with you."

"It starts wherever power needs a reason," said Ember-that-Remains. "The reason changes. The list does not." He shifted, and the movement cost him — she heard it in the catch of his breath, saw the flare of pain-light ripple along his flank. When he settled again, his voice was lower. "Your grandmother understood. That's why she came. Not to beg protection — she knew I had none left to give. She came to tell me that her family would keep the remembering. That as long as one Thornbury drew breath, someone would know the difference between a beast and a neighbor. She said—" and here the dragon's voice roughened, embers collapsing, "—she said, *They will call you monster because monsters can be hunted without shame. So we will call you by your name instead.*"

Jonah wrapped her arms around her knees. Above them the circle of sky had gone the color of a bruise healing, purple shading into rose.

"Ember-that-Remains," she said. "Is that your name? Truly?"

"It is the name I was left with. My true name was spoken only twice in my life — once by my mother at my hatching, once by the last of my clutchmates before she fell. A dragon's true name is not a label, little taster. It is a summary. A whole life folded into a single sound. When everyone who could pronounce it is gone..." He let the sentence die the way his fire was dying, quietly, without drama. "What remains is what people called you. So: Ember-that-Remains. It is accurate. It is not kind."

"Can I—" Jonah hesitated, then made herself finish. "Can I say your name? Would that mean anything, coming from me?"

The great head turned. The amber eye regarded her, and within it she saw reflected a small figure on a black rock — herself, absurdly slight, wrapped in a road-dusted cloak, and she saw something else in the dragon's gaze too, something that loosened the last knot of fear in her chest. Recognition. Not of her face. Of her posture. Of the way she sat listening, hungry for story, guarding her questions the way her grandmother must once have guarded hers.

"It would mean," he said slowly, "that the list they kept of my enemies has, at the very end of it, one friend."

* * *

Night came fully, and with it the cold of the high mountains, but the caldera held its warmth like a cupped hand. Jonah ate — dried meat, a heel of dark bread Odile's caravan had given her, snowmelt from her skin — and was startled to find the dragon watching her eat with unmistakable interest.

"Honey," he said wistfully. "Clover honey. Sixty years, and I am reduced to coveting a dead woman's granddaughter's rations."

She laughed — actually laughed, a short, cracked, astonished sound that seemed to bounce strangely off the black glass walls, as though the caldera weren't used to it. When had she last laughed? On the barge, maybe. Before that — she couldn't find it. Fear eats appetite, her mentor had taught her, but it eats joy faster.

"I don't have honey," she said. "But next spring, if the low-country bees do their work—" She stopped. Next spring was a country she had stopped believing in somewhere around Harrowmere. Saying it aloud felt like stepping onto ice.

"If," agreed the dragon, without pity. "You carry a key with no lock. You understand conditional promises better than most."

"How do you know about the key?"

"I can smell it. Iron cut during the War of Lists carries the maker's intent the way smoked meat carries the smoke. Yours was forged by someone who intended survival — not escape, mind you. Survival. There's a difference. Escape is running from a thing. Survival is carrying a thing forward." His eye half-lidded. "Who gave it to you?"

"A healer named Wenna. In the valley. Her sister was taken in the last purge." Jonah's hand went to the key at her throat without her permission, the way a tongue returns to a broken tooth. "She said it opens nothing she ever found. She gave it to me anyway. Said heirlooms aren't for opening things. They're for knowing you were expected."

The dragon was silent for so long that Jonah thought he had drifted into sleep. Then, very softly, in a voice like the last coals being raked together:

"Bring it here."

She rose. Her legs were stiff and her knees protested and none of it mattered; she crossed the black glass floor of the caldera with the strange ceremony of someone approaching an altar, and when she stood beneath the dragon's chin, he inclined his head further, further, until his snout nearly touched the stone before her feet. His breath washed over her, hot and smelling of deep places.

With trembling fingers, Jonah lifted the cord over her head and laid the cold iron key on the rock before him.

Ember-that-Remains breathed on it. Once. Gently, the way one breathes on a dying ember to learn whether any life remains in it.

The key did not glow. It did not transform or sing or open any hidden mechanism. But Jonah's gift — wide awake, unshielded, reading the world whether she wished it or not — tasted something pass from the dragon's breath into the metal, something ancient and deliberate, like a word whispered into a sealed room for whoever might someday open the door.

"There," the dragon murmured. "Now it remembers what it was for. Whether that serves you or damns you, I cannot say. Fire is honest that way. It burns whatever it is given, without asking why."

Jonah picked up the key. It was warm now. Warm as a living thing, warm as a hand.

"What was it for?" she asked. "Originally. What were the keys?"

The dragon settled his head upon his forelegs, and for a moment he looked less like a monument and more like what he was: an old animal, tired past measure, talking to the last creature willing to sit with him.

"That," said Ember-that-Remains, "is a bargain, and bargains are not told for free. Come closer, Thornbury. Sit where your grandmother sat. And let us talk about what you will carry forward — because I have one flame left in me, child, and I have been waiting sixty years to learn whose hands it belongs in."

Above the caldera, the first stars came out, sharp and indifferent, and the mountain held its breath.
