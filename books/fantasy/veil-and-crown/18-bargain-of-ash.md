## 18. Bargain of Ash

The dragon had counted his heartbeats for three hundred years, and in all that time he had never once asked one of them to mean anything.

Ember-that-Remains lay coiled in the caldera's throat like a coal banked for winter, and he listened — as he had listened since dawn — to the small, stubborn sounds of a human climbing toward him. Gravel shifting under boots. Breath sawed thin by altitude. The occasional curse, soft and inventive, when the mountain shrugged loose a stone. She was slow. She was careful. She stopped four times to drink from the meltwater seeps, and each time she cupped her hands and tasted before she drank, and each time Ember felt the small bright bell of her gift ring out across the ash.

It rang like this: *clean, clean, clean.* A note struck against the world itself, asking it whether it meant her harm.

No other gift did that. He had known fire-singers whose voices could unmake stone, storm-callers who argued with the sky and occasionally won. But a tongue that asked questions of everything it touched — that was an old magic, older than dragons, older than the war that had emptied the sky of his kind. The tasters had been the first order, before crowns, before veils. Before lists.

And she was climbing toward him with no weapon worth naming, which told him she had already guessed what he was. What remained of what he was.

She crested the caldera's rim at the hour when the light went long and copper, and she stood there at the edge of his world with the wind pulling at her hair, and for a long moment neither of them moved.

Ember opened one eye.

He knew what she saw, because humans always saw it: not the dragons of tapestry and terror but a ruin wearing the shape of one. His scales had gone the grey of old cinders, cracked along the flanks where the fire beneath had thinned to embers. His wings, when he spread them — and he did not spread them now — would show the holes the war had punched through them, patched with scar tissue thin as parchment. He was the size of a hill, and he weighed less than he had a century ago, because weight was flame and flame was leaving him.

*So,* he said, and his voice came out as it always came now — not sound so much as heat, a pressure in the air, a warmth against the skin and a resonance in the chest. *You are the bell.*

Jonah Thornbury did not scream. He gave her credit for that. She went very still, the way small creatures go still, and then she said, "You speak."

*I remember speaking. It is not quite the same thing.* He let the eye close partway, a courtesy. Dragons had learned, over the long dying, that their gaze could stop a human heart, and he had no wish to spend her before she was spent on something worth the cost. *You climbed my mountain. Ask your question or sit down. The wind up there is honest about its intentions.*

"The door," she said. Her voice was rougher than he expected — a voice used to silence, used to swallowing words whole. "In the foothills. They say— I was told—" She stopped, started again. "Can you open it?"

*Ah.* The word settled into him like a stone into deep water. So she had come all this way for the gate, the sealed gate, the door built in the last year of the Dragon War by masons who wept while they worked, because they understood what they were building: an exit for the guilty, a mercy with a lock on it. It wanted fire to open. Not any fire. Dragon-fire — the true kind, the kind that was not merely heat but consent, a will poured out and given shape.

There was exactly one creature left in the world who could give it.

*Yes,* Ember said. *I can open it.*

Her breath went out of her all at once, and she sat down right there on the rim of the caldera, legs folding under her like a puppet with cut strings, and laughed — one short, cracked sound with nothing of joy in it. "Just like that? You don't ask why?"

*I have watched your kind for three hundred years, little bell. You always tell me why eventually. It saves time to wait.*

* * *

She stayed the night. Of course she stayed; the descent in darkness would kill her faster than any dragon, and he told her so, and she made herself a hollow in the ash near enough to his flank that his residual warmth reached her, though he suspected she would deny choosing that spot deliberately. Humans were like that. They took comfort and called it accident.

He did not sleep. Dragons past a certain age did not sleep so much as bank, sinking down into themselves until thought went slow and molten, and tonight he sank only partway, keeping one thread of attention on the small breathing shape below him.

She dreamed badly. He could smell it — fear has a scent, sour and mineral, and grief has another, sweeter, like fruit going over. Hers carried both, braided tight, and underneath them something rarer: the smell of a person who had decided, somewhere on the road behind her, that she would rather die moving than live standing still.

Near midnight she spoke into the dark, not knowing — or pretending not to know — that he was awake.

"My grandmother," she said. "You said you remembered her name. How?"

*Names are heavy,* Ember said. *They do not vanish. They sink.* He turned the great head, slow as a season changing, until one ember-bright eye found her in the gloom. *Your grandmother came to the Ashreach in the year the war ended. She was young. She carried a child on her back and a key around her neck, and she stood where you are standing — no. Where you are sitting. And she asked me nothing. She looked at the door-road and turned away, and she walked back down into the valley, and she lived, and she died, and her name sank into me like rain into ash.*

"What was her name?"

*Tell me yours first.*

A pause. Then, quietly, as if paying a toll: "Jonah. Jonah Thornbury."

*Maren,* he said. *Maren Thornbury. She smelled of rosemary and woodsmoke, and she was afraid of the same thing you are afraid of, which is not death. It is being remembered wrongly.*

Below him, the small shape went utterly still. When she spoke again her voice had gone strange and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice over deep water. "What else do you remember?"

*Everything that ever mattered enough to be heavy.* He let the silence stretch, because some truths needed room. *I am the last archive of my kind, little bell. When a dragon dies, the world loses a library. I have spent three hundred years deciding what to do about that, and I have decided nothing, because there was no one to decide it for. Until a bell rang across the valley stones two days ago and would not stop ringing.*

"I'm sorry." The words came out small. "I didn't mean to—I can't help it. The gift. It wakes up when I'm outside the veil, and I can't make it quiet."

*I know,* Ember said, and something in him that had been clenched for centuries loosened, just slightly, just enough to hurt. *That is why I am glad you came.*

* * *

Morning came grey and cold, and with it came the conversation he had been circling for three hundred years without ever having.

She ate the way survivors eat — quickly, watching the horizon between bites — and then she stood, brushed the ash from her knees, and looked at him with the particular straightness of a person who has rehearsed a speech all night.

"Open the door," she said. "Please. I'll give you anything I have."

*Anything,* Ember repeated, tasting the word. *Humans say that so easily. It is a coin you mint without checking the metal.* He raised himself onto his forelegs, and the movement cost him — he felt the fire gutter, felt scales grind where muscle had wasted — but he did it anyway, because some things must be said standing, even if only one of the parties stands. *Come here. Closer. I will not burn you. Look at me.*

She came. Small and brave and trembling, she walked right up under the arch of his neck, into the shadow of a jaw that could have closed on her like a door, and she looked up into his face, and he let her see all of it — the cracks, the greyness, the banked glow deep in the throat like a hearth seen through a ruined wall.

*Understand what you are asking,* he said. *Flame is not fuel to us. It is not something we carry. It is something we ARE. Every dragon is a fire that learned to want things. And when the last of the flame goes out of a dragon, the wanting goes too, and then the body, and then the name.* He paused. *I have perhaps one true flame left in me. One. If I pour it into that door, the door opens, and I begin to die — not that day, maybe not that month, but soon, and coldly, and alone on this mountain. That is the price. There is no other price. There never was.*

The wind moved through the caldera, carrying ash in slow spirals.

"You're dying anyway," she said at last, and her voice was gentle, which was worse than cruelty. "I can see it. Anyone can see it."

*Yes.* He did not insult her by denying it. *But there is a difference between a fire going out on its own schedule and a fire being SPENT. One is weather. The other is a choice. And choices are the only currency your kind and mine have ever truly traded in.* He lowered his great head until his breath stirred her hair — warm breath, smelling of stone and old smoke. *So before I answer you, answer me this. And do not answer quickly, because I will know if the answer is cheap.*

She waited.

*If I die for you,* Ember said, *what will you carry forward?*

Silence. Long silence. The wind filled it, and the distant tick of cooling stone, and somewhere far below, a hawk riding the thermals off the scree.

*Not gratitude,* he said. *Gratitude is a feeling, and feelings evaporate. Not memory — memory fades, and besides, I have lived too long to trust a species that forgets its own wars inside a generation. I mean: what will you CARRY. What will I have bought, with the last fire in the world, when it walks through that door and becomes a life?*

"I don't—" She stopped. Started again. "I don't know."

*Good,* Ember said softly. *That is the first honest thing you have said.*

"It's not good. You're offering your life and I'm standing here saying I don't know what it's for."

*You are standing here saying you do not know what YOURS is for. That is different, and it is more interesting.* He settled back onto his haunches, and the effort of the morning caught up with him; the glow in his throat dimmed, steadied, dimmed again. *I will tell you what I have seen, little bell, in three hundred years of watching your kind from above. I have seen kingdoms dissolve and their laws persist like ghosts. I have seen men build doors for the guilty and then build lists of the innocent. I have seen fire used to open ways out and fire used to seal ways in, and the fire never cared which. Fire is honest. It burns what it is given. Only the hand decides.*

"And your hand?" she asked. "What would you burn for?"

He considered lying to her, and rejected it — partly because she would taste the lie somehow, he suspected, the way she tasted everything, and partly because he had earned the right to honesty by outliving everyone he might have lied to.

*A question,* he said. *I would burn for a question. Three hundred years ago, when the sky was full of wings and the world was loud, I never once wondered what any of it was FOR. We were dragons. We burned, we hoarded, we endured. Purpose was for smaller creatures.* His eye-lid slid half-closed, ancient and tired. *Now I am the last, and the quiet has taught me what the noise never could: that a life is not measured by how long it burns but by what it chooses to burn FOR. Your grandmother chose the valley. She chose silence, and a grave, and a granddaughter she would never meet. I have carried her choice for sixty years wondering if it was worth the carrying. You are the answer to a question she never got to ask.*

"Then open the door," Jonah whispered. "Let me carry it forward. Let me be the answer."

*To WHAT?* The word rang off the caldera walls, and ash leapt in spirals, and for one moment the old fire showed through the ruin of him — vast, terrible, magnificent, the thing that had darkened skies. Then it banked again, and he was only a dying creature on a dead mountain, looking down at a woman who had not flinched. *That is my question, Jonah Thornbury. Not WHERE will you go. Not WILL you survive. Those are the questions of animals, and I have no flame to waste on animal answers. I ask: what survives you? What walks out of that door wearing your name and my death, and what does it DO?*

She opened her mouth.

Closed it.

Stood there in the wind with her hands empty at her sides, and Ember watched her — watched the truth move through her like weather, watched her reach for an answer and find her hands full of nothing, watched her understand, finally, the weight of what sat coiled in front of her. She was not being offered escape. She was being offered a purchase, and she had come to the market with no idea what she wanted to buy.

"I need time," she said finally. Her voice was steady, but he could smell the salt of tears she refused to shed. "I need to think."

*Take it,* Ember said. *I have waited three hundred years. I can wait a day more.* He curled himself back down into the caldera's throat, folding the ruin of his wings around him, and as his eyes closed he added, almost gently: *But know this, little bell. While you think, the world does not. There is a woman with a list coming up the mountain road behind you. I can smell her ink from here. Whatever you decide — decide it before she arrives, because she has already decided about you.*

Below him, in the ash, Jonah Thornbury went very still.

Then she turned and looked south, down the long switchback trail, toward the thin dark line of riders where the mountain met the plain.

And high above, on the ridgeline, the wind changed direction — bringing with it, faint and unmistakable, the smell of horses, and iron, and paper.
