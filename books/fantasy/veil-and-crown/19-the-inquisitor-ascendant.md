## 19. The Inquisitor Ascendant

The promotion came with a seal, a salary, and a room with a window that did not open.

Serane Voss stood in her new office on the fourth floor of the Ministry of Inquiry and looked at that window for a long time. It was a fine window, leaded glass in a frame of pale ashwood, and it looked out over the capital's eastern quarter — the tile roofs, the smoke, the slow brown ribbon of the river. But the sash had been painted shut, or nailed, or simply built that way, and when she pressed her palm against the glass she felt no draft at all. The room was warm. The room was sealed. The room was, she understood, a kind of honor.

She had been in the valley eleven days ago. She had walked through Harrowmere's ashes in her good boots and come back with forty-one names confirmed, six names struck for insufficient evidence, and one name flagged in a margin in her own handwriting: *Thornbury, J. — taster — verify.*

The flag had earned her this office.

She set her satchel on the desk — new desk, unscarred oak, not yet marked by rings of cups or the gouges of impatient pens — and unpacked it with the methodical care her father had taught her before he taught her anything else about the world. *A mind is only as honest as its records,* he used to say, sharpening his ledger-knife by lamplight. *You can lie to yourself all day, Serane. The page won't let you.* He had been a customs clerk in a river town that no longer appeared on maps, a man who believed in lists the way other men believed in saints, and he had died with his ledgers balanced and his town burned around them, and Serane had never once thought these two facts contradicted each other.

She arranged her instruments: the valley file, bound in grey; the Harrowmere annex, bound in black; her commission, signed by the Lord Inquisitor himself, granting her authority over all gifted-detection operations north of the river. She arranged her pens. She arranged herself.

Then she sat down, and began, for the first time in her career, to keep two registries.

* * *

The official one was simple enough. It was the work she was paid for: names, evidence, dispositions. She wrote in the ministry's mandated hand, the small upright script they drilled into every clerk and investigator, legible to any auditor, deniable by no one. *Confirmed. Confirmed. Struck — insufficient. Confirmed. Referred.* Forty-one entries from Harrowmere alone, each one a person she had seen or touched or spoken to, each one now a line of ink traveling through the machinery of the crown toward an outcome she did not permit herself to imagine in detail. She knew the outcomes. Everyone knew the outcomes. The Act was called the Gift Registry Act because *Purge Act* had tested poorly with the merchant guilds, and everyone understood what registration meant the way everyone understands what a headsman's block is for without needing it explained.

Serane believed in the Act. This was the thing her colleagues found either admirable or unsettling, depending on their temperaments, and it was the thing she herself held onto most carefully, the way a climber holds the rock face: not because it is comfortable, but because letting go is death.

She believed it because she had seen what happened when the gifted went unregistered. She had been nine years old, in the plague summer, in the river town, when the fever came through and took one household in three — except it hadn't taken households evenly, had it? It had taken the houses near the hedge-witch's cottage worst, and the hedge-witch's cottage not at all, and when the mob finally came for the old woman they found her well-fed and untouched amid a street of graves, and something in the arithmetic of that — in the obscene, unmistakable arithmetic — had lodged in Serane like a splinter and never worked its way out. Magic was not a gift. Magic was a vector. The gifted drew fortune toward themselves the way a lightning rod draws fire, and everyone downwind paid the toll. Her mother. Her brother Tomas, who had been seven. The girl next door who could whistle like a nightingale and who died coughing blood into a rag while the hedge-witch's chimney stayed warm and smoking.

The registry was not cruelty. The registry was quarantine. Anyone who said otherwise had never counted bodies.

So she wrote her forty-one lines, and her hand did not shake, and she told herself — as she always told herself, and mostly believed — that every name on her list was a fire prevented, a plague summer that would not happen, a street of children who would grow up because someone had been willing to do the counting.

It was the second registry where the trouble lived.

* * *

It started as an accident, which is how most corruptions begin — not as a decision but as a hesitation so small it doesn't register as one until long after, when you look back down the road and realize the fork was there and you took it without noticing.

The Harrowmere annex contained a sub-file. Every purge did: the peripheral names, the ones adjacent to guilt rather than guilty themselves. The woman who had sold bread to a known gifted family. The boy who had run a message and claimed not to know what was in it. The old man who had sheltered his granddaughter for three days after her marking and turned her out on the fourth, weeping, and reported her himself, and whose report had come too late to save him from suspicion.

Serane sat with that sub-file open on her first evening in the new office, lamplight pooling on the desk, and read the old man's statement. His name was Coram Fell. He was seventy-one. He had farmed the same plot above Harrowmere for fifty years. His testimony was coherent, detailed, and drenched in grief — he described his granddaughter's chalk-marked door, the soldiers' lanterns coming up the lane, the sound his daughter made. And then, at the end, in the space reserved for the subject's own disposition, someone had written: *suspected concealment — recommend review.*

Review meant Voss. She was the reviewing authority now. That was what the seal on her commission meant, among other things: that the buck stopped at her desk, that the machinery of the Act passed through her hands the way water passes through a lock, and she decided, gate by gate, which way it flowed.

She read the file twice. She thought about the plague summer. She thought about quarantine, and about how a quarantine that takes the healthy along with the sick is not medicine but massacre wearing medicine's coat. She thought about her father's knife whispering against the whetstone, and the page that wouldn't let you lie.

And then she picked up her pen, and in the official record she wrote, in her small upright hand: *Coram Fell — reviewed. Insufficient evidence of concealment. Struck.*

And in the second book — the plain grey notebook she had bought that morning from a stationer's on Cooper's Lane, unremarked, unlogged, bought with her own coin and carried home in her coat — she wrote nothing at all.

That was the whole of it. That was the entire crime. A name struck honestly, or nearly honestly, or at least defensibly — the old man *had* reported his own granddaughter, and the law's own logic excused him — and a private book in which the striking was recorded nowhere, so that no future auditor, no future Voss, no future version of the machinery itself, could ever reopen the case and undo her mercy.

She told herself it was rigor. She told herself it was case management. She told herself a great many things that evening, walking home through streets that smelled of coal smoke and roasting chestnuts, and the strange thing — the thing she would remember later, much later, when she understood what she had begun — was that none of the things she told herself were lies. Not exactly. They were true statements arranged to conceal a truth, which is the definition of a certain kind of lie, the kind practiced by people who cannot bear to stop believing in themselves.

Ambition, she thought, climbing the stairs to her rooms, tasted like medicine. Bitter, necessary, swallowed in small doses, good for you in ways you'd rather not examine.

* * *

Over the following weeks the grey notebook grew.

Not quickly. Serane was careful the way a surgeon is careful, and she applied the same discipline to her mercy that she applied to her hunting. Each omission had to be earned. Each name she quietly dropped from the flow of the machinery had to meet a standard she defined for herself and revised, uneasily, whenever she caught herself revising it downward. The grandmother who hid a child: omitted, because fear of losing family was not the same as allegiance to magic. The miller who had employed a gifted journeyman without knowing: omitted, because ignorance was not contagion. The schoolteacher in the northern villages whose gift was, by every account, nothing more than an uncanny memory for names — a gift so small it seemed almost a joke, a gift that could hurt no one unless remembering were suddenly declared a plague: omitted, after four nights of argument with herself, because Serane had finally asked the question she had spent her whole career refusing to ask — *what exactly is the danger here?* — and had not liked the silence that answered.

But others she sent forward without hesitation. The hedge-tanner outside Harrowmere whose charms had demonstrably cured a horse the veterinarians had condemned — that was power, real power, unregistered and therefore unwatched, and unwatched power in a plague-prone world was exactly the thing the Act existed to prevent. The canal-witch who sold luck by the inch. The man in the capital itself, a minor customs official, discovered by pure clerical accident to have survived three poisonings that should have killed him — that file she forwarded within the hour, with a note recommending immediate priority, because immunity to poison was not a parlour trick; it was a physiology, a marker, the signature of a bloodline the old kingdom had cultivated deliberately back when Cael still existed and still kept such things.

She did not know why that particular entry pleased her so much. She noted the pleasure, examined it, found nothing actionable in it, and moved on. There was always more work. There was always more work; that was the luxury and the horror of the ministry, that the supply of human lives requiring sorting was effectively infinite, and the only question ever truly before any inquisitor was how fast to feed the machine.

In November the Lord Inquisitor summoned her.

His office was everything hers was not: tall windows that opened, a view south across the whole sprawl of the capital, a fire that someone else fed. He was an old man gone soft at the edges, with the contented look of a career administrator who had never once doubted anything, and he praised her Harrowmere operation in terms that made her skin crawl precisely because they were accurate — *thorough, humane where possible, decisive where not* — and then he gave her the valley.

"The Veiled Valley," he said, tapping a folder on his desk. "Backwater. Half the court forgets it exists. But the Act applies everywhere the crown applies, and the crown applies there, whatever the locals think." He slid the folder across. "There's a flagged name in it. Thornbury. Kitchen staff, royal food-taster, some years' service. My predecessor flagged her for verification and then got promoted sideways and never followed up. You have a nose for verification, Voss. Follow it up."

Serane took the folder. It was thin. Valley files were always thin — the veil that lay over that country suppressed the very signs her craft depended on, and the few inquisitors who had worked the valley spoke of it the way sailors speak of a windless sea, with frustration and a certain superstitious dread. She opened it and saw the marginal note in her predecessor's hand, and beneath it, clipped to the inside cover, a single sheet: the kitchen roster of the steward's household, copied years ago, names in faded ink.

Her eye went down the list the way it went down every list, fast and hungry and thorough, and stopped.

*J. Thornbury — taster.*

And beside it, in the margin, in ink that matched the roster's age, a note in some clerk's hand: *recommended for commendation — detected contamination in the king's breakfast, saved life of Steward Aldric.*

Something turned over in her chest. She kept her face still — she was famous for her still face, they called it the Voss mask behind her back, and she permitted it because masks were professional equipment — but underneath the mask her mind was already moving, quick and cold, laying the pieces side by side. A taster. Immune, or nearly. A woman whose tongue could find bitter almond in honeyed pears, which was not training — training sharpened the tongue, but it did not make the impossible possible — and who had used that impossibility to save a life, and who had then, according to the steward's own deposition appended here, declined to explain how she knew.

A gifted woman hiding inside the one place in the old kingdom where magic could not follow her. Hiding in the blind spot. Using the valley the way a thief uses a crowd.

"Thornbury," Serane said. "I'll take the file personally."

The Lord Inquisitor smiled his soft, satisfied smile. "Knew you would. That's why I gave it to you." He waved her toward the door, already reaching for the next folder, and she rose and bowed and left with the valley file under her arm, and it was not until she was halfway down the stairs that she realized her heart was beating hard, and that the feeling in her chest was not entirely the clean excitement of the hunt.

Part of it was recognition. Part of it was the memory of a grey notebook in her desk drawer downstairs, full of names she had saved, and the sudden unwelcome thought — arriving unbidden, unwelcome precisely because it arrived unbidden — that somewhere in the Veiled Valley there might be a woman doing exactly what Serane did, keeping her own private registry of omissions, deciding gate by gate which lives the machine would swallow, and calling it survival.

She crushed the thought. She was good at crushing thoughts; she had been crushing them since she was nine years old and counting graves.

But that night, in her office with the window that did not open, she took out the grey notebook and read it through from the beginning, all fourteen names, all fourteen quiet mercies, and at the end of it she wrote a fifteenth entry — not a name, but a question, in her small upright hand, the hand that would not let her lie:

*If survival requires the machine, and the machine requires lists, whose side am I on when I omit?*

She looked at the question for a long time. Then she closed the notebook, locked it in the drawer beneath the false bottom she had installed herself, and blew out the lamp, and sat in the dark of the sealed warm room thinking about a kitchen girl with an impossible tongue, and about the dawn courier that would carry her summons north to the Veiled Valley in the morning.

The valley file lay on her desk. On its cover, in the ministry's stamp, the words she had read ten thousand times and never once tired of: *REGISTRY OF THE GIFTED — ALL NAMES TO BE ACCOUNTED FOR.*

All names, she thought, rising to bar the door out of habit rather than need. Except, apparently, the ones I choose.

She fell asleep at her desk, and dreamed of a plague summer, and of a nightingale-whistling girl coughing blood, and woke with the taste of medicine in her mouth.
