## 20. Caught

The river came on her the way bad news always did: suddenly, and from a direction she hadn't thought to watch.

Jonah had been following it since midmorning without knowing its name — a broad, brown-shouldered current sliding down out of the Ashreach foothills, swollen with snowmelt, loud enough that she stopped hearing her own footsteps. That was the danger of rivers. They were generous with noise and stingy with warning. She had learned in three weeks of flight to read roads, hedgerows, the particular quality of silence around a village that had recently been asked questions. But water was water, and she was tired, and tired people believed the world would let them rest.

She came around a stand of alders and saw the ford.

It was a good one — flat stones laid in a spine across the shallows, the kind of crossing that meant a road had once mattered here, back when Cael had been allowed to have roads. On the far bank stood a waymarker wearing the faded stag of the old royal line, antlers worn smooth by weather and by hands. Someone had chalked over it recently. The crown's new sigil, the sunburst, stared out from the stone like an eye that had been painted open.

And below the marker, watering their horses in the shallows, were six riders.

Jonah went still among the alders the way Wenna had taught her — not frozen, because frozen things drew the eye, but *slow*, becoming a piece of the morning. A woman walking. A woman pausing to look at the river. Nothing worth the trouble of a second glance.

Six riders at a ford this far from anywhere was not nothing. They wore no livery she could see at this distance, which meant either they were smugglers or they were the kind of soldiers who preferred not to be identified until identification suited them. Their horses drank with the loose-lipped contentment of animals whose riders had nowhere urgent to be, and one of the men laughed at something, and the sound carried across the water with terrible ease.

Then she saw the banner furled at the lead rider's saddlebow, and her stomach dropped through the ground beneath her.

Not the sunburst. The sunburst was for show, for proclamations and scaffolds and criers in market squares. This was a plain grey pennant with a single black thread running through it, and Jonah had seen that pennant once before, in a dream she'd had after Odile's caravan left her at the crossroads — the pennant of the Inquisitor's Own, the riders who did not purge villages but *found* them.

They were not here by accident. Fords were where you caught people, because fords were where people had to slow down and become visible, one stone at a time, in open water with no cover and no argument.

*Turn around,* said the part of her that had kept her alive for twenty-three years, the part that tasted first and swallowed never. *Walk back the way you came. Find another crossing. There is always another crossing.*

But there wasn't. She knew the country now, better than she'd ever wanted to. Upstream the river cut into a gorge within half a mile; downstream it widened into marshland that would take a day to wade and announce her to every eye along the banks. The mountains she needed were on the far side. The door was on the far side. Everything was on the far side, and six men on fresh horses sat between her and it like a sentence waiting to be finished.

She crouched at the water's edge, ostensibly a traveler resting, and made herself think.

The riders had posted two of their number upstream and downstream, a bowshot out, watching the approaches. The other four lounged near the marker, and one of them — heavyset, grey-bearded, with the bored competence of a man who had done this many times — kept glancing at the road behind Jonah as if expecting someone. Not hunting randomly, then. Waiting. Which meant someone had told them a runner might come this way, and there was only one person who could have described her well enough to make these stones worth guarding.

Odile's face rose in her memory, unbidden — the matriarch's laugh, the stew she'd been fed, the way the old woman had held her hand at parting and said *you owe me nothing but loyalty*. Guilt arrived like a stone in the chest. Then, just as quickly, something colder arrived beside it: Odile had sold her *wrong*. Whatever information had brought these riders to this ford, it had come bent. Maybe the old woman had given a description that fit a dozen girls, or a direction that pointed here while Jonah walked there. Maybe loyalty had a price after all, and Odile had paid it in a currency she'd convinced herself didn't count.

Or maybe Voss was simply better than any of them.

Jonah pushed the thought away. It didn't matter how they'd come. What mattered was the forty yards of brown water between her and the far bank, and the four sets of eyes that would find her the moment she stepped onto the stones.

A fisherman's coracle lay overturned near the alders, hull split, useless. A herd path climbed the gorge upstream and vanished into rock. Downstream, reeds. None of it fast enough. Riders would run her down in open country inside an hour, and then there would be no more thinking at all, only questions, and she knew what followed the questions. Harrowmere had taught her what followed the questions. A child's shoe in the ash. Chalk marks on doors like arithmetic done in suffering.

Her hand went to the iron key at her throat without her deciding it should. Cold even in the warm air. Wenna's key. Wenna, who was somewhere behind her now, in the valley, under the same sky that had produced these riders, and Jonah had not let herself think about that in nine days because thinking about it made the road impossible to walk.

*Survive,* Wenna had said, pressing the key into her palm. *That's the whole of the inheritance, girl. Everything else is commentary.*

All right. Then survive.

She studied the water the way she had once studied the king's breakfast tray — not looking for what was obvious, but for what was *off*. And because she was who she was, because the gift that had marked her for death was also the only tool she had ever been given, she did the thing she had sworn never to do outside the valley's veil.

She opened herself to the river.

It happened the way waking happens — not a decision but a threshold crossed. For three weeks she had kept the gift folded up inside her like a broken wing, dulling it against the constant low roar of the world's flavors, because out here, past the boundary stones, the gift did not whisper; it *shouted*. Every plant, every animal track, every drop of rain carried its taste, and most of them carried worse. But the valley was behind her, and the riders were ahead, and she was out of gentler options.

The river entered her.

Brown water, snowmelt, silt — the honest tastes first, mud and stone and the green rot of last autumn's leaves caught in the eddies. Then the living layer: fish, uncountable, a silver flicker of presence all through the current. Then the far bank, faintly, the way smell crosses water — pine, horse sweat, leather, the sour-metal tang of men who had been riding since dawn. Her gift spread across the ford like oil across a table, and she had to bite down on a gasp, because it had been so long since anything had tasted this *much*, and some traitorous part of her had missed it the way you miss a limb you cut off yourself.

And then, downstream, where the river bent toward the marshes —

Poison.

Not strong. Not aimed. A thread of it, thin as wire, winding down from somewhere upstream — mine spoil, maybe, or a tannery's leavings, the old kingdom's industries bleeding their bitterness into the water long after the kingdom itself had been struck from the maps. It braided through the current in a ribbon perhaps eight feet wide, hugging the southern edge of the channel, invisible to any eye. Any eye but hers.

Any body but hers, it would have killed slowly — a week of cramps, a month of failing kidneys, death arriving so quietly its victim would blame the road. She tasted it fully, catalogued it, set it aside. It was not the point. The point was what its absence told her.

Because the poison ran in a ribbon, and ribbons had edges, and where the ribbon ended the water ran clean — and the clean water ran exactly where the ford's stone spine began to curve north, toward the far bank's willow tangle, away from the marker where the riders stood.

The safe crossing was not the crossing anyone would expect a fugitive to take. It was the awkward one, the one that swung wide and passed within thirty feet of the downstream sentry before bending back to shore. It was the crossing a guilty person would avoid and an innocent person wouldn't think about at all.

Which meant, if she could be an innocent person — if she could perform innocence the way she had performed boredom for the inquisitor's clerk in the valley kitchen, terror dressed in yawns — then the poison's own map was her road across.

Her heart was going hard against her ribs. She noticed it, named it, put it away. Fear was a flavor too, and she had spent her whole life swallowing flavors without letting them show.

She stood, brushed off her skirts, and did the bravest thing she had ever done, which was to walk toward the riders at an ordinary pace, carrying a bundle of alder branches she'd gathered for no reason except that innocent people gathered firewood, and humming — actually humming, some half-remembered market tune from the valley — as if the morning belonged to her.

The downstream sentry saw her first. He straightened in his saddle, hand drifting to his sword, and Jonah felt his attention land on her like a physical weight, and she thought: *boredom.* She thought about the tedium of gathering wood, the ache in her shoulders, the blister on her heel, and she let all of it sit on her face, because a hunted girl runs and a wood-gathering girl trudges, and trudging was a language every soldier in the world could read.

"Where from?" the sentry called.

"Farm past the ridge." She jerked her chin vaguely southward, toward country she'd crossed two days ago. "Grandmother wants a fire going when she wakes. Wants it every morning, mind, and me to make it."

The lie came out easy, worn smooth. That was the thing no one understood about lying: it was a craft like any other, and she had been apprenticed to it at nine years old alongside the tasting. You didn't invent details; you borrowed true ones. There *had* been a farm past the ridge. There *had* been a grandmother, dead now, buried in the valley under a stone that would soon carry another name.

The sentry grunted. His eyes went over her — the bundle, the dust, the blister she made a small performance of favoring — and found what he expected to find, which was nothing.

"You'll want the stones," he said, jerking his thumb at the ford. "Water's rotten further down. Bad ground poisons it." He said it with the flat authority of a man repeating what he'd been told, and something cold moved through Jonah's chest, because he was right, and he didn't know how right, and neither did the man who'd told him.

"Thank you kindly," she said, and shuffled toward the stones.

Four riders watched her from the marker. The grey-bearded one looked longest. He had the stillness of a man comparing faces, and Jonah felt the comparison happening, felt herself weighed against some description carried in a saddlebag or a memory, and she kept her eyes down the way servants do, the way she had kept her eyes down for fourteen years in the palace kitchens, invisible by profession.

Invisible by profession. She almost laughed. All those years of being nobody, of tasting and swallowing and saying nothing — she had thought of it as a cage. Now she understood it had been training. The whole miserable apprenticeship of her life had been training for this forty yards of brown water.

She stepped onto the first stone.

The river roared beneath her, and through the gift she felt it entire — the cold push of the meltwater, the silt combing past her ankles, the poison ribbon sliding by eight feet to her left like a snake keeping its distance. Halfway across, the current grabbed at her skirts and she staggered, arms out, wood scattered, and for one lurching moment she was simply a woman fighting the water, and the riders' attention sharpened—

—and then dulled again, because drowning women were not fugitives, and fugitives did not lose their firewood to the current and swear at it in a market accent.

She reached the willow tangle on the far bank with her heart slamming and her lungs full of river-smell, and she did not stop, did not look back, did not break stride. She walked up through the willows onto the old road, turned east toward the mountains, and kept walking at the pace of a woman with blisters and a grandmother waiting, while behind her the ford and its six riders shrank into the morning haze.

Only when the road bent and the river vanished did she allow herself to stop, hands on knees, breath tearing.

And it was there, bent double in the middle of a dead kingdom's road, that she understood what she had done.

They had seen her. Not her name, not her crime — but her face, her voice, her walk. Six men would carry the memory of a farm girl with a bundle of sticks, and if Serane Voss ever stood in front of that memory and asked the right question, the way she had asked the right question of a kitchen clerk about an untouched wine glass, the whole careful performance would come apart like wet paper. She had bought the ford with a coin she couldn't mint twice. Every use of her gift, every lie told well, every risk survived narrowed the world she could move in. Invisibility was not a possession. It was a candle burning down.

And she had lit it deliberately, in open daylight, in front of witnesses.

She straightened. The mountains stood up ahead of her, blue-grey and indifferent, and somewhere in their folds was a sealed gate that wanted fire instead of iron, and somewhere higher than that, according to the archive fragments sewn into her hem, was a caldera where the last dragon in Cael was dying alone.

*Flame,* the fragments said. *The door knows flame.*

She touched the key at her throat — cold iron, no lock, an heirloom of fear — and then, without letting herself hesitate, because hesitation was a luxury the candle could no longer afford, she turned her face to the Ashreach and began to climb.

Behind her, far downstream, the poisoned ribbon of water slid on toward the marshes, carrying its secret. Ahead of her, high in the thinning air, something vast and old stirred in its sleep — and though she could not yet know it, though no gift of hers reached that far, the dragon Ember-that-Remains lifted his head from the ash of his caldera and tasted, on the wind, a bell ringing.

Someone gifted was climbing toward him. And dragons keep their bargains, even the ones not yet made.
