## 21. What Survives Us

The soldiers came for Wenna on a morning so ordinary it felt like an insult.

She had been in her garden since first light, thinning the feverfew and tying up the last of the summer's yarrow before the frost took it. Her hands knew the work without her; they had known it for sixty years, through three kings she had never seen and one kingdom that had been struck from the maps like a name scraped off a door. The yarrow was late this year. Everything was late this year, as though the valley itself had heard the criers and decided to hold its breath.

She heard the hooves before she saw them — four horses coming down the drover's road at a walk, which was worse than a gallop. Men who gallop are frightened or eager. Men who walk are certain.

Wenna did not stand. She finished tying the yarrow stem, tucked the twine into her apron pocket, and only then rose, pressing her palms against her lower back as old women do when they want to look harmless. There were four of them in crown grey, and behind them, dismounting with the careful neatness of a person who dislikes mud, a clerk with a ledger under his arm.

"Wenna of the boundary stones?" the sergeant asked.

"I'm called that." She wiped her hands on her apron. "Though I've not touched the stones in years. They don't like being handled by anyone who isn't listening."

The sergeant frowned. He was young enough that his beard looked painted on. "You're required to come with us. Under the Gift Registry Act. There are questions regarding your testimony."

"Testimony," Wenna said, tasting the word. It was a clean word, a clerk's word, and it sat in her mouth like a stone from a riverbed — smooth, cold, and dropped there long ago by water that no longer ran. "I gave no testimony. I told a girl which stones were older than the others. If that's a crime now, you'd best arrest half the valley. The other half is too deaf to know what they said."

The clerk opened his ledger. She watched him do it — watched the small ceremony of it, the wetted thumb, the finding of the page. That was how it always began. Not with chains. With paper.

*They came for my sister with paper too,* she thought, and the thought arrived without pain, the way an old scar arrives when the weather turns: familiar, dull, and impossible to argue with.

"I'll get my shawl," she said.

* * *

They let her walk between two soldiers, which she appreciated. Being carried would have been undignified, and dignity was the one possession Wenna had never let the world tax. The road into the market town was the same road she had walked since she was a girl gathering comfrey along the ditches, and she found herself cataloguing it — the split rail fence mended with willow, the hollow where the adders denned, the elder tree that had been struck by lightning the summer her hair went grey. Not because she feared she'd never see it again. Because someone ought to remember it properly, and she was the last one left who could.

The valley held its breath around her as she passed. She felt it the way you feel weather coming in your knees. Doors closed softly. A woman drawing water set down her bucket and did not pick it up again until Wenna had gone by. Nobody shouted. Nobody wept. In the Veiled Valley, grief was a thing done indoors, quietly, like washing.

At the edge of the square, the old tithe barn had been cleared out and given new furniture: a table, two chairs, a brazier, and a clerk's inkstand arranged with the loving precision of an altar. They had hung the crown's grey banner over the door, and it flapped in the wind with a sound like slow applause.

They sat her in the chair facing the table. The clerk took the other chair. The sergeant stood by the door, and the other two soldiers arranged themselves against the wall with the studied idleness of men who wanted very much to be somewhere else.

"You are Wenna, hedge-healer, resident of the valley forty—" the clerk checked his page, "—six years. Is that correct?"

"Fifty-one," said Wenna. "Your records begin when I registered the well. I was here nine years before that."

He made a small correction. She watched him make it, and understood something then that steadied her better than any charm: he believed the page. He believed that the page and the world were the same size. Men like that were dangerous, but they were also, in their way, innocent, and innocence could be led about by the nose if you were patient.

"You were interviewed some weeks ago regarding the boundary stones," the clerk said. "You spoke at length with a traveler. A young woman. Kitchen-trained. We have reason to believe she was gifted."

"Half the valley's kitchen-trained," Wenna said. "It's the only trade that doesn't leave."

"The young woman's name, please."

And there it was. The whole morning folded itself down to that one small question, the way a map folds down to fit in a pocket, and everything else — the garden, the yarrow, the fifty-one years — was just the paper around it.

Wenna looked at the clerk. He was perhaps thirty. He had ink on the side of his right hand, the way all writing people did, and a wedding band worn thin, and the particular tiredness around the eyes of a man who had done arithmetic all his life and never once been asked whether the numbers were kind.

"My sister's name was Maren," Wenna said.

The clerk blinked. "That is not—"

"Maren of the Ashwell line. Born in the spring of the year the river froze in April. Gifted, as it happens — she could tell when rain was coming three days out, and once, when we were girls, she told our mother the exact hour the fever would break, and it broke to the minute, and our mother never slept properly again." Wenna's voice was level. She kept it level the way you keep a full cup level crossing a rough floor — with attention, not ease. "They took her in the last purge. I was fourteen. I stood where you're sitting — no, not exactly; the barn was a barn then — and I gave a man her name because he asked me twice and I was a child and afraid. He wrote it down. She was dead within the year. So you see, I know precisely what happens to names that go into books like yours."

The clerk's pen had stopped moving. "Mistress Wenna—"

"That's the first name," Wenna said. "You asked for names. Let me save us both the afternoon."

* * *

They did not save the afternoon. They tried, over the following days, in the patient, grinding way of institutions — moving her to a cell in the guardhouse that smelled of turnips and old rope, bringing her bread and broth she did not eat, asking the question again each morning with the same clerical gentleness, as though repetition were a solvent and she a stain.

Wenna did not mind the cell. She had slept in worse places during bad winters, and the turnip smell reminded her of a man named Harl who had run the guardhouse thirty years ago and grown the finest turnips in the valley, and thinking of Harl was pleasant. What she minded was the waiting — the sense of a net being woven somewhere above her, thread by thread, and her own small body serving as the anchor point.

On the fourth day, they brought her a woman.

Wenna knew her at once, though they had never met. You learn to read power the way farmers read sky. This one walked like certainty walks — unhurried, upright, interested. Crown grey, but cut finer than the soldiers', and she carried no ledger. She carried nothing at all, which was its own kind of statement: *I am not here to write down what you say. I am here to find out what you won't.*

"Inquisitor Serane Voss," the woman said, and sat down across from her uninvited, arranging herself as though the cell were a parlor. Up close she was younger than her manner, with a scholar's pallor and eyes the color of wet slate. "Your file makes interesting reading."

"Files usually do," Wenna said. "They're mostly lies wearing good posture."

Something flickered at the corner of the inquisitor's mouth. Not quite a smile. A recognition, maybe — the look of one craftswoman seeing another's stitchwork. "You told my clerk fifty-one years. The record says forty-six."

"The record was made by a man who counted from a well registration. I counted from my arrival. One of us was actually there."

"And your sister. Maren. The clerk found no Maren in the purge rolls."

"No," Wenna agreed. "You wouldn't. The purge rolls from that year burned — officially by accident, unofficially by a clerk with a conscience and a candle. Which means my sister exists now only where I keep her." She laid one hand flat on her own breastbone, an old woman's gesture, plain as bread. "Which is why I don't put living names in your book. The dead are safe with me. I bury them properly. It's the living I intend to keep out of the ground."

Voss studied her for a long moment. "You understand why I'm here."

"I understand why you think you're here. Those aren't always the same thing, inquisitor. I've met a great many people who were certain they were hunting monsters and turned out to be carrying them."

"The gifted are a danger to the realm."

"So is a river," said Wenna. "We don't drown everyone who can swim. We teach them, and we post a warning sign, and we mourn the ones the water takes anyway. Your Act does none of those things. Your Act takes every child who ever floated and calls them a flood." She leaned forward, and her voice did not rise; it lowered, which was worse. "I have buried more gifted folk than you have ever arrested. Do you want to hear about them? Truly? Because I will tell you. I have been waiting sixty years for someone in a fine coat to ask me that question, and I promise you, girl, you will wish you hadn't."

"Then tell me," Voss said quietly. "Tell me about them."

So Wenna told her.

* * *

She began with the ones the clerk would never find, because they had died before any registry thought to want them. Old Tam of the mill race, who could taste rot in grain through a closed sack, and who drank himself quiet for forty years rather than be noticed, and died of it, and she had buried him under the alders with a bottle of the good stuff because sobriety had never once been kind to him. The twins born to the ferryman's wife, who could finish one another's sentences across a mile of water, and who drowned together at nineteen when the ice went out early — the river taking back what it had lent, their mother said, and never smiled again. Brigid of the high pasture, whose hands could find a fracture through wool and leather, who set bones for three generations and was denounced at last by a man whose leg she had saved, because saving it had hurt him and hurt men look for someone to blame.

Name after name. She gave them slowly, deliberately, the way you lay stones in a wall — each one fitted against the last, no gaps, no hurry. Some of them she had buried with her own hands. Some she had only stood beside the grave for, holding the hat of a grieving husband or the hand of a child too young to understand why everyone whispered. Some had no graves at all, only the places where graves should have been, and for those she gave the places instead: the bend below the weir, the third milestone on the drover's road, the ash tree behind the ruined chapel where the ground stayed soft no matter the season.

And the soldiers in the doorway grew still, one by one, the way a room goes still when a story gets its hooks in. Even the turnip-smelling guardhouse seemed to lean in. Because it was not testimony. Testimony answers questions. This was something older — a reckoning, a ledger of her own, kept not in ink but in flesh, and read aloud at last in the hearing of the machine that had made it necessary.

"—and Sorrel, the midwife's daughter, who could smell sickness coming on a person a week before the fever showed. She warned the whole east hamlet the winter of the sweating cough. Forty souls moved to the far pastures on her word, and not one of them sickened. And when the crown's men came through the next spring asking after rumors, the east hamlet swore to a soul they'd moved for grazing reasons." Wenna paused, and for the first time her mouth bent toward something like a smile, thin and fierce as a February sun. "Forty liars, inquisitor. Best harvest that valley ever raised. Sorrel lived to be eighty. She's under the ash tree. I planted rosemary, because she hated roses."

Voss had not written anything down. Wenna noted that. Whatever the slate-colored eyes were doing, they were not doing clerical work.

"You're protecting someone," Voss said at last. "This recitation — it's admirable, and I believe every word, and it is also a wall. Somewhere behind these names is a living name you're building the wall to hide."

"The wall is the inheritance," Wenna said. "My mother built it. Her mother built it before her. Every gifted child in this valley for six generations was taught the same lesson before they were taught their letters: *the valley forgets you if you let it.* We pass it hand to hand like an heirloom, and the heirloom is silence, and you will not pry it out of my hands, because I have seen what happens to families that drop it." She spread her hands, knotted and brown and steady on her knees. "You can take my body, girl. It's mostly yarrow and stubbornness at this point. But the names stay with me, and the living name stays behind the wall, and when they take me to whatever you do with old women who won't talk, I will die the way Maren's killers intended her to live: as nobody. Unrecorded. A gap in your beautiful book."

For a moment — one breath, no more — something moved behind the inquisitor's certainty, some hairline crack in the wet-slate calm, and Wenna, who had spent a lifetime reading what the body confessed before the tongue caught up, saw it plainly.

Then it sealed, and Voss stood, and the audience was over.

"You'll be transferred to the assize at the capital," she said. "There will be a formal interview. I advise you to reconsider."

"I advise *you*," said Wenna, "to count the names I gave you. Count them, and ask yourself what kind of country needs a wall of graves to feel safe, and then ask yourself which side of the wall you're standing on."

Voss paused at the door. She did not turn around.

"The rosemary," she said quietly. "Someone should see it's tended."

And she was gone, leaving Wenna alone with the turnip smell and the fading sound of boots, holding that single sentence like a coal cupped in both hands — wondering, for the first time in four days, whether the wall might have a crack in it too, and whether cracks, in the right walls, were how the light got in.

They came for her at dawn with a covered cart. As they bound her wrists — gently, she noticed, gently, and she wondered which of the soldiers had a grandmother under an ash tree — Wenna lifted her face to the pale eastern sky over the boundary stones and sent one thought out along the old roads, past the veil, into the wide and dangerous world:

*Run well, kitchen-girl. Run like the names are running with you.*
