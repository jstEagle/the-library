## 22. The Interrogation of Wenna Thornbury

The cell they had given the old woman was the best one in the garrison, and Serane Voss had chosen it herself.

Not out of kindness — she was careful to tell herself that, and carefuler still not to examine why the telling required such care. It was procedure. Elderly detainees died in damp cells, and a dead witness answered no questions, and unanswered questions were the one thing Serane Voss could not abide. The room had a proper window, barred but south-facing, and a bed with a straw tick less than a year old, and a brazier that actually drew. If Wenna Thornbury froze or starved or rotted before she talked, it would not be for want of Voss's diligence.

She read the file a fourth time on the walk down.

*Wenna Thornbury, hedge-healer, Veiled Valley, age sixty-one.* No registration. No prior citation. A woman who had lived her entire life inside the veil's gray shelter and emerged from it only now, dragged out by an Act that did not care how quietly you had kept yourself. The arresting officer's notes were brief: *cooperative. Unarmed. Asked after her cat.*

That last detail had snagged in Voss's mind like a burr in wool. She had interrogated forty-one gifted persons since her commission. They wept, or they raged, or they went glassy and recited the catechisms of whatever hedge-tradition had raised them. Not one of them had asked after a cat.

She pushed open the door.

The old woman sat at the table as though the table were hers and Voss the visitor. She had arranged the two chairs with some private geometry in mind; hers faced the window, and the light came over her shoulder and fell across the empty seat opposite, so that anyone sitting there would be lit like a specimen while she remained half in shadow. Voss noted it, admired it, and moved the chair anyway before sitting down. Two could play at geometry.

Wenna Thornbury watched her do it with an expression of mild, almost grandmotherly approval.

"You'll want the lamp too," the old woman said. "Window light goes by four o'clock this time of year."

"I have my own light." Voss set her satchel on the table: ink, paper, the file, and the small brass lamp she carried everywhere because garrison lamps smoked and smoked light made witnesses look guilty whether they were or not. She did not light it yet. "You are Wenna Thornbury of the Veiled Valley."

"I am."

"You are recorded as unregistered under the Gift Registry Act."

"I expect I am that too."

"You understand what the Act requires of those who are gifted."

"I understand what it requires of everyone," the old woman said. "It requires them to be counted. Whether they're gifted is nearly beside the point, isn't it? You'd count them anyway if you could find a way."

Voss let the silence sit. She had learned silence from a woman named Maren Doss, her first instructor, who said that most people feared a quiet room more than a loud one and would fill it with anything, truth included. She deployed it now and watched Wenna Thornbury decline to fill it. The old woman folded her hands on the table and looked out the window at the drab garrison yard with the air of someone waiting for bread to prove.

"Your neighbor," Voss said at last, "testified that you delivered her granddaughter with a fever that should have killed them both. That your hands ran hot as coals and the child cooled in minutes."

"Hands run hot in every birth. Ask any midwife."

"The same neighbor says you once drew a fever out of a grown man through his wrist and into a bowl of water, which boiled."

"Bowls boil near fires. Your neighbor is old and frightened, and frightened people remember marvels. It's how they make sense of being afraid." Wenna's voice was level, unhurried, the voice of a woman explaining weather to a child. "You've come a long way to ask me about soup bowls, Inquisitor."

Voss opened the file. "There is also the matter of Jonah Thornbury."

And there — there, at last, was the hairline fracture, though Voss would not see it until much later, turning the day over in her mind like a coin she suspected of being clipped. What happened was nothing. That was what made it remarkable. Every other name she had spoken in forty-one rooms had landed somewhere: a flinch, a prayer, a denial too quick by half. This name landed nowhere at all. Wenna Thornbury received it the way a stone wall receives rain.

"What about her?" the old woman said.

"She was a taster in the king's household below the valley. She fled across the boundary stones eight days ago, the morning after the proclamation. Your testimony places you at the stones with her."

"My testimony places me walking near the stones. An old woman walks where she likes."

"You were seen speaking with her. At length."

"I speak at length with everyone. It's a failing of the aged." Wenna turned from the window then, and looked at Voss directly for the first time, and something in the look made Voss's hand pause above the page. The old woman's eyes were pale gray, the color of the valley itself, and they held no fear whatsoever. "You want to know if I helped her go. And you want me to say it in words you can write down. So let me save us both the afternoon: I will tell you everything I choose to tell you, and none of it will be what you need, and we will both be very tired by supper. Or—" and here the old woman leaned forward slightly, and her voice dropped into something almost gentle, "—you can ask me the questions underneath your questions, and I may answer one or two, because you strike me as a woman who has never once been asked the questions underneath her questions either."

Voss had faced down warlocks in chains. She had sat across from a man who could blister skin with a word and had not blinked until he used it. She was not prepared for kindness from a prisoner, and she found, to her considerable irritation, that it worked on her the way no threat ever had.

"The questions underneath," she repeated.

"You believe the gifted are a danger." Wenna said it flatly, without accusation, the way one states that winter follows autumn. "I can see it in you. It isn't cruelty. Cruelty I'd know how to handle. It's belief. Someone taught you that magic is a fire that starts in people, and that lists and locks are the only thing standing between the fire and the thatch. Am I close?"

"The last Dragon War began with—"

"The last Dragon War began with kings," Wenna said, "and ended with dragons, and the gifted were the kindling both times, and the men who wrote your Act knew it, which is why the Act counts healers and tasters and weather-singers instead of asking who armed the armies. But that's not the question underneath. Here it is." She unfolded her hands and laid them flat on the table, palms up, an offering or a surrender, Voss couldn't tell which. "Ask me what happens to a valley full of quiet people when soldiers come counting. Ask me what your registry does to the ones who aren't gifted but get named anyway, by neighbors with grudges. Ask me about Harrowmere."

The word went through the room like cold water through cloth.

Harrowmere. Everyone knew Harrowmere now; it had become a cautionary tale told in two directions at once. To the crown it was proof of resolve. To everyone else it was ash and chalk marks and a child's shoe photographed in a report Voss had signed her name to reviewing. Three hundred souls, of whom perhaps eleven had been genuinely gifted. The arithmetic of it had kept her awake twice since.

"Harrowmere was a tragedy of local excess," Voss said. "The officers involved are under inquiry."

"They're under promotion," Wenna said. "But go on. Tell me about the inquiry. Tell me its name and its findings, when they arrive. I'll wait. I'm good at waiting. I buried a sister after the last purge, and I waited thirty years for anyone to say her name aloud, and no one ever did, because saying it was dangerous. That's what your lists do, Inquisitor. They don't just count the living. They teach the dead to be silent." Her voice had not risen. That was the terrible thing about it. It had gotten quieter, the way deep water is quieter than shallow. "So ask me your real question, and I'll give you a real answer, and then you can decide what to do with a real answer, which I suspect is harder for you than anything else in this building."

Voss sat very still. Somewhere down the corridor a door slammed, and the sound seemed very far away, as though it belonged to another building, another life.

"Why did you help her?" she heard herself ask. It was not the question she had planned. Her planned questions were written neatly on the pad in front of her, fourteen of them, ordered from safe to incriminating, and she abandoned all fourteen in a single breath. "Why her? Why risk — everything, at your age, for one girl?"

And Wenna Thornbury smiled. It transformed her face entirely, the way dawn transforms a field, and Voss understood suddenly that she had been shown the old woman's working face for an hour and only now her true one.

"Because someone helped me, once," Wenna said. "When the soldiers came the last time, a woman I never learned the name of stood in a doorway and swore I was her niece, and the soldiers believed her, and I lived. Thirty years I've been paying that back, a little at a time, to whoever came next. That's the whole of my crime, Inquisitor. I paid forward a debt I couldn't repay backward. Write it down however you like."

* * *

They talked for three hours.

Or rather, Voss asked and Wenna answered, though the shape of it stopped resembling an interrogation somewhere in the second hour and became something Voss had no protocol for. The old woman spoke of the valley's boundary stones and the way the veil lay over them like a blanket over a birdcage — protective, and a cage, and both at once, and she did not pretend otherwise. She spoke of herbs and fevers and the names of children she had caught coming into the world, dozens of them, and she gave these names freely, in full, looking Voss in the eye as she did it, and Voss realized slowly that this was deliberate: *here are names given freely; compare them to the names taken by force and tell me which list your Act belongs to.*

Once, only once, the composure cracked. Voss had circled back — professionally, she told herself, because it was the central fact of the case — to Jonah Thornbury. Where the girl had gone. What she sought. Whether she had spoken of a door.

The old woman's hands, folded on the table, tightened. Just slightly. Just enough that the knuckles whitened, and then smoothed again, so quickly that a less attentive interviewer would have missed it entirely. Voss did not miss it. Voss wrote nothing down, which was its own kind of note.

"I don't know where she's gone," Wenna said, and her voice was perfectly steady again. "If I knew, I wouldn't tell you. But I don't. She ran, and the country she ran into doesn't exist anymore, and that's all any of us know. Perhaps the kingdom that dissolved swallowed her kindly. It swallows everyone eventually."

"There are reports of movement in the Ashreach foothills."

"There are reports of movement in every foothill. Goats move in foothills."

Voss almost laughed. She caught it behind her teeth, appalled at herself, and Wenna saw the catch — of course she saw it, she saw everything — and something passed between them across the table that Voss would spend many nights refusing to name. It was not friendship. It was not sympathy. It was closer to recognition: two women who had each spent their lives keeping careful accounts, meeting across a table where the accounts did not balance.

The lamp had long since been lit; the window had gone blue, then dark. When Voss finally closed the file, the old woman said, "You'll want to know if I'll try to escape."

"The thought had occurred."

"I won't. Running is for the young, and I've done my running." Wenna rose stiffly when the guard came, and paused at the door, and looked back. "Inquisitor. One more thing, free of charge. Whatever you write in that report tonight — you'll leave something out. Everyone does. The only question worth asking yourself is whether you'll leave out the truth to protect the machine, or to protect someone from it. I've known men who did both. Only one of them slept well afterward."

The door closed behind her.

* * *

Voss sat alone in the interview room for a long time after.

The lamp burned down. She did not trim the wick. On the pad before her lay the record of the day's work, and she read it as though it had been written by someone else, some junior clerk whose diligence she was auditing. *Subject cooperative throughout. Subject denies gift. Subject denies knowledge of fugitive's destination. No evidence sufficient to support charges beyond failure to register. Recommend detention pending further inquiry.*

All true. Every line of it true, and between the lines, a lie the size of a cathedral.

Because the report did not say: *the subject described the registry as a machine for teaching the dead to be silent, and I could not refute her.* It did not say: *the subject's hands tightened at the fugitive's name, and I have elected not to pursue the significance.* It did not say: *there is a door, or the fugitive believes there is a door, and the subject knows more of it than she gave, and I have decided — I, Serane Voss, who built my career on the principle that omission is the mother of treason — to let that thread lie unexamined in the file.*

She thought of Maren Doss again. *A report is a weapon,* the old instructor had said, *and like any weapon, it cuts the hand that holds it carelessly.* Voss had never once held one carelessly. Forty-one interrogations, forty-one immaculate files, and her superiors called her the sharpest instrument the Office had produced in a generation, and instruments did not hesitate, and instruments did not sit in dark rooms listening to the echo of an old woman's voice asking whether she protected the machine or someone from it.

She dipped the pen. She wrote the final paragraph in her small, exact hand:

*Subject poses minimal flight risk. Recommend transfer to regional facility for standard processing. No further action regarding the fugitive Thornbury is warranted at this time on the basis of evidence obtained.*

*At this time,* she wrote, and knew it for the coward's hinge it was — the phrase that kept every door open while appearing to close them. *On the basis of evidence obtained,* which was technically accurate, since the evidence she had obtained and declined to record could have filled a second file.

She signed it. Her signature looked exactly as it always looked, and that frightened her more than anything else that day: that the outside of a person could go on being identical while the inside developed a crack, hairline but real, the kind that lets water in, the kind that in stone means the beginning of the end of the wall.

She carried the report to the duty clerk herself. She watched him stamp it. Then she walked out into the garrison yard, where the night smelled of horses and woodsmoke, and stood for a while beneath stars she could not see for the torchlight, thinking about a doorway and an unnamed woman and a debt paid forward across thirty years.

In the morning there would be new orders. There were always new orders. The purge rolls were growing; the capital wanted results; and somewhere north, in mountains that appeared on no current map, a fugitive with a taster's tongue was moving toward something that made even a composed old healer's hands go white at the knuckles.

Serane Voss went inside, and slept badly, and dreamed of lists — but in the dream, when she reached the bottom of every page, the names had all been crossed out in someone else's handwriting, and she could not stop reading them anyway.
