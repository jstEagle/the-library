## 23. Refuge Among Ruins

The rain found her before the monastery did.

Jonah had been walking for two days on a leg that no longer entirely belonged to her — the ford had taken its price, and the river's cold had settled into the wound like a tenant who intended to stay. Each step sent a dull bell of pain tolling up through her hip, and she had learned to walk in a rhythm that kept the bell from ringing too loudly: left foot, breath, right foot, breath. A taster's discipline applied to agony. You did not flinch at the first bitterness. You held it in your mouth and waited for it to pass.

She smelled the place before she saw it — old stone and older smoke, the particular sweetness of lichen eating mortar, and beneath it all something she hadn't encountered since leaving the valley: beeswax. Someone, or something, was keeping candles.

The monastery crouched in a fold of the Ashreach foothills where the road gave up pretending to go anywhere. It had been a grand thing once; you could tell by the way it had fallen. Grand buildings collapse into architecture. Lesser ones simply crumble into rubble. Here there were arches still standing with nothing above them, a cloister open to the sky like a ribcage picked clean, and a chapel whose roof had gone but whose walls rose high enough to hold the rain clouds at a respectful distance. Over the door, the stone had been defaced — not broken, but chiseled smooth, the way the crown erased names from its registries. Whatever saint or king had watched over this threshold, someone had decided he had never existed.

*Like Cael itself,* Jonah thought, leaning against the doorframe while her leg screamed its small opinion. *A country dissolved. A kingdom that officially isn't.*

But the beeswax smell said otherwise. Beeswax meant hands. Hands meant someone.

"Hello," she said to the dark inside, because her mother had taught her that announcing yourself was safer than being discovered, and because her voice sounded strange to her own ears — roughened by two days of silence and river water. "I'm not armed. I'm barely standing. If you're going to kill me, I'd take it as a kindness if you waited until I'd sat down."

Nothing answered. But nothing answered in a listening way, and Jonah had spent twenty years reading rooms by their silences. This silence had weight. It had attention in it.

She went in anyway. At this point, she told herself, the arithmetic of risk had stopped making sense. Behind her were Voss's riders, the checkpoint at Marrow Ford, the chalk marks on doors in Harrowmere, the child's shoe she still saw when she closed her eyes. Ahead of her was a mountain gate that wanted fire instead of iron, and a dragon somewhere in the calderas above, dying, whose last flame was supposedly the only key. She was a woman hunted for a gift she'd spent her whole life hiding, carrying an iron key that opened nothing, following survey fragments drawn by dead cartographers for a kingdom that no longer existed.

Sitting down in a dry ruin seemed, on balance, reasonable.

* * *

The refectory still had its tables — long slabs of black oak gone silver with age, scarred by centuries of knife-points. Jonah lowered herself onto a bench and discovered that her body, given permission to stop, intended to make the stopping permanent. The wound in her thigh had soaked through the bandage Odile's caravan cook had tied at the ford, and the cloth came away with the reluctant stickiness of things healing wrong.

She knew what infection looked like. She had tasted it often enough — the fever-sourness of milk gone off, the particular metallic tang of meat turning. Her own blood, she now discovered, had begun to speak the same language. When she pressed the wound, she could taste it at the back of her throat: copper and rot, sweet underneath, like pears in bad honey.

Bitter almond. That was how this whole ruinous journey had started, wasn't it? Bitter almond in the honeyed pears, and a steward's life saved, and a question asked that should never have been asked.

She laughed, and the laugh turned into something else, and she let it, because there was no one to see and the refectory had heard worse confessions than hers, surely, in its long career of hearing.

That was when the candle appeared.

It came around the corner of the cloister walk carried by a man so old he seemed less a body than a suggestion of one — tall once, stooped now into a question mark, robed in grey wool gone soft as ash. He held the candle out ahead of him like a lantern of his own courage, and his eyes, when they found her, went wide and then narrow and then, strangely, gentle.

"You're bleeding on Brother Anselm's table," he said. His voice was dry as the pages she could smell on him. "He minded that sort of thing terribly."

"I'm sorry," Jonah said, and meant it more than she'd meant anything in days. "I'll bleed on the floor instead."

"No, no." He shuffled closer, peering. "You misunderstand. It's a joke. We are permitted jokes. Even dissolved orders are permitted jokes." He set the candle down and looked at her properly, and whatever he saw — the mud, the blood, the iron key at her belt, the look of a woman three days past the end of her strength — made him sigh. "You're the one the riders are asking after. The kitchen girl. They came through yesterday. Inquisitor's men, with a drawing." He tapped his own cheekbone. "They had your face wrong. Too soft. Whoever drew you has never been hungry."

Jonah's hand moved toward her knife before her mind caught up, and the old monk watched the movement with an expression of profound scholarly interest, as though she were a marginal illustration come to life.

"If I wanted to give you to them," he said mildly, "I would have already. There is a bell rope in the chapel. One pull reaches the garrison at Vell's Crossing." He folded his hands. "I am Brother Cassian. I am, depending on how you count such things, either the last monk of the Order of the Quiet Page, or the caretaker of its corpse. And you, I think, are going to want to hear what my order did. Before you trust anything else about me."

* * *

He fed her first. This was the order's rule, he explained — the hungry are fed before they are questioned, because questions asked of the starving get lies for answers. He brought barley soup with wild thyme, dark bread, and a cup of small beer, and Jonah ate with the careful, total attention of someone who has spent her life tasting for other people and forgotten what it was to taste for herself.

She tasted everything anyway. Old habit. The soup was clean. The bread was clean. The beer was honest. And under it all, faint as a signature, she tasted the valley's absence still clinging to her — that flatness where her gift should have been, the numb limb of it. Outside the veil, her senses were supposed to be awake. Wenna had promised her color too bright, flavors too loud. Instead she'd gotten a gift that flickered like a guttering lamp, unreliable, painful, waking in flashes — enough to taste poison in the river at the ford, enough to save her life and spend her invisibility in the same motion.

"It's real, then," Cassian said quietly, watching her. "What they hunt. The gift."

"Why do you say it like that?"

"Because my order spent four hundred years writing it down." He rose, joints crackling like green firewood, and beckoned. "Come. You should see what we did. It's only fair. You're carrying the consequences."

* * *

The archive lay beneath the chapel, down stairs worn into shallow bowls by generations of feet. Candlelight followed them along walls of pigeonhole shelves, thousands of them, most empty now but some still holding bundles tied with cord gone brittle. The air was cold and dry and smelled of dust and vellum and time, and Jonah felt the strange shiver of recognition she'd had on the roads outside the valley — homesickness for a place she'd never been. This was what the old kingdom had felt like, maybe. Careful. Ordered. Full of names.

"The Order of the Quiet Page served the crown of Cael for four centuries," Cassian said, his voice dropping into the register of a man reciting something he'd recited too many times alone. "We were archivists. Record-keepers. Our founding principle was that a kingdom that does not remember will repeat its cruelties. That memory is mercy." He stopped before a shelf near the back and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth. "We believed it. Some of us died believing it. And then, in the years before the Dragon War, the crown came to us with a request, and we — " his hands tightened on the bundle — "we said yes."

"What request?"

"A census." He unwrapped the oilcloth with the tenderness of a man undressing a wound. Inside were ledgers, dozens of them, their spines labeled in faded ink. "Of the gifted. Every person in Cael born with the sense, catalogued by parish, by family line, by degree of talent. Names, births, mothers' names. My brothers and sisters compiled it with loving precision. Cross-referenced it. Made copies." He set the ledger on the reading table and did not open it. "And forty years later, when the war ended and the new crown needed someone to blame for the fire and the famine and the dragons' wrath, do you know what they reached for?"

Jonah's mouth had gone dry. "The lists."

"The lists." He nodded slowly. "Every purge since has descended from our careful work. Harrowmere. The Vale of Sorrows. Last month, three villages in the lowlands. All of it built on shelves like these. We thought we were building memory. We were building ammunition." He finally opened the ledger, and even from across the table Jonah could see the columns of names marching down the pages like ants. "I have spent fifty years here keeping these, and I have never decided whether I am a historian or a jailer."

"Why show me this?" she whispered. "Why tell me? You don't know me."

"I know what you're looking for." He closed the ledger gently. "The mountain gate. Everyone who comes through here eventually asks about the mountain gate. The door out of dissolved Cael." Something moved across his ancient face — not quite a smile, not quite grief. "Sit, child. Let me tell you the door's true history. It's short. Most true histories are."

* * *

He told it by candlelight, his voice settling into the rhythm of a lesson taught to novices long dead.

"The gate was built in the last decade of the Dragon War, when everyone understood the kingdom was dying. Not by masons — by deserters. Soldiers, tax collectors, judges, priests. People who had done things in the war's name and could not bear to do them anymore. They called themselves the Penitent Company, and they cut a door into the mountain at the border of the world, a door that led beyond magic's reach, beyond the crown's reach, beyond consequence itself." He turned the candle so its light caught the ledger's edge. "Do you understand what I'm telling you? The door was not built for the innocent. It was built for the guilty. It is an escape hatch for people fleeing their own deeds. The innocent had no need of it — innocence stays and endures. Only guilt runs."

"And it works?" Jonah leaned forward despite her leg. "It opens?"

Cassian was quiet for a long moment. The candle flame stood perfectly still between them.

"It has never once opened," he said. "Not in two hundred years. The Penitent Company cut it, sealed it, and wrote the means of opening into their records — and then, one by one, they died at the foot of it, unable to bring themselves to leave. Do you understand? They built a door out of guilt, and guilt would not let them through. The last of them wrote a final entry in our archive. Would you like to hear it? I have read it perhaps a thousand times."

Jonah nodded.

He recited it from memory, his eyes half-closed: "*'We made a door for cowards and found we were not brave enough to be cowards. The lock wants fire — living fire, dragon-fire, the only flame that burns hotter than shame. But we have learned that the door was never locked against us. It was locked by us. May whoever finds this be wiser.'"*

Silence filled the archive like water filling a hull.

"So it's useless," Jonah said at last. Her voice came out flat. "The whole journey. The key, the dragon, the — " She stopped, because finishing the sentence meant saying *Wenna*, and Wenna's name was a door of its own that she could not afford to open yet. "There's no way out."

"There is a way out," Cassian said. "It just costs exactly what you feared it cost, and gives exactly what you expected it to give. Escape. Nothing more. A country beyond magic's reach where you can hide until you die of old age, carrying your names and your dead with you, having changed nothing." He studied her across the candle. "The gifted have run that calculation for two hundred years, child. Run. Hide. Endure. And the purges keep coming, because running changes nothing. The lists remain. The lists always remain."

Something was happening in Jonah's chest — not hope, because hope had been beaten out of her somewhere between the ford and Harrowmere, but its stubborn cousin, the thing that lives in hope's abandoned house and refuses to vacate. Despair, she realized, was not the opposite of action. Despair was fuel. It burned hotter than fear, if you were desperate enough to feed it yourself instead of letting it feed on you.

"The registry vault," she said slowly. "Under the capital. Wenna's key — I thought it was for the mountain door. It's not, is it."

Cassian went very still. Then he laughed — a dry, rusted sound, like a page turning after centuries.

"Oh," he said softly. "Oh, you clever girl. No. It is not for the door." He pushed back from the table and crossed to a shelf Jonah would have sworn was empty, drawing out a scroll case sealed with wax stamped by hands two hundred years dead. "The Penitent Company had another project. Less famous. The vault beneath the capital holds every list ever made — ours, the crown's, the inquisitors', four hundred years of names. And the Penitent Company, being guilty men who understood guilt, cut a second key. A master key. For the day someone braver than they were decided the lists should end." He held the scroll case out to her. "Drawings. Of the key. Of the vault. Of the tunnels beneath the palace that the Company dug when they still had hope."

Jonah took it with hands that shook — from fever, she told herself, only fever.

"Why give me this?" she asked. "You've kept these secrets for fifty years. Why hand them to a bleeding stranger?"

Brother Cassian looked at her for a long moment, and in the candlelight his eyes were wet.

"Because my order built the machine that kills your people," he said, "and I am the last of it, and I cannot burn the archives myself — my hands shake, and my legs fail, and besides, the crown would only rewrite what we destroyed. It needs to be undone from inside, by someone the lists were made to catch." He bowed his head. "Shame seeks absolution through action, child. It's the only prayer I have left. Take my maps. Take my confession. Burn what we made."

* * *

She slept that night in the novice cells, on a straw pallet that felt like a featherbed, with the wound cleaned and bound with linen and honey — *honey*, she thought, remembering pears, remembering bitter almond, remembering a steward named Aldric whom she hoped had found some peace — and fever dreams came and went like weather.

In the morning she was stronger. Not well. But stronger, the way a blade is stronger for having been folded.

She stood in the refectory doorway at dawn, pack on her shoulder, the scroll case against her spine, and found Cassian waiting with bread wrapped in cloth and a candle stub.

"Downriver," he said. "The grain barges stop at the weir below Vell's Crossing. Kitchen work pays passage, if you can stomach cooking for strangers again." His mouth twitched. "Though I suspect you've had practice tasting food you didn't trust."

"Brother Cassian." She hesitated. "The archive. If I don't come back — "

"Then I will keep it," he said simply, "until someone comes who doesn't need me to tell them where to look. Memory is mercy, child. But some memories must be burned to be remembered truly." He pressed the bread into her hands. "Go. The door in the mountain will wait. Doors are patient. It's people who run out of time."

She walked out through the ruined cloister into thin mountain sunlight, her leg aching, her gift flickering awake at the edges of her senses like a lamp finding its oil, and behind her the defaced doorway stood silent over a silence five hundred years deep.

Ahead, downriver, was the capital. The vault. The lists. Her name among them, and her mother's above it.

Despair, Jonah had learned, was just stubbornness that hadn't found its work yet.

Hers had just found it.
