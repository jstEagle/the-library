## 24. The Archivist's Confession

The monastery of Saint Vellum had no name on any map, which was precisely why it had survived.

Brother Cassian had lived within its walls for fifty-one years, and in all that time he had never once heard a bell rung. The order had melted its bells down during the Dragon War — bronze was needed for cannon, the chronicles said, though Cassian suspected the truth was simpler and sadder: a monastery that could not be found by sound could not be summoned to account. They had traded their voice for their silence, and the silence had held for three hundred years.

He was copying out a psalm he did not believe when he heard the girl collapse in the scriptorium doorway.

* * *

Jonah Thornbury had come through the rain like something the storm had decided against keeping. He had watched her from the refectory window — a slight figure in a soaked wool cloak, limping along the goat track that wound up from the valley road, one hand pressed to her left side where, he would later learn, a rider's whip had opened her skin from hip to ribs. She had looked at the monastery for a long moment with the expression of someone weighing whether shelter was worth the risk of being found in it.

Then she had seen him watching, and instead of running — as any sensible fugitive would have done — she had walked straight up to the door and knocked.

That alone told him what she was. Only the hunted knock like that: openly, deliberately, offering themselves to whatever answer came. The guilty slink; the desperate decide.

"You're bleeding," he said, opening the door before she could speak.

"I'm looking for the archive." Her voice was rough as gravel under cart wheels. "The old kingdom's archive. The one that isn't supposed to exist."

Cassian had spent half a century perfecting the mild, unremarkable face of a man who knew nothing, and he deployed it now. "This is a house of prayer, child. We keep candles and bees."

"Brother," she said, and there was something in her tone that made his stomach drop — not threat, exactly, but recognition, the flat certainty of someone who had already been lied to too many times that season, "I've walked nine days through a country that officially doesn't exist. I've slept in the ruins of towns that were struck from maps by men carrying lists. Every list has a source. Every purge has a paper trail. And every paper trail I've followed ends at a door like this one."

She swayed. He caught her elbow. Her skin was fever-hot and cold at once, the way the dying are, and beneath the grime of the road he could smell woodsmoke and blood and something else — something faintly mineral, like struck flint, that he would remember later without understanding.

"Come inside," he said. "You can bleed on our floor while you insult our hospitality."

* * *

Her wound was ugly but clean, and she bore his needlework with the grim patience of someone who had stitched herself before. He worked in the infirmary, a low room that smelled of yarrow and tallow, while she lay propped on straw pallets watching him with eyes that never quite stopped calculating.

"The archive," she said again, when the last stitch was tied.

"You should sleep."

"I slept on a riverbank two nights ago. I dreamed about ledgers. Rows and rows of them, and my mother's name written in the top margin of the first." Her hand drifted to her side, over the bandages. "I'm done sleeping, Brother. Show me the books."

And because he was old, and tired, and because some part of him had been waiting fifty-one years for this exact conversation, Cassian set down his needle and said: "Why do you want to see them?"

She told him. Not all of it — he could hear the seams where she'd cut things out, the valley, the dragon, the door in the mountain that wanted fire — but enough. The registry vault beneath the capital. The iron key hidden in her cloak lining, which she showed him without hesitation, as if daring him to be its next thief. The Great Purge scheduled for month's end, scaffolds already rising in the ceremonial square, thousands of new names to be added to centuries of old ones.

"They're going to burn the registry publicly," she finished. "As theater. While they write new names into new books behind the scenes. And I need to know—" her voice cracked, just once, and she mastered it, "—I need to know how it started. Who wrote the first list. Because I think whoever it was, they're still doing it. Still writing. Century after century. And nobody ever asks who taught them."

The rain filled the silence.

"It was us," Cassian said.

She went very still.

"The Order of Saint Vellum. The Archivists of Cael." He heard his own voice as if it belonged to another man, older and more foolish. "We built the registry. Not the crown. Us."

* * *

He took her down that night, past the chapel with its bell-less tower, past the dormitory where four other brothers slept — the last four, though Jonah didn't need to know that the order had once numbered six hundred — down stairs worn into shallow curves by generations of sandaled feet, into the deep archive where the air tasted of stone and time.

Lamps bloomed along the walls as he lit them, and Jonah made a small involuntary sound.

Shelves. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf, rising into darkness beyond the lamplight, and every shelf heavy with ledgers bound in calfskin and vellum, spines lettered in faded ink. Not hundreds of volumes. Thousands. Tens of thousands. Three hundred years of careful, meticulous, loving record-keeping.

"What am I looking at?" she whispered.

"The Registry of the Gifted of Cael." Cassian's voice sounded distant to himself. "The original. Every copy the crown ever held was made from these. Every purge, every hunt, every burning since the Dragon War — the names all began here. On these shelves. In our handwriting."

He walked to the first case, the oldest, and drew out a ledger so heavy he needed both arms. He laid it open on the reading table beneath the lamp, and Jonah leaned over it, and he watched her face as she read the first page.

*Registry of Persons Bearing the Gift, compiled by command of the Crown of Cael and the Order of Saint Vellum, in the third year of the Dragon War, that the loyal may be known from the dangerous.*

Below it, in a fine clerical hand: names. Columns of names. Ages. Parishes. Descriptions of each person's gift in language so gentle it might have been a gardener cataloguing roses. *Sings to bees. Mends broken crockery by holding it. Dreams true, twice confirmed.*

"My grandmother's name is in there somewhere," Jonah said quietly. "My mother's above hers. Mine below both." She touched the page without turning it. "Who wrote this?"

"Brother Aldous Merrow. Our founder." Cassian turned to the colophon at the back, where the archivist had signed his work, and beneath the signature — Jonah's breath caught audibly — was a second line, smaller, in the same hand:

*Compiled with sorrow. May God forgive the necessity.*

"He believed it was mercy," Cassian said. "That's the thing you have to understand, girl. He wasn't cruel. He thought that by making the list himself — carefully, accurately, with descriptions that emphasized harmlessness — he could protect them. That a registry in gentle hands was safer than rumors in cruel ones. That if the crown was going to count the gifted regardless, better the counting be done by men who wept while they did it."

"And did they weep?"

"Every name. Aldous kept a private journal alongside the registry. Forty years of entries." Cassian's throat tightened. "The entries get shorter every year. By the end they're one line long. The last one says only: *They used the winter list today. I did not watch.*"

"Winter list?"

"The seasonal updates. Names of children born gifted, discovered late, moved between parishes." He closed the great ledger with a sound like a coffin lid. "Aldous died believing he had built a shield. His successors spent three hundred years discovering they had built a sword. But no successor ever put down the pen, because putting it down felt like abandoning the people in the books to worse hands. So we kept writing. Carefully. Sorrowfully. May God forgive the necessity. Generation after generation, telling ourselves the same lie Aldous told: that the list itself is neutral. That it's what they do with it that matters."

Jonah had gone pale beneath her road-grime. "The lists are the purge. Don't you see? There's no 'what they do with it.' A registry of the gifted *is* the killing, waiting to happen. It's just — inventory. Waiting for a buyer."

"Yes," Cassian said simply. "We learned that. Somewhere around the fourth purge, I think. And we kept writing anyway."

"Why?" It came out of her almost as a scream, strangled down to a whisper by the weight of the archive around them. "If you knew — if you *knew* —"

"Because stopping felt like murder too." He was shaking now, an old man's tremor that had nothing to do with age. "Because every archivist who inherited those shelves inherited ten thousand living names, and if we stopped updating, the crown would take the archive from us and update it themselves, worse, faster, without the gentle descriptions, without the sorrow. We were hostages of our own compassion. Do you understand? We couldn't put down the pen because the pen was the only leash we had on them."

"No." Jonah's voice was very quiet now, and very hard. "No, Brother. You couldn't put down the pen because then you'd have to admit what the pen had done. There's a difference, and the difference is three hundred years of dead people."

The lamp flame bent in a draft from the stairwell. Neither of them moved.

* * *

Later — much later, hours past midnight — they sat at the reading table with a decanter of plum brandy between them, and Cassian told her the rest.

How the order had survived the dissolution of Cael by becoming invisible, its charter folded into a minor monastic rule, its purpose unspoken but never forgotten. How every crown, republican council, and warlord since had eventually found its way up the goat track, hat in hand, asking politely for the current lists. How the brothers had always complied, always with sorrow, always with the same colophon in the same humble hand.

How, thirty years ago, a young novice named Cassian had asked his abbot why they didn't simply burn it all, and the abbot had looked at him with terrible gentleness and said: *Because then we would be the ones who lost the names. And a people whose names are lost are a people who never existed. Someone must remember them, even if remembering is the knife.*

"And I believed him," Cassian said. "For thirty years I believed him. I'm still not certain he was wrong. That's the horror of it, girl — I still don't know. Maybe memory is worth its price. Maybe someone should keep the ledger of the dead even if the ledger gets the dead killed." He drained his brandy. "But I know this. My abbot died in his bed, honored. Aldous died in his bed, revered. And the people in the books died in ditches, in purges, in the ash of Harrowmere and forty towns like it, and not one of them was ever asked whether they wanted to be remembered at that price."

Jonah was silent for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had changed — steadier, colder, the voice of someone who had crossed a boundary at dawn and never stopped walking since.

"The vault beneath the capital. The registry there. Is it a copy of this, or is this a copy of it?"

"This is the root. The capital holds the working copies — the ones the inquisitors use. But the master ledgers, the originals, the chain of custody going back to Aldous's own hand — they're here." He hesitated. "And here is the thing I have not told my brothers. Here is the thing I have carried alone for eleven years."

He rose, went to the oldest case, and knelt. From beneath the bottom shelf he drew a slim volume, wrapped in oilcloth, that no catalogue recorded.

"Aldous's journal. The complete one. And with it—" his hands were trembling again, "—a letter. Written by Abbot Serlo in the year of the Harrowmere purge, addressed to whoever came after him. To me. I found it after he died, and I have never shown it to another living soul."

He unwrapped it and pushed it across the table. Jonah read by lamplight, her lips moving slightly, and Cassian watched her read the words he knew by heart:

*To my successor: If you are reading this, then I failed, and you have inherited the failure. Hear an old man's confession. We tell ourselves the list protects them. It does not. The list is the hunt's map, and we are the mapmakers, and no amount of sorrow in the margins changes the ink. If ever there comes to this house a person whom the list would destroy — a runaway, a marked one, someone the ink is hunting — give them everything. The journal. The keys. The truth. Let them end what we could not. We built the altar; let someone else finally refuse the sacrifice. This is the only absolution our order will ever be offered, and it will not come from God. It will come from fire.*

Jonah looked up. Her eyes were wet, but her jaw was set like the boundary stones of the valley.

"Your abbot waited thirty years to say that," she said, "and then hid it under a shelf."

"He was a coward," Cassian agreed. "So am I. That's why I'm giving it to you instead of striking the match myself. I've stood in this room a thousand nights with a lamp in my hand and told myself tomorrow. Fifty-one years of tomorrows." He reached across the table and closed her fingers around the oilcloth bundle, pressing the journal and the letter and the cold iron key she already carried together into her palm. "You climbed our mountain in the rain with a hole in your side. You knocked. You asked who wrote the first list. I think you're the person Serlo meant. I think you've been the person Serlo meant since the day you were born with a tongue that cannot be poisoned."

"What do you want me to do?" she asked, though he saw in her face that she already knew.

"Burn it." The word tore out of him like something surgical. "All of it. The shelves, the ledgers, the copies in the capital, the whole rotten genealogy of ink. Burn the names out of the world's memory — no. No, that's not it." He stopped, and started again, slower, finding the truth as he spoke it. "Burn the *lists*. Not the people. The people were never the danger; the lists were. Let the gifted be forgotten by the crown and remembered only by those who loved them. That's the absolution. That's the only penance this order can offer, and we can't even perform it ourselves. We can only beg."

He slid off his chair, and to his own distant surprise, knelt — an old monk kneeling on cold stone before a wounded kitchen girl with a thief's eyes and a dead woman's key.

"I am begging," he said. "On behalf of six hundred dead archivists and five living cowards. Destroy what we made. Give us the only forgiveness that counts — the kind that comes too late to save anyone, but not too late to stop the next name being written."

The lamps burned. The rain came down on the bell-less tower. And Jonah Thornbury looked down at the old man kneeling among the shelves of the dead, and then at the bundle in her hands, and Cassian saw her arrive at the thought that would carry her downriver to the capital:

The vault below the palace held the working copies. But the crown's ceremony at month's end — the Great Purge, the public burning that was pure theater — would draw every inquisitor, every guard, every eye in the city to the square above.

While below, in the dark, the real registry would sit unguarded for exactly as long as the fire upstairs lasted.

"You'll have your absolution, Brother," she said quietly. "But I'll need maps of the service tunnels, ink, good paper, and a face that passes for a taster."

And Brother Cassian, last of the Archivists of Cael, wept — and began, at last, to write toward the ending of all his order's writing.
