## 26. Downriver to the Capital

The barge was called *Patient Merrow*, which Jonah thought was either a joke or a prayer, and after three days aboard she decided it was both.

She had found it at the derelict docks of Millbrook, where the river Sennet widened and slowed and forgot itself on its way to the capital. The dockmaster's shed had burned in some older trouble — the char still smelled faintly of it — and so the hiring happened the way hiring happened everywhere in dissolved Cael: by looking, by nodding, by not asking. A broad woman with forearms like mooring rope stood at the gangplank counting sacks of barley onto the deck, and Jonah simply picked up a sack and carried it.

The woman watched her make four trips before she said anything.

"You're thin."

"I'm hungry."

"That's not what I asked."

"It's the answer I have," Jonah said, and set down the fifth sack without letting it bang, because grain bruised like people did, and something in the woman's face shifted at that — the small respect of one careful person recognizing another.

"Name?"

"Marren." It was the name stitched inside the collar of the coat she'd traded for in Harrowmere's outskirts, taken off a dead man who no longer needed it. She'd been Marren for eleven days now. She was getting used to answering to it the way you got used to a stone in your shoe.

"Marta Vell," the woman said. "Cook's dead, cook's daughter ran off with a tinker, and I've forty mouths to feed between here and Corvane and no one to feed them. Can you cook?"

"I can keep forty men from poisoning themselves," Jonah said, "which is most of cooking."

Marta Vell laughed once, a bark like a seal, and jerked her chin toward the stern. "Galley's aft. Don't burn my boat."

* * *

The galley of the *Patient Merrow* was a coffin with a stove in it — six feet by eight, low-beamed, black with decades of grease that no amount of scraping would ever fully confess to. Jonah stood in it for the first time and felt something loosen in her chest that she hadn't known was knotted.

It was the smell, she decided later. Charred onion and river damp and old smoke and the ghost of ten thousand meals. It smelled like the palace kitchen under the old king, like the apprentices' corridor where she'd slept at nine years old with her shoes for a pillow, like Mistress Hale's iron pot that never fully cooled. She had spent two months being hunted through a country that officially did not exist, sleeping in ditches and burned houses and once, memorably, in the hollow of a fallen oak with a family of weasels complaining about it all night, and she had not realized until this moment how much of her fear had been the fear of never smelling this again.

She lit the stove. She inventoried: barley, salt pork, dried peas, a sack of onions going soft at the necks, hard cheese, a crock of honey gone sugary at the edges, pepper in a tin so old the label had worn to illegibility. Forty mouths. She did the arithmetic the way Mistress Hale had taught her, standing behind her small shoulder with a wooden spoon like a scepter: *count your eaters first, girl, then your days, then your pots. Hunger is just arithmetic that hasn't been done yet.*

By dusk the first watch came below decks stamping river cold out of their boots, and found pea porridge with fried onions and a slick of honey over it, and bread she'd raised from a starter she'd coaxed out of flour and water and sheer stubbornness, and they ate it in the silence of men who had expected worse, and then one of them — a boy of maybe sixteen with a chipped front tooth — said, "Mistress Marta said we'd get slop," and another said, "Mistress Marta lies," and Marta herself, passing the galley door, looked in at Jonah stirring the pot and gave her a single nod that Jonah understood was worth more than wages.

That night, wedged into the bunk above the coal store, warm as a loaf, Jonah lay awake and catalogued what she had.

She had a coat with a dead man's name in it. She had a forged journeyman's pass good enough for river work, bought from a forger in Millbrook whose hands shook but whose seals were beautiful. She had an iron key with no lock, sewn into her waistband against her skin, cold even there, cold always. And she had a tongue that could taste bitter almond in honeyed pears, rot in wine sealed since before the Dragon War, nightshade steeped in tea, arsenic in the whitewash of a burned village — a tongue that had kept her alive in a king's kitchen for fifteen years and had nearly hanged her when the Gift Registry Act made immunity itself a confession.

In the Veiled Valley, that tongue had been a liability. Out here, on the water, she was beginning to understand it might be something else entirely.

* * *

The first checkpoint came on the fourth morning, at the weir-lock below Tarn Ford, where the crown had thrown a chain across the river and built a timber tower beside it, and soldiers in the new dynasty's colors — black and gold, gold and black, Jonah couldn't decide which way round and suspected the heralds couldn't either — waved barges through one at a time.

Marta came aft to find her while they waited in the queue, the current shouldering the hull lazily.

"They'll board us," she said. "They'll look at papers, walk the deck, poke in the hold. Sometimes they take a passenger if they feel like it. There's a levy on grain moving toward Corvane, so they'll count sacks too, and they'll count wrong on purpose, and I'll pay the difference because arguing costs more than barley." She looked at Jonah steadily. "You'll be in the galley. Cooks don't interest them. Keep it that way."

"Am I interesting?" Jonah asked mildly, kneading dough.

"You're a stranger with no history who showed up strong and quiet and asks nothing," Marta said. "On my boat that makes you treasure. On their ledger it makes you a question. Questions are expensive. Don't be one."

The soldiers boarded at midmorning. There were five of them, led by a sergeant with a waxed mustache and the particular boredom of a man who has searched a thousand barges and found nothing worth finding in any of them. He took Marta's manifest, walked the deck, kicked a sack or two, and sent two men down into the hold while he drifted toward the galley door with the aimless curiosity of the bored.

Jonah had the stew going. She'd planned for this, actually — she'd been planning for it since dawn, in the same part of her mind that used to plan the king's breakfasts, the part that never stopped running scenarios. The stew was mutton, such as it was, from a wether Marta had bought at Tarn Ford, and it smelled the way mutton stew is supposed to smell: honest, unremarkable, faintly greasy. She'd considered making it smell better and decided against it. Delicious invited tasting. Adequate invited leaving.

The sergeant appeared in the galley doorway, ducking under the beam, his nose wrinkling at the steam.

"Cook, are you?"

"Journeyman, sir." She touched her forehead, didn't stop stirring. "Marren. Papers are with the mistress."

"Papers, papers." He said it the way a man says a word too many times until it means nothing. His eyes moved around the galley — the pots, the crocks, the knife rack, her. "Where'd you learn? You've got an accent. Northern."

"The Veiled Valley," she said, and let a little weariness into it, the weariness of a place rather than a lie. "Valley food. Nobody's heard of it. That's how valley food likes it."

"Hm." He scratched under his mustache. "Lot of folk coming downriver out of the valley lately. Registry business. You registered?"

And there it was — the question, dropped casually as a hook into still water, and Jonah felt the old cold move through her, the cold she knew from fifteen years of tasting death in small doses before breakfast. But she had learned something in two months of flight that she had never needed to learn in the valley: fear, performed well, is indistinguishable from its opposite. She let her shoulders drop half an inch. She let the spoon slow.

"Registered?" She almost laughed, and the laugh came out wrong, brittle, exactly right. "Sir, I left because my mother died and there was nothing left there for me but her grave and my aunt's opinions. If the crown wants me to walk back up that road to write my name in a book, the crown can come carry my pots." She gestured at the galley with the spoon, flinging a droplet of gravy with fine contempt. "This is what I am. Stew and dishes. Write that in your registry."

The sergeant looked at her for a long moment. Then, unexpectedly, he grinned — a gap-toothed, human grin — and said, "My wife says the same about me. Stew and dishes." He tapped the doorframe twice, some soldier's superstition, and withdrew.

Through the galley's little porthole Jonah watched him leap back to the patrol skiff, saw the chain lowered, felt the *Patient Merrow* shudder as the current took her again.

Marta appeared twenty minutes later, counted her crew with her eyes, found all of them present, and said only, "Stew and dishes," with the ghost of a smile, and went forward again.

But Jonah stood at her stove with her heart going like a drum and realized, slowly, with something like wonder, that she wasn't shaking. She had lied to an armed officer of the crown in open daylight and her hands were steady on the spoon, and underneath the fading adrenaline was a feeling she had to turn over twice before she could name it.

Joy. Quiet, unglamorous, competent joy. The joy of a tool doing exactly what it was made for.

They had honed her in captivity — nine years old, dosed drop by drop, taught to know poison the way other children learned their letters, taught never to ask why. They had built her into an instrument and pointed her at a king's plate. Now the instrument had turned itself around and was pointing the other way, and every skill they'd beaten into her was a rung in her ladder out.

*Mistress Hale,* she thought, stirring, watching the river slide past the porthole, *you'd be furious. You'd also be proud. Mostly furious.*

* * *

There were four more checkpoints between Tarn Ford and Corvane, and each one taught her something new, and she collected the lessons the way she'd once collected tastes: greedily, systematically, filing each one where she could find it again.

At the second lock she learned that the soldiers always ate whatever was offered, because soldiers were fed badly and often, and that a bowl of stew placed in a sergeant's hands opened doors that papers could not. She began cooking extra at every checkpoint. By the third lock, the patrolmen were waving the *Merrow* through before the chain was fully down, calling out, "Is the valley woman cooking today?" — and Jonah would lift a hand from the galley doorway, and think: *they're naming me by the place I fled. Let them. A name that points the wrong direction is better than armor.*

At the fourth checkpoint, though, the lesson turned darker.

A new tower had been raised there, taller than the others, and flying from it was not the black-and-gold of the regular garrison but a plain grey banner with a single white circle — the sigil of the Inquisition. Jonah saw it from a mile off and spent that mile very carefully appearing to trim a lamp wick.

The inquisitorial inspectors boarded without asking. There were three of them, and they were different from the soldiers the way a scalpel is different from a hammer. One carried a ledger. One carried a case of instruments Jonah chose not to examine closely. The third — a young man with ink-stained fingers and the apologetic air of a clerk forced outdoors — went directly to Marta and asked for crew manifests, and then, horribly, asked to interview the crew.

"Not all of them," the clerk said. "Just anyone hired in the last month. New hires especially. There's a circular." He produced a paper, and Marta read it, and Jonah, watching from the galley porthole, saw Marta's face do something very small and very controlled.

She knew that face. She'd seen it on Steward Aldric's face, once, the day after she saved his life with a lie about the pears — the face of a decent person holding a bad order and deciding, visibly, what to do with it.

Marta read the circular twice. Then she folded it, handed it back, and said, loud enough to carry to the galley, "New hires. Fine. My new hire is my cook. She's been at the stove since dawn; go bother her if you must, but touch my pots and we'll be here till winter."

The clerk came to the galley. Up close he looked barely older than the boy with the chipped tooth, and tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep.

"Name?"

"Marren."

"Hired when?"

"Millbrook. Eleven days ago."

"Previous employment?"

"Palace kitchens," Jonah said, and watched his eyebrows rise, and continued smoothly, "the provincial palace at Vael's Rest, before it closed. I've cooked for garrisons, monasteries, and one wedding with four hundred guests and a bride who cried through the entire meal. References available from no one living, mostly."

Something flickered at the corner of the clerk's mouth — sympathy, maybe, or recognition, the look of a young man who had also lost an institution and was drifting in the wreckage of it. He wrote *Marren, cook, itinerant* in his ledger. Then he paused, pen hovering.

"There's a supplementary question," he said, not meeting her eyes. "For kitchen staff specifically. Have you, at any point in your employment, detected poison by means other than ordinary caution?"

The galley was very quiet. The stew murmured. Somewhere forward, a deckhand shouted a number.

Jonah looked at the young clerk, and understood suddenly that he hated this question — that someone had written it into his circular and he had to ask it and he already knew what answer he'd get and what the answer would cost whoever gave it honestly. She had seen that look before too. On Aldric. On Wenna. On every person caught between a machine and the people the machine was chewing.

"No, sir," she said. "I've tasted things that disagreed with me. That's why cooks spit."

He almost smiled. He wrote it down — she caught the words *no irregularities* — thanked her in a murmur, and left.

That night Jonah sat on the cargo hatch under a sky rinsed clean of everything but stars, and let herself feel the full strange weight of what she'd just done. She had answered the one question that could hang her, and answered it with a joke, and been believed — believed because she had spent fifteen years learning exactly how a guilty person hides, which meant she knew precisely how an innocent one stands.

They had trained her to be invisible in plain sight. The training held. It held everywhere.

*Corvane,* she thought, and the word sat in her chest like a swallowed coal. *The capital. The vault. Every gifted name ever written down, hers among them, her mother's above it.*

Wenna's key pressed its cold line against her hip, patient as a promise.

* * *

They came into Corvane on a grey morning with rain coming down like the river had decided to change careers, and the city rose out of the wet the way a mountain rises out of cloud — gradually, then all at once, then overwhelmingly. Jonah stood at the rail with the deckhands and looked at it and felt her stomach do something complicated.

She had seen the capital once, at fourteen, on the royal progress, from the window of a supply wagon. She remembered spires and smoke and a great bridge hung with banners for a king who was already dying. This was not that city. The banners now were black and gold, and there were more of them, far more than any celebration required — banners on the bridge, banners on the customs house, banners strung across streets like bunting for a festival that hadn't been announced yet. And on the customs quay, dominating the waterfront, stood a scaffold.

Not a hanging scaffold. Something larger, stranger — a platform of fresh pale timber rising in the great square visible from the river, ringed already by barriers, with a long low structure at its center that might have been an altar or a pyre or a stage, and workmen swarming over it like ants on a kill.

"What is it?" asked the boy with the chipped tooth, squinting through the rain.

"Great Purge ceremony," said the bosun, who had made this run before and spoken of it the way sailors speak of reefs. "Day after tomorrow, they say. The crown burns the old registry publicly — all them lists of the marked, gone up in flame before God and everybody. Reconciliation, they're calling it." He spat over the rail. "Reconciliation with a scaffold that size. I've seen festivals. That's not a festival. That's a statement."

Jonah said nothing. She stood at the rail with rain running off her chin and looked at the scaffold, and beneath the dread — and there was dread, thick and cold — there was also a hunter's attention, quiet and total, the same attention she gave a dish that smelled slightly wrong.

*A public burning of the registry.* Her mind moved fast and cold. *Burned in front of crowds. Which means the registry has to be brought out. Which means—*

Or it was theater. Cassian's archives had warned her: the order that built the vault had learned centuries ago that the safest way to hide a list was to announce its destruction. Burn a copy in the square, keep the true shelves below. She had no proof. She had a key with no lock and a certainty growing in her like a second heartbeat.

"*Merrow!*" Marta's voice cut the rain. "Berth assignment's at Customs Quay Three! Everyone below, we unload before the soldiers wake up hungry!"

The barge swung toward the quay. Ahead, stevedores and clerks and a line of carts waited in the drizzle, and beyond them, somewhere under the city's wet stone, a vault full of names was waiting too — hers among them, her mother's above it, and Wenna's, and Wenna's sister's, and ten thousand others pressed flat in ledgers like flowers in a book that killed them.

Jonah went below to her galley and began, calmly, to make breakfast for forty.

Two days, she thought, cracking eggs into a basin with hands that did not shake. Two days to find a way down.

The rain fell. The city grew. The *Patient Merrow* — joke and prayer both — carried her in.
