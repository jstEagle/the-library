## 27. The Crown's Kitchen

The papers said her name was Mara Vell.

Jonah had written it herself, three weeks ago, in a hand she had practiced until it stopped being hers — a slightly cramped script, a tendency to press too hard on the downstrokes, the *r*'s closed like little doors. Cassian's forger had aged the documents in ash water and folded them against a hot iron until the creases looked like years. The seal of the Guild of Household Servants sat in the corner, wax the color of dried blood, and beneath it the words that had opened every door between the monastery and the capital: *Mara Vell, taster, discharged with honor from the household of Lord Ambrose of Dellin, references available.*

References available. That was the beautiful part, Jonah thought now, standing in the vast white gleam of the crown's under-kitchen while a woman with a ledger looked her up and down. There were no references. Lord Ambrose of Dellin had died childless eleven years ago and his household records had burned with his house in one of the purges — a fact Cassian had known because the monastery kept lists of what the crown destroyed, too. A dead man's dead household could confirm anything.

"You were with Lord Ambrose," the woman said. Her name was Mistress Halloway, and she had the eyes of someone who had spent thirty years counting spoons. "Before the fire."

"Before the fire," Jonah agreed. She let her shoulders curve inward, just slightly. Mara Vell was competent but cowed; Mara Vell had survived something and did not like to speak of it. "I don't remember much about it, mistress. I'd rather not."

Halloway made a small note. "You'll want to be careful saying even that much here. The crown doesn't love talk of fires."

"No, mistress."

"Where did you learn your trade?"

"In service, mistress. My mother was a cook before me." This was true, which was the safest kind of lie to build on. "She taught me to taste before I taught me to read."

"Hm." Halloway looked at her hands. Jonah had thought about this too — had spent an evening rubbing walnut stain into the creases and around the short-trimmed nails so they would look like working hands, hands that had been in kitchens since childhood, because a taster's hands should show her history and a forged woman's hands would have been too clean. "The pay is two crowns a month and your keep. You'll taste everything that passes to the king's table and the table of the Lord Protector. You'll taste in order of rank. You'll report anything bitter, metallic, or strange, and you will report it *immediately*, not after you've decided whether you're imagining it. Do you understand?"

"Yes, mistress."

"And if you're wrong — if you cry poison where there is none — you'll be flogged and dismissed. If you're right, you'll be thanked. If you're right twice, you may be noticed." Halloway's voice went dry as old flour. "Being noticed in this palace is not always a blessing. Ask yourself whether you want it."

"I want the work, mistress," Jonah said, and meant it in four different directions at once, only one of which Halloway could see.

There was a pause. Somewhere above them, muffled through floors of stone, the palace went on being the palace — footsteps, a door, the far-off clatter of the great hall being prepared for the evening meal. Below them, though Jonah had to take this on faith and on Cassian's maps, the vault waited: forty feet of rock and older masonry, shelves of ledgers, centuries of names. Hers among them. Her mother's above it.

*Directly below your feet,* Cassian had said, tapping the archive drawing until the vellum wore thin at the spot. *They built the kitchens over it deliberately. Who watches a vault better than the people who feed it?*

"The post has been open five months," Halloway said, and there it was — the question under the question, the thing every hiring mistress asked and every servant dreaded. "The last taster left suddenly."

Jonah let one heartbeat pass. Two. Long enough to seem reluctant, not long enough to seem rehearsed. "Left how, mistress?"

"That's not your concern." But Halloway's pen scratched, and Jonah understood that the answer had been recorded somewhere, in some file, in some careful list — and that somewhere in the machinery of this building, a clerk knew exactly how the last taster had left, and whether it had been feet first or in irons or not at all. The registry was not only beneath her feet. It ran through the whole palace like a root system, feeding everything above it.

"I only ask," Jonah said quietly, "because I've been turned away from three posts for asking nothing, and once for asking everything. I'm trying to learn the difference."

Something moved behind Halloway's counting-house eyes — not warmth exactly, but recognition. The look of one survivor of kitchen politics acknowledging another. "You'll do," she said. "Start tomorrow. Dawn bell. Don't be late; the king's breakfast waits for no one's conscience."

* * *

The dawn bell found her already awake, already dressed, already standing in the narrow stone cell they had given her — a real cell, with a straw pallet and a hook for her one spare dress, so like the room she'd slept in as an apprentice that she woke the first morning with her heart hammering and her mother's name half-spoken into the dark.

The work itself was almost restful. That was the obscenity of it, and she knew it, and she did it anyway.

She tasted the king's breakfast.

It came up from the lower kitchens on covered dishes carried by boys who never spoke, and it arrived at the tasting station where Jonah stood with her small silver spoon and her goblet of water and her slate for notes. Porridge with cream. Smoked river trout. Bread still warm, honey, stewed quinces. She took each in turn, a measured mouthful, held it on her tongue the way Wenna had taught her to hold a leaf to the light — patiently, without wanting anything from it — and waited for the tongue to tell her the truth.

The porridge was porridge. The trout was excellent, faintly of alder smoke. The honey was honey, and the quinces were quinces, and nothing in any of it whispered *almond* or *arsenic* or the green-copper ghost of hemlock, and she wrote *all clear* on her slate in Mara Vell's cramped hand and passed the dishes upward, and somewhere above her a man she had never seen ate food whose safety lived for a few seconds inside her mouth.

Nine days she did this. Then twelve. Then a fortnight.

She learned the rhythms of the under-kitchen the way she had once learned the valley's boundary stones: by walking them until her body knew them better than her mind. The pastry ovens ran hottest before noon. The fish came at dawn and again at dusk, packed in ice from the mountain road. The head cook, a broad silent man called Perrin who had lost two fingers to a cleaver and said it had been worth it, ruled his domain with a wooden spoon and a vocabulary of single raised eyebrows. Nobody asked Mara Vell questions. Tasters were a necessary unpleasantness, like drains. They were fed apart, spoken to briefly, and pitied in the particular way servants pity anyone whose job might kill them.

And all the while, beneath her feet, the vault.

She felt it sometimes. Not literally — the gift didn't work that way, wasn't a compass, couldn't smell paper through forty feet of stone. But she felt it the way you feel a grave in a field you've been told is a grave: knowledge pressing up through ordinary ground, changing how each step lands. When she walked the long corridor from the tasting station to her cell, she counted her paces. Thirty-one to the point where, according to Cassian's map, the first shelf of ledgers began. She tried not to think of the names down there in the dark. She failed most nights. *Thornbury, Jonah.* Written in some clerk's careful hand, probably beside a column of dates and dispositions, probably with her mother's name directly above it, the way names stack in ledgers, the way the dead stack under the living.

On the fifteenth day, Perrin stopped beside her station while she rinsed her spoon.

"You eat like a bird," he said. It was the longest sentence he'd addressed to her.

"I taste. I don't eat."

"My last taster ate like a horse." He watched the water spiral in the basin. "Grew fat on fear, my mother used to say. Eating everything twice, once for the king and once for herself, in case." His eyebrow rose, a full question mark. "You're not afraid."

It was not a compliment. Jonah set the spoon down carefully, buying a moment. *Nerve-shredding calm*, she thought — that was the whole trade, that was the tightrope, and here was Perrin with his cleaver-scarred hand telling her she was walking it wrong. A taster who feared nothing was either very good or very wrong. A taster who feared nothing in this palace, in this season, with the Purge scaffolds going up in the square outside — she had seen them from the scullery window, black timber rising like winter trees — a taster like that might be many things, but innocent was not one of them.

"I'm afraid all the time," she said. "I've just had practice keeping it in my stomach instead of my face. Where I served before, showing fear got you less food, not more."

Perrin considered this. Whatever he saw seemed to satisfy him, or at least to settle him. "Keep it there," he said, and moved off down the line, and Jonah exhaled slowly and understood that she had just passed a second interview, unannounced and unwinnable, and that there would be more. The palace interviewed you constantly. Every corridor was a form to fill out with your body.

* * *

In the third week, they gave her the evening service, which meant the Lord Protector's table, which meant the wine.

She had been dreading the wine since the day she was hired. Not because of the wine — she could taste arsenic in a vintage through a foot of oak, could find bitter almond hiding in honeyed pears, and alcohol only sharpened her tongue rather than dulling it. She dreaded it because of the ritual. In the great houses, the taster drank first, visibly, from the cup that would be offered, and held the empty glass upside down to show it clean, and this was theater as much as safety — a performance of trust performed by the one person in the room with the least reason to trust anyone.

Mara Vell could perform it. Jonah Thornbury could not, quite, because Jonah Thornbury had spent her whole life being careful with drink — not refusing it, never refusing it, refusal was a flag — but sipping, wetting the mouth, letting the glass travel to her lips often enough to pass. A woman who barely touched wine was a woman with a secret. She had learned that lesson in a valley kitchen at nineteen, when a visiting steward had narrowed his eyes at her untouched glass and asked, in a voice like a knife being drawn slowly, why she didn't drink.

She poured the Lord Protector's wine into the tasting glass. A dark red, heavy, from the southern slopes. She raised it. She let it touch her lips. And then, in the half-second where the glass tilted and no one could count what went down — she had gotten very good at the half-second — she tasted it fully and swallowed almost nothing.

Black cherry, leather, a thread of smoke. Beneath it, faint and wrong, something mineral. Not poison. She chased it with her tongue the way Wenna had taught her to chase a fever through a pulse: not the flavor itself but its edges, its company. Iron. Old iron, and stone dust, and the ghost of a cellar that was not a wine cellar.

Her stomach dropped through the floor, through the tasting station, through forty feet of rock, into the vault.

The wine had been stored down there. Or near down there. Carried up through the same passages, past the same doors, breathing the same air as the ledgers — as the names. She was drinking, in the thinnest dissolved trace, the place where her mother's name lay shelved in the dark.

"Mara." The under-steward was waiting, bored, holding out his hand for the inverted glass.

She turned the glass over. One drop fell and was gone. "Clean," she said, and her voice did not shake, and she marveled at it distantly, the way you marvel at a rope bridge holding.

That night she lay on her pallet and did not sleep and let herself, for the first time since the monastery, feel the true shape of what she was doing. She had come back to the work that had endangered her, taken up the spoon and the slate and the half-second sip, wrapped herself in the exact profession the crown used to hunt her kind — because her immunity was the crime, and her immunity was also the qualification, and the irony was so complete it had circled past bitterness into something almost like comfort. They wanted a taster. They had hired one. They simply hadn't understood what a taster was: a person whose whole body was an instrument for detecting what powerful people put in other people's mouths. Poison. Lies. Names.

She was the best taster the crown had ever employed, and they would kill her for it if they ever understood her fully.

Sleep came eventually, shallow and full of stairs.

* * *

On the morning of the nineteenth day, the summons came.

It arrived the way such things arrive in palaces — not as a shout but as a silence, a small cold current moving through the warm body of the kitchen. A page in crown livery appeared at the tasting station, cleared his throat, and announced that the new taster was requested for interview by the Office of Inquiry, regarding routine vetting of household staff, at the fourth bell.

Routine. The word landed in her chest like a dropped knife.

Perrin heard it. Everyone heard it. The kitchen's noise dipped and resumed, the way water closes over a stone, and in the closing-over Jonah read everything: the last taster had been summoned for routine vetting too. The last taster had left suddenly. The last taster's name was probably in a file downstairs, in a drawer, in a ledger, in ink.

She finished the morning's tasting first. That was important — she made herself finish it, made her hands steady through the porridge and the trout and the honey, made her slate say *all clear* in Mara Vell's cramped script, because whatever happened at the fourth bell, the work came first, the work was the armor, and a taster who abandoned her station on summons day was a taster with something to hide.

Then she washed her spoon, hung her apron on its hook, and followed the page up out of the under-kitchen, up the service stair, up through the strata of the palace — kitchens, offices, corridors of state — climbing away from the vault, away from the names, toward the light and the Office of Inquiry.

At the landing, the page paused and looked back at her with the flat professional courtesy of someone who delivers people to things.

"They'll ask about your previous posts," he said. "Just so you know."

"I know," Jonah said.

What she knew, walking on, was larger than that. She knew that the Office of Inquiry was Serane Voss's office; she had heard the name in the caravan, in the monastery, in every warning Odile and Cassian had ever given her, spoken the way sailors speak of a storm they are trying not to summon. She knew that Voss vetted palace staff. She knew that somewhere in that office was the valley file, and that the valley file contained Wenna's testimony, and that Wenna's testimony described a kitchen girl with a suspiciously accurate tongue who had fled across the stones at dawn.

And she knew — with the same terrible clarity with which she could find bitter almond in honeyed pears — that the woman waiting upstairs had built her career on noticing exactly the things other people trained themselves not to see.

The fourth bell rang as she reached the door.

Jonah Thornbury, who was Mara Vell, who was nobody, straightened her apron, arranged her fear neatly in her stomach where it belonged, and knocked.
