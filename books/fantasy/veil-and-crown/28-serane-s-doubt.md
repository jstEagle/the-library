## 28. Serane's Doubt

The palace kitchens smelled of bread and fear, and Serane Voss had learned to tell them apart.

Bread was warm, yeasty, honest. Fear was subtler — a stillness in the scullery boys when she passed, a too-careful arrangement of hands among the pastry cooks, the way conversation resumed half a beat too late, like actors remembering their cue. She walked the long stone corridor between the ovens and the larder with her ledger under her arm and her boots striking a rhythm no one dared match, and everywhere she went, the work continued with the desperate diligence of people who had decided that industry was a form of innocence.

She did not blame them for it. Innocence by performance was still a kind of order.

The Gift Registry Act required vetting of all crown household staff within ninety days of appointment, and the palace — vast, ancient, riddled with service tunnels and older loyalties — was the last great unchecked chamber of the kingdom. Three hundred names. Cooks, undercooks, tasters, cellarers, laundresses, the woman who candled eggs and the man who carried the king's night soil. Serane had interviewed forty-one of them in six days. Thirty-nine had been forgettable, which was itself a comfort. One had wept, which meant nothing; they all wept eventually, guilty or not. Weeping was the body's way of paying a tax it owed whether or not it had committed the crime.

The fortieth name on her list was Jonah Marsh.

* * *

The girl was already in the small interview room off the buttery when Serane arrived — seated, hands folded, spine straight but not rigid. That was the first thing Serane noted, because most people could not manage it. They either slouched to perform ease or sat so stiffly they might have been nailed there. This one sat like someone who had been interviewed before and found it survivable.

"Jonah Marsh," Serane said, consulting the paper though she didn't need to. "Appointed to the tasting office eleven days ago."

"Yes, Inquisitor."

"Your papers came through the barge guilds. Downriver route. You worked kitchens on the *Merry Adela*?"

"Three months. Before that, an inn at Ferrow's Bend." The voice was level, unhurried, faintly bored. "I can cook. I can taste. I don't drink on duty."

"You don't drink."

"Wine dulls the tongue." A small shrug. "A taster who drinks is a taster who dies stupid."

Serane looked at her. The girl looked back, and there it was again — that flicker at the base of Serane's skull, the sense of a word on the tip of the tongue, a face seen once in a crowd and filed wrong. She had interviewed hundreds of people. Faces blurred together the way ledgers blur when you've read too many lines, and yet this face refused to blur. Not remarkable — that was the maddening thing. It was ordinary in every particular: brown hair pinned back plainly, a working woman's hands with old burn scars across the knuckles, eyes the color of weak tea. Ordinary, and familiar, the way a room is familiar when you've dreamed it.

"Where were you born?" Serane asked.

"The Veiled Valley."

Something turned over quietly in Serane's chest, like a key half-turned in a lock that resisted. The valley. Of course. Half the palace's kitchen staff came out of the valley — it was poor, it sent its children to service young, it produced cooks and tasters the way other regions produced soldiers. Nothing in that.

"And you left it when?"

"Spring. After the Proclamation." The girl's tone didn't change, but something behind her eyes did — a shutter drawn not closed but angled, letting light through at a slant. "Registration means leaving the valley. I didn't want to leave."

"You fled rather than register."

"I *left* rather than register." For the first time, a blade showed under the boredom, thin and bright. "Fleeing is what guilty people do. I had a trade. Trades travel."

Serane wrote nothing for a moment. She was aware of the girl watching her write-not-write, and aware that the girl knew she was aware, and aware that this mutual awareness was itself a kind of duel being fought in silence over a table scarred by fifty years of knife-sharpening.

"Your file says your mother was listed in the valley parish rolls as Thornbury. You use Marsh."

"My mother's name. Not mine. Fathers get named on papers when fathers exist." Flat. Unoffended. Practiced.

It was a good answer. It was such a good answer that Serane felt the hairline crack of doubt widen by a degree — because in her experience, innocent people gave clumsy answers. They fumbled. They over-explained. Guilt was smooth; guilt rehearsed. But so, she reminded herself, was competence. So was a life spent answering questions from superiors. A kitchen girl raised to serve the powerful would learn exactly this fluency, exactly this economy of truth — enough truth to be checkable, arranged to conceal nothing because there was nothing to find.

"Stand, please. Hands out."

The girl stood and extended her hands palms-up without hesitation. Serane examined them: burn scars, calluses, the fine tremorless steadiness of a trained tongue's owner. No marks of the gift — though the gift rarely marked anyone visibly, which was the whole cursed problem of it. She checked the inner wrists, the throat, behind the ears, where hedge-witches' tattoos sometimes hid. Clean skin. Clean, except—

Except nothing. There was nothing. Serane released her hands and stepped back, and the familiarity pressed against her again like a hand flat against glass from the other side.

"Have we met?" she asked, before she could stop herself. It was an unprofessional question and she heard it leave her mouth with a small internal wince.

"No, Inquisitor." And then — and this was the thing Serane would turn over for nights afterward — the girl added, mildly: "But I suppose everyone from the valley looks alike to people who aren't from it."

It was insolence wrapped in apology, or apology wrapped in insolence, and Serane found herself almost smiling as she dismissed her. Almost. The smile died somewhere in transit, unclaimed.

* * *

That evening she took the valley file up to her rooms instead of leaving it in the records office, which was against protocol, and told herself it was thoroughness, which was true, and also that she wanted to read it with wine instead of ink-stained fingers, which was also true, and the two truths coexisted the way most truths did — awkwardly, in the same small space.

Her rooms in the Inquisitorium wing were spare: a bed, a desk, a window overlooking the training yard where recruits drilled at hearing lies. She lit two lamps, poured a finger of the good northern brandy she allowed herself one measure of per week, and opened the file.

*Veiled Valley — Compliance Survey, Spring.* Her own initials on the cover summary. She remembered that survey. It had been her first independent command after Harrowmere — no, before Harrowmere. Before the promotion. She had gone into the valley with two clerks and a mandate to collect declarations, and she remembered the valley itself: green, close-walled, suspiciously serene, its people wearing compliance like borrowed coats. Few registrations. Many excuses. The veil made them bold, or made them blind; she had never decided which.

She turned pages. Declarations received: nine. Refusals logged: thirty-one. Names flagged for follow-up. And there, in Clerk Hessel's careful hand, the interview abstracts — including one for a hedge-healer named Wenna, an old woman with a healer's reputation and a sister taken in the last purge, who had answered questions about gifted persons in the valley with a recitation of the dead.

And beneath Wenna's abstract, a marginal note in Hessel's writing: *Subject claims knowledge of a kitchen taster, J. T—, daughter of M. Thornbury (decd.), believed gifted — immunity to poison. Subject declined to elaborate. Flagged.*

Flagged. And then?

Serane turned the page. The flag was there. The name was there — incomplete, *J. T—*, but unmistakable. And beside it, in the disposition column where a clerk should have written *warrant issued* or *surveillance ordered* or *referred to regional office*, there was instead a single line in her own handwriting:

*Insufficient corroboration. File under pending. Do not circulate.*

She stared at it.

She remembered writing it. That was the obscene part — she remembered the moment, the lamplit clerk's tent, the rain on the canvas, Hessel standing over her with the abstract and saying, *Inquisitor, this one wants a warrant,* and herself reading the old woman's words and feeling — what? What had she felt? Not mercy. She had not thought of it as mercy. She had thought: *an old woman's word, a dead mother, an incomplete name, a girl possibly already fled the valley — insufficient grounds to spend riders on.* Economy. Triage. The arithmetic of limited resources that every inquisitor ran daily, the same arithmetic that let ten suspects go for every one pursued.

She had written *do not circulate* because circulating an incomplete flag would have meant paperwork, warrants, a chase across three provinces for a maybe. She had buried it in *pending*, meaning to return to it when the valley survey concluded, and then Harrowmere had happened, and the promotion, and the capital, and the file had sat in a records drawer doing exactly what she'd told it to do: nothing.

Until now. Until a taster with a dead mother named Thornbury and valley-born hands and a tongue too steady sat across a scarred table from her and said *trades travel* in a voice Serane's memory insisted it had heard before, somewhere, somehow, in a context it could not retrieve.

Serane set down the brandy untasted.

Here was the discrepancy, laid out plain as a confession: the file said *pending*. Her own orders said *do not circulate*. Which meant that if Jonah Marsh was the flagged J. T—, then the only reason that girl stood unpursued in the royal kitchens — the only reason she had crossed three provinces, boarded a grain barge, forged or bought papers, and installed herself directly above the registry vault — was that Serane Voss had once, for one hour, on one rainy night, chosen economy over suspicion.

Or chosen something else. That was the question that would not sit down. Because she could reconstruct the reasoning perfectly, and the reasoning was sound, and yet some part of her — the part that had watched the old woman recite the names of her dead like a rosary — suspected that the reasoning had been sound the way a well-built door is sound: designed, deliberately, to keep something out.

Had she known? Had some instinct recognized the truth and dressed it as triage? Was there a version of that rainy night in which she had looked at *J. T—, believed gifted* and felt the same flicker she'd felt today at the base of her skull, and solved it the only way her conscience permitted — by losing the paperwork?

She hated the question. She hated it precisely because she could not answer it, and an inquisitor who could not answer her own motives was an inquisitor auditing a ledger she couldn't balance.

* * *

She worked past midnight.

She pulled the intake records for the *Merry Adela* — no such vessel registered on the downriver grain routes in the relevant season; there was a *Merry Adela*, a coastal ketch, never inland. She pulled the inn record for Ferrow's Bend — the inn existed; the innkeeper's statement, requested by courier weeks ago, had not yet returned. Every answer was checkable. Every answer was *almost* right, off by exactly the margin that separates forgery from sloppiness, and sloppiness was the one thing that girl had not shown a gram of.

Serane spread the papers across her desk like a general reading terrain. Papers said: liar. Face said: I know you. File said: I let you go. And underneath all three documents, quieter than any of them, a fourth text she had no ink for — the memory of Wenna's voice, retrieved now in full for the first time since spring, reciting names into the rain while her clerks grew uncomfortable and Hessel shifted his weight and the lamp hissed:

*I have buried more of the gifted than the crown has ever caught. Write their names down. Go on. Write them. See how many hangings a list is worth.*

She had written *insufficient corroboration* that night with those words in her ears. She saw it now with terrible clarity: she had not been practicing economy. She had been flinching.

And the flinch had consequences. If the girl was gifted — if she was the poison-taster's daughter with the immune tongue, sitting eleven days in the king's own kitchen — then Serane's single line of handwriting had placed her closer to the registry vault than any hunted person in Cael. The Purge ceremony was in five days. The scaffolds were rising in the square below her window; she could hear the hammers even now, faint, rhythmic, patient as a heartbeat. Thousands of new names would be added to the registry in a public burning of the old lists — theater, the Lord Protector called it, mercy and terror in one gesture. And above the vault that held every name ever recorded, a girl whose name should have been circulated in spring was learning the rhythms of the palace kitchens.

Serane rose and stood at the window. Below, torchlight moved along the scaffold frames. Somewhere in the dark city, the marked were sleeping badly, the way they had slept badly for a year, and she had told herself every night that their bad sleep was the price of a kingdom without plague, without fire-starters, without the gifted children who burned barns and the hedge-healers whose remedies killed as often as cured. She had believed it the way she believed in the load-bearing walls of her own mind. She still believed it. That was the horror — she tested the belief nightly and it held, and yet here was this crack, this hairline, running through it, and cracks in load-bearing things do not stay hairline.

She thought about what she would do tomorrow.

Option one: report the discrepancy. Reopen the flag. Riders to the kitchens by dawn, the girl taken quietly before the ceremony, the machine fed and satisfied, her own lapse buried inside a successful arrest — no one would ever audit a file whose error ended in a capture. Clean. Correct. The kind of solution she would have taught a recruit.

Option two: say nothing. Amend nothing. Let the pending stay pending through the ceremony, through the purge, through whatever the girl intended — and live with knowing that she had done it twice now, and that twice was a pattern, and patterns were the one thing inquisitors existed to detect.

She stood at the window a long time, hammers rising from the square, brandy going pale and untouched behind her.

Then she sat down, drew a fresh sheet toward her, and began — in her smallest, most bureaucratic hand — to draft the morning's staff-vetting summary. Forty interviews. Thirty-nine routine. At the fortieth line her pen stopped above the name *Marsh, J.*, hovered there the way a hawk hovers, and wrote:

*Cleared. Recommend retention.*

She signed it. She blotted it. She told herself it was because the evidence was genuinely insufficient, which was true, and that reopening a flag on a hunch and a familiar face was exactly the kind of procedural rot she despised in lesser officers, which was also true, and both truths sat together in her chest like strangers sharing a bench, not speaking.

Then she did the other thing. The thing she would not draft, would not sign, would not admit even to the lamplight. She took the valley file, turned to the page with her own months-old handwriting, and slid it — not into the pending drawer, where any diligent clerk might find it, but into her personal case folio, the locked leather one that traveled home under her arm each night.

Some discrepancies, she reasoned, an inquisitor investigates personally.

Some hunters, she did not finish thinking, hunt best alone.

Outside, the hammers in the square fell silent all at once, as if the scaffolds themselves were listening. Serane Voss blew out one lamp, kept the other, and began, very quietly, to plan how a loyal officer of the crown might follow a kitchen girl through the palace for five days without either of them being seen — and what she would do, on the eve of the Great Purge, if she caught the girl's eye across a crowded kitchen and finally remembered where she had seen her before.

The answer, when she let herself think it, was not an arrest.

That was the discrepancy that frightened her most.
