## 29. Night of Long Knives' Eve

### Jonah

The scaffolds went up in the grey hour before dawn, and Jonah watched them from the scullery window with flour still on her hands.

They rose in the Ceremonial Square like the ribs of some enormous animal being assembled out of timber — three great pyres of stacked faggots, each ringed by a scaffold platform wide enough for forty men to stand abreast, each platform crowned with an iron brazier the size of a cart. Carpenters swarmed over them like ants on a carcass, hammering, shouting measurements, unspooling ropes that glittered with frost. By full light there were banners too: the crowned stag of the new dynasty snapping above the square, and beneath it, on every pillar and lamp-post, fresh-pasted proclamations whose ink was not yet dry.

*By order of His Majesty: the Great Purification. Let the lists be burned and the land made clean.*

Jonah read it twice, then a third time, because the lie was so beautifully constructed she almost admired it. *Let the lists be burned.* As though fire were mercy. As though the burning of paper had ever once saved a single soul.

She turned back to her work. That was the discipline of the kitchen, the discipline she'd learned at nine years old with a drop of poison on her tongue and her mentor's hand steady on her shoulder: whatever happens above stairs, below stairs you chop, you stir, you taste. The palace kitchens on Purge Eve were a cathedral of controlled panic. Two hundred geese. A hundred loaves. A spiced wine for four thousand cups, which meant Jonah tasted forty separate casks, one after another, her tongue cataloguing each for bitterness, for almond, for the metallic whisper of things that should not be there.

Nothing. The crown would not poison its own feast. It didn't need to. Tomorrow it would kill openly, in front of everyone, and call it purification.

"Thornbury." The under-cook, a red-faced man named Pell, jerked his chin toward the window. "You been looking at those pyres all morning. Superstitious?"

"Counting wood," Jonah said. "Someone's going to ask how much we burned."

Pell laughed, delighted by her coldness, and went back to his geese. Jonah kept her eyes down and let the lie sit in her mouth like a stone. She had gotten very good at stones.

But when the bell rang for the midday change of shift, she climbed the narrow stair to the laundry loft, where no one came after dark, and looked out across the roofs at the square again. The scaffolds were finished now. Workers were carrying up bundles wrapped in oilcloth — she could see them from here, small as dolls, dozens of them, dozens more — and she knew what was in the bundles because Brother Cassian's maps had told her where those bundles came from. Scribes' hall. Chancery. The registry offices. Tomorrow they would carry the old ledgers up those steps in solemn procession and feed them to the flames while trumpets played, and the crowd would cheer, and every gifted name ever written down would seem to die in fire and smoke.

And tonight, in the vault beneath her feet, clerks were adding new pages. Thousands of them. Names taken from the valley files, from parish rolls, from informers' letters sealed in wax. The old lists would burn so that no one could ever check them against the new ones. The past would be erased precisely so the future could be murdered cleanly.

Jonah pressed her forehead against the cold glass.

Her mother's name was down there. She was sure of it now; the archive drawings had left no room for doubt. Somewhere in the dark beneath the Ceremonial Square, in a room no proclamation mentioned, in ledgers bound in calfskin and locked behind a door cut during the Dragon War, a clerk with careful handwriting had once written *Maren Thornbury, gifted,* and that single line of ink had followed her mother all the way to an early grave in the Veiled Valley.

Tomorrow the fire would eat everything except that.

She thought of Wenna, who was dead or caged — she did not let herself decide which. She thought of Ember-that-Remains, somewhere north in the Ashreach, older than the kingdom, older than the lists, keeping his bargain the way dragons keep bargains, which is to say absolutely and at any cost. He had told her once, in his voice like embers settling: *Fire needs a keeper, little taster. Fire without a keeper eats its own house.*

Tomorrow he would come. She could feel it the way you feel weather in an old wound.

Tonight, though, there was only the waiting, and the work, and the vault below her sleeping pallet, humming with names.

### Voss

Serane Voss stood at the window of her office in the Inquisitor's Wing and watched the scaffolds catch the last of the light, and felt nothing she could name, which frightened her more than fear would have.

Below, the square filled with carpenters and lamplighters and soldiers stringing rope barriers for tomorrow's crowds. Above, the sky went the color of a healing bruise. Somewhere a choir was rehearsing the Purification anthem, their voices thin through glass: *cleanse us, cleanse us, make us whole.*

She had drafted the order of ceremony herself. She had reviewed it eleven times. At dawn, the condemned — thirty-one souls taken in the autumn purges, held these months in the cells below — would be brought out in grey robes. At mid-morning, the Lord Protector would speak. At noon, the ledgers of the old registry would be carried up the great stair in procession, and the crowd would watch the paper burn, and understand — this was the theory, and she had believed it once with her whole heart — that the kingdom was turning a page. That the age of hidden lists and midnight arrests was ending. That fire could be a door closing on an ugly past.

And tonight, in the vault, the new registry grew fat in the dark. Fourteen thousand names since spring. She had signed the transfer orders herself.

Voss turned away from the window and looked at her desk, at the file lying open on it. The valley file. *Thornbury, J. — kitchen staff, suspected gift (poison immunity), fled boundary stones [date]. Witness: Wenna Thornbury, hedge-healer, refuses testimony.* And beneath that, in her own handwriting, the annotation she had added weeks ago and told no one about: *No further action warranted. Subject deceased, probably.*

A lie of omission, filed in her own neat clerk's hand. She had told herself it was procedure — a file closed on insufficient evidence was a file closed — but she had known, even as the pen moved, that she was doing something else. She had been building a small dark room inside the machine and putting a girl in it.

Now the girl was in her palace. Voss was nearly certain. The new taster, the one with the forged papers so good they were almost insulting, the one whose hands moved like someone who had been tasting since childhood — Voss had interviewed her for a quarter hour and could not stop hearing the valley in her vowels, could not stop seeing, in the way the woman held her untouched wine, a memory of something Voss had read in a deposition two years ago. *Never touched her cup, m'lady. Not once in six years. We all thought it was piety.*

Tomorrow, after the ceremony, there would be a final vetting sweep of palace staff. Her own signature was already on the order.

Voss sat down and pulled the valley file toward her, and for a long time she did nothing but look at the words *subject deceased, probably,* and listen to the choir, and feel the hairline crack that had opened in her certainty months ago widen by a degree she could measure but not stop.

She believed in the work. That was the terrible thing; she still believed. She had seen what unchecked gifts did — she had stood in Harrowmere's ash and watched a woman's grief burn holes in the ground. Order required lists. Lists required keepers. Someone had to hold the pen.

But she had begun, lately, to wonder who held the pen that wrote the keepers.

Outside, the lamplighters finished the square, and the scaffolds stood black against the dusk like gallows dressed for a wedding. Voss closed the file, locked it in her drawer, and put the key in her pocket instead of the lock's usual hook — a small thing, an absent thing, a thing she would examine later, if later ever came.

### Odile

In the caravan yard beyond the South Gate, Odile counted her wagons twice and lied to her own ledger a third time, because the tax man was coming in the morning and honesty was a luxury priced beyond her margins.

"You're quiet," said Marta, her eldest, handing her down a bowl of barley stew.

"I'm old," Odile said. "Old people are quiet. It saves breath for dying."

Marta snorted and sat beside her on the wagon step. Beyond the yard wall, the city glowed — not with festival light, not yet, but with the particular strained brightness of a place holding its breath. Every inn was full. Every road in had been full for days, farmers and merchants and pilgrims and plain gawkers, all of them come to see the lists burn, all of them hungry, and hunger was Odile's oldest friend and steadiest customer.

"Sold out of everything," Marta said. "Even the bad brandy."

"People'll drink anything on an execution day. Makes them feel like it's not happening to them."

"You think it's wrong? What they're doing tomorrow?"

Odile ate her stew and thought about a kitchen girl she had sold, or half-sold, or sold in the shape of a lie — *went east toward the coast, that one, I'd stake my wagons on it* — a lie that had bought her caravan through a checkpoint and cost her a coin's worth of guilt she wore now like a coat she refused to take off, even indoors, even in summer. The girl had gone west. Odile had checked, afterward, quietly, through people who owed her. West, and alive, as far as anyone knew. Alive and, if the rumors that reached even a smuggler's ears were true, headed for this very city, this very week, this very night.

"There's wrong," Odile said at last, "and there's useful, and mostly they're married and hate each other. Eat your stew."

But after Marta went to bed, Odile sat alone in the dark yard and listened to the city breathe, and thought: *tomorrow something breaks open in that square, I can smell it, same as I can smell rain.* Forty years of moving goods around the edges of other people's laws had given her a nose for the moment when the law itself lost its footing.

She got up, woke her drivers, and ordered the wagons loaded and hitched, ready to roll at a moment's notice, facing south.

"If anyone asks," she told Marta, "we're staying for the ceremony. Everyone stays for the ceremony."

"And if nobody asks?"

Odile smiled with all the warmth of a drawn knife. "Then we're gone before the trumpets finish."

### Ember

High in the Ashreach, in the caldera that had been his cradle and would soon be his tomb, Ember-that-Remains felt the city before he saw it — felt it as heat, as noise, as the sour iron reek of ten thousand fires banked for a burning.

Dragons do not sleep as men sleep. They sink into memory. And all that day, sinking, Ember had found himself surfacing again and again to the same taste on the wind: ink. Burning ink, not yet burning, burning-to-be. Paper soaked in names. He had smelled it before, in the long ago, in the years when the keys were cut and the door was built and the first lists were drawn up by soft-handed men in a monastery who believed they were making the world safe. The lists always smelled the same. Calfskin and iron-gall ink and underneath it, faint as a heartbeat, the fear of the people the lists were for.

His fire was nearly gone. He could hold it in his chest the way a dying man holds a coin, and spend it once, and then the long dark. He had made his peace with arithmetic centuries ago. What remained was choice, and the choice had a name, and the name was a small sharp-tongued woman who had climbed into his mountain half-hoping to die and walked out again carrying his promise like a coal in cupped hands.

*Tonight,* he told himself, tasting the wind again. *Or dawn. When the trumpets sound and the paper comes up the stair.*

He rose, stiff as a ship breaking anchor, and spread wings that had not carried him far in a hundred years. Below him the world lay dark and patient. Ahead, the capital burned with borrowed light.

Dragons keep their bargains. It was the last law left, and he meant to keep it.

### Jonah

Midnight came, and with it the changing of the vault guard, and with that the sound Jonah had been listening for all evening: boots on the service stair, the great lock turning, and then — faint, through two floors of stone — the scratch of many pens.

They were writing all night. She lay on her pallet above the tunnels with Cassian's maps unrolled against her chest, listening to the scratch of thousands of names being born in the dark, and understood that she was listening to tomorrow's dead being invented.

Sleep would not come. Around the second bell she gave up, dressed, and went down to the scullery, where she lit no lamps and stood in the dark among the hanging copper pots, and let herself finally say the thing she had been refusing to say since the mountains:

*I could still run.*

The grain barges ran south at dawn. Odile's people were at the South Gate. There were roads out of Cael that no inquisitor had ever mapped, and she had a tongue that could taste her way through any checkpoint in the kingdom, and if she left tonight, before the trumpets, before the fire, she would live. That was the whole offer. Live, quietly, elsewhere, forever. The door in the mountain had asked her the same question in stone and she had answered it once already: some doors are answers to questions you stop asking.

But her mother's name was downstairs. And fourteen thousand others. And tomorrow the crowd would cheer while the old lists burned, never knowing that the new ones were already warm from the press, never knowing that the fire was not an ending but an alibi.

Jonah took the maps from her chest, folded them into her bodice, and looked out the scullery window one last time. The square below lay empty under a thin moon, the pyres pale as bones, the braziers dark. Waiting.

Somewhere north, a wingbeat older than the kingdom was coming south.

She blew out the candle she had never lit, and went to find the tunnel entrance Cassian had marked with a monk's small cross, and the floor beneath the palace opened for her like a throat.

Above her, the first grey light of Purge Day touched the scaffolds.
