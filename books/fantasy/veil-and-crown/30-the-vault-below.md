## 30. The Vault Below

The service tunnels beneath the palace smelled of tallow and old stone, of rats and rainwater and the particular sourness of a building that had been standing so long it had forgotten what it was built for. Jonah moved through them the way she had once moved through the king's breakfast — slowly, attentively, tasting everything.

Cassian's maps were drawn on vellum gone soft as cloth, inked in a monk's careful hand, annotated in three different centuries of handwriting. *Here the old cistern was bricked over.* *Here the stair to the undercroft collapsed in the reign of Aldous II; take the passage east instead.* *Here the vault door stands. It has always stood. God forgive us for the lock.*

She had memorized the route twice over before she ever set foot below, because paper was a liability now, and because her life had narrowed to a single skill: knowing things without being seen to know them. She could walk these tunnels blind. She almost did — the lamps were few and far between, guttering in niches where some long-dead servant had once thought to put them, and more than once she went by touch alone, one hand trailing the wall's cold slickness, counting turns the way Cassian had taught her to count ingredients. *Bitter first. Then salt. Then whatever sweetness they tried to bury it under.*

The palace above her was awake and roaring. She felt it through the stone: boots on marble, hammer on timber, the scaffolds going up in the ceremonial square plank by plank like a gallows being dressed for company. Tomorrow was the Night of Long Knives' Eve ceremony, when the crown would burn the registry publicly — or rather, burn a copy of it, a beautiful gilded forgery built for flames while the true ledgers slept down here, adding names even as the smoke rose. She had watched the workmen from a kitchen window that morning, and something in her chest had gone very still and very cold, the way it did when she tasted almond under honey.

*They will burn a lie tomorrow,* she thought, descending another flight of worn steps, *and call it mercy.*

Her lamp found the door at the end of the last passage, exactly where Cassian said it would be, and Jonah stopped and let herself simply look at it for a long moment.

It was not grand. That was the first obscenity of it. After everything — Wenna's warnings, the burned village of Harrowmere, the child's shoe in the ash, Ember's dying fire waiting for her in the mountains — after all of it, the engine of centuries of purges sat behind a plain iron-banded oak door in a cellar corridor, no larger than the door to a good pantry. Someone had scratched initials into the wood near the hinge, generations ago. A love token, maybe. Two people who had worked down here, who had passed their whole lives carrying names to their deaths and still found time to carve each other's letters into the grain.

Jonah pressed her palm flat against the door. Cold came through it like water.

*Hers is in there,* she thought. *Mother's name is in there.*

She had known this, of course. Had built the whole terrible architecture of this night around knowing it. But knowledge was one thing, and the weight of a door was another, and for a moment she could not make her hand move to the key at her throat.

Wenna's key hung there on its cord, cold iron, shaped for no lock anyone had ever found. *Keys were cut during the Dragon War for those meant to survive,* Voss's researches had shown — researches Jonah had stolen secondhand from a frightened clerk in a riverside tavern, irony upon irony. And Cassian, weeping in his ruined monastery, had drawn for her the archive plans his order had kept for six hundred years: the vault, the lock, the mechanism. *It isn't a lock for keeping people out,* he'd said. *It's a lock for keeping the names in. We built it so carefully. We were so proud of our precision.*

The keyhole was small, set low in the door at knee height, as if designed by someone who expected the opener to kneel.

Jonah knelt.

The iron slid in with a resistance that felt less like metal meeting metal and more like a held breath being released. For a heartbeat nothing happened. Then, deep inside the door, something turned over — a sound like a great ledger closing, like a spine cracking shut — and the bolts drew back one by one, each with a voice of its own, five small deaths in the dark.

The door swung inward on hinges that had not been oiled in living memory, and the smell of the vault came out to meet her: dust, leather, ink, and underneath it all something faintly sweet and rotten, the smell of paper that remembered hands.

* * *

Lamps hung on hooks just inside, and Jonah lit three of them with fingers that only shook a little. The light climbed the shelves and showed her what six hundred years of careful monastic precision looked like.

Ledgers. Thousands of them. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf, rising into the dark beyond her lamplight, bound in calfskin and dyed leather and, in the oldest cases, in something darker she refused to name. Each spine bore a region and a year. Each book held names.

She walked the central aisle slowly, reading as she went, because she could not help it — because every title was a wound. *Veiled Valley, 201–214.* Her own valley, her own years. *Harrowmere and the Riverlands, 189–196.* She stopped there, laid two fingers on the spine, and thought of chalk marks on doorframes, of ash fine as flour. Somewhere in that book was a list of everyone the crown had decided to kill, written out in a tidy hand, probably with margins ruled straight, probably with a little index at the back.

*We were so proud of our precision.*

Farther in, the books grew older, the heraldry on their stamps shifting — the dead royal line's stag giving way to older sigils, then to none at all, just dates and regions from before Cael had a name. The kingdom had been dissolved. Its laws persisted. Its lists, apparently, were eternal.

Jonah found the valley shelves and began walking backward through time, year by year, looking for one date she knew better than her own heartbeat. The year her mother fled. The year the Veiled Valley swallowed a gifted woman whole and taught her daughter that silence was a food group.

There.

*Veiled Valley, 171–173.*

She pulled it down and the book was heavier than it should have been, heavier than paper and board had any right to be, as if the names themselves had mass. She carried it to the reading table in the center of the aisle — a broad slab of oak scarred by centuries of pens — and opened it under the lamp.

Alphabetical. Of course it was alphabetical. There was an index.

*Thornbury.*

Her mother's name sat on the page in brown ink gone amber with age: **Maren Thornbury**, entered in a clerk's neat slant, with a date of registration and a date of — Jonah made herself read it — a date of *disposition*. Not death. Disposition. As if her mother had been a parcel. As if the crown had signed for her.

Below it, in fresher ink, blacker, newer: **Jonah Thornbury**. Registered under the Gift Registry Act. Flagged. Hunted. Filed.

Her own name, sitting directly beneath her mother's like a child holding its parent's hand across a century of dead paper.

Jonah stood very still. The lamps hissed. Somewhere far above, faint as memory, the hammers kept working on the scaffolds.

She had imagined this moment on the road, in the monastery, on the barge, in the palace kitchen with its forged papers and its familiar knives. In her imaginings she had always felt something clean and simple: fury, mostly, or grief. What she actually felt was neither, or both at once, fused into something that had no name in any language she knew — a heat behind the sternum, a taste at the back of the tongue like copper and honey, the exact flavor of bitter almond hidden under sweetness. Poison. This whole room was poison, six hundred years of it, steeped slow and served daily to people who never knew they were drinking.

And she was the taster. She had always been the taster. That was the joke the world had been telling all along, and she finally got it, standing in the dark among the ledgers, and the laugh that came out of her was small and awful and echoed off the shelves.

*You noticed the almond,* she told the room silently. *Now do something about it.*

* * *

She did not burn it. Not yet. Not tonight.

That was the discipline her whole life had trained into her, the one lesson the valley had taught that mattered: you do not spit out the poison the moment you taste it. You hold it. You learn its shape. You find out how much of it there is, and where it came from, and who else has drunk it — because if you act too fast, you die, and the dish gets served anyway.

So Jonah walked the aisles with her lamp high, and she counted.

Shelves by shelf, region by region, she took inventory the way she'd once taken inventory of a royal pantry. The eastern provinces, thick with entries from the Dragon War era, when the lists had been born. The capital itself, updated continuously — she found the current-year ledger open on a writing desk near the far wall, a fresh pot of ink beside it, a quill laid across the page as though the clerk had only stepped out for supper and might return at any moment. On the open page, new names in wet-black rows. Dozens of them. Names added since the Act. Names that would be read aloud tomorrow, or acted on quietly next week, while the crowd cheered the burning of the pretty fake upstairs.

She copied none of them. Paper was a liability. But she was a taster, and a taster's memory was the finest instrument in Cael — trained since childhood to hold a hundred flavors in the mind at once, to recall the exact bitterness of a leaf sampled once, years ago. She read the page until the names were part of her, and then she closed the ledger gently, the way you close a coffin.

Near the desk, a narrow stair climbed toward a hatch — the delivery route, Cassian's maps said, up which the clerks brought their annual additions. Through the boards overhead she could hear muffled voices, footsteps, the ordinary traffic of a palace preparing a festival. So close. All of it so close. Six hundred years of murder, and it slept thirty feet beneath the ballroom floor.

Jonah set her lamp on the reading table, beside her mother's open ledger, and allowed herself one minute. Just one. She stood with her hand resting on the page, on the two names stacked together, and she spoke aloud, very softly, in the valley accent she usually kept folded away like winter clothes.

"I found it, Mam. I'm here. I'm sorry I'm late."

The vault said nothing. The vault had heard six hundred years of last words, probably, and kept them all filed.

But something in her settled, the way a bone settles when it's set. Grief and fury had been fighting each other in her chest since Harrowmere, since Wenna, since the ford where she'd used her gift openly and lost her invisibility forever — and here, in this room, they stopped fighting and became the same thing. Purpose. Not hot. Not wild. Cold as the iron key, precise as a clerk's ruled margin, patient as poison.

Tomorrow the crown would burn its lie in the square, and the dragon whose last flame waited in her keeping — Ember-that-Remains, who had flown unbidden toward the capital because dragons keep their bargains — would come down out of the mountains like a struck bell given wings. She had seen the red smear of him against the northern sky two nights ago, and the whole city had seen it too, and the panic had already begun to season the festival preparations.

Fire was coming to this square whether she willed it or not.

Her whole task, then, was to be standing at the right door when it arrived — and to choose, in that final breathless moment, what the fire was *for*. Escape, forever, through a door out of dissolved Cael. Or this. The shelves. The names. The machine itself.

She looked down the long aisle at the thousands of spines fading into darkness, at the index of the dead, at her mother's name and her own stacked like hands clasped across a grave.

"You're not a door," she said quietly, to the vault, to the key warm now against her sternum. "Wenna, you stubborn old woman. You gave me the way out, and it was never a way out at all."

Above her, muffled by stone and shelved centuries, the palace bells began to ring the hour — and beneath their music, distant but unmistakable, rising from the direction of the mountains, came a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it with her ears.

A roar. Old, ragged, and coming closer.
