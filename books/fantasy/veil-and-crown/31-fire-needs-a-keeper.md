## 31. Fire Needs a Keeper

The mountain had been his whole world for longer than the world had been called a world.

Ember-that-Remains lay in the caldera with his wings folded like a shroud around himself, and he counted the things that were left of him the way other creatures counted their treasures. One eye that still saw color. Three claws on the left forefoot where there had once been five. A heart that beat slower each season, like a drum struck by a hand growing tired. And fire — not the ocean of it he had been born carrying, not the flood that had turned the skies of Cael to copper in the years when men still sang about dragons instead of hunting them, but a single coal of it, banked deep beneath his ribs, no larger than the last ember in a hearth at the end of winter.

He did not spend it. That was the discipline. That was the whole of what remained of dragonhood: the not-spending.

He had watched the sun come up over the Ashreach eleven thousand times since the war ended, and he had learned from the sun's example. The sun gave its light away every day and was never diminished, because the sun was fed by something vaster than itself. Ember had no such engine. What he gave away, he lost forever. And so he kept his coal, and the keeping was slow dying too, because a dragon who does not burn is only a very large corpse that has not yet admitted it.

But dragons keep their bargains.

He had made one. He could still taste it, three days old now, hanging in the ash-wind of the caldera like smoke that would not rise: a girl with river-mud on her boots and her grandmother's name in her mouth, standing at the edge of his crater with death half-hoped-for in her face, asking nothing. That was the thing that had undone him. She had asked for nothing. She had climbed a mountain full of dying fire expecting to be eaten, and when he spoke to her she had wept — not for herself, he had smelled the salt of it and known — but for him, for the loneliness of him, as if an ancient weapon could be grieved for.

*Ember-that-Remains,* she had said, and used the whole of his name, the name his mother had breathed into the first flame of him before men with iron ladders came up the cliffs. No one living knew that name. He had not heard it spoken aloud in four hundred years.

And then she had gone down the mountain without taking anything, and he had understood, in the slow way mountains understand weather, that she meant to die somewhere below, or worse than die, and that his coal would gutter out unspent inside a corpse on a mountaintop while the last of the people who remembered his mother's name were fed into ovens by men with careful lists.

Dragons keep their bargains. He had offered her his flame. She had not yet accepted it. But an offer made is a debt begun, and Ember-that-Remains had never in his long life left a debt unpaid.

* * *

Rising took the better part of a day.

He had not flown since the war. His wings, when he unfolded them against the grey sky, were maps of everything that had been taken from him — holes punched through the membrane by ballista bolts, healed into translucent scars that glowed faintly when the light passed through them, so that when he finally beat them and lifted his body off the stone, the morning sun behind him lit his own wounds like windows.

The pain was total. It was also, he discovered with something like wonder, familiar. This was the pain of being what he was. He had spent four centuries mistaking stillness for peace, and now, screaming inwardly through every torn inch of wing, he remembered that flight was the truth of his body and stillness only its absence.

He rose above the Ashreach in spirals, wide and laborious, a wounded thing climbing the air the way an old man climbs stairs. Below him the peaks fell away, and then the foothills, and then the brown ribbon of the river road, and then — far off, hazy with distance and morning smoke — the capital of a kingdom that officially did not exist, its towers catching the light like knives laid out on a table.

He could smell it from here. That was the terrible thing about noses like his; distance meant nothing to a dragon's nose. On the wind, thin as a hair, came the capital's breath: woodsmoke, horse, tallow, bread, iron, and underneath all of it, unmistakable, the sour chemical reek of fear in great quantity. A city afraid. A city that had been taught to be afraid, carefully, over years, by people who believed they were doing good work.

And threaded through the fear, fainter than everything, the bright cold ringing he had first felt when the girl's gift woke on his mountainside — except now it was not one bell. It was hundreds. Thousands. All across the low country, all across the valley and the roads and the hidden rooms of the dissolved kingdom, the gifted were waking to terror, and terror made their gift ring like struck glass, and every note of it landed on Ember's senses like a needle.

They were going to burn the registry tomorrow. He had heard it in the girl's mind when she stood at his crater's edge — not her thoughts, dragons do not read thoughts, but her fears, which leak like light under a door. A ceremony. Scaffolds. The lists displayed and destroyed as theater while new names were written into deeper books. The crown burning paper to prove its mercy and keeping the ink to prove its power.

His mother had died in the Dragon War. He had never learned to say that plainly, even to himself. His mother had *ended*, in the third year of the war, over the fields of Harrowmere, and the men who brought her down had cut the scales from her body while she was still warm, and those scales had built the vault doors beneath the capital — he knew this, all dragons knew this, it was carved into the memory they passed down like a curse — and so the last prison of the gifted was walled with the body of his mother, and the key to it was fire, and the only fire left in the world was banked under his ribs.

There had never really been a choice. There had only been the time it takes a dying creature to stand up.

* * *

He flew through the day and into the dark, and flying through the dark was the old grief and the old glory both.

No stars touched him anymore; the clouds had come in from the coast, a low ceiling of them, and he flew beneath their weight through a country lit only by the scattered lamps of towns that were not supposed to exist. Dissolved Cael spread out below him like a sentence someone had tried to erase and only smudged. Roads ran straight toward towns with no names. Bridges crossed rivers to villages struck from every map, and the villages burned their lamps anyway, because the dead kingdom's habit of existing was older and stronger than any decree.

Once, long ago, he had flown this route at the head of a wing of forty. His sire had flown point, a black shape against the moon, and the wind of their passing had flattened wheat for a mile on either side, and the people below had run out of their houses not to fight but to watch, because in those days even terror had a kind of beauty, and beauty was worth standing in a field to see.

Now he flew alone at the speed of a tired ox, and his shadow, when he passed over lamplit roads, made dogs bark and grown men fall to their knees in the mud.

He felt them feel him. That was the strangest part — the gift ringing up out of the darkness everywhere he passed, waking as he woke it. In a farmhouse a child sat bolt upright in bed and said aloud, to an empty room, *the dragon is coming.* In a smuggler's camp a woman with silver rings on every finger looked east and began, without knowing why, to pack. In a monastery ruin a monk knelt among his archives and laughed until he wept. The gift answered the nearness of fire the way iron answers a lodestone, and Ember was the last fire, and the whole sleeping country turned toward him in its sleep like flowers turning toward a sun that had not risen in four hundred years.

He did not know if he was bringing them hope. He suspected he was bringing them something more complicated. Hope, in his experience, was what men called the feeling of watching a dragon land — awe and doom in a single body, the certainty that the world was about to change and no power at all to choose how.

Let it be enough, he thought, in the wordless way dragons think. Let it be enough that I am seen.

* * *

Dawn found him over the plain before the capital, and the city found him before he found it.

Bells. Of course bells. The capital had a thousand years of bells and no imagination for anything else, and within minutes of a shepherd on the western hills seeing a shape the size of a cathedral moving against the clouds, every tower in the city was hammering out alarm, and the sound rolled across the plain and met him like a wall.

He flew into it anyway.

Below, the ceremonial square was already filling. He could see it from two miles up: the scaffolds rising at the center like the ribs of some enormous slaughtered animal, the ranks of soldiers in the crown's new colors, the stands draped in bunting, the pyres stacked high with paper — he could smell the paper, tons of it, ledgers and registries and the private lists of inquisitors, stacked to be burned publicly at noon while the real records slept safe underground. He could smell the crowd gathering, ten thousand bodies and their fear-sweat and their festival bread, parents lifting children onto shoulders so they could watch mercy performed.

And beneath the square, beneath the flagstones, beneath the vaulted dark where his mother's scales had been hammered into doors — the shelves. Rows upon rows upon rows of names. He could not read them from here, but he could feel them, the way you feel a scar: the accumulated weight of centuries of careful handwriting, every entry a life measured and filed, the whole architecture of the purges built ledger by ledger by men who had never hated anyone in their lives and had simply loved order.

The girl was down there somewhere. He could not pick her out — she was one small bell among thousands — but he knew she had come, because the debt in his chest pulled downward toward the square like a plumb line, and debts always pull true.

*Ember-that-Remains,* she had called him. Well then. Let the name mean something one last time.

He tipped his wings and fell out of the sky.

* * *

The descent took ninety seconds and lasted, he was certain, several years.

Wind screamed through the holes in his wings and made music of his wounds, a low moaning chord that preceded him into the square by half a minute, so that the crowd heard him coming before they saw him — a sound like the sky grieving — and the front ranks of the ceremony turned as one body, ten thousand faces tilting back, mouths opening.

Then he was through the cloud layer and visible, and the sound the crowd made was not a scream. He had expected screams. What came up to him instead was a vast collective inhalation, the whole square drawing breath at once, and under it the bells of the gift ringing wild and joyful and terrified in every gifted breast in the city, and the two sounds braided together into something he had not heard since the war: the voice of humanity beholding a dragon.

It was, he thought, falling, almost worth the dying.

He did not land gently. He had planned to land gently — a dragon can set down softer than a moth when he wishes — but his left wing seized at the last moment, the old ballista scar tearing open along its whole length, and he came down on the flagstones of the ceremonial square like a mountain deciding to relocate. The impact shattered the scaffolds. The pyres of registry paper went up in a bloom of loose pages, thousands of names whirling into the air like a blizzard of white moths, and the crowd broke apart around him in a ring, soldiers dropping pikes, dignitaries scrambling down from their draped stands, a child's shoe left standing alone on the stones.

Ember lay in the wreckage of the ceremony with his blood running hot between the flagstones and his breath raising a fog that rolled over the front rows of the fleeing crowd, and he gathered what remained of his strength and did the thing he had flown a night and a day to do.

He raised his head. Slowly, majestically, the way his mother had raised hers over the fields of Harrowmere, and he looked at the broken scaffolds and the scattered papers and the abandoned pyres, and then he looked at the faces at the edge of the square — the ones who had stopped running, because some part of every human cannot stop looking at a dragon — and he opened his jaws, and he spoke.

Not in the tongue of men. Dragons' speech is older than words; it arrives in the chest before it arrives in the ears, and everyone in the square heard it in the place where they kept their oldest memories.

*I AM THE LAST,* he said. *I HAVE COME TO KEEP MY BARGAIN.*

Somewhere in the crowd, small and clear, a bell rang that he knew — the girl's bell, her gift flaring like a struck match — and Ember-that-Remains, lying broken in the ruins of the Purge, felt the last coal under his ribs stir and begin, at long last, to burn.

Across the square, pushing through the frozen guards, a woman in an inquisitor's coat was running toward him — and behind her, from the direction of the vault stairs, another figure was emerging into the light, and the two of them were converging on the same point, and Ember understood with the clarity of the nearly dead that whatever happened next would happen quickly, and would happen without him, and that this too was what it meant to keep a bargain:

to give everything you have left, and trust the living to finish the work.
