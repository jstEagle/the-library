## 32. The Purge Interrupted

The square had become a throat, and the whole city was being swallowed down it.

Jonah felt it before she saw it — a vibration through the soles of her boots, up through her shins, into the cage of her ribs. She had spent twenty years reading rooms by their tremors: the kitchen's hum when the king was pleased, the stillness that fell before bad news, the particular shiver of a household that suspected something and would not say it. This was all of those at once, multiplied past sense. The ceremonial square of Cael's capital was packed shoulder to shoulder with ten thousand people who had come to watch names burn.

They had come to watch *people* burn, really. Everyone knew it. The registry ledgers would be carried out on silver trays and fed to a great iron brazier in front of the scaffolds, and the crowd would cheer, and the crown would declare the gift dead in Cael, and somewhere in the dark of the following weeks the new lists — the real lists, the ones written fresh — would begin their quiet work. Jonah had read enough of Brother Cassian's archives to know the shape of the theater. It was an old play. It had been performed before, under other kings, with other fires. The monks had kept the programs.

She stood in the mouth of the service tunnel beneath the eastern colonnade, one hand flat against cold stone, and listened to the machinery assemble itself above her.

*Not yet,* she told herself. *Not until Ember.*

She did not know if he was coming. That was the terrible arithmetic of it. He had made no promise she could hold — dragons did not promise, he had said; they *kept*, which was a different thing entirely, done in silence, without witnesses, because a bargain spoken aloud could be reneged but a bargain kept in silence was carved into the world. She had left him guttering in his caldera with less than a season of fire left in him, and she had walked away carrying his question like a stone behind her sternum: *What will you carry forward if I die for you?*

She still had no answer. She had an iron key instead, and Cassian's maps inked on vellum soft as skin, and a plan so simple it insulted her: get below, open the vault, and be standing inside it when whatever came next came.

The tunnel breathed around her. Torchlight from the grating overhead striped the walls in gold and shadow, and through the grating she could hear the criers — the same three phrases over and over, worn smooth as river stones: *the age of sorcery is ended, the crown thanks its loyal subjects, present yourselves and be forgiven.* Forgiven. As though the crime were being born wrong. As though forgiveness were something a list could grant.

Jonah moved.

The tunnels were older than the palace above them — Cassian had been clear on this, almost tender about it, the way he spoke of nothing else except his shame. The monastery order had helped dig them, centuries ago, when the first registry was first conceived; the vault had been built by men who believed they were building a mercy, a single locked room where dangerous names could sleep instead of scatter into a hundred petty village accusations. Every purge since had descended from that room like children from a father. She followed his maps down passages where her torch found carvings worn to suggestion: hands, scales, a door with no handle. Once, in a junction where four corridors met, she passed a niche holding a stub of candle and a bowl of ash, offerings left by someone who had come before her and turned back. She touched neither. There wasn't time to honor every ghost, and the ghosts here were thick as dust.

Above her, the crowd's sound changed pitch.

She stopped, one hand on the wall, and listened the way she had been taught to listen at nine years old, kneeling before Master Corvin with a spoon of soup and orders never to ask what was in it. Fear had a taste. So did joy. And joy curdling into fear had a flavor she knew better than any poison — bitter almond was gentler.

Something vast moved over the city.

She heard it before anyone understood it: a pressure change, a groan in the tunnel stones, the crowd's ten thousand voices dropping at once into the same astonished hush she remembered from the market square the day the proclamation was read. Then came the scream of timber and the crash of scaffold poles, and the crowd broke its hush all at once, and the sound that rose was not cheering and not terror either but some third thing, older than both, the sound people make when the sky stops obeying them.

*Ember.*

Her eyes stung. She had no time for it. She ran.

* * *

The vault stairs were exactly where Cassian said they'd be, behind a door disguised as a drainage access, and the lock was exactly what Wenna's key had promised: not a keyhole at all but a socket, a cylinder of black iron sized for a key cut during the Dragon War for those meant to survive. Jonah's hands shook so badly she had to press them flat against her thighs and count breaths, the way Wenna had taught her at the boundary stones — *in for the length of a psalm verse, out for two* — before she could fit the key to the socket.

It turned like the closing of a book.

The door swung inward on darkness and the smell of paper, vellum, leather, dust, and beneath those, faintly, the tallow-and-iron smell of old fear. Jonah lifted her torch.

Shelves. Shelf upon shelf upon shelf, rising into the dark beyond her light, receding into aisles deeper than the palace above should have allowed. Ledgers by the thousand, spines cracked, corners furred with damp. Centuries of careful clerical hands. Somewhere in this room was a ledger with her name in it, entered three months ago by a clerk in the Veiled Valley. Somewhere above that entry, in an older hand, was her mother's name. Her grandmother's. A whole genealogy of the marked, catalogued with the loving precision of men who never once asked what the lists were *for*.

She stepped inside, and the door began to swing shut behind her—

—and stopped.

A boot held it open. A polished boot, government leather, mud-splashed from the square above.

Jonah turned slowly, torch raised, and looked into the face of Inquisitor Serane Voss.

Up close, without the gray uniform and the escort and the weight of office between them, Voss was smaller than Jonah had imagined, and younger, and more tired. There was ash on her cheekbone. Her braid had half-come undone. In her right hand she held a sword, but it pointed at the floor, and her grip on it was the grip of someone holding a tool they have momentarily forgotten the use of. In her left hand — Jonah's stomach dropped — she held a sheet of paper. Fresh. Palace stock. A page of names in a clerk's neat hand, and Jonah did not need to read it to know what it was. The new list. Tonight's real harvest, copied out before the old registry burned for the crowd.

"You're the kitchen girl," Voss said.

"I'm a lot of things."

"The taster." Voss's eyes moved over her face with an intensity that was almost physical, cataloguing, comparing — Jonah could see her doing it, see the file opening behind her eyes, the valley records, the discrepancy she herself had created weeks ago and never resolved. "Thornbury. Jonah Thornbury." She said the name the way a physician says a diagnosis. "I interviewed you in the palace kitchens eleven days ago. You tasted the consommé and flagged the salt. Nobody flags the salt. Salt is never the poison." A pause. "Except once. In the valley. Three years ago. Bitter almond masked in honeyed pears, reported by a taster named—"

"Don't," Jonah said.

"—named Jonah Thornbury." Voss's voice didn't rise. If anything it went quieter, and that was worse. "I told myself it was coincidence. A common name. I wrote the report myself and I left the connection out, and I have spent eleven days telling myself that was thoroughness. Diligence. Checking my work." Her jaw tightened. "It wasn't diligence. I know what it was now. I just don't know why I did it."

"Does it matter?"

"It matters *entirely*." For the first time Voss raised the sword, not aiming it, just holding it between them like a sentence she hadn't finished. Above them, muffled through fathoms of stone, the dragon roared again, and the vault shelves shed a fine rain of dust, and neither woman flinched, because they were both looking at each other and there was no room left over for flinching. "Everything is the why. The Act exists because of whys. The gifted are a plague risk — that is not cruelty, that is arithmetic. Uncontrolled power spreads. Uncontrolled power kills. I have seen what happens in villages where a hedge-witch's charm goes wrong. I have buried the results. You think I enjoy the registries?" Something moved across her face, too fast to name. "I keep the lists because the lists are the only thing standing between order and the fire you can hear right now."

"And tonight," Jonah said, "the lists are going to grow. While the old ones burn for the crowd. I've read your ceremony, Inquisitor. The monks archived it. Your crown has been performing this play for four hundred years — burn the old names, write the new ones, call it mercy. You're not ending the plague. You're *farming* it."

Voss flinched. Actually flinched, a small motion in the muscles around her eyes, and Jonah understood with a strange, sick clarity that no one had ever said it to her face before. That everyone around Serane Voss agreed with her too completely, and agreement was its own kind of blindness, and this woman — brilliant, sincere, genuinely certain — had never once been argued with by someone she couldn't dismiss.

"You're going to open that vault," Voss said. Not a question.

"I already have."

"Then we are past talking."

"We haven't even started talking." Jonah set her torch in the bracket beside the door, deliberately, showing her empty hands. "You want to arrest me. Fine. Write it down. Add me to a list. And then add my mother, who died unregistered in the valley because registration meant leaving the only place magic couldn't follow. Add Wenna, the hedge-healer, who gave me a key and a warning and got taken anyway, and who recited the names of her dead to your interrogators rather than give up mine. Add the child's shoe I saw in Harrowmere. Add all of them, Inquisitor. That's what your machine does. It doesn't end plagues. It makes the dead, and then it makes lists of them, and then it burns the lists and starts again. I've seen the whole archive. Have you?"

"I've seen enough."

"You've seen *nothing*." The words came out of Jonah with a force that surprised her, twenty years of swallowed answers finally finding a door. "You were built by the same machine that built me. It taught you certainty the way it taught me silence. And you are standing in front of the only room in this kingdom that holds every name it ever took, and you are holding a sword to protect it, and a dragon is dying above us right now — do you hear him? Do you hear what keeping a bargain costs? — and you have maybe a minute to decide whether you are the machine or whether you are a woman standing in front of a machine."

The roar came again, closer, ragged, and beneath it a new sound: bells. Alarm bells across the city, dozens of them, discordant.

Voss looked at the sword. At the shelves vanishing into the dark. At the paper in her left hand — the new list, tonight's harvest, thousands of names in a clerk's neat hand — and Jonah watched her look at it, really look, perhaps for the first time, the way one looks at a familiar object suddenly found in a stranger's house.

"My sister's name is on that page," Voss said quietly.

The words landed in the vault like a dropped glass.

"She presented herself last spring. Voluntarily. Because I told her it was safer to be registered than to hide." Voss's voice stayed level, but level the way ice is level. "She is not gifted. She has never been gifted. But our grandmother was, and the clerks took her name anyway, because the new Act covers bloodlines, and I signed the amendment." She looked up, and her eyes were dry and terrible. "I built the net. I caught my own family in it. And I filed the report, and I corrected the error, and I told myself the system would resolve it, because the system always resolves things, because if it doesn't then everything I have done—" She stopped. Breathed. "You asked if the why matters. That's the why."

Somewhere above, close now, impossibly close, came a sound like the world's largest heart failing — a long, grinding exhalation, fire and grief together.

Ember was landing.

Jonah reached out her hand. Not for the sword. For the page.

"Then you already know what has to happen to it," she said.

Voss stared at her hand. At the key, still seated in the vault door's socket. At the shelves of names behind her, centuries deep, patient as sediment.

"If I let you do this," Voss said, "there is no putting it back. No order. No—"

"There was never any order," Jonah said. "There was only the list, and the fear of the list. Burn the fear, Inquisitor. Keep the rest."

And Serane Voss — hunter, believer, survivor of a different machine — closed her eyes, and opened her hand, and let the page fall.

The sound of it landing was very small. The sound of what came next was not: wings like collapsing cathedrals, the crowd screaming, the scaffolds coming down, and Ember-that-Remains descending on the ceremonial square above them with the last of his fire gathered in his throat like a final word.

Jonah turned back to the vault, to the shelves, to the names — hers, her mother's, ten thousand mothers' — and reached for her torch.

Behind her, Voss drew one long breath, sheathed her sword, and said, "Start with the oldest ones. They've waited longest."

And the first flame went up.
