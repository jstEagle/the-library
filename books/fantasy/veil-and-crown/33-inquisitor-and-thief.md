## 33. Inquisitor and Thief

*Jonah*

The vault mouth breathed cold air against her face, and out of that cold came a voice she had heard only once before and never forgotten.

"Stop there."

Serane Voss stood at the top of the service stair with a lantern in one hand and the iron key — *her* key, Wenna's key, taken from her at the ford — held loosely in the other. Not raised. Not brandished. Held the way a scholar holds a page she has read too many times.

Behind Voss, far above, the palace was screaming. Jonah could hear it even here, muffled through stone: the roar of the crowd in the ceremonial square, the trumpets, the beginning of the Great Purge ceremony, all of it filtered down through six floors of granite until it sounded like weather. Like a storm arriving over hills.

She had perhaps minutes. Perhaps less.

"You're supposed to be vetting kitchen staff," Jonah said. Her voice came out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I passed my interview."

"You did." Voss began to descend the stair, unhurried, her boots ringing on each step. "You salted the consommé before tasting it. A taster who salts first is checking for what's already in the pot, not what might be added to her own spoon. I told myself I was imagining it. I have been telling myself that for three weeks."

The lantern light reached Jonah first, then the woman carrying it. Up close, Voss looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with sleep. There were lines around her mouth that hadn't been there at the river ford, carved by something that had been eating at her from inside.

"You let me go once," Jonah said. "At the records office. You filed a report that said the valley file was incomplete."

"I know what I filed. I wrote it."

"Then write another one. Walk back up those stairs, and by morning there'll be nothing down here but smoke and an accident."

Voss stopped three steps above her. Close enough now that Jonah could see her eyes properly — grey, level, and behind them a mind working so fast Jonah could almost hear it, like a mill grinding grain in the dark.

"The vault is open," Voss said quietly. "I can hear the lock mechanism cooling. If I walk away, you burn four hundred years of names."

"Yes."

"And every gifted person in Cael becomes unfindable. Untraceable. Free to hide among us forever."

"Yes," Jonah said again, and did not soften it.

Voss looked past her, into the dark of the vault, where the shelves waited — row upon row of them, receding beyond the lantern's reach, each one heavy with ledgers bound in crown-blue leather. Somewhere in that darkness was a book with Jonah's name in it. Above that name, her mother's. Above that, names going back and back, generation upon generation, written in careful clerical hands by men who had believed, every one of them, that they were doing good work.

"Do you know how many people died in Harrowmere?" Voss asked suddenly.

The question landed like a slap. "Thirty-one," Jonah said. "I counted the chalk marks."

"Forty-three." Voss's voice didn't waver. "Thirty-one marked. Twelve unmarked who died in the fires because the lists told my riders which houses to surround, and fire doesn't check its paperwork. You think I don't know that? I stood in that ash, taster. I picked up the same child's shoe you did. And I went back to my office and I refined the methodology, because the alternative was to decide that forty-three deaths meant nothing, and they don't mean nothing. They mean the system must be made *better*. Precise. So that next time, only the guilty burn."

"And how do you know who's guilty?"

"The registry—"

"Was written by monks," Jonah said. "Brother Cassian. He confessed it to me in a ruined monastery with his hands shaking. His order built the first lists during the Dragon War. Names gathered for protection, he said. For *provisioning*. And every purge since has descended from those careful, precise, well-intentioned pages." She took a step forward, up toward the stair, and Voss did not retreat but her hand tightened on the lantern. "Your lists aren't order, Inquisitor. They're just the slowest form of killing. The kind that takes centuries."

* * *

*Voss*

The girl was wrong. Serane Voss had spent eleven years building the certainty that the girl was wrong, and she was not going to surrender it in a basement to a kitchen thief with flour still under her fingernails.

And yet.

"Order requires lists," she said, and heard how the words sounded — rehearsed, worn smooth as a stair tread. She pushed on anyway. "Every functioning thing in this kingdom runs on record. Who owes tax. Who owes service. Who may travel. You want to live in a country with no memory? I've seen what that looks like. I grew up in a border town where the old kingdom's records burned in the Dragon War, and for thirty years afterward anyone could claim anything. Land, lineage, innocence. Do you know what filled the vacuum? Not freedom. Men with the loudest voices and the longest knives. Lists are not the enemy of the weak, girl. Lists are the *only* protection the weak have ever had."

"Then why do your lists only ever seem to find the weak?"

The question went into her like a splinter. Because it was true, and she had known it was true for weeks now, since Wenna Thornbury had sat across from her in an interrogation cell with her folded hands and her terrible calm and recited forty names of the dead as though reading a grocery list, and Serane had looked at that woman — healer, midwife, buryer of children — and understood that somewhere in her own careful files was a machine that would call this woman a plague risk, and had written a report omitting exactly that.

She had told herself it was strategy. Preserving an asset. Keeping a thread unpulled until the investigation required it.

She had been lying to herself, and she was a good enough inquisitor to know when she was lying.

"The gifted are dangerous," she said, and it came out quieter than she intended. "Not evil. Dangerous. A woman who can taste poison in anything can also put it there and never be caught. A man who can sense a lie can shape a courtroom. Power without accountability is the oldest plague there is. The registry isn't cruelty. It's quarantine."

"Quarantine." Jonah laughed, and there was no humor in it, only a raw exhausted bitterness that seemed to come up from her boots. "I was quarantined my whole life, Inquisitor. In a valley that suppresses everything I am. I tasted the king's food for nine years and saved lives doing it, and the price of that work was that I could never leave, never be seen, never be *known*. You call that quarantine. From in here it looks like a cage with very clean bars." She gestured at the darkness behind her, at the shelves full of sleeping names. "And this — this is your ledger of everyone you've ever caged. Everyone whose cage failed. You want me to believe burning it unleashes chaos? Look at what keeping it has done. Harrowmere. The purge before that. The one before *that*. Four hundred years of precise methodology, and the body count only ever goes up."

"The purges are the crown's doing, not the list's—"

"The list is how the crown finds them." Jonah's voice dropped, and somehow that was worse than shouting. It dropped the way a blade drops. "My mother is in one of those books. Page after page, her name, her mother's name, back to women who were healed and hedged and hid, and every single entry was written by someone who thought they were being *careful*. My mother died in the Veiled Valley because she couldn't leave it. The valley kept her safe from magic and killed her with safety. And now you hold the key to the same cage for everyone who comes after her, and you're standing there telling me it's medicine."

Voss found that her mouth had gone dry. She lifted the lantern slightly, and the light moved across Jonah Thornbury's face — young, gaunt, a healing cut at her hairline from the ford, eyes that held no hatred at all. That was the unbearable part. The girl didn't hate her. She pitied her, the way you pity someone standing in a burning house explaining the excellent fire code.

"If I let you do this," Voss said slowly, "there is no walking it back. Every name gone. Including yours. Including—" She stopped.

"Including the ones you've been leaving out," Jonah said softly.

The words hit the exact wound. Voss felt her own breath catch. *How does she know.* And then, absurdly, she knew how: because the girl had spent her whole life reading people the way Voss read documents, finding the poison in the honeyed pears, the omission in the report. Two professions, two survivals, converging in a basement.

"I don't know what you mean," Voss said.

"You keep a private registry. Names you've omitted. I've met people you've spared — Odile's caravan, half the river towns. You've been softening the machine from inside for months, and you tell yourself it's efficiency, and some nights you know better." Jonah climbed one more step. They were nearly level now, close enough that Voss could smell woodsmoke and river water on her. "You've already started burning your own little list, Inquisitor. I'm just proposing we finish the job."

"That is not the same thing."

"No," Jonah agreed. "It's smaller. Mine is bigger. But it's the same door, and you've already put your hand on the latch."

Above them, muffled through the stone, the trumpets changed key. The ceremony was moving into its second movement. Soon there would be guards sent down to fetch the ledgers for the public burning — the theater of it, the crown destroying its old lists while writing ten times as many new ones in private. Voss had read the schedule. She had signed off on part of it.

Her hesitation, she realized, had lasted too long. Jonah saw it too; something flickered in the girl's face, hope or fear, hard to tell apart at this depth.

"Guards will come," Voss said. "When they find the vault open and me standing here talking instead of arresting you—"

"Then decide," Jonah said. "Now. While deciding is still free."

* * *

*Jonah*

Later — years later, if she lived to have years — Jonah would remember that the decision, when it came, was invisible. No thunderclap. No long dramatic pause. Voss simply looked at her for one more moment, and then turned her head and listened to the distant trumpets, and something in her posture settled, the way a scale settles when the last weight is placed.

"The fire," Voss said. "Where is it? You didn't bring flame down six floors of stone."

"It's coming to me."

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only one I have." And it was true, and it was mad, and she said it anyway because Ember-that-Remains had told her, in his voice like embers settling, *dragons keep their bargains*, and because she had spent the dragon's gift once already in her heart and knew she could not spend it twice on herself. "There's a dragon dying in the Ashreach mountains, Inquisitor. The last one. He's flying here. His last flame is the only fire in the world that will take these books — ordinary fire, the kind your guards carry, the crown tested that centuries ago, that's why the vault is stone and iron and why the ledgers are treated against burning. But dragon-fire is the fire that wrote the first page of this war, and it's the only fire that can end it."

Voss stared at her. "You're asking me to believe a dragon is coming to the capital. On Purge Eve. To help a kitchen thief commit arson."

"I'm not asking you to believe. I'm asking you to wait ninety seconds."

Somewhere above, a bell began ringing — not the ceremony bells, a different rhythm, urgent, wrong. Shouting threaded down through the stone. The crowd's roar fractured into something higher, thinner.

Fear. The square had gone from celebration to fear in the space of a heartbeat.

Voss's head came up. For a moment her face was nakedly, humanly astonished — the look of a woman whose entire model of the world had just been contradicted by the sky itself.

"What," she said, "is happening up there."

And then the whole palace shuddered, dust sifting from the vault ceiling, and from very far away came a sound Jonah had heard only once before, at the caldera's edge, and had hoped never to hear directed at walls full of people: the cry of the last dragon in Cael, growing louder, wingbeats like a storm learning to knock.

"He keeps his bargains," Jonah whispered.

Voss looked at the vault. At the girl. At the stairs leading up into the noise. Her hand closed around the iron key until the knuckles went white — and Jonah saw, in the space of that grip, the entire argument they'd just fought replay itself behind the inquisitor's eyes, lists against ashes, order against mercy, forty-three dead in Harrowmere against forty names recited by a healer with folded hands.

The guards' boots began hammering down the upper stair.

"Move," Serane Voss said, and stepped aside.
