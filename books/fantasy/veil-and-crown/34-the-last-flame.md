## 34. The Last Flame

The vault mouth breathed cold.

Jonah felt it against her face as she stood at the top of the marble stair, the iron key still warm in her fist from the long climb through the service tunnels. Below her, shelves upon shelves of ledgers stretched back into the dark like the ribs of some enormous sleeping animal — centuries of names bound in calfskin and vellum, each one a life reduced to ink and a clerk's careful hand. *Thornbury, Jonah.* Her own name was down there somewhere. Her mother's above it. Wenna's, if Voss had been thorough enough to write it before the old woman's silence outlasted her patience.

And in the square above her — she could hear it even through stone and earth, a sound like the sea deciding something — the Great Purge had begun to come apart at the seams.

Because Ember-that-Remains had come.

She had felt him before she heard him: a pressure behind her ribs, a struck-bell note that only she could hear, the way she had felt him across half of dissolved Cael since the day her gift woke outside the valley stones. Then the roar. The whole city had heard the roar. She had been three tunnels deep when the palace shook and dust sifted down between the flagstones like gray snow, and she had known — the way you know a knock at your own door — that he had kept his bargain without being asked.

*Dragons keep their bargains,* he had told her in the caldera, in a voice like embers settling. *It is the last law we have left.*

Now the last dragon of Cael lay dying in the ceremonial square, and his fire was hers to spend, and there were two things it could buy.

* * *

Ember-that-Remains had not flown in forty years.

His wings had gone to membrane and memory, his scales to slag, and the flight from the Ashreach had cost him what little the dying had spared. He had landed badly, deliberately, in the center of the square where the scaffolds rose and the clerks of the crown sat with their new lists, and he had let them see him — all of him, the ruin and the grandeur both — because terror was the one gift he still had in abundance.

He could smell everything from here. The tallow smoke of the ceremony torches. The sour sweat of guards who had never imagined their careers would include this. The chalk-and-ink smell of the registry ledgers being carried up the scaffold stairs in armloads, to be burned publicly as theater while ten thousand truer names waited below the ground, safe and damning.

And beneath all of it, faint as a thread pulled through cloth, he could smell her. The gifted girl. The taster. The one whose gift had rung across the mountains like a bell struck in an empty cathedral, the first true note he had heard since his mate died in the Dragon War and the world went quiet around him.

She was close now. Below him. In the dark with the books.

He had offered her his flame once already, in the caldera, and she had not answered. He understood why. Flame spent is life spent; every dragon learns that in the eggshell. To give the last fire is to give the last self, and no creature gives that lightly, not even for a door out of a dead kingdom.

But he had flown anyway. Unbidden. Because dragons keep their bargains, and because — though he would never have said it in words, dragons do not waste words — he had wanted, at the end, to be *used*. Not mourned. Not studied by scholars and mounted in memory like a fossil. Used. Fire that is never spent is only hoarded death.

The guards were coming now, in their bright ranks, pikes leveled at a creature older than their kingdom's dissolution. He let them come. He was very tired, and the square was warm with torchlight, and somewhere beneath his breastbone the last flame sat in its chamber like a coal wrapped in ash, waiting to be asked for.

*Come, then, taster's daughter,* he thought, and closed his eyes against the pikes. *Ask me.*

* * *

Jonah came up out of the vault stair into chaos.

The great doors of the archive hall had been blown open — not by dragon-fire but by panic, by clerks fleeing with ledgers clutched to their chests like infants, by guards running the wrong way because no drill had ever covered *dragon in the square*. Torchlight poured in sideways across the marble floor. Through the tall windows she could see him: Ember, vast and ruined, his wings folded like a burnt cathedral around himself, ringed by soldiers who did not dare approach and did not dare retreat.

Her heart broke a little, seeing him. He had flown on wings that should not have held him. He had crossed a country that officially did not exist to die in a square full of people who had written his kind out of their histories. And he had done it for her — or for the bargain, or for whatever ancient pride moved a dragon at the end — and either way the debt was the same, and it was due now.

"Jonah."

She turned.

Serane Voss stood at the far end of the hall, alone. No guards. Her inquisitor's coat was scorched along one sleeve — she had been in the square when Ember landed — and her hand rested on the hilt of her sword without gripping it. Between them, on a wheeled cart abandoned by a fleeing clerk, sat a single ledger, its cover stamped with the crowned serpent of the registry office.

"I followed the tunnel maps," Voss said. Her voice was steady, but Jonah had spent her whole life reading what people tried not to taste, and under the steadiness she caught something else. Something like vertigo. "I guessed you'd come for the vault. I've known since Harrowmere. Since the ford." She took one step forward, stopped. "I could call the guard. There are forty of them within a hundred paces, and they are looking for anything to do besides stand in front of that creature."

"But you haven't called them."

"No," Voss said. "Not yet."

The word hung between them like a blade balanced on its point. Jonah's hand tightened around the iron key until its teeth bit her palm. Behind Voss, through the windows, Ember shifted, and the whole square groaned with the movement, and somewhere a scaffold timber cracked like a shot.

"You've read the archives," Voss said. "Cassian's monastery. I know you have — I found the broken seal on the survey fragments weeks ago and I told myself it didn't matter." Her jaw tightened. "So you know what I know. That flame opens the door. That the door leads beyond magic's reach — beyond *us*, beyond the crown, beyond every list ever written. You could walk out of Cael tonight and no inquisitor would ever find you, because there would be nothing left in you to find. No gift. No trail. Just a woman gone where the law cannot follow."

"Yes," Jonah said quietly. "That's the offer."

"And instead you're standing between me and my vault."

"It isn't yours."

"It is the crown's, and the crown is order, and order is the only thing standing between this country and the knife-work of another century." Voss's voice rose, and for the first time Jonah heard the true thing underneath it — not cruelty, never cruelty, but fear, vast and disciplined and worn smooth by years of carrying it. "You think I love the burning? I have watched villages burn. I signed the orders at Harrowmere and I have not slept properly since. But the gifted are a plague risk, Thornbury. Unregistered power is a knife in the dark. The lists are how we *know*. Take away the lists and you take away the only map we have of where the danger sleeps—"

"The lists are how the dead are made."

Jonah hadn't meant to say it aloud. It came out low and level, the way she spoke when tasting, when the truth was on her tongue and there was no spitting it out.

"Wenna Thornbury," she went on, before Voss could draw breath. "My mother's teacher. Eighty-one years old. She couldn't light a candle with her gift if her life depended on it — she could tell when a fever would turn, that's all, that was the whole terrible power of her. Your riders took her from her garden. She gave them nothing. She recited names — the names of every gifted person she had buried, everyone the purges took before you were born, everyone the lists killed. She recited them until they stopped her mouth." Jonah's voice did not shake. She had learned stillness in a king's kitchen, holding poison on her tongue and smiling. "You want a map of where the danger sleeps, Inquisitor. Open that ledger on the cart. My name is in it. I taste poison for a living. That is the whole of my crime. Somewhere near it is my mother's name, and she died in the valley mending other people's children. Find me the danger. Show me the plague. Tell me which page of that book was worth Wenna's garden."

Voss said nothing. The torchlight moved on her face, and Jonah watched — the way she had watched a hundred courtiers, a hundred stewards, watching for the flavor beneath the flavor — as certainty met something it could not digest.

"The door," Voss said at last, and her voice had gone quieter, almost gentle, which was worse. "Take the dragon's fire and go. I will even look away. That is more mercy than the registry has shown anyone in four hundred years. Why won't you take it?"

"Because the door doesn't answer the question."

"What question?"

"Why any of us have to run at all." Jonah lifted the iron key, and the torchlight ran along its teeth. "Wenna gave me this key and told me it opened a way out. She was wrong, or she was lying to make me move, or she knew — maybe she knew — that the real lock was here, underground, made of paper. Every name ever written. Every list the purges descend from. Burn the door open and I'm free and the book stays whole, and next year there's another girl in another kitchen with a tongue too accurate, and another Serane Voss with another sincere belief, and the machine grinds on with different faces." She looked at the vault doors behind her, at the dark beyond them where the shelves waited. "Burn the book, and the machine forgets who it was hunting. Not forever. Nothing is forever. But long enough. Long enough for someone to grow up unlisted."

"You're asking me to step aside," Voss said, "while you destroy the crown's property and the crown's order with it."

"I'm asking you to choose," Jonah said, "which list you want your name on."

* * *

Ember felt her decision before she made it.

That was the way of it, at the end, when a dragon's flame has shrunk to a single chamber around the heart: the boundaries thin. He tasted her grief and her fury and, beneath them, the strange clean taste of a mind made up — and it rang through him like the bell-note of her gift had rung across the Ashreach, except this note was deeper, and it hurt more, and it was better.

She was not going to run.

He had carried the hope of the door across a mountain range on failing wings. Escape, flight, the far country beyond magic's reach — these were dragon things, the oldest dragon things, the instinct that had driven their kind from sky to sky while the world narrowed around them. He had wanted it for her. He had wanted, perhaps, to spend his last fire on the one act his whole species had lived for: getting out.

And she was going to turn it on a room full of books instead.

He laughed. It came out as smoke and sparks, and the nearest guards flinched back a dozen paces, and high above the square a clerk dropped an armload of ledgers that scattered down the scaffold stairs like startled birds.

Good, he thought. Let them scatter. Let them learn what it feels like.

The flame stirred in its chamber. It was small now — a coal, a seed, the last of a fire that had once melted the passes of the Greywether and lit the night sky over three kingdoms — but small does not mean weak. The last flame of anything is the purest. It has burned away everything it does not need.

He gathered it. He felt the ash of forty years fall away from it, felt it wake and stretch and remember itself, and the pain of that waking was so total and so sweet that he roared — not in defiance of the pikes, not for the frightened clerks, but simply because a body in its last extremity must make some sound, and this was his.

Then he turned his head toward the archive hall, toward the tall dark windows, toward the girl standing in the torchlight with an iron key in her hand, and he spoke — not in embers now, not in the slow language of the caldera, but plainly, in the common tongue, so that every soldier in the square heard it and would carry the hearing to their graves:

*Taster's daughter. The fire is yours. Spend it well, and remember me not as the last — remember me as the keeper.*

* * *

Jonah walked down into the vault with the dragon's flame cupped against her chest.

She could not describe afterward how it got there. There had been no moment of transfer she could name — only that she had stood at the vault mouth with the key in one hand and her empty palm open, and Ember's voice had filled the hall, and then there was warmth in her hands like a bird made of coals, beating gently, wanting only to be released. Her gift woke around it, singing, every nerve alive, and she understood suddenly what her whole life in the valley had muted: this was what she was. Not a taster. A vessel. The valley had kept her safe by keeping her small.

The shelves stretched away into the dark. She walked the central aisle slowly, reading spines as she passed. *Registry of the Gifted, Reign of Aldous III.* *Roll of Suspected Persons, Northern Marches.* *Index of Executions, with Cross-Reference.* Centuries of careful, meticulous, conscientious work — Cassian's order had built this, monk by monk, entry by entry, believing they were making the kingdom safe. Every purge descended from these shelves. Harrowmere's chalk marks. Wenna's garden. The child's shoe in the ash.

Here. *Valley Registry, Current Year.* Her finger found the spine. She pulled the ledger free and opened it, and there in the middle of the page, in a clerk's neat hand: *Thornbury, Jonah, taster. Gift: discernment of toxins.* And above it, in faded brown ink two generations old: *Thornbury, Maren, midwife.*

"Hello, Mother," Jonah whispered.

Behind her, boots on marble. Measured. Unhurried. Stopping at a respectful distance.

"If you burn it," Voss said, "there will be consequences. For the city. For you. For me, if I let it happen. Everything I am is built on the idea that the lists hold us together."

"I know," Jonah said. She did not turn around. "That's why it has to be you who decides. Not me. I decided the moment I picked this up." The flame beat against her palms, patient, warm, waiting. "You get to decide whether you're the kind of person who stands in front of a fire to protect a book of the dead."

A long silence. Through the vault's high vents, faintly, Jonah could hear the square: shouting, the groan of timbers, and beneath it all a sound she would remember for the rest of her life — Ember breathing, slow and vast, a bellows winding down.

"He's dying up there," Jonah said. "Right now. For this. He flew on wings that shouldn't have held him to keep a bargain I never accepted. I can't waste that on a door." Now she turned, and the flame lit her face, and she saw Voss see it — saw the inquisitor understand, finally and completely, exactly what was standing in front of her, and how briefly it mattered. "Step aside, Serane. Or don't. Either way, the book burns. The only question left is what your name means tomorrow."

Voss's hand left her sword hilt.

She looked at the ledger in Jonah's arms — at the two names, mother and daughter, stacked like sediment — and something in her face moved the way ice moves before it becomes water. Not agreement. Not surrender. Something smaller and harder: the first hairline crack in a wall built over a lifetime.

"I was at Harrowmere," she said, very quietly. "There was a girl there. Fourteen. Her whole gift was that she could find lost things. Keys. Rings. A goat, once." Her voice caught on nothing. "We never found what she was lost from."

She stepped aside.

Jonah opened her hands.

The flame went up the shelves like dawn going up a mountain range. It did not spread the way ordinary fire spreads, leaping and greedy; it moved the way Ember had moved, deliberate and total, finding every ledger as if it had always known where each one lived. Calfskin covers curled. Vellum pages blackened and lifted, and the names rose off them — Jonah could almost see them, could almost *taste* them, thousands of names going up as heat and light, unbinding themselves from ink, from category, from the careful clerks' hands that had written them down. *Thornbury, Jonah. Thornbury, Maren.* Gone. Free. Unwritten.

She stood in the middle of it and let it wash over her, and she did not weep, though something in her chest cracked wide enough that she knew she would feel the draft of it for years.

When she finally turned, the vault was an orange roaring country behind her, and Voss was gone — no, not gone. Standing at the foot of the stair, her back to the fire, facing outward toward the hall, toward the guards' shouts in the distance, placing herself between the burning and the world. Standing watch. Jonah would understand later what that posture had cost her, and what it meant, and she would never speak of it and neither would Voss, and that silence would be the truest treaty either of them ever signed.

Above her, through stone and earth, she felt it: the guttering.

Ember's flame, spent, going out of him the way evening goes out of a valley — gradually, then all at once. His breath slowed. His heart, which had beaten for six hundred years, missed, and caught, and missed again. And in his last moments the last dragon of Cael lifted his ruined head, looked at the smoke beginning to pour from the archive windows, and roared — not the roar of landing, not the roar of pain, but the old war-roar, the one that had melted the Greywether passes, given now with the last air in his lungs, so that the whole city would remember that fire had chosen, at the end, to burn the right thing.

Mid-roar, he guttered out.

The square went silent. The pikes lowered, one by one, into the ash-fall.

And Jonah Thornbury climbed the vault stairs out of the smoke with empty hands, carrying nothing — no key, no flame, no escape — carrying only what could not be put down: the weight of every name that had ever been written in that place, and the responsibility of being the one still alive to remember them.

Through the window, over the square, the first embers of the burning registry were rising off the roof and scattering on the wind — small bright points drifting out over the city, over the marked houses and the fearful kitchens, falling like a snowfall of names set loose.

One of them, Jonah would swear forever after, drifted down and settled, still glowing, at her feet.

She bent and picked it up.
