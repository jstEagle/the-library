## 35. Ash on the Wind

The fire did not roar so much as *speak*.

Jonah stood at the vault mouth with her hands still raised from the act — palms blackened, sleeves scorched to the elbow, the smell of her own singed hair mixing with the sweeter reek of burning vellum and ink. Behind her, through the iron door Wenna's key had opened, the registry of the gifted was dying shelf by shelf, and it died loudly, the way a great beast dies: in shudders, in exhalations, in a language of crack and collapse that she felt in her teeth.

Ember-that-Remains had given everything. She had felt it happen — not seen, not exactly, because when the last flame left him there had been nothing left to see with, a whiteness that was absence rather than light — but she had felt it pass through her like a bell being rung from the inside, the same struck-bell sensation she'd first known on the road outside the valley, when her gift woke and called to him across a hundred miles of sleeping country. He had come unbidden. Dragons keep their bargains. And now somewhere above her, in the ceremonial square where the scaffolds stood and the crown's choir had been rehearsing for a week, the sound of the crowd had changed.

She climbed the vault stairs slowly, one hand on the wall, because her legs had opinions of their own and none of them were good ones.

The service corridor beyond was full of smoke that moved wrong — not drifting but *flowing*, drawn upward through the old ventilation shafts the monks' maps had shown her, as though the palace itself were inhaling. Through the shaft-grates overhead, embers rose. That was the thing she would remember all her life, she thought distantly, the thing she would tell the girl someday, years from now, if there was a girl and if there were years: the embers went up in ordered spirals, page-shaped, some of them still legible at the edges, names curling off the burning paper in strokes of gold before the gold too gave way. A name rose past the third grate. She could not read whose it was. It didn't matter whose it was. That was the whole point, and the point hurt more than she expected, because every name had been someone's whole point once — a mother writing it down in fear or faith, a clerk recording it in careful hand, a child growing up beneath it without knowing it hung over them like weather.

Now it was ash on the wind, and the wind was taking it over the city.

* * *

She came up into the square through a servant's postern that Cassian's maps had marked with a monk's wry annotation — *for those who must leave quickly* — and stepped out into chaos that did not notice her.

The Great Purge ceremony had been designed, she understood now, as theater. The scaffolds. The choir. The biers where the registry ledgers were to have been displayed — *cleansed by fire, the crown merciful even in erasure* — while thousands of new names were quietly added to the fresh volumes waiting on trestle tables under silk. She had watched the scaffolds rise for three days from the palace kitchens, tasting the cooks' anxiety in oversalted stock. Theater requires an audience that believes, and the audience had believed right up until the moment the ground under the square shook and a dragon the size of a cathedral came down out of the smoke like the answer to a prayer no one had dared say aloud.

Ember was dead now. She knew it the way you know your own heartbeat has resumed after a stumble — by its absence. The vast shape in the center of the square lay still, wings folded like a finished argument, hide gone the grey of banked coals, and already the crowd was learning what to do with that stillness. Some fled. Some knelt. One woman near the eastern colonnade was weeping openly, without shame, and Jonah thought: *she is old enough to remember the last dragon, or her mother told her, and grief has been waiting in her all this time for a place to put itself.*

But most of the crowd — most of them were looking at the sky.

Because the embers were still rising, and the wind had come around from the west, and the wind was carrying them out over the rooftops of the capital in long glittering skeins, and everyone in that square who had a name in any ledger, anywhere, in any parish copy or garrison roll or inquisitor's file — everyone marked, everyone registered, everyone whose existence was a line of ink in a vault below the feet of the powerful — was watching their own name burn its way eastward and understanding, with the slow avalanche certainty of a whole city thinking the same thought at once, that the lists were gone.

Not hidden. Not stolen. Gone. Burned in front of God and the crown, by a dragon, in the middle of the ceremony meant to sanctify them.

A guard captain was shouting orders that contradicted each other. A herald clutched a scroll he no longer had the authority to read. On the scaffold, three prisoners bound for the morning's demonstration stood unguarded, and one of them — a boy of perhaps fifteen, wrists raw — simply walked down the steps, past the frozen soldiers, and kept walking, and nobody stopped him, because stopping him required a reason and the reasons were all ash now, blowing over the Tanner's Quarter in beautiful golden flakes.

Jonah stood in the shadow of the postern arch with soot in her eyelashes and let it happen around her.

She had imagined triumph. In the tunnels, in the dark, with Cassian's confession still ringing in her ears — *every purge descends from our careful lists; destroy what we made* — she had imagined this moment would feel like victory, clean and bright as a drawn blade. Instead she felt hollowed, scooped out like a gourd, empty in the exact shape of a dragon. Exhaustion sat on her shoulders like a wet cloak. Her gift, unbound since the ford, since the river and the open use of it, hummed faintly under her skin — tasting the smoke, tasting the crowd's terror-sweat and wild hope, tasting the char and the ink and, underneath it all, the mineral ghost of Ember's fire still hanging in the air like the memory of a voice.

There was nothing left to taste of him. That was the worst of it. She cast her senses upward, outward, desperate and ashamed of the desperation, and found only ash where an ancient loneliness had been.

*You asked what I would carry forward,* she said to the smoke, silently, in the direction of the dead. *I didn't answer. I'm answering now. Whether you hear it or not.*

* * *

She crossed the square because Ember lay in it, and she would not leave him among strangers.

Getting to him was like wading upstream through a river of people — guards abandoning posts, clerks from the ceremony running with armloads of documents they were saving on their own initiative, a whole bureaucracy trying to rescue itself piecemeal while its center burned. Someone had already begun prying scales from the dragon's flank. Of course they had. A dragon's scale was worth a farm, a shop, ten years of bread; the poor of the capital had watched wealth land dead in their square and they were not going to waste it mourning. Two men worked with knives, cursing, sweating. A child stood nearby holding a single scale against her chest like a doll.

Jonah shouldered between them. "Enough."

They looked at her — a soot-blackened woman, half her hair burned to stubble at one temple, eyes that made both men step back without knowing why — and they went. The child stayed. The child looked at Jonah, then at the dragon, then held out the scale she'd taken, offering it back with the grave solemnity of the very young.

"Keep it," Jonah said. Her voice came out ruined, a whisper dragged over gravel. "He wouldn't mind. From you."

The child ran off clutching it, and Jonah laid her palm flat against Ember's hide one last time. Warmth lingered in the great body the way warmth lingers in a hearthstone hours after the fire dies. Beneath her hand, near the base of the throat, one scale sat loose — the last one, the one his dying body had slackened its grip on, duller than the rest, veined with a faint ember-light that pulsed once, twice, and steadied.

She worked it free with both hands. It was heavier than it had any right to be, warm as a living thing, and when she closed her fingers around it she felt — or imagined she felt, and decided she did not care which — a final pulse of that struck-bell note, fading, fading, patient.

*I'm here,* she told him. Or he told her. The grammar of dragons probably allowed for both.

Then the bells began.

Not the ceremony bells — the alarm bells, the quarter-master's towers, the garrison calls, all of them at once and all of them contradictory, because the machinery of the Purge had just discovered it had no hands. Every arrest order, every transport manifest, every parish list of the marked had lived in paper, and the paper was smoke over the Tanner's Quarter. The inquisitorate's central rolls — Voss's own files, Jonah realized, with a strange pang — had been housed in the vault annex beside the registry. All of it burning together, indiscriminately, the guilty records and the innocent ones, the hunters' careful work and the hunted's death sentences reduced to the same grey snow.

Across the city, she would learn later, doors were opening. Marked families who had spent generations folding themselves small — not lighting candles after dark, not singing, not healing, not *being* — stepped outside into the falling ash and looked up. In the Weavers' Row, an old man with earth-gift in his hands turned his face to the embers and wept. In the river districts, a midwife who had hidden her craft for thirty years took down the shutter she'd nailed over her window the day of her registration and let the light in. They would tell the story differently, each of them — some would swear they saw the dragon rise again at the end, winging east; some would say a woman in a burnt cloak walked out of the smoke carrying fire in her fist; some would say it was the monks, the ghosts of the monks, coming back to undo their own sin. Stories are how cities digest miracles. By winter there would be forty versions and none of them true and all of them true enough.

The Purge did not end that day — endings are slower than that, and crueller — but it collapsed, the way a roof collapses: a groan, a sag, a fall. Without lists there could be no hunts. Without hunts, no quotas. Without quotas, the whole apparatus of erasure lost its skeleton and lay down in the street like a suit of armor with no one inside. Within a week, officers would be burning their own copies of parish rolls, not from mercy but from self-preservation, because the wind had changed and the wind remembered names. Within a month, the Gift Registry Act would be quietly shelved, unmentioned, the way crowns shelve things they intend to deny ever wanting.

But Jonah knew none of that yet. She knew only the square, the smoke, the weight of a scale in her hand, and the fact that she was still standing, which surprised her more than anything else that day.

* * *

Voss found her at the edge of the square, where the crowds thinned toward the river stairs.

Jonah heard her before she saw her — boots with a soldier's deliberate rhythm, unhurried, and beneath the boot-steps, when Jonah's treacherous gift reached out unasked, the taste of a woman running on something colder than adrenaline: iron filings, candle wax, and underneath, faintly, the green bite of fear finally admitted.

"Inquisitor." Jonah did not turn around.

"You're under no obligation to speak to me," Serane Voss said. "In fact, I'd advise against it. There will be inquiries. Someone will be blamed for the vault fire, and the blame will want a body."

"And you're telling me this why?"

A pause. When Voss answered, her voice had the flat care of a statement being read into a record that only she could hear. "Because an hour ago I stood in front of a locked door with a drawn blade and a clear duty, and I stepped aside. Whatever that makes me, it isn't a woman who hands a survivor to a commission of inquiry." Another pause, shorter. "The official account will call it a vault accident. Storage of ceremonial flammables. Negligence in the archive office. There will be a scapegoat and he will be a clerk named Dorren who cheated at cards and deserves some misfortune."

Jonah turned then. Up close, the inquisitor looked like a woman who had set down something very heavy and hadn't yet learned how to stand without it. Her uniform was immaculate. Her eyes were not.

"You'll keep your own registry," Jonah said. It wasn't a question. She had seen it in the woman at the vault mouth, in the hesitation that had cost her standing — the crack, hairline but real, that Wenna's composure had started weeks ago in an interrogation room. "Names you omit. You can't help yourself. It's the only power you trust."

Something moved behind Voss's eyes — anger, maybe, or its cousin, recognition. "And you'll bury a dragon's scale next to a grave in a valley that doesn't exist," she said. "We're both keeping our religions. Pray mine does less harm than yours did."

"It's not a religion. It's a debt." Jonah lifted the scale slightly, letting the dull ember-veins catch the smoky light. "He paid it. I'm just carrying the receipt."

For a long moment the two women regarded each other across the ash-fall — the hunter and the thief, the list-keeper and the list-burner, two survivors of different machines, each seeing in the other what she might have become with one different turn of luck. Then Voss inclined her head, a degree, no more, and walked away into the smoke, and Jonah understood that she had just been erased from a story by the only person with the power to keep her in it, and that this was the closest thing to forgiveness either of them would ever offer.

* * *

She walked out of the city along the river road, because the gates were jammed with fleeing ceremony guests and the river path belonged to bargemen and beggars, and she had learned on the grain barge how to be invisible among people who fed you.

The ash followed her for miles. It fell finer as she went, sifting down through the evening light like grey pollen, settling on the hedgerows, the towpath, the backs of her hands. Once she stopped and held out her palm and let a fleck land there, and looked at it, and thought about names. Somewhere in that grey dust was her mother's name, and Wenna's, and the hedge-healer's sister taken in the last purge, and Brother Cassian's whole contrite order, and fifteen-year-old boys and midwives and earth-gifted old men, all of it mixed now, indistinguishable, equal at last. Grief and fury had carried her down into the vault. What rose out of it was quieter and harder to hold: an emptiness that was also a clearing. Room. The kind of room you get in a forest after a fire, where nothing grows yet and everything could.

Her body catalogued its damages as she walked — burned hands, a rib that complained, feet that had stopped filing reports — and she let it catalogue, because pain meant alive, and alive meant she had won whatever this was. Hollow triumph. Triumph like the shell of a beetle: perfect shape, nothing inside. She suspected the inside would grow back. She suspected it would grow back different, and that this was the price, and that Ember would have said the price was fair, and that she would spend years deciding whether she agreed.

At the ferry crossing below the city, an old bargewoman took one look at her — the burns, the soot, the thousand-yard stare — and waved her aboard without asking for coin. "Fires in the city," the woman said, spitting over the side. "Saw the glow from here. Whole sky went orange, then it snowed grey. Bad luck."

"Good luck," Jonah said.

The bargewoman squinted at her. "That so?"

"That so." Jonah settled against the rail, the scale a warm weight against her sternum where she'd tucked it inside her shirt, next to the skin, next to the heartbeat. Eastward, over the drowned marshes, the last light was going out of the sky, and high up — so high it might have been a star, might have been imagination, might have been a promise kept past the keeping — something bright traced a long arc and vanished.

Dragons keep their bargains. But this — this was hers now. The valley. The grave. The girl she hadn't met yet, with the suspiciously accurate tongue. An inheritance of silence instead of power, and a scale to bury, and a long road home through a country that officially did not exist, walking out of the smoke with ash on the wind at her back and, for the first time since the criers read the Act in the market square, no name anywhere in the world for anyone to hunt.

She touched the scale through the cloth, once, the way you touch a shoulder in passing to say *I'm here, I'm going on.*

Then the ferry slid out into the dark water, and Jonah Thornbury went home.
