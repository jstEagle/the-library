## 36. What Voss Did Next

The report was due at dawn.

Serane Voss sat in her office above the Inquisitor's Court with the window open, because the smoke still hung over the capital like a second sky, and closing the window would have meant admitting she could smell it. Three days since the fire. Three days since the dragon fell out of the ceremonial square's morning light and died on the flagstones with its throat still open in a roar, and the vault beneath the Hall of Records burned from the inside out, and four hundred years of careful, meticulous, righteous bookkeeping went up as ash and drifted east over the rooftops of the city like black snow.

They were still sweeping it out of the gutters.

Her quill hovered over the official form. *Report of Incident, Pursuant to Article Twelve of the Gift Registry Act.* The parchment was good stock, the kind reserved for accounts that would be read by ministers. The ink was fresh. Everything else was a lie.

She had written three sentences so far.

*A catastrophic failure of the vault's preservation systems — specifically, the ignition of aged sealing-wax stores within an enclosed stone chamber — resulted in the total loss of the registry archives.*

It was even, in its way, true. There had been wax. There had been fire. There had been an enclosed stone chamber. Serane had learned, in eleven years of service, that the most durable lies were built entirely of true materials, mortared together in the wrong order. A lie made of truths could survive any interrogation, because every piece of it would hold under questioning. Only the shape was false, and no one ever thought to question the shape.

Below the window, a clerk crossed the courtyard carrying a stack of blank ledgers. Replacement volumes. The binders had been working day and night; there was talk of reconstructing what could be reconstructed from duplicate copies held in provincial offices, from parish rolls, from the memories of clerks who had spent their careers copying names. The crown wanted its lists back. The crown did not yet understand that the lists were not going to come back, that they had not burned but *dissolved*, that ash does not keep records, and that somewhere between the vault and the wind, several thousand human beings had quietly stopped existing on paper while continuing, inconveniently, to exist in flesh.

Serane dipped her quill. Did not write. Dipped it again.

*The failure is attributed to* —

She stopped. Her hand was steady. That surprised her. She had expected her hand to shake, and when it didn't, she found herself watching it the way one watches a stranger perform a familiar task — with interest, and a small cold unease.

*Attributed to.* Every report needed an attribution. Every catastrophe needed a cause, and every cause needed a name attached to it, because the machinery of the crown could not digest an event without swallowing someone whole. That was the first thing they taught you in the Academy: justice is a system for converting grief into paperwork. She had believed it once the way one believes in the weather — totally, without examination, because it was simply what the sky did.

She thought about the girl at the vault mouth.

Three days, and she still could not assemble the memory into a coherent order. It kept rearranging itself. Sometimes the sequence was: the dragon's shadow, the screaming crowds, the stairwell, the key in the thief's hand, the conversation. Sometimes it began with the conversation, which was impossible, and ended with the dragon, which was worse. And always, always, it ended where it had actually ended — with Serane standing aside. With her boots planted on the stone step and her body deliberately, consciously, unforgivably *still*, while a woman she had hunted across half a dissolved kingdom walked past her into the burning dark with a dying dragon's last flame cupped in her hands like a lamp.

She had told herself, in the first hour, that she had been paralyzed. Shock. A reasonable physiological response to chaos. She had written the word *paralyzed* in her private notes and then looked at it for a long time and then, very carefully, drawn a line through it.

She had not been paralyzed. Paralyzed people do not step backward to clear another person's path.

* * *

The knock came at midmorning, which meant it was important, because Clerk Hessel knew better than to knock before noon unless the building was on fire or the minister was in the hall.

"Enter."

Hessel came in with his ledger hugged to his chest the way he carried everything, as though the building might at any moment demand he surrender his documents and he wished to be prepared to refuse. He was twenty-three, precise, ambitious in the bloodless way of young men who had never been hungry, and he had been Serane's shadow for two years. He stopped in front of her desk and stood at the particular attention that meant he had rehearsed his opening line.

"The reconstruction committee has requested your preliminary findings," he said. "Regarding the vault incident. They want attribution, ma'am. Names." He hesitated. "There's also a delegation from the Binders' Guild asking whether criminal negligence charges should be considered against the vault custodial staff. And the Minister of Purity has sent a personal note." He produced it, cream-colored and heavy, and set it on her desk with two fingers, as though it might bite. "He asks when the perpetrators will be identified."

"Perpetrators," Serane repeated.

"Yes, ma'am."

"Of a fire."

Hessel's composure flickered — just at the edges, the way a candle gutters when a door opens somewhere far off. "Of the destruction of state property, ma'am. Four centuries of registry. The Act's entire enforcement framework depended on those records. Without them—" He stopped himself, visibly recalculating, and finished in a smaller voice: "Without them we don't know who the gifted are."

"No," Serane said. "We don't."

She let the silence do its work. It was one of the few tools she had never had to learn; it came naturally, the way the gift of certainty had come naturally, if gift was the word. Hessel shifted his weight. Outside, the smoke-haze thinned and thickened like something breathing.

"You may tell the committee," she said, "that my preliminary findings indicate structural failure. The vault's sealing-wax stores were laid down during the reign of Aldous the Third — I've confirmed this with the Guild archivists. Wax of that age destabilizes. Compressed for four hundred years beneath a mountain of dry vellum and oak shelving, in a chamber sealed against all ventilation." She turned her hand palm-up, a small gesture of inevitability. "Sooner or later, such a chamber ignites itself. We are fortunate it happened during the ceremony, when the square was already evacuated of nonessential personnel, rather than at night, when the loss of life might have extended beyond one animal."

"One animal," Hessel said faintly.

"The dragon."

"Ma'am, the dragon destroyed a third of the eastern colonnade."

"And dragons are not charged with crimes, Clerk, because the law does not recognize them as persons. Which means the law cannot recognize them as perpetrators either. Do you see? This is why precision matters." She picked up the Minister's note without reading it again — she had read it at dawn, twice, feeling the words arrange themselves around her like a net — and held it to the candle flame she kept for sealing wax. It caught, curled, and was gone before Hessel finished drawing breath to object.

"That was correspondence from—"

"It was a question I intend to answer properly, once my findings are complete. He'll have his answer by week's end. You may go."

He went. At the door he paused, and for a moment Serane saw him weigh something — some small suspicion, some clerk-shaped instinct for discrepancy — and then decide, as ambitious men do, that whatever it was, it was safer unweighed. The door closed. She watched the ash of the Minister's note settle on her blotter and thought: *that is how it begins. Not with a decision. With a note you burn instead of answering.*

She had arrested people for less. She remembered Wenna Thornbury's face across the interrogation table — that extraordinary, unhurried calm, the old hedge-healer reciting names of the dead like a litany while Serane's questions broke against her like water against a sea wall — and she remembered writing the report afterward, and the sentence she had left out of it, the single omitted fact that had cost her nothing at the time and now sat in her chest like a swallowed coal.

She was keeping her own registry now. She understood that. It had started weeks ago — names quietly dropped from her reports, small mercies filed nowhere, a private ledger of omissions that grew heavier each time she added to it. The difference between herself and the vault she had failed to protect was only a matter of scale.

* * *

In the afternoon she went down to the vault.

What remained of it. The Hall of Records was closed, draped in mourning bunting that the ceremony had never gotten to use for its intended purpose, and the guards at the doors waved her through without challenge — everyone in the ministry knew Voss, the Inquisitor Ascendant, the woman whose purges ran clean and whose conviction rates were quoted at the Academy as exemplary. She descended the service stair with a lantern, though the shafts cut into the rock admitted grey daylight, and she walked the long scorched corridor to the vault mouth because she needed to see it, needed to stand where she had stood, needed to test her own account of events against the stones themselves the way she tested testimony against evidence.

The door was gone. Melted, warped, fused into the frame — iron run like tallow. Beyond it, the chamber was a cathedral of ash. The shelves had collapsed into char. Here and there a corner of metal binding survived, a hinge, a clasp, the brass corner-protector of a ledger that had protected nothing. The heat had risen so fast and burned so complete that the room looked less like a fire site than like the inside of a kiln after the potter has removed what she wanted and abandoned the rest.

Serane stood in the doorway and did the arithmetic she had been refusing to do for three days.

No wax store, however ancient, burned like this. No accident produced a fire hot enough to fuse iron and leave the surrounding masonry merely scorched. She had known this when she wrote the first draft of her report; she had known it the way she had known, at the river ford months ago, exactly what the taster girl was doing when she crouched at the water's edge and read the current like a page. Knowing things was her profession. It was the one thing she had never been able to stop doing, even when knowing became unbearable.

Which meant the fire had a source. Which meant the source had a keeper. Which meant that somewhere in her jurisdiction — probably far away by now, probably beyond any border the new maps acknowledged — there lived a woman who had taken the last flame of the last dragon in Cael and spent it not on escape, not on power, not on any of the thousand uses a gifted fugitive might reasonably choose, but on the destruction of a list of names.

Serane turned in a slow circle, lantern raised, and let herself finally think the thought whole:

*She could have opened the door.*

The archive fragments were clear enough; Serane had read them all, in the weeks when the hunt had become a puzzle she loved with an intensity that should have frightened her more than it did. The gate in the Ashreach foothills. The mechanism keyed to dragon-fire. A door out of dissolved Cael, out of the Act, out of the reach of every registry and every inquisitor and every purge forever. One flame. One choice. Flight, permanent and absolute, purchased at the cost of a dying creature's final breath.

Instead the girl had turned around. Instead she had walked back down the mountain, into the teeth of the Purge ceremony, into the capital, into the vault, and burned the names.

Not hers alone. Everyone's. Including, Serane realized, standing in the ash with her lantern trembling almost imperceptibly — including the names of people who had done harm. Including, perhaps, names that a rigorous mind might argue deserved to be found. The registry had not been only an instrument of terror. It had also, occasionally, in its vast indifferent way, been accurate. Somewhere in those four hundred years of ledgers there had been a poisoner, a hex-worker, a man in the northern provinces who had done genuinely monstrous things with his gift. Serane had put two of them away herself, once, on the strength of those records.

And the girl had burned them anyway. Had decided, with a dying dragon's fire in her hands, that a system which required four centuries of lists in order to function was a system that deserved to stop functioning — that the innocent it caged outweighed the guilty it caught, that safety purchased by cataloguing human beings was not safety but inventory.

It was, Serane thought, either the most naive act she had ever witnessed or the most rigorous. She honestly could not tell which. That frightened her more than the dragon had.

Because here was the thing she could not put in any report: for one moment at the vault mouth, with the heat breathing out of the dark and the girl looking at her — not pleading, not threatening, just *looking*, with the terrible clarity of someone who has already chosen — Serane Voss had agreed with her. Completely. Wordlessly. In the place where her convictions actually lived, deeper than doctrine, deeper than the Academy, deeper than the sincere and well-argued certainty that had carried her through eleven years of purges: in that place, she had looked at the registry and seen it plainly for the first time, the way one sees a room clearly only when it is on fire.

A list of names is a machine for making corpses. It does not matter what fuels it. It does not matter how carefully it is maintained. Feed it enough years and enough sincerity and it will grind, inevitably, from Wenna Thornbury's sister to Wenna Thornbury to the orphan girl in the valley kitchen whose only crime was a tongue too honest for her own safety. Serane had fed it gladly. She had been, she understood now, standing in the ashes of the thing she had spent her life feeding, and the only question left was what she would write about it.

* * *

She wrote the final report that night, by lamplight, in her best formal hand.

*A catastrophic failure of the vault's preservation systems, specifically the spontaneous ignition of aged sealing-wax stores within an enclosed and unventilated stone chamber, resulted in the total loss of the registry archives. The failure is attributed to cumulative structural deficiencies dating to the original construction, compounded by the unprecedented thermal event occasioned by the death of the specimen designated Ember-that-Remains during the same hour. No human agency contributed. No charges are recommended.*

She read it through four times. Each time, she tested it the way she tested any testimony: against the physical evidence, against witness statements, against probability. Each time, it held. True materials, wrong mortar. The Binders' Guild would confirm the age of the wax stores. The engineers would confirm the chamber's ventilation was inadequate — it was; she had checked, and the checking had felt like prayer. The dragon's death was public knowledge, witnessed by ten thousand people, and its fire was more than sufficient to explain any temperature the engineers cared to calculate. Nowhere in the document did a kitchen girl appear. Nowhere did an inquisitor appear. Nowhere did a key appear, or a choice, or a woman stepping aside on a stone stair.

She signed it. Then she sat for a long time with the signed page in front of her, waiting to feel something.

Corruption, she thought. That was the word for what she had done. She knew the word intimately; she had spent her career applying it to others, had built cases on thinner paper than this, had watched men hang for falsifications less elegant. If anyone ever proved what she had done tonight, she would lose everything — rank, purpose, the scaffold perhaps, though ministers rarely executed their own instruments, preferring quieter disappearances. She had committed the exact crime she existed to punish, and she had committed it competently, and the competence was somehow the worst part, because it proved how easy it was. One evening. One pen. Eleven years of credibility, spent in a single signature.

And yet.

She thought of the chalk marks on the houses of Harrowmere. She thought of the child's shoe in the ash, which she had never reported seeing because reporting it would have required explaining why an inquisitor had knelt in the ruins of a purged village holding a shoe. She thought of Wenna's litany of names, and of the crack in her own certainty that interview had opened — hairline then, structural now. She thought of the thousands of marked families across the city who had slept, these three nights, without the specific terror of being findable. Their papers were dead. Their names were wind. Whatever came next — whatever new lists the crown attempted, whatever reconstructions limped out of the provincial offices — there would be a gap, a generation of the gifted unaccounted for, and gaps were where survival lived.

She had spent her life believing that order required lists. She still believed it, in the abstract, the way one believes in the necessity of borders one would personally hate to live behind. But she had stood at the vault mouth and been offered a choice between the machine and the woman, and she had learned something about herself that no Academy lecture could undo: given the choice, she stepped aside.

That was not corruption. Or it was, but it was corruption in the direction of mercy, which was either a contradiction or the only kind worth committing. She refused to call it redemption. Redemption was a story people told about themselves afterward, and Serane Voss had written enough false reports to distrust the genre. Call it what it was: a falsification. An abuse of office. The same tool she had wielded for eleven years, reversed, turned at last upon the thing it had built.

She blotted the page, sealed it, and set it in the dispatch tray for the morning courier.

Then she took out a fresh sheet of paper, and after a moment's thought, she began a different document — private, unsigned, to be hidden among her own effects where no clerk would think to look. Not a confession. Confessions were for people who wanted forgiveness, and she had not decided whether she wanted anything of the kind. It was simpler than that. It was a list.

She wrote the names she remembered. The taster girl, Jonah Thornbury, last seen entering a burning vault and officially deceased in the same structural failure that claimed the archives — let the record show a death; deaths were useful; dead women were not hunted. Old Wenna, whose file Serane had quietly flagged *deceased in custody, natural causes* two days ago, a lie that had required three signatures and had gotten all three. The hedge-healers of the outer valleys. The smuggler matriarch Odile, whose information had pointed the hunt the wrong way and whom Serane had never corrected. Name after name, in her precise hand, the handwriting of a woman who had once believed that every name belonged in a ledger, now compiled into a ledger whose entire purpose was that it must never be found.

An anti-registry. A list whose function was erasure. She almost laughed when she saw what she was doing — the habit running so deep that even her rebellion took the form of documentation — and did not laugh, because the office was thin-walled and because it wasn't funny, quite. It was only the truth: that she had been made by the machine she served, and that a made thing can be remade, and that the remaking does not feel like freedom. It feels like forgery. It feels like signing your name to a lie at midnight and hoping the lie outlives your belief in it.

She folded the pages, hid them, and snuffed the lamp.

Dawn came grey and smelling of wet ash. From her window she watched the reconstruction crews cross the courtyard with their blank ledgers, and she composed in her head the briefing she would give the Minister — firm, detailed, blameless, boring — and beneath it, unspoken, the only honest sentence in the building:

*You are looking for a perpetrator, Minister. I am the perpetrator. I am also the reason you will never find her. Both of these things are true, and I have decided, this once, to let the second one matter more.*

The courier's bell rang in the yard. The dispatch tray went out with the morning light, and somewhere in the city, in a room she would never identify, a dead woman was teaching a living child which names to remember.

Serane Voss turned back to her desk, took up a fresh case file, and began, with steady hands, to lose it.
