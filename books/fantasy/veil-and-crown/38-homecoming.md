## 38. Homecoming

The lamp did not waver.

That was the first thing Jonah noticed, standing on the ridge with the cold of the Ashreach still living in her bones: the little flame down at the boundary stone burned perfectly steady, as if the night itself had agreed to hold still for it. Wind moved the grass in long silver strokes. Wind bent the pines along the switchback road. But the light at the bottom of the valley's throat stood straight as a held breath, and Jonah understood, with a tenderness that nearly buckled her knees, that someone had sheltered it with cupped hands for hours rather than let it gutter before she came.

She had imagined this moment a hundred times on the road south. In the monastery's ruined scriptorium, copying Cassian's maps by moonlight, she had imagined it as triumph — herself striding through the stones with Ember's fire still singing in her blood, the registry ash on the wind behind her, a heroine in a ballad. Later, on the grain barge, tasting river water for rot and finding none, she had imagined it differently: quieter, wearier, a woman slipping home the way water slips under a door. And in the worst hours, walking the last miles out of dissolved Cael with her boots split and her ribs aching where Voss's riders had nearly taken her at the ford, she had imagined it not at all, because imagining home when you might never reach it was a cruelty she couldn't afford.

Now she was here, and it was none of those things. It was a lamp, and a small figure beside it, and the long walk down.

* * *

The boundary stones of the Veiled Valley were older than the kingdom that had officially never contained them. Old Wenna had taught her their grammar once, crouched in the heather with her gnarled finger tracing the air above a waist-high pillar of grey granite: *the veil isn't a wall, girl, it's a forgetting. Cross inward and the world forgets what you are. Cross outward and you remember everything you were made to forget.* Jonah had crossed outward at dawn all those months ago and felt her gift wake like a limb gone numb — color too bright, taste too loud, the whole world shouting its ingredients at her. She had lived inside that shouting ever since. She had learned to cook with it, to survive with it, to find the safe ford at the river by tasting the poison trail in the current. She had used it to burn three centuries of names.

Now she put one hand on the first stone, the way you'd touch a gatepost of a house you'd been born in, and stepped through.

The quiet came like snowfall.

It didn't hurt. That surprised her most. She had braced for loss, for the phantom-limb scream of a gift amputated, but instead it was gentleness — the world lowering its voice around her, color settling to its old comfortable register, the thousand small tastes of the night air folding themselves away like linens. The iron-and-rain smell of the mountains became simply *smell*, unremarkable, unspoken-to. Somewhere in her chest the thing that had been singing since dawn went soft and wordless, a hearth banked for the night.

She stood there a long moment, both hands on the stone, letting the silence finish falling.

*The valley forgets you if you let it,* her mother had said, and Jonah had spent her whole life believing those words were a warning. Standing now inside the veil's hush, scale warm against her sternum even here — warmer here, impossibly, as if Ember's last ember refused to be forgotten by anything — she thought maybe they had been a promise all along. *You can rest here. You can be only a woman, here. We will hold that for you.*

"You came back."

The voice was small and cracked at the edges, the way voices get when they've been rehearsing a sentence for days. Jonah turned.

The figure by the lamp was a girl. Twelve, perhaps thirteen — hard to tell, because hunger and grief age children unevenly, and this child wore both like hand-me-down clothes. She had a shawl dragged over her head against the damp and bare feet gone blue with cold, and she was looking at Jonah with an expression Jonah knew intimately, because she had worn it herself at nine years old, standing in a kitchen doorway waiting to learn whether she'd be kept or sent away.

Hope, holding very still so it wouldn't startle.

"I know you," the girl said, before Jonah could speak. "You're the taster. You're Thornbury's girl. You used to give me the ends of the honey loaf when Mam took me to market."

Jonah searched her memory and found her: the rag-and-bone man's daughter, the one who'd sat on the churchyard wall summers, quick-eyed, always watching the food stalls with a hunger that wasn't only for bread. Nell. The name arrived with the smell of beeswax and cut grass, a whole summer reassembling itself.

"Nell."

"They said you ran." The girl's chin came up, defiant, though her voice shook. "When the criers came. They said you were gifted and you ran, and then they said the inquisitor's men took Wenna, and then—" She stopped. Started again, smaller. "Nobody comes back. Nobody ever comes back. I've been coming out here every night for six days because I thought—"

She didn't finish. She didn't have to. Jonah crossed the wet grass and knelt and pulled the shivering child against her, shawl and all, and felt the girl go rigid with surprise and then collapse into her arms with a sound that was half sob and half anger, the sound of someone who has been brave too long being told she can stop.

"I came back," Jonah said into the damp wool of the shawl. "I'm sorry I'm late."

* * *

They walked up the valley road together, the girl carrying the lamp, Jonah carrying the strange doubled weight of homecoming — the road known to her feet and yet changed, every cottage window a small gold question. People had died while she was away. She could feel it in the way the village sat in its skin, the way curtains twitched and stilled, the way no one called greetings across fences anymore. Fear had moved in like a relative and hadn't left.

"Tell me," Jonah said quietly. "All of it. Don't spare me."

Nell told it in the flat reciting voice of a child who has organized horror into lists because lists are easier to carry than screams. The Gift Registry Act. The criers. The clerk with his ledger interviewing the kitchen staff, and how Mrs. Hallow the cook had lied about her sister-in-law and been believed, and how the farrier's boy had not lied and been taken. How Wenna the hedge-healer had stood in the square when they came for her — old, tiny Wenna, who smelled of comfrey and woodsmoke — and how she had not run, had not begged, had recited names instead. Names of everyone she'd ever buried. She'd still be saying them when they put her in the cart, Nell said, and she'd been saying them, the guards' faces going strange, one of them taking off his hat without seeming to know why.

"She got sick in the winter after," Nell said. "In the holding house. Fever. They wrote to the village asking if anyone would claim the body, and nobody would, because claiming her meant writing your name next to hers in their book." Her jaw tightened. "So I did. I'm nobody. I signed *nobody*. They let me have her."

Jonah stopped walking.

The lamp swung. Somewhere a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

"You buried her," Jonah said.

"In the churchyard. By your mam." Nell looked up, and in the lamplight her eyes were fierce and wet. "I knowed you'd come back someday. I don't know how I knowed. I just did. And I thought — somebody should be there when you find out. Not a stranger. Somebody who kept the grave clean."

Jonah could not speak. She put her hand on the girl's shoulder, and the girl leaned into it, and they walked on, and the valley held them both in its forgetting, and for once Jonah was grateful to be forgotten — grateful that her face in the lamplight was only a tired woman's face, and nothing the crown's book could name.

* * *

Her mother's grave sat at the edge of the churchyard where the yew threw its longest shadow, and beside it, unmistakable even in the dark, a second mound — newer, tended, planted with rosemary that had somehow already taken root. Nell must have begged cuttings from half the village. Rosemary for remembrance; even the rag-and-bone man's daughter knew the old grammar.

Jonah knelt between the two graves and did not pray. Prayer had never been part of her inheritance. Instead she did what her hands knew: she worked. She cleared the winter's leaf-fall from her mother's stone with her sleeve, letter by letter, until the old carving stood sharp in the lamplight. *MAREN THORNBURY. SHE KEPT THEM FED.* Four words her mother had chosen herself, laughing, saying a taster's widow should have a taster's epitaph and no poetry about it.

Then she dug.

The ground gave reluctantly, frost-stiffened, and Nell held the lamp low and steady without being asked, and Jonah dug with her bare hands because some things should not be done with tools. When the hole was deep enough she drew out the cord at her neck, and Ember's last scale came free into the lamplight — broad as her palm, dark as cooled iron, veined with a faint red that moved when she breathed on it, the way coals move. All the way across dissolved Cael it had ridden against her heart, warm, always warm, the dragon's final gift and final question both: *what will you carry forward?*

She had answered him with fire, in the vault beneath the capital, and the answer had cost him everything, and it had bought three hundred years of names their deaths undone. This scale was what remained. Not power. Not magic — the veil would have smothered any such thing the moment she crossed the stones, and indeed the scale's warmth here was a warmth like a banked hearth, patient, needing nothing. What remained was simpler and heavier.

A promise. Kept things keep other things. That was the whole of the craft her mother and Wenna and a dying dragon had taught her, each in their own tongue.

"Bury it deep," she said, mostly to herself. "It should rest where the forgetting is kind."

She laid the scale in the earth between the two graves — her mother's and Wenna's, the woman who had dosed her daughter with tolerance and silence, and the woman who had handed her an iron key and a warning and love disguised as urgency — and covered it, and pressed the soil flat with both palms, and stayed kneeling a long time while the lamp hissed softly and the yew dripped.

Then she took out the knife she'd carried since Harrowmere, since the child's shoe in the ash, and knelt again at Wenna's stone.

There was no stone, actually. There was a marker Nell had carved herself, *WENNA*, the letters wobbling with effort, and below it a small scratched image that might have been a sprig of comfrey. Jonah set the knife-tip to the granite and began, stroke by stroke, in the deep slow letters stonecutters use, to add the rest.

THORNBURY was wrong — Wenna had earned her own name entire, whatever it had been, and Jonah didn't know it, and grieved that too, another debt to carry. So she cut what she did know, what Wenna would have wanted, what the old woman had shouted into the faces of armed men until the cart doors closed:

SHE REMEMBERED EVERY NAME.

The work took hours. Her fingers bled. Nell watched without moving, and somewhere in the middle of it began, very quietly, to recite — the names, Wenna's names, the ones the girl had memorized from hearing the story told, keeping them alive the way you keep a coal alive, cupped and fed. Jonah cut letters and listened, and the two acts braided together, chisel and litany, mourning folded into belonging, until the last name was spoken and the last letter cut and the sky behind the eastern hills had gone the grey of a held breath before dawn.

* * *

They walked back through the waking village as the first chimneys began to smoke. At the kitchen door of the old taster's cottage — her cottage, still hers, the crown having larger concerns than a runaway's rent — Nell stopped, shifted the lamp to her other hand, and looked at Jonah with that same still hope from the boundary stone, refined now by a night of graves into something steadier.

"I can't pay you anything," the girl said, in the tone of someone opening negotiations she'd already won. "But I can carry water, and I mind directions proper, and I—" she hesitated, and then, with the recklessness of the very young, played her card: "And I tasted the well water in October. Before anybody else. I told them it had gone bitter, sheep-dip bitter, and they laughed at me, and then the farrier's whole family sickened and mine didn't, because we'd drunk from the rain barrel instead. Nobody knows why I knew. I never told anybody why I knew."

The morning was very quiet.

Jonah looked at this child — barefoot, shawled, orphaned twice over by prudence, standing in her doorway offering the one secret that could hang them both — and felt something turn over in her chest that was not quite grief and not quite joy but was the thing that lives underneath both, the bedrock thing, the thing that survives.

"The kettle's on the hook," Jonah said, stepping aside. "Come in out of the cold. And Nell —"

The girl paused on the threshold, lamplight and dawnlight meeting on her face.

"— the first lesson is which names to remember. Yours just became one of mine."

Behind them, in the churchyard, the sun came over the hills and found the new-cut letters, and the rosemary stirred, and the valley — which forgets, which forgives, which keeps — drew its veil gently over them all.
