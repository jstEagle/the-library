## 39. The New Taster

The girl arrived in the third week of harvest, when the valley smelled of pressed apples and woodsmoke, and she arrived the way all important things arrive in the Veiled Valley: quietly, without announcement, carried on a cart of turnips.

Jonah saw her first from the kitchen window of the steward's house — her house now, though she had never quite stopped thinking of it as Aldric's. The girl sat on the tailboard of the cart with her ankles crossed and her hands folded in her lap like a lady at a funeral, watching the kitchen door with an attention that had nothing casual in it. She was perhaps eleven. Thin as a winter branch. Her dress had been mended so many times it was more patch than cloth, and her hair was the color of wet slate, cut short and unevenly, the way hair gets cut when no one loves you enough to be gentle about it.

She should have looked away from the door. That was the first thing. Children who arrive on turnip carts look at their feet, or at the sky, or anywhere but at the thing they want most, because wanting is dangerous when you have nothing to trade for it. This girl looked at the kitchen door the way a starving man looks through a bakery window — openly, hungrily, with no idea how to hide it.

Jonah dried her hands and went out into the yard.

"You'll be Sef," she said. "From the foundling house at Millbrook."

"Yes, ma'am." The girl slid off the cart and stood very straight, which Jonah recognized as the posture of someone who had learned that slouching invited correction, and correction came with hands.

"Who told you about the post?"

"The matron said there was a taster's household that wanted a girl with a good nose. She said—" Sef stopped. Recalculated. "She said you were particular."

"I am particular." Jonah studied her. "What did you eat this morning?"

"Barley porridge. There was ash in it from the fire, a little. And the milk was going off — not bad yet, but tonight it would be."

Jonah felt the old cold move through her, familiar as her own pulse. *There it is.* "You could taste that? The milk?"

"It tasted like a cellar smells," Sef said, and then, catching something in Jonah's face, added quickly: "Ma'am. If that's wrong—"

"It's not wrong." Jonah turned toward the kitchen. "Come in. Wash your hands. We'll start with bread."

* * *

The training took a year before Jonah trusted it, and three years before she trusted herself to trust it.

She began the way Maren had begun with her, thirty years and one lifetime ago: with simple things, with salt and sweet, with the difference between honey from clover and honey from heather. Sef's tongue was good. Better than good. By midwinter she could distinguish between two wells in the same village by their mineral trace, and by spring she could take a spoonful of stew and name not only the herbs but whether the cook had been angry when she'd made it — because anger makes a hand heavy with pepper, and grief makes it forget the salt entirely.

But a tongue was only half the work. The other half was the part Maren had never taught, because Maren had never needed to. Maren had trained a taster for a king's table in a kingdom that still existed. Jonah was training a survivor for a country that officially did not, in a valley whose protection was conditional, under a crown that kept its lists even when its lists burned — because lists, Jonah had learned, are like weeds. Burn the field and the roots go down deeper.

So on the long evenings, while the kettle sang and the fire settled, Jonah taught her the other curriculum.

"Lesson one," she said, the first night Sef slept under her roof. "When someone asks how you knew, you didn't. You guessed. You're lucky. You're a silly girl with a silly hunch. People forgive luck. They don't forgive knowledge they can't explain."

"What if they don't believe me?"

"Then you were wrong. You taste nothing. You never tasted anything. You apologize for wasting their time." Jonah held her gaze across the table. "Say it back."

"I taste nothing. I'm sorry for wasting your time."

"Good. Lesson two." She pushed the wine jug across the table. "Never drink what you're served in public. Swirl it. Touch your lips to it if you must. Spill it if you can do it gracefully. A dead taster is a failed taster, but a drunk taster is a careless one, and carelessness is the story people believe."

Sef looked at the jug and then at Jonah, and Jonah watched her understand arrive — not the rule, but the reason underneath it. *This child has already been poisoned,* she thought. *Or nearly. Or she's seen it done.* Some things the tongue learns before the mind does.

"Lesson three," Jonah said, softer. "Which names do you remember?"

"Ma'am?"

"There are names you keep and names you give up. You give up mine if they ask — Jonah Thornbury, taster, born in this valley, dull as ditchwater, never tasted anything strange in her life. You give up your own. But you keep the others." She counted them on her fingers, an old habit, a rosary of the lost. "Wenna, who was a healer, and who was taken in the year of the Great Purge, and who never gave anyone up. Ember-that-Remains, who was the last dragon, and who spent his last flame so that no more names would go onto lists. Odile, who sold a lie to buy a life, and whose lie saved mine. Brother Cassian, who built the machine his order spent nine hundred years regretting. And Serane Voss, who hunted me, and who chose, at the end, to look away — and whose choice cost her everything she'd built, and who made it anyway."

Sef listened with her whole body, the way children listen when they sense a story is also a test.

"Why those names?" she asked finally.

"Because they're proof," Jonah said. "That the machine doesn't always win. That people inside it can still choose. When you're frightened — and you will be frightened, Sef, that's not a failure, that's just weather — you say the names to yourself, in order, and you remember that every one of them was afraid too, and every one of them did the next right thing anyway. Fear shrinks when it has company."

* * *

In the fourth summer, the girl lied to save a life, and Jonah knew the inheritance had taken root.

It happened at the wedding feast of the miller's daughter, a great spread under striped awnings in the market square where thirty years earlier a crier had read the Gift Registry Act and the whole crowd had gone silent together, one breath held by a hundred lungs. Jonah was old now, and mostly ceremonial — the valley kept her on the way you keep a boundary stone, out of respect and habit — and it was Sef who moved along the trestle tables with the tasting spoon, quick and light, doing the real work.

Jonah saw it happen from twenty feet away. A merchant from outside the valley — a wool factor with rings on every finger and the flat, assessing eyes of a man who counted everything — watched Sef taste his gift of spiced wine, and watched her pause. It was nothing. A hesitation, half a heartbeat, the kind of pause that means nothing to anyone except another taster. But the factor saw it, and he smiled, and he said something to her — Jonah couldn't hear the words, but she knew their shape. *What's wrong, little girl? Is there something in my wine?*

And Sef laughed. Threw back her head and laughed, bright and stupid and eleven-going-on-forty, and said loudly enough for the whole table to hear: "Oh, sir, it's so sweet I thought my tongue had died! My mistress says I've no refinement at all!" And she drank a full swallow, smacked her lips, and moved on — leaving the cup behind, untouched after that first performative mouthful, exactly as taught.

Later, in the kitchen, Jonah made her spit into the rag and rinsed her mouth with well water, three times, while Sef rolled her eyes.

"There wasn't anything in it," the girl said, around the water. "I checked twice. Bitter, but it's the bark they use for color. I only paused because the man was watching me too closely and I wanted him to think he'd caught me being clever."

Jonah set down the cup and looked at her for a long moment.

"That," she said, "was better than anything I could have taught you. He wanted to catch a taster being careful. So you showed him a taster showing off. No one suspects a child who wants credit." She shook her head slowly. "Maren would have boxed your ears. Then she'd have done exactly what you did."

"Who's Maren?"

"My teacher." Jonah turned back to the sink, to the dishes, to the small honest labor of clean cups. "Dead these forty years. I still make her rules every day. One day you'll make mine, and you won't remember making them, and that's how you'll know they took."

* * *

But the valley forgets you if you let it — her mother's words, older than any lesson — and Jonah had spent a lifetime learning that forgetting is a choice you make daily, like bread, like beds, like forgiveness. So once a season, without ceremony, she took Sef up past the mill road to the churchyard on the hill, where the grass grew thick over the old graves, and they tended three stones.

Her mother's stone, worn smooth, name half-erased by weather and by time's indifference. Beside it, a smaller marker Jonah had commissioned from the stonemason with money she never explained: *Wenna Thornbury, healer* — no relation, but the valley had assumed one and Jonah had let them; some lies are load-bearing. And beneath Wenna's stone, buried where no registry would ever think to look, wrapped in oilcloth against the damp: a dragon's scale, dark as a coal gone cold, big as a serving platter, warm to the touch on certain nights for reasons Jonah had stopped trying to explain and simply accepted, the way you accept that the tide comes in.

"Tell me again," Sef would say, kneeling in the grass, pulling weeds from around the stones. She never tired of it. She was a child built of questions, and Jonah had decided long ago that her job was not to answer them all but to teach the girl which ones were safe to ask aloud.

So Jonah told it again. The valley and the veil. The hunt and the flight. The door in the mountain that stayed shut because some doors are answers to questions you stop asking. The vault full of names, and the fire, and the old dragon's roar cutting off mid-note like a bell struck one final time. She told it plainly, without drama, the way you tell a child about a fever she had as an infant — this happened, it was terrible, you survived it, here is the scar, here is what it means.

"And the crown?" Sef asked, the way she always asked. "Does it still keep lists?"

"The crown keeps whatever the crown keeps." Jonah snapped a weed off at the root. "But here's what matters, and mark it well: somewhere, right now, some clerk is writing down names, and some of those names belong to people like us. That will always be true. Machines like that don't die; they sleep, and they wake hungry. Your job — our job — is to be so good at being forgotten that when it wakes, we're already gone."

"Is that living?" Sef asked. "Being forgotten?"

Jonah considered the question honestly, because the girl deserved honesty and because she had asked herself the same thing for forty years, usually at night, usually with a cup of wine she hadn't tasted.

"It's surviving," she said at last. "Living is this — the apples, the weddings, the girl who laughs at wool factors. Surviving is what makes the living possible. Don't confuse them, and don't sacrifice either one for the other."

* * *

On the last evening of that summer, Jonah found Sef in the kitchen after dark, standing at the counter where the morning's bread was set to prove, tasting the air with her lips slightly parted — the unconscious gesture, Jonah's own gesture, mirrored back across thirty years.

"What is it?" Jonah asked.

"The flour." Sef frowned. "It's fine. Everything's fine. I just—" She stopped, embarrassed. "Sometimes I taste things that aren't there. Not in food. In the air. Before rain, before someone's angry, before—" She trailed off, then finished in a rush: "Before things happen. Matron at the foundling house said it was imagination. Was she right?"

Jonah was quiet for a long moment. Outside, the valley lay blue and peaceful in the dusk, smoke rising straight from a hundred chimneys, the boundary stones invisible beyond the hills but present as ever, holding back a world that had never stopped wanting what the valley kept safe inside itself.

"No," she said. "She wasn't right."

"Then what is it?"

And Jonah, who had spent her whole life teaching one lie after another — *you taste nothing, you guessed, you're lucky* — heard herself stand at a crossroads older than the crown, older than the veil, older than the registry and its ashes. Every instinct of survival said: *dull it down, laugh it off, teach the girl to shrink her gift until it fits inside a spoon.* That was the gentled cycle. That was the inheritance as she had received it, passed hand to hand like Wenna's iron key: fear, folded into love, handed down with instructions to hide.

But the girl was looking at her with those hungry, unhidden eyes, and Jonah remembered a woman named Wenna pressing a cold iron key into her palm and saying *run*, and remembered choosing, at the last, not to run but to burn — and understood, finally, what she had actually inherited. Not silence. Responsibility.

"It's the beginning of knowing," Jonah said. "The same knowing that lives in your tongue, but wider. Most people never feel it. Those who do learn to hide it, or die of it, or—" she smiled, tired and true, "—or grow old in a valley that keeps them safe, teaching children to taste bread."

"Which one will I be?"

"That depends," Jonah said, "on lessons neither of us has written yet." She nodded at the bread. "Cover that with the cloth. And Sef — tomorrow we begin the real lessons. Not the tongue. The rest of it. What your grandmother's generation called the gift, and what the crown called a crime, and what I call—" she searched, and found, and offered it like bread broken and shared, "—what I call remembering."

Sef covered the dough with careful hands. And somewhere far beyond the boundary stones, beyond the sleeping crown and its patient clerks, under ash on a mountain no map remembered, a sealed door waited in the dark — and in a kitchen that smelled of proving bread, a new taster slept soundly, dreaming in tastes she had no names for yet, while the old one sat up late by the banked fire, keeping watch over the inheritance, and listening, always listening, to the quiet.
