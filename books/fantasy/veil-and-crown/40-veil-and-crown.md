## 40. Veil and Crown

The Veiled Valley did not remember the war. That was its nature, and its mercy, and — if you asked certain people who had spent their lives studying such things, though no one in the valley ever asked — its cruelty.

It remembered rain. It remembered the particular green of the orchard slopes in late spring, when the pear blossoms came down like weather and the bees went drunk among them. It remembered the taste of its own water, which was limestone and cold iron and nothing else, because that was all it would permit itself to hold. But of the Dragon War, of the kingdom of Cael that had been dissolved like sugar in dark water, of the registry that had burned in a single night above the capital — of all this, the valley remembered nothing at all.

The boundary stones stood where they had always stood, twelve of them, mossed to the shoulders, each one wearing its veil like a hood pulled low. Travelers passing on the high road saw only a pleasant valley with good farms and a market square where the bread was honest and the ale was cheap. They did not see the way the light bent gently inward at the edges, like a hand cupped around a candleflame. They did not feel the small soft pressure against their thoughts as they crossed the stones, the sensation — so faint most never named it — of being asked to forget something they had not yet been told.

The valley kept its secret the way an old woman keeps a key sewn into a hem: not out of fear, exactly, but out of habit, and because some things are safer carried close than displayed.

And the crown kept its fiction. In the capital, the official account was read aloud every year on the anniversary of the Great Purge-that-was-not: a vault accident, a tragic fire, the loss of irreplaceable records, the heroic efforts of the palace guard to contain the blaze. Serane Voss's name appeared nowhere in it, which was precisely how she had written it. She had retired to a provincial posting by then, and then from that to a house with a garden, and then, eventually, to the ground beneath the garden, having spent her last decades doing something no historian ever quite managed to document: quietly losing files. Misfiling them. Allowing clerks with bad memories to transcribe worse records. The machinery of the hunt ground slower and slower through her careful hands until, one winter, it simply stopped, and no one could say why, and no one thought to ask.

Fictions are like that. They need only one keeper at a time.

* * *

In the valley, on a morning in late spring, Jonah Thornbury woke before dawn, as she had for seventy years, and lay listening to her own heart.

It was a good heart. Slower now, deliberate, like a cook who has stopped hurrying because she knows exactly how long everything takes. She lay under wool blankets in the little stone house at the edge of the orchards — the same house, though the roof had been re-thatched twice and the door rebuilt once after a storm — and she listened to the valley waking around her: the first blackbird, the creak of Wenna's old well rope two fields over, though Wenna herself had been forty years in the churchyard and the new family drew the water without knowing whose hands had worn the wood smooth.

Her gift had gone quiet the day she crossed back over the stones, all those years ago. Quiet, but not gone. It sat in her like a banked fire sits in a hearth — not warmth you could use, but warmth you knew was there. In the valley, magic could not follow, and so the gift slept, and Jonah had learned, over a long life, to be grateful for the sleep. She had spent enough years awake inside it to know what it cost.

She rose. She dressed. She went out into the blue hour before sunrise, because the morning air was the one thing she still tasted with her whole attention, every day, as a kind of prayer.

This is what she tasted: rain coming, two days off, moving down from the Ashreach passes. Woodsmoke from the baker's chimney, and under it the sour-sweet note of yesterday's dough gone slightly too long. The green breath of the orchards. And beneath everything, faint as a watermark, the valley itself — that flavor she had never been able to describe to anyone, not even the girl, not even at the end when words mattered less. Clean. Closed. Like a room swept and shut.

Somewhere beyond the eastern stones, under ash and mountain, a door waited that would never open. She had gone back once, thirty years ago, walking the whole way on legs that complained, and stood before the sealed gate with Ember's scale in her pocket, and found that she felt nothing but a kind of polite curiosity, the way one feels before a locked house where one used to live. Some doors are answers to questions you stop asking. She had stopped asking. The question had been: *is there anywhere I can go where I am safe?* And the answer had turned out to be: yes, here, if you are willing to be the one who keeps it safe rather than the one who is kept.

She smiled at nothing — at the dark orchard, at the invisible mountains, at the long dead dragon whose name she said to herself each morning the way other people said their prayers — and went in to start the fire.

* * *

The girl arrived at midmorning, as she did every day, letting herself in with the ease of someone who had long since stopped being a guest.

Sera was eleven. Orphaned twice over, first by fever and then by an aunt who drank, she had come to Jonah's kitchen three years ago chasing the smell of bread and had stayed because of a mistake: she had tasted the stew, made a face, and said, "There's something bitter under it. Under the sweet part." There had been. Foxglove root, a spoonful, dropped in by an aunt who had decided an orphan's inheritance was worth more than an orphan. Jonah had tasted it too, of course, had known before the girl opened her mouth — but she had waited, and let Sera say it first, and watched the child realize what she had just done, and what it meant, and what it might cost her.

Then Jonah had fed her, and taught her the first lesson: *never say how you know.*

Now the girl moved through the kitchen like a small competent ghost, banking the fire, setting the kettle, chattering about the market and the miller's boy and the new foal at the Hendersons' farm, while Jonah sat in her chair by the window and supervised with the economy of motion that great age grants and demands.

"Master Corren wants his pears today," Sera said. "Honeyed."

"Mm."

"And I checked them already. Twice." A pause. The particular pause Jonah had taught her to hear in herself. "They're fine. But I want you to try them anyway."

Jonah held out her hand. The girl put a slice of pear in it, honey-glossed, and Jonah set it on her tongue and closed her eyes and gave it the full ceremony of her attention, though her tongue had dulled these past years and both of them knew it.

Almond. Faint, bitter, threaded through the honey like a hair in silk.

She opened her eyes. Sera was watching her, hands folded, chin up, doing a very good job of looking calm and a very poor job of achieving it.

"Well?" the girl said.

"It's fine," Jonah said. "Eat your breakfast."

Sera exhaled, laughed, turned away to the kettle — and Jonah slipped the rest of the poisoned pear into her sleeve, smooth as sixty years of practice, and looked out the window at the orchards, and felt the old familiar cold move through her chest and settle, an old acquaintance now, almost comfortable.

Someone in the valley wanted Master Corren dead. Someone always did; that was human nature, and the valley suppressed magic, not malice. She would find out who, in her own time, in her own way. She would decide what to do about it — warn, expose, or simply stand between, as she had done perhaps a dozen times across the decades, invisibly, leaving behind her a trail of stewards and merchants and one young wife who lived to a ripe old age and never learned how close she'd come to dying or how strange her salvation had been.

That was the inheritance. Not power. Not escape. Just this: a tongue that knew, and a silence that protected, and the daily, unglamorous work of standing between.

"You're smiling again," Sera said suspiciously, without turning around. "At nothing."

"I'm smiling at everything," Jonah said. "You'll learn the difference."

* * *

That afternoon they walked up through the orchards to the churchyard, as they did on the first warm day of every spring.

The graves sat under a pear tree older than either of them. Jonah's mother's stone, worn nearly smooth now, bore a name and nothing else — the valley forgetting, as her mother had promised it would, gently, completely. Beside it, a newer stone: **WENNA THORNBURY**, added by Jonah's own chisel in letters that were beginning to soften at the edges. And below Wenna's name, flush with the earth, half-swallowed by grass, a flat grey shape that visitors took for a cobblestone and that Sera, alone in all the world, knew for what it was: a dragon's scale, buried beside the dead as one buries a key beside a lock, or a promise beside the person to whom it was made.

*For the door I did not open,* Jonah had thought, laying it there. *For the life I did not live. For him, who gave what he could not spare, for people he never met, in a country that no longer exists.*

Ember-that-Remains. Somewhere in the high Ashreach, his bones had become part of the mountain, and the mountain did not remember him either, and Jonah had decided long ago that this was not sadness. Mountains keep things differently than we do. Not less truly.

Sera knelt and pulled weeds from around the stones without being asked. She had never been told the full story — not yet. Jonah was waiting, as her own mentor had waited, for the moment when the girl would need the story more than the safety of not knowing it. Perhaps next year. Perhaps the year after. There was no hurry. That was the whole point of the valley.

"Tell me about her again," Sera said, nodding at Wenna's stone. "The hedge-healer. Your teacher."

"She wasn't my teacher," Jonah said, settling onto the bench they had dragged up here years ago. "She was my alarm bell. There's a difference. A teacher tells you what to think. An alarm bell just makes sure you're awake when it matters." She watched the blossoms come down through the slanting gold light. "She gave me a key once. Iron, cold, no lock to fit. Took me twenty-five years to find out what it opened, and by then I'd already opened it another way."

"What did it open?"

"A list," Jonah said. "A very long list of names. Every person like us, going back five hundred years. Written down carefully by careful men so that other men could find them later." She paused. "We burned it."

Sera was quiet for a moment. Then, in the practical tone she used for kitchen arithmetic: "All of it?"

"All of it."

"Good," said the girl, and went back to the weeds, and Jonah laughed — a real laugh, startling a pigeon off the churchyard wall — because there it was, the whole philosophy, delivered by an eleven-year-old in one word over a grave. *Good.* Justice did not require speeches. Sometimes it required only a match and the willingness to strike it.

* * *

Evening came, as it always came, gently, the valley drawing its veil closed around the day.

Jonah sat in her doorway with the last of the light, and Sera walked home along the orchard path, and the first stars came out over the Ashreach, over the ash, over the unopened door, over the capital where a fiction was kept alive by no one at all now, kept alive the way old fires are kept alive — by everyone agreeing not to look too closely at the cold.

The crown believed it had won. The crown believed the gifted were gone, hunted out, burned out, faded into rumor. Let it believe. Rumor was a better shelter than any valley. Somewhere beyond the stones, here and there across the dissolved kingdom, a cook paused over a pot and frowned at a bitterness no one else could taste; a midwife knew a fever before the fever knew itself; a child flinched from a flavor and lied about why. The lists were ash. The names were free. The gift went underground and put down roots, patient as anything planted in the dark.

And the valley kept its secret. It would keep it tomorrow, and the year after, and long after Jonah Thornbury was a smooth place on a stone under a pear tree. It would keep it for the girl, and for whoever the girl taught, and for whoever came after her — each generation handed the same quiet cargo: *know, and don't say; protect, and don't boast; remember, and let the valley forget you if you let it.*

Jonah tasted the night air one last time. Rain, two days off. Woodsmoke. Blossoms. The clean closed flavor of home.

She smiled at nothing, which was to say at everything — at the dead dragon and the living girl, at the burned list and the unburned names, at the door under the ash and the keys sewn into hems everywhere — and went inside, and barred the door, and slept without dreaming, which in her long experience was the surest sign of a life rightly kept.

* * *

Far to the east, under the mountain, the door waited.

It had waited through the war and the dissolution, through the purges and the burning, through the rise and fall of fictions. It would wait through this too, and through whatever came after. It was patient, as doors are. And if, someday, some frightened soul came climbing through the Ashreach with fire in their hands and a question in their heart — *is there anywhere I can go?* — the door would be there, ready with its answer.

But that is another story. This one ends where it began: in a valley that forgets, kept safe by those it cannot remember, where a woman who survived everything by tasting everything grew old in peace, and taught one girl to do the same, and smiled each morning at the air —

— which tasted, always, faintly, wonderfully, of nothing at all.
