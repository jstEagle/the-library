## 1. The Taster's Oath

There are two ways to die at court, and I have spent twelve years perfecting the art of avoiding both. The first is poison, which is my profession and, if the gods are unkind, my eventual epitaph. The second is being noticed, which is worse, because poison at least has the decency to be quick.

So when I say that the morning of the autumn tasting began like any other, understand what a triumph of craft that sentence represents. Routine is armor. I have worn it so long I sometimes forget there's a man underneath.

I rose before the bells, as always. I ate my own breakfast — plain bread, hard cheese, water from my private well, which I had dug myself and guarded jealously — and I reviewed the day's menu in my head while dressing. The king's table for the Feast of First Frost: quail in elderberry glaze, venison with a sauce of black garlic, the honeyed pears he favored, and wine from the southern vineyards that cost more per bottle than my father earned in a decade of honest cobbling. Not that my father was honest, or a cobbler. But that's another story, and one I charge extra to tell.

The kitchens below the Sunward Palace were already roaring when I descended. Cooks moved like soldiers, all shouted orders and flashing knives, and I walked among them the way I always did: pleasant, forgettable, everywhere. A taster who draws attention is a taster who gets poisoned out of spite, or bribed out of greed, or both. I had made myself the most boring man in three kingdoms. It was the finest performance of my life.

"Master Fairweather." Head Cook Brammel, red-faced and sweating despite the frost on the windows. "The quail came in late from the eastern farms. You'll want to look at them."

"I'll want to taste everything," I said, "as usual."

"Of course, of course." He wrung his apron. There was something in his face I didn't like — not guilt exactly, but the particular anxiety of a man who suspects his kitchen has been used without his permission.

I tasted everything, as usual. The quail first, since it had arrived late and late deliveries were how clever assassins liked to work. Nothing. The elderberry glaze, spooned from the reduction pot before it touched meat. Nothing but sugar and a faint bitterness of fruit skin. The venison, the sauce, the bread, the butter, the salt from three different cellars. I worked through them methodically, cataloguing flavors the way a banker counts coins, and found nothing worth dying over.

The wine was last, because wine deserved patience. I poured a measure from the first bottle, held it to the light — clear, deep garnet, no sediment where sediment shouldn't be — swirled it, smelled it, and finally drank.

It tasted fine.

That should have been the end of it. That is what I told myself for the next hour, standing in the corner of the great hall with a silver spoon in my pocket and my hands folded behind my back, watching the king eat quail that would not kill him because I had already died of it in miniature. Everything proceeded as it always proceeded. The courtiers glittered. The musicians sawed away at something festive. His Majesty laughed at a joke from the Ambassador of Vell and ate his honeyed pears with the particular relish of a man who has never once wondered whether his food might betray him.

And then Physician Corvane happened to me.

He materialized at my elbow the way physicians do, smelling of camphor and discretion. Corvane was seventy if he was a day, dry as old parchment, with eyes that had seen every disease the flesh could invent and been impressed by none of them. We had exchanged perhaps four hundred words in twelve years, most of them about bowel habits, none of them by choice.

"Master Fairweather."

"Physician."

"You look well." He said it the way a coroner says it.

"I sleep enough," I lied pleasantly.

"Mm." He produced a small glass vial from his sleeve, the kind used for collecting samples, and held it up between two fingers as though it were a curiosity at a market stall. "A formality. The office of the Taster requires periodic examination of the blood. Old statute. From the reign of Aldous the Second, I believe."

"There's no such statute," I said.

"No," Corvane agreed mildly. "There isn't."

I looked at him. He looked back with the patience of a man who had all day and several decades of practice waiting for bodies to confess things. Around us the feast went on, oblivious, the king laughing his big warm laugh, and I felt the first hairline crack run through twelve years of careful masonry.

"What is this really?" I asked quietly.

"A test," he said simply. "Yours, this time. Not his Majesty's supper." He nodded toward the high table. "The elderberry glaze. Tell me what you tasted."

"Sugar. Fruit. A bitterness at the finish."

"Describe the bitterness."

I closed my eyes. Twelve years of training do not switch off, even when you wish they would. "Bittercress. And under it — wolfsbane root. Very faint. Steeped long, low heat, so the alkaloids barely register." My stomach turned over, slow and cold. "A dose that size should have stopped my heart between the third spoonful and the fifth."

"Yes," said Corvane. "It should have."

* * *

We spoke in his laboratory afterward, a room of glass and bone and dried herbs hanging from the rafters like executed prisoners. He drew my blood with the tenderness of a man milking a viper, sealed it in the vial, and held it to the window light.

"Do you know what immunity actually is, Master Fairweather? Most people think it's armor. It isn't. It's a debt. Every dose you survived taught your body a lesson written in suffering, and your blood remembers every lesson." He set the vial down. "Your blood remembers wolfsbane now. It shouldn't. Wolfsbane wasn't in your training regimen — I checked the records. Your predecessor's regimen included it; yours was amended before you began. Someone removed it from your education and then served it to you today, to see whether you'd learned it anyway."

"Who?"

"If I knew, I'd be dead already, or rich." He capped the vial. "Someone wanted to know what your body can survive. Not to kill you, Master Fairweather. To *measure* you."

"Why does anyone need to measure me?"

Corvane looked at me then with an expression I had never seen on him — something almost like pity, which from Corvane was more frightening than any poison.

"Because you are the only man in this kingdom whose blood has spent twelve years learning to hold death without dying," he said. "If I were a certain sort of person, I'd call that an ingredient."

The word sat in the air between us like a dropped knife.

"Go home," he said. "Eat nothing you haven't prepared. Sleep with a blade. And Master Fairweather — if a physician you don't know asks for a sample of your blood, refuse him. If someone offers you a raise, refuse it. If the king summons you privately—" he paused, and for once the dry old voice wavered, "—be somewhere else."

"The king doesn't summon tasters privately."

"No," Corvane agreed. "Kings don't."

He turned back to his instruments, and I understood I was dismissed. At the door I paused.

"Physician. Why warn me? You've served this crown longer than I've been alive."

He didn't turn around. "Because whatever is coming for your blood, it isn't coming for the king's sake. And I swore an oath to a king, not to whatever wears him these days."

I told myself, walking home through the frosted streets, that the old man was mad. Senile. Addled by camphor fumes. I told myself the bitterness in the glaze had been bad fruit, that my tongue had invented wolfsbane out of professional boredom, that statutes could exist and simply be badly recorded.

But that night I sat awake in my rooms above the chandler's shop, listening to the palace bells count midnight, and I took inventory the way I take inventory of a suspect dish. Corvane had no reason to lie. The glaze had no reason to carry wolfsbane. And someone in that kitchen — someone with access, with skill, with patience — had cooked a poison into a royal dish calibrated not to kill the king, whom it would never harm, nor to frame the cooks, whom it would destroy, but to test a single swallow taken by a single man.

Me.

Twelve years I had been the safest man at court, because everyone needed me alive. Now, apparently, someone needed me alive for a different reason entirely, and the difference between those two kinds of needing is the difference between a guest and a prisoner.

The bells finished counting midnight. Somewhere below, the chandler's dog began to bark — not at a cat, not at the wind, but at something in the street that stood too still to be innocent.

I blew out my candle, put my hand on the knife under my pillow, and waited to find out which of the two ways to die had finally come calling.

By morning, Physician Corvane would be dead. But I didn't know that yet. All I knew was the crack running through my carefully managed life, widening by the hour, and the taste of wolfsbane still ghosting the back of my throat like a promise someone else had made on my behalf.
