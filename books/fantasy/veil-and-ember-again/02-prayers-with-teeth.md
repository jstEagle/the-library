## 2. Prayers with Teeth

I had been praying to a dead god for eleven years, which is either the definition of faith or the definition of madness, and I have never been able to settle the question to my own satisfaction.

The god was Veyra, the Ember-Mother, and she had died — or gone silent, or wandered off, depending on which heresy you preferred — two hundred years before I was born. The Church of the Nine kept her name in the liturgy out of politeness, the way one keeps a chair at the table for a relative who drowned. *Veyra who watches, Veyra who warms,* we recited, in a tone that made it clear nobody expected watching or warming from anyone ever again.

The other eight gods still answered. Barely. A candleflame of attention once a season, if you were diligent, if you were lucky, if you said the right words in the right order with the right degree of groveling. The Nine had grown quiet the way an old house grows quiet — not empty, exactly, but settled, uninterested in the people rattling around inside it.

So when the Church reassigned me to the shrine at Hollowmere — a squat stone chapel wedged into the foothills of Thornfell, three days' ride from anything worth sinning near — I understood it for what it was. Not a posting. A shelving. I had asked one question too many at the seminary in Corvale, something about whether the Nine's silence might be grief rather than indifference, and the Provost had looked at me the way a man looks at milk he suspects has turned.

Hollowmere's shrine had been shuttered for a generation. The last priest there had died of nothing dramatic — bad lungs, worse winters — and no one had bothered replacing him until someone remembered I existed and decided I could be forgotten more efficiently in the mountains.

I arrived in late autumn with two trunks, a cracked icon of Veyra that the seminary had let me keep because they considered it worthless, and a conviction I hadn't yet admitted to myself: that if I was going to be buried alive, I would at least dig.

* * *

The first month, I cleaned. The second month, I repaired the roof badly. By midwinter I had swept out the sanctuary, rehung the ember-lamp above the altar — dark for decades, its oil long evaporated — and begun, each night, to pray the old offices of Veyra from a rite so outdated the Church had formally retired it before my grandmother was born.

I want to be honest here, because honesty is the one virtue I was never accused of possessing in abundance: I did not expect anything. Praying to Veyra was not hope. It was spite, dressed in vestments. Every night I knelt on cold stone and recited words for a god who wasn't listening, and every night the silence answered, and I took a certain bitter comfort in the symmetry. The Church had shelved me; I would shelve my devotion in return. We could ignore each other professionally.

Then, on the third night of the Frostmoon, the lamp lit itself.

I should describe this properly, because I have told this story many times since and people always want the details, as though the details will make it more believable rather than less. The ember-lamp above the altar was empty. I had checked it that morning, because checking it was part of my routine of futility — dust the altar, straighten the icons, confirm the lamp is dead, feel superior about it. There was no oil. There was no wick. And at the hour of compline, while I stood mid-prayer with my arms raised like a fool, flame bloomed inside the glass, small and gold and utterly indifferent to the absence of anything that burns.

I did not scream. I want credit for that. I stood very still and finished the office in a voice that only shook a little, because some stubborn priestess-shaped part of me insisted that if this was madness, I would at least be mad with decorum.

When I finished, the flame was still burning. And beneath it, faint as breath on winter glass, I felt something I had felt only twice before in my life — both times in the great cathedral at Corvale, both times during rites conducted by men far holier than I — the sensation of being *regarded*. Not watched. Regarded. The way a mother regards a child she thought was asleep.

"Hello," I said, to the empty sanctuary, because apparently decorum only extended so far.

The warmth that moved through the room then was not temperature. It passed through me like sunlight through water, and in its wake I heard — not words, never words, the old gods are too large for words the way the sea is too large for a cup — but a *rightness*, a settling, the feeling of a bone sliding back into its joint after years of dislocation.

Veyra was awake.

And she was not alone.

* * *

Here is what I learned in the weeks that followed, alone in a mountain shrine with a lamp that would not go out no matter what I did to it: the old magic was returning across all of Thornfell, and it was returning like floodwater — finding every low place, every crack, every forgotten channel.

I found the first sign in the village below the shrine. Old Marta Cooper, who had buried three husbands and trusted none of them posthumously, had hung a sprig of rowan over her door — a custom so ancient it predated the Church of the Nine, back when the mountains were guarded by hearth-gods and threshold-spirits instead of properly licensed clergy. Rowan over the door was superstition. Everyone knew that. It did nothing.

Marta's rowan had rooted. Into the lintel. Into stone. Green shoots in deep winter, flowering in defiance of every law of botany and several of theology.

I walked the valley after that and saw what I had trained myself not to see. The well-water tasting of iron and memory. A shepherd boy whose whistled tune called down his scattered flock from a mile off, and who had stopped doing it because it frightened him. Ice on the high tarns forming patterns — spirals, eyes, the old sigils of the Ember-Mother — that melted by noon but returned each dawn, patient as handwriting.

The land was waking up. And it was waking up hungry, or at least attentive, which from a god is nearly the same thing.

I wrote to the diocese. I want that understood: I followed procedure. I composed a careful, measured report — *unusual phenomena consistent with residual theurgy, recommend investigation* — and sealed it with the proper wax and sent it down the mountain with the monthly supply cart, feeling very much the responsible cleric.

The reply came back in six weeks, signed by Bishop Aldous Renk himself, a man I had never met and who nevertheless managed to convey, through ink alone, the expression of someone smelling spoiled fish.

*Priestess Vane. The Nine are eternal and their silence is doctrine. Reports of "returning" power are superstition at best and sedition at worst. You will cease these observations. You will cease this correspondence. Reflect on obedience.*

Reflect on obedience. As though obedience were a pond I could sit beside, skipping stones of doubt into it.

I did not reflect. I wrote again. I sent evidence — pressed rowan leaves, a sketch of the ice-sigils, a sworn statement from Marta Cooper who signed her name with an X and dictated a postscript about the bishop that I prudently did not transcribe. I wrote that if the Church would not look at what was happening in Thornfell, then the Church had chosen blindness, and blindness in a shepherd is not innocence; it is dereliction.

That letter got me branded.

* * *

They came up the mountain in early spring — four riders in diocesan grey, led by a Vicar-General named Ossler with a face like a closed ledger. They did not come to investigate. Investigation implies the possibility of findings. They came to conclude.

The tribunal was held in my own sanctuary, which struck me as either laziness or theater. Ossler sat behind a folding table while his clerks arranged my letters on it like exhibits at a murder trial, which, in fairness, they were becoming.

"Sister Vane," Ossler said. "You reported that the ember-lamp burned without oil."

"It did."

"You reported vegetation growing through masonry in midwinter."

"I did. I brought samples." I set the rowan cutting on the table. It had flowered again overnight, little white blossoms, showing off.

Ossler looked at the flowers the way a man looks at a debt he intends to dispute. "You reported that you received a response to prayer directed at a god the Church has declared departed."

"I reported that I received *a* response," I said. "The identity of the respondent is a matter for investigation, which is why I requested—"

"The Church has ruled, Sister. Veyra is dead. Whatever spoke to you, it was not a god. Gods do not return." He folded his hands. "Therefore whatever you have been communing with in this shrine is something else. Something that wears a dead god's name. And a priestess who communes with unnamed powers—" he let the sentence hang, letting me finish it myself, which was considerate of him.

"—is a heretic," I supplied.

"The term used is *heterodox pending correction*, but yes."

They stripped my orders in the sanctuary where I'd received them back. That was the part that hurt, though I'd have let them flay me before admitting it. Eleven years of study, of vigil, of cold floors and colder silences, and a man with a folding table unmade it in twenty minutes. Ossler read the formula of degradation — I am struck, even now, by how much of our liturgy is administrative — and his clerks packed my letters, my samples, my cracked icon of Veyra into a satchel, taking even the worthless things, especially the worthless things.

At the door, Ossler paused. He was not cruel, I think. Cruelty requires engagement. He was simply a man closing an account.

"Burn the lamp," he said. "Whatever answers it, starve it of the invitation."

They rode south. I stood in my sanctuary holding the formal notice of my excommunication, which was written on very nice paper, and I listened to the wind coming down off Thornfell, and underneath the wind — faint, warm, unmistakable — I heard the ember-lamp still burning behind me.

I had not lit it. Neither had they. It had been burning for five months without oil, without wick, without permission from any bishop alive or dead.

I turned and looked at it, and the regard came again, patient and vast, and this time I understood it was not asking me anything. It was waiting for me to ask.

"They say you're dead," I told the flame. My voice cracked on the second word, which was undignified, and I found I no longer cared. "Two hundred years dead. The Church ruled on it. There was presumably paperwork."

The warmth deepened. Somewhere in the high passes, I felt — knew, the way you know your own heartbeat — something stir that had been sleeping under the mountains since before the kingdom had a name. Something vast turning over in its sleep, opening one eye.

And threaded through that knowing, faint but clear as a bell through snow, came a single impression that was not meant for me — spillover, the way light spills under a door. A hunger. An old, patient, terrible hunger, moving through the world like a root through soil, seeking something specific. Seeking a body. A mortal vessel. Someone carrying death in their veins like wine.

I didn't understand it then. I wish to God I still didn't.

What I understood was this: the god the Church declared dead had woken in the mountains, and the Church had just thrown away its only witness. They had taken my orders, my letters, my name in the rolls of the faithful. They had left me everything that mattered.

I knelt at the altar — not in the posture of the degraded, head bowed, hands open, but the old way, the way the mountain-folk had knelt when the hearth-gods were young: spine straight, chin lifted, a servant addressing an equal who happened to be eternal.

"Wake, then," I said to the flame. "But wake carefully. Because I've seen what the Church does with miracles, and next time, they'll send more than a vicar with a folding table."

The lamp burned on. And far to the east, past the peaks, past the free cities, in a direction I could not yet have named, something answered — and began, patiently, to move.
