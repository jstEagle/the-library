## 3. What Wears the Crown

The summons came at the hour when the palace smells worst.

That's a taster's secret nobody puts in songs: you learn a castle by its stinks. Dawn is bread and lye and the chamber pots going down. Noon is roasting meat and horse sweat from the yard. But the hour before evening bells — that's when the stone itself exhales, all the old damp and older secrets rising up through the floors like breath through teeth. Twelve years in this place and I could navigate the whole of it blindfolded, nose first.

So when a footman I'd never seen before appeared at my door with a summons to a private audience — no escort, no scribe, no reason given — it wasn't the strangeness of the summons that set my teeth on edge. It was that he smelled of nothing. Not soap, not sweat, not the tallow-and-turnip reek of the servants' quarters. Nothing. A man-shaped absence walking the halls of Vael Corrin like he belonged there.

"His Majesty requests your attendance," he said, and his voice had the same quality as his smell. Correct. Practiced. Like a dish prepared by someone who'd read about cooking but never been hungry.

I went, because what else does a taster do? Refusing the king's wine is refusing the king. And besides — I told myself, climbing the east stair with my heart doing an unprofessional little quickstep — maybe the physician's note last week had finally reached someone with sense. Maybe they wanted to ask me about the night I survived a dish of river eel in almond sauce that should have dropped me where I stood. *Should have*, by every calculation I'd made in twelve years of calculating exactly such things. The physician, Master Ollenby, had written something in his ledger while looking at me the way a man looks at a lock he suddenly suspects has been picking *him*.

I'd told myself it was nothing. Tasters survive things. That's rather the point of us.

The private audience chamber sat at the top of the east stair, behind a door of black oak banded in silver that was supposed to be blessed annually and hadn't been in living memory. Two guards flanked it. They didn't look at me. That was normal. Nobody looks at the taster; looking at the taster is admitting there's something to fear in the cup.

Inside, the king stood at the window with his back to me, watching the sun go down over the city the way kings do in paintings — one hand clasped behind him, shoulders squared, the very portrait of sovereign patience.

"Your Majesty." I bowed. "You sent for me."

"I did." He didn't turn. "Wendell Fairweather. My taster."

"Twelve years, Majesty."

"Twelve years." He said it the way a man reads a number off an inventory. "And in all that time, have you ever wondered why the position exists?"

Here's the thing about being a taster: you develop a professional relationship with fear. You invite it to dinner. You let it sit across from you and you watch its hands. So when the fear arrived in that room, I didn't panic — I observed. And what I observed was this: the king still hadn't turned around, and the light from the window was falling wrong. It fell *through* him, just slightly, the way late sun falls through smoke. Or I was tired. Or I was about to be very sorry I'd come.

"The position exists," I said carefully, "because kings are poisoned."

"And you are not poisoned." Now he turned, and smiled, and the smile was perfect. Every tooth in its place, every line drawn exactly where a smile goes. "Interesting word, that. *Poisoned.* Tell me, Wendell — what does hemlock taste like?"

"Bitter. Green. Like the stem of a wild carrot left too long in the ground."

"And wolfsbane?"

"Sweet at first. Then cold. Then nothing, which is the part that kills you."

"Good." He crossed to the table by the fire — moving smoothly, too smoothly, like water finding its level — and lifted a decanter of red wine. "Then you'll know if this is sound."

He poured two cups. That was the first true wrongness, though I didn't understand it yet. Kings don't drink with their tasters. Kings watch their tasters drink and then wait out the count. Pouring two cups was either courtesy or theater, and this king had never once, in twelve years, offered me courtesy.

I took my cup. I tasted. Cherry and black plum, oak, a thread of violet, the faint iron ghost of the cask. Sound. I said so.

"Now the second cup," he said, and drank his own down in a single swallow, throat working, eyes closed like a man savoring.

And that's when I understood what my nose had been trying to tell me since the footman.

A room with a man in it has a smell. Skin and hair and breath and the particular chemistry of a body doing the work of being alive. I'd stood close enough to King Aldric to catch his scent a hundred times — sandalwood oil, the pepper of old brandy, the sour-sweet edge of a man who ate richly and slept badly. It was as much a signature as any seal.

This man smelled of nothing. Not cleanliness — clean men smell of soap, of linen, of the absence built on purpose. This was *nothing*. The wine had a smell. The fire had a smell. The king holding the empty cup did not, and my stomach dropped through twelve years of floorboards as I looked up into his face and understood, finally, why the light fell through him.

There was no pulse-beat behind his eyes.

I don't know how else to say it. You can see a man's life in his eyes — the small flicker, the constant motion, the thing that makes eye contact feel like touching. His eyes were the color of life and utterly without it. Lamps lit in an empty house to make the street believe someone's home.

"You survived the eel," the king said pleasantly. "In almond sauce. Bitter almond, Wendell. Do you know how much cyanide was in that dish? Enough to kill three men. Enough to kill *you*, by every account but the one that matters." He set down his cup. "Someone has been feeding you death for twelve years, and you've grown fat on it. Your blood is a library now. Every poison in the realm, catalogued and conquered. Do you know what that's worth?"

My mouth had gone dry enough to crack. "Majesty, I—"

"*Do you know what that's worth?*" The voice didn't rise. That was the horror of it — the same pleasant register, the same courtly calm, while something behind the face leaned forward like a rider leaning over a fence. "To the right buyer? To the right *god*?"

I am not a brave man. I want that on the record, carved somewhere honest. I am a careful man who learned to swallow terrible things, and everything in me screamed to bow, to agree, to pour more wine and laugh at the strange turn the conversation had taken. Instead I heard myself say:

"Your Majesty. Who is asking?"

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Mine, mine, and — I realized with a cold that started in my spine and spread outward like frost on glass — none of his.

"A loyal servant," he said, "serves the crown."

"And if the crown isn't there to serve?"

The smile again. Perfect. Empty. "Then the servant serves what wears it."

* * *

I don't remember leaving the chamber. I remember the stairs, each one landing in my knees like a debt collected. I remember thinking, with the detached clarity that has saved my life more times than any courage ever has: *count backward from ten, Wendell, and decide whether you're mad.*

At four, I decided I wasn't. At seven, I decided I wished I were. Madness would have been a mercy — a fever, a bad supper, anything but the plain truth standing at a window with the sunset shining through its chest.

By the time I reached the infirmary wing, I'd constructed a plan so simple it barely qualified: find Master Ollenby, show him the ledger entry, ask him — quietly, taster to poisoner — what could wear a king's face and leave no scent. If anyone in Vael Corrin would know, it was the man who spent his days cataloguing ways bodies stop being bodies.

Ollenby's door was unlocked. He never left it unlocked.

He was at his desk, or most of him was. The rest was arranged around the desk in a way desks are not designed to accommodate. His ledger lay open beside him, and the last entry was fresh ink, still shining:

*Subject F. exhibits total resistance. Blood confirmed viable. Extraction to proceed.*

Below that, a single line in a different hand — smaller, neater, patient as a spider:

*Not yet. Let him ripen.*

I stood in that room for a long time. Long enough for the candles to gutter, long enough for the guards' boots to pass twice in the corridor outside. I thought about the oath I'd sworn at nineteen, kneeling on cold stone with a cup in my hands: *my body between the crown and harm.* I'd meant it. Gods help me, some ruined corner of me still meant it.

But the crown, I now understood, was a cup too. And something had been drinking from it for who knows how long, and it had developed a taste, and I was the next course.

I took the ledger page. I took the small knife Ollenby kept for cutting specimen roots. I took one look back at the old man who had tested me and noted me and died for the trouble, and I said the only prayer I knew — the taster's grace, the one we mutter over every meal:

"If it's poison, may I know it. If it's death, may I choose it."

Then I walked out of the infirmary, past the guards who never looked at me, down the stairs that exhaled their old secrets, and out toward the east gate where the mountains waited — because I knew every smuggler's path through Thornfell better than the men who'd cut them, and I intended to be very, very difficult to catch.

Behind me, somewhere in the dark above, a bell began to ring.

It wasn't the evening bells. It was the alarm, and it was ringing for me.
