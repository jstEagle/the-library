## 4. The Smuggler's Road

I left the palace through the kitchen, because that is where all honest exits begin.

Cook Marta didn't ask questions when I took the flour sack off the hook and put my knife in its place. Twelve years of tasting her food had earned me that much silence. She only pressed a heel of bread and a wedge of hard cheese into my hands and said, "You were never here, Wendell Fairweather," which is the kindest lie anyone has ever told me.

The physician's body was still warm when they found him. I knew it would be. That was the arithmetic of it: Corvin had written something down — *the taster survived what should have killed him* — and men who write such things down in a court where the king has no pulse do not live to write sequels. I was next on the list. The only question was whether I'd be found dead in my bed with a convenient bottle of wine or dragged before the thing wearing His Majesty's face and asked to smile about it.

I chose the mountains.

Thornfell at night is a country of teeth. The smugglers' paths don't appear on any surveyor's map because the surveyors who tried to chart them kept falling into ravines, and after the third one the crown decided the ravines were doing adequate work. But I had spent twelve years memorizing those paths — not from maps, from men. Every smuggler who ever moved salt, silk, or sin through the passes eventually sat across a table from me, nervous, drinking wine I'd already tasted, and talked to fill the silence. They thought they were flattering me with their secrets. Mostly they were right. A taster is furniture until you need him, and then he is a vault.

So I went east by the Hollow Stair, which is not a stair but a goat track so steep the goats apologize for it, and from there into the Widow's Cut, where the rock walls lean close enough that you can touch both sides of the mountain at once and pretend, briefly, that the world is narrower than your problems.

The riders found my trail by noon of the second day.

I heard them before I saw them — the particular wet crunch of shod horses on scree, four sets of hooves moving with the unhurried confidence of men who believe their prey is tired. Which I was. Poison immunity does not extend to blisters, and my legs had opinions about the Hollow Stair that they expressed freely.

I did the sensible thing: I went up where they couldn't. There's a chimney of broken granite above the Cut that smugglers call the Priest's Finger, on the theory that only divine intervention gets you up it. I have no theology, but I have twelve years of practice putting things in my mouth that want me dead, and climbing that chimney felt much the same. One hold at a time. Don't think about the drop. Swallow the fear like everything else.

From the top I watched them circle below, casting for my scent like hounds who'd lost the fox. Their captain wore the king's colors — the king's colors, on men hunting the king's taster, and no one among them seemed to find this funny except me, quietly, in a way that wasn't really laughing at all.

They turned back at dusk. Not defeated — recalled. Someone wanted me herded east, not caught. That thought kept me company through the cold hours, and it was poor company.

* * *

On the fourth day I stopped being alone, and I want it noted that I did not enjoy it.

I'd come down out of the high passes into a valley the smugglers call the Mute — no birds, no wind, just snow and the sound of your own breathing getting on your nerves. And there, in the lee of a fallen waystone, sat two figures who had no business existing: a woman in travel-stained grey with the shaved-and-regrown haircut of someone who used to be clergy, and a boy of perhaps ten who was watching me with the flat, calculating stare of a child deciding whether I was food.

The woman stood when she saw me. She moved like a deer deciding between flight and fight and settling, reluctantly, on a third option: conversation.

"You're palace," she said. "Court-cut hair, city boots, and you walk like a man counting exits." Her eyes narrowed. "You're the taster."

"And you're a dead woman walking," I said. "Sera Vane. Branded heretic. There are notices in every garrison town between here and the coast. Your likeness is better than most."

"Flatterer." She didn't smile. Neither did I. The boy stood up beside her, and I noticed two things at once: he was half-starved in the way that comes from weeks, not days, of short rations, and he had the king's jaw. Not a resemblance. The jaw itself, the heavy Hallowmere line of it, the one every portraitist flattered and every caricaturist mocked.

My stomach performed a slow, cold revolution.

"No," I said aloud.

"He doesn't know yet," Sera said, quiet. "Tam, this is—"

"I know who he is." The boy's voice was rougher than his years. "Everyone knows the taster. He eats first so nobody else dies." He looked me over with something that wasn't quite contempt and wasn't quite envy. "Must be nice. Being useful."

Children are accurate weapons; that's why we don't let them vote.

"I'm not going anywhere with you," I said to Sera. "Whatever this is — the heir, the rite, the gods coming back to bite the realm on the ankle — I am a man with a specific set of skills and none of them involve prophecy. I taste things. I know paths. I'm currently using both to be somewhere else."

"The riders," she said.

"What about them?"

"There were six yesterday, working the valley mouth. This morning there were twenty, and they've split into pairs." She let that sit. "Someone isn't chasing you anymore, Master Fairweather. Someone is combing. You can outrun a chase. You can't outrun a comb."

I looked back the way I'd come, at the white silence of the Mute, and did the arithmetic I'd been avoiding since the palace. Four riders had found my trail in a wilderness the size of a small country within two days. That wasn't luck. That was someone who knew my habits, my routes, my tells — someone reading a file that should only exist in one place, in the head of one man.

Corvin hadn't been the only one taking notes. Or Corvin's notes had been read by the wrong eyes before his body cooled. Either way: the usurper knew the taster had run, knew roughly where, and cared enough to spend soldiers on it. Which meant I wasn't fleeing anymore. I was being driven, and driven animals get funneled.

"Where are you going?" I asked, hating myself for asking.

"East," Sera said. "The free cities. There's a safehouse in Saltreach, a priestess who owes me, and—" she hesitated, and I watched a woman who had staked her life on faith struggle to stake it on a stranger, "—and a rite that needs doing before the winter solstice, or the thing on the throne finishes whatever it's becoming."

"And you need me because?"

"You know the paths. All of them. Every smuggler's cut and goat track through Thornfell." She said it simply, without flattery, which is how I knew she meant it. "And your blood, though you don't know that part yet. I'll explain when you're too tired to argue."

"That's now," I said. "I'm always too tired to argue. It's my defining trait."

* * *

We traveled east for three days, and I will record honestly that I spent the first two planning my escape and the third realizing I'd stopped.

It wasn't trust. Trust is a coin I keep in a very deep pocket. It was proximity. The boy Tam fell asleep against my shoulder on the second night because I was the warmest object available, and there is no version of a man that walks away from that. Sera kept watch in shifts and shared her tea without being asked, and once, when I slipped on ice coming down into the Ashen Ford valley, she caught my arm with a grip that suggested heretic priests do more cardio than the portraits imply.

She talked, in the low hours. About the shrine where a god two centuries dead had answered her prayer with a voice like a door opening in a house she'd lived in all her life. About the old magic waking along Thornfell like blood returning to a numb limb. About bindings and vessels and a hunger wearing the crown, and how a mortal whose body held death itself — her phrase, delivered while looking directly at me — could unmake it or complete it, depending on whose hand poured the cup.

"So I'm an ingredient," I said.

"You're a choice," she said. "There's a difference. Most people never get one."

"I had a choice once. Liver and onions or the king's soup. I chose the soup. Look how well that's gone."

Tam laughed — a rusty, startled sound, like a hinge remembering oil — and then looked embarrassed about it, and then fell asleep again, and I sat there in the dark with a heretic and a claimant and thought about Corvin, who had died because he wrote the truth down, and about the king, who had died because he trusted the wrong cupbearer, and about myself, who had survived twelve years of poison by never trusting anything, and how the universe appeared to be arranging matters so that I would have to trust everything, all at once, immediately.

The universe has a cruel sense of menu design.

* * *

We came down to the river road on the sixth morning, and that was where the trap closed — or rather, where I finally saw its shape.

Riders on the far bank. Riders behind us on the ridge, patient as weather. And ahead, at the ford, a man in general's black sitting his horse in the shallows like he owned the water rights, which, given who he served, he probably did.

General Vessic. I'd tasted for him twice at border negotiations. He remembered me. He smiled like a man greeting an old debt.

"Wendell Fairweather," he called across the water. "His Majesty misses you at table."

Beside me, Sera's hand found her knife. Behind us, the ridge riders began to come down. And Tam — the king's true son, the last Hallowmere jaw in a realm of impostors — looked up at me and said, in the flat voice of a child who has learned what running means:

"Which way, taster? You're the one who knows the paths."

The river ran fast and cold at my back, and every path I knew ended in Vessic's smile, and for the first time in twelve years of swallowing death for a living, Wendell Fairweather had no idea what was in the cup.
