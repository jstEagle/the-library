## 5. Salt and Sovereignty

The safehouse smelled of salt fish, tallow smoke, and the particular despair of people who had been hiding too long in one room. It sat above a chandler's shop in Marrowgate's low quarter, where the streets ran with brine and nobody asked questions because everybody had answers they'd rather keep buried.

I had been a heretic for eleven days now. The brand on my forearm was still weeping.

"You're staring at it," Wendell said. He sat across the table from me, turning a copper coin over his knuckles in that restless way of his, as if his hands needed constant proof that they belonged to him.

"I'm staring at you," I lied.

"Everyone stares at the brand eventually." He said it without self-pity, which somehow made it worse. "Might as well get it over with."

"I was thinking," I said, "that the gods chose an interesting moment to come back. Two hundred years of silence, and they return just in time to watch a kingdom eat itself."

"Convenient timing is usually someone's design." He flipped the coin, caught it. "In my experience."

"In your experience," I said, "poison is a design. Everything else is weather."

That earned me something almost like a smile. It didn't reach anywhere near his eyes, but then, nothing did. Twelve years at court had taught him to keep his face like a locked pantry — everything inventoried, nothing available.

Tam slept in the corner, curled around a heel of bread he hadn't eaten, saving it. Eleven years old and already rationing hope. When I'd first told him what he was — what the rite said he was — he'd asked if he could still be a boy until spring. I had no answer for that. I still didn't.

"Wendell," I said. "We need to talk about your blood."

* * *

He took it badly, which surprised me. I had expected the flinch of a man hearing something unpleasant but survivable — he'd survived worse flavors, after all. Instead he went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

"My blood."

"The old rites are specific." I spread the page I'd copied from the shrine archive — one of three documents in the world that still described the binding-rites, and the only one not currently in the hands of people who wanted to complete them. "A god cannot walk fully into the world. They need a door. A vessel, for the lesser work. And for the greater work — the binding of a realm, a crown, a line of succession — they need more than a vessel. They need a *keystone*. A mortal whose body holds death itself."

"And you think that's me."

"I know that's you. Twelve years, Wendell. You've swallowed enough toxin to kill a garrison. Your blood doesn't fight poison — it *carries* it. Every dish you ever survived is still in you, held, suspended. You're not immune. You're a library."

"A library," he repeated flatly.

"Of death. Yes."

He set the coin down. That frightened me more than shouting would have. A man who stops fidgeting has stopped performing.

"So when the physician tested me—"

"He wasn't testing whether you were poisoned. He was testing how much you carried. Measuring the depth of the well." I watched him absorb that. "And whoever wears the king's face needs a keystone to finish the binding. To make the vessel permanent. To make the false king true, retroactively, in the eyes of every god still listening. With your blood in the ritual, Tam isn't the heir. He's a pretender. The usurper becomes the rightful line, sealed by divine law."

"And without my blood?"

"Without your blood, the binding frays. The vessel rots from inside — faster than it already is. The usurper has maybe a season before the thing wearing the crown collapses in front of the whole court."

"So they need me alive."

"They need you *willing*, ideally. Blood taken by force works, but poorly — the binding holds for years instead of generations. So they'll offer you things. Titles. Pardons. They'll offer you the one thing they think a man like you wants."

"Which is?"

"Purpose," I said gently. "You've spent twelve years being used. Someone finally offering to use you *formally* might feel like an honor."

He laughed then — one short, ugly note. "You've been a heretic eleven days and you already understand courts better than I do."

"I understood courts long before the brand. That's why they branded me."

* * *

Tam woke near dusk and pretended he hadn't been listening. Children always pretend; it's a survival skill older than speech. He came to the table, climbed onto the stool, and looked at Wendell with that grave, unblinking stare that must have been his father's, because it certainly wasn't anything the streets gave him.

"Is it true?" Tam said. "About your blood?"

"It's true."

"Does it hurt? Carrying all that?"

Wendell opened his mouth — I watched him reach for some deflection, some dry little lie to hand a child — and then closed it again. "Some nights," he said finally. "Not the poisons. Those don't hurt anymore. Just knowing they're there. Like sleeping in a house full of knives."

"My mother said the crown was like that," Tam said. "Heavy even when you're not wearing it."

"Your mother sounds wise."

"She was a laundress." Tam said it with fierce pride. "She washed for the palace for nine years. Nobody knew who she was except Father, and he made her promise never to tell, because the last three heirs died and he thought—" His voice caught, steadied. "He thought love was a bigger target than a name."

I looked away, giving them the privacy of my turned head, which fooled no one.

It was later that night, while Wendell paced and Tam slept again, that I performed the rite. Not the great rite — nothing so grand. Just the verification, the old salt-ceremony that the free cities had used before the gods went silent, back when oaths meant something because someone was listening.

I drew the circle in coarse sea salt on the floorboards. Set the iron nail at the center — iron, for truth; salt, for memory. Tam knelt in the circle barefoot, and I spoke the words that had not been spoken aloud in two centuries, feeling them move through my mouth like cold water.

The god came the way the tide comes. Not arriving — *returning*. The candle flames leaned toward the circle. The salt grains shivered and stood on end, each one a tiny white tooth. And above Tam's bowed head, the air wrote something in light that only I could read:

*BLOOD OF THE LINE. TRUE AND LAST.*

Then, fainter, addressed — I am certain — to the man standing rigid in the doorway:

*AND THE WELL THAT HOLDS DEATH. CHOOSE.*

Choose. As if it were a menu. As if Wendell Fairweather had walked into a tavern and the universe had slid a slate across the bar: tonight's specials — martyrdom or betrayal, chef's discretion.

The light faded. Tam looked up, wide-eyed. "Did it work?"

"It worked," I said.

"Am I really—"

"You're really." I helped him up off the cold floor. "Go sleep. Truly sleep. Tomorrow we plan."

He went. Wendell stayed in the doorway a long moment, arms crossed, watching the dead salt circle like it owed him money.

"'Choose,'" he said at last. "Gods don't choose, priestess. People do. Whatever that thing is, it's shopping."

"Yes."

"And I'm the goods."

"You're the keystone," I said. "There's a difference. Keystones hold arches up. Goods get consumed."

"Comforting distinction." He crouched, touched one grain of salt, rubbed it between finger and thumb. "You should know something about me, Sera. Everyone assumes a taster is loyal. We're not. We're *careful*. Loyalty is for people with options." He stood. "If it comes down to my blood saving this realm or my blood staying in my body where it belongs, I want you to understand in advance that I will disappoint everyone."

"I know," I said. "That's why the gods picked you."

"That makes no sense."

"No," I agreed. "It rarely does. Faith would be very easy if it did."

He left for the narrow cot behind the partition, and I sat alone with the salt circle, sweeping it up grain by grain into a jar, because even spent salt remembers, and I did not want the room remembering us while we slept.

Outside, Marrowgate's bells rang the hour, and beneath their bronze voices I heard — or imagined, or did not imagine — something vast turning over in its sleep beneath the mountains, attentive, patient, hungry.

Three days later, a chandler's boy came pounding up the stairs with a message he shouldn't have been able to afford to deliver, breathless, terrified, holding out a scrap of paper bearing a seal I knew from my life before the brand.

The general's riders had been seen on the north road.

They weren't searching for Tam.

They were searching for a taster.
