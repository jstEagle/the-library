## 6. The Ember Vigil

The shrine sat above the tree line like a wound that had never learned to close. Black stone, older than the kingdom it overlooked, older than the road that had carried us to it — a road, I noted with professional appreciation, that appeared on no map and no ledger, which meant someone had built it specifically to be forgotten. Sera called the place the Ember House. She said the god who lived there had gone by many names once, back when names were still worth giving.

I called it cold. Spectacularly, inventively cold, the kind of cold that got into your joints and set up housekeeping.

"You'll keep the vigil alone," Sera said, wrapping my hands in strips of wool she'd boiled in something that smelled like pine and regret. "From sundown to the second dawn. You won't eat. You won't sleep. When the god comes — and it will come — you don't ask it for anything. You listen."

"And if I fall asleep?"

"Then you'll dream of the king's table," she said, "and you'll wake up having told it everything you know."

That was the part that kept me upright through the first hour. Not faith. Fear. Twelve years at court had taught me every flavor of fear there was — the sharp kind when the soup smelled of bitter almond, the slow kind when a minister smiled too widely, the dull grinding kind that came from watching men you'd saved forget your name between one course and the next. But this was a new vintage: the fear of being *known*. Of sitting in front of something ancient and hollow-eyed while it pried open my skull like an oyster.

Tam had wanted to come. The boy had argued his case with the stubborn logic of the half-starved — that if the god was going to show me things, it should show me things about *him*, since he was apparently the whole point of this expedition. Sera had refused him with a gentleness that surprised me, and then Tam had sulked behind the shrine's broken wall for an hour before falling asleep against a brazier stone, which was, I reflected, the most kingly thing he'd done all week.

Sundown came up out of the valley like a tide. The last light caught the shrine's eastern face and turned the black stone the color of cooling iron, and for a moment I understood why people had once climbed mountains to sit in drafty rooms and wait for voices. It wasn't the architecture. It was the timing. Everything about the place was built to make you feel small at exactly the right moment, the way a good poisoner builds a dish so the victim feels honored right up until the end.

I settled onto the stone bench at the shrine's center. Someone had carved a shallow bowl into it, generations ago, and the bottom was blackened with the residue of a thousand old fires. Sera had left me a handful of embers wrapped in waxed cloth, with instructions I still didn't fully understand: *feed the bowl when the silence gets too heavy.*

The silence got heavy around the time the stars came out.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about vigils: the first hours are nothing. Your body files complaints — knees, bladder, the phantom taste of dinner you didn't eat — and you file them away, because you've spent twelve years standing behind a king's chair while he ate, motionless, for hours, smiling at nothing. Court had been a vigil too, in its way. I'd simply been keeping watch over a man instead of waiting for a god.

Around midnight the embers in the bowl dimmed to a red so deep it was almost a memory of fire. And the silence changed texture. It stopped being empty and started being *attentive*, the way a room changes when someone enters it quietly behind you.

I didn't hear a voice. I want to be clear about that, because the priests' accounts all describe voices, and I've decided they were embellishing. What I felt was more like a hand opening inside my mind — slowly, finger by finger — and whatever had been clenched shut in there coming loose.

And then I saw the night the king died.

Not imagined it. Saw it, the way you see a memory that isn't yours, with details you have no business knowing. The royal bedchamber in the west tower, eleven years ago — no, twelve; I'd tasted for him barely a season then, still learning which ministers salted their own cups. The king — the true king, Aldric, broad and loud and fond of honeyed pears — lying fevered after a hunt, sweat soaking the sheets, physicians coming and going with their little bags of leeches and lies.

And in the corner, half in shadow, a man I recognized from court functions. Not a face I could name even now. A shape, a presence, wearing courtly clothes like a borrowed coat. He stood at the foot of the bed and he *negotiated*. That was the horror of it — not murder, not poison, but terms. I watched the fever burn through the king's body and I watched something pour itself into the space the fever left, the way water fills a boot. The bargain happened somewhere in the middle of that — words exchanged that I couldn't hear, only feel, like pressure changes in a diving bell. The king's price. The usurper's payment. Something about a name given freely, a door opened willingly.

The vessel that rose from that bed wore Aldric's face the way a glove wears a hand. And I understood, with a lurch that nearly emptied me over the edge of the bench, that I had tasted for that thing. For years. Had knelt each morning and sworn the taster's oath to a hollow man, had risked my life daily protecting a body that didn't need protecting because nothing inside it could die of anything as honest as arsenic.

The grief arrived ahead of the anger, which was unlike me. I grieved for Aldric — a man I'd barely known, whose favorite dishes I could recite but whose voice I realized I couldn't remember at all. Twelve years of service and the true king had been dead longer than I'd known him. My entire career had been an offering laid at an empty altar.

*Why show me this?* I asked, into the silence. *What do you want me to do with it?*

The god did not answer in words. It answered by turning the vision, the way you turn a key.

* * *

The second showing was smaller and worse.

A table. A long table in a hall I didn't recognize, hung with banners I did — the free cities' colors, the ones Sera had described, the alliance we were building toward. Seated along it: Sera, older, her hair gone grey at the temples. Tam, grown, wearing a crown that fit him properly, listening to a petition with his chin on his fist exactly the way Aldric used to. Peace. Real peace, the boring kind, the kind where the worst thing happening in the hall is a dispute over river tolls.

And at the far end, an empty chair. Draped in black. With a cup before it that no one touched.

I knew the chair. Some part of me that had spent twelve years reading tables the way other men read faces knew it instantly. Empty chairs at feasts are never empty; they're full of everyone who isn't sitting in them.

The god let me look at that chair for a long time. Long enough for me to understand what it was saying without saying: *this is one path. There are others. But this one is here, and it is paid for, and the price has already been counted.*

My blood. Every poison I'd ever survived, banked in my flesh like coin in a vault. The usurper needed it to finish the binding — and anything that could complete a binding could, fed wrongly, unmake one. I was not a taster anymore. I was the dish.

I thought about running. Honestly, I gave it a fair hearing. There were smuggler paths out of Thornfell that ended on southern shores where a man with my skills could pass as a cook, or a corpse, or a very successful merchant, and live thirty quiet years. The god showed me that path too — showed it generously, without judgment, the way a good physician lays out both treatment options. I saw myself old and safe and anonymous, drinking decent wine in a warm room, while across the mountains a boy who reminded me uncomfortably of myself starved in a court of shadows, and a priestess burned for a heresy that was only the truth told early.

Then the god showed me the third thing, and this was the one I hadn't expected.

It showed me the vessel-king, alone. In some hour of the false morning, in the west tower, weeping. Not performing grief — the thing performed grief beautifully at state funerals — but weeping the way a locked room weeps, soundlessly, hopelessly, the tears of a prisoner watching the sun through someone else's window. Whatever remained of Aldric in there, buried under the usurper's binding, it was still in there. Still aware enough to mourn itself.

I had spent twelve years tasting death for that man. It seemed poor form to stop now, just because the work had gotten harder.

"Fine," I said aloud, to the dark, to the embers, to whatever was listening. "One path. I see it. But I'm telling you now, whoever you are — I don't accept any price I haven't negotiated. That's how I was trained. Nothing goes in a dish unweighed."

The silence held for a moment. Then — and I swear this on every dish I ever survived — the embers in the bowl flared, briefly, bright as a struck match, and went down again. Which either meant the god agreed to terms, or found me funny. With gods, I was learning, the two were rarely distinguishable.

* * *

Dawn came gray and thin, and the second dawn after it came gold, and between them I sat on a cold stone bench having made a bargain I couldn't yet name the price of. Sera found me there, stiff and frost-rimed and probably smelling like a week-old corpse, and looked at my face for a long moment before asking anything.

"You saw him," she said. "The true king."

"I saw everything." I climbed off the bench, joints cracking like green firewood. "The bargain. The binding. And the bill, Sera. I saw the bill."

She didn't ask what the bill was. I think she already knew. Priests always know; it's the rest of us who need visions to catch up to what we dread.

Tam was awake, feeding the last of our travel bread to a mountain crow that had clearly robbed braver men than him. He looked up at me with that ferocious, underfed hope he carried everywhere, and asked if the god had said anything about him.

"It said you'll make a fine king," I lied gently, "if you learn to share the bread."

He grinned, and something in my chest twisted, because I had seen the hall with the black-draped chair, and I had seen him grown in it, and I would do a great deal to make sure that image stayed true — including, apparently, sitting on the far side of that table myself.

We started down the mountain toward the free cities, three figures on a forgotten road. Behind us the Ember House caught the morning light and cooled back into ordinary black stone.

Two days later, at the ford below Ravenmere, a man we'd trusted sold our route to the usurper's general for a purse and a pardon — and the river rose red before we ever saw it coming.
