## 7. Betrayal at the Ford

*Sera*

I smelled the treachery before I understood it, which is the trouble with gods — they give you warnings shaped like weather.

The morning of the crossing, the air over Ravenmere went sweet. Not flower-sweet. Incense-sweet, the cloying myrrh-and-ash smell that had followed me out of every shrine I'd ever been thrown out of, and it sat on the water like a hand pressing down. The old god was awake in the valley, and awake gods do not lean on the world without reason.

"Something's wrong," I said.

Wendell didn't look up from checking the horses' hooves. He had developed a habit of touching everything twice since the vigil, as if the world might be a dish he hadn't tasted yet. "The river's wrong," he agreed. "Too high for the season. But there's no rain upstream, and the ford's been passable every year of my smuggling life."

"The ford isn't what worries me."

He looked up then. Twelve years at court had taught him to read faces the way I read omens, and whatever mine said made him go still. "Sera. Say it plainly."

"I think someone has told them where we're going."

He was quiet a long moment. Then, because he was Wendell, he did arithmetic instead of grieving. "Who knew the route? You, me, Tam. Corvin." His jaw tightened around the name like it was a bone he'd cracked before. "Corvin, who vouched for the safehouse. Corvin, who fed us for three days and asked charming questions about our timing."

"He held the shrine with me through the purges. He hid heretics in his cellar when hiding one cost you your tongue."

"People change," Wendell said. "Or people were always this, and we paid them to keep being something else. Either way, we cross now or not at all. If the general knows our road, she knows our schedule, and every hour we wait is an hour she spends arranging a welcome."

So we crossed at dawn, which is how you die politely — on schedule, exactly where expected.

* * *

*Tam*

I was halfway across the river when I saw the riders come down off the ridge, and my first stupid thought was: *they've come to help us across.*

That is what twelve years in a woodcutter's attic does to a king. It teaches you to hope at the worst possible moments.

There were maybe thirty of them, dark armor, the usurper's black sun stitched on their shoulders, and they fanned along the far bank with the unhurried confidence of people who had set a table and were waiting for dinner. In the middle of them sat a woman on a grey horse, and even across the water I felt her looking at me specifically. Not at Sera, not at Wendell. At me. Like she'd been told exactly what she was collecting.

"Wendell!" I shouted, but the river took most of it. The water was at my chest now, cold enough to make my hands clumsy on the reins, and behind me I heard Sera start to sing.

You have never heard anything like a priestess of the old gods singing in earnest. It wasn't pretty. It was *true*, which is worse, and the river heard it. The current bent. The water stood up in places like it remembered being a wall, and two of the riders' horses screamed and refused the bank.

Then the arrows came, and the song broke, and the wall fell.

I don't remember deciding to turn back toward the east bank. I remember the horse going down under me, and the water closing over my head, and hands — so many hands — hauling me up into the light and the noise and the smell of blood that wasn't all mine.

The grey-horsed woman leaned down as they bound me. She had kind eyes, which I would learn later was the most frightening thing about her.

"Don't struggle, boy," she said. "General Maren Voss keeps her promises, and I'm promised you'll be treated gently. Better than your friends, anyway."

I looked back at the river. Through the spray and the shouting I could see Sera standing hip-deep in the current with her arms spread, holding the ford the way you hold a door against a mob, and I could see Wendell — small, upstream, already turning his horse away from us.

He didn't look back. I hated him for it. I understood it. Both things are true, and both things stayed true for a long time after.

* * *

*Wendell*

Here is what nobody tells you about running away: it feels exactly like betrayal from the inside.

I had Tam in sight until the river took him, and then I had a choice that wasn't a choice. Go back, and three of us die instead of one of us being taken. Keep going, and live with the arithmetic. I am very good at arithmetic. It is practically my profession. I swallowed poison for a crown for twelve years; I know precisely what a thing costs and precisely who pays.

I paid. I turned upstream.

Sera bought me the time with her body. I could hear her — not the song anymore, but the sound of a woman fighting in water, which is mostly splashing and screaming and the flat crack of a staff finding helmets. Then a sound I will hear until I die: the song starting again, ragged, half-drowned, defiant, and then stopping mid-phrase.

Stopping. Not fading. Stopping.

I rode until the trees closed behind me and the river noise died, and then I stopped and was sick, and then I got back on the horse because grief is a luxury and I had a schedule. Somewhere north of me, a boy who was the last true thing about the crown I'd served was in chains. Somewhere south, a woman who'd taught me that the gods weren't dead — just waiting — was probably bleeding out on a riverbank or already in Voss's custody, and either way it was my fault, because I was the one whose blood everyone wanted, and everyone who touched my cause caught fire.

Corvin. I kept turning the name over like a coin I suspected was clipped. Three days of stew and honest talk, and he'd sold us for a purse and a pardon. And the worst part — the part I kept circling back to like a tongue to a broken tooth — was that I understood him. That was the poison-taster's curse: I could taste anyone's reasons. Fear has a flavor. So does greed, and so does the particular bitterness of a man who has been brave for twenty years and wants, just once, to stop.

I'd wanted to stop too. Every single day. The difference between Corvin and me was one bad morning and a purse I happened not to be offered.

By dusk I'd stopped feeling sorry for him, which was its own kind of relief. Anger travels lighter than grief.

I came down out of the tree line above the south road and saw the camp — Voss's rearguard, forty men, cookfires, horse lines. And at the edge of it, under guard, wrapped in somebody's cloak and shivering, a figure I knew by silhouette alone: broad shoulders, grey braid, the way she sat like a woman daring the ground to disagree with her.

Alive. Captured. Alive.

And beside her cage, giving orders in a voice pitched to carry, General Maren Voss herself — tall, plain, patient, the kind of commander histories get written about badly. I watched her check Sera's bonds personally. I watched her post six guards where four would do. And I heard her say, clear across the cooling air:

"She lives until the taster comes for her. He will. They always come back for their people. Put out word the priestess dies at dawn unless he presents himself."

Dawn. A deadline. A trap with my name on it, baited with the only person left who believed I was worth saving.

Twelve years I'd spent tasting other people's food, keeping my own mouth shut, surviving by never being the dish. Now I was the main course, invited to serve myself.

I should have ridden west. The free cities were two days hard riding. I could vanish into smuggler country, live out my life as a rumor, let the realm keep its hollow king and its hungry god and its chained boy-king-in-waiting, and no one would ever again die for the crime of knowing me.

Instead I found myself doing what I always did when a dish was set in front of me.

I studied it. Counted the guards. Noted the horse lines, the watch rotations, the gap in the pickets where the ravine ran close. Noted, with a professional's appreciation, that Voss had built her trap well — and that every trap is also a door, if you're willing to walk through it sideways.

Somewhere in that camp was Tam, or word of Tam. Somewhere was Sera, breathing, angry, alive. And somewhere in my veins was every poison the crown had ever fed me, twelve years of arsenic and nightshade and worse, sleeping in my blood like coals in ash — the one weapon in the world the usurper needed, and the one thing I had never yet thought to use *on purpose*.

"Dawn," I said to myself, and the word tasted like hemlock.

I backed away from the ridge, quiet as a man leaving a table before the toast, and started planning the most dangerous meal of my life.
