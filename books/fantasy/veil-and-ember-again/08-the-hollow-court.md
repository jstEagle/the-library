## 8. The Hollow Court

**Tam**

They dressed me in a dead boy's clothes.

That's what I decided they were, anyway — the doublet was too fine for me, silver-threaded at the collar, and it smelled of cedar and something older underneath, like a room that had been shut for years. The woman who laced it said I looked very handsome. She said it the way you'd compliment a pig you were fattening.

I had been in the Hollow Court four days, and I had learned three things.

First: never eat anything. The food arrived on gilded plates and I pushed it around until the servants took it away, and I drank only from the skin Sera had sewn into my coat lining before the ford. Bitter water, but mine.

Second: General Corvane smiled at me like I was a puzzle he'd already solved. He called me "the foundling" in front of the court and "Your Highness" when no one else could hear, and both times it felt like a hand closing around my wrist.

Third — and this was the one that kept me awake at night, staring at the canopy of the bed they wouldn't let me leave —

The king wept.

I heard him the second night. My chamber adjoined his through a servant's passage with a warped door, and past midnight there came a sound I knew from the orphanage winters, from the fever tents: a man crying as quietly as he could, so no one would come and make him stop or make it worse. Not sobbing. Just leaking. The sound of someone who had given up on being comforted and only wanted to get through it.

In the morning, King Aldric held court from the thorn-iron throne, and his face was smooth as an egg. He greeted the ambassadors. He laughed at the right moments. His eyes went through people like light through water.

But once, when a clerk stumbled over a petition about grain tariffs, the king's hand twitched toward his own chest — pressed flat, just over the heart, the way you do when something aches — and for half a breath his face *crumpled*. Grief moved across it like weather. Then it smoothed again, and he said, in a voice like a well-tuned instrument played by someone who didn't love music, "Proceed."

The courtiers saw nothing. They'd stopped looking years ago, I think. That's what a hollow court means — not that the king is empty, but that everyone agreed to stop noticing.

Except he wasn't empty. That was the horror of it. Something was in there, wearing Aldric like a coat, and sometimes the coat *remembered*.

On the fourth night I put my eye to the crack in the passage door. The vessel-king sat alone by the fire, and tears ran down that perfect face without his expression changing at all, the way a statue weeps when rain finds a seam. He raised one hand and touched his own cheek, puzzled, and looked at the wet fingers as though they belonged to a stranger.

"Please," he said, to no one. To himself. To whoever was left inside. "It hurts. It still *hurts*."

Then the candle guttered, and when the flame steadied, the face was smooth again, and the king rang for wine he would not taste, because there was no one left to taste it.

I sat back against the cold stone and thought of Wendell Fairweather, whom I'd known for all of two weeks and trusted more than any living soul, and I understood suddenly why Sera had gripped my shoulder at the ford and said *run*.

Because if Wendell came back here — and I knew, the way you know weather coming, that he would — then this room, this weeping thing in a king's shape, was what he was walking back to.

**Wendell**

Twelve years ago, I walked up this same road to the palace for the first time, a hungry boy from Thornfell with a forged letter of recommendation and a stomach I hadn't yet learned to treat like a fortress. The guards had laughed at my boots. The steward had looked through me. And I had sworn the Taster's Oath with my hand on a knife and meant every word, because it was the first time in my life anyone had wanted me for something I was actually good at.

Now I walked up the same road with a week of beard, a smuggler's stoop, and the same boots, because some jokes write themselves.

The road was full of soldiers. Corvane had tripled the watch after the ford — after Tam, after Sera bleeding out her strength to buy my escape upstream, after whatever report the traitor Vessic had sold them. Every checkpoint between the river and the capital had my description nailed to a post: *Wendell Fairweather, former King's Taster, wanted alive.* Alive. That word should have frightened me. Mostly it flattered me. Even now, even treasonous, I was worth more unspoiled.

So I didn't sneak in. Sneaking is for people with something to hide and nothing to offer.

At the Eastgate checkpoint, a sergeant with a familiar squint looked up from my papers — good forgeries cost me everything Sera's safehouse had left, and they named me not Fairweather but Marsh, a provisioner's agent — and I watched him almost wave me through, and I thought: *no.*

Because here is the thing about walking back into a trap willingly: you want the trapper to know. Otherwise what's the point? A trap sprung on accident is just bad luck. A trap entered on purpose is a *move*.

"Sergeant," I said, pitching my voice low and bored, "your captain will want to see these papers personally. Tell him the taster has come home."

He frowned. "The taster's a traitor."

"The taster," I said, "is exactly where certain parties have been trying to get him for a month. Check your orders. The ones that don't say *alive*."

He checked. Whatever he read made his face go carefully blank, which in my experience is the face men make when they've realized they're holding something hot.

They took my knife, naturally. They always take the knife. They never think to worry about what twelve years of poison does to a man's blood, because no one ever thinks about the taster at all. That has always been our great advantage, we invisible men. We stand next to kings and no one sees us.

They marched me through corridors I knew better than my own hands, past tapestries I'd stared at during long tastings, past the kitchen doors where old Marta still ruled, God keep her, and into the presence chamber where the whole rotten court had assembled to look at me.

And there he was. The king.

Aldric — the thing wearing Aldric — sat the throne with that terrible smoothness, and beside the throne, in armor polished bright enough to be a mirror, stood General Corvane, smiling his solved-puzzle smile.

"Wendell Fairweather." Corvane's voice carried beautifully; he'd practiced it on battlefields. "You fled your king. You consorted with heretics and pretenders. And now you walk back through my gates and hand yourself to me. Explain yourself."

I bowed. Deeply. Twelve years deep.

"Your Majesty. General. I fled because I was confused, and grief makes cowards of careful men. I saw the physician Halloway dead and I panicked — I thought someone was killing those close to the crown." I let my voice crack, just slightly, on *close to the crown*, and watched courtiers exchange glances. Good. Doubt is cheap to plant and expensive to dig up. "I've spent a month learning how wrong I was. There are forces moving against this realm that I don't pretend to understand. But I know my duty. I swore an oath on a knife, and knives don't take oaths back."

Corvane studied me. He was good — genuinely good — at reading men. But he'd never had to read me, because for twelve years nobody bothered. I was furniture that swallowed poison.

"You expect us to believe loyalty?"

"No, General. I expect you to test it." I straightened and let them all see my hands, steady as a surgeon's. "I hear the binding needs a taster's blood. Poisoned blood. Death held in a living body." Murmurs went around the hall like wind through wheat. "I'm the only such blood in the world. You can hunt me forever and risk breaking it, or you can use me. I came back to be used. It's what I'm for."

Silence. The kind that has weight.

And then the king spoke, and every hair on my arms stood up, because the voice was wrong in a way I remembered from that private audience — a voice like a well played by someone who didn't love music.

"He offers himself freely." The smooth face turned to Corvane. "The god says the offering must be free."

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes — the briefest hesitation, a man checking whether the trap he built might have a door in it — and then the smile returned, wider.

"Then welcome home, taster." He gestured, and guards closed around me, gentle as shepherds, which told me everything: they still needed the sheep whole. "We'll begin preparations at once."

As they led me away, I passed close beneath the throne, and the vessel-king leaned down — just slightly, just enough — and whispered in a voice that was *not* the well-played voice, that was cracked and human and drowning:

"*Run. While it still lets me warn you—*"

And then the smoothness closed over his face like water over a stone, and I was marched out of the presence chamber, toward the ritual preparations, toward the altar where every poison I had ever survived waited to be poured out of me.

I went quietly.

I had counted on being taken. I had counted on the altar. What I had not counted on — what I turned over in my cell that night, staring at the ceiling, feeling my poisoned blood move through me like a garrison of small armed men — was that the king inside the vessel was still fighting too.

Two prisoners, then. One in irons, one in silk.

Tomorrow, we would both be poured out.

*Let it come,* I thought, and slept better than I had any right to.
