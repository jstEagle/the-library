## 9. Every Poison I Have Ever Survived

They let me keep my knife.

That was the first mistake anyone made, though nobody in the Hollow Court would ever admit it. A taster's blade is a small thing — short, blunt-nosed, more spoon than weapon — but it is the one tool in the world I have never once been without, and a man's hands remember what his head forgets. They took my boots, my belt, the little vial of poppy syrup I'd carried against pain for twelve years. They left me the knife because it was *mine*, because taking it would have meant admitting what I was: not a guest at this table, not a prisoner of state, but an ingredient.

I walked into the throne room on my own two feet, which surprised them. I think they expected to drag me. There is a certain dignity available only to men who have already decided how they die, and I had spent the whole ride back through enemy lines deciding mine. The guards flanked me anyway, nervous as cats, and I let them. Fear looks good on a man walking toward a throne. It makes the throne look earned.

The hall was full. Not packed — courts are never full the way markets are full — but *arranged*. Every lord who had bent the knee to the vessel-king was present, seated in strict order of proximity to power, and every one of them had heard the rumors by now: the taster had fled, the taster had returned, the taster had been seen speaking with heretics in the mountains. Sera Vane's name was on enough tongues that I could taste it in the air, that faint charred-sugar smell of gossip left too long over a flame.

And there, above us all, sat the king.

Or the thing wearing the king. I had seen it up close once before, in that private audience that had curdled my loyalty into dread, and I remembered the details the way you remember a face in a nightmare: the stillness behind the eyes, the pulse that wasn't there, the way the light went through him slightly wrong, like he was painted on glass. Up close, from across a hall, at forty feet with torches burning — none of it mattered. Distance is the usurper's friend. At forty feet, Aldric Veyra looked exactly like a king. Tired, perhaps. Grieving, perhaps. A man who had lost a war and didn't know it yet.

Behind the throne, half in shadow, stood the other one. The god-hungry usurper. Magister Corvane, they called him publicly — court sorcerer, keeper of the king's dreams, a title so perfectly bland it could only ever belong to a monster. In the flesh he was unremarkable. Grey robes, grey face, hands folded like a patient man waiting for a slow coach. But I knew what I was looking at now. The vigil at the mountain shrine had shown me. Twelve years of swallowing poison had shown me too, in its own way. You learn to see what's hidden in food. Corvane was hidden in plain sight, and everything bright in that hall was shining out of him like heat off a stove.

* * *

"Kneel," said the herald, and I knelt.

Not because I was told. Because kneeling puts your weight low and your balance under you, and because the floor of that hall was marble and I had walked marble floors for twelve years and knew exactly which ones were polished slick with wax. Also because kneeling, done right, is not surrender. Kneeling is loading.

Corvane came down from the dais himself. That surprised me. I had expected him to send someone, to remain aloof, to conduct his business through heralds and hooded acolytes the way sorcerers do in stories. Instead he descended the seven steps slowly, robes whispering, and stopped three feet from me, close enough that I could smell him — cold stone and old incense and underneath it something burnt, something like a wick snuffed too many times.

"Taster Fairweather," he said. "You've caused a great deal of trouble."

"I've caused a great deal of *inconvenience*," I said. "The trouble was yours."

Something flickered behind his eyes. Not anger. Appetite. That was the worst part — worse than anger, worse than contempt. He looked at me the way I used to look at a dish that arrived at the kitchens smelling of bitter almond. Not with fear. With interest. With *hunger*.

"You know why you're here," he said.

"I know why you want me here." I kept my voice flat, court-flat, the voice I'd used for twelve years to say *this dish is safe, Your Majesty* while my stomach clenched around things no dish should hold. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" He tilted his head, and the tilt was wrong, a fraction too far, the way a puppet tilts when the hand holding it gets careless. "Your blood holds death itself. Twelve years of it. Every poison this crown has ever feared, drunk willingly, survived, catalogued in your marrow. The binding that keeps our king walking requires a final seal. Death, freely offered, from a willing throat. You are the last ingredient, Wendell Fairweather. You always were. Why do you think we let you live?"

That question landed harder than any blow could have. *Why do you think we let you live.* Twelve years I had asked myself gentler versions of it. Why me? Why did the poisoner who killed three tasters before me somehow always miss? Why did the physician test *my* blood and not the king's? And the answer had been sitting in the dark all along, patient as arsenic: because I was not the shield. I was the *storehouse*. They had not been protecting the king with my body. They had been filling it.

I thought about that for a moment. I thought about twelve years of dinners eaten alone, of nights spent retching into basins while a physician took notes, of a career built on the flattering lie that I was important because I kept a man alive. None of it was service. All of it was husbandry. I had been a larder, and larders don't get thanked.

"So I drink," I said. "And the binding seals, and the false king walks forever, and you sit behind a throne until the sun burns out. That's the offer?"

"That is the offer." Corvane smiled, and the smile had too many teeth in it for the shape of his mouth. "Refuse, and we take what we need. It will be less clean. Less *willing*. Willingness matters, taster. Even gods' magic respects consent — twisted forms of it, but it respects the shape of the thing. A forced ingredient sours the binding. Yours will be sweet."

"And if I refuse badly enough to spoil it?"

"Then we try again with someone else. There is always someone else." He said it lightly, dismissively, and I watched Tam's name pass behind his eyes like a fish under ice. The boy. The heir. Half-starved, brave, captured at the ford because a man we trusted sold the crossing for a purse of silver and a promise. Corvane had a use for the boy too. Kings need heirs even when they're hollow; it makes the hollowing easier to sell.

But that was later. First, me.

* * *

Here is what Corvane did not know. Here is what no one in that hall knew, because I had never told a living soul, because telling it would have ended my usefulness and possibly my life:

Poison does not leave the body clean.

Twelve years of immunity is not a scrubbing. It is a *layering*. Every dose I survived left residue — bound in fat, sunk into bone, curled around the proteins of my blood like ivy on a wall. The physicians called it tolerance. Tolerance is just a polite word for *occupancy*. My body was not empty of death. My body was a fortress of it, garrisoned, provisioned, every poison I had ever swallowed still inside me in some form, sleeping, waiting for a reason.

A small dose kills a man who has never been poisoned. A large dose kills a man who has built tolerance to that dose. But what kills a man who drinks *everything*?

Everything.

Corvane wanted death freely offered from a willing throat. Fine. I would offer it. I would offer all of it — every bitter lesson, every night of convulsion, every dish I tasted and survived and catalogued, all of it at once, undiluted, concentrated by twelve years of careful husbandry into a single cup. He wanted a seal. I would give him a key turned the wrong way. He wanted to bind a god's hunger into a dead man's shell. I would feed that hunger the one meal it could not digest: itself, reflected, magnified, served by the only palate in the world trained to make poison taste like nothing at all.

The vigil had shown me the path where I died. It had also shown me another path, branching, uncertain, paid for in ways I couldn't yet count. I chose the second one knowing it might cost everything anyway. That's the trick of sacrifice nobody tells you: it only works if you mean it, and meaning it means accepting that you might not walk out.

I meant it.

* * *

"The cup," Corvane said, and an acolyte brought it forward — black iron, wide-mouthed, ugly as a well. "Your blood, taster. Freely given. Speak the words, and the binding completes."

I looked at the cup. I looked at the throne, at the vessel-king sitting there with his dead eyes and his borrowed grief, weeping, I would learn later, only when no one watched. I looked at the lords in their rows, watching me with the fascination of people who suspect they're attending an execution but hope it's a coronation. And I looked at Corvane, standing three feet away, hungry, patient, certain.

Certain men are easy to serve. Certainty is the finest seasoning in the world. Every poisoner knows it.

"On one condition," I said.

Corvane's patience thinned visibly, a muscle jumping at his jaw. "Name it."

"The rite requires the ingredient offered from the principal party's own hand, yes? The binding flows through the one who performs it. Old law. Your own grimoires say so — I read them, during the vigil. The god showed me." I let that land. Let him wonder how much I actually knew, how much was bluff. Bluff is just poison with better manners. "So you pour. You drink first — a sip, a sealing sip, the officiant's share. Then I drink the rest. That's the order. That's the *law*."

For a long moment he simply looked at me. The hall held its breath. Somewhere near the ceiling a banner swung on its pole with a sound like a slow heartbeat.

He should have refused. Any careful man would have refused. But Corvane had spent years being careful, and careful men at the end of long sieges get greedy, and greed is just hunger that's learned to dress itself. The binding needed willingness. My willingness. And I was offering it — openly, formally, wrapped in the language of his own rites. To refuse would mean forcing me after all, souring the seal, starting over with a boy who might break. He ran the arithmetic. I watched him run it. I saw the moment the answer came back *yes*.

"Very well," he said. "The officiant's share."

He took the cup. He poured — my blood drawn earlier that hour, they told me, from a vein in my arm while I was "resting," which is a word captors use for anything — and he raised it, and he spoke the opening words of the binding in a language older than the kingdom, and he drank.

One swallow. A sealing sip. An officiant's share.

Of every poison Wendell Fairweather had survived in twelve years, concentrated, layered, married, aged in living bone like wine in a cask no one was supposed to open.

* * *

I have watched men die of poison. It is part of my trade, unfortunately — recognizing the signs quickly enough to warn, or too late to matter. Blue lips. The sweat that comes cold. The hands that go first, fingers curling like ferns. I stood three feet from Corvane and watched the signs arrive all at once, in a flood, like guests who were all invited to the same hour.

He did not fall immediately. That was the horror of it, and the justice. The poisons woke in order. The ones I'd taken first, the gentle ones, the ones that merely numb — those woke first, and he felt his mouth go strange, and he frowned, because magisters are proud and pride always checks the wrong explanations first. Then the bitter ones woke, the alkaloids, and his heart stuttered like a bird hitting glass. Then the heavy metals stirred in their long sleep, and his vision went gold at the edges, then brown, then nothing.

"What," he said. Just that. *What.* The most honest word he ever spoke in that hall.

I got to my feet. My knees cracked. Twelve years of kneeling to taste other men's food leaves marks.

"You drank me," I said. "All of me. Every dish I ever survived, every night I spent learning to carry death like a servant carries a tray. You wanted a storehouse. I told you — you should have asked what was stored."

He lunged for me. I'll grant him that; he didn't go quietly. His hand closed on my collar and there was strength in it still, god-fed strength, stolen strength, and for one bad moment I thought the hunger might burn the poison faster than the poison could burn him. The old god's magic was in him, and old gods' magic does not tolerate being interrupted mid-meal.

But that was the design, wasn't it. The hunger was the flaw. A god-hungry thing cannot stop eating, even when the meal is killing it. He drank the sealing sip because the rite demanded it, and the rite demanded it because *he* wrote the rite, and he wrote it greedy — officiant's share, principal's portion, every clause funneling the flow of the working through his own body. He had built a machine with himself as the keystone, and I had handed him a cup and told him it was holy.

His grip loosened. His knees went. He went down the seven steps of the dais the hard way, and the lords screamed and scattered like startled pigeons, and the guards — the guards did nothing, because guards are trained to protect the king, and the king was still sitting on the throne, and the king was the only thing in that hall that hadn't moved.

* * *

The vessel-king rose.

I want to describe it properly, because it deserves proper description. For a year and more, that body had been worn like a coat — animated, articulated, *puppeted*, every gesture fed to it from outside. When the feeding stopped, the coat did not simply drop. It *ended*. Aldric Veyra stood up straight for the first time since his death, and every line of him changed — the shoulders, the neck, the set of the jaw — because the thing arranging those lines was gone. What remained was a dead man's body remembering, briefly, how it had once been held.

He looked at me. And in the last seconds before the shell failed, I swear on every poison in my blood that there was something in his eyes. Not the king — the king was gone, wherever kings go. But something. A recognition. A relief. The look of a man waking from a nightmare in the last moment before drowning, seeing the surface, knowing he won't reach it, being glad anyway that someone else will.

His mouth moved. No sound came. But I am a taster, and reading mouths is half my trade, and I know what the shape of those words was.

*Thank you.*

Then he folded, quietly, the way a tent folds when the last peg comes out, and the Hollow Court of Aldric Veyra ended in a heap of fine clothes on a marble floor, and the hall went mad.

* * *

Corvane burned from within. There is no kinder way to say it, and I don't owe him kindness. The god-hunger in him, finding its vessel spoiled, its binding broken, its great work undone by a cup of wine poured by its own hand — it turned inward, the way hunger always turns when the food runs out. Light came out of his mouth. Light came out of his eyes. He clawed at his own robes and the robes glowed and the lords who had crept closest to power for thirty years discovered, rather suddenly, the value of distance.

I stood at the foot of the dais and watched, and I did not look away, because someone in that hall owed him witness and it was going to be me. Justice outside any law still wants a witness. That's what makes it justice instead of murder.

The last thing he did was turn his burning face toward me, and the last thing he said was almost funny, in the way terrible things are almost funny when they end.

"You *planned* this."

"Twelve years," I said. "You seasoned me yourself."

Then the light took him, all of him, down to the scorch-mark on the marble that would outlast every monument in that hall, and the hall went quiet in the way halls only go quiet after they've screamed.

* * *

I stood there in the wreckage of the greatest court in the free world, surrounded by lords on their knees — kneeling now for me, which I found distasteful on principle — and I felt the poison wake in my own veins.

Because that was the price, of course. Of course it was. The officiant's share was a sip; the rest of the cup was still in my hand, and the rite unfinished is a rite unresolved, and my body was already answering the question I'd spent twelve years preparing: *what happens when everything wakes at once?*

Cold. Then heat. Then the fern-curl in my fingers, the bird-against-glass stutter, the gold at the edges of the world. I had time to set the cup down carefully — carefully, always carefully, some habits outlive their purpose — before my knees decided the marble was a good place to be.

The last thing I saw, before the gold closed over, was doors bursting open at the far end of the hall and a figure in a priestess's grey coming through them at a dead run, screaming my name in a voice that cracked down the middle, and behind her, small and furious and alive, a boy with a stolen sword.

Sera Vane reached me first. Her hands found my face. Her mouth was moving — prayers, curses, both, probably both — and the old god she served was awake now, awake everywhere, awake in Thornfell's stones and Thornfell's rivers, and I felt something vast and green and terribly patient lean down over me and consider.

*This one,* it seemed to say. *We have plans for this one.*

Then the gold took me, and I went, gratefully, like a man setting down a tray he has carried for twelve years — not knowing whether I would ever pick anything up again.
