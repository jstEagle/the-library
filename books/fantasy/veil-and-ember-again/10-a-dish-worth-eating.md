## 10. A Dish Worth Eating

*Sera*

They tell you, in the seminary, that miracles arrive like thunder. They do not tell you that most of them arrive like a man who won't stop coughing.

I had prayed over Wendell Fairweather for three days in the shrine at Ember's Rest while the old god hummed through the mountain like a struck bell that would not finish ringing. Three days of watching his chest rise and fall in a rhythm I could not call breathing so much as negotiation. His blood had been a ledger of every poison the crown ever fed him, and when the usurper drank it, all those debts came due at once — in the wrong direction. The gold burned out of him. The venoms did not. His body, which had spent twelve years learning to survive everything, now had to unlearn nothing and simply endure the arithmetic of it all coming home.

The god could have healed him outright. I asked. I asked rather loudly, in the manner of prayer that is mostly arguing.

*No,* it said, in that green patience of its. *He earned every scar. We will not insult him by erasing them.*

Gods, I have decided, are like old soldiers: sentimental about wounds, useless about comfort.

So I sat with him instead. I changed the poultices. I read to him from a book of free-city trade law because it was the only thing dull enough not to disturb him, and because I had discovered that if I read anything with feeling in it, his brow furrowed, and furrowing cost him strength he did not have. Tam came every evening after his lessons and sat on the floor beside the cot and told Wendell about his day in the flat, dutiful voice of a boy practicing to be a king, and sometimes — only sometimes — the boy's voice cracked, and then Wendell's hand would twitch toward him, an inch of movement that cost more than any sword stroke he'd made at the ford.

On the ninth day, Wendell opened his eyes and said, "If you're going to pray over me, at least pick a god with better taste in music."

The bells of Ember's Rest had been ringing for nine days straight. The god was showing off. Gods newly returned to the world are like puppies; everything must be announced.

"You're awake," I said, with the theological composure of a woman who had already wept twice into her sleeves that morning.

"Barely." He tried to sit up, failed, and settled for looking aggrieved. "What happened."

"You won."

"Doesn't feel like winning."

"It never does at first," I said. "Give it a season. Winning feels like eating breakfast without checking it for arsenic. You'll get there."

He looked at me for a long moment. Then, hoarsely: "Tam?"

"Crowned regent four days ago. In the old rite, with salt and ember and the true names spoken aloud in front of two hundred witnesses. He didn't cry until afterward, and then only where I could see, which I suspect was deliberate. He's learning statecraft faster than anyone expected." I paused. "He asked for you at the ceremony. I told him you were sleeping off a miracle."

"That's one word for it." His eyes drifted to the window, where Thornfell stood green and snow-streaked against a sky that no longer felt watched. "And the vessel? The king's body?"

"At rest. The binding broke clean. Whatever was left of Aldric Vane got his death back, and we buried him under his own name with his own face. Tam stood vigil all night. Said a king should be mourned by someone who knew he was worth mourning." I swallowed. "It was the most kingly thing I've seen anyone do in this whole affair, and he's eleven."

Wendell closed his eyes again. When he spoke, it was barely above the bell-song. "Twelve years I tasted for that crown. And the best act of loyalty it ever got came from a smuggled orphan."

"Yes," I said. "Faith works that way. It turns up in strange vessels." I let the word *vessel* sit there between us, sharp-edged, and watched him accept it.

* * *

The coronation-as-regency became, over the following months, simply how things were. Tam ruled from Ember's Rest rather than the hollow court at Vail — the palace was being scrubbed, literally and otherwise, and no one was in a hurry to move back into a building where the walls remembered a false king. The free cities sent envoys. The smugglers' paths through Thornfell, which Wendell had once memorized for the crown's benefit, were formalized into honest roads with honest tolls, and the toll-collectors were mostly reformed smugglers who found taxation far safer than running from it.

Sera — I — found myself something I had never trained for: an advisor. A heretic no longer, but worse, an authority. Priests came to me asking what the old gods wanted. I told them the truth, which disappointed everyone: the gods wanted to be *included*. Prayers answered, harvests blessed, oaths witnessed. They wanted to be treated as neighbors rather than vending mechanisms. It was, I reflected, the least dramatic divine agenda in history, and exactly the kind of thing that would take a century to implement.

Wendell recovered the way a mountain recovers from fire — slowly, visibly, and with certain slopes never quite growing back. His hands shook for a season. His sense of smell, the taster's finest instrument, came back altered: he could no longer detect nightshade at all, but he could smell grief on people, or claimed he could, and I never found occasion to doubt him.

In spring, Tam summoned him to the hall and offered him a title. I was present. I wish I had a chronicler's memory for the exchange, but the gist survives:

"Lord Protector of the Realm's Table," Tam had proposed, deadpan, having clearly workshopped the name with someone — me, actually, as a joke, which the boy had failed to recognize as a joke.

"No," said Wendell.

"Master of the Royal Kitchens."

"Absolutely not."

"A pension."

"I don't want your money, Your Grace. I want—" and here he had looked around the hall, at the banners, at the throne that was still technically too big for the regent sitting in it, "—out. Respectfully. I've eaten for kings long enough. I'd like to cook for people who say thank you."

Tam, being eleven and therefore incapable of pretending not to be wounded, said, "You could at least stay where I can see you."

And that, I believe, was the moment the tavern was truly founded — though the deed and the hearth-license came later, and the name took Wendell the better part of a summer to choose.

* * *

*Wendell*

The tavern stands at the foot of Thornfell where the new road meets the old smuggler's path, which means half my customers are pilgrims going up to see the shrines and half are former criminals going down to pay their taxes, and both groups drink like they've earned it.

I named it *The Last Dish*, which Sera called morbid until I explained: it isn't the last dish I'll ever eat. It's the last dish I ever ate for someone else's survival. Everything served hereafter is just food.

I still wake some nights tasting gold. The physicians — real ones, mine own hires — say the poisons will live in me the way old songs live in a singer's throat: always there, mostly quiet, occasionally rising unbidden. My hands shake on cold mornings. I have learned to chop on warm ones, and to let the stew do the fine work.

But here is the thing nobody warned me about, the thing twelve years of the taster's oath never taught me: survival and living are different trades. Survival is a discipline. Living is a craft, and I am apprenticed to it late, and badly, and gladly.

I know now how bread should smell when it's ready. I know that Tam visits on the first day of every month, sits at the corner table with two guards pretending to be merchants, and eats exactly one bowl of my lamb stew, and that this is the closest thing either of us has to a family ritual. I know that Sera comes when she can, argues theology with my regulars, and has quietly blessed my cellar so thoroughly that even the ale tastes faintly of green things and patience. I know that when the old god passes over the valley — and it does pass over, the way weather does — the lamps burn a little steadier, as if the flame itself is listening.

Last week a woman came in off the evening road, travel-worn, alone, and ordered whatever was good. I brought her the stew and a heel of dark bread and stood there a moment longer than service required, because she had the look of someone who hadn't been able to trust a meal in years. She looked up at me.

"Is it safe?" she asked. Half a joke. Half not.

I thought about the oath I once swore, the one about my body being the wall between poison and king. I thought about a boy on a throne too big for him, mourning well. I thought about a priestess who argued with gods and usually won.

"It's the safest dish in the realm," I said. "I'd stake my life on it."

She laughed, tired and real, and picked up her spoon.

And I turned back to my kitchen, where the fire was low and steady, and the next course was waiting — ordinary, unhurried, and entirely mine.
