## 1. The Charm-Seller's Silence

The trick to selling charms in a city that drowned the last charm-seller was, Petra had learned, to sell nothing at all.

"Sea-glass," he told the fishwife, holding it up so the lantern light caught its frosted curve. "Found it myself on the Grey Strand. Wears away worry, they say. Or at least gives your hands something to do besides wringing."

She laughed and paid him two coppers. The sea-glass had cost him nothing but an afternoon's walk, and if it wore away her worry, that was between her and the glass. Petra Joubert sold trinkets. He did not sell magic. He had not touched his own magic in seven years, not with a fingertip, not with a whisper, and the storm under his skin had sulked into a low grumble, like thunder three counties off.

Mostly.

He kept a bowl of river stones on his stall for days when it grumbled too loud. Cold stone drank heat, and heat was where the crackle lived. He'd learned that from his mother, back when learning such things was a family inheritance instead of a death sentence. *Cold hands, warm heart, quiet blood,* she used to say, pressing his palms to the well-stones. He was thirty-one years old now and still slept with a river stone in each fist, which he felt was undignified but effective.

Greyhollow's night market smelled of fried onions, tallow smoke, and the particular brine of a city built on pilings over a grey and unforgiving sea. Lanterns swung on their hooks. Somewhere a fiddler murdered a reel. Petra pulled his coat tighter against the wind coming off the water and arranged his wares: sea-glass, river stones polished smooth, knotted cords in lucky colors, and a small tray of tin charms shaped like fish, which were popular because they were ugly enough to be funny.

"Ho, Joubert." Old Sella from the pie cart leaned on his table, flour to her elbows. "You hear the talk? Fishermen swears the deep-water bells rang last night. Nobody pulling them. Just rang."

"Bells rust," Petra said. "Rust sings."

"And the tide came in twice."

"Tides are greedy."

Sella snorted and bought a tin fish for her grandson, and Petra smiled his market smile—the one that cost him nothing—and thought about how many years it had been since he'd let himself feel the weather in his teeth. Seven. Seven years since the storm-line of the old kings had been declared extinguished, its last heirs scattered or dead, and Petra Joubert had walked into Greyhollow with empty hands and a new name stitched inside his collar, and had made himself small. Small as a tin fish. Harmless as sea-glass.

It had worked. That was the terrible joke of it. It had actually worked.

* * *

The beggar came at moonrise, when the market was thinning and the onion carts were scraping their griddles clean.

Petra knew him by sight—old Tam, who slept under the eastern stair and told anyone who'd listen that he'd once been a sailor on a ship with a figurehead that sang. Everyone agreed Tam was mad. Everyone also agreed he was harmless, which in Greyhollow was the more valuable quality.

Tonight Tam wasn't telling stories. Tonight Tam was dying.

Petra saw it from across the lane—the way the old man had folded down against the stair like wet rope, the rattle in his breathing that Petra knew from the fever-year, when half the piers had coughed themselves into the ground. People stepped around Tam the way people step around anything inconvenient. The watch wouldn't come for a beggar. The physickers' hall closed at dusk.

Petra should have stepped around him too.

Instead he found himself kneeling in the cold, gathering the old man's shoulders up, feeling the awful bird-light weight of him. "Tam. Tam, look at me. Where does it hurt?"

"Everywhere, lad." A grin through gray lips. "Been everywhere a long while. Just the everywhere's winning tonight."

There was a physicker two streets over who sometimes took night patients. There was a woman on Anchor Row who owed Petra a favor. There were things he could do—ordinary things, human things, carry him, pay someone, sit with him. Petra catalogued them all in the space of a breath, and beneath the catalogue, under seven years of careful silence, something else stirred and turned over like a sleeper.

Tam's hand found Petra's wrist. His grip was nothing. His eyes were fixed somewhere past the lanterns, past the masts, up.

"Heard the bells," Tam whispered. "Deep ones. My grandmother said—they ring when the old ones wake. When somebody finally—" A cough tore through him, long and wet. And then, with the last air in his lungs, the old man prayed.

Not to the Nine of Vaelric's new temple. Not to any saint with a statue and a donation box. Tam prayed the way sailors' grandmothers pray, to whatever had been listening before the crowns got involved—to the dark under the water, to the wind with no name, to the *old* old ones. *Let me see the singing shore. Let me go home by the deep road.*

And the sky answered.

Not rain. Not lightning, though lightning would have been simpler, kinder, a thing Petra could have blamed on weather. What answered was a *presence*—a pressure like the moment before a summer storm, rolling down the lanes of Greyhollow, through the market, through the stones, through Petra's ribs. Every lantern flame stood up straight. The fiddler stopped mid-murder. The sea itself went flat and silent as hammered pewter.

Then the light came. It came out of nowhere and everywhere, a slow gold rising like dawn deciding to happen early, and it poured through the city and through every living thing in it, and Petra felt it *find* him.

That was the only word for it. It moved through the crowd like a hand moving along a shelf, touching this one, that one, passing over, passing over—and then stopping on him. Stopping *in* him. The storm under his skin, dormant seven years, woke all at once and answered, and the gold light flared around Petra Joubert like a bonfire catching, bright enough that he heard Sella gasp his name, bright enough that he saw his own shadow thrown huge and wrong across the market square, a shadow with lightning in its shoulders.

Around the square, in ones and twos, other lights answered. A herb-wife by the fountain whose basket glowed green. Two children near the pie cart shimmering faint as foxfire. A dockhand whose tattoos lit up like harbor lamps. The old gods, waking after a century of silence, had sent out one great call—and every soul in Greyhollow that carried even a drop of the old blood had just been named aloud.

Under the eastern stair, old Tam sighed, and smiled, and died with the gold light still pooled gentle around him like a tide going home.

Petra didn't see him pass. Petra was looking at the watch-house door across the square, where lamplight had just kindled behind windows that had been dark all evening.

* * *

They moved fast, for watchmen. Within a quarter hour there were six of them with hooked poles and net-irons, working outward from where Tam lay, asking questions in the flat polite voice that in Greyhollow always preceded the drowning-cage. The herb-wife was already gone—fled or taken, no one would say. The children were swept indoors by parents who suddenly remembered urgent business elsewhere.

Petra packed his stall with hands that shook only a little. Sea-glass into the sack. Tin fish into the sack. River stones—he hesitated over those, then took them all; a man about to run needed cold hands more than ever.

"Joubert." Sella stood at his elbow, pale, wiping flour that wasn't there anymore. "I saw. I saw what—"

"You saw a lantern flare," Petra said quietly. "Faulty oil. You've known me seven years, Sella. I sell trinkets."

"I know what I—"

"*Trinkets.*"

Her mouth worked. Then she nodded, once, hard, and turned back to her cart, and Petra loved her a little for it and hated himself more than a little for asking.

A watchman was coming down his row of stalls. Young, thorough, checking under tables with his pole. Petra slung his sack, hunched his shoulders into the shape of a tired vendor, and began to walk—not fast, never fast—toward the lane that led away from the water, away from the watch-house, toward the tanner's quarter where the alleys tangled and the lamps were few.

Behind him, the young watchman reached old Tam's body and crouched over it, and called out to his sergeant that the beggar was dead, and that his eyes were open, and that—here the boy's voice climbed an octave—his eyes were *glowing*, Sergeant, still glowing, gold as a coin.

The storm under Petra's skin crackled, delighted, alive, and utterly indifferent to seven years of silence.

He turned the corner into the dark and ran.
