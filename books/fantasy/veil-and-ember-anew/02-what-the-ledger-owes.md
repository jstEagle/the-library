## 2. What the Ledger Owes

Petra had always assumed that if the watch ever came for him, it would be at night, in his stall, with his hands around something that hummed. He had rehearsed the moment a hundred times: the dive through the canvas awning, the sprint along the tanner's row where the smell would cover his trail, the long drop into the canal. He had not rehearsed being caught in broad daylight by a fishwife who wanted a discount.

"Storm-charm my elbow," said Marta of the herring stalls, holding up the little copper fish he'd sold her three days ago. "It hasn't done a thing."

"That's because it's a *luck* charm," Petra said, "and you're arguing with me instead of gutting herring, which suggests your luck is working beautifully."

She snorted. Around them, Greyhollow's morning market went on in its usual fashion—loud, damp, and aggressively indifferent to the fact that half the city was whispering about miracles. The beggar at the fountain steps, old Tam with the rotting leg, had walked yesterday. Walked. And now every third conversation on the square bent toward it like wheat toward wind.

Petra kept his head down and his hands busy. Under his skin, the storm that had slept for seven years was no longer sleeping. It rolled and muttered like weather coming over the hills, and every hour it grew harder to pretend it wasn't there. He'd spent the night after Tam's miracle sitting very still on his cot, breathing in counts of four, while blue light crawled up his forearms like frost deciding whether to bloom.

*Not here,* he told it. *Not now. Not ever.*

The storm did not answer. Storms never did. That was rather the point of them.

He was wrapping Marta's refund—a fair trade, since the copper fish had cost him nothing but an afternoon—when the bells began.

Not the market bells. The deep ones, the ones in the watchtower that only rang for fire, plague, or sorcery. The crowd's murmur changed pitch all at once, the way water changes before a wave arrives, and Petra felt it in his teeth before he heard it in his ears.

"Proclamation!" someone shouted from the tower steps. "By order of Magistrate Corven! All bearers of the old marks will present themselves for clemency by sundown! Those who conceal—" The herald's voice cracked, and he started again, louder. "Those who conceal their nature will be taken to the Weir!"

The Weir. Everyone knew what happened at the Weir. It was in the name.

Marta looked at him, and her eyes were kind and frightened in equal measure, which was somehow worse than either alone. "Petra," she said slowly. "Your stall. Last night. Halden says he saw light coming off your hands when you closed up."

"Halden also swears he saw a mermaid in the fish barrels."

"He did see light."

"He was drunk."

"He was *less* drunk than usual," she said, and pressed the copper fish back into his palm, and folded his fingers over it, and stepped away from him. Not running. Just stepping. The way you step back from a dog that has begun to growl without meaning to.

That was how it started, then. Not with chains. With a step.

Petra packed his stall in four minutes flat, a personal record, taking only what fit in the satchel under the counter: coin, a change of shirt, a knife, and the oilcloth bundle he'd carried through seven years of exile without once opening. Then he pulled his hood up, became one more grey-faced nobody in a city full of them, and walked out of Greyhollow by the corpse road, because nobody watched the corpse road. Even the watch had opinions about where they wanted to stand.

Behind him, the deep bell kept ringing. Ahead of him, the hills went up into cloud, and somewhere past the cloud, past the passes, there was the house where he'd been born, and the letters his mother had sealed with wax and a warning:

*Burn these unread, or read them and never sleep well again.*

Maerin had laughed herself sick when she found them. He could still hear it.

* * *

**They found Mother's letters today.**

*Petra is being dramatic about it, which means he's frightened, which means I get to write this down before he burns them out of spite. He thinks I don't know about the box under the floorboards. He thinks I don't know lots of things. He's seventeen and enormous and he still thinks I'm small.*

*I read the first one standing in the pantry with the door shut. Mother's handwriting goes strange halfway through—she must have been writing fast, or crying, or both. Here is what it says, as near as I can hold it:*

*"The storm-line was never meant to be kings. We were meant to be the hinge—the door the old gods used when they still bothered with doors. But doors swing both ways, and what comes up from beneath the mountain learned our names. Vaelric's grandfather offered us a choice, and it was no choice at all: the ledger below, or the headsman above. One soul, freely given, to buy the rest of us our exile. Your father chose Maerin."*

*Me.*

*I put the letter down and made tea and drank all of it and made more. Then I read the second letter, which explains what "the ledger below" means, except it doesn't really explain, it just describes, the way you describe a hole in the world. A court beneath the mountain. Keepers who keep accounts of souls the way merchants keep accounts of grain. Our family has been paying interest on a debt for six generations, and the payment isn't coin.*

*Mother writes: "When the time comes, they will come for her gently. That is the worst part. They are gentle."*

*Petra is hammering on the pantry door now. He wants to burn everything and run for the coast and never look back, and honestly, some days I want to let him. But here is the thing he doesn't understand yet, because he is seventeen and enormous: you cannot run from a debt. You can only decide who pays it.*

*I am going to open the door now and tell him I've read them. Pray for me, whoever finds this. Or pray to the old gods, if any of them are still listening.*

*They say they aren't. They say it's been a hundred years.*

*But last week, when I dreamed, someone answered.*

* * *

The house was exactly as Petra had left it seven years ago, which was to say: ruined, roofless, and stubbornly upright, like an old man refusing to sit down. Moss had taken the kitchen. Birds owned the parlor. In the corner where the floorboards had rotted through, the hiding place stood open and empty, because Maerin had emptied it the day they fled, and Petra had carried the bundle ever since without opening it, because opening it would make it true.

He sat down on the hearthstone, built a small fire—for warmth, not comfort; comfort had left this room years ago—and untied the oilcloth with fingers that shook only a little, which he counted as a victory.

His mother's letters. His sister's handwriting between them, filling the margins, spilling onto scraps, alive in a way that made his chest hurt. He read until the fire burned low and the light through the broken roof went from gold to violet, and he learned what he had spent seven years not knowing on purpose.

One soul, freely given. Six generations of interest. And the final letter, in his mother's hand, dated the winter before the soldiers came:

*If you are reading this, then the choice has come due again, and I am sorry beyond all words that it falls to you. Know only this: we did not sell your sister. We bought you. Every year of your life since has been paid for in the only currency they accept. Do not waste it being small. Do not waste it being safe. And if the old gods wake—if the prayers start landing again—then go down beneath the mountain and bring her home. Debts can be renegotiated. That is the one mercy in a ledger: nothing in it is permanent.*

Petra sat for a long time with the paper trembling in his hands.

Seven years he had told himself he ran from the watch, from Vaelric, from the drowning-weight of Greyhollow's law. Lies, all of it, comfortable lies. He had run from a pantry door and a voice behind it saying *I've read them,* and from the morning after, when the soldiers came gentle, so gentle, and took his sister by the hand like an auntie walking her to school, and Maerin had smiled at him over their shoulders—

She had *smiled.* She had known. She had gone knowing, so that he wouldn't have to.

Grief arrived first, the old familiar wave of it. But underneath the grief, something else was curdling, setting firm, turning from sorrow into structure. Resolve, if he was generous. Spite, if he was honest. In his family, the two had always been difficult to tell apart.

Above the mountains, far to the south, the sky over Vaelric's coronation road glowed faintly red with torches and ambition. Armies marched on the free cities. Kings made bargains of their own. And beneath all of it, patient as interest accruing, the court under the mountain waited with its ledger open.

"Well," Petra said aloud, to the ruined kitchen, to the moss, to the sister-shaped silence. "I hope you kept my seat warm."

The storm under his skin rolled over once, like a dog hearing its name, and for the first time in seven years, he let it rise.

It pointed, unmistakably, toward the mountain.
