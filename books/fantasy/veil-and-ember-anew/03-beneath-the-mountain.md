## 3. Beneath the Mountain

The tithe-bearer's robe itched like a bad decision.

Petra had stolen it from a laundry line outside the mountain's pilgrim gate—stolen being a generous word for what he'd done, which was more accurately *borrowed with intent to never return*—and now he shuffled in a line of forty other gray-hooded figures, each carrying their year's offering down into the dark. Salt. Honey. A lamb that kept giving him accusatory looks. One woman ahead of him carried a jar of pickled eels, which struck Petra as either a profound act of devotion or a personal insult to whatever lived below.

Seven years of selling charms that did nothing, and here he was, about to walk into the one market where everything worked and nothing was for sale.

"Name?" asked the door-warden, a creature that might once have been a man and had since been promoted. Its skin was ledger-paper, faintly ruled with lines, and when it spoke, Petra heard the scratch of a quill.

"Petra Joubert," he said, because lying to a thing made of paperwork seemed unwise.

The warden's paper face rippled. Somewhere behind its eyes, an entry was being made. "Joubert," it repeated, tasting the syllables. "Storm-line."

"I sell buttons," Petra said quickly. "My family sells buttons. Very damp buttons."

The warden stared at him for a long moment—the way a clerk stares at a sum that almost balances—and then waved him through. "The court receives tithes at moonrise. Do not speak to the enthralled. Do not touch the archive. Do not *sign anything*."

"That last one seems oddly specific."

"The last one is the only rule anyone ever breaks." The warden smiled, or rather, a line on its face curved upward like a footnote. "Welcome beneath the mountain, button-seller."

* * *

The underworld court was not what the fireside stories promised. There was no lake of fire, no river of wailing. Instead there was a vast cavern lit by slow amber lamps, filled with terraced gardens of pale mushrooms, and music—actual music, strings and hollow drums, played by musicians whose instruments were grown rather than built. It was, Petra thought with a lurch of professional envy, the best-run night market he had ever seen. Everything gleamed. Nothing haggled. The prices were simply *true*, which was the most terrifying thing about it.

He followed the tithe-line into the great hall, keeping his hood up and his eyes down, doing his very best impression of a man thinking only about salt. Above them, muffled through miles of stone, he could feel it: the coronation feast. Vaelric's trumpets. The usurper had crowned himself at dusk, and even the mountain seemed to flinch at the sound, a low tremor running through the floor like a swallowed word.

And then he saw her.

She stood at the center of the hall, in a alcove shaped like an open book, and she was *reading*. Not a book—she was the book. Light moved across her skin in lines of script, silver and patient, and her lips murmured continuously, reciting debts and bargains and the small print of seven hundred years. Her hair had gone white as vellum. Her hands hovered over a lectern she never touched, because she didn't need to touch it. She *was* it.

Maerin. His sister, who had been seventeen when their mother sold her soul to buy the family's exile instead of their heads. Who should have been thirty-one now, arguing with him about whose turn it was to mind the stall, laughing at his terrible jokes, alive.

"Maerin," he whispered, and broke the first rule before he'd finished learning it.

Her head turned. The script on her skin flickered, rearranged itself, and for one moment her eyes found his through the crowd—not the flat gaze of the enthralled but something sharper, something that recognized him and grieved him and *catalogued* him all at once.

"Petra Joubert," she said, in a voice like pages turning. "Brother. Storm-heir. Button-seller." A pause, and then, with the ghost of her old dry humor surfacing through the ink: "You look terrible. Have you been eating?"

"I—" His throat closed. Seven years of rehearsed speeches, and all he could manage was, "You remember me."

"I'm the archive, Petra. I remember *everything*." She said it without pride. That was the worst part. "I remember your first tooth. I remember Mother's signature. I remember the exact weight of my soul as they weighed it against the bargain." Her eyes went distant, scrolling. "Four ounces lighter than yours, incidentally. You always were the heavier child."

"I've come to take you home."

Something in the hall shifted. The music didn't stop, but it thinned, the way air thins before lightning. Courtiers in robes of woven shadow turned toward them with polite, terrible interest.

"You can't," Maerin said gently. "The ledger doesn't release. It only *trades*. A soul of equal weight, freely given, entered willingly. Those are the terms. They are always the terms."

"Then I'll find another soul. Someone wicked. Vaelric's got plenty to spare, I could—"

"Petra." She said his name the way their mother used to, when he was about to do something spectacularly stupid with a kite in a thunderstorm. "Look at me. Look at what I am. Four ounces. That's all I cost. And you—" the script on her arms brightened, reading him, and her expression cracked into something raw and human and horrified, "—you're marked. The gods touched you. Your name is written in three places already, and none of them are mine. You weigh *kingdoms*, you idiot. You weigh the free cities' whole desperate prayer."

"I don't want kingdoms. I want my sister back."

"And I want you to stop standing in my alcove before the Steward notices, because if the Steward notices, the terms get *renegotiated*, and renegotiation is how souls end up as footnotes—"

"Too late," said a voice like a closing book.

* * *

The Steward was tall in the way that architecture is tall. It wore a robe of black binding-leather and carried no pen, because its right hand *was* a pen, grown elegant and terrible, the nib still wet. It regarded Petra with the mild patience of a creditor who has all the time in the world, which, Petra supposed, it literally did.

"A marked heir walks into my court during a coronation," the Steward said. "How wonderfully predictable. Your kind always comes. The storm-line has never once let a debt sit quietly."

"He's not signing," Maerin said. "I forbid—I *request*—"

"The archive may not request. The archive may only record." The Steward extended its pen-hand toward Petra, courteous as a maître d'. "But let us be precise, since precision is our whole religion. The Joubert bargain holds until replaced. A soul of equal weight, freely given. The storm-heir's name, entered willingly, would settle the account with considerable interest. The archive would be released. Whole. Alive. Free to forget us entirely."

Above them, muffled through stone, Vaelric's trumpets swelled—the coronation feast reaching its height, ten thousand voices cheering a king who had burned the free cities' fields and was marching his armies toward Greyhollow even now. Petra thought of the night market. Of the beggar whose prayer had been answered after seven silent years. Of the old gods stirring, choosing *him*, marking every magic-bearer in the city while the watch lit its torches.

He thought of Maerin at seventeen, saying *take care of the stall, Pet, I'll only be gone a little while.*

"How does it work?" he asked. "The signing."

"No," Maerin breathed.

"Ink," said the Steward, "and consent. That is all it has ever required. Ink and consent."

Maerin lunged against her own bindings—script flaring, the alcove groaning—and for a moment she was just his sister again, furious and terrified and seventeen years too late. "Petra Joubert, if you put that pen anywhere near yourself I will haunt you, I will learn haunting *specifically* to haunt you, I have access to seven centuries of hauntings and I will use *all of them*—"

"You always said I was the heavier child." He took the pen. It was warm, like a hand. "Seems only fair I carry the bigger share."

The nib touched the ledger-page, and the whole mountain held its breath.

He wrote his name—*Petra Joubert*—and felt it leave him the way heat leaves a stone at dusk: slowly, then all at once. The script rose off Maerin's skin like startled birds. The trumpets above cut off mid-note. And deep beneath the mountain, in a hall full of watching shadows, something ancient turned a page and said, in a voice like settling accounts:

"*Entered.*"

Maerin fell forward into his arms, solid and weeping and gloriously mortal—but even as he caught her, even as joy cracked through him like the first thunder of spring, the Steward tilted its head, consulting some inner column, and frowned.

"There is," it said, "a complication."
