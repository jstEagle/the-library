## 4. A Name Given Back

Maerin had spent eleven years as a book, and the strange thing—stranger than the enthralment, stranger than the cold archive-light of the court beneath the mountain—was how much of her was still *her*.

She remembered everything, of course. That had been the point. The court had wanted a living record, a soul to hold every debt and bargain the storm-line ever made, and souls remember whether they want to or not. She remembered her mother's hands kneading bread while reciting the terms of exile. She remembered Petra at nine years old, struck by lightning on a dare from no one but himself, sitting up in the scorched grass with his hair standing like a furious dandelion and announcing, "I've decided I like it."

She remembered the moment he signed himself into the ledger in her place, and she remembered screaming without a throat, because that was what being a book meant: you witnessed, and you could not act.

Except now the ledger lay open on the obsidian floor, and the ink of his name was *running*, bleeding off the page like rain down a window, and the court's gray heralds were backing away from it with their featureless faces tilted in something that could only be called alarm.

"Claim refused," said the ledger itself, in a voice like a door closing in an empty house. "A willing heart is not collateral. A willing heart is *payment*, and payment freely given returns to the giver. The account stands corrected."

"That's not—" one of the heralds began.

"The account," said the ledger, with terrible patience, "stands *corrected*."

And Maerin felt herself poured back into her body like water into a cup left out too long—her body, thin and cold and smelling of stone, but hers, hers, hers—and the first thing she did with it was fall to her knees beside her brother.

He looked asleep. He looked worse than asleep. He looked like a page someone had erased, his face smooth with absence, and when she touched his chest there was no storm under his skin anymore. There was nothing she recognized at all.

"Petra," she said. Her voice cracked like dry paper. "You absolute idiot. You beautiful, stubborn, *idiot*."

The mountain groaned around them. The feast above—the usurper's coronation, all trumpets and stolen gold—had gone silent, or perhaps the whole world had; it was hard to tell when old gods were waking up and arguing about jurisdiction.

Maerin got her shoulder under his arm. He weighed nothing and everything. She hauled him up.

"We're leaving," she told the empty court, because eleven years as an archive had taught her that you should always announce your intentions to rooms full of listening things. "And if any of you have objections, take them up with the ledger. It seems to be feeling generous."

* * *

The way out was longer than the way in, because mercy always is.

Maerin carried him through corridors where the walls remembered her—she felt them lean toward her like old dogs—and past the tithe-bearers who had once been people, who turned their hollow faces to follow her passage. One of them, a woman with river-silt in her hair, reached out and straightened Petra's collar as they passed. A small kindness. Maerin would carry that one too.

"You're heavier than you look," she informed her brother's unconscious form, mostly because talking kept the fear at bay. "All those years selling charms in Greyhollow, eating nothing but market bread and dramatics. Honestly, I expected more of you."

The stair up was black and endless. Somewhere above, thunder rolled—not storm-thunder, not his thunder, but something older, with voices in it. She climbed. Her legs shook. She climbed.

At the mouth of the cave, dawn was breaking over the free cities, and the sky was full of gods.

Not metaphorically. Actually full of them—shapes like weather given opinions, vast and half-seen, turning slowly above the mountains like whales in deep water. And one of them, the nearest, the one whose attention fell on Maerin and her burden like a hand on a scale, spoke directly into her bones:

*We do not accept this trade.*

"He already paid," Maerin said, and her voice didn't shake, which surprised her. "You heard the ledger. Payment freely given returns to the giver. So give him *back*."

*The storm-line was ours to spend,* the god said. *It bargained poorly and hid poorly and died poorly.*

"It lived," Maerin snapped. "It fed beggars. It sold lucky charms to children for a copper and charged rich men triple, which I think shows excellent judgment. It survived seven years in a city that drowns people like him. If that's poor living, then I'd like to see your books, because mine say otherwise."

The god was silent for a long moment. Wind moved across the mountaintop. Below, very far below, she could hear horns—Vaelric's armies, marching on the free cities, thousands upon thousands, certain of victory.

Then the god laughed. It sounded like spring thaw.

*You argue like your mother.*

"I'll take that as a compliment."

*Take your brother,* the god said. *We have rewritten the account. Not erased—rewritten. What he gave cannot be returned unchanged. But names are not the only currency, little archivist. Tell him, when he wakes, that he owes us now. Not blood-owed. Choice-owed. There is a difference, and it is the only difference that has ever mattered.*

Light came down like a decision.

* * *

Petra woke to the smell of rain and his sister's elbow in his ribs.

"—up, get *up*, you dramatic disaster, the healers said you might sleep for a week and I told them you'd never waste a week sleeping when there were people to worry about—"

"Mae?" His voice came out wrong. Thin. Like someone else's. "Where—" He sat up too fast in the narrow cot, in the infirmary of—of the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn, of all places, sunlight pouring through windows painted with saints he'd spent seven years pretending not to believe in. "The ledger. My name. Did I—"

"You did." Maerin pushed him gently back down. Her face was thinner than he remembered, older, marked at the temples with faint silver lines like watermarking—but her eyes were exactly as he remembered, and they were wet, and she was smiling. "And then you got it back. Mostly. The gods intervened. They said—" she paused, savoring it, "—they said a willing heart can't be collateral. You broke their accounting, Petra. Eleven years I was trapped in that place and *you* broke it in one signature."

"My name." He flexed his fingers, waiting for the storm, the crackle under his skin, the thing that had defined and doomed him. It wasn't there. The silence inside him was enormous. "It's gone, isn't it. The storms. All of it."

"Gone." Maerin's smile went soft. "But you didn't come back empty. Feel."

He felt. Underneath the silence, deeper than the storm had ever been, there was something else—a stillness, a weight, like bedrock under soil. When he breathed, the room breathed with him. When he listened, he could hear prayers rising from the city below like heat-shimmer, hundreds of them, small and human and hopeful, and each one landed somewhere in his chest and *stayed*, warm as a coal.

"Oh," he said quietly. "Oh, that's going to take some getting used to."

"There's more." Maerin pulled a chair over and sat, taking his hand like she had when they were children and he'd had nightmares about drowning. "Vaelric's army reached the walls three days ago. Forty thousand men. Siege engines. The works. And last night—" her grin turned wicked, "—last night they broke. Not fought. *Broke*. The soldiers woke up dreaming of their grandmothers' gods, the ones Vaelric banned. Whole regiments knelt down in the mud and started praying, and when the officers tried to stop them, the officers joined in. Vaelric's own guard walked out at midnight singing hymns in Old Tarnish. He's fleeing north with maybe two hundred men and a very confused cook."

Petra stared at her. Then he started laughing—that helpless, wheezing laugh she remembered from childhood, the one that got him struck by lightning in the first place, probably, because the gods had always liked him best mid-laugh.

"They defected," he managed. "Forty thousand soldiers defected because of *dreams*."

"Dreams, and the fact that the sky over the free cities has been full of visible gods for three days, and the fact that every prayer spoken here gets answered within the hour. It's very hard to besiege a city when the city's god keeps turning your arrows into fishing rods. The fisherfolk are thrilled, by the way. Best season in decades."

Petra lay back against the pillows, laughing and crying in roughly equal measure, holding his sister's hand.

"So that's it," he said finally. "The storm-line ends. No heir, no crown, no magic anyone can conscript. Just—" he pressed a hand to his chest, to the quiet coal-glow of other people's hopes, "—just this."

"Just this," Maerin agreed. "Which, according to several newly returned deities, is worth considerably more. Oh—and there's a delegation downstairs. The free cities sent one. They want to talk to 'the god-touched mortal.' They've been waiting two days. I may have let them think you were communing."

"Mae."

"They brought *gifts*, Petra. There's a wheel of cheese the size of a shield."

He sighed, sat up, and swung his legs over the side of the cot—no storm in his veins, no name in any ledger, just a body, a sister, and a city full of prayers waiting to be answered.

"Well," said the last heir of the storm-line, reaching for his boots. "Best not keep them waiting, then."
