## 1. The Night the Stars Went Out

The smuggler's memory tasted of wet copper and river mud, the way all lowland memories did. Cyrus Amberdale knelt in the reeds with two fingers pressed to the man's temple and let his past pour through her like water through a cracked jug.

His name was Doss. He'd been born in a house that no longer existed, on a street he couldn't find again if you paid him. He had a daughter somewhere north whose face he could only summon when drunk. He was carrying a satchel of ember-glass to a buyer in Hollowmere, and he was thinking — this very moment, as Cyrus crouched over him in the dark — about how much easier his life would be without a conscience.

She took what she needed and nothing more. A memory of the buyer's face. The location of the drop. The particular way his fingers trembled when he lied, which she filed away for later, because it might be useful, because taking things was what she did.

Cyrus Amberdale, memory-thief of Thornfell, lifted her hand and the man slumped gently into the reeds, snoring. He would wake with an hour missing and a headache like a bell rung inside his skull, and he would never know what she'd carried away. That was the craft of it. The art. You didn't rip memory out; you *unhooked* it, the way you'd lift a ring from a sleeping hand. Clean work. Honest theft, if such a thing existed.

She sat back on her heels and looked up to check the sky, as she always did after a lifting. Old habit. Her mother had taught her that the stars were the realm's first witnesses — every oath sworn beneath them, every border marked by their turning. *If the stars ever go out, little thief,* her mother used to say, half-laughing, *then Thornfell itself has forgotten where it ends.*

Cyrus looked up.

And the sky was empty.

Not clouded. Not veiled. *Empty.* She knew clouds; she'd stolen memories from weather-witches who could taste a storm three days off. Clouds had weight, texture, the grey suggestion of something behind them. This was different. This was as though someone had reached up and taken the stars the way she took memories — cleanly, completely, leaving no hole because there was no longer anything to be a hole *in*.

The dark above her was so total it made sound. It pressed down on the marshland with a low, woolen hush, swallowing the croak of frogs mid-note. Somewhere across the flats, a dog began to howl, then stopped, as if it too had looked up and understood.

"No," Cyrus said aloud, to no one, because saying things aloud made them less true, and this thing needed desperately to be untrue.

Her hands were shaking. She noticed them the way you notice a stranger's hands — from a slight distance, with mild embarrassment. A memory-thief who feared the dark was a joke the gods would appreciate. But this wasn't dark. Dark was the absence of light. This was the absence of *having ever been lit*.

In the reeds, Doss snored on, blissfully ignorant, and Cyrus envied him with a fierceness that surprised her.

She should run. Every instinct honed in fourteen years of thieving said run: get to the low road, get ahead of whatever this was, be somewhere else when it finished happening. But her feet wouldn't move. Her mother's voice was in her ears again, and beneath it another voice, older, from a night she'd never quite managed to steal back from herself — the night the wards of Thornfell had first been raised, an event no living person remembered and yet everyone somehow knew, the way you know your own heartbeat.

*If the stars go out.*

She fumbled for her pack. Her fingers found the buckle by touch alone, working blind in a blackness that had swallowed even the faint phosphor-glow of the marsh fungus. Inside, her kit lay where it always lay: the slender silver hooks, the vials in their padded leather roll, each one stoppered and labeled in her own cramped hand. Memories she'd lifted and hadn't yet sold. A bride's wedding day. A hangman's last execution. A child's imaginary friend, rendered in amber light.

Her thumb moved along the row, counting by feel. One. Two. Three. Four.

Five.

There were four vials in her kit. There had been four vials in her kit since Hollowbridge, since she'd sold the fifth to the collector with the ink-stained fingers. She counted again, slower, pressing each glass cylinder between finger and thumb like a rosary.

Five.

The fifth vial was warm. That was the first wrongness — memories went cool in storage, settling like wine. This one was warm as a held hand, and when she drew it out into the starless dark, it *glowed*. Faintly. A deep red-gold shimmer, like coals seen through a shuttered window, like the last inch of candle before dawn.

Cyrus turned it over. No label. No wax seal. The glass was older than her other vials — hand-blown, slightly uneven, with a seam where a bubble had risen and frozen mid-flight. Someone had made this vessel with care, or with fear, or with both.

She should not open it.

Every rule of her trade said so. Unlabeled memory was unowned memory, and unowned memory in Thornfell belonged to whoever was strong enough to hold it — but unlabeled memory was also, very often, *dangerous* memory. Minds broke on things they weren't shaped to carry. She'd seen a fence in Grayharbor crack a vial of a soldier's final battle and spend the rest of his life flinching at the smell of bread.

But the stars were gone. And the vial was warm. And curiosity was the one hunger Cyrus Amberdale had never learned to steal enough food for.

She worked the stopper loose with her thumbnail.

The memory rose out of the glass like heat-shimmer off summer stone, and poured into her.

---

*A mountain, first. A mountain like a closed fist, black against a burning sky, and she is not herself — she is someone else, someone tall, someone standing on a ridge of frozen scree with wind screaming past — and below, in the mountain's open heart, there is fire.*

*Not forge-fire. Not wildfire. Something older. Fire that thinks.*

*And in the fire — rising out of it, unfolding like a banner, like a sail, like a prayer answered after ten thousand years of silence — wings.*

*Vast wings. Wings that span the caldera. Scales the color of banked embers, of iron cooling, of the exact shade the sky turns at the end of the world. The dragon lifts its head and its eyes are twin furnaces, and when it looks at the person whose eyes Cyrus is borrowing, it does not look with menace. It looks with* recognition. *With relief, almost. With the terrible tenderness of something that has waited a very long time to be seen.*

*Its mouth opens, and the voice is not sound but warmth, pouring through the memory like sunlight through honey:*

***"You came. After everything — you came."***

*And the person whose memory this is begins to weep, and Cyrus weeps with them, because the dragon's face is the most beautiful thing she has ever seen, and also the most dying. Even in the memory — even here, even now — she can see it. The fire behind those furnace-eyes is guttering. The great wings tremble at their joints like an old woman's hands. The dragon is magnificent the way a collapsing star is magnificent: radiantly, unmistakably, in the act of going out.*

---

Cyrus came back to herself on her knees in the reeds, gasping, tears freezing on her cheeks in the strange new cold. The vial lay empty in her palm, its glow gone, its glass suddenly ordinary and dead.

Above her, the starless dark pressed closer.

*The last dragon.* She didn't know how she knew the words fit, but they fit the way a key fits a lock it was cut for. The last dragon, whose fire — whose fire *what?* The memory ended too soon, cut short as though the archivist — for surely this had belonged to an archivist, no smuggler carried glasswork that fine — as though whoever had held it had been interrupted. Had been running. Had died clutching it, maybe, somewhere between the mountain and here.

And it was in *her pack*.

That thought arrived slowly, the way ice arrives on a pond, and when it fully landed Cyrus stopped breathing. She had packed this kit herself at Hollowbridge. She had counted her vials. Four. She remembered counting four the way she remembered her own name, and her own name was the one thing she had never once managed to steal from herself.

Someone had put this vial in her pack. Someone who knew where she'd be tonight, on this stretch of marsh road, lifting a memory from a smuggler named Doss. Someone who wanted it carried — or wanted *her* to carry it.

Or someone who had been trying to get it away from somewhere else, and had chosen her the way you choose a fast horse in a burning stable: not because you love the horse, but because it runs.

Across the flats, far to the north, a light flickered that was not lightning and not fire and not any color Cyrus had a name for. It pulsed once, twice, and went out. Where it had been, she felt a shape in her mind — a *gap*, the way you feel a missing tooth — as though something that had been part of the world simply wasn't anymore.

The wards. Her mother's voice again, thinner now, frightened now: *the border wards, little thief, the wards that hold us in and hold the rest out—*

They were falling. All of them. Whatever fed them — whatever the dragon's fire had sustained, all these centuries, quietly, the way a heart sustains a body without anyone thanking it — was failing. And the realm of Thornfell, which had stood for a thousand years on foundations no one remembered laying, had begun, tonight, under this starless sky, to forget itself.

Doss stirred in the reeds and mumbled his daughter's name, and even that small sound was a comfort — proof that some things still remembered themselves.

Cyrus stood. She slid the empty vial into her kit, next to the four labeled ones, and her hand lingered on the leather roll. In the morning she would sell Doss's secret to the Hollowmere buyer and be gone before noon, because that was the plan, because plans were how thieves stayed alive.

But plans, she thought, looking north toward the pulsing dark where a mountain waited like a closed fist around a dying flame, were for people whose worlds still had edges.

Somewhere out there was an archivist's corpse. Somewhere out there was the answer to who had planted a dragon's face in her pack. And somewhere out there, a fire that thought was guttering down to nothing, waiting — with terrible tenderness — for someone to come.

Cyrus pulled her hood up against a cold that had no business existing in high summer, and started walking north.

Behind her, in the reeds, Doss slept on, dreaming of a house that no longer existed, on a street that was already forgetting where it had been.
