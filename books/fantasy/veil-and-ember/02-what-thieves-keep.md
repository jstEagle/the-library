## 2. What Thieves Keep

The dead man had been dead for three days before the crows found him, and the crows had been at him long enough to make an argument of his face.

Cyrus knelt beside what remained in the ditch off the Ashway, her breath fogging in air that had no business being this cold in high summer. The stars were still gone. Three nights now, and the sky above Thornfell was a smooth black lid, like the inside of a closed coffin, and every traveler on the road spoke of it in whispers as though the dark might hear them and take offense.

She hadn't come for the body. She'd come because the vial in her pack had grown warm again — that faint, wrong heat against her hip, like a sleeping animal — and warmth meant proximity, and proximity meant answers.

The corpse wore archivist's grey, or what was left of it. The robes were fine wool once, Court-cut, hemmed with the silver thread that only the Ember Chancery could afford. His hands — she made herself look, made herself be thorough, because thoroughness kept thieves alive — his hands were curled around something even in death. Fingers locked. Claws of a man who had held on past the holding.

A leather case, split open. Empty except for a wax impression of a seal she didn't recognize and a scrap of vellum, crow-pecked, with writing gone brown at the edges.

*If the wards fail, scatter them. No single hand may hold the founding. Trust no—*

The rest was gone into a crow's belly somewhere.

Cyrus sat back on her heels and looked at the man's ruined face and felt the cold go through her coat and into the marrow beneath.

"Scatter *what*," she said quietly. The dark didn't answer. It never did anymore.

* * *

She knew how to read a dead man's pockets without waking him. That was the trade, more or less — lifting memories from the living was just pocket-picking with extra steps, and Cyrus Amberdale had been picking pockets since she was small enough to fit inside one. But the dead were different. A living mind gave its memories up grudgingly, all friction and flinch; a dead mind gave them up like water from a cracked jug, everything sloshing out at once, unsorted, unguarded.

It was also, depending on which priest you asked, a sin, a desecration, or a felony under Chancery statute seven. Cyrus had stopped consulting priests years ago.

She pulled off her left glove, pressed her bare palm to the archivist's brow, and opened herself the way her mother had taught her — not reaching, just *receiving*, a door unlatched in a wind.

The memories came in a flood, cold and brackish.

*A corridor of black basalt lit by ember-lamps. The smell of hot iron and old paper. Rows upon rows of shelves, and on the shelves — not books. Vials. Thousands of them, each glowing faintly, each labeled in a hand so precise it looked printed:*

*THE FOUNDING OATH OF ALDERMERE. THE FIRST WINTER AND HOW IT WAS SURVIVED. THE NAMING OF THE THORNFELL WARDS. THE DRAGON'S COVENANT.*

*An old man's voice, dry as chalk: "They are not records, child. They are load-bearing walls."*

*And then — chaos. Bells. The floor shuddering. Lamps guttering from red to nothing. Shouts in the stacks: "The wards are down — the wards are DOWN — Ashgrave's orders, take your allotment and RUN—" Hands grabbing vials by the dozen, stuffing them into cases, into coats, into mouths of hidden doors. And through it all, the archivist — Maesin Corvel, the name surfaced like a drowned thing — clutching one vial tighter than the rest, the warm one, the dragon one, running not toward the gates but away from them, down, down into the lowlands, pursued by—*

Cyrus wrenched her hand back.

She was on her knees in the ditch, gasping, her own heart slamming like a fist on a door, and the vial in her pack was burning now, actually warm through the leather, and she understood three things with terrible clarity.

One: the Ember Court had emptied its own archive the night the stars went out.

Two: they had scattered the realm's founding memories across Thornfell like a gambler scattering coins — deliberately, on someone's order, on *Ashgrave's* order.

Three: the man who died in this ditch had been running *from* his own people, carrying the most important memory of all, and whatever had caught him had not been crows.

She looked at the wounds then, properly, forcing herself past the crow-work. Beneath the scavenging there were other marks. Small ones. Neat ones. A ring of punctures at the throat, close-set, almost delicate. And burned into the dead flesh of his palm — a brand, fresh, deliberate: a stylized flame within a circle.

The mark of the Ember Court's hunters. The *pyrelings*. Cyrus had seen it once before, on a fence in Lowmarsh who'd talked too much about Court business, and she had never seen the fence again either.

"They killed their own archivist," she said to the empty sky. "To keep him from coming back."

The sky, starless, said nothing, but the wind moved through the ditch grass with a sound like paper turning, like pages being torn out one by one.

* * *

She walked until dusk, following the Ashway north because north was where the vial pulled, a compass needle of warmth against her side. Around her, the country was doing things country shouldn't do.

A milestone read *Hollowmere, 6 miles* going one direction and *Hollowmere, 11 miles* going the other. A farmhouse stood with its door open and its table set for supper, plates full, chairs pushed back, and no one anywhere — not fled, not buried, just *absent*, the way a word goes absent when you've said it too many times. In the village of Bracken Row she'd passed through that morning, half the people she'd greeted had paused when she asked the day of the week, their faces going smooth and blank as eggs, and one woman had smiled at her with perfect courtesy and said, "I had a daughter. I'm nearly sure I had a daughter."

Cyrus was a thief. She had spent her whole life taking things that weren't hers and telling herself the world wouldn't miss them. But this — this was theft on a scale that made her feel like a pickpocket watching a city burn. Someone wasn't stealing from the realm. Someone was stealing *the realm*, memory by memory, name by name, star by star, and leaving the shapes of things behind like clothes with no one in them.

And she was carrying a piece of it in her pack.

At dusk she made camp in the shell of a wayshrine, one of the old roadside chapels from before the Court, its saint long since chiseled off its plinth. She built a small fire — small, screened, the habit of a lifetime — boiled nettle tea, and took out the vial at last.

Up close, in firelight, it was beautiful the way dangerous things are beautiful. Dark glass, stoppered with wax stamped by a seal she now recognized from the dead man's case: the Chancery archive. Inside, the memory didn't sit still the way stolen memories usually did. It *moved* — a slow coiling glow, gold shot through with deep red, like embers breathing. Like a lung. Like something alive.

Every thief knew what memories looked like. Grey-silver, mostly. Blue for griefs. This was neither.

"This is a piece of a dragon," she said aloud, testing the words. They sounded insane. They sounded true.

Because she'd lifted enough minds to know the texture of what she carried, and this wasn't a *memory of* something. It was a memory *from* something. When she let her senses brush the glass — lightly, the way you'd test a wire — she felt vastness. Wings like weather fronts. Fire older than the borders it warmed. A patience measured in centuries, and threaded through all of it, fading but unmistakable:

Pain. The slow, grinding pain of a flame burning down.

The last dragon was dying. And the Court had taken the memories that anchored it — the founding oaths, the covenant, the naming of the wards — and scattered them to the four winds, and the realm was coming apart at the seams because the seams themselves had been cut loose.

Cyrus set the vial down on the plinth and stared at it for a long time.

Here was the thing about responsibility: it always arrived dressed as someone else's problem. She could walk away. South, maybe, over the Saltmarsh causeway, out of Thornfell entirely, sell the vial to some collector in Vell who'd never know what he was buying, and be rich, and be gone, and let the country finish dissolving around whoever stayed. It was what the old Cyrus would do. The old Cyrus had a whole philosophy about it — *carry nothing you can't drop* — stitched inside her coat like a second lining.

But the old Cyrus had never held a dying country in her hand and felt it *hurt*.

She thought of the woman in Bracken Row. *I had a daughter. I'm nearly sure.* Thought of the milestone lying about distances. Thought of the stars, and how she hadn't realized until they were gone that she'd navigated her whole life by them — every smuggler's road, every midnight crossing, every promise of *meet me where the stars say so* — and how a person could lose a thing so large they forgot it was a thing at all, until the night it went out.

"Damn it," Cyrus said, with feeling, to the empty shrine. "Damn it, Corvel. You should have run faster. You should have run slower. You should have told someone *where*."

But he had told her the only way a dead man can: by dying in her path, warm vial to cold ground, and letting a thief's instincts do the rest.

She banked the fire, wrapped the vial back in its wool, and settled against the plinth with her knife loose in its sheath and her mind open the way she never slept with it open — listening down the dark road for the particular silence that meant hunters.

She must have drifted, because the sound woke her all at once: hoofbeats on the Ashway, several horses, moving fast and not caring who heard. And beneath the hoofbeats, threaded through the night like smoke through a keyhole, came another sound — voices, human voices, raised in proclamation, being shouted from horseback at every farmhouse and crossroads for miles:

*"By decree of the Ember Court! A bounty of five hundred crowns, alive and intact, for the thief who holds the fire! Five hundred crowns for the thief who holds the fire! Harbor her and burn with her!"*

Cyrus lay flat in the shrine's shadow, heart drumming, and watched torchlight swing up the road toward her like a slow orange eye.

*Her.* Not *him*. Not *them*. The Court knew exactly what Corvel had carried, and they knew — or guessed — whose hands it had fallen into. Which meant somewhere behind those torches was someone who could see through the dark where the stars used to be, and what they saw was a wayshrine, a dead fire, and a thief with a country in her pack.

Five hundred crowns. Enough to vanish forever. Enough to buy a hundred lives like hers.

Cyrus picked up the vial. It pulsed against her palm, warm and stubborn and alive, like a small heart refusing to stop.

"Well," she murmured, shouldering her pack and slipping out the shrine's broken back wall into the bracken, "let's see if I'm worth the ink."

Behind her, the torchlight reached the wayshrine and found it empty. Ahead of her, the road north ran black and starless toward the mountains — and toward the pass at Grayfell, where, though she couldn't have known it yet, someone else was already waiting.
