## 3. The Warder on the Pass

The smuggler's road through the Greymarsh Pass was the kind of road that existed only because enough people had needed it to exist — a thread of packed earth and old cart-grease winding between cliffs that smelled of wet iron and rotting heather. Cyrus Amberdale knew it the way she knew most of Thornfell's hidden veins: not from maps, but from other people's heads. A dead courier's memory of this pass, lifted in a tavern in Lowmark three years ago, still walked her along its switchbacks whenever she chose to walk them.

She was choosing them now because every honest road had stopped making sense.

Since the night the stars went out, the world had been misbehaving. Distances lied. She had left a village at dawn and arrived back at it by noon without ever turning around. Twice she had crossed a stream she'd already crossed. And her pack felt heavier than it should — not with the vials she carried, but with one vial in particular, the stranger's, the one holding a dragon's face and a heat that pressed against her spine like a hand.

So she took the dishonest roads, the remembered ones, and for two days they held.

On the third day, at the narrowest point of the pass where the cliff leaned close enough to touch on both sides, someone said her name out loud.

"Cyrus Amberdale."

Her name spoken aloud was a rare and ugly thing. Thieves didn't use names; names were hooks. She froze mid-stride, one boot on a slick of black stone, and did what she always did when a hook came flying: she reached into the speaker's mind before the speaker could reach into anything else.

And found nothing.

Not an empty mind — those she knew, hollow as cracked bells. This was a *walled* mind. Smooth grey stone laid course over course, no windows, no doors, no seams. She had never touched anything like it. It wasn't silence. It was architecture.

"Your trick doesn't work on warders," the voice said, closer now, from above. "We're trained against it before we're trained to ride."

Cyrus looked up. The cliff face, which had been empty a heartbeat ago, held a figure descending it like the rock had grown tired of holding her and let go slowly — a woman in dark leathers the color of banked coals, silver-grey cloak shedding drizzle, boots finding holds Cyrus couldn't even see. She landed ten feet away without sound, and when she straightened, the hood fell back from a face that was all clean angles and watchful dark eyes, a thin scar crossing one brow like a signature.

In her hand was a blade of pale metal that drank the grey light rather than reflecting it.

"The Ember Court," the woman said, "wants you alive."

"Everyone wants something," Cyrus said. Her heart was hammering but her voice stayed flat; she'd spent years teaching it to. "Alive is at least more negotiable than dead."

"It usually is." The warder circled her slowly, and Cyrus turned with her, keeping her eyes, keeping her balance on the treacherous stone. "Cyrus Amberdale. Memory-thief. Eleven confirmed extractions from protected persons. Fourteen unconfirmed. Wanted in six provinces." A pause. "Those numbers are wrong now, aren't they? Since the wards fell, half your record has simply... stopped being true."

"I wouldn't know. I don't read my own press."

"You do worse. You steal it." The warder's eyes flicked to the pack. "You're carrying something that belongs to the realm. Something that was in the Court's archive the night everything went dark. I'm Serah Vey," she added, almost as an afterthought, as if names were currency she spent reluctantly. "Warder of the Third Veil. And I am supposed to put these on you." From her belt she drew manacles of the same light-drinking metal, and held them up between two fingers, letting them turn slowly. "That's my oath. That's my order. Signed and sealed."

"And yet," Cyrus said, "you haven't."

* * *

Serah Vey had arrested forty-one people in nine years of service, and every arrest had gone the same way: the charge, the irons, the road back, the report filed in a hand so neat her superiors used it as an example. She had never once hesitated, and she had told herself the absence of hesitation was the same thing as certainty.

She was hesitating now, standing in the rain in the Greymarsh Pass with manacles in her hand, and the reason was a memory she shouldn't have.

Three nights ago, before she'd ever heard Cyrus Amberdale's name spoken outside a warrant, Serah had dreamed of fire. Not the Court's fire — the siphon-fires beneath the citadel, which every warder was shown at induction, great chained flames feeding copper throats that ran up into the Chancellery. This fire had been free. It had had a shape. Wings like storm-fronts, eyes like the last coal of a dying hearth, and a voice that hadn't spoken so much as *burned*, one word seared across the inside of her skull:

*Find the thief.*

She had woken with the smell of smoke in her hair and her oath feeling, for the first time, like clothing that no longer fit — still covering her, still respectable, but tight in places she hadn't known she had.

Then the orders came down: the thief who held the fire was moving north through Thornfell. Bring her in. Alive. Unquestioned. Deliver her and the artifact directly to Lord Chancellor Ashgrave, who would handle the rest personally.

Unquestioned. Serah had read the order four times, and each time the word *unquestioned* sat wronger on her tongue. Warders questioned everyone. Questioning was the whole of their craft. An order not to ask questions was an order to stop being a warder while remaining one.

And so here she was, on a smuggler's road, having tracked a criminal for two days through a country whose stars had vanished, and instead of closing the manacles she heard herself say:

"Why did the wards fail?"

Cyrus blinked. Rain dripped off her chin. "I was going to ask you that."

"No. You were going to run. There's a goat-track behind you that only exists because a dead shepherd remembers it, and you were already reaching for it." Serah kept the blade low, casual, though nothing about her was casual. "I want to know why the stars went out. I want to know why villages are forgetting their own children. And my order says I'm not allowed to wonder about any of it." Her jaw tightened. "So here is what I offer instead of an arrest. Help me find the truth of what happened. When we have it — if we have it — you walk free. My report will say I lost you in the marshes. I'm apparently very bad at my job."

"That's a generous lie to tell for a stranger."

"You're not a stranger. You're a warrant with legs." But something moved behind the warder's eyes then, quick and unwilling, like a fish under ice. "And I dreamed about a dragon three nights ago, which means either I've gone mad, or whatever you stole knows my name. I'd like to find out which before I spend the rest of my life pretending the sky isn't broken."

Cyrus studied her the way she studied locks: looking for the flaw, the give, the place where pressure would open things. She found none. The woman meant it. That was somehow worse.

"If I say no?"

"Then I arrest you, and Ashgrave handles the rest personally, and neither of us ever learns anything again." Serah extended her free hand, palm up, rain pooling in it. "I'm offering you the only thing I have that's worth anything, thief. My questions. Take them or take the irons."

* * *

They made camp an hour later in the mouth of a cave the shepherd's ghost remembered, and Cyrus built the fire herself, small and shielded, out of habits older than her thievery. The rain thickened to a hiss beyond the overhang. Somewhere far off, thunder rolled with no lightning to explain it — the sky had been doing that since the stars died, grumbling like a dog that had lost its master.

Serah sat across the flames with her sword across her knees and her manacles clipped to her belt, unused, and ate hardtack with the grim efficiency of someone fueling a machine. Cyrus watched her over her own ration and tried to fit her into a category. Court hound. Oath-sworn tool. Beautiful, said some traitorous part of her brain, cataloguing the scarred brow and the rain-dark hair and the way firelight caught the edge of her cheekbone, and Cyrus told that part of her brain to shut up, and it mostly did.

"So," Cyrus said. "Third Veil. What does that mean? Besides sounding expensive."

"It means I guard memories instead of places. The Court keeps the realm's records — births, borders, oaths, debts. Everything Thornfell is made of, written down and warded." Serah turned her tack over in her fingers, not eating now. "Or it did. Three nights ago the deep archive went dark. Not locked — *dark*. Like the shelves had never been full. My superiors called it a theft." Her eyes came up, sharp. "Your kind of theft."

"I've never been within fifty miles of your citadel."

"I believe you. That's the problem." Serah set the tack down. "Because if you didn't take the founding memories, and I certainly didn't, then someone inside did. Someone with the seals. Someone who wanted the realm's past scattered on the same night its border wards collapsed." She said it evenly, but her hands had gone still in her lap, and Cyrus recognized the posture — it was how people sat when the ground had just shifted under a belief they'd stood on all their lives.

"You're accusing your own Court."

"I'm asking a question my order forbids me to ask. You'll notice I'm getting better at it." A ghost of a smile crossed her face and was gone. "Why did you become a thief, Amberdale? Truly. Not the warrant version."

Cyrus considered lying. Lying was cheaper and safer and she was very good at it. But the fire was warm, and the country was coming apart, and there was something about a person who had just committed treason against her own conscience in the rain that made lies feel like a waste of good darkness.

"Because everyone forgets," she said. "Badly. On purpose. They bury the things that hurt and the things they loved right alongside each other, and then they walk around as half-people calling themselves whole. I lift what they buried. Sometimes I sell it. Sometimes—" she shrugged, watching the fire, "—sometimes I just give it back to them when they've forgotten too much. Someone has to remember for this country. Apparently that job just got bigger."

Serah was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice had dropped, gone careful. "Show me the vial."

Cyrus's hand went to her pack before she could decide whether to refuse. That was the trouble with warders — they asked questions like blades sliding between ribs, precise, unavoidable. She drew out the stranger's vial and held it up between them, and the firelight entered the glass and did not come out again.

Inside, faint as breath on a window, the dragon stirred. Heat rolled off the little vessel in waves that had no business existing, and both of them leaned toward it without meaning to, the way people lean toward hearths.

"She's dying," Serah whispered. All the warder's walls were gone from her face; what remained underneath was younger than Cyrus expected, and stricken. "Whatever she is — she's *dying*, and the realm is dying with her, and my Court sent me to arrest the only person carrying proof." She looked up, and the firelight and the vial-light mixed in her dark eyes. "Ashgrave wants you unquestioned, Cyrus. Now I understand why. Questions are the one weapon he can't take back."

Thunder rolled again, closer. And beneath it — so faint that later Cyrus would convince herself she'd imagined it — came a second sound, low and vast and ragged, rising from the north, from the mountains.

A breath. The whole horizon taking a breath.

Serah's head came up. "Tell me you heard that."

Cyrus had heard it. And in her pack, warm against her hip, the vial pulsed once — twice — like a heart remembering its rhythm, pointing north.

"We walk tonight," Cyrus said.
