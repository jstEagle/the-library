## 4. A Country Unremembering

Serah Vey had walked into a hundred towns on the Court's business — arrests, interrogations, the quiet collection of debts owed to power — and she had never once been afraid of them. Towns were predictable. Towns were ledgers: names, taxes, grievances, gossip. You could read a town the way you read a warrant.

Hollowmere refused to be read.

They came down off the pass at dusk, three days north of where Cyrus had heard the horizon breathe, and the first wrongness was the smoke. Chimneys breathed it, hearths burned it, but no one was cooking. The smell of bread and stew that should have hung over any village at evening simply wasn't there. Instead the air tasted of cold ash and river water, as though the whole place had been doused and left to smolder.

The second wrongness was the dog. It trotted up to them in the road, tail low, and looked at Serah with an expression she had only ever seen on animals whose masters had died — patient, puzzled, waiting for someone to come home.

"No one's tending it," Cyrus murmured.

"Maybe they're all at some gathering."

"At dusk? With no supper on?"

Serah didn't answer. She was looking at the well at the center of the village green, where a woman stood with a bucket rope wound around her hands, staring down into the dark water as if she had forgotten what wells were for. As if she had forgotten why she'd come.

"Hail," Serah called, and put the authority of the Ember Court into her voice, the polished brass of it. "I'm Warder Vey, in service of the Court. I'd ask some questions of the town."

The woman turned. Her face was smooth and open and utterly vacant, like a window with the shutters closed behind the glass. She looked at Serah for a long moment, and then her brow creased with genuine effort, the way a child's does when asked to multiply numbers too large.

"I had a name," the woman said. "This morning I had a name. It began—" Her mouth worked. "It began with a sound."

"What sound?" Cyrus asked gently.

The woman's eyes filled with tears of pure frustration. "A warm one," she whispered. "It was a warm sound."

* * *

They went door to door because there was nothing else to do, and because Serah's oath demanded questions even when the answers were ash. What she found in Hollowmere rearranged something fundamental in her chest, some load-bearing wall of certainty, and left the structure standing but groaning.

An old man sat by his fire whittling a stick into nothing, shaving away shavings. He could not tell her his name. He could tell her, in exhaustive detail, how to cure a hide, how to read weather off a cloud's belly, how to graft apple stock — everything he had ever *done* — but when she asked who had taught him, who his father was, where he had been born, he smiled apologetically and said, "That's not mine anymore, miss. That's gone out of me like heat out of a stone."

In the tavern, a dozen villagers sat around tables with full mugs nobody drank. A girl of perhaps ten played with wooden beads, arranging them in patterns and forgetting each pattern before it finished. When Serah knelt beside her and asked her name, the girl said, "Mama calls me something. Mama would know." And then, after a pause, in a small voice with terrible clarity: "Is my mama still my mama if she can't remember me?"

Serah had no answer. She was beginning to suspect no one did.

Cyrus worked differently. The thief moved through the tavern like smoke through a keyhole, touching shoulders, catching eyes, and Serah realized with a lurch that she was *lifting* — pulling memories out of these broken people the way you'd draw splinters. She came back to Serah's table pale and shaking.

"It's not damage," Cyrus said quietly, sitting down across from her. "Do you understand? I've handled damaged minds. Wiped ones, addled ones, minds burned out by botched extraction. This isn't that. Their memories aren't destroyed. They're *gone*. Taken whole, cleanly, professionally — the way I'd take one. Someone reached into an entire town and lifted its past."

"That's impossible. No thief alive has that reach."

"No thief." Cyrus set something on the table between them: a scrap of memory, thin as breath, which she'd drawn from the old man by the fire. "Look. He remembers his wedding day — but only the feeling of it, the warmth, the shape. The content's been cut out and what's left is the scar tissue. Look at the edges of the cut."

Serah looked. She was no memory-thief, but she had trained under Court archivists; she knew the anatomy of recollection the way a swordsman knows anatomy of flesh. And she saw it — saw the clean severance, the precise cauterized edge of an extraction performed with instruments she had only ever seen in one place.

Her stomach dropped through the floor of the world.

"Cyrus," she said, and her voice came out strange, flattened, "we need to see the bridge."

* * *

The bridge over the Hollowmere river had stood, according to every map Serah had ever memorized, for four hundred years. Grey stone, five arches, moss on the northern parapet. She had crossed worse roads to inspect less important crossings.

What they found instead was a gap.

Not a ruin. Not a collapsed span. A *gap* — a place in the road where the ground simply declined to have a bridge, the banks dropping sheer to the water as though the river had always run through a gorge there, as though the gorge had always been the truth of the land. The road ran up to the edge and stopped, cleanly, the way a sentence stops when the rest of the page has been torn away.

Except.

"There," Cyrus said, pointing.

On the far bank, the road resumed. And between the two severed ends, faint as breath on glass, stretched the ghost of the bridge — arches like heat-shimmer, parapets like the memory of a line drawn in water. It flickered. It wavered. And as Serah watched, an old woman came hobbling down the far lane, stopped at the edge of the gap, frowned at the crossing, and *saw* it — Serah watched the shimmer solidify under the weight of the woman's attention, stone gathering itself out of suggestion, until the bridge stood whole enough to bear her steps, and she crossed it muttering to herself about her knees, and the moment she reached the other side and stopped thinking about the bridge, it thinned again like ice in spring.

"The maps say four hundred years," Serah said. Her voice sounded very far away to herself. "Four hundred years of cartographers. Of toll records. Of people crossing it every day of their lives. A thing doesn't stop existing because—"

"Because nothing is remembering it hard enough." Cyrus crouched at the edge of the gap, and Serah saw her do something she hadn't seen before: hold one hand palm-down over the ghost-arches, fingers spread, like a diviner reading water. "The realm isn't being attacked, Serah. Attack leaves wreckage. There's no wreckage here. This is *erasure*. Someone is unmaking Thornfell from the past forward — pulling the founding memories out first, the anchor-stones, and everything built on them is starting to drift."

"Anchor-points," Serah repeated. The word arrived in her mouth from somewhere else, from a briefing she half-remembered, from a seal she had seen on documents she was never cleared to read.

"The vials." Cyrus pulled her pack around, drew out the stolen vial with the dragon's face coiled inside it, and held it up to the last light. Within the glass, the memory turned slowly, and where its glow fell, the ghost-bridge *strengthened* — for one heartbeat the arches rang solid, real, four hundred years deep. Then Cyrus lowered the vial and the bridge sighed back toward nothing. "The founding memories are the nails holding the country together. Scatter them, and the country comes apart along its own seams. Roads circle. Names slip. Bridges exist only where someone loves them enough."

"And the people?" Serah thought of the girl with the beads. *Is my mama still my mama.* "Why take theirs?"

"Collateral. Or fuel. Or—" Cyrus stopped. She was looking at Serah with an expression Serah couldn't bear, a kind of terrible gentleness. "You already know. Don't you. You knew before we came down the pass."

* * *

She had known since the tavern, if she was honest. Since the clean edge of the cut. But knowing a thing and saying it aloud were different countries, and the road between them was short and awful.

They made camp that night in the shell of a shepherd's cot above the village, and Serah sat apart with her back against the wall and took out the one object she carried that she had never shown Cyrus: her warder's commission, folded twice, sealed with wax. She broke the seal now, alone, by the light of a fire she'd built small so it wouldn't reach her face.

The commission was standard. Oath, rank, jurisdiction. But folded behind it, where she had kept it without letting herself understand why, was a routing slip — the kind attached to classified writs, the kind a junior warder wasn't supposed to keep. It recorded the authorization chain for an operation designated in full Court cipher, and beneath the cipher, the seal of the office that had issued it.

The seal was a phoenix devouring its own wing.

Chancellor Ashgrave's own mark.

And the operation's field designation, translated from cipher in her own careful hand from months ago, back when she'd believed it was a border survey: *Recollection Harvest — Founding Strata — Priority: Total.*

Total. Not a sample. Not a study. Total.

Serah sat with the paper in her hands and felt the horror move through her in slow tides. Hollowmere's emptied grandmothers. The girl with the beads. The bridge that existed only while a stranger loved it. Every mile of darkening country between here and the coast — the wards falling, the stars going out, the horizon breathing its ragged breath — none of it was catastrophe. It was *policy*. Somewhere in the citadel at Embergate, men she had saluted were feeding her country into a fire, handful by handful, and stamping the paperwork.

She had sworn an oath to that seal. She had worn its phoenix on her breast for eleven years, and it had warmed her like a hearthstone, and all the while—

"You're crying," Cyrus said from the doorway. Not accusing. Just noting it, the way she noted everything, cataloguing.

"Am I." Serah touched her cheek and found it wet and was distantly surprised. She folded the routing slip along its old creases. "Cyrus. If I told you the Court did this — if I told you the seal on the erasure is the same seal I answer to — what would you do?"

Cyrus considered her for a long moment. Firelight moved in the thief's eyes, and behind her, in the open pack, the vial pulsed its slow patient pulse, northward, toward the mountain, toward the breathing dark.

"I'd ask you," Cyrus said finally, "which oath you're going to keep. Because there's a dragon dying up north keeping this whole country lit, and I don't think it's dying naturally. And I think your Chancellor knows exactly why."

Far to the north, beyond the black ridgeline, the sky shuddered — and this time there was no thunder to hide it. Only the vast ragged breath again, weaker than before, like a bellows running out of arm.

And in the valley below, the ghost of the bridge flickered once, twice —

and went out.
