## 5. The Ember Court

The summons came on a hawk's leg, and Serah knew before she broke the seal that it would be the end of something.

The wax was black, not the Court's usual ember-red. Black wax meant a private matter — a matter the Chancellor did not want logged, witnessed, or remembered by anyone but the three people in the room where it was decided. She cracked it with her thumbnail in the grey light of dawn, on the ridge above Hollowmere, while the town below went on forgetting itself, and read the single line twice:

*WARDER VEY. RETURN. ALONE. THE THIEF IS NOT YOUR CONCERN.*

*Alone.* As if Cyrus Amberdale were a stray dog she'd picked up on the road and could simply shoo back into the hills. As if the last six days hadn't happened — the pass, the campfire, the slow unspooling of a woman who stole memories the way other people stole bread, out of hunger and a strange, crooked tenderness. As if Serah hadn't lain awake two nights listening to Cyrus breathe and hated herself for finding it the most peaceful sound in a country coming apart.

She looked at Cyrus, who was crouched by the dead fire, feeding the last of their tea leaves into a chipped tin pot.

"We have a problem," Serah said.

Cyrus glanced up. In the pre-dawn grey her eyes were the color of river water over stone — unreadable until they weren't, and then so readable it was like being stripped. "We've had a problem since the stars went out. Which one is this?"

"The Court wants me back. Alone."

The tin pot stopped moving. "And what did you tell them?"

"Nothing yet." Serah folded the summons into a square and then unfolded it, because her hands wanted a task. "Cyrus. If I go back and say nothing — if I go back and lie — they'll send someone else after you. Someone who won't bargain at a campfire. Someone who'll put a suppression collar on you and drag you to the citadel in a box."

"And if you don't go back?"

"Then I'm a deserter, and they hunt me too, and we're both fugitives in a country with no stars." She said it flatly, because it was flat. It was arithmetic. It was also, she was beginning to understand, the first honest choice she'd been offered in eleven years of service.

Cyrus poured the tea — one cup, because they only had one cup, and handed it to Serah first, which was a habit now, and habits were dangerous. "You could go back," Cyrus said quietly. "Tell them what you've seen. Hollowmere. The roads that circle. The Court's seal on the erasure writs." Her voice didn't accuse. That was the worst part. Cyrus never accused; she just laid the truth out like a cardsharp laying down a losing hand and watched to see what you'd do with it.

"I could," Serah said. "And Ashgrave would smile at me with that face he keeps for funerals and tell me the seal is a forgery, and I'd want to believe him, because I've wanted to believe him since I was nineteen years old and he pulled me out of the gutter wards and gave me an oath to hold."

"So don't believe him. Come see for yourself." Cyrus stood, brushing ash from her knees. "You're recalled to the citadel. Fine. I'm the most wanted thief in Thornfell, and the Ember Court's citadel is the one place in this country where every memory in the realm is archived — including, if your dead archivist was right, the founding memories we need. Walk me in, Serah. Not as a prisoner." A crooked half-smile. "As a warder with a very unusual prisoner. It's even mostly true."

Serah drank the tea. It tasted of smoke and nothing, like everything did now. "You're insane."

"I'm a thief. It's a related condition."

And that — that was how Serah Vay came to walk through the front gates of the Ember Court with her hand on Cyrus Amberdale's chained wrist, her heart hammering a deserter's rhythm, and the black wax summons burning a hole in her breast pocket like a coal that wouldn't die.

* * *

The citadel had changed.

Serah had served here for eleven years and knew its stones the way she knew her own hands, and the stones were wrong. The great entry hall, which had held ten thousand years of the realm's recorded memory in its glass galleries, now held fewer. She counted without meaning to — the way you count your own fingers after a bad dream — and whole alcoves stood dark where the memory-vials of the Northern Reaches should have glowed. The vials weren't gone. That was the horror of it. They were *dim*, their light drawn thin, like a lamp turned down to a whisper, and at the end of each dim gallery stood a siphon-column of black iron that hadn't been there in the spring, humming with a frequency she felt in her back teeth.

"Where are the memories going?" she asked the under-archivist at the checkpoint, and the man — Tamms, she'd drunk with Tamms, he'd cried at her promotion — looked at her with polite, empty courtesy and said, "Going, Warder? Memories don't go. They're kept."

The hum in her teeth said otherwise.

They took Cyrus's chains at the inner gate, as procedure demanded, and Serah felt the loss of that contact like a missing tooth. Cyrus gave her a look across the checkpoint — calm, amused, *I'll be here when you're done* — and was led away toward holding, and Serah climbed the Chancellor's stair alone, past murals of the Founding that she'd walked beneath a thousand times and never once looked at properly.

She looked at them now. A thief kneeling before a dragon, offering up a handful of light. A realm blooming out of flame. She had always assumed they were decorative.

Ashgrave was waiting in his study, and he was not smiling.

Lord Chancellor Ashgrave had a face built for three expressions: benevolence, gravity, and the particular sorrow of a man burdened by necessity. Serah had seen all three deployed like weapons. What she saw now was none of them. It was tiredness, vast and ancient, the tiredness of a man who had been doing a terrible thing for so long that the terrible thing had worn smooth as a river stone.

"Serah Vey." He poured wine without asking. "You were recalled alone."

"I was recalled alone," she agreed, and did not sit.

"And yet the gate logs say you brought the thief in yourself. Chained, at least. Small mercies." He set the decanter down. "Do you know what I admire about you, Vey? You're the only warder I have who still asks questions. The others stopped asking years ago. It makes them efficient. It makes you *dangerous*." He turned to the window. Below, the city of Emberhold sprawled under a sky with no stars, lit by the citadel's own glow — a glow, Serah realized with a lurch, that had grown brighter every month for a year, that the Court had called a blessing, a renewal, *the Court's fire burning strong*. "You've seen Hollowmere. You've seen the seal on the erasure writs. So let's not waste the wine on lies."

"The wards fell because the dragon is dying," Serah said. "The realm is dissolving because—"

"Because the realm *was* the dragon, yes. Vaelith's fire sustains the borders, the roads, the memory of the land itself. Founding truth. Every archivist knows it; we simply stopped teaching it, because it's a demoralizing sort of truth." He sipped. "What the histories omit is that a dragon's fire can be *directed*. Drawn. Siphoned. For three hundred years the Court has drawn a trickle — enough to keep our wards sharp, our writs binding, our word law. A tax. The realm never noticed a tax." He set down the glass. "But the fire is failing, Vey. Vaelith is dying, and a dying flame gives less and less. So the question before this Court became very simple. Do we let the fire gutter out in an old dragon's chest, and go dark with the country? Or do we take what remains and put it where it can *rule*?"

Serah's mouth had gone dry. "The siphon-columns. In the archives. You're not archiving the realm's memories. You're drinking them."

"We are *consolidating*," Ashgrave said, with the first flicker of something like passion. "Every memory the land forgets, the Court keeps. Every road that unravels, we hold its thread. When Vaelith dies and the borders finish dissolving, there will be a dark year — perhaps a dark decade — and then the Court will reforge the realm. From our fire. From our memory. A new Thornfell, Vey, with no dragons, no wild wards, no smuggler roads that exist only because some thief remembers them. A realm that is *written*, not dreamed. Order. For the first time in ten thousand years, actual order."

"And the people of Hollowmere," Serah said. Her voice sounded far away to herself. "The ones who've forgotten their own names."

"Will remember them again when we give them back. Better memories. Curated ones." He finally looked at her, and his eyes were kind, and that was the most monstrous thing in the room. "You've seen what the realm is, Vey. A dream an old lizard is having. Dreams end. We are offering the country a waking."

Serah stood very still. Eleven years of oath sat in her chest like a swallowed stone: *I hold the memory of the realm; I keep the flame; I serve the Court that keeps the flame.* She had recited it ten thousand times. She had never once asked who kept the Court.

"The thief," Ashgrave said. "Amberdale. She carries founding memories — the archivist Vess stole them the night the wards fell, and died of his own cleverness. Those memories are anchor-points, Vey. As long as they're scattered, the dissolution is slow, controlled. If she reaches the mountain — if she *returns* them —" He shook his head. "The realm snaps back to the dragon. All of it. Every siphon reversed. Every year of our work undone, and Vaelith lives another century to keep the country dreaming." He came around the desk, and put his hands on her shoulders, and she let him, because her body still belonged to the oath even though the rest of her had left the room. "You are the only one who knows where she is. You will give her to me, Serah. And when the new realm is forged, I will remember what you did. I will *make* the realm remember."

*The realm will remember,* she thought. *And I will be a line in a writ. And she'll be a box in a cellar. And the stars will stay dark.*

"Give me until morning," Serah heard herself say. "She trusts me. I can deliver her without a hunt."

Ashgrave smiled — benevolence now, deployed like a blanket over a cage. "Of course. Morning."

* * *

The holding corridor smelled of cold iron and lamp oil, and Cyrus was sitting on the floor of her cell with her back against the bars like a woman waiting for a late coach.

"You're still wearing your sword," Cyrus observed, without turning around. "That's either very good or very bad."

"Ashgrave is going to reforge the realm out of stolen fire," Serah said. "He's going to rule the ashes and call it order. And he wants you delivered to him by morning."

Cyrus turned then, and pressed her forehead briefly against the bars. "And you climbed all the way down here to tell me that instead of sleeping on it. That's very bad," she said softly. "For you."

"I'm getting you out."

"Cyrus Amberdale, memory-thief, terror of three provinces, currently locked in a cell with a door that weighs four hundred pounds—"

"Through the siphon gallery." Serah was already working the lock, her hands steady, her heart not. "The columns draw memory, not flesh. We walk through them fast, we take the founding memories from the deep vault — I know the vault, I've stood ward over it — and we're out the postern before the morning watch."

The lock gave. The door swung. Cyrus stood up, close enough that Serah could see the exhaustion under her defiance, and for a moment neither of them moved.

"Serah." Cyrus said her name like a hand on a wound. "If we run, there's no coming back from it. Not ever. They'll burn your name out of every record in this country. You'll be a deserter in a realm that's forgetting itself. You'll be—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "You'll be giving up everything you are."

"I've been thinking about what I am," Serah said. "Eleven years I thought I was the oath. But the oath was a story the Court told so I'd hold the door while they drank the fire." She reached through the bars — the door was open, the bars were habit — and took Cyrus's wrist, where the chains had been, where the skin was rubbed raw. "I know what I am now. I'm the woman who walked into Hollowmere and watched a child ask her mother what her name was, and I am not holding that door one more hour."

The corridor lamps guttered, as if the siphons had heard.

Cyrus's free hand came up and framed her face, thumb tracing the warder's brand behind her ear — the oath-mark, the thing Ashgrave had given her at nineteen. "When this is over," Cyrus whispered, "when the stars come back — if they come back — I'm going to steal every bad memory the Court ever put in you. Every one. And I'm going to leave something better in the empty places. That's a promise from a thief, which means it's the only honest kind."

"Then let's go steal a country back," Serah said.

They went down through the humming dark of the siphon gallery, hand in hand, two small flames moving against the current of everything being drunk away — and behind them, in the Chancellor's tower, a bell began to ring.
