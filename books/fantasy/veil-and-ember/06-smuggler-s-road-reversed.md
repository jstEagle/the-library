## 6. Smuggler's Road, Reversed

The road out of Ashgrave's shadow did not exist.

Cyrus knew that the way she knew her own heartbeat — not because she had walked it, but because she remembered it. A smuggler named Wren Halloway had once carried a memory of a goat-track over the Kestrel spine, a path her grandfather had used to move untaxed wool through the war years, and Cyrus had lifted it from her mind in a tavern in Marrowgate for the price of a stolen kiss and a bottle of plum wine. The memory had sat in her pack for six years, unopened, one of hundreds she hoarded like a miser hoards coins she never intends to spend.

Now she cracked the vial's wax seal with her thumbnail and let it pour into her, and the road bloomed under their feet like ink spreading through water.

"Stand *there*," she said, pulling Serah back by the wrist. "Exactly there. Don't step off."

Serah looked down. There was nothing to stand on — scree, bracken, the black shoulder of the fell dropping away into fog. But where Cyrus remembered, the fog thinned. Stones surfaced through it, moss-slick and real, laid by hands two hundred years dead. A path stitched itself downhill ahead of them, narrow as a scar, holding only so long as they moved along it. Behind them, where memory ended, the stones sank away again into nothing.

"That's not possible," Serah said, but she was already walking on it.

"It's entirely possible. It's just expensive." Cyrus kept her voice light. Her hands were not light at all; they trembled as she coiled the empty vial back into its cloth. Every road she paved cost her something — not the stolen memories themselves, those were only borrowed goods, secondhand — but the *shape* of them, the certainty. She could feel Wren Halloway's grandfather's track fraying at its edges even now, wearing thin with use, the way any road wears. Memory, spent, did not return.

They climbed through the night with the Court's horns calling somewhere behind them, three long notes and a fourth cut short, the signal for fugitives. Above, the sky stayed starless. It had been nine days since the stars went out, and Cyrus had stopped expecting them back, the way you stop expecting a dead man to walk through the door. But sometimes she caught herself looking up anyway, the way you look up anyway.

* * *

The drowned chapel came to them before they came to it.

It stood — or rather, it *sank* — in the middle of the Vale of Aldern, where a village had been given to the reservoir three generations ago to water the lowland farms. The Court's records said the chapel had been demolished first, its bells melted down, its founding-stone carted off to a museum in the capital. The Court's records were wrong, or lying, which amounted to the same thing these days.

Cyrus found it because she remembered finding it: a memory lifted from a diver, years ago, a man who made his living salvaging lead from drowned roofs and who had once, drunk on brandy, described to a stranger the thing he'd seen at the bottom of the chapel — a stone the size of a loaf, set into the altar, glowing faintly gold beneath thirty feet of green water. He'd sold that memory cheap. He hadn't known what he had.

"You're sure it's down there," Serah said. They stood on the reservoir's rim in the grey of dawn, breath steaming, the water flat as poured pewter. Somewhere under that surface lay a village, its lanes, its graves, its little church with its roof caved in like a broken ribcage.

"I'm sure someone remembers it being down there." Cyrus stripped off her coat, her boots. "Which is nearly the same thing."

"It'll be freezing."

"It will be freezing," Cyrus agreed, "and I'm going in anyway, so you can either hold my clothes or come with me."

Serah came with her. Of course she came with her. That was the thing about Serah Vey that kept undoing Cyrus's every careful calculation — she was an oath-bound warder of the Ember Court, raised from childhood in its citadel, and yet when Cyrus said *jump* into a drowned church, she jumped, jaw set, eyes furious at herself for jumping.

The cold hit like a blow. Cyrus swam down through water gone from green to black, following the shape of the steeple as her stolen memory supplied it — here the bell-cot, empty; here the slates slid off; here the hole in the roof where the diver had slipped through, wide enough for two. Below, the nave opened up, pews furred with silt, an entire congregation of shadows. Light came down in shafts, bent and holy, and motes turned in it like slow golden snow.

The founding-memory sat in the altar stone exactly as the diver had described, except that it was not glowing anymore. It was guttering. A vial of amber glass, older than anything Cyrus had ever touched, and inside it a light no bigger than a spark — the memory of the realm's founding, the moment Thornfell had been called out of namelessness, and it was *dying*, dimming year by year in the dark and the cold while the country above forgot itself street by street, name by name.

Cyrus pried it free and kicked for the surface, and Serah met her at the broken roofline, took the vial in both hands, and held it up against the drowned light, and Cyrus watched her face do something complicated and terrible.

"It's so small," Serah said. "This is what everything is. This little spark, and the others like it. And we've been—" She didn't finish. She didn't have to. *We've been feeding it to the Chancellor's towers while the realm goes out.*

They lay on the reservoir bank afterward, shivering under one shared coat, too cold and too tired for propriety. Cyrus's teeth chattered so hard she could barely speak, and Serah laughed at her — actually laughed, a startled, unguarded sound, the first Cyrus had heard from her that wasn't honed for combat or concealment — and then Serah pressed her warmed hands to Cyrus's face without seeming to decide to, and the laughing stopped, and neither of them moved away.

"We should keep moving," Serah said, not moving.

"We should," Cyrus agreed, closer now.

The horns sounded again across the water, distant, patient. They got up. But Cyrus carried the warmth of those hands in her chest all that day, further than any road she paved, and she noticed herself doing it, and filed the noticing away in the place where she kept things she wasn't ready to look at.

* * *

The bandit king's grave was harder, because the bandit king had never existed.

That was the joke of him, if you could call it a joke: Torvane the Unruled, the outlaw who carved a kingdom out of Thornfell's northern marches during the Interregnum and was never crowned, never recorded, struck from every ledger by the very kings who paid him tribute in secret. He existed now only in the memories people had refused to surrender — ballads sung in defiance of the ban, stories grandmothers told against the law, and one smuggler's tale of a barrow under the Hanging Stone where a thief could still find the king's grave-goods, if the king judged her worthy.

Cyrus had three separate memories of the barrow and they disagreed with each other, which meant the barrow itself was uncertain, flickering between versions. When they reached the Hanging Stone at dusk, there was no barrow at all — just a wind-scoured tor and a standing rock like a broken tooth.

"He has to remember himself," Cyrus said slowly, working it through. "That's the anchor. Torvane was erased on purpose, completely, so the only place he exists is in other people's remembering. If the memories disagree—"

"The grave disagrees," Serah finished. "So which version do we make true?"

"The kindest one." Cyrus closed her eyes and opened all three vials at once, letting the versions pour together — the barrow of the shepherd boy, the barrow of the hanged smuggler, the barrow of the ballad-singer — and wove them, the way she wove any theft, taking the seams and making them invisible. A mound rose out of the tor's shoulder, turf-covered, ancient-looking, its entrance stone grooved with centuries of rain that had perhaps never fallen. It felt like lying. It felt like the truest thing she'd ever done.

Inside, the passage smelled of cold earth and old smoke. The grave-goods were poor — a rusted sword, a cup of horn — because Torvane the Unruled would have wanted it that way, would have laughed at gilded bones. But on the king's breast, where a crown should have been, lay a vial of amber glass, and this one burned steady and bright, undimmed, as if refusal were a fuel of its own.

*He was never allowed to be remembered,* Cyrus thought, cradling it. *So he remembered himself, and made everyone else do it too.* Something about that steadied her. Something about it frightened her more than the horns behind them ever could.

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the barrow, a fire small enough to hide, and Cyrus finally said the thing they had both been not saying.

"The mountain heart will want more than the founding memories."

Serah looked up from cleaning her blade. Firelight moved on her face. "Say it plainly."

"Vaelith's fire runs on sacrifice. Freely given. That's how the realm was lit the first time — someone gave something they couldn't bear to lose, and the flame took it and burned forever after. The founding memories are the *kindling*. But kindling isn't the fire." Cyrus turned her hands palm-up, thief's hands, quick and clever and suddenly useless. "I don't know what the fire will take from me. I know it won't be cheap."

"You could be wrong."

"I'm rarely wrong about value. It's the whole trade."

Serah was quiet a long time. Then she said, "Then we find another way," in the voice she used for orders, the warder's voice, and Cyrus almost believed her, and loved her hopelessly for offering to believe it herself.

Later, wrapped in the same blanket against the mountain cold, Serah asleep against her shoulder with her hand curled loosely around the drowned chapel's vial as though she could guard a dying light by holding it, Cyrus lay awake and counted what she owned. Stolen faces. Borrowed roads. Two sparks of a country's beginning. One warder's sleeping weight, warm against her side.

She was a thief. She knew better than anyone that everything she loved was inventory, and inventory could always be taken.

Far to the south, past the dark hills, torchlight moved along a ridgeline in a long deliberate line — not hunters this time, not a patrol. A procession. Dozens of lights, moving with the unhurried confidence of people who knew exactly where their quarry slept, and at their head a single white lantern that burned without flickering, the way the Chancellor's sorceries burned, cold and certain as a closing door.

Cyrus slid out from under the blanket, woke Serah with two fingers to her lips, and pointed.

"They're not following our trail," Serah breathed. "They don't need one. They're going where we're going."

"To the dragon," Cyrus said. "He's not sending hunters anymore. He's coming to finish it himself."

Above the ridgeline, where stars should have been, the darkness seemed to lean down toward them like a hand.
