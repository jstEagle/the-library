## 7. The Guttering Heart

The mountain did not appear so much as it was remembered into being.

For three days they had climbed through country that thinned like breath on glass — forests going grey at the edges, rivers running shallow and uncertain of their own courses, birdsong arriving a half-second after the birds opened their beaks. Serah walked half a step behind her the whole way, hand never far from her sword, and said almost nothing, as though speech itself might unravel something that was barely holding.

Then, near dusk on the third day, the clouds parted and there it was: Mount Cindral, vast and black-shouldered, wearing snow like ash. It had no business being visible from this valley. Every map Cyrus had ever stolen from agreed the mountain lay two hundred leagues east. But maps were only memory written down, and memory had been coming home to this place for nine days now, drawn along roads that existed because she and Serah walked them.

"It's pulling us," Serah said quietly.

"It's *hungry*," Cyrus answered, and immediately wished she hadn't, because Serah's face did something complicated and then went still.

They climbed through the night. The path was not a path — it was more honest than that. It was simply that wherever Cyrus stepped, the mountain consented to have ground beneath her foot, and wherever Serah followed, the same courtesy extended to her. Once, looking back down a switchback they'd just ascended, Cyrus watched the trail dissolve behind them like sugar in rain, and understood that they were not climbing the mountain so much as being swallowed by it, gently, one remembered step at a time.

The cold changed an hour before dawn. It stopped being weather and became intention — a cold with a pulse in it, faint and arrhythmic, like a heart deciding whether to bother beating again. Cyrus felt it in her teeth first, then in the vials.

She carried six of them now against her sternum, strapped in a leather harness Serah had made from her own warder's baldric, cut down and resewn by firelight while Cyrus pretended to sleep. The founding memories of Thornfell. The realm's first morning, its first oath, its first grief. They were warm. They had been growing warmer for days, and warmer still as the mountain's failing pulse reached for them through the stone.

"They know where they're going," Serah had said, when Cyrus mentioned it. "That frightens me more than Ashgrave does."

* * *

They found the entrance where no entrance should have been: a seam in the cliff face, tall as a cathedral door, breathing. Warm air came out of it in long slow exhalations that smelled of hot iron and, underneath that, impossibly, of summer — cut grass, river water, woodsmoke, all the small warm smells of a living country, distilled to their essence and spent like coin.

Cyrus stood before it a long time.

"I've lifted memories from dying men," she said. "It's not like taking from the sleeping. Everything is loose. Everything is leaving anyway. You just... catch what falls." She flexed her fingers inside her gloves. "This is going to feel like that."

Serah touched her shoulder. Just that. Her gauntlet was cold through Cyrus's coat. "We don't know yet what it wants."

"Yes we do." Cyrus had known since Hollowmere, since the bridge that existed only because a child remembered falling off it. Since the drowned chapel, where the founding memory had sung to her through thirty feet of black water. "I've known since the grave of the bandit king. I just haven't said it out loud, because saying things out loud makes them true, and I'm a thief, Serah. Lying is the first thing they teach us."

"Say it anyway."

So she said it, to the dark mouth of the mountain, where no one but the wind could hear: "Memories are the fuel. And mine will burn hottest, because I'm the one carrying the flame." She turned. "And you're not allowed to argue, because I've seen how you argue and we don't have time."

Something moved behind Serah's eyes — the argument assembling itself, and then, with visible effort, being put away. "Inside first," she said. "Decisions after."

* * *

The mountain's heart was a cavern that should not have fit inside any mountain.

It opened around them like the inside of a held breath — walls of obsidian veined with gold that glowed and dimmed, glowed and dimmed, keeping time with that failing pulse. The floor was warm glass, fused smooth in great sweeping waves, as if a sea of sand had once been caught mid-boil and told to hold still. And everywhere, everywhere, there were images in the walls. Not carvings. Memories, pressed into stone the way flowers are pressed into books: a harvest festival, a coronation, a woman laughing in a doorway, ten thousand ordinary moments offered up across a thousand years, each one banked here like a coal.

"Oh," Cyrus said, very softly. "Oh, it's been *eating well*, hasn't it. All this time."

"Not eating." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, deep enough that Cyrus felt it in the fluid of her eyes. "*Receiving.* There is a difference, little thief, though your kind has rarely troubled to learn it."

She saw the dragon then, and forgot how to breathe.

Vaelith lay coiled in the cavern's center, and she was dying the way a city dies — not all at once, but district by district, light by light. Her body was vast beyond sense, scaled in bronze gone dull as old pennies, and along her spine ran a ridge of spines that had once, clearly, burned like a crown of lanterns. Now only three still held fire. Two guttered. One had already gone out, and the darkness where it stood was somehow worse than the darkness of the rest of the cavern, a hole in the shape of a light.

Her eye found them. It was the size of a cart wheel and the color of a banked hearth, and it looked at Cyrus with an attention so total it felt like being read aloud.

"A thief," Vaelith rumbled. The word shook grit from the ceiling. "Come up my mountain with the realm's cradle-stones strapped to her chest like a breastplate. And a warder of the Court that bleeds me." The great head shifted; the sound of it moving was rockfall slowed to a crawl. "Tell me, Serah Vey. When you swore your oath, did they tell you what the siphons fed?"

Serah's voice came out level, which cost her; Cyrus could see it costing her. "No, lady. They told us we were guarding the realm's fire."

"You are guarding its *drain*. Your Chancellor drinks from me every dawn and calls it stewardship." A plume of smoke curled from the dragon's nostrils, thin and grey, a candle-smoke where once there must have been weather. "But you knew that already. You cut three siphons on your way out of the citadel. I felt each one close. It was the kindest thing anyone has done for me in a century, and I do not even know your face well enough to thank you properly."

"You will," Cyrus heard herself say. "If we fix this."

Vaelith's eye turned back to her, and the attention of it was like standing in front of an open furnace door. "Will you? Tell me what you think fixing means, thief. Say it plainly. You who deal in plain lies."

Cyrus's mouth was dry. "The founding memories rekindle you. We carry them to your heart, you take them, the fire comes back, the stars come back, everyone goes home happy and remembers their own names at breakfast."

"And the memories?" Vaelith asked. "Do they survive the burning? Did the ones you pulled from the chapel and the grave?"

Silence. The kind of silence that answers.

"No," Cyrus said finally. "They didn't. They went into me whole and they came out as heat." Her hands had begun to shake; she folded them into fists. "Fuel burns. That's what fuel *does*."

"And so you understand the shape of it." The dragon's voice gentled, which was somehow more terrible. "A thousand years ago there was another thief. Her name has been burned out of every record because she spent it — spent her name, her mother's face, the sound of her own laughter, everything that made her herself — to forge the wards that keep this realm stitched to the sky. The founding memories you carry are hers. What remained of her, after the giving. I am the vessel she left, and the fire is her gift, renewed each generation by those willing to pay as she paid."

The gold veins in the walls pulsed. Dimmed. Pulsed.

"The realm cannot be saved by what it owns," Vaelith said. "Only by what someone gives it. Freely. Knowingly. With love in the hand that offers." The great eye narrowed, and something ancient and almost tender moved in it. "You have carried six anchor-memories up my mountain, thief. But anchor-memories are only kindling. The log that holds the fire through the night must come from the giver's own hearth. From the memories she loves best. The ones she would fight to keep."

Cyrus already knew. She had known since the campfire on the pass, since the taste of shared tea in a chipped tin cup, since Serah laughing — actually laughing, once, badly, at a joke about the Chancellor's hat — since every mile of road that existed because the two of them remembered walking it together.

She thought of the vials against her chest, warm as sleeping birds. She thought of the space beside them, the empty space, where the memories she treasured most lived in the only vault that couldn't be picked: her own mind.

Every campfire. Every kiss. Every road.

"There's a copy," Serah said suddenly, sharply, and Cyrus realized she'd spoken aloud without meaning to. "There's — I can copy them, Cyrus, I've seen your craft, give me a week and—"

"Copy them onto what?" Cyrus asked gently. "Paper burns. Stone forgets. And if you write them down and hide them somewhere safe, then some part of me goes on without knowing what it lost, and that's worse than losing it. That's being haunted by a ghost of yourself." She looked up at the dragon. "How much time?"

"Three of my lights remain," Vaelith said. "When the last goes out, the realm goes with it. Days. Perhaps fewer. Ashgrave's hunters are in the foothills — I can smell their iron and their fear."

"Days," Cyrus repeated.

"Cyrus—" Serah's voice cracked clean through the middle of her own name.

Cyrus turned to her then, and let herself look — really look, greedy as any thief, stealing with her eyes alone: the frost in Serah's lashes, the scar through her eyebrow from a duel she'd never explained, the way her jaw set when she was frightened and wanted no one to know. Filing it all away in the vault. Not to keep. To say goodbye to properly.

"Don't," Cyrus said softly. "Don't argue. I told you on the threshold — saying things out loud makes them true. So I'm only going to say one thing out loud, and then we're going to get these memories to your heart, dragon, and we're going to do it tonight."

The cavern waited. Even the failing pulse seemed to lean in.

Cyrus took Serah's cold gauntleted hand and pressed it flat against the six warm vials, over her own heartbeat.

"When this is done," she said, "the country won't remember me. Fine. Countries are ungrateful, it's practically their job. But you—" her voice caught, and she let it, because lying was the first thing thieves learned and she was done learning anything new, "—you remember whatever you can carry. That's all I ask. Somebody has to remember, or none of this was ever real."

Above them, far above, through a thousand feet of stone, the second-to-last light on the dragon's spine flickered —

— and began, very slowly, to go out.
