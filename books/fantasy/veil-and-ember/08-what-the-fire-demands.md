## 8. What the Fire Demands

### Serah

The mountain had a throat, and the Court came up it like smoke.

Serah heard them before she saw them — the particular ring of Court-forged boots on scree, the wet hiss of siphon-lances priming, the low disciplined silence of soldiers who had been told exactly what they were allowed to say and nothing more. She stood at the mouth of the pass where Vaelith's mountain narrowed to a corridor of black stone, and she counted lanterns in the dark below.

Nine. Then fourteen. Then too many to count.

*Warder Vey,* Ashgrave's voice had said, three days ago, through the speaking-glass that now lay shattered at her feet. *You will bring me the thief, or you will be remembered as the woman who let the realm die.* He had said it gently. That was the worst of it. Ashgrave was always gentle when he was doing something unforgivable.

She checked her gauntlets. Ember-warded steel, Court-issue, designed to hold back fire — though whose fire, she wondered now, and for whom? The wards drank heat the way the Court drank everything: without thanks, without end.

Below her, the first lanterns crested the switchback.

She thought about Cyrus's face in the dragon's cavern — that thief's bravado stretched thin over something rawer, something Serah had only ever seen in people standing at the edge of a decision they could not take back. *You remember whatever you can carry.* As if it were a small thing. As if memory were a pack one shouldered lightly.

"Come on, then," Serah said to the dark, and drew her blade, and the ward-light along its edge burned the color of an oath about to be broken.

They came up the pass in files of four, siphon-lances forward, their armor drinking the mountain's warmth so that frost bloomed behind them like a wake. Serah met the first file at the narrows, where two could not walk abreast, and she learned very quickly what it meant to fight the thing she had been all her life.

The first lance-bolt took her across the shoulder and she felt the ward-steel drink her own heat to answer it — the Court's machinery did not care whose fire it fed on. She went down to one knee, came up swinging, and put the first soldier off the path into a dark that did not echo.

"I'm sorry," she told the second one, and meant it, and broke his lance across her knee.

They kept coming. That was the thing about oaths; they kept coming too.

### Cyrus

The heart of the mountain was warm the way a dying body is warm — heat without strength, warmth going out of the world rather than into it. Cyrus climbed the last stair with six vials against her chest and felt each one pulse like a second heartbeat, out of rhythm with her own, which was somehow worse than if they'd been cold.

Vaelith filled the cavern the way night fills a valley. Up close, the last dragon was less a creature than a landscape: ridges of scale gone grey as old slate, wings folded like felled banners, and beneath the plates of her breastbone a glow no larger than a coal in a dead hearth. Her eye opened as Cyrus approached — an eye the size of a door, amber shot through with veins of black.

*Thief,* said a voice that arrived not through Cyrus's ears but through her memories, stirring them like fingers through a drawer. *You brought them.*

"All six." Cyrus held the vials up. Her hands were steady. She was quietly furious at how steady they were. "The founding. Every scrap your scattered self left lying around the country."

*And the price?*

"You already showed me the price." She thought of the vision Vaelith had given her — the first thief, a thousand years ago, kneeling at this same coal and feeding it the only thing she had that would burn: not gold, not blood, but the memory of everyone she had ever loved, freely given, until the fire roared and a country stood where there had been nothing but fear. "A willing memory. Freely given. I did the reading."

Vaelith's breath moved through the cavern, hot and slow, smelling of iron and rain. *It is a terrible arithmetic,* the dragon said. *One life's dearest things against a nation's everything. I have watched a thousand years pass wondering if the sum was ever honest.*

"Honest doesn't come into it." Cyrus knelt at the coal. It was smaller than her fist. It guttered as she watched it, and every flicker downward, she swore, dimmed some far-off thing — a village name, a road, a star. "I'm a thief. I've never done an honest day's work in my life. But I know value when I'm holding it."

Above them, faint through a thousand feet of stone, came a sound like thunder learning to be a bell.

Vaelith's head lifted. *They are in the pass.*

"I know." Cyrus set the first vial against the coal. "That's not my post."

* * *

### Serah

By the fourth hour she had stopped counting wounds and started counting lanterns again, because the numbers were easier. Seven left. Six. Five.

Her left arm hung wrong. Her wards were down to a whisper of heat. The pass behind her was littered with Court steel and Court men who would go home to wives who might not remember them by morning, and Serah found she could not make herself feel the proper grief about that, because she had seen Hollowmere, she had seen the bridge that existed only because a child remembered it, and she knew now which side of the arithmetic she stood on.

The fifth-to-last soldier was Captain Ilvane, who had trained her, who had once carried her three miles through a snowstorm with a broken ankle, and who looked at her across ten feet of scorched stone and said, "Step aside, Vey. You're still salvageable. He'll want you salvageable."

"He can want," Serah said.

Ilvane sighed the sigh of a good soldier given a bad order, and came on.

They fought the way old friends fight — knowing each other's openings, hating every one of them. His lance caught her thigh and burned through to the bone and she screamed and did not fall, and when she got inside his reach she did not use the blade. She used her forehead, and then the wall, and then gravity, and Ilvane went off the path into the dark with a sound she would carry for the rest of her life, however long the Court left that to be.

Four lanterns. Three.

Then, from deep in the mountain, from somewhere below and ahead, a sound she felt in her teeth before she heard it: a great intake of breath. A gathering. Something old remembering what it was for.

And above the pass, above the peak, the sky — which had been a blind black ceiling since the night the stars went out — shuddered.

* * *

### Cyrus

The sixth vial cracked open over the coal and the founding poured in — a bridge of woven light over a river that no longer existed, a coronation sung by voices that had been dust for centuries, a border drawn by hand in fire on the living rock — and the coal drank it and grew, and grew, and the heat coming off it made Cyrus's eyebrows crisp and her eyes stream and she laughed, actually laughed, because it was working, it was *working* —

— and then she looked at the last vial in her hand, and stopped laughing.

It was not one of the six. It was the seventh, the one Serah had pressed into her palm at the mouth of the cavern with a look that dared her to refuse it. *In case yours aren't enough,* Serah had said. Inside it, Cyrus knew without looking, were copies — every stolen moment Serah had managed to keep: the campfire on the pass, the kiss in the drowned chapel, the road that existed because Cyrus remembered it, all of it, hoarded like a dragon's own treasure.

The coal flared hungrily toward her hand.

"Not yet," Cyrus told it. "Not that one. That one's spoken for."

Because there was one fuel left that would burn hotter than any founding memory, and she had known it since Vaelith showed her the first thief's face, and she had lied to Serah's face about being fine with it, and the lie had been the truest thing she'd ever said: *you remember whatever you can carry.*

Cyrus reached into herself, into the vault where she kept the things worth stealing, and began to take them out.

* * *

### Serah

Two lanterns left, and then the siphon arrived.

It came up the pass on the backs of eight bearers — a great brass engine on chains, its throat aimed at the mountain's heart, its lines running back down the slope to the relay-stations where the Court had been drinking Vaelith's fire for a hundred years and calling it stewardship. Ashgrave himself stood beside it, small and neat and gentle-faced, and he looked up at Serah swaying on her ruined leg and smiled the smile of a man about to close an account.

"Warder," he called. "Last chance to be on the right page of history."

Serah looked at the siphon. At the thick umbilical of chained pipe running down the mountainside, pulsing faintly with stolen heat. At the coal-thin light glowing through the stone under her feet, weaker than a candle, holding up a whole country.

Her oath lived in her chest like a swallowed coal. *Protect the realm. Obey the Court.* She had recited it at nine years old with her hand on the ward-steel, and she had never once noticed that the sentence contained a contradiction, or that someday it would tear her in half along the seam.

"The realm," she said aloud, testing the words, "is the thing I protect."

Then she cut the line.

The ward-blade went through the primary chain-joint and the siphon screamed — an actual scream, pressurized fire escaping backward through a hundred years of theft, blowing the engine apart in a bloom of orange that lit the whole pass like dawn. The shockwave took Serah off her feet and slammed her into the rock, and she lay there with the world ringing and watched Ashgrave's silhouette stagger, and burn, and not get up.

Somewhere below, the mountain breathed in.

* * *

### Cyrus

The memories of Serah burned best of all.

That was the cruelty of it — the founding memories fed the fire, but her own fed it *joyfully*, the way dry wood takes flame, the way a heart takes a name. The campfire went first, and Cyrus felt it leave not as loss but as warmth, which was worse. The drowned chapel. The road. The kiss. Each one rose out of her and became light, and the light became heat, and the heat became a country, and Cyrus stood in the roaring heart of it feeding the fire with the best of herself and feeling, moment by moment, a great soft whiteness moving through her mind like snow filling footprints.

Vaelith rose around her — rising, rising, wings unfurling through caverns built for exactly this, scales kindling from slate-grey to ember-red to white-hot gold, a thousand years of debt paid and a body remembering flight. The dragon's voice filled Cyrus's hollowing head, gentle as ashfall:

*Thief. What is your name?*

And Cyrus opened her mouth to say it, and found the space where it had been filling with firelight.

*Go,* she said instead, or thought, or simply was. *Fly.*

Vaelith went up through the mountain's crown and out into the blind black sky, trailing fire like a comet learning its old road — and overhead, one by one, shy as survivors, the stars came back.

* * *

### Serah

Serah woke to starlight.

It fell through the smoke of the shattered pass and lay across the dead siphon-engine and across her own outflung hand, and she stared up at it with her leg burning and her ribs singing and wept without deciding to, because the sky was full again, full the way it had been when she was nine years old with her hand on the ward-steel, and whatever it had cost, it had been worth — 

— except that somewhere below, in the heart of the mountain, someone had paid it, and Serah's hand closed around the single vial hanging at her throat, the one the fire had not taken, the one she had sworn on everything left to her she would never burn.

She got up. She started down.

The mountain was bright now, all the way down, and at the bottom of the last stair, sitting very quietly beside a hearth of coals with an empty look on her face and tears running freely down cheeks that did not know why, was a woman Serah loved with every memory she had left.

Cyrus looked up. Her eyes were clear and strange and brand-new.

"Do I know you?" she asked.
