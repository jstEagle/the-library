## 9. Ember

The heart of the mountain was not a cave. It was a wound.

Cyrus felt that the moment she crossed the threshold — a vast hollow in the world's bone, its walls veined with channels of cooled glass where fire had once run like rivers and now ran no longer. The air tasted of ash and old honey. Far above, through a ragged chimney of stone, she could see a smear of grey sky where stars should have been waiting for dusk, and even that was wrong; the sky had the texture of parchment left too long in rain.

And at the center, coiled around a bed of embers no larger than a hearth's worth, lay Vaelith.

The dragon was dying the way a country dies: slowly, then all at once. Her scales — once the color of hammered copper, if the founding memories told true — had gone the grey of Hollowmere's forgotten roads. Her breathing came in long, rattling intervals, each exhale stirring the embers beneath her into a brief orange flutter before they sank back toward black. One eye opened as Cyrus approached. It was the color of the last coal in a dying grate.

*You brought them,* Vaelith said, and her voice was not sound so much as heat — it moved through Cyrus's ribs, through the marrow, through the vials strapped against her chest. *You brought the realm home.*

Cyrus knelt at the edge of the ember-bed. Her hands were steady. She noticed that about herself, distantly, the way one notices weather. Somewhere behind her, far down the mountain's throat, Serah was holding the pass alone against Ashgrave's hunters, and every part of Cyrus that was not standing here was screaming down that corridor of stone after her. But here, in this wounded heart, there was only work.

She unslung the pack. She laid out the memories one by one on the warm glass floor, arranging them as an archivist might — though she was no archivist, only a thief, and thieves know better than anyone that some things are too heavy to steal and must instead be carried.

The founding of Thornfell. Seven vials, gathered across a country busy forgetting itself. The drowned chapel had given up two, prised from the hands of a saint's statue with lake water still beading on the glass. The bandit king's grave had yielded one more, wrapped in a banner gone to rot. And the first — the smuggler's vial, the dead archivist's legacy, the face of the last dragon — sat among them now like a keystone.

*Do you understand what they are?* Vaelith asked.

"Memories," Cyrus said. "Anchor-points. The realm's."

*More.* The dragon's breath stirred Cyrus's hair, warm as an apology. *When your founders made their bargain with my line, they did not pay in gold or blood. They paid in remembering. Every border, every road, every name in this country is stitched to a memory someone gave freely, long ago, so that the land would hold its shape. My fire does not sustain the realm, thief. My fire keeps those memories lit. And when the Court began to siphon me — when my flame fell too low to feed them all — the stitches began to pull loose.*

Cyrus looked down at the seven small vessels. Inside them, faint as fireflies, light moved. She could feel them the way she felt any memory she carried: a pressure behind the eyes, a warmth in the palms. The memory of the first ward being raised, sung into being by a circle of women whose names were already half-lost. The memory of the treaty signed in dragonfire on a mountaintop. The memory of a river agreeing, once, to stay within its banks because a whole village dreamed it so.

"And mine," she said quietly. "You said a willing memory. Freely given. That's what rekindles you."

Vaelith was silent a long moment. When she spoke again, her voice had gentled into something almost like sorrow.

*The founders learned it the hard way, little thief. Fire fed from coercion burns cold. Fire fed from theft burns colder still. Only what is given — given knowing its cost, given whole — makes a flame that lasts.* A pause, long as a held breath. *I will not lie to you. I do not know how much you must give. Perhaps the founding memories alone will suffice. Perhaps they will burn bright for a season and gutter again, and the realm will fade slower, and someone else will stand here in another hundred years holding vials they cannot bear to open.*

"Or?"

*Or you give until the fire says enough.*

Cyrus nodded, once, and picked up the first vial.

* * *

She broke the seal of the drowned chapel's memory first, because it seemed right to begin with water.

The moment the glass cracked, the memory poured out of the vial and into her — that was how it worked, she had learned; a memory-thief's gift ran both ways, and for one terrible, beautiful instant she *was* the chapel again, candles guttering underwater, fish moving through the nave like slow thoughts, a priest pressing a vial into a statue's hands and whispering *hold this, hold us, remember for us*. Then she tipped the open vessel over the ember-bed, and the memory left her, streaming out in a ribbon of gold-white light that sank into the coals.

They flared. Not much. A candle's worth of new brightness in a cathedral of dark. But Vaelith drew a breath that shook the walls, and somewhere far below, Cyrus imagined, a road remembered which way it went.

One by one, she opened them.

The treaty on the mountaintop: the fire took it and roared briefly, throwing shadows of wings across the ceiling that were larger than any shadow Vaelith cast now. The river's dream: the embers turned blue-green for a heartbeat, and Cyrus heard, impossibly, water moving somewhere deep in the stone. The raising of the first ward, the singing women, the naming of Thornfell itself by a girl who said the word aloud and watched it become true — each one went into the flame, and each time the fire climbed higher, and each time Cyrus felt the mountain shudder a little further awake around her.

But it was not enough. She knew it the way she knew a lock's mechanism by feel, without being told. The flames rose and reached and fell back, hungry, unfinished. The sky through the broken chimney stayed grey.

Vaelith's eye found hers. The dragon said nothing. She didn't have to.

Cyrus sat back on her heels. Below, muffled by miles of stone, she heard the clash of steel — faint, rhythmic, stubborn. Serah was still fighting. Serah was still holding the pass, wounded, oath-broken, buying minutes with her body.

*You do not have to,* Vaelith said softly. *No one has ever had to. That is the whole of the law.*

"I know." Cyrus's voice came out strange to her own ears — level, almost calm, the voice she used when lifting a memory from a sleeping man's mind. "That's what makes it worth anything."

She reached into her coat, past the empty vials, and drew out the ones she had never shown the dragon. Eight of them. Small, plain glass, unmarked. She had filled them herself, in stolen hours on the road — at campfires, in waystations, once in the lee of a ruined watchtower while Serah slept with her sword across her knees. She had told herself it was habit. A thief archives her best work. She had known, even then, that she was lying.

Every campfire. The first one, on the pass, when Serah had shared her ration bread and pretended not to notice Cyrus watching the firelight move on her face. The kiss in the drowned chapel's doorway, salt-tasting, terrified, Serah's gauntlets shaking as she pulled them off. The road that existed only because Cyrus remembered it, and the two of them walking it hand in hand through a country coming apart, laughing at the absurdity of paving the world with love. The night in the bandit king's tomb when Serah had said *I don't know how to want something and survive wanting it*, and Cyrus had said *then we'll practice*, and meant it, and been believed.

Eight vials. She counted them twice, the way she'd once counted a mark's coin.

"You'll take all of it," she said to the fire. "Not just the big moments. The small ones. The way she hummed when she thought I was asleep. The exact color of her eyes at dawn — grey, but with amber in it, like her name was a prophecy nobody caught. I want it all to burn. If I'm going to pay, I'm paying in full."

*It will hurt,* Vaelith said. *After. You will be whole, and you will be hollow, and you will not know why the hollow aches. That is the cruelest mercy I can offer you: you will not miss what you cannot remember missing.*

"Good," said Cyrus, who was lying, and who knew the dragon knew it, and who cracked the first seal anyway.

* * *

The memories of Serah burned differently than the founding ones had.

Those had gone into the flame like rivers finding the sea. These went like blood returning to a heart. Each one struck the embers and detonated upward in a pillar of white-gold, and the mountain rang like a bell, and Cyrus fed it everything — campfire, kiss, road, laughter, the weight of Serah's head on her shoulder, the promise neither of them had dared speak aloud in Hollowmere's forgetting streets but had both heard anyway.

She was crying. She registered this from very far away, the tears hissing to steam as they neared the heat, and she kept pouring, because the fire was rising now, truly rising, climbing the walls of the wound in ropes and ribbons, and Vaelith was *breathing* — great bellows-drafts of air that fanned the blaze higher — and scales that had been grey were flushing copper at the edges, spreading inward like dawn overtaking a valley.

The last vial. The last kiss, the one at the mouth of the pass, when Serah had pressed her forehead to Cyrus's and said *go, I'll hold them, go* — Cyrus broke the seal with fingers that no longer felt like hers.

*I love you,* the memory said, in her own voice, in her own memory, words she hadn't known she'd stored away so carefully. *Whatever happens. Whatever either of us becomes. I love—*

The fire took it mid-word.

The roar that followed was not sound. It was the world inhaling. Flame sheeted up the chimney of the mountain in a column visible, perhaps, from the far borders of the realm, and Cyrus was thrown back across the glass floor, and Vaelith rose — *rose*, wings unfurling to fill the entire hollow, copper blazing, eyes twin suns — and the last thing Cyrus saw before the light swallowed everything was the dragon turning toward the broken sky like an answer to a question a thousand years old.

Then: silence, and warmth, and the smell of rain.

* * *

Cyrus woke on the mountainside, in grass, under stars.

Stars. Thousands of them, wheeling overhead in their ancient courses, so many and so bright that the dark between them looked deliberate, look *designed*. Down the slopes, the valleys of Thornfell glimmered with lantern-light along roads that ran straight and sure to towns that knew their own names. The border wards, wherever they stood, stood whole. She could feel it in her teeth, a low harmonic hum, the sound of a country remembering itself.

She was alive. Whole. Unburnt. She checked her hands, her arms, expecting ash, finding nothing.

She was also crying, and she did not know why.

The tears came steadily, without grief's architecture — no memory beneath them, no shape, just a leaking ache behind her sternum, as if something had been amputated so cleanly she couldn't even locate the wound. She wiped her face with the back of her hand and laughed, a small bewildered sound, and the laugh cracked in the middle.

"Why," she said to the stars, to the grass, to no one. "Why am I—"

Below her, at the foot of the mountain where the pass opened onto the old smuggler's road, torchlight moved. Figures. One of them walking slowly, limping, upright, coming toward her through the dark with a gait she found — inexplicably, unbearably — familiar.

Cyrus stood, wiped her eyes again, and started down the slope to meet a stranger who was watching her like her heart was breaking.
