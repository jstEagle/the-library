## 10. Veil

The realm remembered itself the way a body remembers a wound — slowly, and with a great deal of aching.

Serah Vey stood on the walls of Hollowmere in the first true dawn since the stars went out, watching light pour back over rooftops that had been half-erased for weeks. The bridge over the Mere was solid again, all of it at once, stone remembering stone. A woman below was weeping into her hands because she'd woken that morning knowing her name — Marta, Marta Ostell, keeper of bees — and couldn't stop saying it aloud, like a prayer or an apology.

No one knew why. No one knew what had been lost to buy it back.

That was the part Serah could not put down, could not set aside even to sleep: the realm had reknit itself seamlessly, history flowing smooth as river water over the place where something had been cut out. The archives in the Ember Court had rewritten themselves overnight. There was no record of a thief named Cyrus Amberdale. There was no record of the founding memories having ever been scattered. There was no record of Vaelith's mountain, even — the maps showed only unnamed peaks, white with snow, unremarkable.

The Court's siphon was broken; Ashgrave was in chains awaiting judgment by whatever remained of lawful authority, which was more than he deserved. And the woman who had paid for all of it — who had burned herself out of the world's memory like a name scratched from wet clay — walked free somewhere in Thornfell, not knowing why she sometimes stopped in the middle of roads and stared at nothing, not knowing why she wept.

Serah knew. Serah alone knew.

She turned her hand over and looked at the vial hanging from her neck on a leather cord, small as a thimble, warm against her sternum. Inside it swam a liquid light that did not behave like fire or like glass — it moved when she moved, settled when she stilled, and if she held it up to her eye she could see, faint and golden, a campfire on a smuggler's road, a pair of clever hands shuffling stolen memories like a cardsharp's deck, a laugh she would know anywhere, now and always, until her own fire went out.

Every stolen moment. She had copied them all, in those last hours on the mountain, while Cyrus slept exhausted against Vaelith's cooling flank and the dragon's ember-heart pulsed its slow dying rhythm beneath the stone. Serah was a warder of the Ember Court; she had been trained in the handling of memory since she was twelve years old, trained to carry vials through battle without spilling so much as a sigh of another person's past. She had never once used that training for anything but the Court's purposes.

She had never once been glad of it until that night.

* * *

It took her three weeks to find Cyrus Amberdale, which was three weeks longer than it should have taken, because Serah kept telling herself she shouldn't look.

She told herself it on the road south from Hollowmere. She told herself it in the drowned chapel, where she'd gone to stand in the cold water among the forgotten saints and found that the chapel remembered her now, its stones humming faintly under her boots, restored along with everything else. She told herself it most firmly of all in the Ember Court, on the day she surrendered her oath-pin to the interim council and walked out of the citadel without looking back, carrying nothing but her sword, her cloak, and a vial of another woman's love.

But her feet kept choosing smuggler's roads. Her feet, apparently, had made their own oath.

She found her at last near the Thornfell lowlands, where it had all begun — on a rutted track between hedgerows gone wild with autumn, in the gold-and-rust light of late afternoon. Cyrus was sitting on a milestone with her pack beside her and a map spread across her knees, and she was frowning at it the way she must have frowned at every map since the mountain, because the roads no longer obeyed her. The realm remembered its own ways now. It didn't need a thief to pave them out of sheer stubborn recollection.

She looked thinner. She looked tired. She looked, Serah thought with a lurch that had nothing to do with the road, exactly like someone who had been crying recently and didn't know why.

Cyrus heard her boots on the gravel and looked up, and her hand went instinctively to the knife at her belt — old habits, the habits of a woman who lifted memories from strangers' minds for coin — and then stopped, because some deeper habit than habit made her hesitate.

"Do I know you?" Cyrus asked.

It was the question Serah had rehearsed a hundred answers to, standing watch at night with the vial warm against her heart. *You captured me on a pass above Greywater. You bargained your freedom for my help. You kissed me in a corridor of the Ember Court with alarms ringing and told me you'd burn the world down before you let them have me. You fed our whole life into a fire to keep the stars shining.*

"No," Serah said, because none of the rehearsed answers were hers to give. "Not anymore."

Cyrus studied her — the warder's coat, the sword, the road-worn boots — and something flickered across her face, quick as a fish under ice. Recognition of nothing. Grief with no object. "You're Court," she said. "Or you were. The pin's gone."

"I left."

"People leave the Court for a lot of reasons." Cyrus folded the map with sharp, economical movements. "None of them end well for thieves."

"I'm not here for you." Serah came closer, slowly, the way one approaches a bird that might take flight. "I'm here because of this." She drew the cord over her head and held the vial out on her open palm, and the light inside it stirred, turning toward Cyrus like a flower toward sun.

Cyrus went very still. Serah watched her professional instincts war with something older and less explainable — watched her eyes narrow at the vial, cataloguing it, assessing its worth, and then widen slightly, involuntarily, as though the light had said something to her.

"What is it?" Cyrus whispered.

"Yours," Serah said. "Some of it. The best of it. It was going to be fuel for the fire at the heart of the world, and instead—" Her voice caught, and she hated how easily it caught, how three weeks of telling herself *don't look* had done nothing to armor her. "Instead I kept it. Every moment. Copied clean, the night before you carried them up the mountain. I was supposed to burn it with the rest. I couldn't."

"The fire." Cyrus's brow furrowed. "What fire? What mountain?"

And there it was — the whole shape of the sacrifice, laid bare in a single question asked without cruelty, without suspicion, with only the mild puzzlement of a stranger. Serah had imagined this moment would be the hardest thing she'd ever do. It was worse than imagining, because Cyrus wasn't a ghost of what she'd been; she was entirely, maddeningly alive, quick-eyed and guarded and beautiful in the slanting light, and she had no idea.

"You don't remember," Serah said. It wasn't a question. She'd known she would say it and still it came out broken. "Of course you don't. That was the price. The fire demanded a willing memory, freely given, and you gave it—" She stopped. Breathed. Started again, quieter. "You gave it everything we had. To save a country that doesn't remember you did it. That's why the stars are back. That's why Hollowmere knows its own name. That's why the roads go where they're supposed to go again."

Cyrus laughed — a short, disbelieving sound, the laugh of a woman hearing a madman's tale. But her hand had risen without her permission to press flat against her sternum, right over her own heart, as though something in there were knocking to get out.

"That's not possible," she said. "I'd know. Wouldn't I? If I'd given up something that big, I'd feel the hole."

"You do feel the hole." Serah stepped closer. Close enough to see that Cyrus's eyes were wet and that she herself was furious about it. "You cry at campfires. You cry at roads. You've been crying for three weeks at things you can't name, and everyone who sees you thinks you're strange, and you think so too. That's the hole. That's what's left of us."

"*Us,*" Cyrus repeated. Not mocking. Testing the word's weight, the way she'd test a vial for cracks.

"Us." Serah held the vial higher, between them, and the light inside flared gently gold. "There's a way back. Memory can be returned — slowly, a piece at a time, so it takes root instead of just pouring through you. It will hurt. Some of it will hurt a great deal. And you'd have every right to tell me to go to hell, because I'm the one who hunted you, and arrested you, and—" she smiled despite herself, despite everything, "—and fell in love with you anyway, somewhere between a campfire and a drowned chapel, in a country that was busy forgetting how to exist."

For a long moment Cyrus said nothing. The wind moved through the hedges, dry and rustling, full of the smell of coming frost. Somewhere down the lane a cart creaked, and a driver was singing — actually singing, tunelessly, a harvest song with all its verses intact, and Serah thought: *that too. We bought that too.*

"Why keep it?" Cyrus asked finally. Her voice was rough. "Why not burn it? Clean ending. No debt."

"Because a thing freely given shouldn't be spent twice," Serah said. "Because the realm got its memories back and it still owes you, even if it doesn't know it. And because—" She faltered, then refused to falter. "Because I loved you, Cyrus Amberdale, and love that gets erased isn't gone. It's just waiting for somewhere to live. I've been its somewhere for three weeks. I'd like to share the weight."

Cyrus looked at the vial. At her. At the long road running gold behind them both, going exactly where it was supposed to go, because a thief had once remembered it hard enough to hold it in existence.

"If I take them back," Cyrus said slowly, "I'll remember everything. Including that you lied to me on a mountainside. Including whatever else we were to each other." Her jaw tightened. "Including why I can't look at you without my chest hurting."

"Yes."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I'll carry it," Serah said simply. "For the rest of my life. Gladly. It's not a burden I mind."

Cyrus reached out — hesitated — and took the vial. The moment her fingers closed around the glass, the light inside leapt toward her skin like a dog recognizing home, and she made a small wounded sound, involuntary, and pressed the back of her free hand to her mouth.

"There's a campfire in here," she whispered. "I can feel it. Whose—"

"Ours," Serah said. "The first night. You made tea in a dented tin pot and complained about my cooking and then stole the memory of my mother's lullaby out of my head without asking, and I let you, because you hummed it better than I did."

Tears slid down Cyrus's face now, unhindered, unexplained, and she let them. "One at a time," she said at last, steadying herself against the milestone. "You said one at a time. So we start with the campfire. And then—" she turned the vial in her fingers, watching the light turn with it, "—then you'll tell me the rest. All of it. Even the parts that hurt. Especially those."

"It's a long list," Serah warned. "We walked a lot of roads."

"Good." Cyrus stood, shouldering her pack, and for the first time in three weeks — though she didn't know it was three weeks, though she'd never know unless Serah told her — she smiled. It was crooked and tired and it undid Serah completely. "I've got nowhere to be. Apparently I never saved the world or anything."

"No," Serah said, falling into step beside her as the dusk came down gentle and star-strewn overhead, every star in its ancient place. "Nothing like that. Just a road, and time enough to walk it."

Behind them, unnoticed, the milestone they'd sat upon had begun, very faintly, to glow — as if the land itself, remembering everything now except the one thing that mattered most, had decided to mark the spot anyway.
