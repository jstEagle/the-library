## 2. Thirteenth Turn Rising

The almanac was wrong.

Kestrel Thackeray had checked it three times before she allowed herself to believe it, and even then she checked a fourth time, because the alternative — that the sky itself had made an error in its bookkeeping — was not a thought her mind could hold without spilling something important out the sides.

She stood at the narrow window of her room above the Counting House, one shoulder against the cold stone, and counted moons. There were twelve. There had always been twelve: the Shepherd, pale and punctual; the Twins, never more than a hand's width apart; the great copper face of the Harvest Moon that rose fat over the granaries each autumn and set the whole city to counting. Twelve moons, twelve turns of the month, twelve columns in every calendar printed by the Guild's own press. The almanac on her desk — Guild-stamped, Guild-priced, Guild-true — listed them all with their risings and settings calculated to the minute.

And there, low over the eastern rooftops, wedged between the setting Twins like a coin slipped into a crack, hung a thirteenth sliver.

It was thin as a paring of silver. It did not quite seem to touch the sky so much as *insist* upon it, the way a debt insists upon a debtor. When she looked directly at it, it seemed to shrink, almost apologetic. When she looked away — when she caught it at the edge of her vision, over her shoulder, in the corner of the window glass — it swelled, bright and certain, as if it had only been waiting for her to stop watching.

"Twelve," Kestrel said aloud, to the empty room, in the tone she used on clerks who had misfiled entries. "There are twelve."

The sliver did not answer. But it did not go away, either.

* * *

By morning she had convinced herself of fog, of ice-crystal haze, of a reflection off the new glazing on the Temple of Accounts. She was very good at convincing herself of things. It was, in its way, the same skill as balancing a ledger: you found the number that made the columns agree, and you wrote it down, and after enough years the written number became the true one. The Guild paid her well for this skill. The Guild had owned her since she was nine years old, and it had taught her that truth was simply the entry no one had yet audited.

So she went down to work.

The Counting House of the Veiled Guild occupied an entire block in the high city, all grey granite and green bronze, its windows tall and narrow as ledger lines. Inside, three hundred clerks scratched in silence at three hundred desks, recording the commerce of a city that believed itself governed by a Council and a court of law. The Council levied taxes. The courts heard disputes. But the Guild held the debts, and the Guild recorded everything else — every contract, every inheritance, every marriage portion and funeral cost — and what the Guild recorded, the city slowly came to agree had happened.

Kestrel's own desk sat apart from the others, behind a rail of black iron, because she kept the ledgers no other clerk was permitted to open. The Impossible Accounts, the junior clerks called them, in whispers, when they thought she couldn't hear. She could always hear. She had ears like a moneylender.

That morning the topmost ledger was already waiting for her, which was wrong. Nothing arrived at her desk unsummoned. She stood over it a long moment before touching it — a habit from childhood, when anything left where she could reach it usually belonged to someone who would want payment for its loss — and then she opened it.

The entry was dated the previous night. Her own hand appeared to have written it, though she had been asleep, or at least abed, at the hour recorded.

*Received: one (1) prayer, unsolicited. Petitioner: Maren Ostrewe, laundress, Candlewick Row. Supplication offered to Saint Elluin (deceased 214 years). Requested: recovery of child (Ostrewe, Tam, age 6) from fever.*

Kestrel read it twice. Then a third time, more slowly, the way she read contracts that were about to hurt someone.

Prayers were not entered in the Impossible Accounts. Prayers were not entered anywhere. The Guild dealt in goods, debts, favors, futures — in things that could be weighed, or at least priced. A prayer was air given shape by desperation, and desperation was free, and free things did not appear in ledgers.

But below the entry, in the column marked *Consideration Received*, someone had written:

*Answered in full. Price: to be assessed.*

And beneath that, in ink so fresh it still shone:

*Assessment pending. Child recovered. Fever broke at the third bell. Witnessed.*

Kestrel sat down. Her chair creaked. Around her, three hundred pens continued their whispering, three hundred clerks went on believing the world was a place where numbers behaved.

Saint Elluin had been dead for two hundred and fourteen years. Kestrel knew this the way she knew the weight standards for gold: absolutely, professionally, without thinking about it. Dead saints did not answer prayers. Dead saints did not *invoice*.

*Price: to be assessed.*

She got out her pen. It was reflex, pure trained reflex, the clerk's answer to every anomaly: record it, categorize it, make it behave by writing it down. She dipped the nib, touched it to the page —

— and the ink refused.

It beaded up on the paper like water on wax, rolled, and fell off the edge of the world. She tried again. Again. On the third attempt the page drank the ink, but instead of her neat clerk's hand there bloomed a single word, in a script she did not know, all thorns and long descenders, as if the alphabet itself had grown claws:

*PATIENCE.*

Kestrel closed the ledger very gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping animal whose temper is unknown, and went to find her supervisor.

* * *

Master of Clerks Vess was a small dry man shaped like a folded receipt, and he received her report with the expression of a man listening to weather.

"A printing error in the almanac," he said. "An ink blot in the ledger. You've been working late, Thackeray."

"The ink moved, sir."

"Ink does many things when one has been working late." He shuffled a paper or two for the look of it. "You'll take the evening to rest. And Thackeray—" he did not look up, which meant the next words were not his own, "—the Master requires a copyist tonight. Sealed work. He's asked for you by name."

Kestrel's stomach performed a small, precise drop, like a coin into a deep well. "Asked for me, sir?"

"By name." Vess finally raised his eyes, and there was something in them she had never seen before, and it took her a moment to identify it because it was so entirely absent from the Counting House: pity. "Midnight. The Undercroft. Don't eat beforehand; the work is delicate." He returned to his papers, audience concluded. "And Thackeray. Whatever you see down there, you copy. You don't read ahead, you don't ask questions, and you don't remember anything you'd have to lie about later. That last part is a kindness. Take it."

* * *

She did not rest. She spent the hours until midnight doing what she always did with fear, which was filing it.

She inventoried what she knew, and the inventory was short. One: the moon had grown a sliver that belonged to no calendar. Two: a prayer to a two-century-dead saint had been answered and *logged*, logged, entered in the Impossible Accounts like a shipment of dye or a bill of lading, with the price still pending. Three: the masked Master of the Veiled Guild — whom she had seen perhaps six times in nineteen years, always at a distance, always at the head of some procession, his face a smooth blank of veiled silk — knew her name, and wanted her alone, at midnight, underground.

Four: Master Vess, who had never once in eleven years shown her anything warmer than mild disapproval, had pitied her.

She dressed warmly. Underground work was always cold; the Guild kept its deepest rooms at the temperature of cellars on principle, because cold preserved paper and paper was the only thing the Guild loved.

At half past the eleventh bell she let herself out through the clerks' door and crossed the inner courtyard, and there she stopped, because the thirteenth moon was overhead now, directly overhead, and it was larger.

Not much larger. A sliver grown to a fingernail's width of light. But the courtyard was open to the sky, nothing between her and it, and standing under it felt like standing under a held breath. The other twelve moons rode their familiar stations, indifferent and lovely. The thirteenth looked *down*. She could not have defended that word in any audit. But it looked down, and it looked at her, and somewhere in her chest a feeling stirred that she had no ledger-column for — a feeling like being recognized by something very old that had been reading over her shoulder for years.

"Copyist," said a voice behind her. "You're early. Good. Punctuality is the first form of worship."

* * *

The Undercroft lay beneath the Counting House, three levels down past the vaults, past the strongrooms where the Guild kept its hostage-contracts and its deeds to things that should not have been ownable — a river's mouth, a widow's grief, the naming rights to a street. Kestrel had been to the second level once, to witness an audit. No clerk went to the third.

The Master met her at the stairfoot, holding a lamp.

Up close, the veil was worse than the rumors. It was not cloth. It was something smooth and pale and faintly translucent, fitted to the skull like a second skin, and beneath it she could almost — *almost* — see the suggestion of features, the way one sees fish under ice. His voice came from somewhere slightly to the left of his mouth.

"You balance my books," he said. It was not a question, and it was not praise. It sounded like a man confirming the weight of a coin by biting it.

"Yes, Master."

"And you do not wonder about them."

"I wonder about everything, Master. I write down what I'm told."

Something moved beneath the veil that might have been a smile. "Yes. That is why it must be you." He turned, lamp swinging, and led her down a corridor lined with doors — dozens of doors, iron-banded, each stamped with a seal she didn't recognize, each humming faintly against her teeth the way a struck bell hums after the sound stops. "Most copyists are instruments. They reproduce what is placed before them and retain nothing, because there is nothing in them to retain it *with*. You are different. You are a ledger yourself, Thackeray. Everything filed. Nothing lost. I require a copyist who cannot help remembering."

"That sounds like a danger to me, Master."

"It is a danger to everyone," he said pleasantly, "which is why the work is sealed."

The chamber at the corridor's end was round, and cold, and lit by a single shaft of moonlight falling from a lens in the ceiling far above — and Kestrel's skin crawled before her mind caught up, because she knew that light. Pale, thin, insistent. The thirteenth moon was directly overhead, and its light came down this shaft like a thread dropped through a needle's eye, and at the center of the floor, on a lectern of black stone, lay a book.

It was large as a paving slab and bound in something that was not leather. Its pages, where they showed at the edges, were the color of old ivory. Chains ran from its spine to rings in the stone floor, and the chains were not rusted, though they looked older than the city.

"The Codex Meridian," the Master said. "Older than the Guild. Older than the twelve moons, if you can credit such a thing — and you will, before morning." He gestured to a desk set in the moonlight's edge: quills, ink, blank vellum of astonishing whiteness. "You will copy it exactly. Every character. You will begin at the marked page and proceed forward. You will not turn back. You will not skip. If your eyes water, blink and continue. If your hands shake, grip the desk and continue. If you hear the book speak —" the veil tilted toward her, considering, "— do not answer it."

"Does it speak?"

"Only to people it wants something from." He set the lamp down. Its flame bent toward the codex, like grass in wind. "Begin."

He left. The door closed. The chains did not rattle, though the air in the room moved as if something enormous had shifted its weight.

Kestrel stood a long time in the cold. Then, because she was what the Guild had made her — because the alternative to obeying was thinking, and thinking was a luxury billed at ruinous rates — she sat, opened the codex to the ribbon-marked page, dipped her quill, and began to copy.

The script was the thorned alphabet from her ledger. It resisted her at first, squirming under the eye, resolving into sense only when she stopped trying to read and merely transcribed, letter by blind letter, the way one copies a signature one must not understand. An hour passed. Two. The moonlight crept across the floor. Her wrist ached. The words assembled themselves in her hand faster than her mind could refuse them, and so it was several minutes before she realized she was no longer copying nonsense.

It was a list. A census. Names, in columns, thousands of them, each followed by a date and a single word: *sealed*, or *spent*, or — rarely, in a prouder hand — *kept*.

She copied. She told herself she wasn't reading. She read.

*...Aldous Meridian, door-keeper, sealed. Ysolt Meridian, door-keeper, spent. Corvane, door-keeper, kept...*

Door-keepers. The word rang a bell she could not find in any tower of her memory — a phrase from a nursery rhyme, maybe, or a curse her grandmother used to mutter over the wash. *Doors and keepers, doors and keepers.* Grandmother Sef had said strange things at the end. Everyone had agreed it was age.

The columns marched on. Centuries of names. And then the dates changed — the handwriting changed, newer, tighter, and the entries grew sparse, one every few decades, and the word beside each name was always the same: *assessed*.

*Price: to be assessed.* The phrase from her ledger. Her blood went cold and then colder.

She reached the bottom of the page. The next entry began at the top of the following leaf, and her hand turned it before her mind could object — and there, in the newest ink, in handwriting she knew intimately because she sharpened the pen that wrote it every morning, was the Master's entry. Dated tomorrow.

*Received: one (1) copyist, Thackeray line. Assessment: heir, direct. Status:*

And then the ink blurred, swam, reformed — the codex writing itself even as she watched, letters surfacing through the page like drowned things rising — and finished the sentence in her grandmother's hand, in the looping script Kestrel had last seen on a washing-list twenty years ago:

*Status: the door remembers its keeper. Wake her gently. She is the key, and keys break.*

The moonlight in the shaft above flickered, and for one instant the whole chamber was lit silver-bright, and every chain on the codex rang like a struck bell.

Kestrel did not remember deciding to run. She remembered the cold of the stairs, the doors humming against her teeth, the taste of blood where she'd bitten her lip. At the top of the Undercroft steps, breath tearing, she put her hand flat against the wall to steady herself — and felt, faintly, under the stone, under the city, under everything, a slow rhythmic pull, patient as tide, like a vast door being tested on its hinges by fingers the size of seasons.

Above her, through the courtyard's open sky, the thirteenth moon had grown another hair's breadth wider.

And in her pocket, folded twice, was the page she had torn out without deciding to — the page bearing her own name, and the words she would carry into every chapter that followed:

*Wake her gently.*
