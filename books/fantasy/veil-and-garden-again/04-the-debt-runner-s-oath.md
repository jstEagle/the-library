## 4. The Debt-Runner's Oath

Bramble Quince had three rules for a good night's work, and he recited them to himself the way other men said prayers: *never carry what you can't carry laughing; never sell a debt back to the man who owes it; never trust a door that opens too easily.*

The third rule was why he was crouched in the rafters of the Weighhouse annex at half past midnight, watching a clerk in Guild grey copy forbidden pages by candlelight, and wondering which of them was about to have a very bad night.

He'd come for the tithe-schedules. Simple job. The debt-runners' crew had a client in Tanner's Row — a widow whose mill-levey had been sold three times over, each sale buried in a different ledger, until no one could say who owned her debt or what it cost. Bramble's trade was exactly that kind of saying. He smuggled forgiven debts the way other men smuggled brandy: found them, proved them, and delivered them into the debtor's hands like a loaf of bread still warm from the oven. Illegal as sin in six parishes. Legal as morning in none. Profitable everywhere.

The widow's paper was supposed to be in the annex strongroom, third shelf, behind the box of delinquent ferry-tolls. Bramble had picked that lock before supper, lifted the schedules, and been halfway to the window when the candle below him guttered alight and a woman sat down at a desk that should have been empty, opened a ledger that should have been locked, and began to copy pages that should not exist.

He should have left. That was rule one's ugly cousin: *never be where you are when the trouble starts.* But Bramble had looked down through the gap in the joists and seen the ledger's spine, and the spine had a mark on it he knew. A circle with thirteen notches. And Bramble Quince, cheerful smuggler of forgiven debts, coward in all matters except this one, stayed.

* * *

The woman wrote like someone praying. That was the first thing he noticed, after the fear. Her pen moved in long even strokes, unhurried, but every few lines she stopped — not to think, he realized, but to breathe, the way you stop when you've read something that has changed the shape of the room. She was copying out of a slim codex bound in grey leather, and she was copying it into a cheap schoolbook primer, the kind children learned their letters in, because nobody searches a child's primer.

Clever girl, Bramble thought. Clever girls died fastest in this city, but they died interestingly.

Then she turned a page and went absolutely still, and Bramble watched her read her own name.

He couldn't see it from the rafters, not at that distance, not by that light. But he saw her hand come up and press flat against her mouth, and he saw her shoulders do the thing shoulders do when a person is deciding whether to scream or to be sick, and he thought: *well. There it is.* Everyone who touched the thirteenth-notched circle eventually found their own name in it. That was rather the point of the thing.

She worked another hour. Bramble's legs went numb twice. When she finally closed the codex, she did something that made his heart drop through three floors of the building: she took out a razor-blade, slit the binding of the primer, slid four folded pages inside, and restitched it with thread pulled from her own cuff. Then she put the codex back exactly where it had been, licked her thumb, and smoothed away the one spot where her sleeve had dusted the shelf.

And then she blew out her candle, stood, and said clearly into the dark above her:

"You can come down now. Your knee cracked forty minutes ago."

Bramble considered, briefly, the dignity of denial. "That," he said, dropping lightly from the beam, "was the building settling."

"The building settles toward the river. You settled toward me." She had a knife out — small, neat, held correctly, which surprised him more than the knife. Up close she was younger than the ledgers had made her sound, with ink stains to the second knuckle and shadows under her eyes like bruises from a fight she was losing slowly. Kestrel Thackeray. He knew the name; everyone in the low city who owed money knew the name. The clerk who balanced the impossible books. The one whose figures were so clean that even the debt-brokers quoted them like scripture.

"Kestrel Thackeray," he said, and bowed, because flattery was cheaper than fighting. "Bramble Quince, at your service and slightly at your mercy."

"I know who you are." The knife didn't waver. "Debt-runner. You steal debts that have already been paid and give them back."

"Steal is such a—" He waved a hand. "Yes. Exactly that. It sounds terrible when you say it like that."

"It *is* terrible. It's forgery and theft and interference with lawful instruments." She said it in the flat voice of someone reciting something she needed to believe. Her eyes flicked once to the shelf where the codex sat, and Bramble watched the arithmetic happen behind them: *he saw. He knows what I copied. One word from him and I hang.*

"And yet," Bramble said gently, "you're standing there deciding whether to cut my throat or buy my silence, instead of ringing the alarm bell that's four feet from your elbow. So let's skip the part where we pretend you're still the Guild's creature." He spread his hands, empty, smiling his best smile — the one that had talked him past two harbor guards and one very confused bishop. "I saw nothing. That's my trade, seeing nothing. But I'll tell you what I *do* see, free of charge."

"Free things from strangers are how people end up in the canal."

"Ha! True. Very true. You'd love my mother." He sobered, because the next part deserved sobriety. "I see a woman who just read her own name in a book that predates the city charter, and whose hands are shaking, and who has nowhere safe to go tomorrow. Because the Guild watches its clerks closer than its ledgers, Thackeray, and the Master's masks have eyes in the back of more heads than you'd credit. You stole pages tonight. In about three days, maybe five if the moon's feeling lazy, someone will notice the stitch-marks on your cuff are fraying, or a page-count won't tally, or some little bird in the counting room will sing for a copper. And then there'll be a warrant with your name spelled perfectly, because I promise you, they spell the names of people they intend to break *very* carefully."

The knife lowered an inch. Not surrender — recalculation. He'd seen soldiers do the same arithmetic.

"Why would you help me?" she asked. "You don't know me."

"No," Bramble agreed. "But I know the book you touched. My grandmother ran numbers for a shrine in Salt Lane, back before the shrines went quiet. She used to say the old gods kept accounts too, and theirs were the only ones that never balanced." He shrugged. "Anyone who reads that book becomes mine to look after. Crew rule. We're sentimental that way."

"You have a crew?"

"Of the finest disreputable souls in Amberfall. A defrocked priest who talks to her soup. A lock-girl who could open your future. A cook who owes money to everyone including himself." He grinned. "We meet Tuesdays. There's stew."

"That's your pitch. Stew and a defrocked priest."

"My pitches are famously bad. It's why I'm rich." He wasn't, particularly, but confidence was a currency and he minted it freely. "Look — I'm not asking you to trust me. Trust is a debt, and I told you, I only traffic in the forgiven kind. I'm asking you to keep the primer under your cloak, walk home the long way, and if the walls start closing in, come to the Broken Scale on Cordwainer Street and ask for the man who owes the barmaid an apology. That's all. A door, left unlocked. Whether you walk through it is yours."

For a long moment she just looked at him, and Bramble had the uncomfortable sensation of being audited. He imagined this was what it felt like to be a ledger under her pen: every figure weighed, every sum tested, mercy granted only where the columns justified it.

"If this is a trap," she said at last, "it's an elaborate one."

"All the best ones are. Traps built cheap catch rats. Traps built well catch clerks." He tipped an imaginary hat. "Tuesday. Stew. Think on it."

He went out the window the way he'd come, down the drainpipe, along the roofline, dropping into the alley smelling of tar and rain. Only when his feet hit the cobbles did he let himself feel the cold knot in his stomach unclench — and reform immediately into something else. Because he hadn't lied to her, and lying would have been easier. He *did* know the book. He'd seen the thirteen notches once before, years ago, on a document his crew had burned unread at Sister Ovel's screaming insistence, and he had spent every night since wondering what he'd fed to the fire.

* * *

The Broken Scale at midday looked like what it was: a tavern that had lost an argument with a flood and never fully recovered, tables warped into gentle waves, a bar tilted just enough that drinks crept toward the cheap seats. Bramble's crew had claimed the back room, the one with the door that locked from the inside and the window that opened onto a roof no sane person would cross.

Sister Ovel was arguing with her soup again. She did that when she was worried — muttered at it in liturgical cadence, as though grace could be administered to root vegetables. She'd been defrocked eleven years, for reasons she gave differently every time, and she wore her old cassock dyed the orange of cheap saffron so no one could mistake her for clergy. Today the muttering had an edge to it.

"—and furthermore, if you think I don't notice you watering the wine, Ferren, the god of honest measures notices, and unlike me he holds grudges—"

"Sister," Bramble said, sliding into the seat across from her, "I need you to stop blessing lunch and listen."

"I never bless lunch. Lunch needs forgiveness, not blessing." But she set down her spoon, and her eyes — quick, dark, older than her face — sharpened. "You've got your look. The look you get when you've found something and wish you hadn't."

"I found a clerk copying the grey codex."

The room went quiet in the particular way rooms go quiet around Ovel. At the far table, Ferren the cook stopped pretending to inventory turnips. Little Wren, the lock-girl, who had been picking the padlock off the sugar box purely for practice, went still with the pick still in the keyhole.

Ovel said, very softly, "Which clerk."

"Kestrel Thackeray. The Guild's balance-keeper. The one who untangled the Ferryman's Riot accounts." He watched Ovel's face do something complicated — grief and recognition wrestling for the same ground. "You know her."

"I know her *grandmother*," Ovel said. "Maren Thackeray. Door-keeper's line, last of it, or so I believed until about ten seconds ago." She pushed the soup away like it had personally failed her. "Bramble. What did the girl copy?"

"Pages. Four of them, folded into a primer. I didn't read them." He held up a hand before Ovel could speak. "Before you ask — yes, I invited her here. Tuesday. Stew."

"You invited a Guild senior clerk into our only safehouse."

"I invited a frightened woman with her own name in a forbidden book into a room with a back exit and excellent soup. It's called recruitment. You should try sounding pleasant sometime, it works wonders."

"Bramble." Ovel leaned forward, and now her voice had dropped into the register she probably once used from pulpits, the one that made sinners sit up straight. "Do you understand what the grey codex *is*? What the thirteen notches mean? Because I helped close that door, whatever's left of me that remembers, and I will tell you plainly: the people who open it again will not be heroes. They will be brokers. They will find a way to sell what comes through, and what comes through will *let* them, because that is how it hunts."

"So we warn her off. Burn her copies. Scare her straight."

"And if she's already marked?" Ovel's jaw tightened. "If her blood has already answered? Maren's granddaughter, Bramble. Of all the ink-stained clerks in that marble mausoleum, hers is the name in the margins. That is not coincidence. That is *inheritance*."

Little Wren spoke up from the sugar box, around the pick in her teeth. "So we take her in."

"We *what*," said Ferren.

"Take her in. Feed her. Teach her which doors bite." Wren was sixteen and had the moral complexity of a well-thrown brick. "You always say the crew takes in people the books have broken. She's the most broken book in the city right now."

"She's a *risk*," Ferren protested. "She's Guild. She balances their ledgers. For all we know she's bait on a hook with a very pretty knot."

"She stitched stolen pages into a child's primer using thread from her own cuff," Bramble said quietly. "That's not bait behavior. Bait doesn't sew. Bait gets dressed by professionals." He looked around the table: Ovel rigid with old guilt, Wren certain, Ferren calculating the price of everything including loyalty. His crew. His ridiculous, precious, doomed crew. "Here's how I see it. Either she's a trap, in which case we'll know by the second bowl of stew, or she's the most dangerous person in Amberfall walking around with no one at her back. Both ways, I'd rather she ate Tuesday's stew than the canal's water. Motion carried?"

Nobody moved. Then Ovel sighed the sigh of a woman whose prayers had been answered incorrectly for decades, and said, "Carried. But I want to hear her say her grandmother's name herself. If she flinches, we know. If she doesn't flinch—" the old priest closed her eyes "—then gods help us, we know worse."

* * *

Kestrel Thackeray came on Wednesday, not Tuesday, which Bramble decided was promising — cautious people lived longer, and he liked living vicariously through the cautious.

She arrived at dusk through the front door like any customer, ordered nothing, and stood in the tilting bar looking deeply, comprehensively out of place, the way a fish looks in a fruit tree. Bramble ambled over, wiping his hands on a rag that had never once cleaned anything.

"You're a day late," he said.

"Tuesday I followed three separate men to make sure none of them followed me. It took longer than expected." Her eyes swept the room, cataloguing exits. "Your security is terrible."

"Our security is a priest who hears confessions she's not licensed to hear and a girl who can pick the locks of anyone thinking about attacking us. Also the stew is genuinely good. This way."

In the back room, introductions happened the way they always did: Wren stared with frank curiosity, Ferren served food with the grudging precision of a man measuring out portions against possible betrayal, and Ovel sat with her hands folded, watching Kestrel the way one watches a fuse burn toward powder.

They got through the soup — lamb and barley, heavy on the pepper, Ferren's one act of generosity — before Ovel set down her spoon and said, "Your grandmother was Maren Thackeray."

It wasn't a question, but Kestrel treated it like one. Bramble watched her weigh it, watch her decide — visibly, almost physically — how much truth this strange table could hold. "Yes," she said. "You knew her?"

"I knew *of* her. Late in her life." Ovel's voice was careful, each word placed like a stone in a ford. "What do you remember of her?"

"Ledgers." A ghost of a smile, there and gone. "She taught me my numbers before the Guild taught me theirs. She had a book of her own, handwritten. Rules for keeping accounts. Most of it was ordinary — double entries, dates, witnesses. But the last page..." Kestrel frowned, reaching for something. "The last page said: *some debts are paid in coin, some in blood, and some in names. Never sign the third kind.* I thought it was a joke about contracts. Grandmother jokes were mostly grim."

The silence that followed had weight. Ferren stopped chewing. Wren looked between the adults like a spectator at a match she didn't understand.

"*Never sign the third kind,*" Ovel repeated. She looked suddenly very tired, and very old, and Bramble understood that the defrocked priest was looking at a dead woman's granddaughter and seeing a debt of her own coming due. "Child, I need to tell you something, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide anything. Not tonight. Tonight you eat, and you sleep in the room upstairs, and tomorrow—"

"Tomorrow I go back to work," Kestrel said. "I have a shift. If I vanish the day after copying those pages, they'll know. I have to be boring. I have to be *exactly* myself." She said it steadily, but her hand had gone to her cuff, to the frayed place where the thread had been, and Bramble saw that underneath the steadiness she was holding herself together the way she'd stitched the primer — with whatever material was nearest, and not much of it.

"Then be boring," Bramble said cheerfully, "and come back Thursday. And Friday. Consider this your second ledger, Thackeray. No interest, no penalties, no collateral." He raised his cup of watered wine. "All we ask is that when the day comes — and it will come, it's practically a law of nature — you remember there was a door here, and it was unlocked, and there was stew."

Kestrel looked at him for a long moment. Something in her face shifted — not trust, not yet, trust was a debt and she signed nothing tonight — but perhaps the acknowledgment that a door existed.

"A debt with no terms," she said. "My grandmother would have called that either charity or a trap."

"Grandmothers," said Bramble, "are famously suspicious. It's how they live so long."

She almost smiled. It was, he would think later, remembering everything that came after — the warrants, the cell, the moon with its impossible thirteenth sliver rising fat and wrong over the rooftops — the moment he decided she was one of theirs. Not because she smiled. Because she *almost* did, and then checked it, the way careful people check everything, and he thought: *yes. This one counts every coin. Which means when she spends one, it will mean something.*

She left before full dark, walking the long way, checking her shadows. Ovel watched her go from the window with an expression Bramble had never seen on her — not fear exactly, and not hope, but the terrible parent of both.

"She has Maren's hands," the old priest said quietly. "The same way of holding a pen like a knife."

"Well," said Bramble, collecting bowls, "let's pray she never has to use it like one."

Ovel turned from the window, and the firelight caught her face, and for the first time since he'd known her, Sister Ovel looked frightened of something that wasn't her own memory.

"Prayer," she said, "is rather the problem, Bramble. Haven't you heard? The old gods have started answering again."

Outside, over the crooked rooftops of the low city, the moon hung in the sky — twelve silver slivers and, faint as breath on glass, a thirteenth that no almanac had ever promised.
