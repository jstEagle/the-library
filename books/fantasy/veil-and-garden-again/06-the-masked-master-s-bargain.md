## 6. The Masked Master's Bargain

The summons came at the hour when the Guild's lamps were trimmed low and the clerks went home to their suppers — a folded card of grey vellum slipped beneath Kestrel's ledger-weight, unsigned, unsealed, smelling faintly of cold iron and something sweeter underneath, like lilies left too long in a closed room.

*The Master will see you. The Long Stair. Midnight.*

She read it three times. Her hands did not shake, which surprised her; she had spent the day expecting them to. Ever since Bramble's crew had vanished into the low city with her copied pages sewn into a smuggler's coat, she had been waiting for this — the knock, the card, the quiet voice asking her to explain herself. She had rehearsed denials over her cold dinner. *I copied nothing. The codex was misfiled. The margins were already marked.*

Now, holding the summons, she understood that rehearsal had been wasted breath. The Master did not summon clerks to ask questions he didn't know the answers to.

She dressed with care. Not her finest clothes — that would look like ambition — but not her shabbiest either. A clerk summoned at midnight should look like a clerk: sober wool, ink-stained cuffs, hair pinned back without ornament. Armor made of ordinariness. In the pocket of her coat, where no search would think to look because no search would be so rude, she tucked her grandmother's notes, wrapped in oilcloth. If she was walking into the dark, she would not walk into it empty-handed.

The Long Stair began behind the Hall of Weights, past the door no one opened and everyone pretended not to see. It descended farther than the Guildhall had foundations, farther than any building had any right to reach, and the walls changed as she went — dressed stone, then rough-hewn, then stone so old its chisel-marks had been worn to silk by hands long dead. Lamps burned in niches every twenty steps, and none of them guttered, though there was no draft to steady them and no wick she could see. They burned the way things burned down here: patiently, and without needing anything.

Her footsteps echoed wrong. Too many footsteps. As if someone walked a half-pace behind her, matching her exactly, and stopped when she stopped.

She did not look back. Looking back on the Long Stair was one of those things clerks learned not to do in their first year, along with counting the steps aloud and asking what lay below the counting-house. The Guild was full of such lessons, delivered sideways, absorbed like dye into cloth. *Don't ask about the thirteenth column of the great ledger. Don't touch the sealed codex. Don't wonder why the Master's mask has no eye-holes.*

At the bottom, the stair opened into a round chamber lit by a single brazier, and the Master stood beside it with his back to her, reading.

He was smaller than she had imagined. That was always the way with monsters built up by whispers — they came out human-sized, or worse than human-sized, some terrible ordinary height. He wore grey robes without sigil or rank, and the mask covered him from crown to jaw: smooth, pale, featureless except for the suggestion of features, the way fog suggests faces. No eye-holes. No mouth-slit. And yet when he spoke, his voice came clear and dry as paper turning.

"Kestrel Thackeray." He did not turn around. "You balance my books beautifully. Do you know how rare that is? Most clerks balance them adequately. You balance them *honestly*. You find the errors I leave for you, and the ones I don't."

"Master." She bowed, low enough to be respectful, shallow enough to keep her eyes up. "You honor me."

"I ruin you, more likely." He turned then, and the mask caught the brazier-light and gave nothing back. In his hands was a book — not the sealed codex, but something older, its cover scarred leather gone soft as cloth. "Sit. This will take some time, and I dislike conducting business with people standing on ceremony. Ceremony is for people who intend to lie to each other formally."

There was only one chair. She took it. He remained standing, which put him above her, which was, she suspected, the entire point of the chair.

"You copied forty-one pages from the Codex of Turns," he said.

The words landed in her chest like stones dropped down a well. She heard herself say, "Master, I—"

"Don't." The word was gentle, and that gentleness was more frightening than any shout. "I have watched you work for six years, Kestrel Thackeray. I know how you lie, because you barely do. When you're caught, you go still, and your mind runs forward to consequences instead of backward to excuses. It's why you're the best ledger-keeper I have ever employed, and it's why I'm going to tell you the truth now, since lies between us would waste both our evenings."

He set the old book on a stand beside the brazier and opened it. From where she sat she could see pages dense with script in a hand that slanted hard, written fast, by someone who feared running out of time.

"Forty-one pages," he said again. "You chose well. The passages on the moon-counts. The rites of closing. The names of the door-keepers." A pause, delicate as a blade laid flat against skin. "You looked for your grandmother's name. You found it. Page two hundred and six, third line from the bottom, struck through in red — the way we strike through debts that have been paid."

Kestrel's mouth had gone dry. "How long have you known?"

"Since the morning after you did it. The codex is sealed with more than wax, child. It remembers who opens it, and it tells me." He tilted the blank mask, and though it had no eyes, she felt *seen*, thoroughly, the way a ledger page feels seen under an auditor's lamp. "What I have not known — until tonight — is what you intend to do with what you took."

"Nothing," she said. And then, because he had asked for truth and because lying to him felt like lying to a mirror: "Not yet. I don't know yet. That's the truth. I wanted to understand what the Guild is buying. What the prayers are. Why a dying child's miracle costs blood, and whose blood, and why the price goes up every season."

"Ah." Something moved behind the mask — or perhaps it was only the light. "You've been reading the small miracles."

"The Ledger of Small Miracles. Yes."

"And what conclusion did you come to? Before you ran to the debt-runners and their defrocked priest?"

The chamber seemed to lean in. Even the fire held its crackle. She thought of Bramble's grin, of Ovel's ruined hands, of the wine weeping from the shrine in the gutter-district, and she chose the truth again, because it was the only coin she had left.

"That the Guild isn't selling miracles," she said. "It's selling *doors*. Little ones. Cracks. And every sale pries them wider."

For a long moment the Master said nothing. Then he laughed — a soft, dry sound, like wind through a keyhole, and somehow worse than anger.

"Yes," he said. "That's exactly right. Which is why you're the only person in this city I can have this conversation with, and why the next thing I say will sound like madness or mercy, depending on how much you trust me. Which, I grant, is not much."

He crossed to her — three steps, unhurried — and held out his open palm. On it lay a ring of black metal, plain as a funeral band.

"This is an offer of freedom," he said. "Complete. Your family's debt — all four generations of it, including the portion recorded as your grandmother's asset transfer — dissolved. Your contract burned. Your name struck from every book I keep. You could walk out of here tomorrow and no warrant, no brand, no collector would ever touch you again. You could go to the low city and live with your smugglers and your priest until you die comfortably of old age, and the Guild would forget you ever balanced a line."

Hope rose in her like water filling a hold — sudden, warm, terrifying. She knew, even as she felt it, that hope offered this easily was a hook dressed as a gift. But knowing did not stop the rising. Four generations. Her father's bent shoulders. Her mother's silence. Her grandmother, entered in a ledger as *property transferred*, and Kestrel had grown up believing that was simply what happened to poor people who died owing.

"What does it cost?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved.

"The moon," the Master said.

He let that sit. Outside, far above them, past tons of stone and earth, the sky was doing its impossible arithmetic — twelve moons where almanacs promised eleven, and a thin thirteenth sliver growing at the edge of the count like a crack growing across glass.

"In nine nights," he said, "the thirteenth turn completes. The old gods have found the seam. They are prying, Kestrel, and they are patient, and they are *hungry* — hungrier than you can imagine, because they have been shut out for a hundred years while this city grew fat on scraps sold through my market. When the turn completes, the door opens. All the way. And everything that comes through it will make the Guild's worst night-market auction look like a children's festival."

"So close it," she said. "You have the codex. You have the rites. You have—" she gestured at the black ring, at the chamber, at the whole buried weight of the Guild, "—all of this."

"The rites require a door-keeper." The mask inclined, very slightly. "And the last door-keeper's line runs through exactly one living person. Through you, Kestrel Thackeray. Through your blood."

The brazier popped. Somewhere above them, faint as memory, a bell rang the midnight quarter.

"My blood," she repeated.

"A door-keeper seals with what they are. Your grandmother sealed once — that's why her death was logged as an asset transfer. The Guild acquired what remained of her, and it was considerable." He spoke of it the way he might speak of grain futures, and she hated him for it, and hated more the part of her that understood: this was what the ledgers did to everyone eventually. Everything became entries. Even grief. Even gods. "The rite will take yours. Not all of it — I am not asking for your death. But enough that you will feel winter in your bones for the rest of your life, and enough that no god will ever mistake you for an open door again."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then in nine nights the city learns what the old gods kept in their fists before we locked them out. And your smuggler friends, and your priest, and ten thousand souls in the low city learn it first, because hunger starts at the bottom." He said it without threat, without relish — a man reporting weather. That was the horror of him. He was not cruel. He was *accurate*. "Or — third option — you take the codex pages you copied, run to the priest Ovel, and attempt the rite yourselves, badly, with half the knowledge and none of the authority. I give that perhaps one chance in five of working, and four chances in five of opening the door faster."

The trap of it unfolded in her mind with terrible elegance. Every road led through her veins. Refuse, and the city fell. Flee, and probably hasten the fall. Agree, and she bound herself to the masked master of the miracle-trade — the man whose books she had balanced, whose purchases she had begun to suspect of being crimes against heaven itself — trusting him to take only what he promised and nothing more.

"And the freedom?" she said. "The ring. Is that part of the payment, or the bait?"

"Both," he said simply. "I find honesty works better than flattery with people like you. Take the bargain, and the moment the rite is done — successfully or not — your debt dies and you walk away free. That is my oath. And unlike the gods, Kestrel, I am still capable of keeping mine."

*Are you?* she almost asked. *Which god did you eat to live this long?* Because she had begun to see it, in the margins of the ledgers, in the arithmetic that never quite closed: the Master did not age the way men aged. His purchases went back further than any mortal contract should. Whatever he was, he had bought his centuries one miracle at a time, and she was standing in the vault where he kept the change.

But she said none of it. Instead she looked at the black ring on his palm and did the thing she did best, the thing that had made her the finest ledger-keeper the Guild had ever employed.

She counted.

Nine nights. Enough time to buy — time for Ovel to teach her what the codex really said, time for Bramble's crew to move, time to learn whether the Master's rite and her grandmother's notes agreed or contradicted, time to discover what a door-keeper's heir could do besides bleed. Nine nights was not freedom. Nine nights was *leverage*, and leverage was the only currency that had ever meant anything in this city.

"I agree," she said.

The word left her and the chamber seemed to exhale.

The Master extended the ring. She took it — the metal was cold, then warm, then cold again, as if deciding what it thought of her pulse. He guided it onto the fourth finger of her right hand, the ledger-hand, and it settled there snug as a scar.

"Wear it always," he said. "It marks the bargain. The gods will smell it. Let them. Let them know a door is being guarded." He stepped back, and the brazier-light bent away from him slightly, the way crowds part. "Return to the Long Stair in eight nights, at this hour. Come alone. Come fed and rested — the rite is long, and I need your blood slow, not spilled."

"Master." She rose. Her legs held, which felt like a private miracle. "One question. The child. The prayer I found in the small ledger — the girl with the fever, priced in blood. Whose blood paid for her life?"

The mask regarded her for a long, still moment.

"Mine," he said. "It is always mine, in the end. That is the secret of the miracle-trade, Kestrel, and the reason I need a door-keeper so desperately. I am spending myself to hold the seam shut, and I am very nearly spent." The dry voice softened by a degree, and somehow that was the most frightening thing yet. "Whatever you believe I am — and you believe such inventive things — remember that I am the lock, not the key. The key has your face."

She climbed the Long Stair with the ring cold on her finger and her grandmother's notes pressed against her heart, and with every step she told herself the same lie she intended to spend nine nights perfecting:

*I agreed. I didn't deceive anyone. I'm going to do exactly what I promised.*

Behind her, far below, the brazier-light failed all at once, like a held breath finally released — and in the dark it left behind, something on the stairs began, softly, to follow her up.
