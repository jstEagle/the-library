## 7. What the Priest Buried

The salt cellar was the first thing to go wrong.

Sister Ovel had been a priest of the Ninefold Choir for thirty-one years, and even defrocked, even hiding in a smuggler's den beneath a tannery that smelled like boiled leather and regret, her hands still knew the old offices. She salted the stew the way she had once salted altars — three pinches, clockwise, a blessing disguised as seasoning — and when she set the cellar down, it cracked clean through, spilling white across the table in a perfect ring.

Bramble looked at the ring of salt. Then at her.

"That's new," he said.

"Old," Ovel said. "It's very old."

She should have lied. She had been lying for eleven years, since the night she'd walked out of the Cathedral of the Turning Veil with her vestments folded under one arm and her name struck from the rolls, and lying had become as natural to her as breathing. But the salt had drawn its circle, and circles were for truth-telling, and some habits went deeper than survival.

She sat down across from Bramble and said, "I need to tell you what I did. Before Kestrel finds out some other way."

* * *

The den beneath the Tallowman's Rest was not built for confessions. It was built for hiding things: casks of untaxed brandy, ledgers scrubbed of names, debt-bonds bought cheap from desperate families and held until they could be sold dearer or forgiven outright, depending on Bramble's mood and the phase of the moon. Six people lived there when everyone was home, sleeping in hammocks strung between shelving like moths in a cabinet. The walls sweated. The single lamp burned whale oil that Bramble swore was legally acquired, which meant it wasn't.

Ovel waited until the crew had scattered — Ferro up top minding the trapdoor, the twins out running a forgiveness run through the Weavers' Quarter, little Sef asleep in his corner with a stolen almanac open on his chest. Only then did she take down the tin box she kept behind the dried herbs, the one she'd carried out of the cathedral eleven years ago, and set it on the table between herself and Bramble.

"You were a priest," Bramble said. Not a question. He'd known since the night he'd fished her out of the canal, half-drowned, still wearing one sleeve of a cassock. He'd never asked which order, which office, which sin. That was why she'd stayed. "You want to unburden yourself now? Eleven years later?"

"I want to arm you," Ovel said. "There's a difference."

She opened the box.

Inside lay a strip of grey silk, a knife with a bone handle worn smooth as river stone, and a page torn from a book so old the vellum had gone the color of tea. She did not touch any of them. She let Bramble look.

"Thirty years ago," she began, "I was not yet ordained. I was an acolyte in the Choir's lowest circle — the circle that handled what we called *residue*. Prayers that arrived without addressees. Miracles that came back wrong. The Church taught that the Ninefold gods were the only gods, that everything older was superstition wearing a mask." She smiled, and it hurt her face. "We were the ones who wore the masks, Bramble. The whole hierarchy was a mask, laid over something the founders had buried so deep that within three generations even the burying was forgotten."

"The sealing," Bramble said quietly. "You're talking about the Sealing. The Quiet Century. Every child learns the song. *The gods grew cruel, the saints grew wise, they shut the sky and closed their eyes—*"

"The song is wrong." Ovel turned the torn page toward him. It bore a diagram: thirteen circles arranged in a ring, twelve filled with script, the thirteenth empty. "It wasn't cruelty. It was arithmetic. The old gods fed on devotion the way a fire feeds on a house — and there were too many of them, and too many doors between their country and ours, and every door they opened let more of them through. The city was drowning in answered prayers. You'd pray for rain and get a flood. Pray for your mother's health and wake to find her cured and everyone else on the street struck blind, because the god who heard you didn't understand *cost*, didn't understand *limits*, didn't understand anything except the shining of being worshipped."

"So the Church sealed them."

"The Church and others. There were orders then that don't exist anymore. Door-keepers." Her voice caught on the word, and she let it catch; she had earned the stumble. "People born with blood that could close what the gods opened. Rare. Maybe one family in a generation. They stood at the thirteenth turn — the moon you've seen lately, the sliver no almanac explains — and they gave of themselves to shut each door. And when the last door closed, the Church did what churches do."

"Which is?"

"Made sure there would never be another door-keeper. Burned the genealogies. Scattered the families. Turned the rite into a hymn nobody understood and the hymn into a nursery rhyme. Because a sealed door is safe, but a door-keeper is a key walking around loose, and keys make institutions nervous." She tapped the empty thirteenth circle. "And because the sealing was never meant to be permanent. It was meant to be maintained. Renewed. By keepers. And we killed them all, or married them into anonymity, or — worst of all — simply forgot them, and called the forgetting peace."

Bramble was quiet for a long moment. In the lamplight his cheerful face had gone careful, the way it did when he was counting risk. "And you? What was your part, Sister? You'd have been a child when the last door closed."

"I was twenty-two when I learned the truth," Ovel said. "And my part was smaller than the crime. I was a copyist. I copied the codices — the true ones, the ones under the cathedral, not the sanitized versions. I copied the list of keeper-bloodlines. All but one." She drew a breath. "One line survived the purges by being useful. The Church couldn't erase them entirely, because the seal needed *something*, some thin thread of the old blood to anchor it, the way a knot needs a tail end. So they found the last family and bound them instead. Debt, Bramble. Holy debt. A covenant dressed up as a contract, generation after generation, keeping the bloodline close and poor and watched, until the blood ran so thin the Church convinced itself the thread no longer mattered."

"Thackeray," Bramble said.

The name dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"You knew," Ovel said.

"I suspected. When she started balancing our books, the way she holds a pen — like it owes her money. And her grandmother, old Maren Thackeray, used to come by the Rest before she died, buying candle-ends, always asking after strange weather. I put it together late. Too late to matter." He looked at the tin box. "Until now. Until the thirteenth sliver showed up over the city and prayers started getting *answered*, and suddenly the thread matters quite a lot."

"She doesn't know," Ovel said. "Kestrel. She thinks her family's debt is money. Coin, interest, penalties. She balances accounts for the people who own her grandmother's grave and calls it diligence. She has no idea that the ledger she keeps is a copy of a much older ledger, and that her name is written in it in a hand three hundred years dead."

"Then we tell her."

"No." Ovel's hand came down flat on the table, harder than she intended. The lamp guttered. "Not yet. Not tonight. Bramble — do you know what it does to a person, learning their whole life was a cage someone else built? That their obedience was designed? That the Guild that owns them is just the newest mask on the same old face?" Her voice frayed. "I've watched it done. I helped do it. I copied the lists that told the Church where the keepers' grandchildren lived. I was twenty-two and I thought I was serving the Ninefold, and every name I inked was a nail in a coffin, and I have spent eleven years telling myself I was only a copyist, only a pen, only a—"

She stopped. The salt ring gleamed between them.

"Bramble," she said, steadier now, "when I was copying those lists, there was a girl born to the Thackeray line. An infant. The last of the last. My superior ordered me to record her as deceased — a clerical fiction, a small mercy, so the Church would stop watching the family and the blood could go dormant in peace. I wrote the false entry myself. I thought I was saving her." She laughed, low and terrible. "I may have been. Or I may have been blinding the only sentry we had. Either way, that infant grew up balancing ledgers for the Veiled Guild, and the Guild answers prayers now, did you know that? Real ones. Priced in blood. Someone up there has found the old machinery and started turning the crank, and the thirteenth moon is showing its face again, and the one person alive whose blood can shut the door doesn't know she's holding the key."

Bramble turned the bone-handled knife slowly on the table, watching the lamplight slide along its edge. "What's the page say? The one you tore out."

"It's the opening of the Rite of the Thirteenth Turn. The true text. I tore it out the night I left, because I couldn't decide whether destroying it was mercy or theft." She pushed the tin box aside and pressed both palms flat against the table, as if she could hold the whole weight of the coming days down with her hands. "I still haven't decided. That's my confession, Bramble. Not what I did thirty years ago. That I still don't know if I'm a coward or a protector, and I've had eleven years to work it out."

* * *

He found her an hour later on the roof of the Tallowman's Rest, wrapped in a blanket, looking up.

The sky over Vellamarch was clear for once, the smog blown east by a wind off the hills, and the moon hung fat and silver above the tannery chimneys — and beside it, faint as breath on glass, the thirteenth sliver. A curve of light where no light should be. Ovel had seen it in diagrams for thirty years. Seeing it in the sky made her feel like a woman reading her own obituary.

Bramble settled beside her without a word. For a while they just watched it.

"My grandmother," he said at last, "used to sing that nursery rhyme. *The gods grew cruel.* I always thought it was about the far past. Something finished." He shook his head. "It's not finished, is it. It never was. We've just been living in the pause between verses."

"No," Ovel said. "It isn't finished."

"And Kestrel."

"What about her?"

"When you tell her — and you will tell her, Ovel, because you'll drive yourself mad otherwise, and because she deserves it, and because if you don't I will — when you tell her, don't do it the way the Church would. Don't hand her a duty. Hand her a choice." He glanced sideways at her. "That's the difference between us and them, isn't it? That's supposed to be the difference."

Ovel looked at the impossible sliver of moon and felt the guilt settle over her shoulders like the blanket, warm and suffocating, an inheritance passed hand to hand down the years — from the founders who sealed the gods, to the priests who murdered the keepers, to the copyist who falsified a birth-roll, and now, if she was brave enough, to a ledger-clerk who balanced books for her own jailers.

Guilt passed down like an inheritance. The only question was whether the heir would spend it or burn it.

Below them, the trapdoor scraped. Footsteps on the stairs — quick, purposeful, familiar. Then Kestrel Thackeray's voice, calling up through the floorboards, tight with something between fear and fury:

"Ovel! Bramble! Are you up there? I need — I found something in the codex, I need to ask you about—" a breath, ragged, "—about a name. My grandmother's name. Why is my grandmother's name in the Guild's sealed codex?"

Ovel closed her eyes.

The salt ring below had known. The salt ring had always known.

"Go down," she said to Bramble. "Pour her something warm. I'll be there in a moment." She opened her eyes and looked one last time at the thirteenth sliver, hanging in the sky like a door left ajar. "It seems," she murmured, "that the truth has decided to stop waiting for me."

She rose, gathered her courage around her like the old vestments she no longer owned, and went down the stairs to confess — thirty years late, to the last door-keeper's granddaughter, with the moon already turning.
