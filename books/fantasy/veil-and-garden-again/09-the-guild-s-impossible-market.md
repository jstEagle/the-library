## 9. The Guild's Impossible Market

Bramble had smuggled a great many things through the veins of Vellamarch — salted eels past the toll-wardens, letters that could hang a duke, once, memorably, a live bear dressed as a bishop. But he had never smuggled hope before, and it turned out to be the heaviest cargo of all.

It sat in his chest now like ballast gone wrong, tipping him sideways every time he thought about what they were about to do. He was crouched in the bilge-shadow of a grain barge, three fingers deep in cold canal water, watching the Guild's night market assemble itself out of nothing.

That was the trick of it. That was the beautiful, rotten trick of it. By day, the Weighhouse district was all sober stone and lawful arithmetic — clerks with ink-stained cuffs, porters hauling honest tonnage, everything stamped and countersigned. The Guild of Veils had built its reputation on being boring. Boring was the best camouflage in the world.

But at moonrise, when the tide turned and the lamps along the Processional went dark for the changing of the watch, the water gates opened, and the impossible came in on barges with no names.

Bramble counted them under his breath. Seven barges tonight. Lanterns hooded in green glass so the light fell like pond-water on the deckhands' faces. And the cargo — he'd seen the manifests, or rather, Kestrel had copied them for him in her neat, damning hand, and he'd read them eleven times trying to make them mean something else.

*One jar, tears of Saint Ferrow (authenticated). One vial, last breath of a dying wish. One fragment, divine, provenance: sealed war, unlisted god. Reserve: negotiable.*

Divine. Fragment. Written in the same ledger-hand that recorded sacks of barley and barrels of pitch, priced the same way, taxed the same way, *buried* the same way when nobody bought.

"Stop counting," murmured Sister Ovel beside him. She was wedged into the bilge-rot in her plain grey traveling dress, looking less like a defrocked priest than a woman who had been disappointed by furniture her whole life. "Counting is how they find you. Everything in this city gets counted eventually."

"That's why we're here," Bramble said. "To do some counting of our own."

"To *witness*," Ovel corrected, sharp as a whetstone. "There's a difference. Witnessing you can survive."

He grinned at her because grinning was free and everything else cost. "Sister, I've survived falling off the Saltspire bridge twice, marrying into the Corvane family for six weeks, and eating my own cooking for a decade. I can survive witnessing."

She didn't smile. She never smiled at his list anymore, not since Kestrel had shown them the page from the codex with her own name written in it, and the whole crew had understood, all at once, that they were no longer stealing things. They were stealing toward something.

Above them, boots crossed the barge deck. A voice called down through the planking, bored and official: "Manifest?"

A second voice answered, smooth as oiled rope. "Seven lots. All sealed. All *clean*."

Clean. Bramble turned the word over and felt its underside. Clean meant the prayers had been harvested properly. Clean meant whatever god had been scraped into those jars hadn't left enough of itself behind to scream.

The water gates groaned open ahead, and the barges slid into the dark heart of the Weighhouse, where the Guild kept its deepest cellar — the one that appeared on no map, the one Kestrel called the Null Cellar because every ledger she'd ever balanced simply stopped at its door, like ink afraid to dry.

* * *

They came up out of the canal an hour later, through a maintenance grate Ovel had known about from her temple days, dripping and silent, into the Guild's outer yard where the night market was already breathing.

And oh, it was beautiful. That was the obscenity of it. Bramble had expected squalor — back-alley haggling, greasy men with stolen relics stuffed in their coats. Instead the courtyard had been transformed into something out of a festival dream. White lanterns floated above the flagstones without strings. Tables of dark wood bore their wares under glass cloches, each lot lit from within by a soft gold glow that made everything look edible, look *holy*. Musicians played on instruments that harmonized with themselves, two notes where one should be, and the sound made the teeth ache pleasantly, like sugar cut with salt.

The buyers wore masks. Not the crude carnival kind — these were veils of grey silk that clung to the face and blurred it, so that a magistrate looked like a merchant looked like a widow, and no one could swear afterward to having seen anyone at all. Deniability, tailored and fitted.

"The Veiled Guild," Ovel breathed beside him, and there was something raw in her voice. "We thought the name was about secrecy. It was never about secrecy. It's about *this*. Everyone veiled. Everyone unable to testify, even against themselves."

Bramble adjusted his own mask — plain grey silk, acquired from a fence who owed him, fitted by a woman who owed him more — and felt it settle against his skin like a second face. Beside him, Tansy, their youngest debt-runner, tugged hers down nervously. She was sixteen and had volunteered for this job with the bright-eyed eagerness of someone who had never yet watched anything holy get sold.

"Remember," Bramble said quietly, "we're buyers. Rich ones. Rich people don't hurry, don't gawk, and never ask the price of anything until they've insulted it first."

"What if someone talks to me?" Tansy whispered.

"Then be vague and expensive. You're the daughter of a spice magnate with disappointing parents. Lean into it."

The first lot went under the hammer — or rather, under the bell, because the auctioneer rang a small silver bell instead, and the sound of it did something strange to the air, made everyone lean forward slightly, made wanting feel like gravity.

"*Lot one*," the auctioneer said. He was an elegant man with the voice of a lullaby, and his mask was finer than the rest, stitched with silver thread. "*Tears of Saint Ferrow, authenticated by the Office of Provenance. Seventeen drops. Guaranteed to reconcile any estrangement — parent and child, debtor and creditor, the living and the recently dead.*"

A murmur ran through the crowd like wind through wheat. Paddles rose — no, not paddles; small brass scales, each buyer holding up a little balance, and the weights on the trays shifted as bids were placed, though Bramble couldn't see anyone adding anything.

"Four hundred crowns," said the auctioneer. "Five. Six hundred. Do I hear seven? For seventeen drops of forgiveness, friends. Cheaper than a lawsuit, kinder than a confession."

Sold, finally, to a heavyset figure whose veil couldn't hide the magistrate's chain of office glinting beneath — Magistrate Corvane, unless Bramble missed his guess, the same Corvane whose name appeared in Kestrel's ledgers forty times as a buyer of *reconciliations*. The man cradled the little jar like a newborn and wept behind his mask, and Bramble understood with a lurch that the tears were working already, that the man was buying forgiveness and receiving it in advance, pre-melted, ready to pour.

"That's the trade," Ovel said, very low. "That's what we broke when we sealed the gods away. Or thought we broke." Her hands were fists inside her sleeves. "We starved them, Bramble. We walled them up behind the door and told ourselves faith would die of hunger. But the Guild found a way to milk what was left. To keep the gods alive just enough to *harvest*."

"And the gods let them?"

"The gods are *asleep*, mostly. Dreaming. And a dreaming god doesn't know its tears are being bottled." She paused. "Mostly."

* * *

The lots went by. A vial of last breath, sold to a young widow who wanted to hear her husband's final word again. A lock of hair that guaranteed good harvests, bought by a farm-broker representing six estates. A small brass key that opened, the auctioneer promised, "any door that has been closed against you," which fetched the highest price of the evening so far and made Bramble's professional soul sit up and take furious notes.

Tansy had drifted toward the refreshment table, playing her part beautifully, declining wine she couldn't afford to be seen drinking and accepting compliments she didn't want. Bramble kept her in the corner of his eye and kept his own attention on the auctioneer, because Kestrel's copied manifest had listed seven lots and they'd only seen five.

The sixth lot changed everything.

"*Lot six*," the auctioneer said, and his lullaby voice took on a new register, something reverent and hungry at once. The other masked attendants dimmed the white lanterns. A single green lamp swung low over the central table, and beneath its glass cloche sat something that hurt to look at.

It was small. That was the first obscene thing — how small. A lump of pale luminescence the size of a fist, shifting slowly, like a cloud seen through milk, like a thought trying to remember itself. Light moved *inside* it, and the light wasn't light exactly; it cast shadows that fell the wrong way, toward itself, and where those shadows touched the table the wood bloomed briefly with tiny white flowers that wilted as soon as they opened.

"*Fragment, divine*," the auctioneer intoned. "*Provenance: the Sealed War. Unlisted god. Recovered intact during cellar excavation, forty years past. Effects verified: healing of mortal wounds, growth of true crops in barren soil, dreams that come true at a rate of one in nine. Note well, honored buyers: the fragment is alive. It responds to devotion. Feed it faithfully and it will grow. What grows can be—*" the pause was surgical, "*—divided.*"

The courtyard went very quiet. Even the self-harmonizing instruments faltered.

Bramble felt Ovel go rigid beside him. When she spoke, her voice had dropped into a register he'd never heard from her, flat and ancient, the voice of someone reciting scripture in a dead language. "That's Ashen-Vey," she said. "Or what's left. The Kindler. The god of hearths and small mercies. He was the gentlest of them. He was the one who argued for surrender when the rest wanted war."

"You know this how?"

"Because I helped carry the pieces." Her voice cracked on the last word. "Forty years ago. There were twelve of us, and we carried twelve pieces down into the dark, and we sang the sealing song, and we were told it was mercy. And apparently one piece never made it all the way down."

On the table, the fragment pulsed. Slowly. Rhythmically. And Bramble realized, with a horror that started in his stomach and climbed up into his throat, that the rhythm matched a heartbeat. That the thing was not glowing. It was *breathing*.

"It knows we're here," Tansy whispered, appearing at his elbow, all her spice-magnate poise gone. "Bramble. It's looking at me. How is it looking at me, there aren't any eyes—"

"Don't look back," Ovel said sharply. "Devotion draws it. Fear feeds it too, but devotion — it mistakes fear for devotion sometimes, it's been alone so long—"

The bidding began.

It was quiet bidding, reverent bidding, the brass scales rising one by one around the courtyard. Three thousand crowns. Four. The numbers stopped meaning anything to Bramble very quickly, because he was watching the fragment instead, watching it strain against the glass like a moth against a window, watching the wrong-way shadows reach toward the crowd, toward *him*, toward the place where Ovel stood with forty years of guilt written on her body in a language only gods could read.

Five thousand. Six.

And then a new voice joined the bidding, and the courtyard rearranged itself around it the way water rearranges around a dropped stone.

"I'll give seven thousand," said Magistrate Corvane, stepping forward, dropping his veil entirely, because a man that rich and that ruthless no longer needed deniability. "And I'll tell you why, since we're among friends." He smiled at the assembled masks. "My district is dying. The mills are closing, the poor are restless, and there's talk — *talk*, mind you — of the old shrines reopening in the gutter districts. Of wine weeping from stone. If the people are going to pray to something anyway—" he gestured elegantly at the cloche, "—I would prefer they prayed to something I *own*."

The auctioneer's bell rang once, softly. "Seven thousand. Do I hear eight?"

Silence. The kind of silence that has money in it.

"Going once."

Ovel's hand closed on Bramble's arm hard enough to bruise. "Do something," she breathed. "You're the one who's always saying justice outside the law — well, here it is, the law is *buying a god*, right in front of us—"

"Going twice."

Bramble's mind raced the way it always raced, fast and crooked and full of exits. They had no seven thousand crowns. They had no crowns at all that wouldn't be traced. They had a forged provenance letter, a stolen seal, and Tansy, and none of it was worth a god's heartbeat.

"Going—"

And then the fragment screamed.

No mouth. No throat. But every person in that courtyard heard it, heard it *inside* the skull, a sound like a hymn played backward, like a name being erased, and the white lanterns guttered, and the flowers bloomed and died across the whole table in a single wave, and Magistrate Corvane staggered back with his hand pressed to his chest and his face, for one naked moment, showing something underneath the wealth and the power.

Fear. Pure, animal, human fear.

The auctioneer recovered fastest. Of course he did. He'd sold miracles long enough to know that even the merchandise's grief was inventory. "The fragment expresses enthusiasm," he announced smoothly, to a crowd that was not fooled and did not care. "Such vitality! Shall we resume? Seven thousand stands. Going once—"

Corvane raised his scale again, hand trembling only slightly, and the bell rang, and it was done.

"Sold," said the auctioneer, "to the Magistrate of the Lower Mills. Delivery at your convenience, honored sir. The Guild thanks you for your patronage, and reminds all guests that what is witnessed here remains unwitnessed."

The crowd exhaled and became people again. Wine circulated. The instruments resumed their sweet doubled harmony. Somewhere a clerk was already writing the sale into a ledger, pricing a piece of a god the way you'd price a horse, and somewhere in the Null Cellar below, ten more fragments waited in the dark for their turn at the bell.

Bramble stood very still in the crowd of veils and felt something settle in him, something that had been loose and joking his whole life finally setting its weight down.

He'd run debts his entire career. Forgiven ones, mostly — that was his specialty, his pride, his cheerful crime: finding the ledgers where a debt had technically been paid and making it *officially* paid, freeing the debtor with paperwork and nerve. He'd always told himself it was enough. Small justices, small mercies, the hearth-god's own work, though he'd never have put it that way before tonight.

But you couldn't forge a signature that freed a god.

"Tansy," he said quietly. "Get the manifest copies to the drop. Ovel—" he turned to the old priest, and found her staring at the table where attendants were packing the fragment into a lined case, and the wrong-way shadows reaching after her like hands. "Ovel. We need to know where Corvane takes delivery. Tonight, before it goes underground."

"Why?" she asked, and her voice was ash. "What could we possibly do?"

"Nothing. Tonight, nothing." He pulled his veil back up over his face and felt, for the first time, that the mask and the man underneath had finally agreed on something. "But Kestrel's ledgers record every impossible thing this Guild ever bought, sold, or buried. Every one. Which means somewhere in those pages there's a price list for god-pieces, a route map, a *buyer's history*. And I have never yet met a debt that couldn't be called in — I just need to find out who owes what, and to whom."

He looked back once at the case, at the faint pulse of light visible through its seams. Breathing. Waiting. Being carried toward a magistrate's private shrine to become a private miracle, a hearth-fire owned by the man who was letting the hearths go cold.

"Ashen-Vey," Bramble murmured, using the old name like a key. "Hold on, you gentle idiot. Help's coming. Probably. Eventually. From extremely unlikely directions."

Behind them, unnoticed by anyone, the last lot of the night was being wheeled out on a covered cart — and the auctioneer, checking his manifest, frowned at the seventh entry, the one Kestrel's copy had flagged with a single annotation in her tight, careful hand:

*Lot seven: provenance disputed. Seller claims descent from the original door-keeper.*

Bramble didn't see it. But the city did. And by morning, everyone would be talking about the prayer that arrived at the Weighhouse that night, addressed to no living god — with an answer attached, written in handwriting the Guild's archivists swore belonged to a woman thirty years dead:

*Kestrel Thackeray's grandmother.*
