## 10. A Prayer with Teeth

The codex did not sleep, and neither, lately, did I.

I had taken to reading it in the small hours, when the debt-runners' house above the tannery went quiet and even Bramble's snoring — a sound like a millstone arguing with itself — settled into something almost rhythmic. I sat on the floor of my narrow room with the book open across my knees, candle stubbed down to a coin of wax, and I copied. Not for the Guild this time. For myself.

The pages were worse by candlelight. By day, in the Guild's scriptorium, the sealed codex had been an object — vellum and iron-gall ink and a lock that answered only to the Master's ring. Here, in the dark, it was what it had always been: a ledger of gods. What they had been promised. What they had been paid. What had been *taken* from them, itemized with the same dry precision I used for grain shipments and coffin nails.

*One fragment, minor deity of thresholds, sold to House Verrin, price: three generations of firstborn names.*

*One dream, harvested from the sleeping god Ashen-Vey, distributed among forty subscribers, quarterly.*

*One prayer, unanswered by contract, held in escrow.*

That last one appeared again and again. Prayers in escrow. Thousands of them, logged like outstanding invoices. The Guild hadn't just brokered miracles; it had *warehoused devotion*, letting the desperate pray into a void the Guild owned, then selling the void's silence back to them as peace.

I turned a page and my blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with the draft under the door.

The entry was old — older than the Guild's current numbering system, written in a hand that slanted left like it was trying to escape the page.

*One door-keeper's line, preserved. The heir lives. Do not approach. Do not bargain. The debt is not ours to collect.*

Someone had underlined that last sentence so hard the nib had torn through the vellum.

I sat very still and listened to the house breathe. Somewhere below, the tannery vats gurgled their sour song. Somewhere above, the thirteenth sliver of moon — visible now even to eyes that didn't want to see it, a hairline crack of light where no light should be — would be sliding behind the chimney pots.

*Do not bargain,* the page said.

And some traitorous part of me, the part that had spent twenty years pricing other people's salvation in columns of neat figures, thought: *bargain with whom?*

* * *

It happened three nights later, and it happened because I was weak, and tired, and I said the words out loud.

I hadn't meant it as a prayer. That's what I told myself afterward, and what I knew, even as the words left my mouth, was a lie. You can dress a prayer in sarcasm, wrap it in contempt, and it is still a prayer — still a key shaped exactly like the lock it fits. Sister Ovel had warned me. She'd said it with her defrocked priest's flat certainty, scrubbing tannery stink off her hands: *"The old ones don't need you to believe, Kestrel. They only need you to ask."*

I was hunched over the codex at the kitchen table, past midnight, chasing a reference through the escrow entries, and I couldn't find it, and my eyes were burning, and Bramble's crew had eaten the last of the good bread, and the thirteenth moon hung in the window like a wound in the sky — and I said:

"Gods below, if any of you are actually listening, someone show me where the Verrin contract is filed."

The candle went out.

Not guttered. Not blown. It went out the way a sentence stops when the speaker dies — instantly, completely, with a small sound like a held breath released. And the dark that replaced it wasn't empty. It was *attentive*. I felt the room listening back at me, every shadow angled like an ear.

On the table, the codex opened itself.

Pages turned without a hand, fast, faster, vellum whispering like wind through wheat, and stopped on a page I had never seen — one that hadn't been there when I'd copied the codex's full extent twice over. The ink on it was wet. Fresh. Shining faintly, the way wine shines when the light hits it wrong.

*You asked,* said a voice, and it came from everywhere the dark was, which was everywhere. It was not a booming voice. That was the worst of it. It was a reasonable voice, a clerk's voice, warm and unhurried, the voice of someone who had all the time in the world because time was one of the things it owned stock in. *We heard. We have always heard. Your grandmother heard us too, before she chose the door.*

My mouth was dry as ledger dust. "Who are you?"

*A fair question, from a fair keeper of accounts.* Something moved in the corner of the room — not approaching, just *arranging itself*, the way a merchant arranges goods before a customer arrives. *Names have weight. Yours especially, though you don't yet know why. Call us the Creditor. It is close enough, and it flatters neither of us.*

"I didn't call anyone."

*No,* the Creditor agreed, and I could hear something ancient and hungry underneath the courtesy, like a blade wrapped in velvet. *You prayed. Unwillingly, bitterly, with no faith in your mouth and contempt in your heart. Do you know what that makes you, Kestrel Thackeray?*

"Careful," I said, and my voice shook, and I hated it. "You know my name."

*We know everything the Guild has ever written down, and the Guild wrote you down before you could walk.* The wet page gleamed. *It makes you honest. Every soul in this city prays the way they've been taught to pray — kneeling, hoping, believing. Their prayers are soft. Full of give. Yours came to us like a knife thrown in the dark. We liked it. We have been waiting a long time for someone who asks the way you ask.*

The candle stayed dead, but light was beginning to seep into the room anyway — a thin, moon-colored glow rising off the codex's fresh page, off the ink, off the letters themselves. I could read the new entry now. It was addressed to me.

*ITEM: One codex, sealed, containing the true accounts of the Veiled Guild.*
*PRICE OFFERED: Full cancellation of the Thackeray family debt. All ledgers closed. All claims released. Freedom, unconditional and permanent.*
*DELIVERY: At the debtor's convenience. The Creditor is patient. The Creditor has, after all, been paid in patience for a thousand years.*

I stared at it until the letters swam.

Twenty years. My father's twenty years before that, and his mother's before his — three generations bound to the Guild for a loan whose principal had been repaid twice over before I was born, kept alive by interest, by fees, by clauses written in hands like mine. My grandmother had died owing them. Her death certificate, which I had found in the deep files in another life, another self, read *asset transfer* in the same neat script I used every morning. They had literally entered her corpse into the books.

And here was a voice in the dark offering to burn all of it with a word.

"You're lying," I said. "Nothing cancels a Guild debt. Not death. Not payment. The Master told me once — he said the debts were structural. Load-bearing."

*He told you the truth,* the Creditor said, and laughed, and the laugh was the worst sound yet, because it was kind. *The debts ARE load-bearing, little clerk. They hold up the whole architecture of the city — the Guild, the courts, the almanacs, the calendar itself. Which is precisely why we can cancel yours. We built the load. We know which stones are ours to pull.*

"Then why offer? What do you get?"

The dark went quiet in a way that made my skin crawl, because it was the quiet of a negotiator deciding how much truth the deal can bear.

*The codex contains the terms of our imprisonment,* it said at last. *Every clause. Every seal. Every name that was used to bind us beneath the world. Your grandmother helped write those names. Her blood is in the margins of that book — we can smell it from here, the way you can smell rain coming.* A pause, delicate as a knife being set down on silk. *Give us the book, and we will unwrite your family's chains. Keep it, and you keep your chains, and ours, and everyone's. We do not threaten. Threats are for creatures with deadlines. We merely describe the merchandise.*

"And if I say no?"

*Then you will say it knowing the price of yes. That is more than the Guild ever offered anyone. That is more than your own Master ever offered you.* The voice softened, and horribly, it sounded like pity. *You balance books for men who steal prayers from dying children. We are offering you the one clean transaction of your life. No hidden fees. Read the fine print yourself — you're the only one in this city qualified to.*

I looked down at the page. And I confess it here, in whatever account this confession eventually lands in: I read the fine print. Line by line. Clause by clause. Because that is what I am, and because some part of me was already negotiating, already weighing, already *pricing* — and the thing in the dark saw me do it, and let me, and waited with the patience of something that had watched empires amortize.

The terms were clean. Cleaner than anything the Guild had ever drafted. Full cancellation, all lines, all generations, irrevocable. In exchange: the codex, delivered freely, by my own hand, at the turn of the thirteenth moon.

There was only one clause that gave me pause, buried near the end in letters so small I had to lean close:

*Delivery constitutes acknowledgment of the Creditor's standing claim to all escrowed prayers currently held in Guild custody.*

All of them. Every prayer in the warehouse. Every dying child's plea, every widow's whisper, every desperate bargain struck with a void — transferred, upon delivery, to the old gods' keeping. To be answered, presumably. Or collected. Or *spent*.

"That clause," I said slowly. "What happens to the escrowed prayers if I sign?"

*They come home,* said the Creditor, and for one moment the reasonable clerk's voice fell away and something else looked out through the dark at me — something vast and starved and *imprisoned*, something that had been listening to ten thousand locked-away prayers for a hundred years with its face pressed against the inside of the seal. *Every word ever spoken to us and stolen. We will answer them. Oh, we will answer them all.*

And I understood, sitting in the dead-candle dark with my grandmother's inheritance open on the table, that this was the trap. Not the debt. Not the codex. The trap was that the offer was *fair*. That every word of it was true. That a creature old enough to remember the world before doors could look at the Guild's crimes and mine and offer me the one trade that a decent person couldn't refuse — and that accepting it would pour a thousand years of imprisoned hunger back into a city that had forgotten how to survive its gods.

Ovel had told me what the sealing was for. She'd wept telling it. Whatever massacre the old stories dressed up as rebellion, she had seen the truth of it, and she had helped build the door anyway.

"No," I said.

The dark did not move.

"Not like this. Not with that clause. You can have your answer at the turn — but it won't be the codex, and it won't be my signature, and if you touch those prayers before I've decided what they're owed, I will spend every night of my life finding new names to nail that door shut with. I'm a Thackeray. We're very good at binding things to paper."

Silence. Long enough that I began to hope I'd bluffed a god.

Then, softly, almost tenderly: *There she is. There is the door-keeper's blood.* The wet page began to dry, the offer fading into the vellum like frost off glass. *Keep the book, Kestrel. Study it. Learn what your grandmother learned and what she died knowing. And when the thirteenth moon comes full — and it will, it is already halfway to full — ask yourself one question.*

The candle relit. Just like that. A small, ordinary flame, as if nothing in the room had changed.

*Ask yourself who taught the Guild to put prices on prayers. Ask yourself where the Master learned his arithmetic. And ask yourself, when you finally meet the thing behind the mask — ask yourself whether it is a man who became a god, or a god who learned to keep books.*

The presence withdrew. I felt it go the way you feel a hand lifted from the back of your neck — the air colder where it had been, the silence louder. The codex lay closed on the table, ordinary again, except that the page it rested against was warm as skin.

I sat there until the candle burned down to nothing and grey dawn came through the window, and the thirteenth sliver faded into the pale sky like a scar healing wrong.

Here is what I did not tell Bramble when he shuffled down at sunrise, hair like a startled hedgehog, asking why I looked like I'd wrestled the tannery vats and lost. Here is what I did not tell Ovel, whose eyes went straight to the codex on the table and then to my face, and narrowed, because defrocked priests miss nothing.

The god had offered me freedom, and I had refused it — and in refusing, I had named a price of my own. *At the turn,* I'd said. *You'll have your answer at the turn.* As if I were the one setting terms. As if I had leverage.

But lying awake in the grey light, I made myself honest, because honesty is the one discipline they never trained out of me: I hadn't refused the deal. I'd *countered*. Some cold, competent, ledger-keeper's part of me had looked at a starving god across a table and started negotiating — and it had felt *good*. It had felt like power, real power, the kind the Guild hoards and the courts pretend at, and it had fit my hand like a tool I'd always been meant to hold.

That frightened me more than the voice in the dark. Gods I could resist. The thing in myself that wanted to bargain with them — that wanted to *win* — I wasn't sure I could.

By full morning I had made a decision, and it was the kind of decision that splits houses down the middle. The codex couldn't stay hidden in my room, passed between my knees and the candlelight while the crew slept around me, ignorant of what I was carrying and what it was worth and who was hunting it. Sooner or later the Guild would come, or the gods would, and my friends would die for a secret they'd never agreed to keep.

So that evening, when the crew gathered around the long table — Bramble dealing cards nobody was playing, Ovel mending a robe she'd sworn she'd never wear again, the twins arguing over the last heel of bread — I carried the codex down and set it in the middle of the table with a sound like a gavel falling.

"We need to talk," I said, "about what we're going to do with this."

Bramble looked at the book. Then at me. Then, slowly, at Ovel — and I watched something move behind his cheerful smuggler's eyes, something quick and calculating and terribly, humanly greedy, and I understood that the hardest bargain of my life was not going to be the one with the god.

It was going to be the one with my family.
