## 11. The Crew Divided

Sister Ovel had spent thirty years learning that the worst arguments were never about the thing itself. They were about what the thing cost, and who would pay.

She stood in the kitchen of Bramble's safehouse — a converted counting-house in the low city, all sagging beams and a hearth that smoked more than it warmed — and watched the codex sit on the table between them like a guest no one had invited and everyone was afraid to evict. It was a heavy thing, bound in grey leather gone green at the corners, its clasp shaped like a hand mid-blessing. Even closed, it seemed to listen.

The rain came down hard on the roof tiles. Somewhere above them, the thirteenth sliver of moon hung behind the clouds like a splinter working toward the skin.

"Three hundred crowns," Bramble said. He said it the way other men said *I love you* — softly, reverently, with his whole face open. "Minimum. And that's if we sell it small. A private buyer, one of the magistrates who's already bought a miracle or two. Quiet, clean, done by week's end." He spread his hands, palms up, a smuggler showing empty pockets to a customs man. "Ovel. Kestrel. Think what three hundred crowns does. It buys Pip out of his indenture. It buys Marla's lungs a winter of medicine. It buys us passage anywhere in the world, on any ship, under any name we like."

"It buys nothing," Ovel said, "because you'd be selling a door."

Kestrel hadn't spoken yet. She sat at the far end of the table with her hands wrapped around a cup of tea she wasn't drinking, and Ovel watched her the way you watch a fuse burn down toward powder. The girl had changed since the night her blood had opened the locked page. Not in any way you could name — she still wore the same threadbare clerk's coat, still had ink stains on her fingers from copying pages she should never have touched — but there was a weight to her now, a gravity, as though the secret in her veins had made her denser than the room could hold.

"Bramble," Kestrel said at last, "who would you even sell it *to*?"

"Anyone with coin and sense enough not to ask questions."

"That's the problem." She set the cup down. Her voice stayed level, which somehow made it worse. "Everyone who can afford it wants the same thing. The Guild wants it because it tells them how the door works. The gods want it because it tells them how to open it. The magistrates want it because they think everything is for sale, and they're mostly right." She looked at him across the table, and Ovel saw something bleak and loving in her face. "You'd be handing a lit lamp into a powder store and calling it commerce."

Bramble's grin flickered — the real one underneath, the frightened one — and then he planted it back in place like a flag. "And what's your plan, then? Burn it? That's your answer?" He turned to Ovel before Kestrel could reply. "Because I've heard her say it twice now, and I keep waiting for the part where burning a book stops a war."

"You haven't read it," Ovel said quietly.

"I've read enough. I read the page where it says the old gods were sealed behind the moon, and I read the part where it says who holds the key, and I read the part where it says—" He stopped himself. His jaw worked. "I read enough to know that book is worth more than every debt I've ever run. And I know what debts are worth."

"There it is," Kestrel said, very softly.

"Don't." Bramble pointed at her, and his finger wasn't quite steady. "Don't do that. Don't look at me like I'm the Master with better manners. I have carried forgiven debts through checkpoints sewn into my coat lining since I was fourteen years old. I have watched men hang for paper. You think I don't understand what this thing is? I understand it better than either of you. It's a ledger. It's the biggest ledger ever written, and ledgers are power, and power sitting idle in a safehouse in the low city gets people killed."

"Power used gets people killed faster," Ovel said.

"Then we agree!" He threw up his hands. "We agree it kills people! So let's argue about *which* people!"

The rain filled the silence. In the corner, the cat — some nameless creature Marla had adopted off the docks — lifted its head, considered the tension, and went back to sleep. Ovel envied it profoundly.

* * *

They had been arguing, in one form or another, for two days.

It had started civilly, over breakfast, with maps. Kestrel had spread her grandmother's notes beside the codex and begun, in her dry clerk's voice, to explain the structure of the sealing rite — the turns of the moon, the thresholds, the names. Ovel had listened with her heart going cold, because she recognized the architecture. She had helped build it.

Thirty years ago, she had been Sister Ovel of the Vigil of the Quiet Hours, youngest of the twelve who walked up the mountain in the last year of the Godswar, carrying chains of consecrated iron and singing the hymn that unmade names. Twelve had gone up. Nine had come down. The ones who didn't come down had given their names to the door — freely, as the rite demanded — and become nothing at all, not dead, not gone, just *unwritten*, their places at the supper table empty in a way that hurt worse than graves.

She had helped seal the gods away, and she had told herself for thirty years that it was mercy. That the gods had gone mad with grief and hunger, that their answering of prayers had curdled into demand, that the massacres in the river provinces — whole towns kneeling themselves to death, praying until their hearts gave out because a god had asked — had to be stopped by any means. She had told herself that right up until the night she learned whose blood ran in Kestrel Thackeray's veins, and understood that the Vigil had known, and had let the last door-keeper's line fall into debt-slavery rather than watch over it.

Guilt, Ovel thought now, watching the girl argue, is the one inheritance that passes cleanly through every generation.

So yes: she wanted the codex burned. Every page. Every diagram. Every careful record of how to open what her sisters had died to close. Let it be ash, and let the thirteenth turn come and find no one who knew the way, and let the door stay shut out of simple ignorance. Ignorance had kept more peace than wisdom ever had.

But Kestrel wanted to use it. Use it — that was the word she kept choosing, precise as everything else about her. Not destroy it, not sell it. *Use* it. Learn the rite. Understand the door. Because — and here was the crack running through all of them — the door was already opening. The thirteenth sliver hung in the sky whether they burned the book or not. The old gods were massing at the city's edge like weather. Burning the codex wouldn't close anything; it would only blind the people standing closest to the flood.

And Bramble — dear, foolish, generous Bramble — wanted to sell it, because Bramble had grown up poor in a city where everything was owned, and somewhere deep beneath all his charm he believed, with the faith of a drowning man, that enough money could buy a family out of harm's way. As if the Guild gave receipts. As if the Master of the Veiled Guild, who had worn his mask for longer than the city had had walls, ever let a seller walk away knowing too much.

On the second evening, the argument stopped being civil.

* * *

It broke over the question of Pip.

Pip was eleven, a debt-runner's apprentice, all elbows and earnestness, currently upstairs asleep with a fever he couldn't shake because proper feverfew cost more than his mother made in a month. Bramble brought him up first, as he brought everything up eventually — not as a weapon, exactly, but as a truth, and truths cut the same regardless of intent.

"Pip is dying slowly," Bramble said, "in the room above us, and we are arguing philosophy around a book that could buy him a hundred years. Tell me again how burning it is the moral choice. Say it slow. I want to write it down and read it to his mother."

Kestrel stood up so fast her chair went over backward. "You don't get to use Pip—"

"Somebody has to use *something*! You want to use the book for your grand plan, she wants to use fire for hers, and I'm the only one at this table willing to admit that using things is what people *do*—" His voice cracked, and for a moment he was not the cheerful smuggler at all but a boy in a checkpoint line, watching soldiers burn his father's papers and call it law. "I am tired," he said, quieter, "of being pure and powerless. I did that already. It looked exactly like this room."

Ovel put her hand flat on the codex. Everyone stopped.

The leather was warm. It was always slightly warm, like a sleeping animal, and she hated that she knew that, hated that thirty years hadn't dulled her feel for consecrated things. "Listen to me," she said. "Both of you. I was on the mountain."

Bramble blinked. "What?"

"The Vigil of the Quiet Hours. The twelve who sealed the door. I was the youngest." She heard her own voice go flat and formal, falling back into the cadences of a liturgy she'd sworn off decades ago. "I sang the hymn while my sisters unwrote themselves. I held the chain. I watched Sera Vane give her name to the door and walk back down the mountain without it, smiling, because she didn't remember what she'd lost, and that was the mercy of it." She looked at Kestrel. "Your grandmother was the last door-keeper. She didn't climb with us. She *held* the door after. Alone. For years. And the Vigil let her do it alone, and then let her die owing money to the very guild that trades in miracles, because watching her was easier than serving her. I know. I read the correspondence when I left the order. I stole it. It's in a box under the floorboards, and I've never had the courage to give it to you."

Kestrel had gone very still. "Ovel—"

"So believe me when I tell you what's in that book." Ovel lifted her hand from the cover. "It isn't power. It's a price list. Every rite, every opening, every turning of the door — and next to each one, the cost. Names. Blood. Whole lines of descent, spent like coin. Your grandmother's notes say the same thing, Kestrel. You've read them. The door doesn't care who opens it or why. It only cares what's paid. And the moment anyone — the Guild, a magistrate, a god, *us* — starts thinking of that price as a resource instead of an abomination, we have already lost. That's how the Godswar started. Not with evil. With accounting."

"Then teach me the closing," Kestrel said.

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well.

"Not the opening. Not the trading. The closing." Kestrel's voice shook, and she steadied it the way she balanced a ledger — deliberately, line by line. "You want to burn the book because you're afraid of what we'd do with it. Bramble wants to sell it because he's afraid of what happens to us without it. But the thirteenth turn is coming whether we're afraid or not, and I am the heir of the woman who held that door, and I will not stand in a kitchen arguing while it opens. Teach me the rite. All of it. And then—" her jaw tightened— "and then we can decide together what to do with the rest. But I won't be blind, and I won't be sold, and I won't be *managed*. Not by the Guild. Not by gods. Not by either of you."

Silence. Rain. The cat woke, stretched, and left the room with the air of a diplomat abandoning a failing summit.

Bramble looked at Kestrel for a long moment, and Ovel saw the fight go out of him the way air leaves a sail — not all at once, but steadily, leaving him becalmed and smaller. "You really mean to stand at that door," he said. It wasn't a question.

"I mean to be ready for it," Kestrel said. "There's a difference."

"Is there?" He rubbed his eyes. When he lowered his hands, he was smiling again, but it was the tired smile, the one that cost him. "Fine. Fine. We don't sell it. Yet. We don't burn it. Yet. We learn what it says, all three of us, together, and nobody copies pages alone at midnight anymore, because that's how this crew nearly broke the first time." He stuck out his hand, rough-palmed, half-offer and half-surrender. "Truce?"

Kestrel took it. "Truce."

"And you'll teach her?" Bramble asked Ovel, and there was something raw under the casualness. "The rite. Whatever it costs you to open that box under the floorboards and hand over thirty years of guilt — you'll do it? Because if she stands at that door half-taught, we all die of it."

Ovel looked at the codex, warm as a heartbeat under the lamplight. She looked at Kestrel, who had her grandmother's stubbornness and, God forgive them all, her grandmother's eyes. She thought of Sera Vane walking down the mountain smiling, nameless, free, and wondered — not for the first time, but for the first time aloud —

"Yes," Ovel said. "Tomorrow. At dawn. Bring your grandmother's notes."

Kestrel nodded and gathered the codex into her arms, and it was as she turned toward the stairs that they all heard it: a knocking at the front door. Slow. Deliberate. Six knocks, a pause, and six again — the rhythm of the Vigil's old summons, a signal that had not been used in thirty years, from someone who should not have known it.

Ovel's blood went cold as mountain snow.

"I'll answer it," she heard herself say, and reached for the poker by the hearth.
