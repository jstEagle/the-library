## 15. The Master Unmasked

The waterways spat us out beneath the Weighhouse, and Bramble's crew scattered like spilled coins — but I did not run with them. I should have. Every sane instinct said *run*, said the Guild's hunters would be sweeping the canals by now with lanterns and warrants and that soft, terrible efficiency I had once called bookkeeping.

Instead I climbed.

I had spent eleven years in the Guild's ledgers. I knew its architecture the way a confessor knows a sinner: not by what it showed, but by what it hid. The Master's tower had twelve floors of offices, archives, weighing rooms. The ledgers never mentioned a thirteenth. But the stairwell above the twelfth landing kept going, one narrow spiral of steps worn hollow in their centers by feet far older than any clerk's, and at the top was a door with no lock — only a scale, brass and green with age, set into the wood.

A scale with two empty pans.

I understood it the way you understand a sentence in your mother tongue, without translating. It wanted something weighed. Something given against something taken.

I cut my palm on my belt-knife and let three drops fall into the left pan.

The right pan sank. The door opened on silence.

* * *

The room at the top of the tower was not a room. That was my first thought, standing on the threshold with blood running down my wrist: *this is a ledger.* Not paper and ink — a ledger made of air and light and time. Shelves rose out of the dark in curving rows, and every shelf held objects instead of books: a child's shoe gone silver with age. A wedding ring fused to a finger-bone. A jar of breath, still fogging the glass. A lock of hair that whispered when the draft touched it. Each one humming faintly, the way a struck bell hums after the sound you can hear has ended.

Miracles. Traded, priced, shelved. Everything the ledgers had ever recorded as a *purchase* or an *asset transfer* or, in one memorable entry, *miscellaneous devotional inventory*.

And at the center, in a chair that might once have been a throne and had been worn down to something more like a cradle, sat the Master.

He did not wear the mask.

That was the first shock — that there was no mask, no golden face, no ceremonial horror. There was only an old man in a plain grey robe, thin as a candle burned down to its stub, hands folded in his lap like a man waiting for a coach that stopped coming centuries ago. His skin had the waxy translucence of something preserved rather than alive. When he turned his head toward me, his eyes were the worst part: they were ordinary eyes, brown, tired, human, set in a face that had clearly once been kind.

"Kestrel Thackeray," he said. His voice was dry paper. "You came up the stairs yourself. Do you know how long it has been since anyone came up the stairs? They always send someone. Or they send nothing, and simply hope."

"You know why I'm here," I said. My voice held. I was almost proud of it.

"I know why you *think* you're here." He gestured, a small motion, at the shelves. "You want to see the machinery. You've been reading my grandmother's notes—" and I flinched, because he said *my* grandmother as if she were his too, "—and you think you've found the crime. A man who sells prayers. A man who bottles gods. Go on. Say it. Accuse me. Everyone does, eventually. It's the last honest transaction most of them ever make."

"The codex," I said. "The thirteenth moon. The door. You're prying it open, and you need—"

"A door-keeper's blood. Yes." He smiled, and the smile was the loneliest thing I had ever seen. "Sit down, Kestrel. You've earned the truth. Very few people ever have. And the truth is so much worse than the crime."

There was a stool near the chair, old and low, positioned exactly where a servant's stool would sit. I refused it. I stood, and he seemed to find my refusal funny, or sad, or both.

"Do you know who built the first door?" he asked.

"Sister Ovel says the gods were sealed behind it. To stop a massacre."

"Ovel." He tasted the name. "She was the youngest of them. The sealers. She would have been a girl when the door closed. Yes — the massacre is true, or true enough. Seven gods went mad in the same season, and the things they did in the low city..." He closed his eyes. "Your history books call it the Year of Open Hands, because the dead were found with their palms turned upward. Begging. Even at the end, begging." He opened his eyes again. "So the remaining gods agreed to be shut away, and mortal door-keepers were raised to hold the lock — blood-bound, name-sworn, one family for each hinge of the moon. Twelve keepers. Twelve turns. And above them all, the first keeper. The one who held the key itself."

"And that was your grandmother," I said slowly. "The woman whose notes I've been reading."

"No." He leaned forward, and the candlelight — there was no candle, but there was light, sourceless and patient — moved across his translucent skin and showed me, briefly, the shape of the skull beneath. "That was *your* grandmother, Kestrel. Alveric Thackeray. First door-keeper. The strongest of them. The one the others trusted with everything."

The room tilted. I put a hand against a shelf to steady myself, and the silver shoe trembled under my fingers like a small animal.

"That's impossible. My grandmother was a laundress. She died owing the Guild four hundred crowns. Her death is in your ledgers as an asset transfer—"

"Because I transferred her assets." He said it gently, horribly. "Her assets were her office, her oath, and her bloodline. All three passed to her heir. Her heir was supposed to be her daughter — your mother. But your mother refused the keeping. Ran from it. Married a debt-runner's son and tried to become no one." His brown eyes rested on me with unbearable patience. "Blood doesn't let you become no one, Kestrel. It only waits. So when your mother died, and then your grandmother — when the last keeper of the key died without a sworn successor — the door began, very slowly, to come unlatched. And someone had to hold it."

"You," I whispered.

"I tried." For a moment the waxen mask of age fell away entirely, and underneath it I saw him: young, maybe twenty, with ink-stained fingers and his grandmother's stubborn jaw. Alveric's grandchild. *My* cousin, or near enough. "I took the key. I spoke the words. And the door rejected me." He spread his hands, those folded, waiting hands. "Do you know what it means to be a failed heir? The door tests the keeper's surrender. You must be willing to give your whole self — name, blood, future, all of it, freely. I went to the threshold willing to give my blood. Willing to give my life. But not my *name*. I wanted to survive it. I wanted to be remembered. I stood at the door of the gods and I bargained."

"And it refused you."

"And it cracked me." He touched his own chest, lightly, the way you'd touch a cracked bell. "The keeping poured through me anyway — half of it, the part that binds — and none of the part that seals. I became a hinge with no door. I could feel every god on the other side, Kestrel. Every day, for two hundred years. Their hunger. Their grief. Some of them still singing. Do you understand what that does to a person?"

I looked at the shelves. At the shoe, the ring, the jar of breath. At the humming.

"No," I said. "Tell me."

"It makes you need to *stop feeling it*. And the only thing that quiets it is a miracle moving through you." He rose from the chair — it took him a long time, joints creaking like ship's rigging — and walked the aisle between the shelves, trailing his fingertips across the objects. Where he touched them, the humming stilled, and his face eased, just slightly, like a man scratching an itch he'd carried for decades. "Every prayer answered, every miracle traded, passes through the failed keeper. That was the design — the door-keeper channels the divine traffic; that's what keepers are *for*. But a broken channel leaks. And the leak feels... good. Like warmth on frostbitten hands. So I began to trade. Small things at first. A fever broken. Rain where rain was owed. Then the Guild grew up around me — clerks, contracts, weights and measures — because a trade this size needs bookkeeping, and I have always loved bookkeeping." He laughed, a dry rustle. "You and I are alike in that. Don't look revolted. You balanced my ledgers for eleven years and slept well. Mostly."

"I didn't know what I was balancing."

"Neither did I, at first." He stopped before a shelf near the back, and lifted down a small frame. Inside it was a pressed flower, white, impossibly fresh. "This was the first one. A prayer from a drowning girl, two hundred years ago. She promised anything for one more breath. I gave it to her. And I felt quiet for a full day afterward. One whole day of silence, after two hundred years of screaming gods." He set the flower back with the tenderness of a man handling an infant. "After that, how could I stop? Every miracle I brokered bought me another hour of peace. Every hour of peace I spent finding more prayers to answer. The Guild isn't a conspiracy, Kestrel. It's an addiction with a charter."

"And the thirteenth moon," I said. "The door opening. Is that you?"

For the first time, he looked away from me.

"Not at first," he said. "The door has been loosening since your grandmother died — since the key went unheld. The old gods push at it from their side. I have spent two centuries holding it shut with both hands and a warehouse full of purchased faith. But I am *tired*, Kestrel. The trades don't buy what they used to. A miracle used to buy me a day of quiet. Now it buys me an hour. Last winter, minutes." He turned back, and his ordinary, human eyes were wet. "So yes. I sent for you. I had the codex copied into your hands. I let you find your grandmother's notes. I am trying to do what I should have done two hundred years ago — put a true keeper at the door before it fails. Your blood is the last unbroken line. You can hold what I couldn't."

"At the thirteenth turn," I said. "When the moon opens. You'll feed me to it."

"*Feed* you." He shook his head. "I will ask you to succeed where I failed. To give everything — freely, wholly, including the name. And if you do, the door seals for another age, and the gods stay behind it, and the city goes on buying its little mercies and never knowing the price." He stepped closer, and up close I could smell him: dust, and candle smoke, and underneath it something older, like the air in a tomb that has been opened once a year for centuries. "Or refuse, and watch it break open, and every mad god of the old pantheon comes through into a city of ten thousand souls. Those are the choices. I'm sorry. I didn't write them. I've merely lived inside them longer than anyone should."

And here is the thing I will be ashamed of until I die, whatever name I die under: for one moment — one long, cold, luminous moment — I *pitied* him.

He was my kin. My grandmother's other grandchild, the one who stayed, who took the burden, who broke under it and spent two centuries alone in a tower trading pieces of other people's faith for minutes of silence. He had been nineteen years old and afraid, and the door had asked him for everything, and he had held something back, and it had ruined him. I looked at him and saw the shape of my own future with terrible clarity: the ledgers, the shelves, the slow erosion of hours into minutes, the moment when keeping the world safe becomes indistinguishable from feeding on it. He wasn't a monster. He was a warning. He was what happened when duty met fear and fear won by inches over two hundred years.

"You could stop," I said quietly. "Give it up. Let go. Tell the truth. Help us find another way."

"Another way." He smiled again, and this time there was nothing lonely in it at all — something colder, older, and I understood that the young man with ink-stained fingers had died a very long time ago, and what wore his face now had learned patience the way stone learns water. "Child, I *am* the other way. I am two hundred years of holding the line while better people than either of us slept soundly. You think Sister Ovel's little rite will save anyone? You think burning my ledgers frees anyone? The debts are real. The door is real. The hunger on the other side of it is *real*, and it is pressing, and it has smelled your blood already." He reached out — I flinched, but he only touched the cut on my palm, barely, with one waxen fingertip, and where he touched it the bleeding stopped and the skin closed smooth. A gift. Free. Which frightened me more than any price ever had. "One turn left, Kestrel. Come to the door willingly, or be dragged there by things with more teeth than I have. Either way, you'll stand at the threshold. The only question is whether you arrive as a keeper..."

Behind him, on the highest shelf, every object began to hum at once — the shoe, the ring, the jar of breath, the whispering hair — a chord rising, urgent, like ten thousand small voices clearing their throats.

"...or as an offering."

Through the high window behind his chair, I saw the sky over the low city, and the moon rising fat and wrong, and along its lower rim — unmistakable now, visible to anyone who cared to look up — the thirteenth sliver, thin as a knife's edge, beginning to shine.

It was not shining silver.

It was shining gold, the exact gold of a Guild ledger's gilded edge, and somewhere below us, at the city's edge, something old began to sing.
