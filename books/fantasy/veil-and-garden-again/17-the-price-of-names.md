## 17. The Price of Names

The rite fit on a single page.

That was the first cruelty of it — all that weight, all that history, compressed into ink and vellum no larger than a prayer card. Ovel had written it out for me in the back room above the Weighhouse, her hand steady until the last line, where it trembled just enough to smear the final word. *Blood.* She had underlined it twice, as if repetition could soften it into metaphor.

It could not. I had read my grandmother's notes. I knew what the door asked, and I knew what it accepted, and the two lists were identical down to the last drop.

So I did what Thackerays have always done with unbearable truths. I entered it in a ledger.

* * *

The safehouse smelled of tallow smoke and Bramble's terrible cooking — onions gone bitter in a pan he kept forgetting, because Bramble forgot nothing except whatever was currently on a stove. Downstairs, the debt-runners were counting the night's take: forgiven debts, forged releases, the small illegal mercies we had been dealing in since the Guild put its seal on my face as a thief's mark. The city below us was learning to breathe without its masters, and every ragged breath of it came through rooms like this one.

I sat at the corner table with a lamp pulled close and three ledgers open before me, and I lied.

Lying in a ledger is a craft. Anyone can write a falsehood; it takes an accountant to make a falsehood *balance*. Every entry must lean against another entry, every column must kiss its opposite column at the bottom line, and if you do it well, the lie becomes load-bearing. It holds up other lies. It holds up the truth, too, when the truth would crush the people reading over your shoulder.

The first ledger was for Ovel. In it, I recorded the rite's requirements exactly as she'd given them — blood, name, hour, moon — and then, beneath them, in my neatest hand, the substitution I claimed to have found in my grandmother's marginalia: *a willing vessel may stand proxy; the blood may be drawn, not given.* A fiction. My grandmother's notes said the opposite, in words so plain even a child could read them. But Ovel wanted desperately to believe there was a gentler door, and grief makes a generous reader. She would check the citation. She would find the page. And on that page, thanks to an evening's careful work with a pen knife, diluted iron-gall, and a sheet of blotting paper pressed at precisely the right dampness, she would find my forgery waiting for her like a planted flower.

The second ledger was for Bramble. Bramble could not be lied to with documents; he read people the way I read columns. So I lied to him with arithmetic instead. I showed him a plan — a real one, mostly true — for getting me to the door alive: the waterways, the Guild's broken patrols, the route the crew had bled for once already. I priced it honestly. I let him see the risk and watch me flinch from it, because Bramble trusted a flinch more than a smile. What I left out was the last line item. The cost marked *paid by the account holder.*

The third ledger was for myself. That one held the truth, because someone ought to know it, even if only paper. I wrote the rite out in full. I wrote what it would take from me. And then, because I am my grandmother's granddaughter and cannot help myself, I wrote the entries that would come after — the ones I would not be present to post.

*Debt of Kestrel Thackeray, Guild-assessed: settled.*
*Debt of Kestrel Thackeray, bloodline obligation: transferred to bearer.*
*Bearer: the Door.*

There is a strange peace in writing your own closing balance. I recommend it to no one.

* * *

"You've been at those books since Vespers," Ovel said from the doorway. She had a mug of something hot in each hand and the particular walk of a woman who had spent thirty years processing down nave aisles and now had nowhere to process to. "You'll ruin your eyes."

"My eyes are the least of what I'm ruining." I said it lightly, and hated how easily lightness came, how much practice I'd apparently had without noticing.

She set a mug beside me and looked at the open pages upside down, which was habit, not suspicion. Priests and accountants share that affliction: we both assume any document near us might be meant for our review. "The substitution," she said. "Show me again."

I turned the first ledger toward her and watched her read my forgery of her own hope. Her shoulders came down half an inch. Thirty years of guilt loosened their grip, just slightly, and I felt the lie do its work, warm and awful, like a poultice on a wound that should have been cauterized.

"It's in her hand?" Ovel asked. "The margin note?"

"Her shorthand. I've been copying it for weeks." True enough to survive inspection. My grandmother's shorthand lived in my fingers the way prayers live in old priests' mouths — recited so often they'd worn grooves.

"She was trying to spare you," Ovel said softly. "Even dead, she was trying to spare you."

"Yes," I said, and let her believe the sentence referred to the forgery.

She squeezed my shoulder on her way out. Her hand was warm through my shirt, and I sat very still until the stairs stopped creaking, and then I turned to the third ledger and added a line:

*Cost of this lie: one friendship, briefly. Forgiven in advance, if she never learns to hold it against me.*

* * *

Bramble caught me at the window past midnight, which was either bad luck or the universe's sense of humor, because the thirteenth sliver hung over the rooftops like a splinter working its way out of the sky's skin. One turn left. Tomorrow night, the moon would finish its impossible arithmetic, and the door behind it would be as open as it would ever be — or as closed, depending on whose blood arrived first.

"Can't sleep either?" He dropped onto the sill across from me, long legs folding like a compass. Up close, in the moonlight, I could see how thin he'd gotten since the Weighhouse. Rescue costs. He'd paid mine in freedom and hadn't once mentioned the invoice. "Or are you balancing the world again?"

"Someone has to. It's badly kept."

"Mm." He looked at the moon. I looked at him. This was our arrangement, and it worked. "Ovel says you found a way to spare you the knife."

"Ovel says a lot of things."

"Kess." He used the name the crew had given me, the small one, the one that didn't weigh anything. "I've run debts my whole life. You know what I can smell?"

"Forgiveness?"

"Fear wearing forgiveness' coat." He wasn't smiling. Bramble almost always smiled; it was his armor and his weapon and his native language. Tonight he wore none of it. "Whatever you've got planned for tomorrow, it ends with you walking away. Promise me that. Not clever-away. Not ledger-away. *Away*-away."

And here was the trap of loving people: they ask you for promises, and every promise is a choice between lying to them and lying to yourself, and only one of those lies keeps them fighting beside you when the fighting matters.

"I promise," I said, "that when this is done, there will be no debt left on my name."

He studied me for a long moment. Somewhere below, a debt-runner laughed at some joke I would never hear, and the sound rose up through the floorboards like warmth from a hearth. Bramble heard it too. I watched him decide, deliberately, to trust me — watched him choose it the way you choose to step onto ice, knowing the sound it makes.

"All right," he said. "But I'm posting Sef and Marla on your route anyway. Non-negotiable. Consider it interest."

"Consider it noted," I said, and made a show of writing it down, and the little laugh he gave me was the most expensive thing I stole all week.

* * *

I worked until the third bell. The forged pages needed finishing — my grandmother's hand was cramped and slanted, and mine wanted to sprawl, and matching a dead woman's fatigue took concentration I usually reserved for the Guild's worst accounts. Twice I caught myself pressing too hard, the nib biting through, and twice I started the page over. Forgery, like sacrifice, does not forgive haste.

Somewhere in the second hour, I realized I was humming. An old tune, wordless, the kind grandmothers hum over cradles. I stopped. Then, deliberately, I started again, because if these were among my last hours, I refused to spend them in silence out of superstition.

I laid out the rest the way I'd lay out any operation: the route to the door, timed against the Guild's patrol rotations, which I still knew better than they did — habits die hard in organizations built on ledgers. The vial. I had been letting it fill for six days, a slow red tithe taken in private, hidden in a hollowed-out binding of a hymnal Ovel never opened because she couldn't bear to. Blood keeps, if you keep it cold and say nothing over it. Grandmother's notes were specific about that too. So many specifics. She had expected her heirs to need them.

The rite itself I memorized and then burned the draft, feeding it page by page into the stove, watching each line curl and brighten and go. *By name the door was opened. By name alone it closes.* The words went up with the smoke, and I felt lighter and heavier in equal measure, the way you feel after signing something irrevocable.

One thing remained unwritten, and it was the hardest entry of all.

I took a fresh page and addressed it to no one, or to everyone, or to whichever of them would be standing in this room tomorrow night wondering where I'd gone. I told them the truth sideways — that the door required a price, that the price had been paid, that payment freely offered binds tighter than any contract the Guild ever drafted. I told them not to look for a body, because doors do not leave bodies; they leave absences shaped like people, and absences are worse to mourn but quicker to heal. I told Bramble his debt to me was voided, retroactive to the waterways, and that arguing with a closed account is a fool's errand he of all people should respect. I told Ovel that my grandmother's forgiveness had never been hers to seek, because there was nothing to forgive — that sealing the gods had been an act of love performed by terrified people, and terror is not a sin, it is a weather.

Then I signed it.

That was the moment my hand finally shook. Not at the blood, not at the burning, not at the moon. At the signature. Because a signature is a small death every time — you set down the self you were when you began the page — and I knew, writing mine, that it might be the last time anyone saw those letters together, that the name itself might go where I was going, that somewhere in the low city a child would someday ask a gardener her name and receive a smile instead of an answer.

I signed it anyway. Thackerays balance their books.

* * *

Dawn came up gray and honest over the rooftops, the kind of light that shows every smudge on every window. I sealed the letter in oilcloth and tucked it beneath the loose brick behind the stove, where Bramble hid the good brandy and therefore would never, ever look.

Downstairs, the crew was waking. I heard Sef complaining about the cold, Marla telling him the cold was character-building, Ovel's quiet footsteps crossing to the shrine-corner where she still prayed, defrocked and unrepentant about it. Ordinary sounds. The sounds of people who believed they had one more day of ordinary ahead of them.

I put on my coat. I checked the vial against my ribs, cold as a coin. I picked up the third ledger — the true one — weighed it in my hand, and understood that I could not leave it. If they found it, they'd follow. If they followed, they'd try to stop me, and Bramble would spend his freedom buying mine back a second time, and Ovel would break herself against the rite trying to substitute, and the whole beautiful reckless family of them would pile their lives onto the scale next to mine, and the scale would tip, and the door would stay hungry.

So I did the last thing an accountant can do. I closed the book, and I carried it with me — not to hide it, but because some entries you don't entrust to shelves. Some you carry to the counter yourself, and pay in person.

At the door, I paused. Below, Bramble was frying eggs, badly, humming my own wordless tune back at the morning without knowing it. The thirteenth sliver had faded with the dawn, but I could feel it waiting behind the sky like a held breath.

"One turn," I said quietly, to the moon, to the door, to my grandmother wherever the dead keep their ledgers. "One turn, and I'll settle everything."

I stepped out into the gray light, and behind me, faint through the floorboards, I heard Sef shout that the eggs were burning — and Bramble laughing, unbothered, certain that tomorrow would come and everyone he loved would be in it.

I walked faster.
