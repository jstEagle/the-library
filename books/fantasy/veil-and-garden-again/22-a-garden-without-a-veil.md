## 22. A Garden Without a Veil

The first thing Kestrel learned about growing things was that they kept no ledgers.

A bean did not record what it owed the soil. A tomato did not itemize the rain. The marigolds she'd planted along the broken wall of the old Weighhouse bloomed and died and bloomed again without once asking her to sign for it, and every morning when she knelt in the dirt with her hands black to the wrist, she felt something in her chest unclench a little further, like a fist remembering how to be a hand.

Two years had passed since the thirteenth turn.

She knew this because Bramble told her, every year, on the same day — arrived at the garden gate with a paper bag of sugared plums and an expression he tried and failed to make casual. *Happy anniversary of not existing,* he'd said the first year, and she'd thrown a trowel at him, and he'd caught it, because Bramble caught everything; that was his whole talent, catching things other people dropped. Debts. Names. Friends.

This morning there was frost on the ruins. The Guild's great hall had come down in the months after the fall — the debt-runners had pulled it apart stone by stone, cheerfully, lawfully, under warrants Ovel had drafted from memory of a legal code nobody else alive could recite — and now its bones made terraces, and the terraces made gardens, and the gardens fed three streets of the low city that had never once been fed by anything the Guild had built there. The vaults below were flooded and sealed. The auction floor was a cistern where children fished for eels. The Master's tower was gone entirely; even the site of it had been argued over so long by the new street-councils that nothing had been built, and wild roses had settled the dispute by simply taking it.

Kestrel walked the rows with a watering can and did not think about any of that, because thinking about it was like pressing a bruise she couldn't quite locate. There was a shape in her where certain memories should have been. She knew the shape the way you know a missing tooth — with your tongue, constantly, involuntarily. She knew she had done something enormous. The whole city had done something enormous; you couldn't live through the night the sky split open and the old gods came to the walls and then *didn't* — you couldn't live through that and be unaware of it. But her own part in it was a door painted on a wall. She could see the frame. She could see everyone else's handprints around the edges, where they'd pushed it open and pulled it shut. Her own prints weren't there.

Ovel said that was the price, and also the gift, and that she was not to apologize for it ever again.

Bramble said less, but he looked at her sometimes across a table with an expression she'd learned to read as *there you are* — as if he were checking, every time, that she was still someone, even without the name.

She was someone. That was the strange, slow discovery of these two years: that a person was not a ledger entry. That identity wasn't a line in a book with a price attached, transferable, seizable, burnable. She had thought — whoever *she* had been, the woman with the ink-stained fingers in the stories people still told too loudly in taverns — she had thought a name was the whole of a self. Now she suspected a name was only the cover of the book, and covers could be replaced, and what mattered was whether the pages were worth anyone's reading.

She set down the watering can and checked the winter beds. Kale, good. The garlic she'd planted in October had come up in neat green spikes, obedient as clerks, and she laughed at herself for the comparison and let the laugh go where it wanted.

That was another thing the garden taught her. Let things go where they wanted. Everything she'd grown from seed had opinions. The squash wanted the whole south wall. The nasturtiums staged a quiet invasion of the onion bed every spring and had to be negotiated with, not commanded. You could not balance a garden. You could only tend it, and forgive it, and be forgiven.

*You could not balance a garden.* Somewhere in her, faint as a bell rung underwater, something recognized the sentence as a kind of epitaph.

* * *

The child arrived at midmorning, as children did, because the garden wall had a gap in it exactly the size of a determined eight-year-old and the whole low city knew it.

This one was new — a girl, thin as a beanstalk, with flour on her sleeves and the ferocious caution of a kid who had learned early that adults asked questions with prices attached. She stood at the end of the kale row with one foot already angled toward retreat and stared at Kestrel with the frank, total appraisal only children and cats permitted themselves.

"You're the one," the girl said.

"I'm the one what?"

"The one from the stories." The girl's chin came up, defiant, as though daring her to deny it. "My gran says you closed the sky. My gran says you were the Guild's tally-keeper and you read all their secret books and you burned ten thousand debts and the moon went back to twelve and it was your fault and it was your gift and—" she ran out of breath, glared, and finished, "—and my gran cries about it sometimes, which is stupid."

"It's not stupid," Kestrel said. "People cry about true things. It's the false ones you can keep dry through." She crouched, slowly, the way you crouch near strays. "What's your name?"

"Petra." Then, immediately, suspiciously: "Why?"

"Because I'm going to ask you to do a job, and I don't pay people who won't tell me their names. That's the rule here. No name, no wages." She held up her own hands, dirt-blackened, callused, unmarked by any brand or ring. "Names are the only coin I deal in anymore."

Petra considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. "What's the job?"

"See those beds?" Kestrel pointed to the lowest terrace, where the frost had silvered everything and the last hardy greens stood in ranks. "Somebody has to learn them before spring. Somebody young, with strong knees and no respect for tradition, because I've been doing it wrong for two years and I need fresh eyes to tell me so."

"You haven't," Petra said. "It looks right."

"It looks right," Kestrel agreed, "and the onions are still crowded, and I refuse to admit why. So. Apprentice. Unpaid at first, paid in plums and knowledge after. Do we have terms?"

The girl's face did something complicated — the flicker of a child who had expected to be shooed away and was instead being handed a place in the world. "Do I get a tool?"

"You get a tool."

"Do I get to know yours? Your name?" And there it was, the real question, the one she'd circled from the wall-gap onward, delivered with a child's terrible directness while the frost melted off the kale and the morning light came gold through the ruined arch behind them.

Kestrel smiled.

She felt it happen — felt the smile arrive from somewhere deeper than her face, from whatever unnamed country she'd been living in these two years, the country with no borders and no taxes and no master. Because here was the thing she had finally, finally learned, the thing the door had taught her when it took her name and left her standing: a person who chooses is never empty. She had woken nameless in a borrowed bed with Bramble's crew arguing over who would make the tea, and she had chosen, that very morning, to stay. She had chosen the garden over the sea, the trowel over the pen, the long slow work of feeding strangers over the loud fast work of being a legend. Every day since, she had chosen again. Two years of small choices, laid down like courses of stone, and the wall they built was more hers than any name had ever been.

"My name," she said, "is whatever you decide to call me. That's the wage for apprentices. Naming rights."

Petra's eyes went wide with the enormity of the power being handed her. She looked at the garden. She looked at the ruined arch, the roses on the tower's bones, the woman kneeling in the dirt with her black hands open.

"Gardener," she said at last, testing it. Then, with sudden fierce satisfaction: "*My* Gardener."

"Done," said Kestrel, whose mother — whoever she had been, whoever anyone had been — would have found this either tragic or hilarious, and who had decided, long ago, on hilarious. "Terms sealed. First lesson: show me how you'd fix my onions, and be brutal."

* * *

They worked until the light went long and amber. Petra turned out to have a natural eye and no mercy whatsoever, and by the time the shadows reached the old Weighhouse steps she had reorganized two beds and pronounced Kestrel's compost "basically fine, actually," which Kestrel chose to receive as high praise.

Bramble came by at dusk, as he often did, carrying bread and pretending he hadn't baked it himself. He was grayer at the temples now and no quieter; he sat on the wall with his boots swinging over the gap Petra used and watched the girl march the rows with her new trowel like a general inspecting troops.

"She'll run this place inside five years," he said.

"She'll run the whole street inside five years. I'm just the soil she's rooted in for now."

"Mm." He broke the bread, gave her the bigger half out of habit, and was quiet for a moment — a rare enough event that she glanced over. He was looking at the western sky, where the moon was rising: round, whole, and twelve-turns honest, keeping its accounts in order at last. "Festival next week," he said. "The city council wants to put up a plaque. On the arch, maybe. Or the well. Something about the one who ended it."

"There's no 'the one,'" Kestrel said. "There was a crew. There was a priestess who broke a thirty-year oath. There was a smuggler who traded his freedom for mine and got it back by being insufferable in front of a tribunal. There were ten thousand people who tore a guild apart with their bare hands because somebody finally showed them the arithmetic of their own chains." She bit the bread. "Put the plaque on the well. Wells serve everybody."

"He'll want to carve a name anyway. They always do. Councils, plaques, names — it's how they think." Bramble smiled sideways at her, and it was the old smile, the door-opening one, but there was something underneath it tonight, patient and careful, the way he always was on this particular week of the year. "You could choose one, you know. If you wanted. Nobody would ask questions. Well — Ovel would ask questions, but she'd cry while doing it, so it balances out."

Kestrel looked down at her hands. Dirt in every line, a blister forming where the trowel had rubbed, a scratch from the rose canes she'd been training along the south wall. Hands that had held ledgers full of blood-priced miracles, once, in a life she could see only as others described it — a room seen through a keyhole. Hands that had, apparently, written her own name into a door between worlds and walked away lighter.

"No," she said, and found she meant it all the way down. "I had a name. It did its job. It's resting." She bumped her shoulder against his. "Besides, if I take a name, I'll have to put it in a ledger somewhere, and I swore off those."

"You never swore off anything. You wouldn't remember swearing."

"That," said Kestrel, "is the beauty of it."

Below them, Petra had discovered the eel-fishing children at the cistern and was negotiating — loudly, skillfully, with gestures that would have made a Guild auctioneer proud — for a share of their catch. The frost was coming back down out of the hills. Lamps were lighting street by street through the low city, ordinary lamps for ordinary evenings, and somewhere a neighbor was singing the new song, the one without a god's name in it, the one about the year the books all burned.

Bramble hopped down from the wall. "Plums tomorrow," he said. "And Ovel sends scripture. She says you're to stop calling it scripture. She says it's a *pamphlet*."

"Tell her pamphlets don't have forty pages of footnotes."

"Tell her yourself. You're coming to dinner Thursday. Non-negotiable. It's in the terms of your apprenticeship — I heard you offer naming rights, so I'm invoking seniority." He backed away down the path, pointing at her with the bread. "Thursday, Gardener. Bring the girl. Bring the onions. Bring your fresh eyes."

He went, whistling, into the blue evening, and Kestrel stood a while longer among her rows with the cold coming up out of the ground and the stars coming out over the city that governed itself now, badly some days, gloriously on others, without gods or guilds or anyone's permission.

A name, she thought. People kept offering her one back, the way you offer a coat to someone you believe is cold. And someday — maybe soon, maybe when Petra's grandchildren worked these same beds — she might choose one, deliberately, the way you plant a tree: for shade you'll never sit in, for fruit you'll never taste. A name chosen freely, given freely, costing nothing, owning nothing. Not a lock. Not a debt. Just a word, warm as bread, to hand to the world.

But not yet. The garden wasn't finished, and neither, it turned out, was she.

From the bottom terrace, Petra's voice rang up through the frost-dark, imperious and delighted:

"Gardener! The eels! Come look what I got!"

Kestrel smiled, and went down toward the lamplight and the child and the living dark earth, carrying nothing, owing nothing, arriving — as she did now, every single day — exactly on time.
