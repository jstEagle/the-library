## 2. Tonight, By Name

The rain came to Vellamarch the way debts did: slowly at first, then all at once, and always on the night you had planned to sleep.

Lior Oyelaran stood behind the counter of the Gilded Ledger and watched it hammer the cobbles of Copperlane Street, each drop striking with the particular insistence of a creditor who would not be put off until morning. The shop's windows were diamond-paned and old, and the rain turned the lamplight inside into a hundred small trembling coins scattered across the floor. Lior counted them out of habit. Forty-one. Forty-two. A pane rattled in its leading and the count broke, and they began again.

This was how Lior prayed. Not with words—words were for gods who answered—but with numbers. The silent god Veth had no temples in Vellamarch, only a niche above the guild's counting room where a small stone figure sat with its hands cupped, palms up, receiving nothing. Nine years ago, the offerings had stopped being accepted. Nine years ago, Veth had gone quiet as a closed book, and Lior had kept tithing anyway, because a debt was a debt whether or not anyone was watching to collect it. Especially then.

*One year of good weather for the barley-farmers of the Ashen Vale,* Lior thought, setting the intention into the ledger's margins the way one might slip a coin beneath a door. *Take it from my account. I have years to spare.*

No answer came. No answer ever came. But somewhere in the marrow-deep quiet of their chest, Lior felt the prayer land the way a letter lands in an empty house—received by no one, delivered all the same.

The bell above the door rang at half past the ninth hour, which was wrong, because the Gilded Ledger received clients by appointment only, and tonight's appointments had ended at the eighth. Lior looked up from the day-book with the particular stillness they had perfected over eleven years of guild work: not frozen, merely *unremarkable*, a body arranged so that eyes would slide off it like rain off oiled leather.

The woman in the doorway was soaked through and entirely unbothered by it. She was perhaps fifty, built like a question that expected an answer, with grey-shot hair pinned up with what appeared to be two bone styluses and a pair of spectacles pushed up onto her forehead that she clearly did not need, because her eyes—pale, quick, cataloguing—were already reading the room. She carried a long oilcloth-wrapped case against her chest the way other people carried infants.

"You're closed," she said. It was not an apology or a retreat. It was an observation she intended to overcome.

"We are," Lior said. "Appointments are made through the front office, on Quill Street, during daylight hours."

"I know. I've been there three times." She crossed the threshold without invitation, dripping on the imported rug that Guildmaster Ombre had paid too much for, and set her case on the counter with the reverence of a priest laying down relics. "Sefa Ilvane. Formerly senior authenticator of the Vaulted Archive at Correnholt. Currently—" her mouth twisted around the word— "*disgraced*. Which is why I'm here at half past nine in the rain instead of sending a clerk. Nobody respectable will touch what I'm carrying, and I need someone whose discretion is absolute and whose curiosity is professional rather than personal."

"And you decided I was both?"

"I watched you for two days." She said it without embarrassment. "You water the plant in the window at the same minute every evening. You lock the strongbox with your left hand and write with your right, so you're never without a hand free. You gave the grief-seller yesterday exactly what he asked for and not one coin more, and you didn't blink when he wept on your counter. You are either the most honest merchant in this city or the best liar, and frankly, either will serve."

Lior did not move. Inside, something small and cold turned over—the feeling of being *seen*, which was a sensation they had spent a lifetime avoiding and had never quite learned to survive. "What is it you want authenticated?"

Sefa peeled back the oilcloth with fingers that shook slightly—not from fear, Lior judged, but from the effort of handling something carefully when every instinct screamed to tear it open. Beneath lay a book.

Even before the wrapping fell away, Lior knew it was old. Old things had a smell: vellum and iron-gall ink and the faint sweetness of boards seasoned in smoke. But this book smelled of something else besides—a mineral tang, like the air in deep cellars, like stone that had never seen sun. Its cover was slate-grey leather gone soft as skin, and its clasps were glass.

"The Codex of the Veiled Century," Sefa said. "Written over thirty years by an author history has misplaced. Prophecies, if you believe in them—which I did not, professionally, until last month." Her pale eyes lifted to Lior's face. "It surfaced in a dead man's estate in the Saltrows. The estate's executors want it sold to the Gilded Ledger, because the Gilded Ledger trades in impossible things, and a book that predicts the future is the most impossible thing there is."

"Prophecy is fraud," Lior said automatically. It was guild doctrine. You could sell someone tomorrow; you could not honestly tell them what tomorrow held. "Cold readings, retroactive forgery, self-fulfilling suggestion. We authenticate such books to price them as curiosities, nothing more."

"Mm," said Sefa, in the tone of a scholar listening to a first-year student recite something confidently wrong. "Then you'll want to check the ink oxidation against known samples. And the watermark in the endpapers, which matches a mill that burned eighty-nine years ago. And the marginalia, which references events that had not occurred when the binding was sewn." She opened the glass clasps. "I've spent four weeks on it. It's genuine. It was written a century ago by someone who could see forward the way you and I see across a room. And tonight—" she drew a breath, and for the first time her composure flickered, "—tonight is why I came in person."

She turned pages. Lior watched her hands more than the book, because hands told truths faces were trained out of. Hers moved with love and dread in equal measure, hovering, barely touching, turning leaves as though each were a sleeping animal that might wake angry.

The codex was organized by date. That much Lior registered with professional detachment—a calendar structure, entries in a fine, slanted hand, the letters narrow and tall like reeds. Dates through a hundred years. Some pages dense, some nearly blank. Here and there, whole entries struck through in a different ink, as though the author had argued with themselves.

Sefa stopped turning.

"Tonight's date," she said quietly.

Lior looked down.

The entry was short. Shorter than most. The fine reed-slanted script curved across the page in lines that seemed, in the lamplight, almost to lean toward the reader:

*Tonight the veil thins over Vellamarch, and the account comes due. The child sold at the Well Gate returns to the mountain's mouth, though she does not know the road home. Her name is written thrice in the book beneath: once as goods, once as payment, once as the key.*

And beneath that, in the same hand, in letters slightly larger, slightly slower, as though the author had wanted no ambiguity for whoever read it a hundred years on:

*Lior Oyelaran.*

The rain filled the silence. Forty-one panes of light trembled on the floor. Somewhere upstairs, the guild's old clock ticked with the patience of a thing that had all night.

"That's not a common name," Sefa said. Her voice had gone careful, the way voices go when approaching a wounded animal—or a person discovering they are one. "Oyelaran. I checked the census rolls of three cities. There is one Lior Oyelaran in the world, according to paper. Employed at the Gilded Ledger, Vellamarch. Age consistent with—"

"Stop." 

The word came out of Lior flat and small, and they did not recognize their own voice saying it. They were aware, distantly, of performing the motions of composure: straightening the day-book, aligning the pen with the counter's edge, the small mechanical tidinesses that had held their life together for eleven years like stitching through cloth. Their hands did these things. Their hands were very good at doing things while the rest of them went elsewhere.

Because here was the truth of Lior Oyelaran, the truth they kept folded so small and so deep that even they rarely unfolded it: they had no childhood. Not the way other people meant it. They had arrived at the Gilded Ledger at fourteen with a satchel, a bill of apprenticeship signed in a hand they did not recognize, and nothing before that—no face of a mother, no smell of a kitchen, no memory further back than a road and rain, rain like tonight's rain, and a gate. A well gate. They had told themselves for years that the gap was grief, that trauma had eaten the early years whole, that this happened to people, that it was survivable if you became excellent at arithmetic and invisible at everything else. They had built a life on top of the absence the way you build a house on filled earth, knowing, always knowing, the ground underneath was hollow.

*Sold at the Well Gate.* The words sat on the page like a knife sitting on a table, ordinary and impossible.

"Lior Oyelaran," Sefa repeated softly. Not cruelly. An archivist confirmed citations; it was reflex. "That is you."

"It's a name," Lior said. "Names are traded here daily. You know that better than anyone. Half our inventory is names. Someone wrote a name in a mad book a century ago. Coincidence is not prophecy."

"No," Sefa agreed. "Coincidence is not prophecy." She turned the page with one fingertip, and Lior should have stopped her—they would later understand, with terrible clarity, that they could have stopped her—and there, filling the next leaf in that same reed-slanted hand, was more.

*The child sold at the Well Gate was bought for the Court Beneath the Mountain, and the price was one impossible thing, as is the custom of that court. The court does not forget its purchases. On the centennial night it calls them home. What the court bought, the court intends to use. What the court uses, the court consumes.*

Beneath, smaller, cramped into the margin as though the author had been afraid even of their own handwriting:

*I have tried to warn her. I do not know if warning is mercy or cruelty. If you are reading this, child of the well gate: run, or read everything. Those are the only doors.*

The lamp flame leaned in a draft that had no source. Lior became aware that they were gripping the counter's edge hard enough that the wood bit crescents into their palms, and that their breath was doing something rhythmic and wrong, and that Sefa was watching them with an expression that was not pity—Sefa, to her credit, seemed constitutionally incapable of pity—but was perhaps its scholarly cousin: the look of someone witnessing a document destroy itself.

"There's more," Sefa said gently. "There's an entire section, near the end, about the court. About a dragon. About—" she hesitated, and Lior understood she had been deciding all evening how much to say, and had come through the rain anyway because the deciding had failed, "—about souls. Kept accounts of. Stored. I didn't believe it. I still don't, not wholly. But the book is genuine, the date is tonight, and the name is yours, and I have been an authenticator for twenty-six years and I have never once had a forgery *wait for me*."

"What does that mean?"

"It means," Sefa said, "that three days ago, the entry for tonight was one line. Tonight, when I opened the codex to bring it to you, it was this." She tapped the page, her finger steady now, steadier than her voice. "The book is still writing itself, Master Oyelaran. Or something is writing in it. And whatever it is, it knows your name, and it knows where you work, because it addressed the entry to this shop. To be found. By you. Tonight."

The floor dropped.

Not the real floor—the real floor stayed exactly where floors stay, worn oak over cellar stone, solid as any ledger line. But the other floor, the one Lior had laid down plank by plank over the hollow place: the competence, the invisibility, the eleven years of unremarkable excellence, the certainty that they were a person who had *chosen* to be a person of small surfaces and clean accounts—that floor went out from under them all at once, silently, the way floors go in dreams, and Lior stood in the falling space between the life they had constructed and the sentence on the page, and the sentence said: *goods*.

Once as goods.

They had spent their whole career handling things that had been people, or pieces of people—bottled grief that had been someone's wedding day, borrowed years that had been someone's daughter's childhood, names sold twice until the original owner couldn't remember answering to them. They had told themselves the work was honest because the accounting was honest. They had never once let themselves think about the moment before the ledger line: the moment a person became an entry.

Now they were the entry. They had maybe always been the entry. Perhaps the whole of their remembered life—the road, the rain, the gate, the satchel, the bill of apprenticeship—was simply the paperwork of a sale, the receipt trailing after the merchandise.

"Master Oyelaran." Sefa's voice, distant, then nearer. "Sit down. You've gone the color of the vellum."

"I don't sit with clients," Lior heard themselves say, absurdly, and the absurdity cracked something, and they sat.

The chair received them. The rain went on. Lior looked at their own hands—clean, precise, ink-stained at the second knuckle of the right middle finger, hands that had counted ten thousand honest sums—and felt the strangest sensation, as though looking at the hands of a stranger wearing their gloves.

"My parents," they said. It came out as a croak. They cleared their throat and tried again, because if nothing else in their life was going to hold, diction would. "The codex says *sold*. Sold implies a seller. Do you know—does the book say—"

"I haven't read that far ahead," Sefa said. "I stopped when I reached your name. I thought—" she took off the unnecessary spectacles and folded them, a small ritual of honesty, "—I thought you deserved to hear it from the page yourself, not from a stranger's summary. Archivist's ethics. Also human ones, though those are less codified."

Outside, boots splashed through the flooded gutter. A cart groaned past. The city went about its night, ignorant, and Lior envied it with a purity they had never managed to feel about anything.

Above the counter, in the little niche Ombre had installed years ago—*every shop needs a god, dear one, even a silent one*—the small stone figure of Veth sat with its cupped hands open, receiving nothing, saying nothing, as it had for nine years. Lior had always taken the silence as indifference. Now, staring at the page that called them *goods* and *key*, they felt the silence differently: not a god declining to answer, but a god unable to speak. As though somewhere, the words themselves had been taken into custody.

It was a strange thought. It was not a thought they had trained themselves to have. It arrived whole, like a memory that wasn't theirs, and it frightened them more than the name on the page.

"Who else knows?" Lior asked. "About the codex. About the name."

"The executors. Two rival buyers who've made offers. My former colleagues, who revoked my credentials for insisting on authenticity." Sefa shrugged. "And now you."

"And Ombre."

Something crossed Sefa's face—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "Guildmaster Ombre," she said carefully, "is the one who recommended I bring it to you personally."

The words landed strangely. Lior turned them over. *Recommended.* Ombre, who vetted every acquisition. Ombre, who had hired a fourteen-year-old nobody with no history and no references and had never once asked about the missing years—not once in eleven years, which Lior had always read as tact, as kindness, as the courtesy of a woman who understood that everyone has locked rooms. 

Or as a woman who already knew what was behind the door.

The clock upstairs struck the tenth hour. The sound rolled down through the floorboards, patient and enormous, and Lior sat in the dropped-away space where their ordered life had been, and understood that the night had only begun.

"Sefa Ilvane," they said, rising, taking the codex in both hands—it was heavier than a book should be, heavier than paper and board and glass, heavy the way guilt is heavy, the way inheritance is heavy—"you'll stay tonight. We'll read it together. All of it."

Sefa raised an eyebrow. "I was given to understand the Ledger was closed."

"It is." Lior blew out the counter lamp, and the dark came in close and mineral-smelling, like the bottom of a mountain. "But apparently I'm open."

* * *

They read until the rain stopped, somewhere past midnight, two figures bent over a century-old book in a shop full of impossible things. And it was Sefa, near the end of the section on the court, who went suddenly rigid and pulled the lamp close, and read aloud in a voice that had forgotten to be careful:

*"On the centennial night, the buyer comes to collect what was purchased at the Well Gate. She will wear a kind face. She remembers the mother."*

Lior looked up.

Somewhere far above the city, above the rain-washed roofs, thunder rolled—or something that was not quite thunder, a sound with intention in it, rolling down off the mountains that stood black against the sky to the north.

The mountains. The mountain.

Lior had lived their whole life in Vellamarch and had never once, they realized, been able to say why they never looked north.
