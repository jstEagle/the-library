## 3. What the Guild Owes

Guildmaster Ombre Vess had three rules for the Gilded Ledger, and she had broken all of them before her fortieth year.

The first rule was *never trade in what you cannot price*. The second was *never let the guild know your face twice in the same grief*. The third—the one she repeated to every new hire, the one carved into the lintel above the counting room door in letters that had been gilded by a man who later sold his own shadow and regretted it—was *the ledger does not lie*.

She was thinking about the third rule when Sefa's messenger came up the back stairs with a folded page wrapped in oilcloth, and she was still thinking it when she broke the seal, and she was not thinking anything at all when she read the first line.

The rain had been coming down since dusk, a soft grey insistence against the windows of her office on the third floor. Below her, through the floorboards, she could hear the ordinary music of the guild at work: the scratch of quills, the murmur of brokers quoting prices on things that had no business having prices, the clink of the tea trolley that Ferro pushed through the halls at this hour every night because ritual was the only currency that never devalued. Somewhere down there, Lior Oyelaran was closing out the night's accounts with that terrible, beautiful precision of theirs, entering each transaction in a hand so even it looked printed, and Ombre had spent nine years telling herself that the evenness of that hand was discipline and not damage.

She unfolded the page.

It was a copy—Sefa's hand, careful and small, each letter placed like a stone in a wall—but the original had been described to her in enough detail over the years that she could see it behind the copy: vellum gone the color of old honey, margins crowded with a script that slanted like rain falling sideways. The Codex of the Well Gate. She had never held it. She had made very sure, for eleven years, that she would never have to hold it.

She read.

*On the centennial night, the buyer comes to collect what was purchased at the Well Gate.*

Her hands did not shake. That was the first thing she noticed about herself, the way you notice the absence of a sound—a clock stopping, a bird going quiet. Her hands were perfectly steady, because they were guildmaster's hands, trained across thirty years to sign away other people's sorrows without tremor, and she hated them in that moment with a clean and total hatred.

*She will wear a kind face.*

Ombre set the page down on her desk, next to the lamp, next to the cup of cold cinnamon tea she had poured at dusk and never drunk. The lamplight caught the ink and made it gleam wetly, though the ink was dry, though everything on the page was dry and finished and a hundred years old.

*She remembers the mother.*

She stood up. She walked to the window. She watched the rain come down over Vellamarch, over the tiled roofs and the crooked bell tower of the Chantry of Small Hours, over the north road where the mountains stood up against the sky like a wall someone had built to keep a promise in—or keep something else in—and she thought: *I should have burned it.*

That was the thought. Not *I should have told them*. Not *I should have prepared them*. Burned it. Eleven years ago, when she had first learned what slept in the guild's deep archive, catalogued under a number instead of a name, she had stood in the vault with a candle in one hand and the codex's chain in the other, and she had thought about fire with an honesty that still shamed her. And then she had thought about what the codex contained besides the prophecy—provenance, bills of sale, the whole paper skeleton of a century of transactions—and she had done what she always did, what the guild had trained her to do, what she was apparently incapable of not doing.

She had priced it. She had weighed the cost of burning against the cost of keeping, and keeping had won, because keeping always won, because she was a woman who believed in ledgers more than she believed in mercy, and now the bill had come due on the centennial night, exactly as written, and somewhere below her feet Lior Oyelaran was balancing accounts in a hand as even as print.

* * *

There was a knock. Two knocks, soft, spaced wrong—Ferro's knock, which meant *I know you want to be alone and I am ignoring that*, which was why she kept him.

"Enter."

Ferro came in carrying the tea trolley he had already brought an hour ago, with the same pot, and poured her a fresh cup without comment on the cold one, which was his way of saying everything he ever said. He was sixty, built like a door, bald as a coin, and he had been the guild's porter since before Ombre had been guildmaster, since before she had been anything but a runaway clerk with forged references and a talent for arithmetic that frightened people.

"Well?" he said.

"You knew I'd send for you."

"I've known you'd send for me since Tuesday." He settled into the chair across from her desk without being asked, which no one else in the building would have dared, and looked at the page under the lamp. He didn't touch it. Ferro never touched documents; he said paper remembered fingerprints and he didn't want to be remembered. "Is it tonight?"

"It's tonight."

He nodded slowly, the way men nod at weather. "And the child?"

"They're not a child."

"They're twenty-six years old and they eat standing up because sitting still feels like wasting time, and they flinch when anyone says their full name, and they haven't taken a single day off in nine years." Ferro folded his enormous hands over his stomach. "I've carried the tea past their desk every night for nine years, Ombre. You don't have to tell me what they are. I'm asking if they know."

"No."

"And now?"

Ombre turned back to the window. The rain had thickened; the mountains were gone entirely, swallowed into cloud, and somehow that was worse, as if the north had leaned closer while she wasn't watching.

"Now the archivist knows," she said. "Sefa found the entry. Tonight. In front of Lior."

Ferro swore, quietly, comprehensively, in the manner of a man who had once been a soldier and had retired the loud version of himself along with the uniform.

"How much did they read?"

"All of it, from what the messenger says. Their own name. The date. The buyer." Ombre pressed two fingers against her temple, where a headache was assembling itself out of old materials. "They're down there right now, Ferro. Closing the books. Because that's what they do when the world breaks—they straighten it. They alphabetize it. I taught them that."

"Did you?"

The question landed harder than he probably intended, or perhaps exactly as hard as he intended; Ferro had a porter's genius for knowing which crates needed dropping. Ombre looked at him.

"What's that supposed to mean?"

"I mean," he said, "that I've watched you train that kid for nine years, and I've never once seen you teach them anything you didn't already know how to do yourself. Even-handedness. Invisibility. Never wanting anything where anyone can see you wanting it." He shrugged, a landslide in miniature. "Maybe you didn't teach them to straighten broken things. Maybe you just showed them a mirror."

* * *

After Ferro left—taking the cold tea, leaving the hot, which was the entire vocabulary of their friendship—Ombre unlocked the drawer at the bottom of her desk.

Inside, wrapped in black silk, was a contract.

She had put it there four years ago, after taking it out of the vault, after reading it forty times, after deciding that some debts a guildmaster carries personally rather than letting the archive hold them. It was written on court-paper, which is to say paper that had never been a tree—smooth, faintly warm, the color of candlelight through skin—and the seal at its foot was pressed in wax the red of a mouth. She did not need to unwrap it. She knew every line.

*A soul, half-portioned, purchased from the Court Beneath the Mountain by the Gilded Ledger of Vellamarch. Purchased back, the document said, in the guild's most careful legal language. Half of a soul belonging to one infant, Lior Oyelaran, sold at the Well Gate one hundred years minus twenty-six years ago.*

Half. The court had sold half. The other half was still beneath the mountain, in whatever vault the court kept its inventory in, and Ombre had paid eleven years of the guild's profits for the half she could reach, and she had hidden the purchase in the ledger under a category that existed nowhere else in the guild's taxonomy: *miscellaneous acquisitions, ongoing.*

Ongoing. As if it were a shipment of saffron still in transit.

She had told herself a hundred stories about why she hadn't told Lior. That the knowledge would break them. That the court would feel the disturbance, like a spider feeling its web touched, and come early. That Lior deserved a childhood—no, that word wouldn't survive inspection; Lior had never had a childhood, Lior had been sold out of one—that Lior deserved *years*, unhaunted ones, before learning they were merchandise. Every story had been true the way a good forgery is true: accurate in every detail except the one that mattered.

The truth was simpler and it sat in her chest now like a swallowed stone: she had not told them because telling them meant admitting what she was. A broker. A woman who had bought a piece of a person and shelved it. She had wanted to be, in Lior's eyes, something other than what she was, and she had let a child's peace pay the interest on her vanity.

And now the centennial was tonight, and the buyer was coming with a kind face, and Lior had read their own name in a dead man's prophecy, and Ombre Vess was sitting alone in her office holding half a soul in a silk wrapper like a woman holding half a map to a treasure she'd buried too deep to dig up in time.

*The ledger does not lie.* No. It didn't. That was the horror of it. It lay awake all night, every night, patiently waiting for you to read it.

* * *

She went down.

The counting room at midnight had a particular light—not lamplight, though lamps burned at every desk, but something underneath the lamplight, a goldness that rose off the ledger-books themselves, off decades of ink and handling, so that everyone who worked there worked inside a kind of amber. Ombre had always found it beautiful. Tonight it looked like preservation. Tonight it looked like insects in resin.

Lior was at their desk.

Of course they were. Everyone else had drifted home through the rain hours ago, but Lior's lamp was lit and Lior's pen was moving, and Ombre stopped at the edge of the amber light and simply watched them for a moment, the way you watch a candle flame when you are trying to memorize it.

They were thin. They had always been thin, but tonight the thinness looked less like economy and more like erosion. Their hair was tied back with the same plain cord they used every day. Their sleeves were rolled to the elbow, forearms ink-stained to the wrist. And their face—

Their face was doing what Lior's face always did, which was nothing, which was the most frightening thing about them, a smoothness like the surface of water over something drowning. But Ombre had nine years of practice reading the water. She saw the way the pen paused a fraction too long between entries. She saw the left hand lying flat on the desk, pressing down, as if holding the desk to the floor. She saw that the ledger open before them was the night-book, and that the last completed line was dated correctly, and that Lior had entered the same figure twice.

Lior Oyelaran had never in nine years entered a figure twice.

"Lior."

The pen stopped. For a moment they didn't look up, and Ombre understood—they were putting their face back on, assembling it piece by piece, the calm and the competence and the courteous blankness, and she wanted to cross the room and take their hands and say *stop, stop, you don't have to do that here, you never had to do that here,* and she knew with equal certainty that if she did, they would only assemble it faster.

"Guildmaster." They stood. Of course they stood. "The night-book is nearly closed. There were two trades after dark—a bottled grief, out; a borrowed year, returned early, which I've noted as a partial refund per section twelve. Sefa the archivist authenticated the codex and—" a pause, a hairline fracture running through the level voice, "—and left. Is there something you need?"

*Yes,* she thought. *I need to tell you that I've known since before you could write your own name. I need to tell you that I bought back half of you and hid it in my desk like contraband. I need to tell you that the mountain in the north has been reaching for you your whole life and I built my career on standing between you and it and calling that love.*

What she said was: "Sit down, Lior."

Something flickered across their face—surprise, quickly filed. Guildmasters did not ask; they instructed, gently, but they instructed. She pulled Ferro's abandoned chair around to the near side of her own desk, the visitor's side, and sat facing them across the blotter like a client, which was deliberate, which was the only honest posture she had left.

"I'm going to tell you something," she said, "and then you're going to be angry, and you're entitled to every minute of it, and I'm not going to defend myself while you're angry because there is no defense. Do you understand?"

Lior sat. Their hands folded on the desk's edge. Waiting. Always waiting, always ready to receive instructions, and Ombre thought: *I did this. Nine years of wages and training and I taught them to wait like a servant in a house that was supposed to be theirs.*

"The codex," she said. "The Codex of the Well Gate. It has been in our archive for eleven years. Catalogued under Vault designation 114-Vessel, cross-indexed under regional prophetic materials, restricted access, sealed by order of the guildmaster." She heard her own voice doing it, the broker's cadence, precise and bloodless, and she made herself break it. "I sealed it. I read it eleven years ago, Lior. I read your name in it eleven years ago, and I locked it in the dark, and I hired you anyway, and I never said a word."

The lamp between them hissed softly, the way lamps do, the way rooms do when they are listening.

Lior's expression did not change. That was the worst of it. Ombre had braced for fury, for tears, for the table struck with a flat palm—anything but this perfect, patient stillness, this face like a closed account.

"You knew," Lior said at last. Their voice was quiet and terribly even. "You knew what I was. What was written. And you—" They stopped. Restarted. "You gave me a desk."

"I gave you a cage with better lighting," Ombre said. "I know. I've known for years that's what it was. I told myself it was protection."

"Was it?"

And there it was, the question she deserved, hanging in the amber air between them, and Ombre found that her hands were finally shaking, now, when it was too late for shaking to mean anything except the truth.

"Partly," she said. "Which is the most dishonest answer available, because partly true is how the guild makes all its worst trades." She reached into her desk drawer, and drew out the black silk bundle, and laid it on the blotter between them, and unwrapped it, and turned the warm court-paper so that Lior could read the first line, the one with their own name in it, the one describing the half of them that had been bought back and hidden for four years under *miscellaneous acquisitions, ongoing.*

"This is yours," she said. "Not the paper. What the paper represents. I should have given it to you years ago, and I didn't, and I will spend the rest of my life paying for that, but tonight—" her voice cracked, finally, the ledger of her composure finally overbalancing, "—tonight the rest of your life may be a shorter term than we assumed, and I will not have you walk into that mountain owing me the truth."

Lior looked down at the contract. Their fingers hovered above the court-paper without touching it, the way people hover over relics, over wounds, over sleeping animals. And Ombre watched them read, and watched the stillness of their face begin, very slowly, to fail—not breaking, nothing so dramatic, but failing the way a dam fails, a seam of wetness at the corner of one eye that they did not wipe away because wiping it away would be admitting it existed.

"Half," Lior said. Barely a sound.

"Half. The other half is still theirs. Under the mountain." Ombre leaned forward. "Lior. Listen to me. Whatever you're planning—and I know you, I know that look, it's the look you get before you reorganize a filing system, except aimed at fate itself—whatever you're planning, you will not walk out of this guild alone. That is not a request. That is the one debt this house has always owed you, and I am calling it due."

For a long moment Lior said nothing. The rain filled the silence, drumming on the roof, streaming down the dark windows, and far to the north, beneath the clouds, the mountain waited with its century-old patience.

Then Lior lifted their head, and their eyes were dry again, and steady, and burning underneath with something Ombre had never seen in them before—something that looked almost like hope, if hope had been forged in a fire.

"Then tell me everything," Lior said. "Start with the Well Gate. And don't leave anything out to protect me. I've had enough of that to last a lifetime."

Ombre bowed her head. "Yes," she said. "You have."

And downstairs, in the sealed dark of the vault, under designation 114-Vessel, the codex lay open to a page whose final line neither of them had yet read aloud—the line beneath the prophecy, added in a different hand, fresher ink, a hand that had written:

*The guildmaster knows. She has always known. Watch what she does with what she owes.*
