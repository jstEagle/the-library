## 4. Sold at the Well Gate

The archive beneath the Gilded Ledger smelled of cold iron and older paper, and Sefa loved it the way other people loved their mothers.

She had been dismissed from the Royal Athenaeum for loving it too much—for pulling a thread of forgery through the collected correspondence of a dead chancellor until the whole reputation unraveled, taking three careers and one marriage down with it. *Disgraced archivist*, the broadsheets said, as though the disgrace were hers and not the documents'. She had stopped arguing about it years ago. The documents never lied. People did. That was the whole of her creed, worn smooth as a river stone: *the documents never lie.* They only wait.

Tonight they were waiting for her.

She had claimed the guild's lowest reading room—the one with no windows, where the lamps burned tallow that gave off a faint honeyed smell—and spread the codex open on a slab of slate rather than wood, because she had already found one thing in its binding that made her want to burn everything it touched, and slate did not burn.

The book was called, in an elegant hand on its first leaf, *The Veilward Codex*. A century old, give or take a decade; the vellum said closer to ninety-eight years, because whoever had prepared the skins had been in a hurry and left the telltale follicle patterns of spring slaughter. Someone had wanted this book to seem older than it was. Sefa filed that away the way she filed everything: precisely, and without mercy.

She worked by lamplight with a jeweler's loupe screwed into her eye socket, tweezers in one hand, a bowl of ash-water at her elbow for lifting fragments. Provenance was a chain, and every chain had links, and every link could be tested. She tested them one by one, humming under her breath—a habit from her Athenaeum days, some half-remembered counting song—while above her, in the guild's upper rooms, the mercenary named Lior Oyelaran was presumably still standing very straight and very silent, holding a page that bore their own name under tonight's date.

Sefa had seen their face when they read it. She had spent thirty years learning to read faces the way she read marginalia—quickly, in bad light, without being noticed doing it—and what she had seen was not fear. It was recognition arriving late. Like someone finding their own handwriting on a letter they had no memory of writing.

That was why she was here instead of abed. Not the prophecy. Prophecies were a coin the guild minted by the barrelful, and she had long ago stopped biting them. But a name written a century before its owner's birth—that was not prophecy. That was *record-keeping*. And records had authors, and authors had reasons, and reasons left fingerprints.

*Find the author,* she told herself, turning the first gathering of leaves with bone folders, gentle as handling a sleeping bird. *Everything else follows.*

* * *

The codex's binding was the first crime scene.

It was bound in something that was not leather. She had known it since the moment Guildmaster Ombre unlocked the case and let her lift the book out—a wrongness in the grain, a faint warmth that vellum should not hold an hour after coming out of a cold strongbox. Under the loupe, magnified forty times, the surface showed no follicles at all. It showed pores. Fine, regular, human-scale pores, arranged in the whorls of skin that had once known sunlight.

Sefa set the book down. She walked to the corner of the room. She was quietly, thoroughly sick into the ash-bucket, which was there for exactly that purpose; the guild's archivists before her had all, apparently, arrived at the same discovery eventually. There was a scrubbed patch on the flagstones near the bucket that suggested many arrivals.

Then she rinsed her mouth, wiped her hands, went back, and kept working. This was the discipline the Athenaeum had actually taught her, the one no degree parchment mentioned: the work continues after the vomiting. Especially then. Whoever bound this book in human skin had counted on the finder recoiling. Recoiling was a kind of silence. She refused to be silent.

The endpapers were more instructive. Beneath a decorative wash of gilt—applied later, crudely, to disguise—she lifted a flake with her tweezers and found, underneath, a bookseller's stamp: *Harrow & Vetch, Binders, Well Gate, Lower City.* Well Gate. The poor quarter's market gate, where the city's forgotten things changed hands at dawn. She knew the shop. Or she knew what had replaced it; Harrow & Vetch had burned some sixty years back, and the lot had become a chandler's, then a pawnshop, then nothing.

But shops leave waste. And waste survives.

* * *

Guildmaster Ombre had given her the run of the guild's deep storage with a single warning: *"Some of those boxes are older than my grandmother, and some of them bite."* Sefa had taken this as encouragement. She spent four hours in the storage gallery off the reading room, working through crates marked in a dead clerk's hand, until she found the one labeled simply: **WELL GATE — ACQUISITIONS, MISCELLANEOUS**.

Inside: forty years of receipts, a dried bouquet of funeral roses gone grey as smoke, three ledgers of dubious provenance, and a bundle of papers tied with wire that had rusted into the knot so thoroughly she had to cut it free strand by strand.

The topmost paper was a bill of sale.

She knew it was before she finished reading the header, because her hands began to shake, and her hands never shook over paper. She pressed them flat against the slate table until the trembling stopped, and then she read it properly, twice, three times, the way she read everything that mattered: once for content, once for structure, once for the spaces between the words.

---

**BILL OF SALE AND CONVEYANCE**

*Be it known to all parties, witnessed under seal, that on this day the undersigned seller conveys unto the undersigned buyer, freely and without coercion, the following goods:*

**One infant, female-presenting, approximately six months of age. Name given: LIOR OYELARAN.**

*Condition: healthy. Marked. Suitable.*

*Consideration received: one (1) item of the class designated IMPOSSIBLE, nature undisclosed per buyer's privilege, receipt held in confidence.*

*Seller's mark:* [a signature Sefa did not recognize, small and hurried, the letters leaning away from each other like people avoiding one another's eyes]

*Buyer's mark:* [a seal she recognized instantly, and wished she did not]

---

The seal was a mountain drawn in negative space—a peak rendered by the absence of ink around it, so that the paper itself seemed to rise up white against the black wax. Beneath it, in a hand so fine it looked engraved: *For the Court Beneath the Mountain, by the hand of its Steward of Acquisitions.*

Sefa sat very still for a long time.

The lamp hissed. Somewhere overhead, muffled through floors of stone and timber, the Gilded Ledger went about its midnight business—doors opening and closing, footsteps, the soft clink of trades being made. Bottled grief changing hands. Borrowed years. Names sold twice. She had watched it all evening with scholarly detachment, cataloguing the guild's commerce the way she'd catalogue any corrupt institution: interesting, damning, survivable.

Now she understood that she had been watching the retail operation.

Because here was the thing—the thing that made her press her palms flat again, that made the lamp's honey-smell turn cloying in her throat. Bills of sale for infants existed. They were monstrous, but they existed; poverty and war produced them in every century, and archives were full of them, and every archivist she respected had learned to carry that weight without setting it down. But those bills sold children to households. To farms. To temples, sometimes, which was its own quiet horror. They sold children *into the world*.

This bill sold a child *out of it*.

*Suitable*, the document said. *Marked.* As if the child had been inspected like a bolt of cloth. As if someone had turned the infant over in the lamplight, checked the stitching, pronounced the goods sound. And the consideration—one impossible thing, nature undisclosed—meant that somewhere in the world, or somewhere under it, a person had been traded for a miracle. A whole life, with all its mornings and hungers and griefs and small unrepeatable joys, weighed against a single wonder, and the wonder had won.

And the name. The name was the worst of it, though she couldn't have said why at first, and then she could: because the name was *given*. *Name given: Lior Oyelaran.* Someone had named that baby. Someone had held a six-month-old child and chosen sounds for her, sounds meant to be called across a room, sung over a cradle, spoken at a wedding—and then had written those sounds on a line between *goods* and *condition: healthy*, and signed below, and walked away.

Sefa thought of Lior Oyelaran standing in the guildmaster's office, spine straight as a ledger rule, saying *I am difficult to surprise* in a voice that had never once been surprised because it had never once been allowed to expect anything. She thought of the meticulous invisibility of them, the way they moved through the guild like a figure in someone else's dream. She had assumed temperament. Training. The armor that careful people forge for themselves.

She looked down at the bill of sale and understood, with a scholar's cold certainty, that it was neither.

*They were priced before they were a person,* she thought. *Of course they grew up feeling like an entry in a book. They've been one since before they could walk.*

* * *

She worked until the lamps burned low, because grief was no excuse for sloppy method and she had a lifetime of using work as a raft.

The seller's signature she traced and re-traced until she could reproduce it blind. Small, hurried, letters leaning apart. She cross-checked it against every sample in the crate, then against the guild's own historical rolls, which Ombre had grudgingly unlocked for her. No match. Whoever had sold Lior Oyelaran had not been guild. An outsider. A private party—which meant the sale had come *to* the guild, or past it, or through some door in it she hadn't found yet.

The buyer's side was worse. The Court Beneath the Mountain was a story told to frighten children and entertain travelers: a city of glass roots and candle-lit nobility who did not age, tucked in the hollow places under the world. Every culture within five hundred miles had a version of it. Sefa had catalogued eleven variants herself and dismissed all of them as folklore wearing each other's clothes.

But folklore did not sign bills of sale. Folklore did not have a *Steward of Acquisitions* with an engraved hand and a wax seal that matched, exactly, the seal pressed into the codex's colophon—the same seal she now realized appeared on the codex's final page, small and unobtrusive, the way a watermark appears on currency. The book and the child had come from the same house. The prophecy naming Lior Oyelaran and the sale of Lior Oyelaran were, in the most literal archival sense, *related documents*.

Which raised the question that made her stop humming.

Who writes a prophecy about a child you have just purchased? You do not buy a cow and then commission a poem about how the cow will one day return to the barn. Unless—

Unless the purchase was not the beginning. Unless the sale was itself a step in something longer, a transaction planned across decades, the infant acquired *because* of what the book said. Unless the codex was not a record of the future at all, but an invoice. A schedule. A century-old promise to collect.

*Centennial*, whispered some patient corner of her mind, the corner that had unraveled the dead chancellor. *A hundred years. The book is ninety-eight years old. Do the arithmetic, Sefa. Do it slowly, and then be afraid.*

Ninety-eight years. Two years short. Tonight's date falling inside the window like a coin into a slot.

She wrote none of this down. Some conclusions you keep in your body, where no one can steal them.

* * *

Near dawn, she found the last document, and it was the smallest.

Folded inside the wire-cut bundle, stuck to the back of the bill of sale by a century of pressure, was a scrap of paper no bigger than her palm. A fragment, torn from something larger. On it, in the same small hurrying hand as the seller's signature, two lines of text survived:

*—forgive me, little one. The fire keeps them, and they keep—*

And then the tear took the rest.

Sefa held the scrap under the loupe for a long time, reading the fibers, the ink's bleed pattern, the direction of the tear. Written in haste. Written by someone whose hand shook—not with age; with feeling. Written by the seller, almost certainly, the same person who had signed the bill and leaned their letters away from each other like a person who could not bear to look at what they'd done.

*Forgive me, little one.*

Not a merchant's note. A parent's.

Sefa set the scrap down with hands that had begun, again, to shake, and this time she let them. She thought: *someone sold their own child to a court out of a story, and begged forgiveness on the wrapping, and whatever "the fire" is, it was worth more to them than their baby's whole life—or they believed it was, which may be the same thing or may be infinitely worse.* She thought: *and the court paid them in an impossible thing, and I would give a great deal to know whether they ever got to spend it.*

The lamp guttered. Above her, the guild was going quiet, the night's trades done, the impossible things locked back into their cases. Somewhere up there, Lior Oyelaran was spending tonight's date awake or asleep, carrying a name they'd found in a dead book, not knowing—they could not know—that they had been a line item before they were a person. That somewhere in the dark under the world, a receipt with their name on it had been waiting a hundred years for collection.

Sefa blew out the lamps. In the dark, the codex on its slate slab seemed almost to breathe.

She gathered her notes, tucked the bill of sale and the torn scrap into the oilcloth wallet against her ribs, and climbed the stairs toward the guildmaster's office, moving fast and quiet, an archivist with a chain of evidence and no idea yet how far down it went.

She was halfway there when she heard, from behind the sealed door of the guild's tithe-vault, the unmistakable sound of a lock being picked—and knew, with the particular dread of a woman who has just read a bill of sale, exactly whose hands were on the picks, and exactly whose name was going to be waiting inside.
