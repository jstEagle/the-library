## 5. The Silent God's Tithe

Sleep did not come to the Gilded Ledger that night. It came to everyone else—Ombre behind her door of black oak, the tally-clerks in their narrow cots above the counting room, even the cat that kept the cellar free of debt-rats—but not to me. I lay in my room on the third floor with the codex's words printed on the inside of my eyelids, and when I closed my eyes I read them again: *Lior Oyelaran, under tonight's date, a hundred years before tonight.*

A name is a small thing. I had spent my whole life making mine smaller. Lior to the clerks, *the Oyelaran girl* to the clients who bothered to learn it at all, *you* to Ombre in the rare soft moments when she forgot herself and spoke like family. A name kept small does not snag on prophecy. A name kept small can slip through a century without leaving a wrinkle.

Except it hadn't. Except someone, a hundred years ago, had written me down like an entry in a book, and entries in books are things that get bought, sold, called due.

I got up. I dressed in the dark by habit, which is its own kind of prayer—the god Veth appreciates precision—and I stood a long moment at my window watching the lamplighters work their way down Vellum Street, each flame a small surrender to the dark. Nine years ago, Veth had stopped answering me. Not gradually. Not fading, the way a fire fades, apologetic in its going. One night the god's presence had been there, that dry papery attention at the back of my skull, like a clerk reading over your shoulder, and the next night it was gone, and every night since had been silence.

I had told myself all the comfortable stories. The god was testing me. The god was busy; gods have ledgers of their own. The god had never been there at all and the first years of my devotion had been a child's imagination wearing religion like a borrowed coat.

But I was twenty-six years old now, and I had spent nine years keeping accounts for impossible things, and I knew what a falsified entry looked like. I knew the smell of a book that has been made to lie.

And tonight I had seen my own name in a book that should not have known I existed.

There was one place in the Gilded Ledger where questions went to be buried alive. Every guild has its sealed room. Ours was beneath the counting floor, behind a door that three different locksmiths had built and then, each of them, asked to have their names struck from the guild rolls. We called it the Tithe Vault, and we called it that in the flat voice you use for things you've agreed not to think about.

Because the Gilded Ledger traded in impossible things, and some of those things were owed upward.

* * *

The counting room at midnight was a landscape of pale shapes—desks like sleeping animals, the great brass scale in the corner catching a coin of moonlight through the high window. I moved through it the way I moved through everything: quietly, precisely, as though invisibility were simply good bookkeeping. My keys were legitimate. That was the joke of it, the ugly little joke I only admitted to myself as I knelt at the trapdoor behind the master scale: I had access to the Tithe Vault. Every senior ledger-keeper did. We were trusted with the key the way you trust a person not to look directly at the sun.

The lock took my key and then took something else—I felt it, faintly, a tug behind my sternum, the door tasting me, confirming I was the same person who had opened it last. Doors in this city learned your soul the way dogs learned your smell. I wondered, with a cold new attention, how much of mine it found to taste.

Stairs went down into air that smelled of candle smoke very old, tallow burned past remembering, and under that something sweeter and worse: the smell of preserved feeling. Anyone who has worked the trade knows it. Bottled grief has a scent like rain on hot stone. Bottled joy smells, horribly, of bread. The vault smelled of both, and of years.

I lit no lamp. There was light down there anyway—a gray, sourceless glow, the kind that lives in cellars and churches and other places where things are stored rather than used. It showed me shelves. Row upon row of them, receding into the dark like the ribs of some enormous patient animal, and on the shelves, jars.

Nine rows deep. I counted them the way I count everything, because counting is the wall I build between myself and the world, and this time the numbers did not protect me.

One jar per year. Each labeled in Ombre's hand, in her careful merchant's script that I had learned my letters from, that I knew better than my own handwriting: the year, the tithe's nature, the intended recipient.

*To Veth, the Silent God. In payment of the Guild's founding covenant.*

Year one: a memory, sealed in amber glass, still faintly warm. Year two: a first breath, taken from a dying founder and offered whole. Year three: grief, bottled, the label noting it had been harvested willingly from a widow who wished her sorrow to serve a purpose. Year four. Year five. Year six. I walked the row slowly, reading each label like scripture, because that is what it was—this was our rent for existing, our proof that we understood the difference between trading in impossible things and *being* impossible things.

Seven. Eight. Nine.

Nine jars, and every one of them unbroken, unopened, dusted but untouched. Nine years of offerings, set out on the shelf like meals left at a grave, and never collected.

My hand rose toward the ninth jar and stopped. Because here was the thing I had never let myself think in nine years of silence: offerings do not go stale. Devotion is not milk. If the god had wanted them, they would have been gone. Which meant either the god could not take them—

—or would not.

Or could not *reach* them.

I stood in that gray light with my hand hovering over a jar of someone's harvested sorrow and I thought about the word *silent*. How I had always heard it as refusal. Silence as a door closed in the face. But silence has more than one grammar. There is the silence of the one who will not speak, and there is the silence of the one who has been *muzzled*, and there is the silence of the one standing in a burning building too far away to shout, and I had never once, in nine years, considered that the failure might be in the delivery. That the letters might have been written and lost. That the prayers might have piled up somewhere, unread, like correspondence to a house where no one can get to the door.

The guild sent these. Someone carried them down here, shelved them, dusted them. And no one—not once—had asked why the shelf stayed full.

We were a guild of accountants. We noticed everything except the debts owed to us.

* * *

It was on the tenth row—the row that shouldn't exist—that I stopped being angry and started being afraid.

The first nine rows were tithes. Offerings, upward, in good faith. The tenth row held contracts.

They hung from hooks like cured meat, scrolls in oilcloth, each tagged with a lead seal. Founding documents. The oldest covenants of the Gilded Ledger, the ones Ombre referred to the way you refer to a dead parent: with reverence, and never in detail. I had assumed I'd find the guild's original charter here, the terms under which we were permitted to traffic in bottled grief and borrowed years. I had come down here, if I was honest with myself—which I am paid to be, so I occasionally manage it—to read the founding covenant and learn what exactly we owed a silent god, and whether the silence was breach or forgiveness.

I found the charter third hook from the left. I did not read it. I never got to it.

Because two hooks down hung a contract with my name on it.

Not *Lior*. Not the small name, the worn-smooth name I had sanded down over twenty-six years until nothing snagged on it. The full one, inked in a formal hand across the top of the oilcloth tag: *Oyelaran, Lior — instrument of covenant — see rider.*

Instrument. Of covenant.

I took it down. The lead seal was intact, which meant whatever was inside had never been executed—or had been executed and resealed, which is a different crime entirely. My fingers, which do not shake over ledgers, which have not shaken since I was eleven years old and Ombre taught me that a trembling hand writes false numbers, shook now. I broke the seal. The sound it made was very small and very loud, a click like a lock turning in a room where I had believed there were no locks.

The contract inside was written in the old style, the dense legal liturgy the guild abandoned forty years ago because it was, according to Ombre, "inhospitable." Reading it was like wading upstream through cold water.

*Be it known that the party herein named, being of age six years, having no kin of record and no claimant of record, is bound into service of the covenant between the Gilded Ledger and the god Veth, said service to consist of the keeping of accounts, the witnessing of trades, and the maintenance of the Ledger's good faith, for the term of the party's natural life.*

Six years old. They had drafted it when I was six. Before I could write my own name properly, someone had written it here, and bound my whole life into a document, and filed it in the dark.

But it was the rider that took the floor out from under me a second time, the way the codex had, the way the world apparently intended to keep doing until I learned to walk on air.

*In consideration of said service, the covenant grants the party the use of faculties sufficient to the work: attention, memory, precision, and the quieting of excess feeling, that grief and joy alike may not disturb the figures.*

*The quieting of excess feeling.*

I read it four times. The gray cellar light did not change. The jars on their shelves did not shift. Somewhere above me the guild slept on, Ombre slept on, and I stood holding the contract that explained my own heart.

All my life people had called me cold. Kindly, mostly—Ombre called it *composure*, clients called it *professionalism*, Sefa earlier tonight had looked at me across the authentication table with something like pity and said, *You don't feel things the way other people do, do you?* And I had said, *No,* the way you confirm a measurement, because it was true, because I had built my entire self on the truth of it: I was a person made of precision, of clean lines, of numbers that behaved. I had thought it was temperament. I had thought it was the shape my soul happened to come in, the way some people are tall.

It was a clause. It was a *provision*, granted "in consideration of service," like a uniform issued with the job. Someone had reached into a six-year-old and turned down the lamp of her feeling so that she would make a tidier instrument, and then they had filed the receipt in a cellar and let her grow up believing the dimness was her own face.

I want to tell you I raged. What I actually did was worse. I checked the arithmetic.

That is what nine years in the Gilded Ledger does to a person. Even gutted, even standing in a vault full of unanswered prayers with my own life's bill of sale in my hands, I checked the arithmetic. The clause said *quieting*, not removal. Quiet, not absence. So the feelings were in there. Everything I had ever failed to feel—the griefs that slid off me, the joys that arrived secondhand, the strange flatness at funerals and weddings both—they weren't missing. They were *withheld*. Accrued. Sitting somewhere in the dark of me like undelivered mail, compounding interest, nine years of Veth's silence and twenty years of my own flattened heart stacked up in an account I had never known I held.

Rage came late, and when it came, it came quietly, which is the most dangerous way rage arrives in a person like me. It did not burn. It *balanced*. I looked at the ninth jar of uncollected tithe and the contract with my childhood in it and the codex waiting upstairs with my name under tonight's date, and I did the sums, and the sums came out wrong in every direction, and wrongness in a ledger is not despair.

Wrongness in a ledger is a summons.

Someone had cooked these books. Someone had muzzled a god and dimmed a child and filed the evidence in the one room where no one looks, and they had done it inside the only family I had, under the signature of the woman who taught me my letters. Ombre had sent Sefa to authenticate that codex. Ombre had watched me read my own name tonight with an expression I had catalogued, in my meticulous way, as *concern*. I had believed her. I had believed her for twenty years, because believing her was cheaper than auditing her.

No longer. I wrapped the contract around my forearm the way a courier wraps dispatches, and I took the ninth jar—the last tithe, the one marked for this year, still warm through the glass as though freshly poured—and I climbed the stairs out of the vault of my own stolen life, and I locked the door behind me with a hand that had gone perfectly, dangerously steady.

Above me, behind black oak, Ombre slept. Or did not sleep. It didn't matter which. By morning I would know which side of the ledger she stood on, and God help her—God help us both, the silent one and the silenced—if the answer was the wrong column.

I blew out my lamp and began, softly, to plan.
