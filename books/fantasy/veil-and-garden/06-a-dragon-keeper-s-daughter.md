## 6. A Dragon-Keeper's Daughter

Nim Ashvael had learned to measure time in embers.

Not years—years were a thing for people whose lives did not depend on the color of a dying fire. She measured in shades: the deep gold of Emberless's prime, which her mother described to her in stories like a woman describing a lover; the copper of Nim's own childhood, when the dragon's breath still warmed the whole of the Ashvael hold and the snow on the roof melted by midmorning; the dull orange of her adolescence, when they had begun banking the fire at night and waking to frost on the inside of the windows.

And now this. Now the color of old wine held up to a dying sun. Now the color of almost-gone.

She stood on the guild steps with her hood up and her hands buried in her sleeves, because her hands were always cold now, and watched the doors of the Gilded Ledger swallow merchants and spit them out again. The building was exactly as ugly as her mother had promised: all gilt and grimness, a facade like a coin bitten too many times. Above the doors hung the guild's sign—a set of balanced scales wrought in gold so old it had gone green—and beneath the scales, in letters that caught the lamplight, their motto: *All Things May Be Priced.*

Her mother had spat when she'd said it aloud. Nim only thought: *I hope you're right.*

Because she had come to sell something. And what she had come to sell was the last thing in the world she owned that was worth anything at all.

* * *

The doorman looked at her the way doormen are trained to look at people: as though weighing her for resale. "Business?"

"Guild business." She pulled the letter from inside her coat. It was written on dragon-hide vellum, which was not a flex but a necessity—paper burned too fast near Emberless, even now, even sleeping—and it bore her mother's seal, pressed in wax mixed with ash from the fire-pit itself. The doorman read the seal and his eyebrows climbed. Whatever he saw there, he stopped seeing her as a beggar in a good coat.

"Wait here."

She waited. The street smelled of rain coming and chestnuts roasting and, beneath both, the faint mineral tang of the mountain—the mountain she had spent her whole life looking at from below without ever once wondering what was underneath it. That was about to change. Everything was about to change, or nothing was, and either way the waiting made her teeth ache.

When the doorman returned, he brought with him a person made entirely of edges and silence. Small, dark-skinned, dressed in gray so precise it looked poured rather than sewn. Their eyes went over Nim the way an appraiser's eyes go over a stone—not unkindly, but completely.

"Nim Ashvael," the person said. Not a question. "The keeper's daughter."

"The keeper," Nim said. "My mother is ill. I keep the fire now."

Something moved behind those eyes—too quick to name, gone before it arrived. "Lior Oyelaran. Accounts receivable." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "You've come to make a purchase?"

"A sale." Nim's mouth was dry. "A very large one."

"Everything is large until we price it." Lior turned toward the doors. "Come in out of the rain. The guild dislikes wet merchandise."

"I'm not merchandise."

"No," Lior said, and opened the door, and did not look back to see whether Nim followed. "That's usually the first thing people say."

* * *

Inside, the Gilded Ledger was less a building than a throat.

Nim had expected vaults. She got corridors instead—long ones, lit by lamps that burned without oil, lined with shelves that climbed past sight into a brown-gold gloom. And on the shelves: everything. Jars of every glass and clay and crystal, each labeled in a hand so fine it might have been etched by needle. Bottled grief, one shelf announced, sorted by vintage. Borrowed years, said another, interest accruing. Names not yet spoken, whispered a third, and Nim felt the hair rise on her arms because she could have sworn the jars on that shelf were warm.

"This way," Lior said, and walked without hurrying through the labyrinth as though the shelves parted for them out of respect. Or fear. Nim couldn't decide which unsettled her more.

They passed a counter where a fat merchant was weeping quietly while a clerk decanted something silver from his cupped hands into a jar. He kept saying *thank you*, thank you, thank you, in the voice of a man thanking a surgeon for a limb he hadn't known he could live without. Nim looked away. Lior didn't look away at all. Lior watched with the still, level attention of someone keeping count.

"You don't approve," Lior observed, without turning.

"Do you?"

"I approve of accurate records." They stopped before a door of black wood banded in iron, produced a key from somewhere in their sleeve, and unlocked it. "Approval is not my department."

"And what is your department?"

Lior held the door open. Beyond it lay a room with a single table, two chairs, and a lamp turned low—the kind of room built for conversations that would never be admitted to having happened.

"Pricing," Lior said. "Sit down, Keeper's daughter. Tell me what you're selling."

* * *

Nim told it plainly, because her mother had taught her that plain speech was the only armor worth wearing into a merchant's hall.

"There's a dragon under the Ashvael hold. Emberless. Last of her line—last of any line, if the archivists are right, and my mother says they're right, which is how I know it's bad." Her hands wanted to shake, so she flattened them on the table. "She's dying. Not sick—dying the way a candle dies, the way a river dies in drought. Slow, and then all at once. When I was born her fire could melt stone. This morning I fed her a cord of heartoak—heartoak, which burns underwater—and she took three days of warmth from it. Three days. Ten years ago it would have been a season."

Lior listened without interrupting. It was, Nim would think later, the most unnerving quality she had ever encountered in another person: the total, patient attention of someone who intended to remember every word.

"Why does it matter?" Lior asked. "Forgive me. I mean no disrespect to your dragon. But the world has lost its dragons already, one by one, and the world goes on. Why does this fire matter enough to bring you to us?"

And here Nim hesitated, because here was the part her mother had sworn her to secrecy about, and here was also the part that had driven her down the mountain anyway, because secrecy was a luxury for people whose fires weren't going out.

"Because of what she's burning *for*," Nim said. "It isn't just heat. Emberless's fire doesn't only give warmth. It holds something. My mother called it the banked debt. She said—" Nim heard her own voice drop, the way voices drop in churches and sickrooms, "—she said the fire keeps a door shut. Under the mountain. And that when the fire goes out, the door opens, and what's behind it comes out hungry."

Silence. The lamp flame stood perfectly still, as though listening.

"That's a story," Lior said at last. But their voice had changed by a degree so small that anyone who wasn't raised reading the moods of a dying fire would have missed it. "Keepers tell stories. It keeps the loneliness bearable."

"My mother doesn't tell stories." Nim leaned forward. "My mother hasn't left the hold in thirty years. She sleeps in the fire-chamber. She gave the guild her wedding ring when I was nine, do you know why? To buy a single jar of *concentrated flame*—the bottled fire you people trade in—to feed Emberless through the worst winter. She paid a fortune for one jar. And when it was delivered, the courier wouldn't say where the guild sourced it, and my mother opened it, and she went white as bone, and she burned the packing paper and never spoke of it again. But I found the ash. And I found the label fragment. And it wasn't ordinary flame, Master Oyelaran. It was *dragonfire*. Someone sold my mother dragonfire, and there is only one dragon left in the world, and she is sleeping in my cellar."

For the first time since Nim had met them, Lior Oyelaran moved as though the movement cost something. A small shift backward. A blink held a half-second too long.

"You're accusing the Gilded Ledger," they said slowly, "of harvesting fuel from the very creature you're trying to save."

"I'm telling you somebody is. And whoever it is knows what the fire does. Knows what's behind the door. And is selling it off piece by piece anyway." Nim's voice cracked and she hated it and pushed through anyway. "So yes. I came to the guild. Because you trade in impossible things, and I need to buy the most impossible one there is. Enough fire to keep her burning. However much it costs. However long it takes. I will pay anything."

"Anything," Lior repeated. It was not mockery. It was arithmetic. Nim watched them do it behind their eyes—the terrible, gentle math of a professional hearing the word *anything* and translating it into terms.

"Anything within my power to pay," Nim amended, because her mother had taught her that too: never sign what you haven't read.

"Then let us find out what's in stock." Lior rose. "There is a vault of tithes—offerings owed to a god, held in trust. Among them, historically, certain quantities of preserved flame. If any legitimate source of dragonfire exists in this city, it will be catalogued there." A pause at the door, hand on the iron band. "Keeper's daughter. One question first. Answer honestly, because I will check the answer against other answers, and lies compound."

"Ask."

"If the price of saving your dragon is higher than you can pay—if it comes due in a currency you don't have—what then? Do you walk away? Do you bargain? Do you steal?" Those dark eyes held hers across the little lamp-lit room. "What kind of debtor are you?"

Nim thought of her mother's hands, cracked and blackened, feeding the fire. Thought of the frost on the inside of the windows. Thought of the door under the mountain, and the hunger behind it, and the ten thousand small mornings she had woken and gone down into the warm dark and said *good morning, old girl* to a creature older than every kingdom she'd learned about in school.

"I don't walk away," she said. "I've been watching something die my whole life. I know exactly how slow it is. And I know that slow is not the same as safe. So—I bargain. And if bargaining fails, I steal. And if stealing fails—" she shrugged, and was surprised to find her eyes hot, "—then I put my own hands in the fire and burn as long as I burn. Somebody should be warm at the end. Even if it's nobody."

Lior Oyelaran looked at her for a long moment. Something passed over their face that Nim would spend a long time, later, trying to name—recognition, maybe, or grief, or the memory of grief. Then they opened the door.

"Come with me," they said. "And keep your voice down past this point. What I'm about to show you, the guildmaster doesn't know I'm showing anyone."

* * *

The corridor down was older than the rest of the guild. Nim felt it in the walls—in the way the stones stopped being fitted and started being *grown*, veined with something pale and translucent that caught the lamplight like frost catching sun. Glass, maybe. Or glass's older cousin. It hummed faintly under her palm when she touched it, a note too low to hear properly, felt more in the teeth than the ear.

"What is this stuff?" she whispered.

"I don't know," Lior said. It did not sound like ignorance. It sounded like a wound being stepped around. "It runs under the mountain. The guild was built on top of it. Most people never notice."

*I noticed,* Nim almost said. *Anyone raised beside a dying fire notices when stone starts singing.* But the corridor had begun to slope steeply downward, and the air had changed—cooler, then warmer, then cool again, cycling like breath—and she saved her questions.

The vault door was not locked. That frightened her more than any lock would have. It simply stood ajar at the bottom of the stairs, black and enormous, exhaling a smell she knew in her bones: hot metal, old smoke, and beneath it, impossibly, the dry sweet scent of a dragon's sleep.

Lior lifted the lamp. The light fell into the vault and Nim's heart rose and broke in the same instant, because the shelves were full—full of jars, hundreds of them, thousands, each glowing softly with its own captive color. Gold and copper and wine-dark ember-red. An entire hoard of fire, enough to warm Emberless for a century, enough to—

Lior raised one jar to the light and turned it slowly, reading the label. And Nim watched the color drain from their face by degrees, the way color drains from a cooling coal.

"What," Nim breathed. "What is it?"

Lior didn't answer at once. They set the jar down with a care that bordered on ritual, and their hand, when they withdrew it, was not quite steady.

"These aren't tithes," they said quietly. "These aren't offerings to Veth. These are *withdrawals*. Dated. Signed. Nine years of them, drawn against a single account." They looked up, and their eyes had gone somewhere far away and very cold. "Someone has been draining your dragon's fire for nine years, Keeper's daughter. Selling it through this guild. And the account they're drawing against—" they touched the ledger chained to the shelf-end, open to a page filled with entries in a hand Nim recognized, because she had seen it that very hour, neat and gray and meticulous, "—is mine."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere above them, muffled by stone and distance, a bell began to ring the midnight hour, and Nim thought—with a certainty that settled into her like a swallowed stone—that the fire under her mother's mountain had just burned a little lower, and that neither of them had any idea yet how much everything was about to cost.
