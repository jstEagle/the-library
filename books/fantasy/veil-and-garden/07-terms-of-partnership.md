## 7. Terms of Partnership

The Gilded Ledger kept a room for negotiations that had no windows. Ombre called it the Quiet Room, and it was fitted with velvet so deep it swallowed footsteps, and a table of black walnut polished to the sheen of still water, and three chairs—one for each party, and one for the trade itself, because the guild believed every bargain was a third thing, alive, that sat between people and listened.

Lior had sat in that room four hundred times. They knew the exact pressure of silence required before naming a price. They knew how long to let a client sweat, how to make the pause feel like arithmetic rather than cruelty.

Tonight they needed none of it. Nim Ashvael had already named her price by walking through the door.

She stood across the black table with her arms crossed and her boots leaving crescents of road-mud on the carpet, and she looked like someone who had been arguing with the world since dawn and intended to win. Her hair was bound back with cord, singed at the ends—Lior noted that automatically, the way they noted everything, the ledger-mind turning without being asked—and her hands were mapped with old burns in the shape of talons.

"You want passage beneath the mountain," she said. "I want fuel for a fire that's dying. So let's find out which of us is more desperate."

"Desperation is a currency," Lior said. "It inflates fast. I'd rather we deal in facts."

"Facts." Nim laughed once, short and unamused. "Fine. Fact: Emberless has maybe nine days of burn left in her. Fact: what feeds her isn't coal or wood or any damn thing that grows. She eats memory. Given freely. And there is nowhere in this city with enough freely given memory to matter except—" she tapped the table with one burned finger, "—your vault."

Lior did not let anything move in their face. Nine years of practice at being unreadable made it easy. But beneath the stillness, something turned over—the way a page turns when you're not touching it.

"You know about our vault," they said.

"I know about your *tithes*. Every offering this guild ever took for its silent god. Bottled years. Grief in glass. Names." Her eyes were the color of banked coals, amber going gray at the rim, and they did not blink often. "A dragon can smell preserved time from a mountain away. I've smelled yours since I crossed the river."

*Since I crossed the river.* Which meant she had come here first, deliberately, before trying anywhere else—or after everywhere else had failed. Lior filed both possibilities and found they disliked the second one more, because it meant she was out of options, and desperate people signed badly, and Lior could not afford a partner who signed badly.

Not a partner, they corrected themselves. A guide. Nothing owed either direction beyond the terms.

"The tithes are consecrated," Lior said. "They belong to Veth. The guild holds them in trust. We don't sell trust."

"I'm not asking you to sell it. I'm asking you to *spend* it." Nim leaned forward, palms flat on the black wood, and the candlelight caught the fine scars across her knuckles. "Your god hasn't answered a prayer in nine years. Everyone knows. Half the city prays to Veth out of habit and half out of spite, and the god sits there like a stone in a shrine. What use is a hoard to a silent god? Feed it to my dragon instead. Let one act of devotion actually *do* something."

The words landed harder than she could have known. *What use is a hoard to a silent god.* Lior had asked a version of that question every night for nine years, kneeling in their narrow room with their hands open and empty, saying *Veth, I have kept the accounts, I have kept them clean, say anything, say one word,* and the silence had answered only with more silence, patient as interest accruing.

"That is blasphemy," Lior said quietly.

"It's bookkeeping," said Nim Ashvael. "You of all people should appreciate the difference."

For a moment—half a breath—the corner of Lior's mouth threatened to move. They killed it. They had killed such things for so long that the killing was automatic, a clerk's motion, stamping the impulse *denied* and filing it where no one would read it.

"Here are my terms," Lior said, and heard their own voice settle into the register clients called *the cold count*, though Lior thought of it as simply careful. "The guild releases a portion of the tithe-hoard—not all—to feed your dragon. In exchange, you carry me and two others through the passes to the Court Beneath the Mountain, by whatever road a dragon-keeper's daughter knows that soldiers and customs men don't. You protect the cargo. The cargo is us."

"And if the court kills you?"

"Then the contract voids and the remaining tithe reverts to the shrine. Standard clause."

Nim stared at them. "You're negotiating your own funeral terms like it's a grain shipment."

"Everything is a shipment," Lior said. "Some things are just heavier than grain."

Something shifted in the dragon-keeper's face then—not softening exactly, but recognition, the look of one stubborn creature meeting another across a fence line. She pulled out the chair opposite and sat, hard, mud and all, and put her burned hands on the table between them like a gambler showing empty palms.

"One condition of mine," she said. "You don't treat Emberless like an asset. She's older than your guild, older than your city, older than the word *guild*. She's dying, and she's dying *on purpose*, holding a door shut with the last of herself, and if you go down that mountain thinking of her as transport, I will leave you on a glacier to reconsider."

"A door shut against what?"

Nim's jaw worked. For the first time since entering, she looked away, toward the velvet wall as if it might open onto somewhere better.

"I don't know," she admitted, and the admission cost her visibly, like a coin pried from a fist. "My mother knew. Her mother knew. It's passed keeper to keeper, the *why*, but mine died before she finished teaching me. All I have is the duty and the fire and the rule: *it must not go out.*" She looked back up, and her coal-colored eyes were wet at the edges and furious about it. "So no, mercenary, I don't know what's under that mountain. I only know that whatever it is, it's been waiting a hundred years for my dragon to blink. And now your name's in some dead prophet's book tied to the same date, and you want to walk straight into it. Which tells me the waiting might be over."

The candles guttered, all three at once, though there was no draft. Lior noticed. Lior noticed everything, and hated the noticing sometimes, the way a man with perfect hearing hates a ticking clock.

"Then we're agreed on terms," Lior said. "I'll draw the contract tonight. You'll have your fuel by morning."

"We're agreed," Nim said, "if your guildmaster agrees. That's how families work. Somebody always has to sign."

*Families.* The word went through Lior like a splinter finding old wood. They rose, gathered the blank contract paper, and did not answer.

* * *

Ombre was in her office above the counting floor, and she was not alone with her ledgers, which was itself an omen. She stood at the window looking down at the lamplit street, and she had heard the door open and had not turned around, which meant she already knew why Lior had come, which meant Sefa had talked, or the walls had, or the prophecy had traveled ahead of them the way bad news does, on feet of its own.

"You've hired the Ashvael girl," Ombre said.

"I've contracted her. Provisionally. Pending your seal."

"Unseal it."

The word dropped into the room like a coin into a well. Lior stood in the doorway and waited for the rest, because Ombre never spent one word when ten would do, and the silence stretched, and Lior counted it the way they counted everything—three heartbeats, five, seven—and on the ninth heartbeat Ombre finally turned.

Guildmaster Ombre Vess was sixty-one years old and had run the Gilded Ledger for thirty of them, and she had taught Lior letters on her own knee with a stick of charcoal on the back of an invoice, and she had never once, in nineteen years, told Lior what to do outside the bounds of trade. She was not tall. She was not warm. She wore grief the way other women wore shawls, draped and practical, and everyone in the guild knew better than to ask what she mourned.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard the terms."

"I don't need the terms. The answer predates the terms." She came around the desk, and up close Lior could see that her hands were trembling, very slightly, the tremor of someone gripping something invisible very hard. "You are not going under that mountain. Not with the Ashvael girl, not with the archivist, not with a legion and a blessing. I forbid it as guildmaster, and I'll invoke the old clauses if I must. Your contract binds you to the Ledger's interests, and the Ledger's interest is that you stay alive and stay *here*."

"My name is in a century-old book under tomorrow's date," Lior said. Each word laid down flat and even, like coins in a row. "Staying here doesn't keep me safe. Staying here just means it finds me in a place I love."

"*If* the book means anything. Old prophecies are wrong more often than dice. Sefa will tell you—"

"Sefa traced the provenance. The codex is genuine. And there's more." Lior hesitated, and hated the hesitation, and pushed through it anyway. "There's a bill of sale. An infant. Sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain. The name on it is mine."

He watched it land. He watched Ombre Vess, who had faced down debt-lords and plague-years and the winter the river froze solid, go pale in a specific order—lips first, then cheeks, then the steady brown of her eyes going glassy—and he watched her hand find the edge of the desk behind her and hold on.

"How long," Lior said. Their voice came out lower than intended. "How long have you known."

"Lior—"

"*How long.*"

The counting floor murmured below them, pens scratching, clerks calling figures, the whole honest machinery of dishonest trade humming on, indifferent. Above it, in the small office that smelled of ink and lavender and thirty years of Ombre's pipe smoke, the guildmaster of the Gilded Ledger closed her eyes.

"Eleven years," she said. "I've known for eleven years."

The number went through Lior like a blade slipped between ribs—clean, precise, angled to miss nothing vital, because whoever wielded it had wanted the wound but not the death. Eleven years. Lior had been nineteen. Lior had been a year into service, still sleeping in the dormitory, still flinching at loud voices, still praying every night to a god who hadn't yet gone quiet.

"You bought me," Lior said. "Out of the well gate market. You bought me and you put me to work and you never told me I'd already been sold once before."

"I *saved* you—"

"You *purchased* me. There's a difference, and I keep accounts, so I would know." The cold count was cracking now, and Lior could hear it crack, could hear the evenness fraying thread by thread, and could not stop it. "Was I an investment? Was I a kindness? Did you ever once intend to tell me what I am? Or were you going to let me die ignorant under tomorrow's date, tidy and convenient, one less liability on your books?"

"Do not," Ombre said, and her voice broke on the second word, actually broke, a sound Lior had never heard from her in nineteen years, "do not stand in my office and tell me what I felt when I carried you out of that market with a fever and a name tag and nothing else in the world. Do not. You were a *child*, and I was a woman who had buried everyone she loved, and I bought you because the alternative was watching someone else buy you, and I have regretted it every single day since—not the buying, Lior. The *silence*. There's a difference, and I raised you, so I would know."

The room rang with it. Below, a clerk laughed at something. Somewhere a pen scratched on.

Lior stood very still and did the thing they were best at, which was to keep standing when standing was impossible. But inside, in the vaulted dark where they kept everything, something was coming loose—a wall they had built stone by stone across nineteen years, mortar of routine, stone of competence—and behind the wall was not rage, exactly. Rage would have been simpler. Behind the wall was the smaller, older wound: *she knew. All those dinners. All those lessons. All those nights she checked my lamp was out. She knew, and she chose the silence, and the silence is the one thing I cannot forgive, because I have lived inside it too.*

"I'm resigning," Lior said.

Ombre flinched as if struck. "You can't. The contract—"

"Burn the contract. Keep the indenture fee. Consider it the cost of eleven years of my ignorance, paid in full." Lior set the unsigned partnership papers on the desk, squared their corners with two fingers—an accountant's last gesture, neatness as armor. "I'm going under the mountain, Ombre. With or without your seal. If you invoke the old clauses, I'll fight them, and you know how I fight. I fight like you taught me. Slowly, and with receipts."

"Lior." Her voice was wrecked now, stripped to the timber. "That court doesn't release what it takes. Whatever you think you'll find down there—your soul, your answers, your *self*—you may find only teeth. Come to me first. Talk to me. There are things I can give you, things I've held back for your protection—"

"Protection." Lior almost laughed. It came out as breath, no sound in it at all. "You protected me right up until the prophecy came due. That's not protection, guildmaster. That's deferred payment. And I've learned tonight that everything deferred comes due."

They walked to the door. Their hand was on the latch when Ombre spoke again, and what she said was not a command or a plea but something worse, something that reached back nineteen years and took hold of the child at the charcoal lesson:

"You were the best thing I ever stole," she said. "Go, then. But take the guild seal off the wall on your way down. The old one. The iron one. You'll want it where you're going, and I'd rather you carry it than owe it."

Lior did not turn around. Turning around would have ended in staying, and staying would have ended in dying comfortable, and Lior Oyelaran had decided, sometime in the last hour, in the windowless room with the burned dragon-keeper, that they would rather die *informed*.

They took the iron seal from its hook. It was heavier than it looked, the way true things usually were.

At the bottom of the stairs, Sefa waited with a lantern and a satchel of notes, and beside Sefa, arms crossed, smelling of woodsmoke and high altitude, Nim Ashvael looked up from sharpening a knife that did not need sharpening.

"Well?" Nim said.

"I'm unemployed," Lior said. "Which means I'm available. We leave within the hour, before I change my mind or the guild changes it for me."

Above them, faint through the floorboards, they heard Ombre's office door close—gently, gently, the way one closes a door on a room where something beloved has just been broken.

And far above the city, past the cloud-line, on a shoulder of black rock, something vast and ancient exhaled a thread of fire into the night—and the thread guttered, and shortened, and held.
