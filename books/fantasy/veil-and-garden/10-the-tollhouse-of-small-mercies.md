## 10. The Tollhouse of Small Mercies

The road changed its mind about being a road three miles past the last farmstead.

One moment it was packed earth and wheel-ruts, ordinary as any trade route Lior had walked in nine years of guild errands; the next it was cobbled with pale stone that sweated in the cold, each flag carved—when Lior knelt to look—with a single word in a script older than the Ledger's oldest contract. The words were worn nearly smooth. Travelers had been walking on them for a long time, grinding meaning into nothing.

"Memories," Sefa said behind them. Her voice had gone thin and careful, the way it did when she touched a codex whose binding was human skin. "Roads like this appear on old maps as *pilgrim ways*. The stone remembers what walks over it. That's why it's sweating."

"Sweating," Nim repeated flatly. She had Emberless's ember in a lantern at her hip, and she kept one hand on the lantern's handle the way other people kept hands on knives. The light inside was the color of a hearth an hour after the fire dies—not out, but thinking about it.

"Not sweat." Sefa crouched, not touching. "Condensation. Grief does that."

Lior stood and said nothing. They were good at saying nothing; they had made a profession of it. But their hand had drifted, without permission, to the ledger-slate at their belt—the small wax tablet where they recorded every transaction—and they found themselves wanting, absurdly, to log *road, anomalous, cost unknown* just to have something orderly between them and the mountain growing ahead.

The mountain did not look like a mountain anymore. It looked like a jaw.

* * *

They smelled the tollhouse before they saw it: woodsmoke and beeswax and, underneath both, something like the air inside a locked room where someone has been crying. Then the trees opened onto a clearing, and there it stood—a low building of black timber, roofed in shingles that might once have been slate and might once have been something softer. A lantern hung by the door, unlit, though the windows glowed warm from within. Above the door, burned into the lintel in letters that seemed to rearrange themselves when Lior wasn't looking directly at them:

**THE TOLLHOUSE OF SMALL MERCIES**
*All who pass pay what they can spare. All who pass spare more than they know.*

"Well, that's reassuring," Nim muttered.

"It's honest," Lior said. "That's rarer."

Inside, the tollhouse was smaller than its outside suggested—one room, a counter of dark wood polished by centuries of elbows, shelves rising into shadow and holding, instead of goods, an impossible inventory: glass bells, stoppered vials of colored smoke, keys to doors Lior felt certain existed nowhere, a birdcage containing only silence. Behind the counter sat a figure that took Lior a long moment to resolve into anything at all.

It was old the way rivers are old—not wrinkled but *worn*, features smoothed down to essentials: two eyes the color of tea left steeping too long, a mouth made for neither smiling nor frowning, hands folded on the counter like a book at rest. It wore gray. Everything about it was gray except the eyes.

"Three travelers," it said. Its voice was dry paper turning. "Going down."

"Going through," Lior corrected. "Not stopping."

"No one stops." The keeper unfolded its hands. "That is the tragedy of my establishment. Sit. We will do accounts."

There were three stools. There had been no stools when they entered; Lior was almost sure of it. Nim sat first, because Nim treated every chair as a potential ambush and preferred to claim them early. Sefa sat like a scholar approaching an oral examination, spine straight, hands visible. Lior sat last, and caught themselves arranging their posture the way they arranged a ledger line—balanced, legible, revealing nothing.

The keeper produced three small dishes of white clay, and three brass pins.

"The toll is memory," it said, without preamble, without apology. "Small mercies only. Not your name, not your first love, not the face of your mother. Those are great mercies, and I am not licensed for great mercies." Something flickered across the worn face—pride, or its ghost. "I take what you can live without. You will not always know what you can live without. That is why I ask, rather than take."

"And if we refuse?" Nim asked.

"Then you turn back, and the road remembers you turned back, and the mountain hears of it before you reach the tree line." The keeper's tea-colored eyes moved to her lantern. "And the fire goes out a little faster, I think, for the walking wasted. But refuse if you like. Refusal is also a payment, of a kind. Everyone pays here. Some pay in coin. Most pay worse."

Lior's training rose up in them like cold water. *Name the transaction. Define the terms. Never sign what you haven't read.* "What exactly leaves us?" they asked. "The memory entire? Or a copy?"

"Entire. I deal in originals. Copies are the guild's vice, not mine." The keeper said *guild* without inflection, which somehow made it worse. "You will lose the thing itself. You may keep the shape of having lost something. That shape is what most people mistake for wisdom."

Sefa laughed—a short, startled sound, quickly strangled. "Sorry. Sorry. It's just—that's the truest sentence anyone's spoken to me this year, and I work in an archive."

"What do you want from me?" Nim leaned forward. "Fair warning, most of my memories are either about feeding a dragon or being told I'd fail at feeding a dragon. Take whichever. I'm attached to both, but I'm practical."

"I will show you," the keeper said, "and you will choose. That is the mercy part. The smallness is yours to determine."

It reached beneath the counter and brought up a shallow bowl of water so still it looked like a hole cut in the table. "Touch the water. Your memories will rise. Choose one that floats near the top—near the top means near the surface, easy to lift, easy to survive the lifting. Do not reach deep. Deep things take the arm off at the shoulder, metaphorically speaking, and I have no bandages for that."

Nim went first, because of course she did. She pressed two fingers to the water, frowned, and then her face went soft and complicated.

"There's a woman," she said quietly. "In Vell Hollow. She sold me bread every market day and always gave me the end piece with the extra crust, and never charged for it, and I never learned her name because I was always in a hurry to get back to Emberless." She swallowed. "Take that. It's small. It's the smallest thing I own."

"You'll pass bakers all your life," the keeper said, lifting the memory out of the water—it came up like a bead of amber light balanced on one fingertip, "and never again be given the end piece. Or you'll be given it and feel only confusion, like a word in a language you used to speak."

"I know how it works," Nim said, rougher than she meant to. "I keep a dying dragon alive on scraps. I know how it works."

The keeper set the bead of light into a clay dish, where it dimmed to a coal and went out. Nim blinked twice, shook her head like a dog shedding rain, and stared at the middle distance. "Huh," she said. "I feel like I forgot to bring something somewhere. Is that—is that normal?"

"That is normal," the keeper said gently, and Lior hated how gentle it was.

* * *

Sefa's turn took longer. She hovered over the bowl like a heron over shallow fish, fingers twitching, cataloguing herself.

"This is obscene," she murmured. "Do you understand what you're asking? Memory is provenance. A person without their memories is a codex with the margins scraped. Palimpsest. You're making palimpsests of people."

"Yes," the keeper agreed placidly. "The mountain taught me the trade. I do it smaller, and I tell people first. Between us, that makes me the moral one."

Sefa's laugh came out cracked. She touched the water. What rose in her face was private and painful; Lior watched her lips move around some silent argument, watched her choose, watched her flinch away from one memory and settle on another.

"My grandmother's kitchen," she said at last. "Not her—I keep her. Just the kitchen. The smell of the tallow candles and the way the window stuck and you had to lift it with a knife. I've described it in four different footnotes. It's practically public record anyway." Her voice wobbled on the last word. "Take the kitchen."

The keeper lifted it out. Amber light, clay dish, done. Sefa sat very still afterward, hands flat on the table, breathing in the measured rhythm of someone who has decided not to cry and is treating it as a scholarly exercise.

"Fascinating," she whispered. "I know I wrote those footnotes. I remember writing them. But the referent is—" She stopped. Started again. "The citation remains. The text is gone."

"Now you know," the keeper said, "what it is like to be everyone the mountain has ever touched."

Then the tea-colored eyes turned to Lior, and Lior understood, with the particular dread of an accountant finding the error on their own page, that the whole evening had been arranged around this moment.

* * *

They touched the water.

Their memories rose like silt in reverse—settling upward, sorting themselves into layers Lior had never examined because examining them would have required believing they mattered. Guild corridors. The weight of coin counted correctly. Nine years of prayers thrown at a silent god like letters into a well. Ombre's voice saying *count it again, little shadow; the second count is for you, not the money.*

Near the top, easy to lift, floated a hundred small things. The taste of a specific plum bought from a specific cart. A rainstorm on the guildhouse roof the night they'd decided to stay. The way Sefa had pushed her spectacles up before lying to them about the codex's condition, and how they'd found the lie almost charming.

Any of those. Any of those would do. Reach for one, pay, move on, forget the price—

But deeper, just below the surface layer, something else floated. Not deep enough to take the arm off. Just deep enough that reaching for it was a choice.

A room with a slanted floor. Dust in the light. Ombre younger than Lior had ever pictured her, sleeves rolled, ink on her knuckles, dragging a stool to the table and saying, *You're seven and you can't read and everyone in this city thinks that makes you stupid, so we're going to make them all liars.* The letter A drawn large and crooked. Ombre's big hand closing over Lior's small one. *No—here—curves are just lines that decided to be generous.* The smell of the tallow candle. The pride in Ombre's voice, badly hidden, when Lior read their first full sentence aloud: *The ledger balances.*

Lior's fingers were already in the water, reaching down, before they understood what they were doing.

"No," the keeper said sharply—the first sharp note it had struck. "Traveler. That one is not near the top."

"It's mine," Lior heard themselves say. Their voice sounded far away, like someone reading aloud in another room. "It should be worth the same as anyone's bread-end or kitchen window. You said small mercies. This is—this is a small thing. It's one evening. One candle. It's nothing."

It was everything, and they knew it even as they said otherwise, and they reached anyway, because some traitorous part of them—the part that had spent nine years being invisible, being competent, being *nothing personal*—had looked at that memory and judged it the cheapest thing they owned. The least essential. The part of themselves they could best afford to lose.

That was the horror of it, they realized later. Not that the tollhouse took. That Lior chose, and the choice told them exactly what nine years of the guild had taught them to believe about their own heart: that it was overhead. That love was a cost center. That the memory of being taught to read by a woman who pretended not to love them was *spendable*.

The keeper hesitated. For a moment its worn face did something almost like grief.

"Are you certain?" it asked. "I am obliged to ask once. Only once. No one ever asks me to ask twice."

"Take it," Lior said.

The keeper lifted it out. The amber light came up trailing something like thread, like a filament of candle-smoke, and for half a heartbeat Lior saw it whole—the slanted floor, the ink-stained knuckles, the crooked letter A—and then the keeper set it in the clay dish and it went out, and the room got quieter in a way rooms cannot actually get quieter, and Lior sat back on the stool.

"There," they said. "Paid in full."

* * *

Walking out was strange. Walking out was like leaving a house where a clock has stopped—you don't notice the missing tick, only that the silence has a shape now, a hole with edges.

Nim was arguing with the road about its cobbles. Sefa was muttering footnote numbers under her breath like a rosary. Lior walked between them with their ledger-slate at their belt and their accounts perfectly balanced and a hollow in their chest the exact size and shape of one evening, one candle, one crooked letter A.

They stopped at the edge of the clearing. The mountain stood against the stars, jawed and waiting, and somewhere inside it was a bill of sale with their name on it, and a vault of souls, and whatever else a century of quiet theft builds.

"Lior?" Nim had turned back. Lantern-light caught her face. "You went somewhere just now. Where'd you go?"

"I don't know," Lior said honestly. "I feel like I forgot to bring something somewhere."

"Same," Nim said, and offered her arm, and Lior took it, because the body knew things the ledger didn't.

Behind them, faint through the tollhouse door, came the sound of the keeper working—clay dishes being washed, one by one, with terrible tenderness. And Lior found, as they walked down into the dark toward the Court Beneath the Mountain, that tears were running down their face, steady and unexplained, grief arriving like rain on a window with no storm behind it.

They wiped them away, logged nothing, and kept walking.

They did not know what they had lost. They only knew that somewhere behind them, in a dish of white clay, something that loved them had gone out—and that ahead, under the mountain, something was waiting that had been counting on exactly this: travelers who paid their tolls, balanced their books, and never asked what the small mercies cost.
