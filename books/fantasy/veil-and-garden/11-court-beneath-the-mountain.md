## 11. Court Beneath the Mountain

The door in the mountain was not a door.

Nim had expected something grander, or something grimmer—a gate of black iron, a mouth of carved stone, warnings etched by hands that had meant them. Instead the entrance was a seam, a hairline crack in the cliff face that Sefa found only because she had spent three days cross-referencing the codex's marginalia against survey maps older than the guild itself. The crack was barely wide enough for a person turned sideways, and it breathed.

That was the word Nim's mind kept returning to as they stood before it in the last grey light of evening: *breathed*. Warm air came out of it in slow, even pulses, smelling of candlewax and hot glass and something underneath both—something old and sweet and wrong, like fruit past its ripeness, like honey left too long in the sun.

"Emberless's fire," Nim said quietly. "She's breathing through it."

Lior looked at her. In the days since they'd left the guild road behind, Nim had learned to read the mercenary's silences the way other people read speech—the tilt of the head that meant *go on*, the stillness of the hands that meant *I don't believe you yet*. "Dragons breathe," Lior said.

"Not like this." Nim pressed her palm flat against the stone. It was warm, faintly, rhythmically warm, and beneath her hand she felt—not heard, *felt*—a vast slow exhalation from somewhere far below, the way you feel thunder through the soles of your feet. "Her fire has been burning down there for a hundred years. She's been keeping something contained for a hundred years. And she's dying, and she knows it, and she's—" Nim stopped. Her throat had gone tight. "She's breathing out toward the surface. Like she's calling for help. Like she's been calling for help this whole time and no one ever thought to put their hand on the rock."

Sefa, crouched at the base of the seam with her lantern and her notebook, glanced up. The archivist's face was pale in the light, smudged with ink to the second knuckle. "The codex calls it the Veil," she said. "Not the mountain. The Veil. As if the stone were only a curtain hung over something else." She closed the notebook. "I would like it noted, for the record, that I think this is an extremely bad idea, and that I am coming anyway."

"You always note that," Lior said.

"It's important to be thorough."

Nim laughed—a short, startled sound, the first one in days—and then pressed her shoulder to the seam and slid sideways into the dark.

* * *

The passage went down for a long time.

It was not a natural cave. That became clear within the first hundred paces, when the rough stone gave way to walls that had been smoothed—not cut, not chiseled, but *melted*, as if the mountain itself had once run like wax and someone had shaped it with patient fingers while it cooled. The walls were veined with glass. Not windows; veins, running through the rock like sap through wood, catching Sefa's lantern-light and holding it, bending it, passing it along ahead of them so that they walked inside a slow ripple of amber glow.

"The roots," Nim whispered. She had stopped walking without quite meaning to. "These are her roots. Emberless's. Dragons root themselves when they settle—they grow into the deep places, tap the heat of the world. I grew up on stories about it. Keeper's stories." She touched one of the glass veins. It was warm under her fingertips, and it pulsed, very faintly, in time with that vast slow breath. "But no story ever said what happened to the roots when the dragon settled somewhere *inhabited*. No story said the roots could grow into a city."

"Then either the city grew around her," Lior said, "or she was planted here. On purpose."

Nobody answered that. Nobody wanted to.

They descended. The air grew warmer, sweeter, thicker; the candle-and-glass smell strengthened until Nim could taste it on her tongue, waxy and faintly floral, like the inside of a temple after a long service. Twice the passage branched, and twice Sefa consulted the codex and chose the branch marked in faded marginalia with a small drawn flame. *The author knew the way down,* she murmured, more to herself than to them. *Walked it often enough to annotate it.* And once—only once—they passed a side corridor sealed with a curtain of glass so thick it was opaque, and behind the glass, something shifted. Something large. Something that did not make a sound but that Nim felt in her back teeth, a pressure like a held note, and she found herself walking faster without deciding to, and neither of the others asked why.

They had been walking perhaps two hours when the passage opened without warning into light.

Nim stopped so abruptly that Sefa walked into her back.

Below them—far below, though the eye could follow every detail as if it were arm's length away—the city spread out through the belly of the mountain like a garden grown in a bottle. Glass roots everywhere: great branching columns of them rising from the depths and spreading across the cavern ceiling, and smaller ones threading down between buildings, through streets, into windows, glowing with that same amber light, so that the whole city seemed lit from within, lit like a lamp, lit like a heart. Terraces of white stone stepped down the cavern walls. Bridges arched over streets where figures moved, unhurried, elegant, dressed in colors that glowed softly in the rootlight—deep blues, wine reds, golds. And everywhere, everywhere, candles. Thousands of candles, millions, clustered on every sill and balustrade and windowsill, their flames perfectly still, perfectly upright, unmoved by any draft, burning with the steady unblinking patience of stars.

"It's beautiful," Sefa said, and her voice cracked strangely on the word.

It was beautiful. That was the first thing, and Nim hated how completely true it was. It was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen, and she had been raised on a dragon's hoard-glow and the aurora over the high passes. The light moved through the glass roots like music made visible. The city below arranged itself in terraces and gardens and long colonnades with the effortless grace of something that had never once needed to be practical. Even the people—even the distant, gliding, candle-lit people—moved beautifully, unhurried, their garments trailing like brushstrokes.

And underneath the beauty, so faint she might have imagined it, Nim felt the thing she had felt at the sealed corridor: a held note. A pressure. A hunger with its mouth closed.

"They're not moving right," Lior said.

Nim followed the mercenary's gaze. At first she couldn't see it—the figures below flowed through the streets like water, graceful, seamless. Then she saw it. They didn't hurry. Not one of them. Not one of them stumbled, or paused, or doubled back, or stopped to speak to another. They moved through the city the way blood moves through a body: purposeful, continuous, and utterly without choice. And none of them were touching each other. In all that glittering crowd, in all those gardens and bridges and colonnades, not two figures stood near enough to share a shadow.

"A hundred years," Nim said quietly. "You said the nobles down here don't age. A hundred years of wearing the same face, walking the same streets, eating the same—" She stopped. She didn't want to finish the sentence. She finished it anyway, because her mother had raised her to finish sentences. "Eating the same souls."

"We don't know that yet," Sefa said. "We don't actually know anything yet. We have a codex, a bill of sale, and a hypothesis." But her knuckles were white around her lantern handle, and she did not look up from the page she was pretending to consult.

Down in the city, a bell rang. It was the loveliest bell Nim had ever heard—deep and silver and round, a sound like the memory of a sound—and at its ringing, every figure in every street below stopped at once. All of them. Simultaneously. Ten thousand bodies halting mid-stride in perfect unison, and then, as one, turning their faces upward.

Toward the ceiling of the cavern.

Toward the roots.

Toward, Nim realized with a cold sliding feeling in her stomach, *her*. Because the roots ran up through the rock, and the rock carried vibration, and she had been touching the glass since the moment they entered the mountain. Every vein she'd laid her hand on was a nerve, and the whole city was strung with nerves, and somewhere far below, something had just felt three small tremors walking down its throat.

The figures below held their upward gaze for exactly as long as it takes to draw a breath. Then the bell rang again, softer, and they turned, and resumed, and the city flowed on as if nothing had happened—as if ten thousand faces hadn't just tilted toward the dark like flowers finding the sun.

"Tell me," Lior said, in the flat, careful voice Nim had learned meant the opposite of calm, "that was a coincidence."

"The codex mentions bells," Sefa said faintly. "Hour bells. Devotional bells. It says—" she flipped pages with a hand that wasn't entirely steady— "it says the court keeps the hours because time is the one thing they can't keep anymore. So they ring it instead. To remember what it sounds like."

"And the looking up?"

"The codex doesn't mention the looking up."

* * *

There was a stair. Of course there was a stair; a city does not build itself a throat and leave it unused. It spiraled down along the inside of the cavern wall, white stone worn smooth in the centers of the steps by centuries—or a century—of feet, and it delivered them, step by inevitable step, into the city itself.

Up close, the beauty got worse.

Nim had expected decay, or menace, or at least the ordinary shabbiness that collects on any place old enough. There was none. The streets were immaculate. The gardens were flawless—not merely tended but *finished*, every bloom at its perfect peak, which should have been impossible, because flowers peak and fade, unless nothing here was permitted to fade. The candles burned without shortening. She looked closely at one as they passed, a fat ivory pillar on a garden wall, and its flame was motionless as a painted flame, and the wax beneath it showed no melt, no gutter, no history at all. A candle that had never been allowed to burn down. A candle preserved at the exact moment of its own lighting, forever.

Everything here was preserved at the exact moment of its own lighting.

And the people—

A woman crossed the square ahead of them, tall, robed in wine-dark silk, her hair pinned in a style Nim had seen only in paintings four hundred years old. She was beautiful the way the city was beautiful, and she passed within six feet of them, and her eyes—her eyes were the problem. They were dark and clear and depthless, and they did not flicker toward the strangers in her street. Not a glance. Not a pause. She looked through them the way you look through a window at weather: registering nothing, because nothing out here could matter to her. Nothing out here was *food*.

Except that at the last moment, at the very edge of passing, her gaze snagged. Just for an instant. On Nim's chest—on the little iron dragon-tooth pendant Nim had worn since she was seven years old, her keeper's badge, her inheritance. The woman's stride hitched, half a heartbeat. Her nostrils flared, delicately, like a deer scenting smoke.

Then she was gone, gliding on, and Nim's whole body had gone cold.

"She smelled Emberless," Nim whispered. "On me. I've been sleeping in a dragon's hollow for eleven years. It's in my skin. It's in my *hair*—"

"Then we find the vault fast," Lior said, "and we get out fast," and their hand brushed the ledger-satchel at their hip in a gesture Nim had come to understand was the mercenary's version of gripping a sword hilt.

They turned a corner into a long colonnade, and the colonnade ended at a terrace overlooking a sunken garden, and in the garden, at last, the court was waiting for them.

Nim counted nine figures seated around a table of black glass, and every seat was filled except one, and the empty chair was the exact right size for Lior Oyelaran. She knew it the way you know a key fits a lock: instantly, horribly, from across the distance. The table was set for a meal she couldn't identify—dishes of crystal, fruits she didn't recognize, candles whose flames leaned, very slightly, toward the newcomers, as if scenting them. And the nine who sat there were the most beautiful people Nim had ever seen, and the most terrible, because their beauty had the stillness of the candles, the perfection of the gardens, the awful finished quality of things that had been kept from changing for a hundred years and had gone slightly, subtly mad from the keeping.

One of them rose. An elderly man, silver-haired, his mask—it took Nim a moment to realize he was wearing a mask, a fine porcelain half-mask so close to his skin it might have been grown there—tilted in something like welcome.

"Travelers," he said, and his voice was warm as candlewax, and it carried across the garden without effort. "How long it has been since anyone came down the stair *on foot*. Do come in. We have been setting a place for such a long time."

Behind him, the ninth chair gleamed.

And above them all, far overhead, beyond the branching glass roots, Nim felt—through the iron tooth at her throat, through eleven years of keeper's blood—Emberless's fire gutter, once, like a caught breath.

Like a warning.

The old man gestured graciously toward the empty chair, and smiled behind his porcelain, and said, in the tone of a host who has waited a very long century:

"Lior Oyelaran. Your seat has been kept warm."
