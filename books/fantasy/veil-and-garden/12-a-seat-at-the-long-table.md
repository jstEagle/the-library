## 12. A Seat at the Long Table

The doors of the Long Hall were taller than any door had a right to be.

They were not wood. Lior understood that much in the first heartbeat of standing before them—understood it the way a ledger-keeper understands a forged signature, by the wrongness beneath the beauty. The doors had been grown, not built: pale glass-root, veined with slow amber light, and carved—or perhaps *persuaded*—into shapes that resolved the longer one looked. Vines. Faces. Hands cupped around small flames. And at eye level, exactly at Lior's eye level as though the door had been measured to them specifically, a single uncarved panel, smooth and waiting, like a page left blank on purpose.

"They open for family," said the servant.

The servant was called Wren, or said they were; their voice had the pleasant, worn smoothness of a stone in a stream. They wore livery the color of candle smoke, and their face was kind in the way that well-made things are kind—a chair is kind, a warm bath is kind—and Lior could not stop looking at the place where their pulse should have been visible at the throat. There was nothing there. No flutter, no rhythm. Just skin, smooth as the blank panel in the door.

*They do not age,* Nim had whispered on the road down. *That's what Sefa found. That's what the fire is for.*

Lior had not answered then. They did not answer now. They had learned, in nine years of keeping accounts for impossible things, that silence was often the only honest entry available.

The doors opened without being touched.

Warmth poured out—real warmth, honey-and-hearth warmth, the warmth of a house where someone has been waiting up for you. It struck Lior like a physical thing, like a hand laid gently between the shoulder blades, and they hated how much their body leaned into it. Nine years of cold counting-houses. Nine years of sleeping above the guild vault because a bed felt like an extravagance. Nine years of eating standing up so that no one would ask them to sit.

The hall beyond was lit by ten thousand candles, and every flame burned perfectly still, as though the air itself had been taught manners.

* * *

The table ran the length of the hall like a river of dark wood, and it was set for a feast, and every seat but three was full.

The court of Beneath-the-Mountain did not look up all at once, which was the first courtesy and the first cruelty. In the world above, when a stranger enters a room, eyes swing to them like compass needles—instant, hungry, forgettable. Here, the nobles turned slowly, one by one, each granting Lior the gift of individual attention, and each face behind its mask was arranged in an expression of such precise, practiced delight that Lior's professional instincts screamed before their heart could.

They knew masks. The Gilded Ledger dealt in them. Grief wore masks; so did buyers pretending they weren't desperate, sellers pretending they weren't afraid. Lior had spent a career reading the gap between a face and what moved underneath it.

Here, the gap was infinite. The porcelain faces—white, gold-veined, serene—showed delight. Underneath, if there was an underneath, something was *counting.*

"Little ledger," said the woman at the head of the table, and her voice carried without effort, the way a bell carries. "Come. Sit."

She rose. She was tall and silver-haired and her mask was different from the others—older, plainer, cracked faintly at the left cheekbone like glaze that had survived too many firings. She did not wear jewels. She wore, instead, a chain of small keys, and Lior's eyes snagged on them the way a thief's eyes snag on locks.

"You know me," Lior said. Not a question. Questions gave ground.

"I have known your name since before you had one worth speaking." The woman—she had not offered a title, and no one else at the table seemed to require one—extended both hands, palms up, in the gesture merchants used to show they held nothing. "I am called the Buyer. It is honest, at least. This court buys things. It has bought very few things as carefully as it bought you."

The words should have been horrifying. Lior catalogued them instead—the old reflex, the ledger-mind closing over feeling like water closing over a dropped coin. *Bought you.* Filed. Dated. To be examined later, alone, at 3 a.m., in the dark, where all the worst entries went.

But the chair—

The chair was the problem.

It stood at the table's right hand, between the Buyer and an empty place set with a single glass. It was plain compared to the others, which were carved with vines and faces and little burning hands. This one was simple, dark wood, high-backed, and when Lior sat in it—because some traitor part of their body simply *did*, some part that had been tired for nine years and recognized rest when it was offered—the chair fit.

Not comfortably. *Perfectly.*

The back met their spine at the exact angle their spine wanted. The arms caught their elbows. The height put their eyes level with the Buyer's, neither looking up nor down. It was the chair of someone who belonged here, and Lior sat in it and felt, for four full seconds, a happiness so complete and so alien that it frightened them more than anything the mountain road had shown them.

"We kept it oiled," said the servant, Wren, materializing at their shoulder with a bowl of something fragrant—dark broth, root vegetables, bread still steaming. "One hundred years. Every season. The joiner's art survives here; we would not let the dust have it."

"You knew I'd come," Lior said.

"The book knew." The Buyer lifted her glass. Around the table, the masked nobles lifted theirs in perfect unison, a field of white faces tilting together like flowers finding the sun. "The book knows everything about this house. Tonight is a homecoming, little ledger. Eat. You've walked far, and the road above takes memories as toll. We do not take anything here that is not given."

*Everything here is taken,* Lior thought, and ate anyway, because the broth was good, because refusing food was a card they couldn't afford to spend yet, and because a mercenary who keeps accounts knows that sometimes you must accept a gift in order to learn its price.

* * *

The feast unfolded around them with the terrible smoothness of a ritual rehearsed for a century.

The dishes came and were exquisite: fish from waters that had never seen light, glazed in something that tasted like the memory of lemon. Fruit that burst cold and bright. Bread that made Nim, seated stiffly at Lior's left, go quiet mid-sentence with her first bite and then visibly angry at herself for going quiet. Sefa, at Lior's right, ate nothing, and kept their hands below the table where Lior knew—they knew without looking—that fingers were moving, memorizing, counting exits.

The nobles talked. That was the second horror: they talked *well*. They talked of music and grafting and the philosophy of borders, and their conversation was witty and generous and turned always, gently, inevitably, toward Lior. What did they think of the road? Had the tollhouse treated them fairly? Did they remember—did they *remember*—the garden courts, the winter festivals, the little room with the blue tiles?

"I don't," Lior said, the fourth time.

"But you might," said a noble across the table, mask painted with tiny gold stars. "Memory is a ledger too. Some entries are misfiled. Some are held in trust." A pause, delicate as a knife laid flat. "We hold yours in trust. All of them. Your first steps. Your first word. The way the light came through the blue tiles on the morning you were born. Would you like them back? They are yours. We have never disputed it. We are merely—" the star-masked noble spread elegant hands "—custodians."

*Custodians.* Lior turned the word over and found the hook inside it, the barb dressed as care. *We keep what is yours. Therefore we keep you.*

Nine years of silence from Veth had taught Lior this much: the cruelest thefts are the ones that leave you grateful.

"I'll want an inventory," Lior said. "Before anything is returned. Itemized. Witnessed."

The table laughed—warmly, delightedly, all those porcelain faces tipping back at once—and the Buyer's cracked cheekbone caught the candlelight.

"Oh, *yes,*" she said softly. "Oh, they told us true. You were worth every season of oil on that chair."

* * *

Later—hours later, though the candles never guttered and the court never tired—Lior excused themselves, and the court let them go with such gracious ease that it felt like being released by a hand that was certain of its grip.

They stood in a colonnade of glass roots, watching amber light climb the walls in slow pulses, and tried to breathe like a person who had not just spent an evening being loved by predators.

Nim found them first. She moved quietly for a dragon-keeper's daughter; she had learned quiet tending a dying animal, because noise woke grief. She leaned against the colonnade beside them and said nothing for a long moment.

"The chair fit you," she said finally.

"Yes."

"That's what's got you shaking."

"I'm not shaking."

"No," Nim agreed. "You're doing the thing where you don't shake." She looked out at the pulsing light. "My father used to say Emberless purrs loudest when she's most afraid. Big creatures learn to hide it last." She bumped her shoulder against Lior's arm, brief and warm and real in a way nothing in this hall had been. "Sefa's gone below. Toward the throne. Said something about archives under the foundations—said the servants' stairs don't have eyes."

"Sefa will get us killed."

"Sefa will get us *in.* Those aren't the same thing down here." Nim pushed off the wall. "Eat something real when you can. Sleep if they let you. And Lior—" she paused, and her face, smudged and windburned and utterly unmasked, was the truest thing Lior had seen since the doors opened. "Whatever they offer you tonight. Whatever fits perfectly. Ask yourself who measured it, and when, and why they never once let it gather dust."

She went. Lior stayed, and the glass roots pulsed around them like the heartbeat of something vast and patient, and they thought: *a hundred years of oil on a chair for a child sold at a well gate. A hundred years of waiting for tonight.*

No one waits a century for something worthless.

Which meant Lior Oyelaran was worth a great deal to this court—which meant, by the oldest law of the Ledger, that whatever the court intended to buy with them, it expected to sell for more.

* * *

They came for Lior near what the candles insisted was midnight.

Wren again, with the stream-smoothed voice: "The Buyer asks whether you would care to see the household accounts."

Lior went. Of course they went. It was bait and it was also, infuriatingly, irresistible; there was no version of Lior Oyelaran, sold or unsold, numb or whole, that could refuse a look at another keeper's books.

The study was small after the hall—almost humble. One lamp. Shelves of ledgers bound in grey leather, spines labeled in a fine antique hand. The Buyer stood at the desk with two glasses of something amber, and she poured without asking, and slid one across the wood until it stopped precisely at Lior's fingertips.

"You audit," she said. It was not flattery. It was diagnosis. "You have audited since you could hold a pen. I watched the guild's reports come through intermediaries for years—entries signed with a mark like a closed eye. Yours?" 

"My mark."

"A beautiful mark. Economical. It says everything and confesses nothing." She opened the topmost ledger and turned it toward Lior, and the past hundred years of the court's accounts lay open under the lamp: acquisitions, holdings, disbursements. Souls, Lior saw, though the word was never used. *Assets. Holdings. Long-term custodial instruments.* Each line neat. Each line dated. Each line, somewhere in its margins, bearing a small notation Lior's eye caught and could not release:

*a/c V.*

Account of Veth.

Every page. A century of pages. The silent god's name running through the court's books like a vein of ore through stone—not as enemy, not as debt, but as *counterparty.*

"You have questions," the Buyer said, watching Lior read. She did not watch hungrily. She watched the way a jeweler watches a stone she has already priced. "You have had questions for nine years, I think. About a god who stopped answering. About prayers that fell like coins down a dry well." She folded her hands. "Ask them here, little ledger. In this house, the accounts are open. In this house, *everything balances.*"

And Lior—who had prayed into silence for nine years, who had kept faith like a debt no one would acknowledge, who had sat in a chair that fit them perfectly and hated how much it felt like home—heard the trap in the mercy and stepped toward it anyway, because the margin note *a/c V.* was glowing in their mind like a door left unlocked.

"What," Lior asked, "does the god of silence owe *you?*"

The Buyer smiled behind her cracked mask, and outside, far above, so faint it might have been imagination, the mountain shuddered—as if something enormous had drawn a breath it could not quite hold.

"Nothing," she said. "That is the tragedy. Come. It is time you saw the vaults."
