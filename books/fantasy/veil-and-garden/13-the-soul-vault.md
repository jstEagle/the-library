## 13. The Soul-Vault

Sefa had spent thirty years learning to read rooms the way other scholars read manuscripts: for the parts that had been erased.

The throne room of the Court Beneath the Mountain was a palimpsest of cruelty. To a casual eye it was merely beautiful—candlelight pooled on floors of veined glass, and the great roots of the mountain's heart grew down through the ceiling in columns of translucent amber, each one lit from within like a lamp with something alive inside it. The nobles drifted between them in their porcelain masks, unhurried, ageless, courteous in the way of things that have never once needed to hurry because nothing has ever escaped them.

But Sefa saw the margins.

The candles were wrong. She had catalogued eleven thousand varieties of flame in her career—tallow, beeswax, whale-jessant, the grave-wax of Veth's own altars—and none of them burned this color. This light was gold shot through with a flicker of blue at the base, the way a fire burns when it is fed something that once lived and remembers living. And the glass roots: she pressed her palm flat against one while a servant's back was turned, and felt not the cool stillness of stone but a faint, arrhythmic pulse, slow as a sleeping heart, warm as a hand held too long over a candle.

*Something is burning,* she thought. *Something is always burning here.*

She withdrew her hand and smiled at a passing noble, and the mask tilted in polite acknowledgment, and Sefa walked on with her ledger-satchel against her hip and her fury folded small beneath her ribs, where it would keep.

* * *

They had been welcomed, which was the first horror. Welcomed with wine the color of garnets and chairs that fit their bodies too precisely, by hosts who called Lior *the returned asset* as though reciting an inventory line. Sefa had watched Lior's face across that terrible table—the mercenary's expression smooth as sealed wax, but the fingers of their right hand tapping out, very softly, the rhythm of an accounting prayer—and she had understood that Lior was holding themselves together with bookkeeping, and that the seam would not hold forever.

So while Nim went to find the dragon-keeper's old stairways and Lior accepted the court's hospitality with a smile Sefa recognized as a weapon being sharpened, Sefa did what Sefa always did.

She went to the archives.

Every power keeps records. That was the first lesson of her disgrace, learned young and paid for dearly: tyrants are not careless. Tyrants are meticulous. They must be, because theft on scale requires inventory, and inventory requires ink, and ink can be read by anyone patient enough to learn the hand. The court beneath the mountain had stolen centuries of souls, and somewhere in its glass-and-candle depths there was a room where those thefts were written down, filed, cross-referenced, and shelved.

Sefa intended to find it, read every line, and carry the whole monstrous catalogue out into the light if it cost her the other half of her life.

The servant who led her—because of course they were led; the court permitted no unaccompanied wandering—was a thin woman with kind eyes above a mask of plain white clay. Her name, when Sefa asked, was given as *Wren*, and then, after a pause, as though correcting an error: *Wren-of-the-East-Stair.* Not a name. A designation. A shelf-mark.

"Where do you file your histories?" Sefa asked pleasantly, as they descended a spiral stair cut from living amber.

"The court does not have histories," Wren said. "The court has *now*."

"And yet you said 'east stair,' which implies a west."

Something flickered behind the kind eyes—the smallest spark, quickly smothered, of a person who had once been amused by cleverness. "You ask dangerous questions, archivist."

"I am paid to." A lie; no one was paying her anymore. "Dangerous questions are the only ones worth the ink."

Wren stopped at a door of black glass set into the root-wall, and made a gesture Sefa did not understand, and the door opened without sound. Beyond it lay a corridor lined with shelves, and the shelves were full of books, and the books were beautiful, and Sefa felt the old ache rise in her chest—the archivist's sickness, love of the collection before thought of the collector—before she looked closer and saw what the books were bound in.

Not leather. Not vellum.

Skin. Faintly luminous, faintly warm, each cover carrying the ghost of a whorl of fingerprint or the faded shadow of a birthmark, pressed flat and cured and gilded at the edges.

"These are the court's *guests*," Wren said quietly. "Those who asked questions. Their accounts are kept in themselves. It saves paper."

Sefa's hands did not shake. Thirty years of handling cursed codices, plague-grimoires, and one book that screamed had taught her body stillness the way soldiers learn it. But inside, something cold and enormous turned over, like a leviathan shifting beneath ice.

"How many volumes?" she asked, and her voice came out level, and she hated how level it was, hated the scholar in her who even now wanted to *catalogue*.

"Four hundred and six," said Wren. "But the guests are only the overflow." She glanced up the corridor, toward the distant glow of the court above, and lowered her voice to almost nothing. "The true collection is below. If you are what I think you are—if you came with the returned asset—then you should see it. Someone should see it. Someone should *write it down*."

"Why?" Sefa asked. "Why help us?"

Wren's hand rose and touched the edge of her clay mask, just barely, the way a prisoner touches a wall they have measured a thousand times.

"Because I had a name once," she said, "and I cannot remember what it was. Only that it was short. Only that my mother used to sing it." She stepped back through the black-glass door and closed it, and the corridor swallowed her footsteps, and Sefa stood alone among four hundred and six people who had become their own prison files.

* * *

The way down was not marked, because things that are never visited do not need signs. But archives leave drafts, and drafts leave dust, and dust leaves trails, and Sefa followed a scuff-pattern in the amber floor that repeated at intervals too regular for accident—a maintenance route, walked by someone, perhaps by Wren herself, on some long-ago errand of replenishment.

Three levels below the guest-corridor the air changed. It grew warmer, and sweeter, and wrong—the sweetness of honey left too long in sun, of fruit going soft, of something fermenting gently in the dark. The glass roots here had thickened and merged until the walls were solid amber, and inside the amber Sefa began to see shapes.

At first she thought they were bubbles.

Then she understood they were faces.

Small, curled, dreaming—each one suspended in its own pocket of golden resin like a fly in a saint's keepsake, eyes closed, mouths slack with sleep or something deeper than sleep. Hundreds of them in the walls alone. Thousands. The mountain's own veins had been used as shelving.

Sefa pressed her forehead against the cool amber and breathed, once, twice, the way Ombre had taught her—*breathe, girl, a broken archivist reads nothing*—and made herself walk on.

The vault opened at the end of the corridor without ceremony: a cavern so vast that its far wall was a rumor, lit by ten thousand candles arranged in perfect rows upon tiered stone shelves, and before each candle, a jar.

Sefa had imagined many things on the road beneath the mountain. Screaming souls in iron bottles. Ghosts chained to anvils. Something gothic, something operatic, something that would let her rage burn hot and clean.

What she found instead was *filing*.

Each jar sat on its shelf in strict alphabetical order—or rather, in strict order of acquisition, numbered in a hand so fine and regular it might have been typeset. Each jar bore a label. Each label bore lines:

*Acq. 14,203. Female, aged 34, weaver. Yielded under Contract of Necessity, Well Gate district. Quality: grief-tempered, high resilience. Reserve status: centennial collection. Candle allocation: two.*

*Acq. 14,204. Male, aged 9. Inherited debt, paternal line. Quality: bright, unformed, excellent yield projected. Note: mother contested sale; contest dismissed per Clause 11.*

A nine-year-old. *Clause 11.* There was a clause for children. Of course there was; evil this old writes its bylaws.

Sefa lifted a jar from its shelf with both hands, the way she would lift any irreplaceable text, and held it to the candlelight. Inside, swirling slow as smoke in water, was a color she had no name for—not light, not liquid, but the *substance* of someone, turning and turning. As she watched, the swirl pressed itself against the glass nearest her hands, spread wide, thinned, and she understood with a lurch that it was reaching. That after however many years in the dark, it still knew the difference between glass and skin. That it still tried.

She set it back exactly where it had stood, label facing out, number aligned, because to do otherwise would disturb the order and order was the only thing protecting these souls from being *consumed* rather than merely stored—but her hands shook now, all thirty years of stillness failing at once, and she had to stand for a long moment in the candle-hush with her fists clenched, doing sums.

That was the trick of it. That was how she kept from screaming in a place built to muffle screams. She did sums.

Ten thousand candles. Say one soul per candle, conservatively—no, the labels ran higher than the candles; there were dark shelves beyond the light, jars cold and unlabeled, older acquisitions whose numbers had worn away. Call it twelve thousand. Twelve thousand souls, filed. Twelve thousand names reduced to acquisition numbers and quality assessments. Twelve thousand mothers' contests dismissed per Clause 11.

And the handwriting on the labels—

Sefa froze.

She knew that hand. She had spent three years of her disgrace studying it, defending her attribution of it before a tribunal of lesser scholars who had laughed her out of her chair. The fine, regular, upward-slanted script with the distinctive crossed sevens and the little hook on terminal *r*s. The hand of Master Ellun Vaire, court archivist of the old kingdom, author of the Codex of Veiled Years, the prophetic manuscript that had started everything—that had put Lior Oyelaran's name under tonight's date.

Vaire had labeled these jars. Vaire, whose marginalia Sefa had been decoding for weeks in inns and ditches by candlelight, whose coded notes she was increasingly certain concealed not prophecy but *confession*. Vaire had stood in this room, or one like it, and written *quality: bright, unformed* about a nine-year-old child, in a script so beautiful it was taught in academies.

*Evil is not careless,* she reminded herself. *Evil is meticulous. Evil has excellent penmanship.*

And then, because she was who she was, she took out her notebook and began to copy.

Label by label. Number by number. Name where a name survived—some of the oldest jars bore true names beneath the acquisition numbers, in a different, hurried hand, as though someone had once tried to smuggle identity back into the record and been stopped. She copied for what felt like hours, kneeling on cold stone, her knees aching, her ink freezing at the nib until she warmed it in her fist. She copied the weaver and the child and a fisherman marked *yielded voluntarily, note: he believed he was buying his daughter's medicine* and a woman marked simply *reserve, centennial* with tonight's date beside it, and beside *that*, in Vaire's immaculate hand:

*Acq. 16,901. Oyelaran, Lior. Infant. Sold at Well Gate. Quality: unprecedented. Note: half reclaimed by outside party, terms unknown. Balance due at centennial. See master ledger, final page.*

Half reclaimed. *Ombre.* The guildmaster's pale face rose before her, the guilt braided into the protectiveness, the years of knowing. Half of Lior's soul had been bought back and hidden, and that was why Lior moved through the world like a sealed room, why they prayed to a silent god with the devotion of someone filling a well that never rose. Not temperament. Arithmetic. Someone had subtracted from them, and everyone who loved them had agreed to round down.

Sefa wrote the entry into her notebook with a pressure that nearly tore the page, and her fury, which had been cold all night, finally found its temperature—not the heat that burns out, but the cold that preserves. The cold of a thing kept for use. She would copy every label in this vault if it took the rest of her life. She would publish them in editions so cheap that beggars could buy them. Every name restored to its owner, every clause quoted aloud in market squares, every beautiful penmanship dragged into daylight and made ugly again, which is what truth does to it.

"You should not be reading those."

She did not startle. She had known someone was there for some minutes—the hush of the vault had changed texture, the way a room does when it gains a listener. She closed her notebook, rose stiffly, and turned.

Wren stood at the vault's mouth, mask off, held in both hands like something breakable. Without it her face was ordinary and tired and human, and her eyes were wet.

"The changing of the watch," Wren said. "We have perhaps an hour before the tally-clerks descend for the pre-centennial count. You cannot copy them all, archivist. There is no time."

"No," Sefa agreed. "There isn't." She looked out over the ten thousand candles, the ten thousand jars, the ten thousand colors swirling in the dark, reaching, always reaching, toward warmth, toward hands, toward the memory of having a body. "So I won't copy them."

Wren frowned. "Then what—"

"I'll take the *index*." Sefa patted her satchel, where the codex pages slept alongside her notebook. "Vaire kept a master index—I've seen references to it in his marginalia. Acquisition numbers cross-referenced to names, locations, surviving kin. One document. Everything." She met Wren's eyes. "Where does the court keep its master ledger?"

The silence stretched. Somewhere far above, muffled through miles of amber and stone, music began—the court, feasting, celebrating its hundredth year of harvest. The jars on their shelves seemed to lean toward the sound, the way plants lean toward a window.

Wren put her mask back on. When she spoke again, her voice was steady, and it was the steadiest thing Sefa had heard since descending into this beautiful, buried hell.

"The master ledger sits on the throne itself," she said. "Under the king's hand. He touches it when he holds audience, so that it will know him." A pause. "But there is another way to it. Through the dragon's mountain. Through the old keeper's stairs, where the fire comes down." Her kind eyes fixed on Sefa. "Your friend—the dragon-girl. Ask her about the Ember Stair. And archivist—"

"Yes?"

"When you write this down—and you will write it down—write our names. All of them. Even the ones we've forgotten. Write that we were people."

Sefa gripped the woman's shoulder through the thin servant's cloth, and could not speak for a moment, and did not try.

"I will," she said at last. "In ink that outlasts them."

Above them, deep in the mountain's crown, something vast exhaled—a long, guttering sigh of heat that shivered down through the amber veins, and every candle in the vault flickered at once, ten thousand small flames bowing together like a congregation, and in the sudden dimness Sefa heard, unmistakably, from the direction of the fire, a voice like settling coals begin—slowly, painfully, in a tongue older than the court—to speak.
