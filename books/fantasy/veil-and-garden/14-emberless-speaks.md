## 14. Emberless Speaks

The dragon's den was not what I had been told it would be.

I had grown up with the stories my mother told before she stopped telling stories: that Emberless slept in a cavern of gold, that her scales were the color of storm-light, that when she breathed the whole mountain smelled of lightning. The stories were wrong in every particular except one. She did breathe. That was nearly all she did now.

Her den was a hollow of black stone at the mountain's root, and it was not gold that lined it but glass — thin veins of it running through the rock like frost through a windowpane, glowing faintly amber wherever her warmth touched them. She was smaller than the stories said. Dragons shrink as they die; I had known this since I was six years old and had watched my mother measure Emberless against a chalk line on the den wall, year after year, the marks marching downward like a staircase descending into the earth. When I was born, the chalk line had been drawn at the height of two men standing on each other's shoulders. Now I could have laid my palm flat against the crown of her skull without stretching.

She lay curled around the one thing she still guarded: the fire. Not a fire you could cook with or warm your hands at. This fire burned in a basin of fused glass at the center of her coils, and it made no heat I could feel from three steps away, and yet every hair on my arms stood up when I looked into it, the way they stand up before a storm breaks. It was the color of the moment just after sunset — not orange, not gold, but that bruised violet-red that means the light is leaving and everyone knows it.

"Nim." Her voice came out of the stone itself more than out of her throat. Dragon voices are low things; they travel through rock the way water travels through roots. "You brought strangers to my door."

"I brought no one," I said, which was true, and then, because lying to a dragon is a poor habit even when the lie is technically honest: "They followed me. A ledger-keeper and an archivist. They know about the court."

The great head lifted. It cost her something — I could see the tremor run down her neck, the effort of it, like watching an old woman rise from a sickbed — and her eye found me. Dragon eyes are the size of dinner plates and deep as wells, and hers had gone the pale gray of ash over coals. But there was still coal under the ash. There always had been.

"Ledger-keeper," she repeated, and the word moved through the glass veins in the walls and made them flicker. "One of theirs."

"One of ours," I said. "Mostly. It's complicated."

"Everything worth keeping is complicated." Her head settled again onto her foreleg, and the basin of fire guttered — actually guttered, the flame flattening and rising like a candle in a draft — and I felt the old fear climb my spine on its familiar ladder. *Not yet. Not tonight. Please not tonight.*

I knelt by the chalk lines on the wall, because kneeling near her while standing felt like presumption, and because the ritual of it steadied me. I took the chalk stub from my pocket — my mother's chalk stub, worn to a nub, kept forty years past any sensible use — and drew the new mark at the height of her skull. The line of measurements went back further than my mother. Further than her mother. The earliest marks were cut into the stone rather than drawn, made by keepers whose names had worn away entirely, and the first of them all was so high on the wall that I had to squint to see it in the dimness.

"You're smaller," I said, uselessly, because someone had to say it aloud or I would carry it alone.

"I am always smaller." A sound came out of her that might once have been laughter. It rattled the glass. "You are late, little keeper. I called for you three nights ago."

"The road was long, and the tollhouse took—"

"I know what the tollhouse took. I felt it leave you. Grief travels; it has a scent." Her eye closed, opened. "You gave away a memory to reach me faster. Your mother taught you that trade?"

"She taught me everything about this trade."

"Then she taught you badly, or well. I've never decided which." The fire guttered again, longer this time, and the amber glow in the wall-veins dimmed with it, and somewhere far above us — I could not say how I knew, but I knew — candles in the court above flickered in sympathy. Everything in this mountain was connected. That was the trap of it. "Sit, Nim Ashvael. What I have to tell you will want sitting for."

I sat. The stone was warm where her body had leaned against it for decades, and cold everywhere else, and I thought: *this is what my whole life has been.* Warmth in a narrow band, cold beyond it, and me keeping the accounts of both.

* * *

My mother died four years ago, in autumn, with her hands still smelling of chalk and tallow. In her last week she tried to tell me something about the fire — I remembered that much, though the memory had gone strange at the edges, the way memories do when grief has handled them too often. She had gripped my wrist hard enough to bruise and said, *It isn't what we were told, Nim. It was never what we were told,* and then the fever took her words and scattered them, and by morning she could only ask for water, and by evening she asked for nothing at all.

I had spent four years telling myself she had been raving. Fever-talk. The dying saying dramatic things because dying is dramatic.

Emberless began speaking before I finished arranging myself on the cold stone, and every word of her was my mother's sentence, unscattered, restored.

"You were raised to believe my fire is a prison," she said. "That it holds the stolen souls of the court fast, so they cannot flee their bodies and haunt the mountain. Yes? That is what the keepers teach their daughters. That is what the court permits them to believe."

"It's what everyone believes." My voice sounded small in the vast dark. "The souls are in the jars beneath the throne. Your fire keeps them sealed. When you die, the jars open, and the dead walk."

"Mm." The sound she made was almost gentle. "And who benefits from that belief?"

I opened my mouth. Closed it. Thought of the court above — the candle-lit nobility who did not age, the servants who never seemed to tire, the beautiful glass-rooted streets where nothing ever rotted or rusted or changed. I thought of how the court had let the keepers' line continue, generation after generation, unbroken, unmolested. How they had never once sent soldiers to end us, though a single dying dragon guarded the thing they supposedly feared most.

"They do," I said slowly. "The court benefits."

"The court benefits." Her tail shifted, and the glass veins flared briefly amber along its path. "Now listen, child of keepers, because I am old and tired and I will only say this once, and afterward you will wish with all your heart that I had said it twice. My fire does not imprison the souls below. Nothing imprisons the souls below. They are held by contract, by ink, by the oldest magic there is — debt. A soul sold cannot be recalled until the sale is voided. That is the law beneath all laws, older than gods, older than dragons. No fire can break it. No fire ever could."

"Then what—" My mouth had gone dry. "Then what does the fire *do?*"

The flame in the basin rose. For a moment — one long, terrible moment — it stood tall and bright as it must have stood a century ago, and I saw what lived inside it: not light exactly, but attention. A vast, weary, burning attention, turned downward, turned inward, pressed against something.

"Hunger," Emberless said. "Mine is the fire that contains hunger. Not theirs." A pause, and the flame sank again, and with it my heart. "*Mine.*"

* * *

I did not understand. I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward depended on understanding, and in that first hour I understood nothing at all. I sat on the cold stone with my mother's chalk in my fist and I said, "Yours?" like a fool, like a child, and the dragon looked at me with her ash-gray eye and had mercy on me anyway, because she had known my grandmother and my mother and apparently had decided the line produced fools but lovable ones.

"When the first soul-seller made the first sale beneath this mountain," she said, "the act required a furnace. All binding requires a furnace — you cannot forge a chain without one. The seller built his forge of glass and fed it with the first soul he took, and the forge worked, and he grew rich, and the court grew fat, and no one asked what became of the furnace when the forging was done." Her breath moved through the den, stirring dust that glowed like slow snow. "Furnaces remember what they burn, little keeper. Every soul that passed through the making of those contracts left a residue. A taste. And the furnace — the fire — learned to want it."

"You're the furnace."

"I am what the furnace became, once it was given flesh and a name and a keeper's line to tend it. The seller bound the fire into a body so it could be managed. So it could be *kept.*" The word came out bitter enough to etch glass. "Kept. As if I were a jar myself. And the bargain was this: the fire would contain its own hunger forever, pressing it down, starving it, holding it behind my ribs — and in exchange, the court would live undisturbed, and the keepers would live undevoured, and the fire would be tended until the end of the world or the end of the fire, whichever came first."

"And when the fire ends—"

"Then the hunger ends too." Her eye fixed on me, and I swear the temperature of the room dropped. "Because the hunger dies with the vessel. That is the truth the court will never tell you, Nim Ashvael, and the truth your mother died trying to give you: when I go, the hunger goes with me. The souls will not be freed by my death — but neither will they be hunted. The court will simply be *hungry*, and hungry forever, with nothing left alive in this mountain strong enough to contain the wanting. They will feed. On the servants first, because the servants are closest and weakest and their lives have already been siphoned half-empty. Then on anyone else they can catch. And they will call it a centennial feast, and they will dress it in candlelight, and it will not stop until there is nothing left beneath this mountain but full bellies and empty skins."

The silence afterward was total. Even the glass veins had gone dark.

"That's why they need the collection," I whispered. "The prophecy. Tonight. The hundred-year date."

"A hundred years ago, the last time the fire ran this low, the court gathered souls in bulk — dozens, scores — and fed the strongest of them to the forge-fire to bank my flames, the way you'd bank a hearth for the night. It bought them another century. It bought *me* another century." Something moved across her face that I had no name for. Shame, maybe. Or gratitude. Or both wearing the same mask. "Every year of my life has been paid for with stolen souls. Do you understand what I am telling you, keeper? I am not the prison guard. I am the prison *pantry*. And every day I burn a little lower, and every day the court looks at me the way a family looks at the last pig in the larder, waiting for winter."

* * *

I want to record what I did next, because I am not proud of it, and because pride has no place in an honest account.

I cried. Ugly, graceless crying, the kind I hadn't done since my mother's funeral, hunched on the cold stone with my arms around my knees while a dying dragon watched me with the patience of mountains. Because I had spent my whole life believing my work mattered — that the chalk lines, the tallow, the nightly tending of the flame, were a sacred duty, a wall between the dead and the living. And it was. It was all of that. But it was also this: I was the keeper of a larder. I was the daughter of larder-keepers going back nine generations, and the thing we had guarded so faithfully was the court's next meal, and the only thanks our line had ever gotten was permission to keep existing alongside it.

"She knew," I said finally, wiping my face with the heel of my hand. "My mother knew. That last night — she tried to tell me."

"She tried for years to tell you. The court watches the keepers closely, child. Words spoken too plainly have a way of being... collected." Emberless's voice dropped lower, and the stone carried it up through my knees, through my spine, into my teeth. "Your mother found another way, in the end. You carry it without knowing."

"What way? What do I carry?"

But the fire guttered then — a long, bad guttering, the flame dropping to a blue whisper — and the dragon shuddered the length of her diminished body, and all questions fled my head like birds off a scarecrow's arm. I was on my feet and moving before I decided to move, hauling the tallow I'd carried up the mountain on my own back, feeding it to the basin's edge the way nine generations of my family had done, hands steady, breath prayed-out, doing the only work I had ever been made for.

The flame took the tallow. Rose. Stabilized. Held.

For now.

"How long?" I asked, when I could speak again. I didn't specify what. We both knew.

"With careful tending? A season. Perhaps two." Her eye had drifted half-closed, the ash thickening over the coals. "Without the collection to bank me — less. And they will come for the collection soon, little keeper. The date is upon them. Their ledgers are open. Their masks are polished." A pause, long enough that I thought she had fallen asleep, which she sometimes did mid-sentence now, sleep taking her like a tide takes a swimmer. Then, so quietly I had to lean close:

"There is a ledger-keeper in your company. The one whose name is written in the old book."

"Lior." The name felt strange in my mouth, half-owned, borrowed. "What about Lior?"

"The contracts beneath the throne are written in a hand that can be audited. All contracts can. Even the oldest ones. Even the ones the court believes are perfect." The ash-gray eye opened fully, and for one instant the coal beneath it blazed, and the whole den lit amber, and I saw reflected in the glass veins a hundred images of a fire that would not be contained much longer. "Tell your ledger-keeper to read carefully. Tell them the court's greatest strength is also its oldest crime: everything in that mountain was *bought*. And anything bought—"

"—can be voided," I finished, barely breathing.

"Go," said Emberless, and settled her skull back onto her forelegs, and closed her eye, and the fire burned on between us, banked and starving and patient as a debt. "Climb fast, keeper's daughter. The feast is already being set."

Above me, faint through a mile of stone, I heard the court's bells begin to ring.
