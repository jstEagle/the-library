## 15. Nine Years of Silence

The sealed vault beneath the Gilded Ledger was not locked.

That was the first thing Lior understood, standing before the door with Sefa's borrowed picks still warm in their pocket: the door had never needed a lock. It needed a reason not to open, and the guild had spent a century making sure no one had one. The iron was thick as a coffin lid, banded in brass stamped with the Ledger's scales-and-quill sigil, and across its face ran the inscription every apprentice learned to recite before they learned to count:

*What is owed is kept. What is kept is owed.*

Lior pressed their palm flat against the cold. Somewhere below, in the dark, nine years of tithes sat waiting for a god who had stopped answering.

"Veth," they said quietly. It was not quite a prayer. It was closer to a knock on a door that had been shut in your face so long you'd begun to apologize for knocking at all. "I'm coming down. If you're there. If you can hear me. I'm coming down anyway."

The god did not answer. The god had not answered in nine years, three months, and eleven days—not that Lior kept count, except that they did, because keeping count was the only prayer they knew how to make.

They spoke the opening words of the guild's oldest rite, the ones Ombre had taught them at fourteen, her hand over theirs on the ledger-line, her voice rough as unpolished silver: *By debt and by devotion, by what was given and what was taken, open.* The brass bands warmed under Lior's palm. The sigil's scales tipped—first toward the quill, then, after a long breathless moment, toward nothing at all—and the door swung inward on hinges that made no sound, which was somehow worse than if they had screamed.

Cold air rose to meet them. Not the cold of stone. The cold of a room where something had been weeping for a very long time and had finally run out of tears.

* * *

Lior went down alone. They had considered asking Nim or Sefa to come—Nim would have come without question, torch first, stubbornness second—but this was not a thing to be witnessed. This was a thing between an accountant and an account.

The stairs spiraled into the mountain's root, each step worn concave by generations of guild feet, though the dust on them lay undisturbed, a grey velvet record of abandonment. Lior counted the steps out of habit. Two hundred and six. At the bottom, the passage opened into a chamber that stole the breath from their chest and held it hostage.

Shelves. Shelves upon shelves upon shelves, rising into darkness the lamplight couldn't reach, and on every shelf, jars.

Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Glass blown thin as soap-bubble skin, each one stoppered with wax the color of dried blood, each one labeled in the fine copperplate script that was the guild's pride. Lior lifted the nearest jar and read the label by lamplight, and their hands began to shake before they finished the first line.

*A tithe owed to Veth, Silent God of Accounts. Contents: the last spoken word of Maren Colvane, given freely at her deathbed, per contract dated—*

Given freely. That was the doctrine. Every guild child learned it: the tithes were gifts, freely offered, set aside for the god who kept the world's accounts balanced. A widow's gratitude. A merchant's first honest profit. A soldier's thanks for surviving what should have killed him. Each year, on the longest night, the guild carried the year's tithes down here and left them on Veth's altar-shelf, and each year the god received them in silence, and that silence was holy. That silence was *Veth*. The god who did not speak, who answered prayers instead with small mercies—a coin found when you needed one, a storm delayed an hour, a name remembered at the exact moment you feared you'd forgotten yours.

Nine years ago, the mercies had stopped.

Lior had told themselves a hundred stories about it. The god sleeps. The god has turned away from me specifically, because I am unworthy, because I am too careful, because I once fudged a column by half a copper when I was nineteen and terrified. The god was never there at all, and the whole faith was a story the guild told itself to make the arithmetic feel like love.

They walked the aisles now, lamp held high, reading labels. And the stories died one by one, like candles pinched between wet fingers.

Because the jars were wrong.

Not all of them. The oldest shelves—the ones nearest the entrance, the ones a century deep—held tithes that hummed faintly against Lior's senses, warm as bread, patient as seed-corn. But the further Lior walked, the more the jars changed. The glass grew darker. The contents shifted from light to something viscous, something that moved when you weren't looking directly at it. And the labels—

Lior stopped before a shelf of jars labeled in a different hand. Not copperplate. Something older, looped and elegant, the handwriting of someone who had been taught penmanship by tutors rather than ledgers.

*Tithe collected in lieu of payment. One voice, partial. Donor: the god Veth.*

Lior read it four times. Their mind, trained on impossible trades, on bottled grief and borrowed years, on names sold twice—Lior's mind, which could parse any contract however crooked, refused this one the way a healthy body refuses poison.

*Donor: the god Veth.*

Gods did not pay tithes. Gods *received* them. That was the entire architecture of faith, the load-bearing wall of everything the guild was. You gave; the god kept; the balance held.

Unless someone had found a way to make the god a party to a contract. Unless someone had written terms the god could not refuse—or had forged the god's consent, the way you might forge a dying man's signature, and taken payment in the only currency a god of accounts possessed.

Lior lifted the jar. Inside, behind the dark glass, something coiled and uncoiled. It had no shape, exactly, but it had *weight*, the weight of a word held in a closed mouth, and when Lior tilted it toward the lamplight the contents caught the flame and threw back not light but sound—a single syllable, muffled, straining, like a voice calling up through deep water.

Lior could not make out the word. But they knew, with the certainty that lived in the marrow rather than the mind, whose voice it was.

It was the voice that had never spoken to them. The voice that had answered nine years of prayers with silence. It was here. It had been here the whole time. Bottled. Shelved. Filed.

*Collected in lieu of payment.*

"No," Lior said aloud, and the vault ate the word without echo. "No, no, no—"

They moved faster now, lamp swinging shadows across the shelves, reading label after label after label. *One voice, partial. One voice, remainder. One silence, compounded annually.* The dates marched backward through the years like soldiers deserting a losing war, and every date fell within the last decade, and the handwriting on every jar was the same elegant, tutor-taught loop, and Lior knew that handwriting.

They had seen it two nights ago, in a codex a century old, beneath their own full name and tonight's date.

The author of the prophecy. The previous soul-seller. The court beneath the mountain had a scribe, and the scribe had a hand, and the hand had reached up out of the dark and taken the god's voice jar by jar, year by year, while the whole faithful world above assumed the silence was holy.

Lior sat down on the floor between the shelves. The stone was cold through their clothes. They did not remember deciding to sit. Their legs had simply ended their employment.

* * *

Grief came first, and it came strangely—not for themselves, though there was plenty of that owed, but for the god. Nine years Lior had prayed into a silence they believed was divine, had poured their loneliness into it like water into a well, had built their whole self around the shape of an absence. *You are quiet because you are vast. You do not answer because answers are cheap and you deal in true price.* They had made a theology of their own abandonment, and now the theology lay shattered around them, and underneath it was not mystery but theft.

Someone had robbed their god. Someone had gagged the silent god and called the gag sacred, and every worshipper above had bowed their heads to the sound of a muzzle and named it grace.

And then—slowly, terribly—the grief turned.

Lior thought of the codex. Of their own name under tonight's date. Of the bill of sale Sefa had found: *one infant, Lior Oyelaran, sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain.* Of the contract in the guild's records bearing their own soul-mark, inked before they could walk. They had spent days believing they were the exception, the anomaly, the single thread of their own life that had been stolen. But sitting here among ten thousand stolen voices, Lior understood that they were not the exception.

They were a line item. One entry among thousands. The court beneath the mountain had been running accounts against the surface world for a century—against gods and infants and widows and merchants alike—and the Gilded Ledger, the great and honorable Gilded Ledger, had been either its victim or its partner, and Lior did not yet know which was worse.

Their hands had stopped shaking. That was how Lior knew the grief had finished becoming something else. Rage was quieter than grief. Rage kept books.

"All right," they said to the dark, to the shelves, to the coiled voice in the jar still cradled against their chest. "All right. Then let's audit."

* * *

They worked until their lamp burned low and they had to relight it twice from the emergency taper every guild officer carried sewn into their cuff. They worked the way they did everything: methodically, completely, with the terrible patience of someone who has decided that precision is the only weapon they will ever be handed.

The pattern emerged within hours, and it was beautiful, and it was obscene.

The thefts had begun nine years, three months, and twelve days ago—one day before the mercies stopped. The first jar was small: *one whisper, incidental,* collected from a shrine in the low town where Veth's presence pooled thickest. The second, a month later: *one blessing, unspoken, intercepted.* And then larger. *One voice, partial. One voice, remainder. One silence, compounded annually.* The scribe's hand grew more confident with each entry. By the third year the labels stopped pretending. *Voice of the god Veth, installment seven of a projected forty.* Projected. As if even a god's soul could be amortized.

Forty installments. Lior counted the jars on the god's shelf, and then counted again, slower, touching each one.

Thirty-one.

Thirty-one pieces of a god, shelved in a cellar, and somewhere—here, or beneath the mountain, or both—nine more remained uncollected, and tonight's date sat in a century-old prophecy like a signature waiting on a line.

Lior wrapped the first jar—the whisper, the smallest one—in cloth from their coat and tucked it inside, against their heart, where it pulsed faintly like a second pulse that wasn't theirs. They could not carry thirty-one. They could barely carry one; the weight of it was not glass and wax but meaning, and meaning does not lighten with practice. But they would not leave empty-handed. You did not walk out of a vault holding nothing when something had been stolen. That was not accounting. That was surrender.

Before they left, they stood a long moment at the altar-shelf at the chamber's far end, the one where the yearly tithes were laid. It was bare. Of course it was bare. Everything laid here had been gathered up and carried away, and the god had received nothing for nine years, and had been unable to say so.

Lior knelt. It was not a gesture they had ever made easily; kneeling had always felt like a lie, like performing a humility they didn't possess. But they knelt now, on the cold stone, in the dark, among the shelved voices of their god, and they spoke—not a prayer, exactly. Closer to a promise entered into a book.

"They took your voice," Lior said. "I know. I found it. I don't know how to give it back yet, but I know how to find things, and I know how to read contracts, and I know how to make thieves regret their arithmetic. So I'm going to say this once, and you can hold me to it, because I know you keep accounts even when you can't speak them."

The jar against their chest pulsed. Once. Twice. Like something listening. Like something *hoping*, which was worse and better than anything Lior had felt in nine years.

"I will get your voice back," Lior said. "Every installment. With interest."

Silence. But it was a different silence than the one Lior had lived inside for nearly a decade. This one had weight and warmth and attention in it, the silence of a presence leaning close rather than a void staring back. Lior rose, knees cracking, throat tight, and climbed the two hundred and six steps with the stolen whisper of a god beating gently against their ribs.

* * *

Sefa was waiting at the top, asleep over a spread of the codex's facsimile pages, ink-stained fingers curled around a cold cup of tea. She woke the instant the vault door sighed shut behind Lior—took one look at their face, and whatever she saw there made her sit up very straight.

"You found something."

"I found the god." Lior's voice came out flat and strange. "Or what's left of the god. Thirty-one jars of it, filed under tithes collected. The court's scribe has been harvesting Veth for nine years, Sefa. Installment by installment. Contract by contract." They set the wrapped jar on the table between them, and unwrapped it, and let the lamplight touch the dark glass. Inside, the syllable strained against its prison, and Sefa flinched as though struck.

"That's—" She swallowed. "That's a voice. That's an actual—gods' mercy, Lior, that's *singing*, I can almost hear it trying to—"

"It's one piece of a god in a jar," Lior said, "and there are thirty more downstairs, and the people who did it wrote the prophecy that has my name in it, and tomorrow night is the centennial collection." They looked at the codex pages, at the elegant looping hand they now knew as intimately as their own. "Which means tomorrow night, they come for the last nine installments. And I intend to be there when they do."

Sefa stared at the jar a long moment. When she looked up, the scholarly dread in her face had hardened into something Lior recognized, because they were wearing it too.

"There's something else," she said quietly. "Something I decoded an hour before you came up. About what the collection actually is. About what the date means." She hesitated. "You're not going to like it."

Lior touched the jar, feeling the small desperate pulse beneath the glass, and thought of a god gagged for nine years, and an infant sold at a well gate, and a chair waiting beneath the mountain that fit them perfectly.

"I haven't liked anything since Tuesday," Lior said. "Tell me anyway."
