## 16. The Price of a Chair

They gave me a room with a window.

That was the first thing I should have noticed—the window. We were a mile beneath the mountain's spine, deeper than any well I had ever drawn from, deeper than the roots of the oldest oak in Veth's abandoned grove, and yet there in the wall of my guest chamber bloomed a window of pale glass that showed a garden in full summer light. Bees moved among lavender. A fig tree bent under its own sweetness. Somewhere beyond the glass, water ran over stones.

There was no sky behind it. There was nothing behind it at all.

I stood before it for a long time after the servants withdrew, my breath fogging the surface, and the garden did not care. The bees did not startle. The light did not shift as light should when a body stands between it and its source. It was not a window onto anything. It was a painting of elsewhere, hung where a prisoner would want it most.

*They know what I miss,* I thought. *They learned it before I ever arrived.*

I had been in the Court Beneath the Mountain for three days. Three days of candlelit corridors where the wax never guttered and the air smelled faintly of honey and cold stone. Three days of nobles in masks of porcelain and gilt who bowed to me—*bowed*, to a mercenary with ink-stained fingers and a guild badge I'd surrendered at the gate—and called me *the Returned*. Three days of meals I could not taste properly, though every dish was arranged like an offering: pomegranate seeds laid out in spirals, bread torn rather than cut, salt served in a dish carved from a single pearl.

And through all of it, beneath everything, the hum. The vast, slow vibration of Emberless's failing fire, transmitted through the mountain's bones into the soles of my feet, into the roots of my teeth. A heartbeat too large for any living chest. Nim heard it and wept quietly the first night. I heard it and did arithmetic.

Nine years I had prayed to Veth for a sign and received silence. Now silence itself had a texture, a weight, a temperature. This place was made of it.

* * *

On the fourth day, they came to make their offer.

The summons arrived not as a servant with a message but as a change in the corridor outside my door. Footsteps—many, unhurried, soft as snowfall on wool. When I opened the door, six masked figures stood in a line, and at their center was the one I had already begun to think of as the Steward, though no one had given her a name. Her mask was white porcelain, unadorned except for a single hairline crack running from brow to chin, and through the crack I could see something moving. Not flesh. Something like candleflame seen through frosted glass, bending toward whatever came near.

"Lior Oyelaran," she said, and my name in her mouth sounded like a key turning in a lock I hadn't known was on me. "The Court requests your presence at the Long Table."

"I've eaten," I said.

"This is not a meal." A pause, delicate as a held breath. "This is an inheritance."

Nim would have said something sharp. Sefa would have asked three questions to every one answered. I said nothing, which is its own kind of fluency, and followed them down.

The corridors changed as we walked. The glass roots that veined the walls—those enormous translucent conduits through which the city drank its strange light—thickened here, brightened, until walking among them felt like walking inside a lantern. And within the glass, if I let my eyes unfocus, I could see shapes. Slow-turning motes. Threads of gold suspended in the pale green light, drifting like pollen in still water.

Souls. I knew it the way you know your own handwriting. The court wore its stolen dead as decoration, and called it architecture.

The Long Table occupied a hall so vast that the ceiling vanished into darkness above the reach of the candles. The table itself was black wood—or something wearing wood's shape—polished to a depth that swallowed reflection. Chairs lined both sides, dozens of them, most standing empty, and at each empty place a single candle burned with a flame that did not flicker. At the far end, on a dais of veined marble, sat the Lord and Lady of the court side by side, their masks of beaten silver so old that the metal had gone soft-edged, featureless, like faces remembered by someone who had long ago forgotten them.

A single chair waited at the table's near end. Alone. Facing the length of the hall, facing the Lord and Lady, facing everything.

It fit me perfectly.

I understood that the moment I saw it—not because I sat in it, but because I recognized it. The height of the backrest matched the curve of my spine. The arms were set exactly where my elbows fell when I folded them over a ledger. The seat's depth was the seat's depth of every chair I had ever shifted uncomfortably in, corrected. Someone had measured me. Not with calipers or cord—with years. With ledgers. With the patient accumulation of everything the guild had ever recorded about what I bought, what I sold, what I hesitated over, what I refused to look at twice.

"Please," said the Steward, gesturing.

I did not sit. I put my hand on the chair's back instead, and the wood was warm, blood-warm, and I took my hand away faster than dignity allowed.

"The Court has watched you for some time," the Lady said. Her voice was lovely the way a frozen lake is lovely—smooth, and hiding the fact that nothing beneath it moves. "Even before you knew of us. Even before the codex found its way into your guildmaster's vault. A child sold out of the world, raised in the gray country above, learning to count. Do you know how rare that is, little ledger-keeper? To be sold and not broken? To keep accounts with such—" she searched, delicately, "—such *clean hands*?"

"I'm a mercenary," I said. "My hands are many things."

"They are *precise*," said the Lord, and his voice was his wife's voice an octave lower, the same lake, colder. "You balance what cannot be balanced. You find the fair price in unfair rooms. We have read your work. The grief-bottlings alone—do you know how few traders seal a grief without spilling any? Without taking a cut for themselves?" He leaned forward, and the candlelight slid across his mask without catching. "You have been practicing for us your whole life without knowing it."

The hum in the floor deepened. Or perhaps I only imagined it answering him.

"You're going to offer me a job," I said.

The Steward laughed—a small sound, quickly gathered back, like a coin snatched from a fountain. "Oh, she's wonderful," she murmured to no one. "Ombre trained her *wonderfully*."

"We are going to offer you," the Lady said, "your soul."

* * *

They did it beautifully. That is the thing I could not stop admiring, even as admiration curdled into horror: they did it the way a master forger signs a name, with total commitment, no tremor anywhere.

The Steward produced a jar.

It was small—no larger than a fist—of dark glass blown thick and old, sealed with wax stamped by a sigil I did not recognize and then, underneath that sigil, one I did: the Gilded Ledger's scales-and-quill, pressed crooked, the mark of a junior clerk working late. Inside the jar, something turned. Not liquid. Not smoke. A slow golden turbulence, the color of lamplight through honey, and when it turned toward me I felt it turn—a sensation like a door opening in a house I had lived in all my life without ever visiting certain rooms.

My soul. Or half of one. Ombre's half—I knew that now, had known it since the road, since the tollhouse, since the memory of her teaching me letters had been scraped out of me and left a wound shaped exactly like her voice. She had bought it back from someone, hidden the debt, hidden the whole architecture of what I was, and this jar was the other half. The court's half. The part that had been carried down through the Well Gate in an infant's swaddling and entered into a book a century before I was born.

"Yours," the Steward said simply. "Freely given. No price. No contract. No signature required."

Every instinct I owned stood up at once and began shouting. Nothing in the Gilded Ledger was free. Nothing anywhere was free; I had built a life on that axiom the way other people build lives on faith. A gift with no terms was either a lie or a hook, and usually both.

"And in exchange," I said carefully, "you want nothing."

"We want your *service*," the Lord said. "Which is not a price. It is a calling." He rose, and the movement was wrong in a way I couldn't parse—too smooth, as though his joints were suggestions rather than hinges. "Our ledger-keeper is retiring. A hundred years of faithful accounting. He is tired, Lior. He is *so tired*, and the work requires new hands. Young hands. Hands that understand the world above, where our... suppliers... live."

Suppliers. The word went through me like a splinter finding its depth.

"What work," I said, "exactly?"

The Steward spread her hands, and the hall obliged her by dimming, candles bowing low, until the only bright things were the glass roots in the walls and the jar in her grip. "Entries," she said. "Acquisitions. Collections. Every hundred years the Court collects what it is owed, and between collections, the Court maintains what it holds. Names. Years. Griefs. Voices." A pause, silk over stone. "Souls."

"You're offering me the soul-seller's desk."

"We are offering you the *soul-seller's chair*," the Lady corrected gently. "The desk follows. As does the title. As does—" and here her voice softened into something almost maternal, almost warm, and it was the warmth that frightened me more than anything else in that cold hall, "—as does your whole self. Made entire. You have spent your life half-feeling, haven't you, child? Half-grieving, half-loving, half-present at your own table? We can give you back the missing rooms of your own house. You could feel the whole weight of a friend's hand on your shoulder. You could remember your mother's face without reaching for it. You could pray to your silent god and hear, for the first time in nine years, whether He was ever listening at all."

The hall was silent. The hum filled it, vast and slow, and I realized my hand had found the chair again without my permission, my fingers resting on the warm wood as if it were a railing on a high ledge.

That was the trap. Not the offer—offers can be refused. The trap was that every word was true. I *had* spent my life half-feeling. I had told myself it was discipline, temperament, the mercenary's armor, and I had worn that story so long it had grown into my skin. But the road had shown me memories that weren't mine, and the tollhouse had taken one that was, and somewhere between those two wounds I had begun to suspect the truth: that the flatness in me was not character but theft. That there had been a boy who felt everything, and someone had sold him by the pound.

They were offering to sell me back to myself. And charging me with a chair.

* * *

"I'd like to see the retiring keeper's books," I said.

The Steward blinked. Behind her, two of the masked attendants shifted—a rustle like dry leaves. It was not the answer anyone had prepared for.

"The books," I repeated, and made my voice into the thing Ombre had forged it into, the voice that had closed a thousand deals with men who thought they were closing them with me. "You want a ledger-keeper. Any competent accountant inspects the accounts before accepting stewardship. Unless," I let the pause do its work, "the accounts won't bear inspection."

Silence, of the kind that has furniture in it.

The Lord and Lady looked at each other—or turned their masks toward each other, which may have been the same ceremony once—and something passed between them that I was not meant to read. Then the Lady laughed, low and delighted, and the sound rang off the black table like a coin dropped in a crypt.

"She audits us," she said to the hall, to the empty chairs, to the souls drifting in the glass roots. "One day at our table and she audits us. Oh, we chose *well*, a century ago."

"A century ago," I said, "you chose an infant."

"We chose a *lineage*," the Lord said, and for just a moment the smoothness dropped and something older looked out through the silver mask, something hungry and patient and very tired of waiting. "The codex named you before your mother named you. Tonight the collection comes due. You may stand inside the century or outside it, Lior Oyelaran. Inside, you keep the books. Outside—" he spread his soft-edged hands, gracious as a headsman, "—the books keep you."

The Steward set the jar on the table beside the chair. My soul rolled slowly against the glass, gold and blind, like a fish in winter water.

"Take the night," she said kindly. "The chair will wait. It has always waited for you."

* * *

They escorted me back through the bright corridors, and I counted my footsteps because counting was the rope I climbed down into myself when the floor of the world tilted. Four hundred and eleven steps from the Long Table to my door. On step three hundred, we passed a service alcove, and behind its curtain I heard voices—servants, the tireless polite ones who never slept and never aged—and one of them was crying. Quietly. The way you cry when you have learned that being heard costs more than you are paid.

On step four hundred, the Steward touched my elbow, feather-light.

"For what it is worth," she said, and through the crack in her mask the candleflame-thing bent toward me like a plant toward sun, "he resisted too. Our retiring keeper. In the beginning. They all do, the ones with anything left in them." She smiled, and the crack widened very slightly, and I understood that the crack was not damage. It was wear. A hundred years of smiling had split her face open, and whatever was inside had been leaking out ever since. "Then they sit in the chair. And the chair fits. And fitting, little ledger-keeper, is the gentlest prison ever built."

She bowed and left me at my door, and I stood a long time in the false light of the false window, watching bees visit flowers that had never grown from any soil.

* * *

I did not sleep. I sat on the floor with my back against the bed—refusing the chair in my own borrowed room, a small stupid defiance, but defiance keeps its edge best when honed on small things—and I made lists, because lists were prayer, now, the only kind Veth seemed able to hear.

What I knew: the codex named tonight. The collection comes due tonight. The court wanted me seated, signed, and settled before the century turned, which meant my refusal—if I meant to refuse—had to come *before* the bells, in writing, witnessed, notarized in whatever fashion this place honored, because a verbal refusal to creatures like these would be an invitation to negotiation, and they had a century of practice at negotiating.

What I suspected: the retiring keeper's books contained the shape of the trap. Contracts always do. I had spent nine years reading the Gilded Ledger's fine print, and fine print is where mercy goes to be itemized. Somewhere in those volumes was the clause that made a free gift cost a life, or a lifetime, or a voice.

What I feared: that the chair fit. That some traitor room in my chest had already sat down in it, had already felt how perfectly it held me, and that the war ahead was not against the court at all but against the half-starved creature in me that wanted, more than freedom, more than truth, more than Veth's own voice—to *belong* somewhere, even here, even built on lies. Especially built on lies. Lies are load-bearing. They hold up houses the truth could never support.

Toward what must have been morning—there was no morning, there was only the hum and the honey-colored dark—I heard footsteps in the corridor. Two sets. One light and quick, one dragging slightly on the left, favoring an old knee injury Sefa had never stopped complaining about.

I was at the door before the second footfall landed.

Sefa's face appeared in the gap first, spectacles fogged, ink to the second knuckle, eyes wide with the particular terror of a scholar who has learned something the universe would prefer undiscovered. Behind her, Nim's hand rested on a knife she was pretending wasn't there.

"Lior," Sefa whispered, glancing down the empty corridor. "We found the archives under the throne. We found the jars. Thousands of them." She swallowed. "But that's not why I risked my neck crawling through the glass roots at the hour the servants change watch. Lior—the marginalia. The codex. I decoded the author's hand." Her voice dropped so low I had to lean into the doorway to catch it, and what she said next rearranged every list I had made, every plan, every fear, into a single new and terrible column:

"The prophecy isn't about your death. It never was. Tonight isn't an ending." Her eyes shone wet in the candlelight. "Tonight is *inventory*. And someone has to show you what the retiring keeper's last entry says—because it's dated tomorrow, and it's signed with your name."
