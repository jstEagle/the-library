## 17. Refusal, Notarized

Sefa had spent thirty years learning that every document has a body.

Ink has a body—its grain, its bite into vellum, the way iron-gall browned at the edges of a stroke where the acid ate the page. Wax has a body: the whorl of a seal pressed too hard, the hairline star of a seal pressed too soft. Even a lie has a body, if you knew how to read it—the hesitation in a hand, the too-even spacing of words rehearsed before they were written. Sefa had built her whole disgraced career on the principle that paper remembered what people wanted forgotten.

So when the court beneath the mountain slid the contract across the long table toward Lior Oyelaran, Sefa did not look at the terms.

She looked at the document's body.

It was beautiful. That was the first obscenity about it. The vellum was calfskin so fine it was nearly translucent, glowing faintly from within like a lamp behind cloth, and the ink was not ink at all but something metallic and living that moved when she tilted her head—a script that adjusted itself to the reader's eye, that wanted to be legible, that *courted* legibility the way the court above them courted everything: with patience, with polish, with the assumption that beauty was an argument no one could refuse.

The clauses were written in a fair hand. The terms were generous. The chair they'd given Lior fit him perfectly, which was the second obscenity, because furniture does not know your shoulders unless someone measured you a hundred years ago.

And at the bottom, waiting, was a line for his signature—and beside it, already inscribed in that living metal script, a facsimile of Lior's soul-mark, hovering half-formed, eager, like a dog that recognized its name.

*They've had it all along,* Sefa thought. *They've been holding his mark for a century, waiting for him to walk in and claim it.*

She kept her eyes on the page and her breathing even, because the nobles of the Court Beneath the Mountain watched faces the way hawks watched fields, and she had learned, in these last hours among the glass roots and candle-lit halls, that the safest expression here was mild scholarly interest. She was, after all, an archivist. She had been invited to witness. Witnesses were furniture with eyes.

Lior sat in the perfect chair with his hands flat on the table, and Sefa saw—because she had spent three weeks on a mountain road learning to read this particular face, this face that had been sanded down by nine years of unanswered prayer—that he was not tempted.

He was *counting.*

His lips barely moved. His fingers tapped once against the vellum, twice, a third time—the rhythm he used when auditing, the rhythm Nim had mocked and Sefa had come to find as comforting as a heartbeat. He was reading the contract the way he read everything: not as language but as arithmetic, hunting the flaw, the imbalance, the place where the sums went wrong.

Sefa leaned forward slightly, as if adjusting her spectacles, and murmured, "Clause eleven."

"I see it," Lior said, so quietly the words were almost breath.

"The consideration clause. It says your soul is returned *freely.*"

"It says freely." His finger came to rest on the word, and the living ink shivered under his touch like water disturbed. "But clause two defines me as *an asset of the court, returned to active service.* You can't freely give something back to someone who claims it never stopped being theirs." He looked up—not at the nobles arrayed along the table, but at the servant standing behind his chair, the one who had poured wine that tasted like candle smoke and honey. "That's not a gift. That's a repossession with better lighting."

The servant smiled. They all smiled. That was the thing about the court that Sefa's dreams had not prepared her for, though her nightmares had tried: the smiles never moved. They were painted on, or worn, or grown, and they stayed exactly where they were while the eyes above them did all the work—eyes old and patient and hungry in a way that had nothing to do with food.

"You find our terms wanting," said the noble at the table's head. Her mask was porcelain white with a single gilded tear painted beneath the left eye, and her voice was warm as a banked fire. "Tell us what you want, ledger-keeper. We have had a century to prepare for you. Whatever you ask, we have anticipated it."

"That's the problem," Lior said. "You anticipated everything except the possibility I'd do the math."

He stood. The perfect chair released him with a sound like a sigh—or like something inhaling to speak—and Sefa felt the whole hall attend to him, the candle flames leaning, the glass roots overhead chiming faintly, a thousand small attentions converging.

"Give me paper," Lior said. "My own. And my own ink. If I'm to answer a contract, I'll answer it properly. Notarized. Witnessed. In a form your archives can't revise after the fact."

A ripple went through the masked faces. Not laughter. Something more like appetite.

"Properly," the gilded-tear noble repeated, delighted. "Yes. Let us be proper."

*Oh, you fool,* Sefa thought, with a surge of terror and pride so tangled she couldn't tell them apart. *Oh, you magnificent, impossible fool.*

Because she understood what he was doing. She alone at this table understood it fully, and it was the most beautiful thing she had ever seen a person build out of nothing but precision: Lior was not refusing yet. He was establishing *form.* He was invoking the guild's oldest law—the law Ombre had beaten into every apprentice, the law older than the Gilded Ledger itself—that a refusal entered in proper form, witnessed and sealed, was binding on both parties *and on any power that honored contracts.* And this court, this hungry elegant machine, ran entirely on contracts. It could not dismiss his refusal without dismissing its own foundation.

They had offered him mercy dressed as a trap. He was answering with a trap dressed as courtesy.

Nim Ashvael stood at Lior's shoulder, arms crossed, dragon-ash still ground into the creases of her knuckles, and she caught Sefa's eye across the table with an expression that said, plainly: *he told us the plan in the wine cellar, pretend to be surprised.*

Sefa arranged her face into scholarly concern and asked for parchment herself, "to record the proceedings," and the servants brought it—of course they brought it, they brought everything, instantly, silently, tireless as water finding its level—and the hall settled into the theater of formality, and the trap closed around the trappers.

* * *

Writing under observation, Sefa had learned, was a discipline of the hands.

Her fingers wanted to shake. She let them shake—but only in the way an old archivist's fingers shook, the honest tremor of age and cold, nothing that would read as fear. She ruled lines down the page with a bone stylus, true and even, and she wrote the heading in the formal hand of the Ledger's certification office, the one she'd been expelled for forging and had since perfected:

*RECORD OF REFUSAL, ENTERED AND WITNESSED.*

Beside her, Lior wrote his own document in his small, merciless accounting hand, each letter identical to its siblings, each figure balanced. She did not read his text as he wrote it. She kept her eyes forward, on the nobles, cataloguing them the way she catalogued everything now—out of habit, out of defiance, out of the scholar's stubborn faith that naming a thing diminished it.

There were forty-one nobles at the long table. Their masks differed—beasts, moons, weeping queens, a fox with a coin in its mouth—but their stillness was uniform, and their stillness was wrong. Living people shifted. Living people swallowed, flexed, grew bored. These figures sat like objects that had learned to imitate sitting, and the candles around them burned without flickering, and the air of the hall tasted of hot glass and old honey, and underneath it all, so faint Sefa might have imagined it, there was a sound like a vast slow breathing that came from everywhere—from the walls, from the roots, from the mountain's stone heart.

Emberless. The dying dragon far above, whose guttering fire was all that held this court's hunger leashed. Sefa thought of Nim's face when she spoke of the dragon—stubborn hope stretched over certain grief—and made her letters very straight.

Lior finished. He blotted the page. He rose, and the hall leaned in again, candles tilting, and he read his refusal aloud in the flat clear voice of a man reading accounts to a room that owed him money.

"I, Lior Oyelaran, having reviewed the instrument placed before me, decline its terms entire. I acknowledge no debt to this court. I acknowledge no ownership, past or continuing, of my soul, my name, my labor, or my person. Any claim to the contrary is void ab initio, being founded on a sale conducted without consent of the sold, who was at the time of sale an infant, and without lawful title in the seller, whose authority I hereby formally dispute and require this court to produce and prove. Entered this night, witnessed and sealed. So refused."

Silence.

Then the noble with the gilded tear began, softly, to laugh—and the laugh was the worst sound Sefa had heard in the mountain, worse than the breathing walls, because it started warm and human and then continued a beat too long, two beats, three, going on and on without breath until it curdled into something mechanical, a clockwork of mirth, and around the table the other masks turned toward their laughing colleague with expressions that had not changed at all.

"You dispute," the noble said, recovering, smoothing her voice back into warmth the way one smooths a bedsheet. "How charming. How very *guild* of you. But child—" and the warmth dropped away between one word and the next, like a mask slipping, except the mask was the warmth, "—we bought you fairly. Your mother signed. Your mother *wept* signing, which made it sweeter. Consent of the sold is a modern invention. We deal in older law."

"Then you'll have no trouble proving it," Lior said. "In writing. Before witnesses. Under seal." He set his refusal on the table and weighted it with the wine cup. "Refusal stands until disproven. Those are your laws. I've read them in your own archive, in your own hand, and so has she." He nodded at Sefa. "She's an archivist. Disproving things is her profession. She's very good at it. Ask the institution that expelled her."

"They expelled me for being right," Sefa said, and heard her own voice come out steadier than she had any right to expect. "I intend to be right again. Thoroughly. On the record."

The gilded tear gleamed. The noble studied Lior for a long moment, and then she did something that Sefa would carry to her grave: she reached up and touched her own mask, lightly, the way a woman touches her cheek, and said, almost tenderly:

"We waited a hundred years for you. We can wait a little longer. But the collection is tonight, little ledger-keeper, and the mountain is large, and our servants are *very* patient. Do run along and disprove whatever you like."

She snapped her fingers—or made the gesture of snapping; no sound followed—and every servant in the hall turned its head at once.

All of them. Every servant, in every doorway, along every wall, dozens upon dozens of them, turning in unison with that same fixed smile, and the smile was no longer warm. It was simply teeth arranged politely.

"Show our guests to the door," the noble said. "Take your time. Enjoy the tunnels."

* * *

They ran anyway.

Not because running was wise—Nim said so, gasping, as they took the third fork away from the route their escort indicated, choosing descending passages over ascending ones, choosing dark over candlelight—but because the alternative to running was walking at the servants' pace, and the servants' pace was the pace of a tide coming in, and Sefa had read enough drowned cities to know how tides ended.

The glass-root tunnels sang as they fled.

That was the horror of them. The roots of the mountain were veins of translucent glass, thick as temple pillars, branching through the dark, and every footfall rang up through them and came back changed—came back carrying fragments of sound that were not theirs. Sefa heard her mother's kitchen, the rattle of the tea tin, though her mother had been dead twenty years. She heard the archive bell at the institution, the one that had tolled the morning of her expulsion. She heard, horribly, a few bars of a song she did not know and then realized she *did* know, had always known, had known since childhood—which meant it wasn't hers either, which meant the road and the roots had been feeding her borrowed memories for days and she had swallowed them like sweet water.

"Don't listen," she panted. "The roots replay. It's how they hunt—they make you stop and grieve in the middle of the corridor."

"I know," Nim said grimly. "It showed me my father's forge three miles back. Keep moving."

Behind them, unhurried, tireless, came the servants.

They did not run. That was the detail Sefa's mind kept snagging on as she stumbled down stairways cut like throats, past alcoves where candle-lit nobility watched from behind veils of glass and did not intervene, merely *watched*, amused, proprietary. The servants walked. They walked at exactly the speed of exhausted people fleeing, never gaining, never falling behind, and their footsteps made no echo, and when Sefa glanced back—and she hated herself for glancing back, and glanced back anyway—she saw them in the glass-light: pale, neat, smiling, their livery immaculate, their hands empty and open, as if they were not pursuing but *escorting*, as if the whole chase were a courtesy they were extending at great personal inconvenience.

One of them called out, pleasantly, from fifty yards back: "The exit is the other way, honored guests."

Another, from a side passage: "You'll want your rest before the collection."

Another, closer than the others, its voice gentle as a nurse's: "Master Oyelaran. Your mother's name is written in our books. We keep *everything* here. Wouldn't you like to—"

"*Don't,*" Lior snarled, and the word cracked off the glass roots and rang away into the dark, and Sefa heard in it not fear but fury, nine years of fury finally finding a shape, and she loved him a little for it, in the fierce impersonal way she loved well-made things.

They burst through a curtain of root-glass into a junction where six tunnels met, and Nim skidded to a halt, hand raised, listening. Above them—far above, through a mile of stone—the light dimmed. Not torchlight failing. Something else. A great slow pulse of warmth and shadow, like a heartbeat missing beats.

"Emberless," Nim whispered. Her face in the glass-light was terrible to see—all the stubborn hope burned off it at last, leaving only the keeper, only the duty. "She's guttering. Sefa, she's *guttering.* We don't have until dawn. We may not have an hour."

"Then we need the tomb," Sefa said. "The forger's grave. If the original seller's signature is forged—if he was dead when the court claims he signed—every sale voids. Every jar opens. Everything they've stolen for a century walks free, and their precious collection becomes a room full of empty glass."

"And you're sure?" Nim demanded. "You're sure it's forged?"

Sefa thought of the codex's marginalia, of the dying seller's cramped confession hidden in the margins of his own prophecy, of the hand preserved in the tomb below—she thought of it and felt the old scholarly dread transmute, at last, into something clean.

"No," she said. "I'm sure it's *checkable.* That's better. Certainty is for priests. I work in evidence."

Lior laughed—a short, sharp, astonished sound, there and gone—and put his hand briefly on her shoulder, and through the fabric of her coat Sefa felt it like a brand, like a signature.

Then the servants came around the corner behind them, all of them at once, filling the tunnel wall to wall, smiling, walking, patient as arithmetic, and the nearest one said kindly:

"This way to the collection, if you please."

Lior stepped in front of them both, and Sefa saw him reach into his coat and draw out not a weapon but his ledger-book, his actual book, and hold it up so the glass roots caught and multiplied its cover, and he said, in the voice he used for closing accounts:

"We please not. Refusal entered, witnessed, sealed. Now—we run."

And they ran, down the sixth tunnel, the one that sloped toward the mountain's buried heart, toward the forger's grave and the preserved dead hand and the truth that might unmake a century of theft—while behind them the light above guttered again, weaker, and somewhere in the dark ahead something stirred that was neither servant nor noble nor god, and Sefa, sprinting through the singing glass with her evidence clutched to her chest, heard the mountain groan like a ledger being opened—

—and heard, impossibly, distantly, from far above, the sound of the well gate grinding open, and voices. Many voices. One of them rough, familiar, furious, calling a name down through the stone:

*"LIOR OYELARAN! THE GUILD IS COMING TO COLLECT!"*
