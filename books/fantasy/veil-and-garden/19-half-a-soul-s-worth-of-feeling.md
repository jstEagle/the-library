## 19. Half a Soul's Worth of Feeling

The road between them was twenty feet of packed earth and a decade of silence, and Lior made Ombre cross every step of it.

Not out of cruelty. Out of habit — the accountant's instinct to make the other party come to the table, to let them feel the distance they had chosen. But as the guildmaster walked toward them through the remembering road's pale shimmer, her boots stirring up ghosts of other travelers' laughter, Lior felt something new crackling under their ribs. Not anger. Anger they knew; anger was an old coat that fit well. This was rawer. This was the feeling of a number not adding up.

Ombre stopped three feet away. Close enough to touch. Far enough for honesty.

"You look different," she said.

"I feel different." Lior held up the hand that had touched Nim's shoulder on the road, that had gripped Sefa's arm in the dark of the tollhouse. "That's rather the point, isn't it? You said you brought proof."

Ombre's jaw worked. She was not a woman accustomed to being audited, and it showed in every line of her — the guildmaster who could stare down a dragon-debt broker or a grief-smuggler without blinking, now fumbling at the clasp of the satchel she'd carried across half a country. She drew out a document wrapped in oilcloth, and unwrapped it with hands that were not quite steady.

"I need you to understand," she said, "that when I found this, I told myself I was protecting you."

"They always say that," Nim murmured behind Lior. Not unkindly. Just true.

Lior took the paper.

It was old — older than their memory of the guild, older than the first night they'd stood in the Gilded Ledger's counting room and been told *you have a gift for this*. The vellum had gone soft as cloth at the edges. The seal was one Lior knew intimately: the Court Beneath the Mountain, pressed in wax the color of dried blood. And the text—

The text was a bill of sale. Their own name sat in the buyer's line, written in a clerk's careful hand. *One soul, whole and unencumbered.* And beneath it, in a second script, cramped and hurried, a rider:

*Sold subject to partition. Vendor retains fifty percent interest pending collection at centennial date.*

Fifty percent.

Lior read it twice. Three times. The words did not change, which was somehow worse. The world tilted slightly, and Nim's hand arrived at the small of their back without being asked, warm and steady, anchoring them to the road.

"Half," Lior said. The word came out flat. "You're telling me I've been walking around with half a soul since before I could walk."

"I'm telling you what the contract says." Ombre's voice was low, roughened. "I bought the debt, Lior. Twenty-two years ago, from a broker in Vell's Crossing who didn't know what he was holding. He thought it was a novelty. A curiosity for collectors. I recognized the seal because—" She stopped. Swallowed. Started again. "Because I'd seen it once before, years earlier, on a shipment manifest I should never have read. Souls moving under guild cover. I bought your contract to keep it out of their hands. To keep *you* out of their hands. You were four years old. You'd been left at the Well Gate orphanage, and there were men asking questions about children born in the winter of the comet, and I—"

"You bought me."

"I *hired* you." The words cracked down the middle. "When you were old enough to hold a quill. I hired you, and I fed you, and I taught you letters, and I never once told you that the reason you couldn't cry at funerals, the reason you watched other people love each other like a man watches weather through glass — I never told you because telling you meant admitting what I'd known since the day I signed my name next to yours. That I owned a piece of you. That the piece I didn't own was still owed to something under a mountain, and that if I ever moved against the court openly, they'd collect early."

The remembering road chose that moment to be cruel. It shimmered around them, and Lior felt it reach — the way it had all journey, sifting through them for memories to replay. But this time what surfaced wasn't theirs alone. It was Ombre's memory, given up by proximity, spilling into the air between them like ink into water:

A small child at a counting table, too big for the chair. A woman's hand guiding a small fist around a quill. *This is how you write your name, little ledger. L-i-o-r. See? Now no one can take it from you.*

The memory dissolved. Nobody spoke.

"That's the memory I paid at the tollhouse," Lior said finally. Their voice sounded far away to their own ears. "I gave up the memory of you teaching me letters, and I grieved it without knowing why. I stood in that tollhouse and wept over a hole in my head and I thought — I thought *this is what it's like, this is what everyone else feels all the time, no wonder I was always so bad at it.*" They laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "But it wasn't the tollhouse, was it. The tollhouse just took one more brick from a wall that was already half-built out of absence."

"Lior—"

"No. Let me finish. I need to finish, because I've spent nine years praying to a silent god about this exact feeling and calling it faith." Lior turned the contract over, as if something might be written on the back that would make it kinder. There wasn't. "Do you know what I thought I was? All those years? I thought I was *disciplined*. I thought numbness was competence. I built a whole self out of it — precise, invisible, unbothered. I was proud of it, Guildmaster. I was proud of being a person nothing could get its hooks into."

"And now?"

"Now I know it was never temperament." The words tasted like copper. "It was theft. Someone reached into me before I had a name for anything and took half of whatever I was supposed to be, and I've been balancing my books around the missing weight my entire life without once asking why the sums kept coming out light." They looked up. "Do you know what I felt tonight, on this road? Real fear. Real grief. Real—" their voice caught on it, "—real *fondness*, for two people I've known for weeks. Weeks, Ombre. And it's overwhelming. It's like standing in a river after a lifetime of drinking from cups. So tell me what I'm actually mourning here. Tell me what ten years with you would have felt like with a whole soul. What did I lose that I don't even have the capacity to imagine?"

Ombre didn't flinch. Lior had seen her face down everything the trade could offer, and she didn't flinch now, but her eyes went bright and wet in the way of someone who has decided crying is a debt she'll pay later, privately, with interest.

"You want an inventory," she said. "Fine. Here's mine. When you were seven, you fell out of the pear tree behind the guildhouse and broke your arm, and you didn't cry — and I told myself, *good, the child is tough.* When you were twelve, the ferryman who used to slip you honey-cakes drowned, and you filed his death in the daybook like a shipment lost at sea — and I told myself, *good, the child is steady.* Every time, Lior. Every flat affect, every missing tear, I found a way to file it as a virtue, because the alternative was opening that contract and reading the rider and knowing I was raising a half-emptied child and calling it character." Her breath shuddered. "You want to know what you lost? You lost the version of you that would have cried in the pear orchard. That would have wept for the ferryman. That would have — I don't know. Loved someone badly and loudly at sixteen instead of not at all at thirty. I can't give you the inventory of what was taken. No one can. That's the cruelty of it. You mourn a person who never got to exist."

The road was quiet. Somewhere above them, muffled by a hundred fathoms of stone, the mountain hummed its low note — Emberless's fire, guttering. Lior felt the sound in their teeth.

Nim stepped forward. "For what it's worth," she said to Lior, gruff and gentle at once, "the version of you that exists is the one who bargained a dragon-keeper out of despair in one conversation. The one who walked into a vault full of stolen souls and came out angrier instead of afraid. Half a soul, maybe. But it's the half that chose to come down here." A pause. "And the other half is sitting in jars under a throne. We're going to go get it."

Sefa, who had been silent at the edge of the lamplight, adjusting spectacles in the way that meant a thought was assembling itself, finally spoke. "There's a scholarly point worth making. Grim, but useful." They tapped the contract in Lior's hands. "*Pending collection at centennial date.* Partition contracts like this — and there are precedents, rare ones, in the pre-Imperial codices — are structured as *retained interests*. Which means the court doesn't hold your soul whole. It holds a claim. And claims, Lior, are exactly the kind of thing you know how to break."

Lior looked down at the vellum. At their own name, small and sold. And something moved through them that was not quite hope and not quite rage but had the heat of both — the feeling, unfamiliar and enormous, of wanting something *for oneself*. Not survival. Not duty. Wanting.

"I spent nine years thinking Veth had gone quiet because I was unworthy of answer," they said quietly. "Turns out the god's voice was stolen and half my heart was in a jar, and everyone who knew said nothing out of love." They met Ombre's eyes. "I'm not forgiving you tonight. I want that entered plainly. But you crossed a country to bring me the truth, and you're standing in front of a door that leads to the people who did this, and I find that I—" the new feeling again, vast and clumsy, "—I don't want to do the next part without you. That's what a whole soul does, isn't it? Wants people in the room."

Ombre made a sound that was half laugh, half wound. "Yes," she said. "That's exactly what it does."

Above them, the mountain's hum stuttered.

Everyone heard it. The low note faltered — caught — resumed a half-tone thinner than before. Nim's head snapped upward, and the color drained from her face with a speed that made Lior's new-won heart clench.

"That's not guttering anymore," she whispered. "That's *failing*. The fire's failing, and we're still on the wrong side of the door."

Far off, deeper in the dark beneath the road, came a sound none of them would forget for the rest of their lives: the ringing of a great bell, once, twice — and then voices. Hundreds of them. Rising through the stone like water through cracks.

The centennial collection had begun.
