## 20. The Centennial Collection

Nim Ashvael had spent her whole life learning to read a dragon's fire the way other people read weather.

Her mother had taught her before she could write: *the flame speaks first, girl, and it never lies.* A steady gold meant contentment. A blue-white hiss meant pain. A green flicker at the edges—rare as frost in summer—meant the old one was dreaming of the sky. Nim had catalogued every color Emberless could make, every stutter and surge, the way a priestess memorizes liturgy. She had thought herself fluent.

She had never seen this color before.

It was the color of a candle in a room where all the air was being slowly taken away. A thin, apologetic orange, sunk so deep in the dragon's throat that it barely gilded the scales around her jaw. It made Emberless look hollowed, like a lantern with nothing left inside but the memory of light.

And beneath her—the whole vast city of the Court Beneath the Mountain had turned its face upward to watch it happen.

* * *

They had come down through the glass roots three days ago, and Nim still hadn't stopped flinching at them. The roots grew everywhere, thick as temple pillars, translucent as spun sugar, and they carried light the way veins carry blood—a slow pulse of pale gold traveling up from somewhere deep, toward the throne hall above. When she pressed her palm against one, she could feel a rhythm. Not a heartbeat. Something more patient than that. Something counting.

Lior had touched one too, on their first night, and gone very still, and then very quiet, and said only: "It's keeping time."

Nim hadn't asked what it was keeping time *for*. Some questions you save until you have the strength to survive the answers.

Now she stood on a balcony of black stone—one of hundreds cantilevered from the cavern walls like swallows' nests—and watched the collection begin.

The nobles came out at dusk, or what passed for dusk down here, when the great chandeliers of living crystal dimmed themselves on schedule. They emerged from their towers in slow processions, wearing masks of porcelain and silver leaf, faces smooth as eggs behind them. Nim had learned quickly not to look for eyes. There were eyes, but they were set too far back, the way pearls sit in the flesh of an oyster, and meeting them felt like falling into a well that had been waiting a long time for someone thirsty.

The nobles gathered in the great plaza below, and the servants brought out chairs.

That was the detail that would stay with Nim, she knew, longer than any horror. Not screaming. Not chains. Chairs—hundreds of them, arranged in perfect concentric rings facing the center of the plaza, each one cushioned in wine-dark velvet, each one angled just so, because the court believed even its atrocities deserved good sightlines.

Into the center of those rings, the servants led the people.

They came from every quarter of the city. Nim watched them emerge from doorways she'd passed a hundred times without wondering what happened behind them: the lamplighters with their long brass poles, the bakers who sold bread that tasted faintly of honey no matter what flour they used, the gardeners who tended the glass-root orchards, the laundresses, the cobblers, the children who ran messages between towers for copper coins that never tarnished. The servants of the court. The ones who had smiled at her in the market, who had shown her where to buy lamp oil, who had told her the best route to the dragon's caldera and warned her which staircases creaked.

They walked to the chairs and sat down in them. Nobody forced them. That was the worst part. They walked the way people walk to their own weddings—dazed, obedient, dressed in their finest clothes, because someone had told them tonight was an honor.

A woman near the front wore a shawl Nim recognized from the spice stall. She folded her hands in her lap like a child waiting for a story.

Above them all, Emberless's fire guttered.

* * *

"She's dying faster," Nim said. Her voice came out flat, which was how grief sounded when she refused to let it be anything else. "I told you the rhythm would change once we were inside the mountain. The proximity feeds the containment—or starves it, I don't—I don't know which way it works anymore."

She was talking to Lior and Sefa in the small chamber the court had given them. Given. That word again. Three beds with clean linens, a washbasin, a window of thick glass looking out onto the plaza. Hospitality, the steward had called it, bowing with a hand over his heart. *Guests of the centennial.*

Sefa sat cross-legged on the floor with the codex open across her knees, surrounded by her own handwriting—translation notes, marginalia transcriptions, little maps of the text's argument drawn in ink that shimmered faintly, because everything down here shimmered faintly, because everything down here was soaked in stolen light. She didn't look up. "The codex says the collection takes nine nights. Nine rings of chairs, nine processions, nine pourings." Her finger traced a line of cramped century-old script. "'On the ninth night the table is full, and the fire may rest.'" She looked up then, and her face was the face of a scholar who has finished translating something and wishes desperately she hadn't. "That's what it says. *May rest.* Like it's a kindness. Like the dragon gets to retire."

"Nine nights," Nim repeated. She did the arithmetic without wanting to. Six days since they'd arrived. "Three nights left."

"Two and a half," said Lior, from the window. "The first pouring already happened. Look."

Nim looked, and wished she hadn't, and looked anyway, because Emberless had taught her that watching was a kind of duty.

In the center of the chair-rings stood a basin the size of a wagon bed, wrought from the same black stone as the balconies, and into it the masked stewards were pouring something from tall glass vessels. It caught the chandelier-light strangely—not liquid, exactly, but not smoke either. It moved like mercury remembering it used to be water. And as it poured, the nearest seated figures began to lean back in their chairs, gently, the way wheat leans when wind passes over it, and their outlines went soft at the edges, and the light that lived behind every living face—that light Nim's mother had called *the tenant*, the thing that rents the body and pays its rent in warmth—rose out of them in thin bright threads and drifted down into the basin.

Nobody screamed. One old man sighed, deeply, the sigh of a man setting down a heavy load after a long road. Then he sat straighter than he had in years, and smoother, and younger-looking, and his eyes had the far-back pearl-set quality of the nobles now, and a steward stepped forward and hung a small porcelain mask around his neck on a cord, the way you'd hang a medal on a veteran.

"That's not death," Nim whispered. "Tell me that's not death."

"It's worse," Sefa said quietly. "Death leaves something behind to bury. This—" She closed the codex. "This is a withdrawal. Look at the ledger logic of it. They're not killing the servants. They're calling in a debt the servants never knew they owed. Their wages, their bread, their untarnishing coins—it was all paid out of their own principal. A hundred years of compound interest, collected tonight."

Below, the woman in the shawl rose from her chair. She moved beautifully now, gracefully, with the unhurried poise of the court, and took her place among the stewards without being asked. Her body walked away carrying none of her.

Nim turned away from the window and put both hands flat against the wall, because the room seemed to tilt, and the wall at least was honest about standing still.

* * *

She went up alone, two hours later, because there are things you cannot do in company.

The climb to the caldera was carved into the cavern's ceiling—an inverted mountain, the court built beneath it, and Emberless coiled in the crater of stone overhead like a moon that had fallen and decided to stay. Nim knew the path by heart now, though she'd only walked it twice; her mother had walked it every day for forty years, and some knowledge lives in the blood whether or not the blood remembers why.

The heat hit her halfway up. Faint. That was the wrong word for it—faint was for ladies and smells—but there was no better one. Where the caldera air should have pressed against her skin like a held breath, warm enough to taste of sulfur and hot iron, it merely *suggested* warmth, the way a letter suggests a voice.

Emberless opened one eye when Nim crested the rim.

The eye was the size of a cartwheel, amber shot through with veins of cooling grey, and it regarded Nim with the terrible patience of a creature who has watched empires be born, flourish, and forget themselves. When Nim was small, that gaze had frightened her. Now it was the only thing in this entire mountain that felt honest.

"You're worse," Nim said. No preamble. Emberless hated preamble; it wasted breath, and breath was fuel now.

*I am,* said the dragon, and her voice was not sound so much as heat shaped into meaning, arriving directly behind Nim's sternum. *You count well, keeper's daughter.*

"Don't do that. Don't be polite. Polite is what they do down there." Nim dropped to her knees on the scorched stone beside the great jaw and pressed her palm flat against it, the way she'd done since she was six years old, the way her mother had done, the way her grandmother had done before the courts of the world forgot dragons existed. The scales were cool. Cool. Emberless's scales had been warm the day the dragon lay dying on a mountainside two centuries ago, and now they were *cool*, and Nim's eyes burned and she refused to let anything fall from them because dragons could smell self-pity and despised it.

"How much longer?"

The great eye turned toward the city below, toward the plaza where the second ring of chairs was being carried out by hands that used to belong entirely to the people using them.

*Three pourings,* Emberless said. *Perhaps four, if the hunger is patient. It is rarely patient in its hundredth year.*

"And when your fire goes—"

*You know what happens when my fire goes.*

"I want to hear you say it." Nim's voice cracked and she let it. "I've kept you fed for six years on scraps and prayer and guild coin. My mother starved herself feeding you. I want to hear the truth out loud, in words, from you, because everyone else in this mountain lies in complete sentences."

Emberless was silent a long moment. Below them, faintly, came the sound of the second procession assembling—hundreds of feet, shuffling politely, saying *thank you* to their stewards.

*They will tell you,* the dragon said at last, *that my fire is a prison. That I burn to keep the souls of the court contained, that when I die the prisoners escape. Your mother believed it. Her mother believed it. It is a tidy story, and tidy stories are how the powerful launder their cruelties.*

Nim went cold, in the middle of the caldera. "Then what—"

*My fire does not keep them in, child. It keeps the hunger* asleep. *There is a difference wide as the sky. A prison holds bodies. I hold back an appetite—a hundred years old, grown vast, fed on sips and trained to wait for feasts. While I burn, the court's hunger dreams. When I gutters out—* the eye closed, opened again, and Nim saw reflected in it the tiny lights of the plaza, going out one thread at a time *—it wakes. And it will not sip anymore. It will eat until the mountain is a clean-picked bone, and then it will climb, because appetite always climbs.*

"The souls in the basin—"

*Are not the feast. They are the seasoning. The* appetizer *course, taken nightly to stretch the wait. The true meal, the centennial collection, the reason your prophecy-named friend carries a date instead of a birthday—* the dragon's breath stirred Nim's hair, and it smelled of ash and, underneath the ash, of something older and colder, like a cellar where meat was once stored *—is written in a book your archivist carries. Ask her what the ninth night is* for. *Ask her why the court needed a soul marked from birth. Ask her what a table requires, when the guests have dined on servants for a century and grown bored of the vintage.*

Nim stood up. Her legs shook. She made them hold anyway.

"You should have told us this on the road."

*Would you have come faster?*

"Yes."

*Then I failed you, and I am sorry, and I am old, and sorry is the last coin in a dying creature's purse.* The eye slid shut again, but the voice remained, softer. *Your mother knew. In the end she knew, and she sent you anyway, because someone had to be inside the mountain when the fire failed, and she loved you too much to ask, and too little to stop you. Forgive her that arithmetic. It is the arithmetic of all keepers.*

Nim pressed her forehead to the cool scales, once, briefly, the way you kiss a sleeping parent.

"Hold on," she said. "Three nights. Hold on, old one. We're going to find the seam in this thing. Lior's found seams in contracts no one else could read. If the court runs on ledgers, then ledgers can be broken."

*Ledgers,* said Emberless, and there was something in the heat-voice that might, in a younger throat, have been laughter—or weeping. *Yes. Go and audit the hungry, keeper's daughter. Mind you bring your own scale.*

* * *

The chamber was empty when Nim got back down—no Lior, no Sefa, only the codex left open on the floor where Sefa had been working, weighted at the corners with four smooth stones, as if even a book needed guarding down here.

Nim was reaching to close it when the light changed.

She felt it before she saw it: the pulse in the glass roots slowing, deepening, shifting key. Through the window, the plaza blazed with new brilliance—the third ring of chairs filling, the basin running over, the chandeliers burning brighter than they had all week, drunk on fresh-poured light. And high above, visible even from here, the caldera's glow stuttered.

Stuttered. Like a heart skipping. Like a lamp remembering it was empty.

The whole city saw it. Nim watched the masked faces tilt upward in unison, hundreds of porcelain ovals catching the failing gold, and though masks cannot smile, the way the crowd went still—the way the stillness *settled*, comfortable, expectant, like diners hearing the kitchen door swing open—told her everything the masks wouldn't.

They weren't afraid of the dark coming.

They were hungry for it.

Nim looked down at the codex, at Sefa's frantic marginal notes, and one line leapt out at her, underlined twice, ink still wet:

*Ninth night = presentation of the Named Soul. The collection is not complete without the birthright entry. They are saving a place at the table for Lior.*

From somewhere deeper in the palace came the sound of a bell—single, silvery, patient—and beneath the bell, footsteps in the corridor outside their chamber, many feet, walking politely closer, stopping at the door.

A knock. Soft as a servant laying a napkin in a lap.

"Honored guests," said a voice through the wood, warm as honey and patient as hunger. "The Court requests the pleasure of the ledger-keeper's company. There is a matter of accounts we believe is finally... due."
