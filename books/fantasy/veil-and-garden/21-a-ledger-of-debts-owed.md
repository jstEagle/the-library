## 21. A Ledger of Debts Owed

The cell they gave us was beautiful, and that was the point.

Everything beneath the mountain was beautiful the way a snare is beautiful—silk over teeth, candlelight over glass roots that drank warmth from the air itself. Our quarters had been appointed with a care that made my skin crawl: a bed turned down, a ewer of water sweetened with something floral, a writing desk positioned precisely where the light from the root-lanterns fell best across paper.

They wanted me comfortable. They wanted me *writing*.

So I sat at the desk, and I wrote—but not what they hoped for.

* * *

Sefa had smuggled up three folios from the archive levels before our escort grew suspicious, tucked against her ribs beneath a borrowed court coat two sizes too large for her narrow frame. She laid them out on the bed now like a surgeon laying out instruments, and Nim sat cross-legged at the foot of it, whittling nothing in particular from a stick she'd carried up from the surface, because Emberless's daughter could not keep her hands idle any more than she could keep her heart quiet.

"Three contracts," Sefa said. "Out of thousands. But they're representative. A soul-sale from the second decade. A wage-bond from the fortieth. And—" her voice caught, just slightly, "—a tithe-agreement between the court and a god."

"Veth," I said.

"Veth." She smoothed the oldest document flat with both hands, reverent and furious at once, which was very Sefa. The parchment was vellum so fine it was nearly translucent, inked in a hand so controlled it looked printed. "Look at the seal work. Look at the *margins*. This isn't a contract drafted by nobles. This is a contract drafted by an accountant."

"I know the hand," I said quietly.

She looked up. "What?"

I turned the page toward the lantern-light. The numerals were old-fashioned—the long-tailed sevens, the barred zeros—but the structure underneath them, the way each clause nested inside the last like boxes within boxes, the way every obligation was paired to a consideration down to the smallest fraction—that was grammar I had been speaking since I was nine years old, when Ombre first put a quill in my hand and said, *Numbers don't lie, little ledger, but people who write numbers do.*

"This is the same school of drafting as the Gilded Ledger's founding charters," I said. "Older. Cruder. But the same bones. Whoever sold souls for this court learned their trade from the same tradition Ombre did. Maybe the same teacher. Maybe—" I stopped, because the thought arrived fully formed and cold, "—maybe the guild's oldest contracts came *from here*. Maybe we weren't founded. We were *exported*."

Nim's knife stopped moving. "That's grim."

"That's useful," I said. "Because if the court drafts like a guild, then the court can be audited like one."

* * *

Here is what no one tells you about being raised invisible: you learn to see everything, because seeing is the only power you're permitted. For twenty years I stood in the Gilded Ledger's counting room and watched merchants lie with their faces and tell the truth with their margins. I watched grief priced by weight and years priced by season. I watched Ombre sign things she shouldn't have and notarize things she regretted, and through all of it I kept the only discipline that never betrayed me: read the whole document. Read it twice. Read the parts written small.

The court beneath the mountain had grown lazy. Why wouldn't it? For a hundred years, no one had come down here intending to argue terms. Souls didn't negotiate. Gods didn't answer. The nobles behind their porcelain masks had spent a century signing their own paperwork without reading it, because reading was servant-work, and servants were furniture.

But I had been furniture too, once. Furniture remembers.

"Read me the recital clause," I said, and Sefa began to read aloud, her voice low and steady, while I closed my eyes and built the architecture of the thing in my head.

Every contract has a skeleton. Under all the velvet language about *perpetual conveyance* and *irrevocable transfer*, under the seals and the blood-ink and the witnesses in their masks, there is always the same spine: parties, consideration, term, condition, remedy. You find the spine, you can make the body dance.

"The parties," Sefa read. "'The Court Beneath the Mountain, herein styled the Buyer, its heirs and assigns perpetual—'"

"Heirs and assigns," I murmured. "Note that. Everything hinges on succession. Write it down."

"'—and the undersigned Seller, being of sound mind and free agency—'" Sefa paused. "Free agency. That's doing a lot of work in a sentence about selling someone's soul."

"It's load-bearing," I agreed. "Keep going."

"'—in consideration of the sum and substance enumerated in Schedule A, conveys unto the Buyer the whole and undivided essence, spirit, and continuing selfhood of the named subject, together with all rights, memories, affections, and futures appurtenant thereto, for the term of—'"

"Stop." My eyes opened. "*Appurtenant.*"

"What about it?"

"It's a property word. It means things attached to the thing sold. Memories, affections, futures—all attached to the essence, conveyed *with* it." I leaned forward, and the lantern-light moved across the vellum like water. "Which means the drafters considered the soul to be real property. Alienable. Transferable. Which means—Nim, give me your stick."

She handed it over, bemused. I held it up between two fingers.

"Property law has one great terror," I said, "and it isn't thieves. Thieves you can pursue. It's *defective title*. If the seller didn't own what they sold—if the sale was made by fraud, or forgery, or by someone without authority—then the buyer doesn't get the property. The buyer gets *nothing*. Not a share. Nothing. Every downstream transfer fails. Every heir and assign holds dust." I set the stick down on the vellum, dead center, like a pin through a moth. "And this court has been buying souls for a hundred years from exactly one seller."

* * *

We worked through the night, or what passed for night down there, where the glass roots dimmed on a schedule some ancient steward had decreed and no sun had ever ratified. Sefa fetched more folios—she had a talent for looking like she belonged wherever she stood, a talent I recognized because I'd built my whole life on it—and by the time the false dark deepened, we had eleven contracts spread across the bed, the floor, and, when Nim protested, Nim.

"They're all the same," she said, lying flat on her back amid the parchment with her arms folded behind her head. "Same clauses, same seals, same creepy little signatures. What are we even looking for?"

"A flaw," Sefa said.

"No." I shook my head. "Flaws are what amateurs look for. Flaws get patched. We're looking for a *dependency*—something the whole structure rests on that nobody thought to protect, because nobody ever attacked it before." I pulled the oldest contract toward me again, the one with the accountant's hand, and read the final page for the fourth time. "Condition precedent. There. Listen."

Sefa leaned in. Nim rolled onto her stomach.

"'This conveyance shall be and remain valid and binding upon all parties, heirs, assigns, and successors in interest,'" I read slowly, letting each word land, "'*for so long as the original instrument of sale bears the true and living signature of the Seller.*'"

Silence. Even the glass roots seemed to hush, their faint amber pulse slowing.

"True and living," Sefa repeated. Her voice had gone strange.

"True and living," I confirmed. "It's boilerplate. It's supposed to be meaningless—of course the signature is true; of course the seller was alive when they signed. It's there to prevent disputes about whether a contract was executed before or after death. Every guild charter has some version of it." I tapped the line twice. "But look at what it actually says. Not *was* living. *Is* living. Present tense. Continuous. The validity of the conveyance isn't fixed at the moment of sale—it *persists*, contingent on the seller's signature remaining true and living. Which means—"

"Which means if the seller dies," Nim breathed, sitting up so fast she scattered two folios, "every sale they ever made—"

"—becomes voidable. All of them. Simultaneously. A hundred years of purchases, unwound in a single stroke." I felt something move through my chest that I hadn't felt in years—not the numbness the court had left me with, not the careful competence I wore like armor, but something hotter and stranger. Hope, maybe. Hope with a column of figures attached. "And better. Read the next sentence."

Sefa read it, and her scholarly dread curdled into open delight, which on Sefa looked almost like mischief. "'Should the signature be found forged, or the Seller deceased, or the Seller's hand otherwise incapable of authenticating the instrument, the Buyer shall forfeit all consideration received and all subjects conveyed, and the subjects shall revert *to themselves*, entire and unencumbered.'"

"To themselves," Nim whispered. "Entire and unencumbered."

"Every soul in those jars downstairs," I said. "Every servant whose wages were their own siphoned life. Every noble's pretty immortality, purchased secondhand. All of it reverts. Automatically. By the court's own drafting, in the court's own ink, sealed with the court's own seal." I laughed—a short, disbelieving sound that didn't feel like mine, because so little of what I felt these days felt like mine, and I was learning to be gentle with myself about that. "They wrote their own execution order a century ago and filed it under routine boilerplate."

"But wait." Nim's brow furrowed, and she pointed at the vellum with her half-carved stick. "You said the seller's signature has to be *true and living*. Is he? The soul-seller. The one who wrote the prophecy, the one who sold—" she glanced at me, careful, the way she'd been careful with my name since the well gate, "—sold you."

"We don't know," Sefa said. "The codex calls him the First Quill. The court's servants speak of him like a founder. Retired, they say. *Retired.*" She let the word sit there, dripping. "No one retires from selling souls. Either he's preserved somewhere in this mountain, still signing, still validating every fresh horror—or he's dead, and the court has been maintaining his signature somehow. Forging continuance."

"Or both," I said quietly. "Preserved *and* dead. Kept just alive enough, just long enough, that his hand still counts as living. Courts like this don't let their assets expire. They embalm them."

The lantern-light guttered, steadied. Somewhere far above us—I thought of it as above, though direction meant little down here—Emberless was dying by inches, her fire thinning toward the centennial collection, and every hour we spent among the folios was an hour off her life and off the containment her fire provided. Nim knew it. I watched her know it, watched her jaw set the way it did when she was choosing patience over panic, and I loved her a little for it, in the fierce uncomplicated way I was only recently learning I was capable of loving anything.

"So we need him," she said. "The First Quill. Dead or alive, we need proof. His body, his hand, his grave—something we can carry to the Long Table and put in front of every masked face in this city."

"Not just show," Sefa said. "*Prove.* The clause says 'found forged' or 'found deceased.' Found. There has to be a finding—an authentication, witnessed. That's why they gave me access to the archives, I'd wager. That's why they let an archivist wander. They think scholarship is harmless." Her smile was thin and sharp as a blade's edge. "Scholarship is how I'm going to kill them."

"And I know where to start looking," I said.

They both turned to me.

"The contracts have to be stored somewhere. The originals, not copies—the clause turns on the *original instrument*. A court this obsessive about its own legitimacy keeps its founding documents close. Sacred. Buried." I spread my hands over the eleven contracts, over a century of theft rendered in beautiful, damning script. "And a man who signs himself into eternity doesn't get buried outside the walls. He gets enshrined. Tomb, crypt, reliquary—call it what you like. Somewhere in this mountain is the grave of the person who sold me, and if his hand is still attached to it—"

"If his hand is still attached to it," Sefa finished softly, "then we don't need him alive. We need him *authenticatable*. A corpse with a signature is a verdict waiting to be delivered."

* * *

We spent the last hour before the false dawn preparing, and preparing, for me, meant accounting.

I tore a blank leaf from the court's own fine stationery and drew columns down it in my neatest hand—the hand Ombre taught me, the hand that had survived every theft of my selfhood because it lived somewhere deeper than memory, somewhere in the marrow. Assets. Liabilities. Contingencies. On the asset side I wrote: *one voiding clause, universally applied; one archivist who reads marginalia like scripture; one dragon-keeper who does not know how to quit; one guild family, disavowed but not destroyed; one god, silent but perhaps not absent.* On the liability side: *a court that does not age, does not tire, and does not forgive; a fire failing overhead; a soul of my own, half-missing, held in a jar with my name on it.*

Under contingencies, after a long moment, I wrote: *Ombre.*

I didn't know yet what that entry meant. I only knew that when I thought of her racing down the mountain road with whatever ancient papers she'd deemed worth carrying, my chest did something complicated, and complicated was new, and new was probably good.

Nim watched me write. "You really love this," she said. Not mocking. Wondering.

"I love that it's *true*," I said. "Down here, everything is performance. Masks, courtesy, comfort, welcome. Numbers are the only language they can't lie in, because they built their whole empire on believing no one would ever check the math." I blew the ink dry. "They checked my name into their prophecy. They checked my soul into their vault. Fine. I'm checking them into *mine*."

* * *

The knock came at dawn-o'clock, soft and polite, the way everything in the court was soft and polite. A servant in gray livery, face smooth and eyes empty as unsold stock, bowed to inform us that the Court requested the pleasure of the new ledger-keeper's presence at morning audience, and that the Lady Vessarine especially hoped he had rested well.

Tell her I rested excellently, I almost said. Tell her I dreamed of clauses.

Instead I smiled the smile I'd practiced for twenty years in the Gilded Ledger's counting room—the smile of a man with nothing behind his eyes but competence—and said, "I'll be along directly."

As the door closed, Sefa slipped the eleven contracts back into their hiding places, and Nim pressed her carved stick into my palm—she'd finished it during the night, though I hadn't seen her finish. It wasn't a whistle, exactly, and it wasn't a key. It was a small figure of a dragon, wings folded, head raised, no bigger than my thumb.

"For luck," she said. "Emberless says luck is just debt that hasn't come due yet."

"Then we're owed a great deal," I said, and pocketed the dragon, and went up to lie beautifully to the beautiful people, carrying in my breast pocket the one thing their whole shining kingdom could not survive:

Their own signature, read closely.
