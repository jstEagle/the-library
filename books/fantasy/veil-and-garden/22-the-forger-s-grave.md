## 22. The Forger's Grave

The mountain had a pulse.

Sefa felt it first as a tremor in the soles of her boots, faint and arrhythmic, like a heart that had forgotten what it was counting. She had spent thirty years among documents and had learned to trust her body's small alarms—the prickle at the nape when a forgery sat too neatly in its frame, the dry mouth when a colophon lied. Her body was telling her now that they were walking down the throat of something alive, or something that had been alive so long it could no longer tell the difference.

"Slower," she said, and raised her lantern.

The tunnel had been glass once. That was the only way to describe it: the walls were root-shaped, branching, translucent as cooled honey, and behind them moved slow constellations of light—souls, she thought, or the memory of souls, drifting in their vessels like fish in dark water. Each time the mountain pulsed, the lights flared in answer, thousands of them, a tide of small attentions turning toward the surface and then away again.

Nim walked ahead with Emberless's ember cupped in an iron lantern-cage, and the little fire leaned toward the walls the way a dog leans toward a door it knows. "It doesn't like this," Nim said. Not a question. The dragon-keeper's daughter spoke to the ember the way other people spoke to horses—short, certain, expecting to be understood.

"It doesn't like *anything* down here," Lior said from the rear. They kept their voice level, ledger-flat, but Sefa had learned to read the mercenary the way she read marginalia: the meaning lived in what was *not* written. Lior had not slept since the vault. Their hands, usually so precise, kept making small unnecessary adjustments to their belt, their cuffs, the strap of their satchel—correcting errors no one else could see.

Sefa understood. She had spent three days decoding a dead woman's handwriting, and now her own hands would not stop moving either.

* * *

The codex had led them here. That was the phrase Sefa kept returning to, because it was more accurate than any alternative: not *they had followed the codex*, but *the codex had led*. Marguerin Vael—the previous soul-seller, the forger whose signature anchored every contract in the Court Beneath the Mountain—had written her confession in the margins of a prophecy, in a cipher keyed to guild accounting notation, hidden where only someone trained in double-entry despair would ever look. Sefa had wept over those margins, once, briefly, alone in the archive carrel at three in the morning. Then she had blown her nose, squared the pages, and gotten back to work, because grief without method was just noise, and noise had never freed anyone.

Vael's final entries described her own burial. *They will want my hand,* she had written. *A signature is only ink until it is attached to a will; they mean to keep mine willing past death. I have asked for the deep crypt, under the third root, where the glass grows thinnest. If anyone reads this: the hand remembers what it signed. Hands are honest longer than hearts.*

*The hand remembers what it signed.*

Sefa turned the sentence over as they descended, testing its weight like a coin bitten for gold. It was either a scholar's poetry or a literal instruction, and everything else Vael had written had turned out to be literal. The woman had forged contracts for sixty years—sales of names, years, griefs, souls—and somewhere in that long apprenticeship to cruelty she had developed a conscience like a scar: ugly, sensitive, and permanent. Her last act had been to hide the flaw in her own masterpiece inside the one book the court would never destroy, because destroying prophecy was bad for business.

"The third root," Sefa said aloud, mostly to hear a human voice against the pulse. "Vael said the glass grows thin there. We should be able to see the crypt from inside the root itself."

"And if the glass is thin," Lior said, "then whatever's on the other side can see us."

"Yes," Sefa said. "I'd counted on that."

Lior looked at her. In the ember-light their face was all planes and shadow, the face of someone who had been told, three days ago, that they had been sold before they could walk, and who had decided—with the terrible efficiency Sefa was coming to recognize as their defining trait—to treat their own soul as a line item pending audit.

"You *wanted* to be seen?"

"I wanted to be *witnessed*." Sefa lifted the lantern higher. "There's a difference, Lior. A document witnessed by no one can be altered by anyone. That's how the court has survived a century. Every crime committed in private, every contract sealed in the dark. Vael hid the truth in a margin because margins are where witnesses live." She touched the wall of glass-root as she said it, and the lights behind it drifted closer, curious, pressing their pale faces—no, not faces, suggestions of faces—against the inner curve. Watching. Remembering being watched.

Nim made a soft sound. "They're following you," she whispered. "The lights. They're following your hand."

Sefa looked down. Where her palm pressed the root, the drifting souls had gathered in a loose ring, and though they had no eyes she felt observed with an intensity that made her teeth ache. She thought of her catalogue—the list she had begun keeping, every freed soul to be named, dated, returned to the record—and thought: *this is what unlabeled things do. They crowd toward anyone holding a pen.*

She took her hand off the glass. The ring of lights held its position a moment longer, then dispersed, reluctantly, like a congregation after a sermon that hadn't ended.

* * *

They found the third root an hour later, and Sefa knew it before Nim's map confirmed it, because the glass here was wrong. Everywhere else the roots glowed warm amber, lit from within by their cargo of stolen light. This one was clouded, gray-veined, cold as a window in winter—and through its fogged curve, maybe two arm-spans beyond the glass, stood a door.

It was a modest door. That was the horror of it. Bronze, unadorned, set into living rock, with a lintel carved in letters so worn they might have been decorative: *MARGUERIN VAEL, WHO KEPT ACCOUNTS.* No dates. No epitaph. The court had buried its greatest criminal the way a merchant buries a spoiled ledger—in the cellar, upright, out of sight, but never destroyed.

"How do we get through?" Nim asked. She had her father's old pry-bar across her shoulders, both hands hooked over it, and she was staring at the glass the way she stared at weather: measuring it for what it would cost.

"We don't break it." Sefa crouched, setting her lantern on the stone, and drew from her satchel the tools of her second trade—not the archivist's brushes and knives but the burglar's kit Ombre had pressed on her at the guild gate with the words *you'll thank me, probably.* "Glass roots grow around things. Around doors, around bones, around anything the court wants kept but not seen. Which means there's a seam. There's always a seam where the growing stopped." She ran her fingers along the root's base where it met the floor, feeling for the join, the place where two kinds of forever met.

Lior knelt beside her. For a while neither spoke. The mountain pulsed; the gray glass did not answer it, and Sefa realized that was why the root was clouded—it was dead, or had been cut off from the flow of souls deliberately, starved so that nothing inside it would be nourished, nothing inside it would *grow*. A tomb inside a body, walled off from the body's blood.

"Here." Lior's fingernail found a hairline gap. "Cold air. Feel it?"

Sefa felt it: a thread of moving air, ancient and mineral-smelling, breathing out of the mountain's interior like the last exhale of a very patient corpse. She fitted the thinnest blade of her kit into the seam and worked it with the patience of thirty years spent opening books whose spines hated being opened.

The glass did not shatter. It parted—a whole curved pane swinging inward on a hinge of hardened root, silent as a page turning—and cold air rolled over them, carrying the smell of bronze polish, dried flowers, and something older underneath, something like the inside of a sealed jar.

Lantern-light entered ahead of them and found the crypt.

* * *

It was a small room, and it was full of writing.

That was Sefa's first coherent thought, and her second, and for several heartbeats her only one. Every surface bore script. The walls were covered in columns of careful, cramped, beautiful hand—contracts, drafts, revisions, crossed-out clauses, marginal notes in three alphabets. The ceiling was a diagram of the court above, drawn in ink that had gone brown with age, annotated with figures. Even the floor was inscribed, a spiral of text winding inward from the threshold like a drain, and at the center of the spiral—

At the center, on a bier of black stone, lay Marguerin Vael.

She had been dead a century and she did not look it. The court's preservation arts had been thorough: her skin was parchment-pale and smooth as vellum, her features composed in an expression of mild professional attention, as if she had died mid-audit and expected to resume. She wore the gray robes of a guild scrivener, and her hands were folded on her breast.

Her right hand had been taken off at the wrist and replaced.

Sefa saw it and could not stop seeing it. The left hand was flesh, aged, spotted, human. The right was a thing of jointed bronze and yellowed bone, articulated at every knuckle, the fingers curled in the exact posture of a writer gripping a pen—a pen that was not there. The stump of the wrist was banded in silver engraved with contract-script, binding metal to meat, and from the bands ran thin wires up the bier, along the floor, into the walls, into the veins of the mountain itself.

"They kept the hand," Nim breathed. She had gone gray around the mouth. "The whole hand. Why would anyone—"

"Because a signature needs a hand," Lior said. Their voice was very quiet. "And hers was the only one that made the sales valid. Chapter twenty-one of the master code: *all conveyances require the seller's living mark.*" A pause. "*Living.*"

Sefa stepped forward, and her boots rang on the inscribed floor, each footfall landing between lines of text, and she heard herself say, in the lecturing tone she used for first-year archivists, "Then the court has spent a hundred years committing fraud. Look at the wiring. Look at the—" She crouched at the bier's edge, close enough to see the bronze fingers' joints, the wear-marks on the index finger where a century of signing had polished the metal bright. "They've been *using* it. Moving it. Making it sign. Every renewal, every centennial clause, every new soul sold—they brought the contracts down here and moved a dead woman's hand across the page."

"That's why the contracts never lapsed," Lior said. Something was happening in their voice, a pressure building behind the flatness, a kettle beginning to sing. "That's why nothing expires. Nothing in that court has expired in a hundred years, because the seller never died. On paper." They looked at the bronze hand, and Sefa watched the mercenary's face do something complicated and terrible: recognition. *This,* the face said. *This is what was done to me. Taken apart and made to keep working.*

"She wanted us to find this," Sefa said. She tore her gaze from the hand and forced herself to read the walls instead, skimming the columns, the drafts, the desperate marginalia. "Read the floor spiral. It starts at the door. *If you have come this far, you already know what I sold. Do not forgive me; use me.*" Her throat tightened. "She left instructions. She left *everything*. The original sale templates, the authentication marks, the—here." Her finger stopped on a wall panel near the head of the bier, a section fresher than the rest, the ink still black. "The centennial collection order. Dated tonight. Signed—"

She didn't need to finish. The signature at the bottom of the order was Vael's, flowing and confident, and beneath it, in different ink, the countersignature of the masked buyer, and beneath *that*, in a third hand, neat and bureaucratic: *executed per standing instruction, hand applied by proxy.*

"Applied by proxy," Nim repeated. "Meaning someone comes down here. Regularly. To work her hand like a—like a pump handle."

"Someone comes down here," Sefa said slowly, and the scholarly dread in her chest curdled into something harder, colder, better. "Which means someone has a key. Which means someone knows we're capable of standing exactly where we're standing." She straightened. "We don't take the hand."

Lior's head came around. "We *don't*—"

"We take a *cast*." Sefa was already unpacking her kit properly now, laying out the plaster, the oil, the fine trowel, her movements quickening with the particular joy she had only ever felt in archives, the joy of a problem resolving into method. "If we take the hand itself, the court knows instantly—the wire goes slack, the next contract fails to sign, and they seal the tunnels with us inside. But a cast? A cast takes ten minutes. And a cast, pressed to a page with the right ink, in the right hand, by someone who has spent thirty years studying how forgeries fail—" She looked up at Lior, and let herself smile, and knew it was not a kind smile. "Chapter twenty-one says the seller's *living mark* validates a sale. Vael wrote me a loophole a century wide. If the mark on the founding contracts was applied by proxy—if the hand was dead when it signed—then every sale downstream of her signature is void. Not renegotiable. Void. Every soul in every jar. Including yours."

The silence that followed had texture. The mountain pulsed against the dead glass, and got no answer, and pulsed again.

"Including mine," Lior said at last. They said it the way people say words they've been chewing for days without swallowing. Then they crouched at the foot of the bier, eye-level with the bronze fingers, and said—to the hand, or to the woman it had belonged to, or possibly to the silent god whose name they carried like a debt—"You kept accounts your whole life and they made your corpse keep them after. I'm going to close your books. All of them."

The ember in Nim's cage-lantern flared, sudden and orange, throwing all their shadows huge against the written walls, and for one moment the crypt's thousand columns of text seemed to lean inward, listening.

Sefa mixed the plaster with hands that did not shake. She had catalogued atrocities before, in paper form; she had believed she understood what bureaucratic evil looked like. But understanding a thing in a document, she reflected, pressing oil into the creases of a dead woman's forged fingers, was like understanding drowning from a diagram. The real thing had a temperature. It smelled of bronze polish and dried flowers. It wore a mild professional expression and waited, patiently, to be useful again.

She worked fast. Oil, plaster, patience; the cast took shape finger by finger, a white negative of a century of crimes. Nim kept watch at the glass door, the ember low and wary. Lior stood over the bier the entire time, arms folded, a sentinel of one, refusing to look away from the preserved face—as if looking away would be one more theft from a woman who had already been robbed of her grave's silence.

The cast set. Sefa lifted it free with a sound like a sigh, wrapped it in wool, and stowed it against her spine where a fall couldn't reach it.

And that was when the lights outside the gray glass went out.

All of them, at once—the drifting constellation beyond the root snuffed like candles pinched between wet fingers—and in the sudden dark Nim said, very quietly, "Someone's coming down the tunnel. More than one. And they're not hurrying."

Of course they weren't, Sefa thought, slinging her satchel, her heart hammering a scholar's rhythm—steady, steady, cite your sources. They never hurried. Why would they hurry? The hand still lay on its bier. The contracts still held. As far as the court knew, nothing down here had changed at all.

Except that in the wool at her back, a dead forger's hand waited to sign its final, unwitnessed, unforgivable entry—and the three of them had perhaps forty steps of inscribed floor between themselves and the polite, tireless footsteps closing in from the dark.
