## 23. Witnessed by Glass

The tomb did not look like a tomb.

That was the first wrongness, and Lior had learned—over nine years of unanswered prayers and one week of unraveling truths—to trust wrongness the way other people trusted instinct. A proper grave announced itself. It wore its grief on the outside: black stone, carved names, offerings left to rot in honest rain. This place wore nothing. The passage opened off a glass-root tunnel where the walls pulsed faintly with light, like veins seen through skin, and then simply widened into a chamber of white marble so clean it seemed embarrassed by the idea of dust.

No name above the door. No dates. Just a single line of script cut into the lintel in a hand so precise it could only have been paid for:

*Here lies the Hand That Signed.*

"They kept the signature," Sefa whispered behind them. Her voice had gone thin, the way it did when her scholarship collided with something her scholarship couldn't hold. "I thought—I assumed it was a figure of speech. *The hand that signed.* But they mean it literally. They preserved the hand."

"Archivists," Nim said softly, "are always surprised when the horror is literal."

Lior didn't answer. Their attention was fixed on the center of the chamber, where a plinth of smoked glass rose from the floor like a tooth pulled up through the mountain's jaw. On it, under a bell jar of clear crystal, lay a human forearm and hand—desiccated, brown as old parchment, the fingers curled not in death's looseness but in a deliberate, elegant curve. The posture of a man mid-signature. Preserved for a century so that, presumably, he could go on signing.

*One impossible thing,* the bill of sale had said. Lior had been the impossible thing. And this had been the seller: a man who sold souls out of his own living hand until someone decided the hand itself was worth more than the rest of him.

"The contracts," Sefa said, already moving toward the plinth with the hungry reverence she usually saved for marginalia. "If the clause holds—if the original seller's signature is forged or dead—then we need proof. We need to compare the hand against the signatures in the master ledger's antecedents. If I can document that every sale after his death was executed in his name but not by his hand—"

"Then every sale voids." Lior heard their own voice come out flat, ledger-flat, and was grateful for it. Flatness was a floor you could stand on. "Every soul in those jars walks free."

"Every soul," Sefa agreed. She set down her satchel, drew out her lenses, her stylus, her kit of measuring threads. Her hands were shaking, but they were shaking *precisely*, which Lior had come to understand was simply how Sefa shook. "Give me an hour. Maybe less. The preservation work is good but it's arrogant—they wanted the hand legible, which means they wanted it comparable, which means—"

Which meant they'd never imagined anyone would use it against them. Lior finished the thought silently and turned to watch the tunnel mouth.

Nim stood there instead, arms crossed, ember-lantern at her feet throwing her shadow long and wavering across the marble. Above them—far above, past forty levels of candlelit nobility and glass roots and polite, tireless servants—the dragon Emberless was dying by degrees, and everyone in this mountain could feel it the way you feel a storm coming in your teeth. The court's music had gone slightly sharp tonight. The servants walked slightly faster. Somewhere in the vaults below the throne, thousands of jars sat in their thousands of niches, waiting for the centennial collection that would begin when the last fire went out.

"We should hurry," Nim said, unnecessarily. Everyone knew. No one said it again.

* * *

Sefa worked the way water works—finding every crack, filling every gap, patient beyond all reason. Lior held the lens-light for her while she measured the dead hand's finger-lengths against wax impressions she'd lifted from the archive beneath the throne. Nim kept watch at the tunnel mouth, and twice reported footsteps passing—servants, gliding rather than walking, their candlelight bending oddly around them as though even flame preferred not to touch them.

It was on the third pass that the footsteps stopped coming.

They simply stopped, all at once, the way a clock stops—not winding down but *deciding*. And into that silence came a sound Lior had heard only once before, in the Long Table hall: the soft, silken whisper of a mask being adjusted.

"They're here," Nim breathed.

"I know," Lior said.

"How do you know?"

Because the air had changed texture. Because the glass roots in the walls had dimmed, the way a crowd dims when someone important enters a room. Because nine years of praying to a silent god had taught Lior to feel absence the way sailors feel weather—and this absence had a shape now, a direction, an intention.

"Behind the plinth," Lior said quietly. "Both of you. Let me do the talking."

"You don't know what you're going to say," Nim protested.

"No," Lior agreed. "But whatever it is, it will be accurate. That's the only weapon I've ever needed."

She went, reluctantly, drawing Sefa with her, and Lior turned to face the tunnel mouth alone just as the noblewoman entered.

She was tall in the way furniture is tall—built for a room, proportioned to an architecture. Her gown was the color of deep water and moved without sound. Her mask was porcelain white, featureless except for two slits of darkness where eyes should be, and yet Lior had the immediate, vertiginous certainty that behind it, the woman was smiling.

"Lior Oyelaran," the noblewoman said. Her voice was warm. That was the worst part. It was genuinely, skillfully warm, the voice of someone greeting family at a door. "You have your mother's habit of standing between danger and the people she loved. She never learned to stop doing it either."

The words went through Lior like a blade finding a gap in armor they hadn't known they were wearing.

"You knew my mother."

"I *paid* for her," the woman said gently, "in a sense. Or rather—I paid for what she gave away. Come. Look at me properly. You've spent your whole life being looked at by no one; allow yourself one moment of being seen."

Lior did not move. "You bought an infant."

"I bought a *life*," the woman corrected, and there was something almost tender in the correction, which made it obscene. "Your mother came to the Well Gate with a child in her arms and collectors at her back, and she had one impossible thing to trade, and she traded you. Do you imagine I don't know what that cost her? I watched her sign. I watched her hands shake. I remember—" the mask tilted, a small, intimate gesture, "—she sang to you while she waited. A lullaby in a language neither of us spoke. You stopped crying when she sang. I have thought about that, over the decades, more times than I can account for."

*More times than I can account for.* Even her tenderness was bookkeeping. Lior felt something hot rise in their throat and forced it down, forced themselves to stand still, ledger-still, because the alternative was to cross the marble and do violence they couldn't afford.

"Why are you telling me this?"

"Because you deserve to know you were loved." The woman took a step closer. The crystal bell jar caught her reflection and fractured it into a dozen pale ghosts. "Because you've spent your life believing you were *sold*, and there is a difference—a vast, ledger-deep difference—between being sold and being *given up*. Your mother didn't price you. She saved you the only way the world allowed her to. And I—" another step, and now Lior could smell her perfume, something floral gone slightly to rot, like a garden behind a locked gate, "—I have kept that memory safe for a hundred years. I can give it to you. All of it. Her face. Her voice. The song. Every detail, perfectly preserved, because we keep things *perfectly* here, Lior. That is what we are *for*."

"And the price?" Lior asked, because there was always a price, because price was the native language of this entire mountain.

The masked woman spread her hands—an open, generous gesture, a merchant displaying empty palms.

"No price," she said. "A gift. Freely given, freely received. Just walk out of this room with me. Leave your friends their hour with the dead man's hand. Leave the ledger, the prophecy, all these grim little crusades to people who enjoy them. Come upstairs. There is a chair that fits you. There is a table where you would be *expected*. There is a whole court that has been waiting a century for you to come home, and I—" the voice dropped, went soft, went true in a way that was somehow more terrible than any lie, "—I would very much like to hear that song again. I have only ever heard it once. Isn't that a cruel thing? To carry a melody for a hundred years with no second verse?"

* * *

Later—much later, in the version of events Sefa would write down and Nim would refuse to discuss—Lior would try to explain what happened inside them during those minutes, and fail.

Because the trap wasn't a trap. That was the genius of it. A trap you could see, could brace against, could refuse on principle. This was worse. This was a door opening onto a room Lior had been furnishing in secret their entire life without ever admitting the room existed: the room where someone remembered them. Where their existence predated the Gilded Ledger, predated Ombre's careful lessons, predated the meticulous invisibility they'd built like armor over an infant-shaped wound. Someone had seen them before they were anyone. Someone had heard them cry and sung to them and grieved.

And this woman held the only copy.

Lior thought of the tollhouse, of the memory of Ombre teaching them letters—paid away, gone, mourned without knowing why. They had already lost one parent-shaped memory to this mountain's arithmetic. It was standing right here offering to sell the others back.

"That's not a gift," Lior said at last, and their voice came out steadier than they had any right to expect. "That's collateral. You're asking me to sign myself over and calling the signature a homecoming."

"I'm asking you to stop fighting," the woman said, "and start belonging. There is no cruelty in that. There is no clause. There is no fine print. There is only a chair, and a song, and a hundred years of waiting."

"There's a vault full of jars," Lior said. "There are thousands of people in niches downstairs with labels on them like entries. Whatever you're offering, it's priced in those."

Something shifted behind the mask. Not anger—Lior had expected anger—but a kind of weary patience, the patience of a creditor who has explained terms many times.

"Those souls were *purchased*," the woman said. "Fairly. Lawfully. By contract, witnessed and sealed. We are not thieves, Lior. We are *holders*. When the collection comes, we will honor every obligation. And when it is done, the court will endure, and you could endure with it—fed, honored, remembered. Or you can stay here in the dark with a dead man's hand and two friends who cannot save you, and be collected like all the rest."

"There it is," Lior said quietly. "The invoice."

"There it is," she agreed, almost kindly. "Choose."

And Lior looked at her—at the porcelain mask, at the fractured reflections in the bell jar, at the preserved hand curled forever mid-signature between them—and understood, finally, with total clarity, what this room actually was. Not a tomb. A *showroom*. The hand was displayed the way merchants display their finest sample: proof of craft, promise of quality. *Look what we can hold onto. Look how long we keep things.*

His mother's song. His mother's face. Kept here too, somewhere, in some jar or scroll or polished corner of a perfect mind—for exactly this purpose. For exactly this night. Weaponry, curated over a century, waiting for the child to grow up and come looking.

"You remember her," Lior said slowly. "Do you remember what she said when she signed?"

The mask went very still.

"Because I do," Lior lied—or half-lied; the road had shown them fragments, implanted memories tangled with real ones, and somewhere in that tangle a woman's voice saying words they couldn't verify and refused to surrender. "She didn't sell me. She *filed a complaint*. Right there at the Well Gate, in front of you. She told you someone would come back for this. She told you the debt doesn't close just because the debtor dies. Didn't she?"

The silence stretched. Behind the mask, behind a hundred years of perfect keeping, something flickered—doubt, or its expensive imitation.

"You're guessing," the noblewoman said.

"I'm an accountant," Lior said. "Guessing is just auditing you haven't finished yet."

From behind the plinth, Sefa made a small sound—the sound of a scholar who has just found the thing. Lior heard it and did not turn around, did not let their eyes move one inch from the mask, because the moment they did, the woman would look too.

"Sefa," Lior said, without turning, without breathing differently, "tell me what you found."

"The signatures," Sefa said, and her voice rang with terrible, beautiful certainty. "Every sale in the antecedent ledgers—the hand-position matches. The pressure marks match. The ink flow matches. It's the same hand, Lior. The same *exact* hand, on documents dated forty years after this man died." She stepped out beside Lior, holding up the measuring thread like a verdict. "They forged a dead man's signature for a century. Every contract in this mountain is void. Every single one—including yours."

The noblewoman did not move. Did not speak. But the temperature of the room changed, the way it changes when a fire goes from warmth to threat, and Lior understood that the warm voice, the tender memories, the mother's song—all of it had been the opening position of a negotiation that had just collapsed.

"You should run," the woman said, and the warmth was gone from her voice now, leaving something older and hungrier underneath, something with a hundred years of patience in it and no patience left at all. "The collection begins at moonrise. The dragon is guttering. And everything in this mountain that is owed—" the mask tilted toward Lior, and behind the porcelain slits, for just a moment, Lior saw eyes that had forgotten how to blink, "—will be *collected*."

Glass roots flared along the walls, bright and blinding. Somewhere far above, a note of the court's music snapped clean off.

Nim's hand found Lior's shoulder. Sefa clutched her evidence to her chest like scripture. And the noblewoman stepped aside—not fleeing, merely yielding the doorway with terrible courtesy—as three thieves walked out of the showroom of a stolen childhood, carrying the only thing in the mountain the court truly feared:

Proof that the debt was never valid at all.
