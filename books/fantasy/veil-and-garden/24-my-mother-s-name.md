## 24. My Mother's Name

The tomb smelled of cold stone and old ink.

That was the first thing I noticed—before the preserved hand in its reliquary of glass, before the candlelight that burned without flickering in air that did not move, before the masked nobles arranged along the walls like ornaments on a shelf someone had stopped dusting a century ago. It was the smell. Ink and stone and something faintly sweet beneath it, like fruit going soft in a bowl no one had emptied since I was an infant.

Sefa stood at my left shoulder with her satchel clutched to her chest like a shield. She had not let go of it since we entered the mountain, and I suspected that if we died down here, they would have to pry the codex fragments from her fingers one by one, and she would haunt them for it. Nim stood at my right, close enough that I could feel the heat coming off her skin—the dragon-keeper's inheritance, Emberless's warmth banked in her blood like coals in ash. In this place of glass roots and candle-lit stillness, she was the only living fire.

And between us, on a plinth of black marble, sat the signing hand.

It was exactly what Sefa had promised it would be, and somehow worse. A human hand, severed at the wrist, preserved in some alchemy I did not want to understand—skin still supple, nails still trimmed, the fingers curled around a ghost of a quill. The original soul-seller's hand. The hand that had written my name into a bill of sale when I was three days old. The hand whose signature, if forged or proven dead, could unravel every contract the court beneath the mountain had ever signed.

*Dead,* I reminded myself. *Proven dead. That is all we need.*

"You came," said the noble in the silver mask.

She had been waiting for us—I understood that now, the way one understands a trap only after stepping fully into it. She sat apart from the others on a chair of woven glass, her mask plain where theirs were ornate, her gown the color of deep water. When she rose, the candlelight bent toward her, and I felt the weight of her attention settle over me like a ledger being opened.

"Vessarine," she said, and offered her name the way one offers a blade hilt-first. "I bought you."

The words landed in the silence like coins dropped down a well. I heard them fall a long way before anything answered.

"You'll forgive me," I said, and was proud of how steady my voice came out, "if I don't curtsey. I wasn't told I'd been purchased until four days ago. The paperwork was irregular."

Something moved behind the silver mask—not quite a smile, but the space where a smile might once have lived, worn smooth by centuries of not using it. "Irregular," she repeated. "Yes. Your guildmaster would say the same. She has your guild's habits, doesn't she? Deflection as armor." She took a step closer, and the sweet smell thickened. "You have her hands, though. Not hers. Your mother's. I remember them."

The floor did not drop out this time. I had learned, in the last four days, that there were worse sensations than falling. This was one of them: standing perfectly still while the past reached up through the stone and closed its fingers around your ankles.

"You knew her," Nim said quietly. Not a question. Nim Ashvael did not waste questions; she spent them like a keeper spends feed, only when the animal needed it.

"I knew everyone, once." Vessarine turned, and her gown whispered against the marble. "That is the curse of not dying. You accumulate people the way a river accumulates silt, and then the river changes course and leaves you full of things that used to be alive." She looked back at me over her shoulder. "Your mother came to the Well Gate on a night much like this one. Rain, if you can believe it. We do not get rain here. She carried you wrapped in a shawl that had been dyed twice because the first color faded, and she bargained with me for six hours. Six hours. Do you know how long it has been since anyone bargained with me? They sign now. They are grateful to sign."

"What did she trade?" Sefa asked. Her voice was thin, scholarly, appalled—but she asked it anyway, because Sefa had never once in our acquaintance let horror stop her from taking notes.

Vessarine laughed. It was a terrible sound, lovely and hollow, like wind through the glass roots overhead. "Nothing. That is the tragedy your archivist is hunting for, little cataloguer. She traded nothing. I gave her the sale freely." The mask tilted. "Because I wanted what was inside you. And because—" the pause was small, but I heard it, the way I hear a mispriced entry in a column of correct ones— "because she asked so beautifully."

*Don't,* I told myself. *Don't lean toward it. You are a mercenary of the Gilded Ledger. You know every shape a hook can take.*

But my body leaned anyway. My whole life I had been a creature without roots—sold, resold, passed between ledgers like a coin too worn to read—and here was a woman who had watched my mother bargain for six hours in the rain, who remembered the color of a twice-dyed shawl, who could give me the one thing no guild contract had ever listed under my assets: where I came from.

"The hand," I said, and made my voice into a clerk's voice, flat and professional. "We're here for the hand. Its death voids the sales. That's the law of your own contracts, Vessarine. You wrote the cage; don't be surprised the bars fit you too."

"I wrote nothing." For the first time, something cracked in her voice—a hairline fracture in porcelain. "The seller wrote. I only ever bought. Do you imagine I wanted this?" She gestured, and the gesture took in the tomb, the walls of watching masks, the glass roots glowing their slow green above us like veins in a sleeping wrist. "Do you imagine any of us *wanted* it? We were hungry, and then we were fed, and then we could not stop being fed. That is all a court is, child. A hunger that learned table manners."

"Then starve gracefully," Nim said.

The candles guttered. Every one of them, all at once, down the length of the tomb, and in that half-second of darkness I felt the whole mountain inhale. Then the flames steadied, and Vessarine's voice came softer.

"It is not graceful, starving. It is loud, and it is cruel, and it eats the servants first because they are closest." She drew a breath she did not need. "But we are not here to discuss my appetite. We are here to discuss yours."

She crossed the remaining distance between us, and up close the silver mask was not featureless after all—it was engraved, so finely you would miss it unless you were a person who spent her life reading fine print. Names. Hundreds of names, worked into the metal in script smaller than prayer.

"I will give you her name," Vessarine said.

The world went very quiet. Even Sefa's breathing seemed to stop.

"Not a description. Not a memory. Her *name*—the one spoken over your cradle, the one the collectors erased from every register when they took you, the one that would let you find whatever remains of her line, wherever it survived. Names have weight, Lior Oyelaran. You of all people know that. You keep accounts of them." The mask inclined. "In exchange, you put down the pen. You walk out of this tomb. You leave the hand, and the codex, and the audit you've been building in that beautiful merciless head of yours, and you go home to your guild and live the long comfortable life of a woman who chose not to look."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then you leave with nothing. No name. No mother. Only the ashes of a prophecy and the company of the dying." Vessarine spread her hands, and I saw that they trembled—actually trembled, this ancient thing in its water-colored gown. "I am offering you mercy. I am even, I think, sincere about it. That should frighten you more than cruelty would."

It did frighten me. That was the worst part. Because I could feel the offer working its way into me like water into mortar, finding every crack that nine years of unanswered prayers and twenty-six years of careful invisibility had left in my foundations. *A name.* All my life I had been Lior—the short version, the portable version, the version that fit easily into other people's mouths and left nothing behind. Somewhere there was a longer word for me. A word my mother had chosen, syllable by syllable, out of everything language had to offer, and someone had taken it away before I was old enough to hold it.

Sefa's hand found my sleeve. "Lior." Just my name, a warning, a plea.

Nim said nothing. Nim was watching me the way she watched Emberless when the fire ran low—not with fear, but with the steady attention of someone who has decided that whatever happens next, she will be present for it.

I thought about the tollhouse. About the memory I had paid without knowing its worth—a woman's laugh, ink-stained fingers guiding mine through the shapes of letters, patience like a warm room. I had grieved that memory for two days without knowing why I grieved. And now I understood: Ombre had given me letters, but someone else had given me my first word. Someone had held me in the rain outside the Well Gate and bargained for six hours, and I did not know her voice, or her face, or her name, and I was never going to stop wanting those things. Wanting them was the oldest bone in my body.

Vessarine knew that. Of course she knew that. She had been buying souls for a hundred years; she knew precisely which door in a person opens most easily, and she had walked straight up to mine and knocked.

"All right," I heard myself say.

"Lior—" Sefa's grip tightened.

"All right," I said again, and stepped forward, and held out my hand. "Tell me her name."

The silver mask came very close. Behind its eyeholes there was darkness, and in the darkness, something that might have been pity. "Her name," Vessarine began—

—and Nim's hand closed on my shoulder.

Not hard. That was the thing I would remember afterward, in all the chaos that followed: how gentle it was. Just five fingers and a palm, warm as banked coals, settling onto the shoulder where no one had touched me in years except to shake my hand over a concluded deal. Warmth sank through the wool of my coat, through the shirt beneath, into skin that had forgotten it was allowed to be warm.

"No," Nim said.

One word. She didn't argue. She didn't plead. She simply said it the way she said everything—as a keeper stating a fact about fire. *This burns. Don't touch it.*

"She's giving me my mother's name," I said, and hated how young my voice sounded.

"She's giving you a leash with a name tied to the end of it." Nim's thumb moved once against my shoulder, a small unconscious motion, the way one soothes a spooked animal—or a friend. "You told me on the road. The road that remembers, remember? You said the court put memories in your head that weren't yours, and the cruelest part was that they *fit*. They fit so well you couldn't tell." Her eyes, when I turned to meet them, were the brown of hearthstones. "This fits too. That's how you know."

"She remembers the shawl," I said. "Six hours in the rain. Those details—"

"A hundred years old and she remembers the dye lot of a stranger's shawl?" Nim shook her head slowly. "Keepers learn this, Lior. Dragons hoard, and everything a dragon tells you about treasure is shaped like the treasure it wants. She wants you to walk away. Everything she says will be shaped like walking away." Her hand did not tighten. It did not need to. "Besides. You already have a family. You just haven't finished admitting it."

The words went through me like a blade going through wet paper—no resistance at all, all the way to the hilt.

*I resigned,* I thought. *Four days ago I put my resignation on Ombre's desk and walked out of the only home I ever trusted myself to want, because I believed I had to save myself alone, because I had never once believed anyone would help me carry it.* And yet here was Nim, who owed me nothing but a bargain struck in desperation, standing between me and the thing I wanted most in the world, saying *no* as if my soul were a fire she had been entrusted with. Here was Sefa, whose fingers were still knotted in my sleeve, who had followed me into a mountain on nothing but footnotes and fury. Here was Ombre somewhere behind us on the road, racing toward us with contracts and guilt and whatever half-truths she had carried for twenty-six years.

Blood was a ghost. Blood was a twice-dyed shawl in the rain, a story someone else remembered, a name I couldn't pronounce because I'd never heard it spoken aloud. But the hand on my shoulder was warm *now*. The grip on my sleeve was real *now*. Found family outweighed blood's ghost—not because blood meant nothing, but because the living will always outrank the dead, and I was done letting the dead do my accounting.

I looked back at Vessarine. "No," I said, and my voice came out level, and mine. "Keep the name. If my mother wanted me to have it, she'd have sewn it into the shawl." I swallowed. "Maybe someday someone gives it to me free. That's the only price I accept anymore. Freely given. Write it down—it's going to be guild policy."

For a long moment the tomb was silent except for the candles' hiss. Then Vessarine laughed again—that hollow, lovely sound—but this time there was something underneath it, something almost like respect, or grief, or both wearing each other's clothes.

"Oh, you are hers," she said softly. "Stubborn as the rain. Very well." The mask lifted, and behind us, along the walls, the other masks lifted too—a hundred faces turning in unison, and the movement had the smooth wrongness of things that had practiced being human for so long they had polished away the seams. "Then we return to less tender business. You came for the hand. You will not leave with it. And you will not leave at all, I'm afraid, because the collection begins tonight, and the court does so hate to begin a feast with empty chairs."

Above us, far above, through fathoms of stone, I felt it rather than heard it: a vast, weary exhalation. A fire, somewhere, guttering.

Nim's head snapped upward. Her face went white around the eyes. "Emberless," she breathed. "She's failing. *Now.*"

The candles began to die—one, then ten, then all of them, drowning the tomb in dark—and in the dark, all around us, the sound of a hundred masks smiling.
