## 25. Emberless Falls

Nim had spent her whole life learning to read a fire the way other people read faces.

Her mother had taught her before she could hold a poker: *the flame tells you everything, little ash, if you stop looking at it and start listening.* A fire that was hungry crackled high and thin. A fire that was content breathed slow, like a sleeper. A fire in pain hissed at its own edges and wept resin-tears of blue. And a fire that was dying—

A dying fire went quiet.

That was what frightened Nim most, standing on the obsidian gallery above the Court Beneath the Mountain with soot to her elbows and Emberless's last great heart shuddering beneath her palms. Not the dimming. She had watched the dimming for years, had measured it in spoonfuls and prayers, had fed the old dragon everything from pitch-pine to bottled lightning to—gods forgive her—the guild's expensive grief, which burned hotter than anything and smelled like someone else's funeral.

No. What frightened her was the quiet.

Emberless had stopped speaking three days ago. Not in words—she had never spoken in words, not to Nim, not since Nim's mother died—but in the language of heat, which was older and more honest. Her body was a country of warmth once, valleys and rivers of it, and Nim had navigated by it all her life. Now the rivers were drying. The warm places pulled back from Nim's hands like tide from a shore, gathering inward, hoarding themselves around the one small furnace behind the dragon's breastbone that still did the work of holding back the dark.

And the dark here was not empty. That was the thing no one outside the mountain understood. The dark beneath the Court was *crowded.*

Nim pressed her forehead against Emberless's scaled jaw. The scales had gone from banked-coal orange to a gray like old iron, and they were cold enough now that her skin stuck faintly to them.

"I know," she whispered. "I know. Hold on. Lior's close. They're doing their ledger-thing, tearing the whole rotten court apart line by line. You just have to hold on a little longer."

The furnace behind the dragon's breastbone flickered.

It flickered the way a candle flickers when someone walks past it—not wind, not draft, but *attention*. Something noticing. Something leaning closer from the other side of the flame.

Nim's stomach dropped. She had felt that flicker only twice before, both times when the court's collectors came too near the deep vaults where the soul-jars slept, both times followed by hours of Emberless burning wildly, spending herself recklessly, shoving the hunger back down into its glass prison.

But this time the flicker didn't pass. This time it stayed.

* * *

She heard the change in the city before she saw it.

Sound traveled strangely beneath the mountain. The Court was built inside a hollow vast as a swallowed cathedral, its streets grown from glass roots—translucent veins of mineral that the founders had coaxed down through the living rock until the whole cavern glittered like the inside of a lantern. Sound came down those roots from the world above sometimes, rain and birdsong and once, memorably, a wedding, so that the entire court had paused in its candlelit streets to listen to vows spoken by strangers under an open sky none of them would see again.

What came down the roots now was screaming.

Not the good kind of screaming, if there was such a thing—not the theatrical despair the nobility wore like perfume at their endless dinners, not the exquisite boredom of people who had not aged in a hundred years and had run out of ways to be interesting to each other. This was raw. Servants' voices. Kitchen voices. The voices of people who worked the candle-vats and the root-gardens and the long tables, who kept the court beautiful for wages that Nim now understood had been paid in something far worse than coin.

Nim ran.

She took the keeper's stair two steps at a time, the spiral cut into the cavern wall that wound from the dragon's caldera down into the upper city, and as she descended the light changed around her and told her everything.

The glass roots glowed. That was their nature; they drank Emberless's fire and carried it through the city like blood through a body, and for a century they had burned a steady amber, warm and constant, the color of safety. Now they pulsed. Amber, then rose, then a sickly white, then amber again, stuttering like a heart losing its rhythm. Shadows swung across the streets in time with the stutter, long and short, long and short, and in the swinging shadows the noble houses stood with their doors open and their masked inhabitants gathered on their thresholds, all facing the same direction, all very still.

Facing the caldera. Facing Emberless.

"No," Nim said aloud, to no one, to everyone. "No, you look away from her."

They didn't. Of course they didn't. Masks turned toward her as she passed—one porcelain, painted with lilies; one of black glass; one stitched from pale leather, featureless except for a smile—and behind the masks she felt the same thing she'd felt in the flicker of the dragon's flame. Attention. Leaning. Hunger with nowhere left to put itself, because the fire that had penned it in for a hundred years was guttering out.

A woman in the lily mask spoke as Nim passed, and her voice was gentle, almost kind, which made it worse.

"Little keeper. You should go home. It will be easier if you're home."

"Home is a dead word in this place," Nim snapped, and kept running, and heard behind her the soft sound of many silk-clad bodies beginning, at last, to move.

* * *

The first feeding happened in Candlemaker's Row, and Nim saw it because she couldn't not see it—the street lay directly below the stair, lit by the pulsing roots, impossible to miss.

A servant girl was running. Sixteen maybe, seventeen, still wearing her work apron, a scald-mark up one forearm that Nim recognized because every vat-worker had it. She ran the way people run when they have finally understood something, that flat-out, arms-pumping sprint of pure animal refusal. Behind her walked a man in a dove-gray suit and a mask of silver filigree, and he wasn't running at all. He was strolling. He had all the time in the world, and the horrible thing, the thing that made Nim's hands close on the stair rail hard enough to hurt, was that he *did* have all the time in the world. He'd had a hundred years of it. He'd bought it.

He caught the girl at the mouth of the row, where the glass roots converged into a knot thick as a temple pillar, and he caught her almost tenderly, one hand on her shoulder, turning her like a dance partner.

"You'll barely feel it," Nim heard him say. His voice carried in the cavern acoustics, soft and reasonable. "Everyone says they'll feel it, and no one ever does. It's like falling asleep in a warm room."

The girl said something Nim couldn't hear. It sounded like begging. It sounded like a name—someone else's name, thrown out into the air like a rope.

The man leaned in, and where his masked face met the girl's throat, the light changed.

It was subtle. That was the obscenity of it. No gore, no violence worth the name. Just a dimming—a hand's-breadth circle of the girl's glow going out, the way a window goes dark room by room in a house at night. The glass roots above them flared, drinking, redistributing, and the servant swayed, and her eyes went vague, and the man in the dove-gray suit sighed with something that sounded terribly like relief, like a starving man's first bite after a fast he'd been pretending wasn't killing him.

Then he set her down carefully on the cobbles, patted her apron straight, and walked on, already looking for the next one.

The girl sat where he'd left her. She didn't cry. She looked at her own hands as though counting them, and Nim understood, with a clarity that felt like a blade between the ribs, that the girl was trying to remember how many fingers she used to be sure of.

Nim was moving before she decided to move—down the last turns of the stair, across the street, kneeling in candle-wax and glass dust beside her.

"Hey. Hey. Look at me. What's your name?"

The girl's eyes focused slowly, like instruments coming back to true. "Maret," she said. Then, frowning: "Maret. Maret Sool." She said it twice more, quieter, testing whether it still held weight. "He took—I don't know what he took. I can't remember my mother's face. I know I had one. I know I loved her. I can't—" Her breath hitched. "I can't see her."

"Yes you can." Nim took the girl's hands. They were cold. Everything down here was cold now, cold as the dragon's graying scales, and Nim thought: *she's failing faster, the fire's failing faster, this is what it looks like when the containment goes, this is the prophecy's date arriving early or late or exactly on time.* "You hold onto the loving. The face will come back or it won't, but the loving is yours. He can't drink the loving. Do you hear me? That part's not in the jars."

Maret stared at her. And then, horribly, gently, she smiled. "You're the keeper's daughter," she said. "You come down sometimes, to buy wax. You always overpay."

"I do not overpay."

"You always overpay." The smile wavered. "Why are you crying, keeper's daughter?"

Because the roots overhead had gone dark along the whole length of Candlemaker's Row, Nim didn't say. Because the light was leaving this city in mouthfuls and everyone could feel it and no one had ever planned for it, because a hundred years ago some clever monster had built a paradise on top of a furnace and called the furnace eternal. Because her mother had spent thirty years keeping a fire alive for people who fed on the firewood, and had died believing Nim could do the same, and Nim had just realized, kneeling in the wax, that she couldn't. That no one could. That the arithmetic had finally, tonight, come due.

"Because I'm angry," Nim said instead, which was true, and let go of Maret's hands, and stood up.

Above the city, through the great throat of the caldera, she could see it: the last light of Emberless, the small red coal of her guttering heart, visible now from the streets themselves because the fire had shrunk so far inward. It pulsed with the roots. Amber, rose, sickly white. Long and short. Long and short.

And then—long.

And then nothing.

* * *

The silence that followed lasted four seconds. Nim counted them, because counting was the family trade, her mother's discipline drilled into her bones: *when everything fails, count, little ash, count and keep your hands busy.*

One: the roots held their darkness, every vein in the cavern, a city of glass gone blind.

Two: somewhere a bell began to ring, high and panicked, the alarm the court hadn't needed in a century.

Three: the masks on every threshold turned upward, all together, a field of flowers tracking the sun.

Four: from the caldera, faint but unmistakable, came a sound Nim had waited her whole life to hear and now received like a knife—Emberless, silent for three days, spoke.

Not in words. In heat. One final pulse of it, rolling down through the glass roots and out through the stone, and the pulse said, in the old language of warmth that Nim's mother had taught her to listen for, the only sentence the dying fire had left:

*I held as long as I could, little ash. Now run.*

Then the containment broke.

Nim felt it rather than saw it—a pressure lifting off the world, a door opening somewhere below the deepest vaults, and with it came the hunger, no longer penned, no longer rationed, no longer forced to wait politely behind glass. It rolled up through the city like fog with intent. The nobles didn't chase it. They simply turned, all those masked faces, from looking up at the dead caldera to looking at the nearest living glow, and the nearest living glows were the servants, hundreds of them, caught in the open streets between the vats and the long tables, between their work and their beds.

The bell kept ringing. Nobody came to answer it.

Nim grabbed Maret's wrist. "Up. On your feet. The keeper's stair—it runs hot even now, residual heat, they don't like it, they won't follow you up. Can you run?"

"The stair's for keepers—"

"The stair's for anyone I say it's for." She hauled the girl upright. "Run, Maret Sool. Count your steps if it helps. My mother taught me that. Counting keeps the self in the body."

They ran. Behind them the beautiful streets filled with soft footsteps and softer voices, the court moving through its own collapse with the same terrible courtesy it had brought to everything, murmuring apologies, offering comfort, *you'll barely feel it, everyone says they'll feel it.* Ahead the stair spiraled up into darkness that still, faintly, blessedly, held warmth—the ghost of a dragon's body, the memory of fire.

Halfway up, Nim stopped. She couldn't have said why. Some keeper's instinct, some daughter's ear, tuned across a lifetime to one particular frequency of heat.

High above, in the dead caldera, in the cold shell of the greatest heart she had ever tended, something remained. Not the fire. The fire was done, spent, given wholly to its hundred-year duty. But an ember—one ember, no bigger than a seed, stubborn as her mother's ghost—had curled itself down deep where no hunger could reach, and it was beating. Slowly. Stubbornly. Like a heart deciding, against all advice, to continue.

Nim pressed her hand to the warm stone and laughed, or sobbed, or both.

"All right," she said to the mountain, to the dark, to the ember, to the servants fleeing below and the nobles feeding and the god-knew-what that Lior and Sefa were doing in the vaults right now. "All right. You held as long as you could. So will I."

Below her, the city of candles began to scream in earnest—and somewhere beneath it all, in the archives under the throne, she knew, the real fight was only beginning.
