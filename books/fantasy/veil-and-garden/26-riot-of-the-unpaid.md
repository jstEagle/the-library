## 26. Riot of the Unpaid

Sefa had spent thirty years learning to read a room the way she read a manuscript: by its margins. The court beneath the mountain was a book whose margins were all wrong—too much white space where grief should have been annotated, too many small corrections in hands that trembled. The servants were the marginalia. They moved through the glass-rooted halls with their eyes down and their trays level, and Sefa had catalogued them the way she catalogued everything: the candle-boy with the burn scar shaped like a moth; the linen-woman who hummed a lullaby in a dialect dead two hundred years; the tall footman whose shadow lagged half a heartbeat behind his body, as though it had forgotten, over the decades, exactly how fast he was.

She had thought, at first, that they were content. That was the court's great trick, she understood now—the trick that made her cold with a fury she had no shelf to store on. The servants did not look enslaved because they did not look like anything at all. They looked *polished*. And polish, Sefa knew from a century of damaged codices, was what vellum looked like after someone had scraped off everything written there.

Now, in the hours since Emberless's fire had begun to fail, the scraping was showing.

* * *

It started, as revolutions often do among the careful, with an accounting error.

Sefa saw it happen in the servants' corridor below the Long Table hall, where she was pressed into an alcove behind a rack of ceremonial masks, the codex's decoded pages folded against her ribs like a second heartbeat. A steward—a gray-templed man with the unlined face of someone who had not aged since some decade he could no longer name—was distributing the week's wages. Small glass counters, each one glowing faintly amber, dropped one by one into cupped palms.

The candle-boy with the moth-scarred hand took his counter, turned it in the light, and said, very quietly, "This is warm."

The steward did not answer.

"It's warm," the boy said again, louder, "like the jars upstairs. Like the jars in the vault under the throne." He had been sent down there, Sefa realized; some errand of delivery, some innocent carrying. His voice cracked along a fault line. "I carried them. I counted them. There were ones with names on them." He looked up at the steward, and his face was doing something terrible and young. "There was one with *my* name on it."

The corridor went still in the way a held breath is still.

"Boy," the steward said gently, "you are tired. The mountain makes us all strange—"

"My mother sold a year of her life when I was born," the boy said. "One year. To pay a debt. I know because my aunt told me before—" He stopped. Before what? Sefa wondered. Before the aunt was collected? Before the memory itself was sold out from under him? "One year. But I've been here—" His brow furrowed, and Sefa watched him reach for arithmetic the way a drowning man reaches for rope. "How long have I been here?"

No one answered him. That was the answer.

He opened his hand. The wage-counter sat in his palm, glowing amber, and now that Sefa looked—now that she let herself look properly—it glowed the exact amber of the soul-jars she had seen shelved beneath the throne, thousands of them, each labeled in a neat clerk's hand. *Wages*, the court called them. Wages paid in the only currency the court had ever truly minted.

"They're paying us with ourselves," the boy said. "They've always been paying us with ourselves."

A linen-woman near the back—the lullaby-hummer, Sefa noted, her scholar's mind filing even now, even here—stepped forward and held up her own counter to the light of the root-lamps. Behind the amber glow, suspended like a fly in resin, was a flicker of image: a kitchen. Sunlight. Someone laughing, face blurred by time or theft.

"That's my sister's house," the woman whispered. "That's—I haven't thought about my sister in—" Her hand began to shake. "I don't remember her name. I remember her *house*. Why do I remember her house and not her *name*?"

Because they take the center first, Sefa thought, pressing herself deeper into the alcove, her heart hammering against the folded pages. Because a name is load-bearing. Take the name and the whole structure of a person sags. She had seen it in books—palimpsests where the original text had been scraped away so thoroughly that later scholars built theories on the ghost of it. The court had made palimpsests of people. And now the under-text was bleeding through, because Emberless was dying, because the containment was failing, because everything the court had scraped smooth was beginning to show its scars.

The steward set down his tray of counters with a click that sounded, in the silence, like a lock turning.

"You should all return to your stations," he said.

And the candle-boy threw his wage at the wall.

* * *

The sound it made was small. Glass on stone, a bright little death. But Sefa felt it go through the corridor like a crack through ice, and she understood in that instant something she would write down later, when there was later, when there was paper: that the oppressed do not find each other through speeches. They find each other through a single shattering that everyone hears and no one can unhear.

The linen-woman threw hers next. Then the tall footman, whose shadow finally caught up to him in the moment he moved. Counters hit the floor, the walls, the root-glass ceiling, and each one burst with a small released breath of light—an image, a warmth, a stolen afternoon escaping back into the air—and the servants stood amid the glittering wreckage of their own returned moments, weeping without knowing yet what they were weeping for, because so much of the reason had been taken along with the rest.

"Where is mine?" someone shouted. An older man, a gardener by his earth-stained knees, holding up empty hands. "I worked forty years. Where is the rest of mine? They gave me back an hour. They gave me back an *hour*—"

"The vaults," Sefa said.

She hadn't meant to speak. Her body simply decided, the way it had decided once, years ago, to leave the Grand Athenaeum rather than stamp a false provenance, and she stepped out of the alcove into a corridor full of turning faces, feeling the codex pages shift against her ribs like wings.

"The vaults beneath the throne," she said again, louder. "Every jar is labeled. Every jar can be found. I've seen the shelves. I've read the labels." She was an archivist; precision was her native tongue, and she spoke it now like a battle-cry. "Nothing that was taken has been destroyed. It's all still *filed*."

The word went through them strangely. Filed. Catalogued. Their lives, their mothers' names, their sisters' kitchens, sitting on shelves in the dark with neat little calligraphic tags, waiting for a centennial feast. Sefa watched the horror move across their faces and become something harder, and she recognized it because she had felt it in the archive beneath the throne herself: cold fury, the kind that organizes.

"The dragon's fire is failing," said the tall footman, and his voice was deep and slow, like something rising from a long sleep. "My grandmother served here when the last collection came. She told me—" He frowned, groping. "She told me something. I can't—there's a hole where she told me something." He shook himself. "When the fire fails, the nobles will feed. On whoever is nearest. On *us*. We are nearest. We have always been nearest."

"Then we get out," said the linen-woman.

"We get our people out," said the gardener.

"And we get the jars," Sefa said. "All of them. If the fire fails and the court falls, every soul in those vaults goes home tonight—not just ours. Not just the staff. Everyone they ever bought." She thought of Lior, somewhere above or beyond, carrying a contract with their own soul-mark on it, and her chest tightened. "Especially the ones who were sold here as children."

The candle-boy wiped his face with the heel of his moth-scarred hand. "How do we get past the household guard?" he asked, and it was a practical question, a servant's question, the question of someone who had spent his whole remembered life finding routes around locked doors, and Sefa loved him for it.

"We don't get past them," said the footman slowly. "We ask them."

Someone laughed—bitter, automatic. Then stopped. Because the footman was serious, and because everyone in that corridor had, at some point, poured wine for a guard whose eyes were flat and hungry, and had wondered, in the way you wonder about things too dangerous to finish wondering: *what are they paid?*

"They're paid the same as us," the linen-woman breathed. "Counters. Warm counters. Oh—oh, saints, they don't know either. Some of them don't *know*."

* * *

After that, it moved the way water moves through old masonry—finding every crack, going everywhere at once, unstoppable precisely because it had been seeping for a hundred years and the wall was already rotten. Sefa went with it, because the vaults beneath the throne were also the archives, and if there was chaos coming, an archivist belonged at the heart of it, salvaging truth from the collapse.

She moved through service corridors she had mapped in her head during three days of feigned compliance, and everywhere she passed, the revolt was blooming. In the scullery, a cook had upended the entire wage-tray into the boiling vat, and the steam that rose was full of ghost-images—weddings, harvests, a dog running through wheat—so that the whole room smelled briefly of other people's happiness. In the lamplighters' stair, a dozen men and women had linked arms across the width of the passage, and were singing, badly, in that dead dialect the linen-woman hummed, a work song none of them fully remembered but all of them somehow knew the shape of, the way you know the shape of a room you lived in before you could talk.

And everywhere, the same question, passed mouth to mouth like a flame down a fuse: *what did they take from me? What did they take from me?*

Some of the answers were unbearable. Sefa saw a chambermaid sit down abruptly on the stairs because she had remembered, mid-stride, that she'd had children—two of them, she was almost sure, two—and could not remember a single thing about their faces except that one had a habit of tilting his head when he laughed. She saw a valet tear open his own uniform shirt as if the missing years might be written on his chest. She saw the steward—the gray-templed steward who had tried to gentle the candle-boy—stand alone in the wrecked wage-corridor, turn his own counter over and over in his fingers, and then, with the terrible dignity of a man closing a ledger he can no longer balance, swallow it whole.

Then he picked up a candelabrum and walked toward the throne hall, and a hundred servants followed him.

* * *

Chaos, Sefa reflected, ducking through a doorway as the first squad of household guards clattered past—running *toward* the servants' quarters, which meant the news had reached them too, which meant the guards were asking their own questions now—chaos was a kind of cover, but it was also a kind of clock. Above them, felt more than heard, a vast heat was guttering. The air itself had begun to stutter, warm and cold in alternating waves, like a candle deciding. When Emberless failed entirely, the hunger contained for a century would come off its chain, and it would not be polite, and it would not check labels.

Which meant the jars had to be found before the feeding started. Which meant—

"Sefa!"

Nim Ashvael came around the corner at a dead run, soot-streaked, hair escaping its braid in copper wisps, and grabbed Sefa's arm hard enough to bruise. Behind her, moving with the grim economy of a mercenary in a crowd, came Lior Oyelaran, and behind *them*, breathing hard and dusted in ash, in robes that had no business this far underground—

"Ombre?" Sefa said stupidly.

"No time," Nim gasped. "The fire's failing faster than Emberless said it would. Something's pulling at it from inside the court—some working, some *drawing*—and the old dragon's holding on with claws and spite but she can't hold long. We need the vaults. We need the master ledger. Lior thinks—" She glanced at Lior, and something passed between them, quick and absolute. "Lior thinks there's a clause."

"There's always a clause," Lior said. Their voice was flat and steady, the voice of a person who had decided to be calm the way other people decide to be brave, and Sefa saw what the tollhouses and the road and the court had cost them, and loved them for standing anyway. "Every fraud has one. I just need the book."

"The book is guarded by the whole rotting court," Sefa said.

Lior looked past her shoulder, down the corridor, where the light of a hundred liberated wage-counters lay scattered on the floor like fallen stars, where servants and guards stood facing each other in ragged lines, both sides holding the same warm glass, both sides beginning, terribly and wonderfully, to understand.

"No," Lior said quietly. "It's guarded by whatever's left after *that* finishes."

Above them, through miles of stone and glass-root, the last steady note of a dying dragon's fire shivered—and dimmed.

Sefa gripped her codex against her ribs and ran toward the throne.
