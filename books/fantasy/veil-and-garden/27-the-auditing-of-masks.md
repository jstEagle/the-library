## 27. The Auditing of Masks

The riot sounded like rain at first.

That was the terrible thing about it—how gentle it began. A thousand small sounds rising through the glass-root tunnels of the court beneath the mountain: the tap of serving trays set down and not picked up again, the soft unclasping of collars, the whisper of servants comparing their wage-stubs in the candlelight. Then a jar broke somewhere—a soul-jar, or only wine, no one could tell—and the sound of it shattering ran through the city like a crack through ice, and after that it was not rain anymore. It was a flood finding its old riverbed.

Lior stood on the steps of the Hall of Accounts with Sefa's decoded contracts clutched to their chest like a breastplate, and listened to the flood come.

"They'll kill you," Nim said. She had Emberless's last ember banked in an iron lantern at her hip, and its light made her face look carved out of copper and shadow. "You understand that. The moment you open your mouth down there, you stop being a clerk and become a target."

"I've been a target since before I could walk," Lior said. "I was sold. That's what a target is—something priced." They looked down at the papers again, at Sefa's neat marginalia, the red thread she'd drawn connecting clause to clause, fraud to fraud. "But they've never once been afraid of me. I'd like to change the arithmetic."

Sefa pushed her spectacles up her nose. Her hands were shaking, but her voice, when it came, was the dry steady voice of a woman who had spent thirty years cataloguing other people's lies. "Article Seven of the Codex of Public Reading. Any contract read aloud, in full, before witnesses of sound mind, must be honored as written—or voided as written. It's the oldest law they have. Older than the masks." She almost smiled. "They wrote it themselves, back when they were sure no one would ever dare."

"Then let's use their own ink to hang them," Lior said.

* * *

The great hall of the court was built to make people feel small, and for a century it had succeeded. Glass roots arched overhead like the ribs of some buried leviathan, each one threaded with candlelight, so that the whole ceiling glowed with a warm amber lie—the lie that this place was cozy, domestic, safe. The long table ran the length of the floor, and behind it sat the nobility in their porcelain-smooth masks, watching the riot spread through their city the way one watches weather from a window.

Lior walked down the center aisle alone.

Nim flanked the left wall with the lantern held high. Sefa took the right, codex open across her forearms like a lectern. And between them, in the aisle, the servants of the court were gathering—not fleeing anymore, but turning, faces smudged with ash and fury, holding their broken wage-stubs like relics. Lior counted them the way they counted everything: forty-one, sixty-three, past a hundred now, and more coming up from the kitchens and the glass-root galleries, drawn by the one thing the court had never stocked its shelves with.

Truth, read aloud.

A masked noble rose from the table's head. Her voice came out warm as mulled wine. "Little ledger-keeper. You're causing such a mess. Come, sit—there's still a chair with your name on it."

"There is," Lior agreed, and did not stop walking. "There's a chair with everyone's name on it, eventually. That's rather the point of tonight's audit."

They stepped onto the dais. They turned to face the hall. And they unfolded the first page.

"By the authority of Article Seven," Lior said, and their voice, trained by nine years of silent prayers and quiet columns, carried further than they expected—as if the mountain itself wanted to hear, "I begin the public reading of the contracts of the Court Beneath the Mountain. Witnesses, mark what is true. Signatories, mark what is owed."

They read.

"*Contract of Service, household staff, perpetual term.* 'The servant shall receive in wages one measure of vitality per annum, drawn from the servant's own reserve, administered by the Court's physicians.'" Lior paused, let the sentence sit in the air like a blade balanced on its point. "For those in the gallery who don't speak contract: your wages were your own life. They have been paying you with you. Every coin in your purse is a year off your heart."

The silence lasted three heartbeats. Then a woman near the front—a laundress, Lior thought, judging by the burn scars on her forearms—held up her wage-stub, and the paper caught the candlelight, and everyone in the hall could see the fine print glowing faintly through it, the way souls glow through skin when they are being siphoned.

She tore it in half.

The sound was small. The sound it made inside every other servant in the hall was enormous. A hundred hands went to a hundred pockets all at once.

And on the dais's edge, three feet from Lior, the nearest noble's mask cracked.

It was not dramatic. That was the horror and the glory of it. A single hairline fissure opened across the porcelain cheek, thin as a pen stroke, and through it seeped something gray and hungry and *old*—and beneath the mask, where a face should have been, there was only need. The noble pressed one perfect hand to the crack as if staunching a wound.

Lior turned the page.

"*Deed of Purchase, infant, itemized.* 'One child, female, approximately six months, purchased at the Well Gate for the sum of one impossible thing, condition: soul intact, delivery: immediate.'" Their voice did not waver, though their hands wanted to. "This is my own bill of sale. I have read it before, privately, in the dark, and hated myself for shaking. I am not shaking now. Because here is what the deed does not say, and what the Codex requires me to note: the seller's signature on this document is forged. We have the exemplar. We have the tomb. We have the hand." They lifted the page higher. "And under Article Twelve, a sale founded on forged signature is void *ab initio*—void from its first breath. Which means the Court never owned me. Which means the Court has never owned *anyone* whose chain of title runs through this seller. Which means—" and here Lior raised their voice until it rang against the glass roots, "—which means every soul in the vaults below this floor is being held under a lien that does not exist."

The second mask cracked. Then a fourth. A fifth. Down the length of the long table, hairline fractures spidered across porcelain cheeks and browbones, and behind each fracture the gray hunger pressed outward, furious, exposed. The nobles were rising now, and their movements had lost their courtly smoothness; they moved like things that had worn their shapes too long.

"You dare," said the noble who had bought Lior, the one who remembered Lior's mother, her voice fraying at the edges like rotted silk. "You stand in our house and read our own law—"

"Your house?" Lior said. "Show me the deed."

* * *

They read for an hour. Perhaps two. Time behaved strangely in the hall, stretching like taffy around the candlelight, and Lior stopped tracking it and simply tracked the pages—Sefa passing them one by one, each annotated in her small fierce hand, each a little bomb of plain language dropped into the amber glow.

The contract that bound the dragon-keepers to silence, in exchange for a pension paid in borrowed years that the Court had never intended to honor. Nim, at the wall, read that one over Lior's shoulder and laughed—a short, hard, wounded sound—and Lior saw her straighten as if a weight she'd inherited at birth had been ruled illegitimate. Her father's silence had never been a debt. It had been theft.

The indenture of the archivists, including a young Sefa's own signature, extracted under threat of erasure. "Voided," Lior read, "for duress, per Article Nine," and Sefa whispered *voided* to herself like a prayer, twice, three times, pressing her palm flat against the codex as though steadying a bird.

The tithe-contracts with the god Veth—nine years of offerings collected and never delivered, receipts stamped *received* in a clerk's hand that Lior recognized with a jolt as their own, from a day they did not remember working. The Court had used them. Had used their meticulous, invisible competence as a tool to rob a god. Something in Lior's chest that had been numb for nine years woke up and howled.

And with each reading, another mask cracked, then split, then fell.

By the time Lior reached the final page, the long table was littered with porcelain shards, and the nobility stood revealed in their true attire: shapes of gray appetite wearing the rags of elegance, faces like smoke given just enough structure to sneer. They should have been terrifying. Some part of Lior registered that they were. But mostly they looked *smaller* than they had behind the masks—hungry things are always smaller than their costumes—and the servants of the court, a hundred strong now, two hundred, closing in from every gallery with their torn wage-stubs raised like banners, were not retreating from them at all.

"The final entry," Lior said, and their voice was hoarse now, and they found they didn't care. "*Master Ledger, holdings summary.* Souls in vault: four thousand one hundred and six. Status: collateral. Collateral for *what*, the entry does not say, because there is no debt. There has never been a debt. You cannot hold four thousand souls as security on a loan you never made." They lowered the page. They looked at the gray, starving shapes of the court's masters, and they said, quietly, in the tone they'd used for nine years to close out the guild's books:

"The accounts do not balance. They never balanced. And an account that does not balance is not a fortune—it is a confession."

The hall erupted.

Not into violence—at least, not only. Into *noise*. Servants shouting their own names, some of them, names they hadn't been permitted to use in decades; others shouting numbers, the numbers of years stolen, of children sold, of jars labeled; the laundress with the burn scars climbed onto the long table itself and began tearing the candle-lit glass roots down from the ceiling, and where the roots broke, cold clean air poured in from the mountain above, smelling of snow and stone and morning, and people wept at the smell of it as if it were a language they'd forgotten.

Through it all, the nobles pressed forward—not toward the exits, but toward the crowd, toward the warmest, brightest souls, their containment visibly failing now, their hunger no longer housebroken. Nim's lantern flared in answer, the ember inside roaring up like a tiny defiant sun, and she planted herself between the nearest noble and a cluster of kitchen children, and even from the dais Lior could see her arms shaking and her feet not moving one inch.

"Lior!" Sefa grabbed their sleeve. "The vaults! If the containment's going—if the fire fails—the master ledger is the only thing that frees them *before* the hunger reaches them!"

Lior already knew. Had known since the first page, maybe since the Well Gate, maybe since the cradle. The ledger below the throne, the book with four thousand names in it, theirs among them. The book that could be shattered.

They folded the last contract with hands that finally, finally felt like their own, and stepped down from the dais into the flood.

The crowd parted for them—not because they commanded it, but because word was running ahead of them faster than their feet, passed mouth to mouth through the smoke and the falling glass: *the clerk, the little clerk, the one who read us free.* Hands touched their shoulders as they passed. Someone pressed a candle stub into their pocket, a traveler's blessing. A child looked up at them with soot-striped cheeks and said, "Is it true? Are we real?"

"You were always real," Lior said, and meant it, and heard in their own voice the ghost of every year they had spent not believing it of themselves.

The stair to the vaults yawned beneath the broken throne, breathing cold air upward, and far below—deeper than the vaults, deeper than the roots, in some place older than the mountain's hunger—Lior felt something stir. Not sound. Not quite presence. More like a column in a ledger that had been blank for nine years suddenly showing the faintest impression of ink, as if somewhere a patient hand had begun, at last, to write.

Nine years of prayers. Nine years of silence. And now, as Lior descended into the dark with the freed crowd roaring overhead and the ember-light of a dying dragon flickering down the stairwell behind them, the silence broke—not with thunder, not with trumpets, but with a voice as dry and precise as a quill on vellum, speaking directly into the marrow of their bones:

*"Ledger."*

One word. One word after nine years. And Lior Oyelaran, standing on the threshold of the soul-vault with tears freezing on their cheeks, answered the only way an accountant ever could:

"Yes," they breathed. "Let's balance the books."
