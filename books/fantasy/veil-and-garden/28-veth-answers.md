## 28. Veth Answers

The first jar broke the way a held breath breaks.

Lior heard it before they saw it—a sound too small for the catastrophe around them, a crystalline *tick* beneath the roar of the riot, like an ice crack spreading across a winter pond. They were pressed against a pillar of glass-root near the vault doors, Sefa's shoulder hard against theirs, Nim somewhere in the smoke ahead, and the whole court was coming apart in slow, polite pieces: nobles shrieking behind their masks, servants with fire in their eyes and stolen years in their veins, chandeliers of woven candlelight swinging like hanged men.

But that small sound cut through all of it.

*Tick.*

Then another. And another.

The melee had reached the tithe-vault. Lior saw it through the crowd's gaps: the long shelves where the court stored its most precious inventory, the jars of siphoned life arranged by vintage and quality—and among them, at the far end, in a section even the rioting servants had ignored because they did not know what it was, the row of plain grey vessels marked with a symbol Lior had inked ten thousand times into their own prayer-book.

A closed ledger. A line beneath it. The mark of Veth.

"No," Lior said, and did not know they had said it aloud until Sefa turned. "No—*not those*—"

A servant—a kitchen woman with flour still ghosting her knuckles, her face stripped of the numbness the court wore like livery—had climbed the shelf on a ladder of broken chair-legs. She had a jar in both hands. She could not have known what it was. It looked like nothing: unglazed clay, no gilding, no label beyond the god's mark. To her it was simply one more thing the beautiful people had locked away, one more thing to break.

She raised it over her head.

"Wait—" Lior lunged forward, shouldering through the press of bodies, through the heat and the screaming, through the smell of scorched candlewax and old money burning. "Please—it isn't yours to—"

The woman threw it anyway.

The jar struck the marble floor and shattered, and the world *stopped*.

Not the riot—the riot went on, would go on, was going on in some corner of Lior's awareness—but something underneath the riot stopped, the way a heart stops between beats, the way a quill stops above the page when the writer realizes what the numbers are trying to say.

Sound came out of the broken jar.

Not words. Not at first. What came out was nine years of silence given back all at once—a sound like wind through a counting-house at night, like pages turning in an empty room, like the vast patient respiration of something enormous that had been holding very, very still. It poured across the floor in a flood of grey light, and where it touched the other jars on the shelf they began to sing, each one a thin note of imprisoned voice, and the notes braided together into a chord that made Lior's teeth ache and their eyes stream and their knees buckle against the pillar.

Sefa caught their arm. "What is it? What's happening?"

"It's Him," Lior said. Their voice came out cracked, childish, nothing like a ledger-keeper's voice at all. "It's His voice. They stole it. They stole it and they *shelved it*."

Nine years.

Nine years of kneeling at the little shrine in their rented room with its single candle, speaking their accounts aloud to a god who never answered. Nine years of tithes paid into the guild vault—hours of devotion, drops of honest grief, the small coin of attention that Veth asked of his keepers—nine years of offerings that had gone down into the dark and never come back up as so much as a whisper. Nine years of telling themselves that silence was the god's nature, that Veth was the god who kept books and not conversation, that faith meant working the columns without ever hearing the totals read aloud.

They had built a theology out of their own abandonment. They had called it discipline.

And all the while their god's voice had sat in a clay jar in a cellar beneath a mountain, labeled and inventoried, an asset on someone else's books.

The rage that rose in Lior then was unlike anything they had ever felt—not the cold anger of an auditor finding a discrepancy, but something molten and enormous, something that felt less like their own emotion than like a door in their chest being kicked open from the inside. Because this was what the numbness had been hiding. This was what half a soul could not carry. Somewhere under all those carefully balanced years there had been a child who loved a silent god with their whole loud heart, and that child had been waiting nine years for exactly this moment, and now it was here, and the grief and the fury and the love were all one thing, and it was *too much*, it was far too much, it was—

The second jar broke.

The kitchen woman had not stopped. She stood amid the shards of the first, weeping without knowing why, and she had taken down another, and another after that, and now others were helping her—servants climbing the shelf two and three at a time, passing the plain grey vessels down hand over hand like buckets in a fire-line, smashing them against the marble, against the pillars, against the floor of the only hell any of them had ever known.

And Veth's voice came home.

It did not speak. Not yet. It *flooded*. It poured through the vault and up through the glass-roots, which lit from within like veins carrying dawn, grey-silver light racing along every translucent root and rib of the mountain, and Lior felt it pass through them the way a tide passes through a sea-cave—without asking permission, without pausing, filling every hollow place. And oh, there were hollow places. There were hollow places Lior had spent a lifetime furnishing so they wouldn't echo.

The light found them anyway.

It found the memory-toll they'd paid at the tollhouse, the gap where Ombre teaching them letters used to be, and it pooled there, warm and attentive, like a hand resting flat on an old scar. It found the missing half of their soul, the ragged edge of them, and it did not heal it—Veth was an accountant, not a miracle-worker; he knew the difference between a balance and a gift—but it *acknowledged* it. It wrote the loss down. Lior felt themselves being entered into a book somewhere, precisely and tenderly, every wound itemized, and the feeling of it was so close to grace that they stopped trying to stand and simply folded to the floor among the breaking jars and let it happen.

Around them the riot was changing.

Lior saw it through streaming eyes: servants freezing mid-stride, heads lifting, faces turning toward the light in the roots. A footman dropped the candelabrum he'd been wielding and pressed both hands over his mouth. An old gardener sank down beside a shattered jar and began to laugh, a wet, disbelieving sound, saying over and over, "I can feel my hands, I can feel my *hands*." The stolen voices were going home. Every jar that broke released not only the god's stolen speech but the resonance that had been holding a hundred smaller thefts in place, and all across the vault people were feeling, for the first time in decades, the full weight and warmth of their own lives pressing back against them from the inside.

And the masks were cracking.

Not the porcelain ones—the ones beneath. Lior watched a noblewoman stagger as the light hit her, watched the perfect ageless composure slide off her face like wax off a candle, watched fifty years of deferred aging arrive all at once in the deep lines around her mouth. The court had fed on time, and the god's returning voice was calling in every debt at once.

"LIOR."

Sefa was shouting their name. Or maybe it wasn't Sefa. Maybe it was the light itself, because the word arrived not through the ears but through the bones, through the ledger-marks scarred into Lior's soul, through the covenant written in them before they could walk.

"You're glowing," Sefa said, gripping their shoulders. "Lior, you're—you're *glowing*—"

They were. Grey-silver light moved under their skin like ink moving through water, gathering in the lines of their palms, tracing the scars of their trade. And in the middle of it, in the center of their chest where the hollow place lived, something spoke.

Not in thunder. That was the unbearable thing about it. After nine years of imagining the answer—after rehearsing thunder, choirs, the voice of a mountain—what came was quiet. Dry. Almost amused. A voice like a pen scratching across good paper, like a shopkeeper greeting a customer he has waited a long time to see.

*"You kept excellent accounts,"* said Veth.

Lior made a sound they would deny for the rest of their life.

"I prayed," they said. Into the light, into the noise, into the arms of a god nine years late. "Every night. For nine years. I prayed and you never—I thought you were dead, I thought you'd abandoned me, I thought—"

*"I know."* The voice was gentle the way a well-worn ledger is gentle, soft at every corner from handling. *"I heard every word. I could not answer. Do you understand what it is, little keeper, to hear your own name called and have no mouth? They took my voice the way your guild takes a year from a dying man—by contract, by clause, by the letter of a law written by thieves. I heard you balance your ledgers at midnight. I heard you forgive me. I heard you stop forgiving me. All of it is written here."*

"Where?"

*"Here."*

And the light turned—there was no other way to describe it—all at once, the way a head turns, the way attention turns, and Lior followed it with their eyes and understood.

Beyond the vault, beyond the rioting hall, deep in the roots of the mountain, something vast was waking. Lior could not see it, but they could feel it the way a clerk feels the master ledger in a locked drawer: the true book, the original, the codex from which every contract in the court was copied. The book that held Lior's sale. The book that held the god's mortgage. The book whose every line was a chain and whose binding was the law the court hid behind.

"The master ledger," Lior whispered.

*"One hundred years ago they bought my silence,"* Veth said, and now there was something in the voice that had never been there in all Lior's imaginings of him—weariness, yes, but under the weariness a banked and patient iron. *"Tonight the last of my voice comes free, jar by jar, thanks to a woman with flour on her hands who broke what she was told not to touch. But a voice is not enough. A voice must be answered with action, or it is only noise. You taught me that, Lior Oyelaran. Nine years of prayers with no reply, and still you worked the columns. Still you paid the tithe. Do you know what that is?"*

Lior shook their head. They could not speak.

*"That is faith,"* said the silent god. *"I have received very little of it. Most of my keepers quit at the first unanswered year. You gave me nine."*

The light gathered. It drew itself up out of the floor, out of the roots, out of the thousand breaking jars, and it stood before Lior in the shape of a figure neither tall nor short, neither young nor old, wearing robes the color of ash and carrying—Lior laughed, or sobbed, or both—a ledger bound in grey leather, thick as a tombstone, its corners worn round with use.

The god of accounts looked down at them with eyes like inkwells.

*"I cannot enter the throne room,"* Veth said. *"My name is voided there, by clause and seal. But yours is not. Yours, little keeper, is written in the oldest book in this mountain—under tonight's date, in the hand of a dead forger. The prophecy named you because the ledger named you. And the ledger can be opened by the name it holds."*

Nim appeared out of the smoke, soot-streaked, dragging Sefa by the sleeve, Emberless's last ember glowing in the lantern at her hip like a stubborn heartbeat. She skidded to a stop when she saw the figure of ash and ink standing over Lior, and Lior watched her decide—watched her look at the god, look at Lior's shining hands, and plant her feet instead of running.

"That's him?" she said. "Your god?"

"He's real," Lior said. Their voice was wrecked and they did not care. "He was always real. He was *shelved*."

"Then ask him how we burn this place down," Nim said, "because the fire's failing, and when it fails, everything in those jars gets eaten."

*"There is no burning,"* Veth said, and the whole mountain seemed to lean in to listen. *"There is only accounting. Every theft in this court was committed in ink, and what is done in ink can be undone in ink. The master ledger sits behind the throne, sealed with a hundred-year lock that opens tonight—opens for the name written under tonight's date."*

The inkwell eyes fixed on Lior.

*"I am forbidden to touch it. You are not. Go down, little keeper. Open the book. And when you find your own name in it—when you find the entry that made you currency—remember what you taught me these nine years."*

"What did I teach you?" Lior whispered.

The god smiled. It was a small, dry, tired smile, and it was the most beautiful thing Lior had ever seen.

*"That a debt unpaid is not a debt forgiven,"* said Veth. *"Go collect."*

The light withdrew—not gone, Lior felt him everywhere now, felt him in the roots and the stone and the marrow of their borrowed bones, a presence at their elbow like a senior partner reviewing the day's work—but the figure of ash folded away into the grey glow running through the glass, and the vault rang with the last of the breaking jars, and the path beyond the far doors stood open, unguarded, every servant in it kneeling or weeping or walking free.

Sefa pulled Lior upright. Nim checked the ember in her lantern. Somewhere above, faint through miles of stone, came a sound like a great breath failing—the dragon's fire, guttering toward its end.

"The throne room's below the archive," Sefa said, already moving, already reciting the maps they'd memorized. "Behind the soul-vault. We passed it—we *passed the door*, Lior, I thought it was a wine cellar—"

"Nim." Lior caught her arm. "When we get there. Whatever the book offers me. Whatever it looks like I'm about to do. Don't let go of my shoulder."

Nim looked at them for a long moment, ember-light and god-light fighting across her face.

"Deal," she said. "Same goes for you."

They ran, three small figures down into the roots of the mountain, toward the oldest book in the world—and behind them, patient as interest accruing, the god of accounts walked with them, taking notes.
