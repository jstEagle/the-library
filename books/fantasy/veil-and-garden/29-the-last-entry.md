## 29. The Last Entry

The mountain breathed around me.

I had thought, in the tunnels below the riot, that I understood what a mountain was: stone, weight, patience. But this one was alive in the way a ledger is alive—full of debts still moving through it. The glass roots overhead pulsed with light that rose and fell like slow lungs, and each pulse carried sound with it: whispers, weeping, the polite clink of masks being adjusted, somewhere far above the scream of a dragon whose fire was failing.

Veth walked beside me.

I say *walked*, though the god did not quite touch the floor. Nine years of silence, and now Veth was everywhere at once—a pressure at my left shoulder, a warmth in my palm where the guild-mark sat, a voice that was not a voice but a certainty arriving fully formed behind my eyes.

*Left,* the god said. *Then down. The stairs will try to be endless. They are not.*

"You could have said something," I murmured, taking the left passage. My voice came out rougher than I intended. "Nine years. You could have said *anything*."

*The court took my voice,* Veth answered, and for the first time I heard something beneath the calm of it—something scraped raw. *They took it and shelved it. You are walking past the shelf now. Do you feel them?*

I did. I had been feeling them since the vaults below the throne room: jars upon jars, thousands of them, each labeled in a hand too neat to belong to anything kind. Souls in rows. Debts collected from people who had never known they owed anything. And among them—nine years' worth of tithes meant for a silent god, prayers bottled instead of heard.

My own soul was on one of those shelves. Somewhere. A jar with my name on it, sold before I could speak, kept like an heirloom by people who wore porcelain faces and did not age.

*Not much farther,* Veth said. *And Lior—*

"Yes?"

*Whatever you find at the end of this, remember that you were never the entry. You were always the hand holding the pen.*

I didn't answer. I didn't know how. The god of accounts saying something kind to me was somehow more disorienting than everything else tonight combined—the riot, the shattering masks, Nim's hand burning hot against my shoulder as she pulled me toward the deep stairs while Sefa held off three servants with nothing but a chair leg and a truly impressive vocabulary.

The stairs went down.

They tried to be endless. Of course they did. This court had spent a century perfecting the art of making things seem other than they were—making theft look like gift, making hunger look like hospitality, making a child's sale look like destiny. So the stairs unspooled beneath me in pale marble, and after the two-hundredth step they began whispering.

*Lior Oyelaran,* they said, in my mother's voice, which I had only learned yesterday. *Lior Oyelaran, born under the veil, promised to the garden. Come down. Come home. The chair is waiting. The chair has always been waiting.*

"The chair can burn," I said, and kept counting steps, because counting was mine. Counting was the first thing Ombre ever taught me, back when I was a scrap of a child who flinched at kindness, and even if the tollhouse had taken the memory itself, the skill remained in my hands like a scar that remembers the wound. One hundred and forty. One hundred and forty-one. Numbers didn't lie. Numbers were the one language this mountain couldn't counterfeit, because every lie it told required arithmetic, and arithmetic would eventually confess.

At step three hundred and eleven, the stairs ended.

* * *

The chamber beyond was not grand. That was the first thing that undid me.

After the candle-lit halls, the glass-root cathedral, the long table set for a feast of souls—I had expected the master ledger to rest in splendor. Instead it sat in a small round room, low-ceilinged, lit by a single lamp of dragonfire so old and dim it had gone the color of amber. Dust lay on everything except the book itself. The book was clean. The book was *tended*. Someone had loved this thing the way I loved my ledgers, and that recognition felt like looking into a mirror hung in a slaughterhouse.

It rested on a stone plinth, open to no page in particular, its cover the grey-brown of old skin. It was not large. It was not beautiful. It was the most dangerous object I had ever stood in front of, and I had once inventoried a crate containing a bottled plague.

*There it is,* Veth said quietly. *Every sale. Every signature. Every soul this court ever took, bound to its line in ink that cannot fade because the ink is fed.*

"Fed with what?"

*You know.*

I knew. Emberless's fire guttered above us even now; I could feel the dimming in my teeth, a pressure like a storm coming. The dragon's flame hadn't imprisoned the stolen souls—it had contained the court's hunger—and as it failed, the nobles above were feeding on whatever souls were nearest. The servants first. Always the servants first. That was the oldest rule of any ledger, in any city, under any mountain: when the accounts run short, take from those who cannot refuse.

The book's pages stirred without wind.

I approached it the way I'd approach any audit: slowly, hands visible, breathing even. Up close I could see that the vellum was strange—not animal, not plant. Glass-root parchment, grown rather than made, faintly warm, faintly pulsing. Each page held columns of entries in a dozen hands across a hundred years. Names. Prices. Terms. Signatures.

And there, glowing faintly gold against the grey, one line burned brighter than all the rest:

*Oyelaran, Lior. Infant, whole. Sold by [signature effaced]. Bought by the Court Beneath the Mountain. Price: one impossible thing. Term: perpetual. Status: DUE TONIGHT.*

Due tonight. A century to the day. Sefa had decoded it hours ago in a tunnel, her finger shaking against the marginalia: *the date marks not your death but the collection.* The centennial. The feast. Me, the anniversary purchase, come due like a bond maturing.

"Show me the rest," I said aloud, though there was no one to hear but a god and a book. "Show me what happens if I claim it."

The pages turned themselves.

* * *

Here is what the ledger showed me, and I will set it down plainly because plainness is the only honesty I have ever trusted:

If I erased the line—if I struck out my own sale with my own hand—the power of the sale would revert to me. That is how these contracts work. I have written enough of them. Void the transaction, and the goods return to the seller; void a sale of a soul, and the soul returns *whole*, undivided, with a century of accrued interest. The interest was the terrible part. The interest was *power*. A soul sold whole and held for a hundred years compounds. The ledger showed me the sum of it in cold, elegant figures, and the sum was this: I would become the strongest thing in this mountain. Stronger than the masked nobles. Strong enough to hold the court's hunger closed with my own will when Emberless died. Strong enough, perhaps, to do it forever.

Strong enough to be the new containment. The new dragon. The new wall between the hungry and the eaten.

And the ledger, helpful as any good clerk, showed me what else came with it.

I saw myself seated at the long table—not as a guest but as its head. I saw the chair that fit me perfectly, because of course it did; it had been carved from the wood of the well gate where I was sold, seasoned for a hundred years, fitted to a body its makers had purchased in infancy. I saw myself keeping the master ledger, my handwriting neat, my invisibility perfected into something grander: authority. No one would ever overlook me again. No one would ever sell me again. I would be the one who kept the accounts, and everyone knows the one who keeps the accounts is safe.

I saw myself growing old in that chair. I saw my face learning the stillness of the nobles. I saw centuries pass and my hands stay steady and my heart stay quiet, because a quiet heart keeps better books.

I saw Nim outside the mountain, climbing away alone, carrying one ember and no Lior.

I saw Sefa's catalogue unfinished, the freed souls still shelved, still labeled, still *owned*—because freeing them would require breaking the very contracts I would now be the keeper of, and a keeper protects the books. That is the first law of the profession. I had lived by it for twenty years.

*Do you see the shape of it?* Veth asked, very gently.

"I see it." My voice sounded far away. "It's a trap with my name engraved on the lid."

*It is worse than a trap. Traps want to hurt you. This wants to make you comfortable. Look again at the terms. Read them as an auditor.*

So I did. I leaned over the plinth and read the reversion clause the way Ombre taught me to read everything—twice, once for meaning and once for what was being hidden. And there it was, in the sub-clause, in letters so small they might have been etched by a needle:

*Reversion of goods shall vest full title, term perpetual, contingent upon continued maintenance of the Ledger. The holder shall preserve, protect, and perpetuate all extant entries.*

All extant entries.

Every jar on every shelf. Every servant's siphoned life. Every soul bought and labeled and stored like vintage wine for the next centennial feast. To claim myself, I would have to keep all of them claimed. The contract didn't offer me freedom. It offered me wardenship—an eternity of guarding the prison that held me, promoted from asset to jailer, which is the oldest trick captivity knows.

"They built the choice so that either way, I'm theirs," I said. "Erase the line and become their wall. Refuse, and be collected tonight with the rest."

*Yes,* Veth said. *That is what they believe.*

"But?"

The silence that followed was not the old silence. It was the silence of a god choosing words with care, and I waited for it the way I had waited nine years without knowing I was waiting.

*But a ledger is only as strong as its binding,* Veth said. *And bindings, Lior, are made by hands.*

* * *

I put my palm flat on the page.

The glass-root vellum was warm as skin and it pressed back against my hand, gently, the way a living thing does when it wants to be soothed. I could feel the entries humming under my fingers—thousands of small captivities, each one a name, each name a person who had once laughed or grieved or wanted tea in the morning. Somewhere in these pages was my mother's sale, reversed and rewritten until even she believed she had abandoned me out of carelessness rather than desperation. Somewhere was Ombre's secret purchase of half of me, hidden in a false column, paid for with twenty years of guilt. Somewhere was the first entry ever made, the original sin of this place, signed by a seller whose preserved corpse Sefa and Nim were even now guarding in the dark.

The lamp of ancient dragonfire flickered. Above us, faint through a hundred fathoms of stone, I heard Emberless cry out—a sound like a bell cracking—and the light in the glass roots dimmed by half.

The collection had begun.

"Lior," Veth said, and the god's voice had an edge now, urgent and human. *Decide. When the fire fails fully, the binding weakens, and if the book is intact when it goes, the court will simply write new sales in the confusion. A hundred years from now there will be another child at another well gate.*

"And if I destroy it?"

*Then every contract voids. Every sale unwinds. Every soul in every jar goes home—including yours, including mine, including the nine years of tithes they bottled. The nobles will wake up mortal and ordinary and horrified. Some of them will not survive the horror. Some of them will not deserve to.* A pause. *And you will lose the power. All of it. The compounded century of you, gone the moment the ink burns. You will walk out of this mountain exactly as strong as you walked in—which is to say, a clerk with quick hands and good friends.*

"That's the pitch?" I almost laughed. "Destroy the book and get nothing?"

*You get everything,* Veth said. *You get everyone. You get the version of yourself that was never for sale. I know it doesn't compound, child. I am a god of accounts. I know precisely what it doesn't do.* The warmth at my shoulder pressed closer. *But I also know what you wrote in your first ledger, age eight, in a hand Ombre still keeps framed: 'A debt forgiven is not a loss. It is a door.'*

I hadn't known I remembered that. The tollhouse had taken the memory of being taught—but not, it seemed, the memory of what I'd done with the lesson.

The book turned its final page for me. There, at the bottom of the last leaf, a blank line waited, and above it a heading in the same needle-fine script:

*Final disposition of lot: Oyelaran, Lior.*

Two options, printed like any contract's closing terms. *Cancelled and returned to holder.* Or: *Cancelled and destroyed, all associated entries void.*

The book was offering me the choice honestly. That was the cruelest part. It had no stake in which I chose; it was only paper that wanted to go on existing, and it would serve whichever master held the pen. Evil, I thought, is rarely hungry. Mostly it is just tidy.

Above us, Emberless screamed again, longer this time, and the amber lamp gutted. Through the ceiling I felt the court's hunger come off its leash like a smell—sweet, patient, enormous. Nim was up there. Sefa was up there. Ombre was racing up the mountain road with twenty years of hidden contracts in her saddlebags, trying to reach me before the night reached its terms.

Twenty years ago, a woman sold me at a well gate because the collectors were coming and a sale was the only shield she could afford. She chose the lesser cage. Everyone in my life had always chosen the lesser cage for me—Ombre hiding my debt, the guild teaching me to be invisible, even Veth going quiet rather than let the court hear a god's voice and trace it back to the child it protected.

Everyone had chosen cages. Nobody had ever chosen the door.

I picked up the lamp.

* * *

The dragonfire in it was older than the court, older than the ledger, maybe older than the mountain's hunger—it was a splinter of the first flame, the kind that remembers being given freely. It weighed almost nothing. It weighed everything.

"You should know," I said to the book, conversationally, because I am who I am, "that I audited you. Line by line. Your seller's signature is forged—Sefa proved it, and his dead hand confirms it. Under your own rules, every sale you hold is already void. I'm not destroying anything that belongs here. I'm enforcing your terms."

The pages fluttered wildly now, all of them at once, a bird beating itself against its own covers. The whispering started again—my mother's voice, Ombre's voice, Nim's, offering me the chair, the table, the quiet heart, the safe books—

"No," I said.

And then, because some things must be entered to be true, I spoke the last entry aloud, the way a clerk closes a day's book:

"Gilded Ledger, subsidiary account, Court Beneath the Mountain. Final reconciliation. All assets returned to their rightful holders. All debts forgiven. All balances zeroed. Entered by Lior Oyelaran—" my voice caught, and I let it catch, because a numb voice would have been their victory "—who was never yours, who was always free, who simply hadn't been told. Witnessed by Veth, god of accounts, who kept faith with me through nine years of silence, and whom I hereby pay in full."

*Witnessed,* said the god, and the word rang through the chamber like a struck bell.

I tipped the lamp.

The fire took the vellum the way dawn takes a window—gently at first, then all at once. The book did not scream. It sighed, a long exhalation a century in the holding, and as the flames ran down the columns every line of ink lifted off the page like sparks returning to a bonfire, thousands upon thousands of small bright souls rising through the stone, through the roots, through the mountain itself—

—and above me, muffled by fathoms of rock, I heard the first jar shatter, and then ten, and then a sound like rain beginning, and then a sound like the sky opening, and the hunger of the court met the returning dead all at once, everywhere, in the dark.

The chamber filled with light. The chair, wherever it was, cracked down its middle—I felt it go, felt a hundred years of carved patience split. The heat pushed me back a step, then another, and Veth's hand found mine, solid at last, a small dry human-shaped hand gripping hard.

*Run,* the god said, with feeling. *Now, child. *Run.**

Behind us, the mountain began, at long last, to fall.
