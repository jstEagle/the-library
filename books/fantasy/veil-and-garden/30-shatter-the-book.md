## 30. Shatter the Book

**Lior**

The master ledger lay open on its lectern of black glass, and it was breathing.

Lior had not expected that. Nine years of accounting had prepared them for many things—forged columns, impossible sums, debts written in blood and bone—but not for a book that breathed. Each page rose and fell in a slow rhythm, like a sleeper, like a lung, like the mountain itself. The vellum was warm. When Lior set a palm against it, they felt a pulse that was almost, but not quite, their own.

*Every soul in this book is still alive,* Veth's presence said—not in words now, for the god's voice was still finding itself after nine years of theft, but in pressure, in certainty, in the way candlelight bent toward Lior as if listening. *They are only filed.*

"Filed," Lior repeated aloud. Their voice cracked on the word.

*Filed. Indexed. Accruing interest.* A pause, vast and tired. *I kept accounts too, little clerk. I know what filing means when the filer has no heart.*

The vault around them was shaking. Somewhere above, Emberless's fire had failed entirely—the heat of her dying had come down through the glass roots like a tide going out—and the court's containment was unraveling thread by thread. Lior could hear it: the distant music of the Long Table turning discordant, the polite footsteps of tireless servants stumbling for the first time in a century, the sound of masks cracking like river ice.

Sefa stood at the lectern's edge, ink-stained fingers hovering over the page where Lior's own name sat in a century-old hand: *Lior Oyelaran, one (1) soul, infant, condition excellent, delivered to Court Beneath the Mountain, paid in full.* Beside it, in fresher ink, the amendment Ombre had hidden for twenty years: *half reclaimed, debt outstanding.*

"You don't have to do this," Sefa said quietly. "There's another way. We could audit it. Amend it. Erase your line only, keep the guild's leverage, keep your—"

"My power." Lior laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "Half a soul's worth of feeling, and they'd call it power."

Nim was at the door, holding it against the press of fleeing servants, ash from the failing dragon streaked across her face like war paint. "Whatever you're going to do," she called, "do it soon. The nobles are feeding. Not on us yet—we smell like dragon—but on anyone who doesn't."

Lior looked down at the ledger.

They thought of every entry they had ever made in the Gilded Ledger's books. Bottled grief, weighed to the dram. Borrowed years, amortized with interest. A name sold twice because no one checked whether the first buyer still lived. They had told themselves for years that meticulousness was a virtue, that invisibility was safety, that the numbness in their chest was simply temperament—a cold gift that made them good at their work.

It was theft. It had always been theft. And here, under their hands, was the account book of every theft ever committed beneath this mountain.

*You can erase your line,* Veth said gently. *Take back what is yours. Walk out whole. No one would blame you. Even I would not blame you.*

"I know," Lior said.

And that was exactly why they couldn't.

* * *

**Nim**

Nim Ashvael had watched a dragon die before. She had been seven, and it had been small—a gutter-wyrm that the city poisoned by accident, curled in the drainage channel with its wings folded like a broken umbrella. Her father had held her hand and made her watch, because he said a keeper who looked away once would look away forever.

This was nothing like that.

Emberless was dying above them, and the whole mountain knew it. Nim could feel the old dragon's fire going out the way you feel a hearth go cold in a large room—not all at once but in degrees, warmth withdrawing like a tide, and everything the warmth had been holding back beginning to stir.

Through the vault's high vents came the sounds of the feast hall. Nim had imagined, all her life, what would happen when the fire failed. Her father's stories had been full of teeth and wings and noble monsters unleashed. The reality was worse and stranger: the nobles were not roaring. They were *dining*. There was a sound like many people eating politely, quickly, with growing urgency, and between bites, conversation—pleasant, unhurried, discussing vintage years and the quality of this century's harvest.

The harvest was people. The servants who had polished their floors and lit their candles and smiled their painted smiles for a hundred years were learning, all at once, that their wages had been their own lives, siphoned a spoonful at a time.

Nim heard the first scream of realization echo down through the glass roots, and then the second, and then a sound she had never heard in any story: the servants screaming *together*, and then not screaming at all but shouting, and then the unmistakable thunder of a hundred years of unpaid labor remembering its own strength.

"They're rioting," Sefa said, looking up from the ledger with wide eyes.

"Good," Nim said, and meant it, and was surprised by how much she meant it. Her father would have said a keeper's duty was to contain, to hold, to keep the old balances balanced even as they killed you. But her father had also died keeping a fire lit for people who never once said thank you, and Nim had spent the whole climb up the mountain road learning the difference between duty and devotion.

"Lior." She crossed the vault in three strides and put her hand on their shoulder—the same gesture that had stopped them from signing away everything to a masked woman with their mother's voice in her mouth. "Whatever you choose, we're behind you. That's not a line. That's an entry. Write it down somewhere."

Lior looked at her hand. Looked at her. Something moved behind their eyes that Nim had learned to read on the road—the flicker of a person discovering, late and painfully, that they had feelings after all, and that the feelings had opinions.

"The book breathes," Lior said. "If I destroy it while it's still—while everyone's still in it—"

"Then everyone comes out," Nim said. "That's how it works. My father kept souls the same way he kept coals—in containers, banked, alive. You break the container, the fire goes home. It doesn't die. Fire always goes home."

"And if some of them have been in there so long there's no home left?"

"Then they get to find out," Nim said, "instead of being filed."

* * *

**Sefa**

Later—much later, when Sefa sat in the rebuilt archive of the Gilded Ledger with ink on her hands and dawn in the windows—she would try to write down what happened in the moment Lior Oyelaran lifted the master ledger from its lectern, and she would fail, because archives are for facts and what happened next was not a fact. It was a verdict.

But she was an archivist, and she tried anyway, and this is what she managed:

The book screamed.

Not metaphorically. Sefa had spent her career among texts; she knew the difference between a binding cracking and something else. The master ledger had been bound in the skin of the original soul-seller's craft—she had provenance for that, terrible provenance, traced through six hands and one grave—and when Lior raised it off the lectern, every page strained against its stitching like a bird against a cage it had forgotten it hated.

And inside the pages, behind the ink, Sefa could see them. Souls. Thousands of them, each rendered as a line of elegant script, each script a person, each person pressed flat into two dimensions and stored at temperature. Some of the lines were bright and recent. Some were faded nearly to nothing—a century of accrual, of interest paid in vitality, until the original entry was barely legible.

One line near the middle glowed faintly gold. Half-reclaimed. Debt outstanding.

"That one's yours," Sefa said. Her voice sounded very far away. "When it breaks, you'll feel it. All of it. Twenty years of stolen feeling, coming home at once. Lior—it might be too much. People have been unmade by less."

Lior turned the ledger over in their hands, weighing it the way they weighed everything: precisely, seriously, as if the weight mattered, as if the weight had always mattered to them even when they pretended it hadn't.

"Sefa," they said. "In your catalogue of the freed—you started it in the tunnels, I saw you writing—what did you write for me? Just now. What am I?"

Sefa blinked. "What?"

"When you document everyone. What am I, right now, in your book?"

Sefa didn't have to think. She had been thinking it since the well gate, since the tollhouse, since the moment a numb little clerk had grieved a memory they couldn't remember losing. "A person," she said. "Just a person. Named. Accounted for. Free."

Lior nodded slowly, as if confirming a figure against a source document.

"Then the rest is arithmetic," they said, and shattered the master ledger against the lectern of black glass.

* * *

**Lior**

The sound was not loud. That was the strangest part. After the scream of the binding, after the groan of the mountain, after a night of riots and dragonfire and polite monsters, the actual breaking of the master ledger was quiet—a sound like a spine of ice giving way, like a held breath released, like a single pen stroke ending in a period.

Then the pages came apart.

Not torn—*released*. Every leaf separated from its neighbor and lifted into the air, and as each page rose, the ink lifted off it, rising in threads of light, thousands upon thousands of threads, each one a soul unflattening, unfolding, remembering its dimensions. The vault filled with them. They moved like migrating birds, like snow falling upward, like a congregation standing after long kneeling.

And they went home.

Lior felt it happen. Felt it in the glass roots, which shivered and rang like struck bells as the servants' stolen vitality poured back into its owners. Felt it in the walls, where the imprisoned dead of a century—the ones whose bodies had long since failed—rose up as light and passed outward through stone without troubling to open doors. Felt it, most of all, in their own chest.

The gold thread found them last. Of course it did. Theirs had always been the most complicated entry in the book: sold, half-bought-back, hidden, outstanding. It came home slowly, the way warmth comes back to a room, the way feeling returns to a hand that slept wrong. Lior braced for pain.

What came instead was grief—enormous, specific, *wonderful* grief—for a memory of Ombre teaching them letters that they had paid away at a tollhouse and could now mourn properly, knowing why. Grief for the self they might have been. Grief for nine years of prayers thrown at a silent god. Grief for every trade they'd ever witnessed and called business.

And underneath the grief, like a coin at the bottom of a well: joy. Small, stubborn, entirely theirs.

"Oh," Lior said. "Oh, *this* is what it's supposed to feel like."

Nim caught their arm as they swayed. "Too much?"

"No," Lior said, laughing and crying in a manner that would have horrified their former self. "Exactly enough. For the first time in my life, exactly enough."

Above them, through the vents, the mountain changed its voice.

* * *

**Nim**

The court did not explode. Nim had expected explosion—her father's stories again, all teeth and collapse. Instead, the Court Beneath the Mountain *deflated*.

She saw it as they ran for the ascent tunnels, through corridors where glass roots were shedding their light like trees dropping leaves in autumn. The nobility stood in the feast hall amid the wreckage of their centennial meal, and the masks that had hidden their faces for a hundred years were sliding off—not torn away, just slipping, suddenly too heavy for the faces beneath.

And the faces beneath were ordinary.

That was the horror and the mercy of it, all at once. Nim watched a great lady of the Long Table—six feet of porcelain menace an hour ago—become a small grey woman blinking in confusion at her own hands, counting her fingers, counting again, discovering wrinkles she had not earned honestly and did not know how to be proud of. A chamberlain who had pursued them through the tunnels with tireless courtesy sat down abruptly on the floor and began, very quietly, to sob, because his knees hurt, because his back ached, because he was ninety years old and had not felt either in a century.

No one had told them mortality would feel like this. No one had told them it would feel like *anything*.

"They're aging," Sefa whispered, watching a young-looking lord's hair silver between one breath and the next. "All at once. All the borrowed time coming due."

"It was never theirs," Nim said. "None of it was ever theirs."

Around the deflating court, the servants were standing very still, watching their masters shrink into human beings. Nim braced for violence—the riot was still hot in the corridors, still singing. But what she saw instead was stranger and better: a scullery maid stepping forward and taking the sobbing chamberlain's arm, not gently, not cruelly, just practically, the way you steady someone who has fallen.

"Up," the maid said. "You'll want to learn to walk again. It takes practice, being old."

Somewhere far above, the last ember of Emberless drifted down through the mountain's throat like a leaf, and Nim felt the old dragon's long duty end the way you feel a held note resolve—not silence, but rest.

* * *

**Lior**

They climbed out through the ascent tunnels as the mountain came apart around them—not violently, but the way a fist opens. The glass roots dimmed and cooled and became merely beautiful. The candles of the Long Table burned down to honest stubs. And everywhere, in every corridor, souls were arriving home in bodies that had forgotten how to hold them, and the mountain filled with the sounds of people relearning themselves: coughing, laughing, weeping, asking each other their names because for a hundred years no one had needed names, only functions.

Veth walked beside Lior the whole way up. Not invisibly anymore. The god had taken a shape—small, tired, ink-stained, carrying a ledger of their own under one arm—and though the god said nothing, Lior felt the silence differently now. It was not absence. It was attention.

At the tunnel mouth, where the mountain's collapsed throat opened onto a slope of scree and the first grey suggestion of dawn, Lior stopped and turned back.

Below, the Court Beneath the Mountain settled into ruin with a sound like a long sigh. Above it, the sky was doing something Lior had no ledger-line for: lightening, slowly, indecently, as if the world intended to simply continue.

"Nine years," Lior said to the god. "Nine years I prayed and you said nothing."

Veth looked at them with eyes the color of dried ink. Then the god opened the small ledger, licked the tip of a pencil, and wrote one word on a fresh page. Turned it around.

*Ledger.*

Lior stared at it. Then, despite everything—the ruined night, the stolen decades, the grief still sitting in their chest like a swallowed stone—they laughed. It came up from somewhere new, somewhere the gold thread had refilled, and it hurt, and it was the best thing they had ever felt.

"Yeah," Lior said. "Yeah. One word. Took you nine years and a riot to say it."

Veth shrugged, a small, tired, profoundly accountant-like shrug, and began writing again, faster now, the pencil moving with purpose. Lior leaned over to see.

The god was starting a new book. At the top of the first page, in careful, ancient handwriting, was a heading: *Debts Owed To Me — Outstanding.* And beneath it, the first entry, freshly inked:

*Lior Oyelaran. Nine years of unanswered prayer. One soul, returned. Status: creditor.*

Lior read it twice. The morning came up over the mountain, and somewhere below them a freed city was learning to be hungry, mortal, and alive, and Nim was saying something about embers, and Sefa was already writing.

"Well," Lior said to the god, tucking the numbness away forever and pocketing the ache like a coin they intended to spend carefully. "I suppose we should discuss terms."

* * *

They stood together at the mouth of the mountain—two clerks, a keeper, an archivist, and a god—as the sun cleared the ridge and the world, unfiled at last, began.
