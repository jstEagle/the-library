## 32. The God Who Keeps Accounts

The garden was not yet a garden. It was a wound in the mountain's flank, a mouth of collapsed glass and powdered stone where the Court Beneath the Mountain had breathed its last, and Lior had been kneeling in it since before dawn with dirt under their fingernails and no memory of deciding to kneel.

They had brought seeds. That was the strange part. They had walked out of the ruin with Nim and Sefa and Ombre, had eaten, had slept twelve hours like the dead, and had woken before the sun with their hands already moving—wrapping seeds in cloth, filling a waterskin, walking back up the mountain road alone while the others slept. The road that remembered had nothing left to show them now; it was only a road, rutted and ordinary, and Lior had passed the tollhouse of small mercies without stopping, though something in their chest had leaned toward it like a plant toward light.

Now they knelt in the ash-soft soil at the mountain's ruined mouth and pressed seeds into the dark with two fingers, one by one, the way they entered figures into a ledger: deliberate, exact, each one given its own small space.

"You're planting too deep."

Lior did not startle. That was the second strange part. Nine years of silence, nine years of prayers poured into a god who gave back nothing but the taste of wax and the weight of unanswered tithes—and now, on the morning after the world broke open, a voice spoke at their shoulder as if it had never been gone, and Lior's heart did not so much as stumble.

"Half a finger's depth for these," the voice went on. "Any deeper and they'll rot before they find the light. You'd know that if you'd ever kept a garden instead of only keeping accounts."

Lior sat back on their heels.

The god of Veth was small.

That was the first thing, and it took a long moment to reconcile with everything Lior had imagined—the vast silent presence they had prayed to over candle flames, the tithe-jars stacked nine deep in the guild vault, the single word *Ledger* that had flooded the mountain like a bell struck inside their skull. Lior had imagined something enormous. Something with wings, maybe, or eyes like ledger-lines, or a voice made of every prayer ever spoken into the quiet.

What stood at the edge of the ruined mouth of the court was a person roughly Lior's own height, wrapped in a coat that had been mended many times and mended well, with ink stains on the fingers and shadows under the eyes so deep they looked carved. Their hair was gray, not with age but with the particular grayness of someone who has worked through too many nights. They held a ledger under one arm—a real one, leather-bound, corners worn soft—and a pencil behind one ear, and they looked, all things considered, like a clerk at the end of a very long fiscal year.

"You're—" Lior began, and stopped, because there were too many sentences queued behind that word and none of them would fit through the door.

"Tired," said Veth. "Yes. Sit down if you're going to stare. Kneeling is bad for the knees; I'd know, I invented them."

"I didn't invent you kneeling," Veth said, before Lior could speak. "You did that yourself. I want that noted. There's a version of this conversation where you take credit for my devotion, and I'd like to strike it from the record now."

Lior's mouth did something complicated. "Noted," they managed.

Veth came closer, stepping carefully over the broken glass roots that still laced the soil like the veins of some slaughtered animal, and crouched beside Lior with the easy economy of someone whose body had done this ten thousand times. Up close the tiredness was worse. It wasn't the tiredness of a night without sleep. It was the tiredness of years—of a long, grinding, unthanked labor—and Lior recognized it with a jolt, because they had seen it every morning for fifteen years in the polished surface of the guild's counting-room mirror.

It was their own tiredness. Or rather: it was the tiredness Lior had always assumed was theirs by nature, the flat gray weather of their inner life, the numbness they had believed was temperament. Here it was, worn openly by a god.

"You went silent," Lior said. The words came out rougher than intended. "Nine years. I prayed every night. I lit the candles. I paid the tithes. I—" Their voice caught on something sharp. "I thought I had failed some test. I thought you were testing whether I would keep faith without reward, and I kept failing it, because some nights I couldn't make myself feel anything at all when I prayed, and I thought *that* was the failure."

Veth was quiet for a moment. When they spoke, their voice was gentle in the way that only very tired people can be gentle—without any performance in it at all.

"The court stole my voice," they said. "A hundred years ago, and then again, more thoroughly, nine years ago, when they found a way to bottle what remained of it. They didn't steal your faith, Lior. They stole the *answer*. And then they let you believe the silence was yours."

The words landed in Lior's chest like stones dropped down a well.

"They kept your voice-jars in the soul-vault," Lior said slowly. "With the rest. Shelf forty-one. I saw them. I thought—I thought they were offerings. Tithes owed *to* you. I catalogued them myself, three years ago, and I never once wondered why a god's tribute would be stored beneath a throne."

"Because you were fifteen and meticulous and taught never to ask questions that weren't on the inventory," Veth said. "You catalogued my stolen voice and filed it correctly. I have never been angrier about anything being handled *competently*." A ghost of humor moved across the worn face, there and gone. "You want to know the worst part? I heard every prayer. Every one. I just couldn't answer. Do you know what that's like—to hear someone call your name for nine years and have your mouth full of someone else's glass?"

"Yes," Lior said quietly. "As of about four days ago, yes."

Something passed between them then—not forgiveness, not yet, but the recognition of two people who had been robbed by the same thieves and blamed themselves for the robbery.

Lior looked down at their hands. Dirt in the creases, a scratch across one knuckle from the glass roots. Half a soul's worth of feeling, Ombre had called it, and even that half was new, barely a week old, raw as a burn. They tried to imagine what it would be like to feel whole—not more, not better, just *present* in their own life the way other people seemed effortlessly to be—and found they could not. It was like trying to remember a room they'd slept in as an infant.

"Why here?" they asked. "Why now? You've been free since the vault shattered. You could have gone anywhere. Gods must have... options."

"This is where my accounts are." Veth patted the worn ledger under their arm. "Every prayer you ever prayed is in here. Every candle. Every tithe. Every night you knelt on cold stone and got nothing back and knelt anyway. I've spent the last day reading them." They paused. "Do you know what an accountant does when the accounts are finally balanced?"

"Audits," Lior said. "Then closes the books. Then opens new ones."

"Closes the books," Veth agreed. "And then opens new ones." They set the ledger down on the flattened earth between them, and turned it around, and opened it, and Lior saw—with a strange lurch—that the pages were not full. The early pages were dense with entries in a hand like their own but older, wearier, each line a date and a candle and a silence. But the last written page ended nine years ago, and every page after it was blank, waiting, the paper still crisp.

"Nine years of blank pages," Veth said. "Because I couldn't write back. Now—" they tapped the page with an ink-stained finger, "—now I can. Which brings us to business."

"Business," Lior repeated.

"I'm offering you a covenant. A new one. The old terms are void—they died with the master ledger, along with everything else that book held. So we renegotiate." Veth's eyes, Lior noticed now, were the color of strong tea, and absolutely unblinking. "But I want to be clear about something first, because I know how your mind works, Lior Oyelaran, and I know you'll spend the rest of the day finding the trap in this if I don't name it aloud. There is no trap. There is no test. There is no hidden clause. I watched the court dress a cage up as mercy and offer you a chair, and I am telling you plainly: I am not the court. What I have to give, I give freely, and what I ask, I'll ask to your face."

Lior sat with that. Around them the morning was warming; somewhere below, birdsong had begun, tentative, the first birds daring the mountain's ruined slopes. The seeds lay in their shallow furrows between them and the god, tiny and patient.

"Say it, then," Lior said. "The terms."

"Term one." Veth held up one ink-stained finger. "No more invisibility. The old covenant asked you to be unnoticed, unremarkable, a shadow that kept perfect books. That was *my* covenant, made in fear, after the court took my voice—I thought if my faithful were silent and small, the court wouldn't notice them either. It was cowardice dressed as humility, and it cost you fifteen years of being overlooked by everyone including yourself. That term is dead. You will be seen. You will be *remembered*. People will know your name and you will have to bear that."

Lior flinched. Actually flinched, physically, at the idea—and Veth watched them do it with something terrible and tender in their tea-colored eyes.

"It will be hard," Veth said. "I know. But term one stands."

"Term two," they continued, before Lior could argue. "No more unpaid devotion. Every prayer answered gets an entry. Every entry gets a price. Not a sacrifice—I don't want your grief, the grief-trade was the court's invention and it rotted them from the inside. A price. Something honest. You're a mercenary and an accountant; you understand fair exchange better than anyone alive. So we'll trade. You bring me your attention, your honesty, your work when work is needed. I bring you answers. Actual ones. Out loud. In words."

"That's it?" Lior said. "That's the whole covenant?"

"There's a third term." Veth hesitated—and Lior, watching closely, saw the god look, for one bare instant, uncertain. Small. Ashamed. "Term three. This one's mine to pay, not yours. I owe you an apology, and apologies go in the ledger same as everything else. Nine years I listened to you and said nothing. Fifteen years I let you believe your numbness was your nature. I told myself it was strategy, survival, patience—but the truth is I was ashamed, and shame makes cowards of gods same as men. So: I'm sorry. Entered formally, witnessed by the morning, dated today. It stays in the book forever. I don't get to erase it, and neither do you."

The garden-soil was soft under Lior's knees. The birdsong was getting braver. And Lior found, to their considerable surprise, that their eyes were burning—which was itself remarkable, because a month ago they had not known their eyes *could* do that.

"I used to talk to you," Lior said. Their voice came out low and uneven. "When I was doing the inventory at night. I'd narrate the whole thing. Bottle of grief, one, condition fair. Borrowed year, one, seller deceased. I thought—if I couldn't pray properly, at least I could keep you company while I worked. I thought you probably found it tedious."

"I found it," Veth said, "to be the finest accounting I have ever heard performed. And no. Not tedious. It was the only conversation I'd had in nine years where the other party didn't know they were talking to a prisoner." They closed the old ledger gently. "You kept me company, Lior. You just didn't know it. We were both in the dark, filing our entries, waiting for morning."

Lior bowed their head. The tears came then, quietly, without drama—half a soul's worth of feeling, and even half was apparently enough to drown a person when the dam finally went. They cried the way they did everything: precisely, silently, with excellent posture. Veth waited, and did not comment, and handed them, after a decent interval, a handkerchief that smelled faintly of ink and candlewax.

"So," Lior said at last, wiping their face with the brisk efficiency of someone closing a difficult account. "If I accept. If we sign. What happens tomorrow? What does a god and their... their accountant *do*, exactly, once the great evil is audited and the souls are freed and the mountain's growing beans?"

"Beans," Veth said, with approval, glancing at the seed rows. "Good choice. Fixing nitrogen." They settled back against a slab of fallen glass-root, getting comfortable, clearly prepared to stay awhile. "Tomorrow? Tomorrow you finish planting. Tomorrow Sefa starts her catalogue and you help her, because no one alive labels things like you do. Tomorrow Ombre reopens the Ledger and discovers that running an honest guild is twice the work and half the profit, and complains magnificently. Tomorrow Nim teaches that ember of hers not to sulk." A pause. The tea-colored eyes came back to Lior's, and gentled. "And tomorrow, when you pray—or talk, or narrate your inventory, or whatever we're calling it now—I answer. Same time every evening, if you like. Regular hours. I'm told mortals find that reassuring."

"Regular hours," Lior echoed. A laugh escaped them, small and cracked and real. "You're offering me *office hours* with a god."

"I'm offering you predictability," Veth said. "Which is what mercy looks like, when it's been through an audit." They held out one ink-stained hand, palm up, over the open blank pages of the ledger. "Well? Terms are stated. Prices are named. Apology is entered. Do we have an accord, Lior Oyelaran—or do you want to sleep on it and negotiate harder terms in the morning? I should warn you, I respect the second option enormously, and I'll match any counteroffer."

Lior looked at the offered hand. At the worn ledger with its nine years of blank pages. At the shallow furrows of seed-earth between them, each seed tucked in at half a finger's depth, patient in the dark, waiting for light.

Somewhere below, faintly, carried up the mountain road, came the sound of Sefa's voice—already awake, already talking, already cataloguing—and Nim's laugh answering it, and Ombre's gruffer tones rising over both. Their family, arguing about breakfast on the mountainside. Waiting for them. Knowing, this time, exactly where they were.

"No counteroffer," Lior said. They reached out and took the god's hand. It was warm, and human-sized, and ink-stained, and it gripped back. "One amendment, though. Entered now, before witnesses." They nodded at the birdsong, the morning, the distant voices. "*Them.* Whatever covenant we sign—it has room in it for them. I'm done making bargains that only have space for one."

Veth smiled. It transformed the worn face entirely, the way dawn transforms a ledger-page—suddenly there was color where there had only been columns.

"Noted," the god said, and wrote it down. "Witnessed by the morning, dated today. Room for all of them." The pencil paused. Then, in a lighter tone: "Although I should mention—your mother's name surfaced in the catalogue last night. Sefa found it among the returned. She's alive, Lior. She's in a port city three weeks south, and she's listed under her own name, in her own hand, and she has been asking the harbormaster's clerks about a child sold from the Well Gate a hundred years' worth of winters ago."

The world went very still and very bright.

"She's been *asking*," Lior repeated.

"For weeks," Veth said. "Since the mountain fell. She felt it, I think. Souls know when they come home—even the ones that were never taken, only left behind." The god closed the ledger and rose, joints cracking, tired and smiling. "Sefa's packing her catalogue as we speak. Nim's arguing about provisions. Ombre's pretending she isn't coming and packing anyway."

Lior knelt a moment longer in the soil of the garden that was not yet a garden, hands dirty, face wet, heart—half a soul, newly returned, still learning its own weight—beating like a second, smaller ledger, every line suddenly worth keeping.

Then they stood, and brushed off their knees, and followed the sound of their family down the mountain road—toward the harbor, toward the south, toward a woman at a well gate who had been asking after them for weeks.

Behind them, unhurried, taking notes, the god who keeps accounts fell into step.
