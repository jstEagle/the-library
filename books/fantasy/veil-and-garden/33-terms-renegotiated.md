## 33. Terms Renegotiated

The god of silent accounts was smaller than a prayer.

That was the first thing I understood when the wonder burned off and left only morning behind us — real morning, mountain air gone thin and clean above the collapsed mouth of the court, the sun coming up over the Ashvael ridgeline like a coin being turned to catch the light. Veth sat on a fallen slab of glass-root, feet not quite touching the ground, and looked for all the world like a clerk at the end of a very long fiscal year.

Small. Tired. Robes the grey of old ledger paper, ink-stained at the cuffs. A face like someone you'd pass in a counting-house and forget by supper — except that when I looked directly at those eyes, they held the same patient weight as the deep shelves of the Gilded Ledger's vault, the ones that had never once been inventoried because no one dared.

Nine years I had prayed into that silence. Nine years of tithes laid on the altar-shelf in my rented room: a copper for every contract closed clean, a thread of my own hair for every one that went sour, a written confession on the nights I'd lied to myself about what the guild traded in. Nine years of nothing coming back.

And now here the god was, sitting on a rock, holding a small battered book against their chest the way a child holds a thing taken from them and only just returned.

"You're staring," Veth said. Their voice was dry and quiet, an abacus clicking somewhere far away. "Most of mine do, at first. Then they get angry. You may skip ahead if you like. I've read your file."

"I don't have a file."

"Everyone has a file." The god patted the slab beside them. "Sit. We have terms to discuss, and I am told — " something flickered across the tired face, wry and wounded at once — "that I have nine years of negotiations to make up for."

I did not sit. Not yet. There was a discipline to this, and I clung to it the way a drowning accountant clings to a ledger: numbers first, feelings after. Feelings were chapter nineteen's business, and Ombre's, and mine alone, later, in the dark, where grief could be entered properly under its own heading.

"I want to talk terms," I said.

Veth's eyebrows rose a fraction. "You want to *negotiate*. With me."

"With the god who took my devotion for nine years and gave back silence." I heard my own voice go flat and precise, guild-voice, counter-voice, the voice I used on merchants who thought a mercenary's sword-arm came with a soft head. "Yes. That's exactly what I want to do."

* * *

We walked down from the ridge while we talked, because Veth said gods think better in motion and I said clerks think better at standing desks, and we compromised on a slow descent through the scrub-pine slopes where the snow still held last night's shape. Nim was asleep by Emberless's cooling flank, wrapped in the dragon-keeper's coat, one hand resting on the single ember she carried in an iron lantern. Sefa had claimed the ruined tollhouse half a mile below and was already, I knew without looking, cataloguing it. The three of us had developed a habit, these last weeks: each of us finding our own corner of any ruin and making it ours. It was, I suspected, what family looked like when it was built instead of born.

"So," Veth said. "Terms."

"Terms." I stopped walking and turned to face the god fully, and I made myself hold those tired eyes. "First clause. No more invisibility."

"Invisibility?"

"You heard me." My hands wanted to shake. I let them. Let the god see it. "Nine years I prayed to you, and you know what I prayed *for*, most nights? Not power. Not protection. I prayed to be seen. By anyone. And the one being I gave everything to couldn't be bothered to answer — "

"I could not *speak*," Veth said, and the quiet of it cracked something open. "Lior. The court stole my voice. Every word I tried to send you went into a jar in their vault. Do you know what it is to watch a faithful soul kneel night after night and to be unable to say even *I hear you*? To feel their prayers land on you like letters delivered to a house with no mouth?" The god's hands tightened on the little book. "I wrote answers. Hundreds of them. In frost, in spilled salt, in the arrangement of crows. But writing is not voice, and you — " a pause, and something almost shy moved through the ancient tired face — "you never learned to read frost. You learned to read ledgers. So I waited for the day you would come to the vault yourself. And you did. Three days ago. You broke into a sealed room to steal back your own soul, and while you were there, you shattered forty-one jars of my stolen voice without knowing whose they were."

I remembered that. The melee of the riot, glass bursting around me, a sound like a choir being released. I had thought it was the servants' souls singing free.

"Some of it was," Veth said, reading me as easily as Sefa read marginalia. "And some of it was me. Saying your name. For the first time in nine years."

"*Ledger*," I said. "That was the word you answered with. One word. After nine years."

"It was the truest word I had." The god smiled, faint and crooked. "You were standing in front of the master ledger of the Court Beneath the Mountain, holding a hammer. What else would I have said?"

Despite everything — despite the rage still banked in my chest like a coal I intended to carry for a long time yet — I laughed. Once. Short. It surprised us both.

"That," I said, "is exactly the kind of answer that makes me think negotiation might actually work. Which brings me to clause two."

* * *

We reached the tollhouse as the sun cleared the peaks. Sefa had gotten a fire going in the ruined hearth and set out three tin cups and a dented kettle, and when she saw the god walking beside me she went very still, then bowed — a quick archivist's bow, the kind given to rare manuscripts — and put out a fourth cup without a word. Veth looked at that fourth cup for a long moment.

"No one has poured for me in two hundred years," the god said quietly.

"Then drink," Sefa said, "and note it in your file. Witnessed: Sefa Anselm, archivist, third rank struck but never struck in her own opinion, offers tea freely given." She pushed the cup across the table. "No tithe attached. I've had enough of tithes to last several lifetimes, and I imagine you have too."

Veth drank the tea like it was a sacrament. Maybe, for a god of accounts, it was.

Nim woke when the smell of the fire reached her, came in rubbing sleep from her eyes, took one look at Veth, and said, "Huh. Smaller than I pictured." And then she sat down and poured herself tea, and just like that the god of silent accounts was having breakfast with three people in a ruined tollhouse, and something in the shape of the world had quietly rearranged itself.

But I hadn't come this far to be comfortable. I set my cup down and folded my hands, and the others — bless them, they knew me by now — went quiet.

"Clause two," I said. "No more unpaid devotion."

"Lior — "

"Hear the whole term before you rule on it." Guild-voice. Counter-voice. I had spent my whole life making sure no one could take advantage of me in a bargain, and I was not going to stop now, not even — *especially* not even — with a god. "For nine years I paid you. Copper, hair, confessions, hours. Hours, Veth. Do you know how many hours of my life went onto that altar-shelf? I counted once. Four hundred and sixty-two, over nine years. That's nineteen full days of my one short mortal life, given to a silence." I kept my voice level. It cost something. "Now. I understand why the silence happened. I believe you. I *felt* you, when the jars broke; I know the difference now between absence and theft. But understanding the debt doesn't erase it. The court stole your voice — fine, that's between you and the court, and the court is rubble. But the *arrangement* between you and me, the covenant — that was ours. And it was broken. And in the Gilded Ledger, when a contract is broken, the injured party renegotiates. So. Renegotiate we will."

The god set down the cup. For a long moment Veth simply looked at me, and I felt that look the way I felt the vault's cold: thorough, itemizing, missing nothing.

"You could have asked for anything," Veth said at last. "Power. Long life. The return of every hour you lost. Most of my faithful, when they finally stand in front of me, ask to be repaid."

"I'm not asking to be repaid. Repayment ends a contract. I'm asking to *amend* one." I pulled a blank page from my coat — I always carried paper now; Sefa had infected me with the habit — and a pencil, and I smoothed the page flat on the tollhouse table between the teacups. "Because here's what I've learned about contracts, and about gods, and about courts beneath mountains. Any agreement where one party cannot see the terms is not a covenant. It's a cage with good lighting. I will serve you, Veth. I think I always would have; I think whatever piece of me chose you at seventeen chose rightly. But I will do it *knowing*. With open books. On equal footing. Or I will walk away and keep accounts for no one but myself, and you can explain to the next faithful soul why the god of honest dealing lost the one accountant who ever audited him."

Sefa made a small sound into her tea that might have been a laugh or a prayer.

"And what," Veth said slowly, "do you imagine the amended terms should be?"

I had been drafting them since dawn. Of course I had. I turned the page toward the god, and I read aloud:

"*Amended Covenant between Lior Oyelaran, accountant, and Veth, keeper of accounts.* Clause one: visibility. The god shall be known to the faithful by name, by face, and by voice, and shall not again accept worship conducted into silence. Silence breeds courts. I've seen what grows in it." Veth flinched, and I did not soften the line. "Clause two: consideration. Devotion flows both directions or not at all. For every tithe given, the god owes an answer — not necessarily a gift. An answer. A sign. A word. Even a *no*, properly rendered, is worth more than nine years of nothing, and I will tell you now that a timely *no* would have saved me a decade of believing myself unworthy of notice."

The fire popped. Nim had gone very still, watching me with the expression she wore watching Emberless breathe — reverence braided with fierce protectiveness.

"Clause three," I continued, "scope of service. I am not your sword. I am not your martyr. I am your *accountant*. You keep the books of the world; I propose to help you keep them honestly. Souls, debts, tithes, names — wherever the numbers are being cooked, that's where you send me. Nothing more. Clause four: exit. Either party may dissolve this covenant with ninety days' written notice, no penalties, no curses, no smiting. A contract that cannot be left is not a contract. Ask the court." I looked up. "Clause five. The hard one."

Veth's eyes had not left the page. "There's a harder one than demanding a god answer his mail?"

"Clause five." My voice dropped. This was the part I had rewritten four times since dawn, and it still didn't sit right on the tongue. "*The god shall tell the faithful the truth, even when the truth costs the faithful something they love.*" I met those tired eyes. "You knew, didn't you. About the numbness. About the half-soul. Ombre bought back half of me twenty years ago and hid the debt, and you watched me live my whole adult life at half-feeling and never told me. Maybe you couldn't speak. Maybe you wrote it in frost and I didn't read frost. But clause five means next time, you find another way. You bang pots. You arrange the crows harder. Because a god who protects his faithful from painful truths isn't protecting them. He's doing what the court did. Deciding what they can bear. And I would rather be broken by truth than comfortable in a lie. I've *been* comfortable in a lie. It came with a chair that fit perfectly."

Silence in the tollhouse. The wind moved through the broken roof and stirred the ash of the hearth.

"You understand," Veth said at last, and the god's voice had changed — less clerk, more something older and wearier and strangely young, "that these terms bind me more tightly than any covenant I have accepted in three thousand years. Gods do not usually offer their faithful the right of exit. Gods do not usually offer their faithful *answers on demand*. If I agree to this, every charlatan who claims to speak for me becomes a fraud I am obligated to contradict. Every injustice done in my name becomes a debt on my own ledger. You are asking me to be accountable, Lior. To *be* what my own books demand."

"Yes," I said.

"Why?"

"Because I watched a court of immortals spend a century avoiding accountability, and I watched what it made of them. Beautiful, ageless, hungry, empty." I tapped the page. "Accountability is the difference between keeping accounts and being kept by them. You taught me that, or something did — maybe you, in the years when you could still teach. Take the terms, Veth. Be small and tired and answerable. It's better work than being worshipped. I have it on excellent authority."

The god looked at me for a long time. Then Veth picked up the pencil — my pencil, a cheap stub of cedar I'd bought in a market town for two coppers — and in a hand that was startlingly beautiful, looping and exact, the hand of someone who had written ten thousand thousand entries and never once been sloppy, the god signed beneath my five clauses.

Then Veth turned the page around, slid it across the table, and pointed at the empty line.

"Countersign," the god said. "A contract witnessed by neither party is just a wish. And Lior — " the tired eyes lifted, and for a moment I saw through them all the way down, to some vast dim hall where every debt ever owed was shelved and dusted and *remembered* — "for what it is worth, and I know precisely what it is worth because valuation is my entire nature: of all the souls who ever knelt at my shelf, yours was the devotion I grieved most. Not because it was largest. Because it was loneliest. These terms ensure that never happens again. To you, or to anyone."

I signed. My hand shook. I let it.

When the pencil lifted, the page glowed — briefly, softly, like a coal remembering fire — and then it was just paper again, and the covenant was just words, and the god was just a small tired figure drinking cold tea in a ruined tollhouse. And I felt — 

I felt *light*. Not emptied. Lightened. Like a weight I had carried so long I'd mistaken it for my skeleton had been formally, notarially, removed.

"There," Nim said into the quiet. "Now it's official. Does the god do weddings too, or — "

"Nim." Sefa, warning and amused.

"What? I'm asking for a friend."

* * *

We walked down out of the hills that afternoon, the four of us, toward the valley road and the long way home. Toward the Gilded Ledger, and Ombre, and whatever waited there — forgiveness, or judgment, or the terrible awkward work of rebuilding a family that had lied to itself for twenty years. I had a resignation letter to unwrite and a partnership to consider and a guildmaster to forgive, in whatever order mercy required.

Veth walked beside me, hands clasped behind their back like any clerk taking constitutionals, and did not offer to carry my pack, which I appreciated more than any miracle.

"One last matter," the god said, as the first rooftops of the valley town came into view below us, chimneys already smoking against the evening. "Your former employer. The Gilded Ledger trades in impossible things. Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names not yet spoken." A pause. "Names not yet spoken are, strictly speaking, inventory in *my* domain. And the guild has been trading in my domain for a century without so much as a licensing agreement."

I stopped on the road. Below us, the town lights were coming on one by one, and somewhere among them was a guildhouse with a green door and a woman inside it who had bought back half my soul and never told me, and I found — to my considerable surprise — that I was smiling.

"Are you saying what I think you're saying?"

"I am saying," Veth said, with the ghost of my own earlier laughter in that dry quiet voice, "that the god of accounts may require representation. An advocate. Someone to audit the guild's practices and negotiate a formal accord — trade in freely given things only, tithes honestly rendered, full disclosure of all spiritual inventory." Those tired eyes glinted. "Someone the guildmaster cannot intimidate, cannot deceive, and cannot, under any circumstances, refuse."

"You want me to walk into the Gilded Ledger," I said slowly, "with a god's power of attorney, and audit my own family."

"I want you to *renegotiate*," said Veth, "with your family. It's become rather your specialty."

I stood on the road a moment longer, the evening wind coming up the valley smelling of woodsmoke and rain and ordinary human suppers, and I thought about the child sold at the well gate, and the mercenary who grew from her, and the accountant standing here now with a signed covenant in her coat and a god at her elbow — visible, answerable, *hers* — and I squared my shoulders and started down the hill toward the lights.

"Fine," I said. "But I'm billing for travel."
