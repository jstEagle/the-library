## 35. Sefa's Catalogue of the Freed

The archive smelled different now.

Sefa noticed it on the first morning back, standing in the doorway of what had been the Gilded Ledger's authentication room with a cup of tea going cold in her hand. For thirty years that room had smelled of sealing wax and old vellum and the faint metallic tang of contracts that had been signed under duress—a smell she had never been able to name until she stood in the mountain's soul-vault and recognized it there, ten thousand times concentrated, rising from jars labeled in a neat clerk's hand. Now the room smelled of tea and morning light and the green things Nim had insisted on putting in every window: little pots of mint and thyme and one stubborn rose cutting that had come, impossibly, from the garden Lior was planting over the collapsed mouth of the mountain.

Growth where there had only ever been preservation. That was the difference. She could smell it.

She set her cup down, unrolled a fresh sheet of paper, and wrote at the top of it, in her careful archivist's hand:

*Catalogue of the Freed.*

Below it: *Being a true and complete record of every soul returned by the shattering of the master ledger of the Court Beneath the Mountain, compiled so that no person shall again be bought, sold, siphoned, or unlabeled.*

She had argued for three days about the title. Ombre had wanted *The Redemption Rolls*, which sounded like a debt forgiven rather than a theft corrected. Lior had wanted no title at all—"just names, Sefa, only names"—but Sefa had put her foot down with the full stubborn weight of her profession. A list without a purpose statement was just inventory. And inventory, she had learned in the dark beneath the throne, was how evil kept its books.

*No one is ever unlabeled again.* Not labeled like property—labeled like people. Named, dated, witnessed, *belonging to themselves*. It was the same information. It was an entirely different document.

She dipped her pen and began.

*Entry the first: Maren Duskwater, laundress, taken from the city of Vell forty-one years ago. Returned to her body whole. Currently residing above the old tollhouse, teaching her granddaughter to read. Witnessed by N. Ashvael.*

*Entry the second: Corvin Hale, fiddler, taken from—*

It would take years. She knew that going in. The court had been collecting for a century, and though most of the souls had flown home to their bodies the moment the ledger shattered—Sefa still dreamed of that moment, the sound of it, ten thousand small sighs released at once like a held breath finally let go—not all of them had. Some bodies had died in the intervening decades, and those souls had lingered, dazed, in the glass-root tunnels until Nim's ember-light guided them out into the air. Some had scattered. Some, the servants of the court who had fed on siphoned life without knowing it, were still learning how to be hungry in ordinary ways, how to eat bread instead of borrowed years. Every one of them deserved a line in the catalogue. Every one of them deserved to be *found*, the way Sefa wished someone had found her when she was twenty-three and freshly disgraced, her name struck from the Grand Archive's rolls, unlabeled, unclaimed, a person the world had decided to stop keeping records of.

She worked through the morning. Tea went cold; she made more. Sunlight crawled across the floor. Outside the window, the guild's courtyard rang with the sounds of its strange new life—the smith reforging the vault door into plowshares, because Ombre had a flair for symbolism and Sefa had stopped pretending she didn't love her for it—and somewhere below it all, faint as a heartbeat, she could feel the absence of the mountain. The city beneath had not vanished when the court fell. It had simply become a place, ordinary and mortal, its glass roots clouding slowly to quartz, its candle-lit halls open now to anyone who wanted to walk through them and shudder at what they remembered.

*Entry the eleventh: Pell Ashgrave, midwife, taken from the Well Gate district sixty-two years ago—*

Her pen stopped.

The Well Gate.

She had traced that provenance once before, in another life, in the first week after the codex had arrived and the floor had dropped out of everything. *Sold at the Well Gate.* An infant named Lior Oyelaran, sold to the Court Beneath the Mountain for one impossible thing. Sefa had spent four sleepless nights on that bill of sale, following its chain of custody backward through a century of misfiled documents, and she had found where the trail ended—in a tomb inside the mountain, in a preserved signing hand, in a forgery that had unraveled a kingdom—but she had never followed it forward. Forward meant the mother. Forward meant the woman who had stood at the Well Gate with a baby in her arms and made whatever terrible arithmetic had ended in a bill of sale. Sefa was an archivist. She could read anything written down. But some entries she had not let herself write, because writing them would have made them hers to carry.

Now the catalogue demanded it. That was the point of the catalogue. No omissions. No mercies of silence. If she left blanks, the blanks would become shadows, and shadows were where the next court would grow.

She turned back eleven pages, to where the returned souls from the mountain's final days were listed, cross-referenced against the court's own intake rolls—which Sefa had spent two weeks transcribing from the surviving fragments, hating every line of them, needing every line of them. The intake rolls recorded souls as they came in. The catalogue recorded them as they went out. Somewhere between those two columns was the truth of a hundred years of theft, and if you read them together, entry against entry, date against date—

She read them together.

Her hands were steady. Archivist's hands. They had held fragments of the master ledger itself; they did not shake for dates.

But her breath went strange.

There, in the intake roll for the year of the great fever, ninety-eight years before the shattering: *One (1) soul received in trade, adult female, name unrecorded per seller's request. Seller: private party, Well Gate district. Purpose of sale: protection of minor dependent. Payment rendered: passage and erasure.*

And there, in the catalogue she was building, in the column of souls that had lingered longest in the tunnels before following the ember home: *Adult female, name unrecorded per seller's request. Returned to body upon shattering. Body located: alive. Current residence: the port city of Sablehaven, four hundred leagues east, where she has lived these ninety-eight years under the name—*

Under the name the court had given her when they erased her.

Sefa sat very still.

Alive. Ninety-eight years old and alive, because the court's stolen years had flowed both ways—they had siphoned life from their servants, yes, but they had also paid their debts in it, and a woman sold to protect a child had been paid in decades she was never meant to have. She was alive. She was in Sablehaven. She had been alive this entire time, through every year of Lior's silence and every year of Lior's service and every year of Lior's careful, meticulous invisibility, alive four hundred leagues away under a name that wasn't hers, keeping a shop or tending a garden or walking a sea-wall every evening, not knowing that the child she had sold to save her had grown into a person who kept accounts for impossible things and prayed to a silent god and had never once believed themselves worthy of being loved.

Sefa thought about Lior's face when they talked about their mother, which was to say: the particular blankness they wore, the professional smoothness, the tell. She thought about chapter thirty-six of a story that hadn't happened yet, waiting in the future like a page not yet cut.

Then she did what archivists do. She finished the entry. She wrote the woman's assumed name, her location, her condition—*alive, aware, returned to self upon shattering; reports fragmented memory, improving*—and she dated it and signed it and blotted it, and only then did she allow her hands to shake, briefly, over the blotting paper, where no record would show.

* * *

Ombre found her at dusk, as Sefa had known she would. The guildmaster had a nose for unfinished business the way sharks had a nose for blood, and Sefa had left the intake roll and the catalogue open side by side on the desk, which was either carelessness or an invitation, and Sefa had never once in her life been careless.

Ombre read. Her face did what faces did around Ombre: nothing, then everything, compressed into the space of a breath. When she looked up, her eyes were wet and furious and proud, all at once, the way they'd been the day Lior resigned.

"You're certain."

"I'm an archivist," Sefa said. "I'm certain about paper. The rest wants witnessing." She tapped the final line. "But the paper is unambiguous. She came back to herself the night the ledger shattered. She remembers selling a child at the Well Gate. She has spent the last season trying to find out whether the child lived." Sefa's voice caught, just slightly, on the last part. "There's a letter. She sent it to the mountain's new custodians asking after 'the infant Oyelaran, sold in the year of the great fever.' It arrived three days ago. I've been sitting here deciding whether to give it to them since noon."

Ombre crossed the room and took Sefa's face in her two strong hands, the way she did when words weren't sufficient currency.

"Give it to them," Ombre said. "Tonight. Before you lose your nerve, and before they find it themselves—because they will, Sefa, they're better at finding things than either of us, and they deserve to hear it from family, not from a filing error."

"Family," Sefa repeated. The word sat strangely in her mouth, warm and terrifying. Three months ago she had been a disgraced archivist sleeping in a reading carrel, and now she stood in a guild that called itself a family and meant it, holding a letter that would remake a person she loved more than any book she'd ever authenticated.

"They're in the garden," Ombre said. "With the god. Taking notes." A ghost of a smile. "Go."

* * *

The garden over the mountain's mouth was young and chaotic and perfect. Lior had planted it without plan—Nim's suggestion, freely given, like everything in the reformed guild—and so roses fought with beans and a fig sapling leaned drunkenly against a trellis of moonflowers, and the whole slope breathed the green smell of things growing over graves, which was exactly what it was, and exactly what it should be.

Lior knelt in the dirt with ink-stained fingers and a small book propped on a stone. Beside them, visible now to anyone who looked with the right kind of attention, sat Veth: small, tired, gray-robed, an accountant-god with a second notebook, taking notes on the germination rates of moonflowers with an expression of profound scholarly satisfaction.

Sefa stopped at the garden gate. Her heart was doing something arrhythmic and embarrassing.

Lior looked up. Saw her face. Set down the pen.

"Sefa." They rose, brushing soil from their knees, and nine years of mercenary composure narrowed into focus. "What is it. What's wrong."

"Nothing's wrong." Her voice came out wrong anyway. She made herself hold out the letter. "Something's *right*. Which is worse, I know. But—"

Lior took the letter. Read the direction. Sefa watched them read it: watched the moment the unfamiliar hand and the unfamiliar port-city seal resolved into meaning, watched their breath go out of them the way it had gone out of ten thousand souls the night the ledger shattered.

"She's alive," Lior said. Not a question. Their voice was very quiet and very even, the voice of a person holding themselves together with accounting.

"Alive. Awake. Remembering." Sefa stepped through the gate, close enough to touch. "Lior. She's been looking for you. Since the night the mountain fell, she's been sending letters, asking after a child sold at the Well Gate. She never stopped—" Sefa stopped herself, because the next word was *loving* and it wasn't hers to spend. "She never stopped looking."

Behind Lior, the god Veth closed their notebook and looked up, and their ancient tired eyes held something that in anyone else Sefa would have called hope.

Lior stood in the middle of their own garden, in the last light, holding a letter four hundred leagues and ninety-eight years wide, and Sefa saw them do the thing they always did—the invisible thing, the retreat toward competence, toward the ledger, toward the safe arithmetic of *I am not worth traveling for*—and she saw them stop. Deliberately. The way you stop yourself from biting a wound. Nine years of numbness stolen and returned; a season of learning to feel at half a soul's worth and then a whole one; a family standing in a doorway behind her, drawn by the gravity of the moment, Nim with dirt on her hands and Ombre with tears she wasn't hiding anymore.

"I'll need passage," Lior said at last, and their voice cracked clean through the middle of the sentence, and they let it. "East. To Sablehaven." They looked at Sefa, and then past her at the others, and something in their face was younger than Sefa had ever seen it—young and frightened and blazing. "Come with me. All of you. I don't—" A breath. "I don't want to meet my mother alone. I want to meet her *accompanied*. By witnesses. By—" they gestured helplessly at all of it, the garden, the guild, the god, "—by family."

Veth opened their notebook to a fresh page and wrote, in a small precise hand, a single word, and held it up so all of them could see:

*Yes.*

Nim was already talking about ships. Ombre was already talking about letters of credit. And Sefa stood in the gate of a garden planted over a fallen kingdom, watching the person she had helped pull out of a prophecy walk toward the one entry no archivist could write for them, and felt her own hands tremble with anticipation—not dread, not anymore, but the trembling of a page about to turn.

Four hundred leagues east, a woman who had traded everything to save her child was waiting by the sea.

They sailed with the morning tide.
