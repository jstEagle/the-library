# Chapter 37: Dinner with Strangers Who Aren't

The house at the edge of the well district smelled of cardamom and woodsmoke, and Lior stood before its door for the length of three full breaths before knocking.

Three breaths. In the guild, that was an eternity. In the guild, decisions were made in half-breaths, trades sealed with a nod, names exchanged like coins across a counter. But this was not the guild. This was a door painted the blue of faded indigo, with a brass knocker shaped like a sleeping bird, and behind it lived a woman named Yewande Oyelaran who had sold her infant son to save his life and had not seen him in twenty-six years.

Lior knocked.

The door opened almost immediately, as though she had been standing on the other side of it for hours. Perhaps she had. She was smaller than Lior had imagined, though Lior could not have said what they had imagined. Round-faced, gray-streaked hair braided close to her skull, hands stained faintly with ink—she kept accounts too, Sefa had discovered, small ones, for fishermen and fruit-sellers, honest sums in honest books. Her eyes were Lior's eyes. That was the worst part. Not the resemblance in the jaw or the set of the shoulders, but the eyes, dark and watchful and tired, that looked at the world the way Lior looked at ledgers: measuring, cataloguing, bracing for loss.

"You came," she said.

"You asked," Lior said.

"I asked months ago." There was no accusation in it. Only wonder, worn smooth by waiting. "I wrote every week after your friend's letter came. I didn't know if—" She stopped herself, pressed her lips together, and stepped back from the doorway. "Come in. Please. The stew will be overdone. It's already overdone. I've made it four times this week and thrown it out three times because I couldn't—I couldn't get it right, and then today I decided you'd have to take me as I am, so."

So. The word hung between them like a coin neither dared pick up.

Inside, the house was small and fiercely clean. A shelf of books—real books, cheap printings, histories and herbals and one novel with a cracked spine. A table set for two, then hastily rearranged for four when Lior mentioned, voice carefully casual, that their companions were waiting at the inn down the lane. Yewande's hands had gone still over the plates at that. *Companions.* Plural. People who waited for Lior. People Lior wanted at their table.

She had set out the good cups. Lior recognized them without knowing how—the glaze pattern was Veth-blessed ware, common enough in the well districts, but something in Lior's chest tugged at the sight of them, some memory too deep to have been stolen, or perhaps newly grown in the space where stolen things leave roots.

They sat. The stew was, in fact, slightly overdone. Lior ate every bite.

---

The conversation began badly, which is to say it began politely, and politeness between strangers is a kind of lying.

"How is your work," Yewande asked, "your—the accounting?"

"Quiet now," Lior said. "We're restructuring. The guild only trades in what's freely given."

"That sounds—" She searched for the word. "Kind."

"It sounds impossible," Lior said, and then heard the sharpness in it and winced. "Sorry. Old habits. We used to say that about everything. Impossible things. It was our whole trade."

"What did you trade? Before."

"Bottled grief. Borrowed years. Names." Lior turned the cup in their hands. "Once, a name not yet spoken. A baby's name, sold twice before anyone ever called her by it."

Yewande's spoon stopped halfway to her mouth.

"The court bought names," she said quietly. "That's how they found children. They bought the *name* first, so the child couldn't be claimed by anyone else. So when the collectors came, there was no one with legal standing to say *mine*." Her voice did not break. It went very flat and very precise, the way voices do when breaking has been postponed for decades. "I sold yours first because if I didn't choose the buyer, they would choose for me. The woman who bought you—Sarai, she called herself then—she promised me you'd be kept, not eaten. Kept. That was the best word available to me. *Kept.*"

"I know," Lior said. "Sefa found the bill of sale. And I met Sarai. Under the mountain." They hesitated, then gave her the truth like a payment rendered: "She remembered you. She used that. It nearly worked."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. It worked on me for exactly as long as it took Nim to put her hand on my shoulder." Lior looked up. "That's why they're here. Nim and Sefa. At the inn. I told them I'd send word if—if dinner went well. If I sent word at all."

Yewande studied them across the table, and Lior watched her do the arithmetic every parent must do with a child returned from war: *what is left, what is missing, what can never be replaced.*

"You don't have to decide tonight whether I'm your mother," she said. "You've had twenty-six years of deciding who you are without me. I'd just like to be someone you know. Someone who knows you. The rest is—" she gestured vaguely at the stew, the good cups, the whole fragile arrangement of the evening— "the rest is seasoning."

Lior laughed. It startled them both; it came out rusty, half a soul's worth of laughter, but real. "Ombre says something like that. About contracts. *Terms are terms, but people are seasoning.*"

"Then I'd like to meet these people," Yewande said. "Your seasoning. If they're still at the inn."

"They'll wait all night," Lior said. "Nim would wait until Emberless's ember went cold. Sefa would wait longer, on principle, so she could document it."

---

Nim arrived first, of course—Nim moved through doors the way she moved through everything, like weather. She filled the small kitchen with the smell of dragon-smoke and mountain air and immediately complimented the stew with such sincerity that Yewande flushed with pleasure, and then admitted it was overdone, and Nim said overdone stew was the only honest food, that her father used to burn everything deliberately because *a meal that admits its flaws can't betray you*, and Yewande laughed and said her own mother had said nearly the same thing about bread, and just like that the two of them were off, trading grandmothers' proverbs across the table while Lior sat between them feeling something in their chest crack open along a seam they hadn't known was there.

Sefa came last, hovered at the threshold with the particular agony of an archivist invited into a home containing books, and was waved inside with such warmth that she forgot her manners entirely and went straight to the shelf. Within minutes she and Yewande were arguing happily about a disputed translation in the herbal, Sefa producing citations from memory, Yewande countering with practical experience, and Lior realized they were watching two kinds of knowledge fall in love with each other—book-learning and hand-learning, ink-stains and dirt-stains—and that their mother was holding her own against a disgraced archivist of the Gilded Ledger, which meant their mother was formidable, which meant Lior had inherited something after all, and it wasn't the court's cold inheritance at all.

Four cups of tea went around. The good cups stayed out; Yewande declared them everyday ware now, since they'd already been broken in.

Somewhere past the second pot, Nim asked the question gently, the way she asked everything: "What was he like? Our Lior. As a baby."

And Yewande set down her cup and thought, really thought, and said: "Loud. You wouldn't think it, looking at them now—all folded up quiet like a letter nobody's opened. But you cried like the world owed you money." She smiled, wet-eyed. "I used to tell you the well spirits would hear and come collect. And you'd cry louder. Like you were daring them."

"I still do that," Lior said. "Dare things. I just do it in writing now."

"And stubborn." Yewande counted on ink-stained fingers. "You refused the left side always. Held everything in the right arm. Slept facing the door. Even then you were keeping accounts of who came in."

*Slept facing the door.* Lior's throat closed. Twenty-six years of a habit they'd thought was temperament, was training, was the guild's making—and it had been theirs all along, older than the theft, older than the sale, something the court's collectors and the ledger's silence had never managed to reach.

"There's more of me left than I thought," they said aloud, not meaning to.

"Yes," Yewande said simply. "There always was. I'm sorry the world spent so long convincing you otherwise."

Under the table, Nim's shoulder pressed warm against Lior's. Across the table, Sefa had stopped writing—had actually closed her notebook, which those who knew her would recognize as the highest form of reverence—and was watching with her chin in her hand.

---

It was late when the candles burned low, and Yewande began gathering plates, and Lior stood to help, and their hands brushed reaching for the same bowl, and neither of them pulled away fast enough, and both of them pretended not to notice, and both of them noticed.

"You'll come back," Yewande said. Not quite a question. A line item, entered hopefully into a book that had run twenty-six years in deficit.

"Next week," Lior said. "I owe you a debt. I keep accounts."

"Then let it be a gift," she said, "and come anyway."

At the door, the night air smelled of rain coming. Nim and Sefa waited on the step, giving Lior and Yewande a last moment alone, pretending great interest in the weather.

"He sleeps facing the door," Yewande murmured to them, loud enough to be heard, meant to be heard. "Take care of that. Take care of him."

"We do," Nim said. "He takes care of us too. It's a whole terrible system."

"It's called family," Sefa said. "I've read about it extensively. The documentation is surprisingly consistent."

Yewande laughed, and kissed Lior's cheek—quickly, before either could make it strange—and Lior walked away into the wet-smelling dark with their hand pressed to their face where the kiss had landed, cataloguing the warmth of it, entering it into whatever book they kept now behind their ribs.

Behind them, the blue door stayed open a while longer, spilling candlelight onto the step.

Ahead, the road home. The garden. The god at their elbow, taking notes.

*One cup of tea,* Lior thought, remembering the guild's first honest trade. *A memory of the mountain, given freely.*

Tonight had cost nothing and been worth everything. Somewhere, Veth was surely entering it into the record.

And next week, there would be stew again—overdone, honest, seasoned with everyone Lior had chosen and everyone, at last, who had chosen them.
