## 38. New Entries Only

The Gilded Ledger had always smelled of ink and iron—ink for the contracts, iron for the vault doors that had once sealed away nine years of a god's stolen voice. Now, months after the mountain came down and the masks cracked like old lacquer, the smell was changing. Ombre noticed it first, standing in the doorway of the front room with her morning tea going cold in her hand: beeswax from the new shelves, cedar from the rebuilt counter, and underneath it all, faintly, the green scent of the potted things Nim kept insisting on placing in every window.

A guild that smelled like growing things. She would have laughed at the idea a year ago. She found instead that her throat tightened, and she drank her tea to cover it.

The front room itself had been remade. Where the trade floor had once been a maze of velvet booths and soundproofed alcoves—places where desperate people could sell desperate things without being seen—weeping or bargaining or both, there were now long tables in plain light. No curtains. No shadows deep enough to hide a signature. The guild's oldest rule, *the Ledger keeps what is given*, had been amended in Ombre's own hand and hung above the door on a board of pale ash wood:

**NEW ENTRIES ONLY. NOTHING TAKEN THAT WAS NOT FREELY GIVEN. NOTHING GIVEN THAT IS NOT UNDERSTOOD.**

Beneath it, smaller, because Sefa had insisted on precision even in scripture: *All prior debts under review. All prior sales subject to audit. If you were sold something you did not understand, come inside. We will make it right.*

Some days people came just to read it. They stood in the doorway with their collars up and their eyes down, reading the same words over and over, as if the letters might rearrange themselves into the old cruelty they'd learned to expect. Ombre let them read as long as they liked. Patience, she had learned, was also a form of repayment.

* * *

Lior arrived at midmorning through the well gate—the old well gate, the one whose name still made their chest do something complicated—and paused, as they did every day now, to look at the ledger board by the door.

It was a small thing, a slate where the day's trades would be posted openly. Today it held a single line in Sefa's careful archivist's hand:

*One (1) memory: the Court Beneath the Mountain, as experienced by a servant of the glass-root kitchens. Donated freely. Price: one cup of tea.*

Lior read it twice. Their hand drifted to their chest, to the place where half a soul had lived hollow for thirty years and now, since the shattering, lived whole and loud and sometimes almost too much. Whole souls felt everything. That was the bargain no one had warned them about. Grief came back with interest. So did joy, which was worse, some mornings, because joy made them stop in doorways and stare at nothing like a fool.

"You're doing the doorway thing again," said Nim, coming up behind them with an armful of kindling for the back-room stove. Emberless's ember rode in its lantern at her hip, wrapped in wool against the autumn damp, glowing a soft stubborn orange. "You know people talk."

"Let them talk. I'm a reformed criminal enterprise; I'm allowed eccentricities."

"You're a *clerk*," Nim said, grinning. "The most dangerous kind."

Inside, the donor was already waiting at the long table nearest the window—a thin woman with kitchen-callused hands and the particular stillness of someone who had spent years learning not to be noticed. Lior knew that stillness intimately. It had been their own armor for three decades. Seeing it on another face made something in them go fierce and gentle at once.

She was one of the freed. One of the hundreds who had walked out of the collapsing court when the master ledger broke and every stolen soul flew home to its body like birds to a roost at dusk. The servants had woken in the middle of the riot with decades of siphoned life flooding back into them, and most had wept, and some had raged, and this one—her name was Perrin, Lior remembered, Perrin of the glass-root kitchens—had simply stood in the ruins holding a serving tray she no longer needed, looking at her own hands.

Now those hands were folded on the table, and across from her sat Sefa with the new ledger open, and between them steamed a cup of tea.

"The price is as posted," Sefa said gently. "One cup. You may drink it before, during, or after. You may change your mind at any point until the memory leaves you, and it will return to you untouched, and no one will think less of you. Those are the terms. Read them." She turned the contract around. It was short. Lior had helped draft it, and had fought to make it shorter still. Three sentences. Plain language. A line for the donor's mark, drawn freely, witnessed by two.

Perrin read it four times. Then she looked up at the small audience that had gathered without meaning to—Ombre at the counter pretending to inventory candles, Nim by the stove, Lior trying very hard to be furniture—and said, "I want it out of me. Not gone. Just—" Her hands turned over, palms up, empty. "Out where I can look at it. Where it isn't *in* me anymore. Do you understand?"

"I understand exactly," Lior said, before they could stop themselves.

Everyone looked at them. Lior crossed the room and sat down at the table, uninvited, which a year ago would have been unthinkable, and which now felt like the most natural motion in the world.

"I carried the mountain too," they said. "Memories that weren't mine, planted in my head before I could walk. When they came out, when I finally knew which ones were real—I didn't want them destroyed. I wanted them *filed*. Somewhere I could find them when I needed proof of what happened. Somewhere they couldn't sneak up on me in the dark." They slid the tea closer to her. "That's what this is. Filing. Nothing more."

Perrin looked at the tea for a long moment. Then she picked it up and drank it all, steadily, like medicine, and set the cup down, and said, "Ask me anything first. So you know I'm giving it awake."

So Sefa asked her questions, gentle ones, the kind archivists are trained for: What color were the glass roots? (Green-gold, like light through bottle glass.) What did the nobles' laughter sound like? (Like bells dropped down a well.) Was there kindness in the kitchens? (Yes—oh, yes—that was the worst part, that there was kindness, that they were kind to each other while it all went on.) And Perrin answered each one clear-eyed, and each answer Sefa wrote down in the margin as provenance, so that the memory would never be mistaken for invention.

Then Perrin signed. Lior and Ombre witnessed. And the extraction—Veth's own craft, taught now openly in the back room, no longer a god's secret hoarded in a sealed vault—took less time than brewing the tea had.

What came out was small. A bead of grey-green light, no bigger than a currant, that settled into a little jar of plain glass and stopped moving, patient as anything on a shelf. Perrin watched it settle, and her breath came out of her in a long shaking thread, and she put her hands over her face and cried—not the crying of loss, Lior thought, watching. The crying of a pack set down after a very long road.

"It's so *small*," she said through her fingers. "It lived in me like a mountain. And it's so small."

"They usually are," said a voice from the corner.

No one had seen him arrive. He was very good at that, for a god. Veth stood by the window in his ordinary shape—small, tired, dressed like a traveling accountant, with ink stains on his fingers that never quite washed out and spectacles he didn't strictly need but had grown fond of. He had taken to visiting the guild most days now. Ostensibly to supervise the tithe-shelf, which was no longer a tithe-shelf but a shelf of freely given devotions, priced at whatever the giver chose, which was often nothing, which Veth insisted was correct.

"Small things weigh the most," he said, coming to look at the jar. He did not touch it. He never touched the entries; that was part of the new covenant, written in Lior's own hand and countersigned in the god's silent, careful script. *Nothing taken that was not freely given.* Gods included. "In my experience. Would you like to see where it will live?"

Perrin nodded. So they all processed—donor, god, guildmaster, dragon-keeper, clerk, archivist—to the back wall, where the old iron vault door had been left standing open forever, propped with a wedge of mountain glass that Nim had carried down from the ruin herself. Inside, the vault of tithes had become something else: the Open Archive. Shelves of light. Jars labeled in Sefa's immaculate hand, each one readable, each one visitable. Anyone who had given something could come and sit with it, could ask to hold it for an hour, could even—after a season's reflection—ask to take it back. Sefa had fought hard for that clause. *No archive is honest if it becomes another vault,* she had said, and Ombre had signed it into the guild charter with a steady hand and wet eyes.

Perrin's jar went onto a low shelf, at the height a tired person could reach without stretching. Sefa wrote the label while everyone watched:

*Memory of the Court Beneath the Mountain. Given by Perrin, late of the glass-root kitchens, freely and awake, this day. Price paid: one cup of tea, drunk in full. May be visited. May be reclaimed. Belongs, always and only, to Perrin.*

"There," Sefa said softly. "Filed."

And Perrin laughed—a startled, rusty sound, like a hinge remembering how to swing—and said, "It's the first thing anyone's ever given me a receipt for."

* * *

That evening the guild closed early, which was becoming a tradition nobody announced and everybody kept. Ombre locked the big doors not to keep people out but to keep the family in, and the family gathered in the kitchen—which had once been a counting room, and still had the old safe built into the wall, now used to store bread, because Ombre said there was poetry in it and dared anyone to argue.

Nim cooked. This had surprised everyone, including Nim, who had discovered that feeding people was easier than feeding a dying dragon and considerably more rewarding, though she still talked to the stew the way she used to talk to Emberless, in a low encouraging murmur. The ember-lantern sat on the windowsill, wrapped in its wool, glowing like a patient heart. Some nights it flared when she laughed. She pretended not to notice. Everyone else noticed.

"So," Ombre said, ladling out bowls. "First honest trade in the history of this institution. Completed. Witnessed. Priced at a cup of tea." She sat down heavily in her chair, the one with the broken arm she refused to replace. "Forty years I ran this guild, and tonight is the first night I'm not afraid to open the books."

"You're still afraid," Lior said. It wasn't a jab; it was an observation, delivered in the tone they reserved for line items. "You checked the locks twice."

"I checked the locks twice because habits are the last thing to die, and I intend to outlive mine." But she smiled when she said it, and the smile reached further than her smiles used to, before. Half a soul's worth of feeling, returned to her too—Ombre had sold pieces of herself over the years, quietly, to pay other people's debts, and the shattering had given those back along with everything else. She was louder now. Warmer. She cried at least once a week and threatened dismissal to anyone who mentioned it.

Sefa had brought her work to the table, as always: the great catalogue, the one she called, with scholarly gravity, *A Register of the Freed*. Page after page of names recovered from the shattered master ledger, cross-referenced against the soul-jars, against the court's own records, against the testimony of survivors. Thousands of lines. Each name a person who had been a number. Each number restored to a name.

"Progress?" Nim asked.

"Two hundred and six entries verified this week." Sefa pushed her spectacles up. "And something else." She hesitated, which was unlike her. She laid one hand flat on the closed register, as if holding something down.

The table went quiet. Even the stew seemed to listen.

"Lior," Sefa said carefully. "You told me once—when we were still on the mountain road, the night you couldn't sleep—you told me the only thing the court never gave you was your mother. Not her name. Not her fate. Just whether she had one."

Lior set down their spoon. The fire ticked. Somewhere upstairs a shutter knocked in the wind, ordinary as breathing.

"I remember," they said.

"I've been tracing the earliest sales. The ones the original seller recorded before the court took over his book. Most of the mothers are listed as deceased, or untraceable, or—" Sefa's jaw tightened; bureaucratic evil still made her furious, coldly, permanently furious. "Or *expended*, which I refuse to transcribe without annotation. But one entry survived the shattering intact. A woman who bought her way out of the collectors' route by paying the only coin they accepted besides souls: silence, and distance, and a name struck from every record she appeared in." She opened the register to a ribbon-marked page and turned it around.

Lior looked.

The name on the page was written in a dead man's hand, in faded brown ink, under a port town Lior had never visited, in a far province where the ships went to trade in cloth and salt and nothing impossible at all. Below the name, in Sefa's newer, steadier script: *Status: living. Verified. Last recorded residence: the well district.*

"Well gate," Lior said. Their voice came out strange, distant, like a memory being extracted. "She was at a well gate."

"She's alive," Nim said quietly. Not a question. She had put her hand on Lior's shoulder once before, on a night that decided everything, and her hand was halfway there again now, waiting.

Lior stared at the page for a long time. Thirty years of meticulous invisibility, of loneliness disguised as competence, of prayers thrown down a silent well—and at the bottom of the well, all this time, a woman with a struck-out name, alive, buying silence the way Lior had learned to buy it, wearing invisibility like a coat cut from the same cloth.

Whole souls felt everything. What Lior felt then had no line item. It was too large for the ledger.

"When," they said at last, "does the next ship sail?"

Ombre was already reaching for the tide-tables pinned above the safe. "Tomorrow's morning tide," she said. "I'll write you a letter of credit. And Lior—" her voice caught, and she let it, because she was done hiding things, done hiding costs, done with all of it— "take whatever you need. Freely given. You know the terms."

Lior closed the register gently, the way one closes a door on a sleeping child, and looked around the table—at the archivist who had found them in a book, the keeper who had found them on a road, the guildmaster who had found them at a well gate long ago and never said so—and heard themselves say, in a voice steadier than they expected:

"One cup of tea says I'll need a bigger bag for the road."

Nim laughed. The ember flared. Outside, the autumn wind came down off the ruined mountain carrying the first smell of winter, and inside the Gilded Ledger, the kettle went back on the stove—for the road, for the morning, for whatever waited at the end of a far port's well district—and the newest entry in the guild's long, crooked, mending history was written not in ink but in steam:

*One journey, freely undertaken. Price: to be determined.*
