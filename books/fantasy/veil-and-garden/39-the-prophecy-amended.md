## 39. The Prophecy, Amended

The archive of the Gilded Ledger had always smelled of dust and candle-tallow and, faintly, of money—the dry mineral scent of gold kept long in the dark. Sefa had catalogued that smell for thirty years without ever thinking to name it. Now, three months after the mountain came down, she sat at her old desk with its scarred oak top and its lamp of green glass, and the archive smelled different. It smelled of ash that had been carried home on boots and hems. It smelled of open windows.

She had requested the open windows herself.

Ombre had raised an eyebrow—*the ledgers will warp*, she'd said, *the vellum will drink the damp*—but she had not refused, and Sefa understood why. The guild was learning, slowly and with the gracelessness of any large creature learning a new gait, that some things mattered more than preservation. That a record kept airtight was a record kept dead.

On the desk before her lay two books.

The first was the codex. The old one. Bound in grey leather gone soft as moth-wing, its spine cracked into five segments like something that had tried to kneel. A century of prophecy, written by a soul-seller's careful hand, its margins crowded with annotations in three different inks and at least four different levels of honesty. Sefa had spent ninety days with this book. She had read it forward, backward, and sideways. She had held it over candlelight looking for palimpsest. She had dreamed about it twice, both times waking with ink on her fingers that turned out not to be there.

The second book was blank.

It had cost her an entire afternoon to choose it. She'd walked the paper-sellers' row on Copperlane Street until the shopkeepers began to watch her with the wary patience merchants reserve for madwomen, touching every ream, testing every binding. She wanted paper that would last—not guild-stock, which was made for entries meant to be amended and struck through, but something closer to scripture. In the end she'd bought from a widow whose late husband had been a bookbinder for the Temple of the Ninefold Dawn, and the woman had sold her a quire of rag-paper so heavy and cream-colored it seemed to hold light the way water holds a stone. *He made it for a psalter*, the widow said. *Never got the commission.* Sefa had paid double and did not haggle, because some purchases are prayers and should be priced accordingly.

Now she dipped her pen, and hesitated.

Not from fear. She had faced down masked nobles in glass-root tunnels; she had read fraudulent contracts aloud while polite servants closed in with tireless, terrible courtesy; she had watched a dragon die and a god speak. Fear, at this point, was an old colleague she no longer took meetings with.

She hesitated because she was about to do the thing archivists were trained never to do.

* * *

Her apprenticeship, forty years ago, had begun with a single lesson delivered by Master Corvane, who smelled of pipe smoke and disapproval: *"An archivist records. An archivist does not judge. The moment you decide what deserves to be remembered, you have stopped being a keeper of truth and become a maker of it."*

She had believed him. She had built her whole life inside that belief, the way a snail builds inside its shell—faithfully, spiraling inward, each chamber smaller than the last. She had authenticated forged relics because authentication was her office and judgment was not. She had catalogued the codex's provenance and noted, in her neat hand, *acquisition unremarkable,* on the very day she found a bill of sale for a human infant filed between a deed for a haunted millstone and a receipt for seven years of someone's sleep. She had recorded. She had not judged.

And the recording itself had been a kind of violence. She saw that now. Every time she transcribed the codex's prophecy without comment—*on the night of the centennial, the child of the well gate shall return to the mountain, and the fire shall fail, and the ledger shall be balanced in blood*—she gave it weight. Authority is cumulative. A lie written down a hundred times becomes a load-bearing wall. For a century, scribes like her had copied that page, authenticated that page, shelved that page where scholars could find it, and every careful, neutral, professional act had helped build the prison of expectation around a child who hadn't even been born yet when the first line was inked.

Lior Oyelaran had spent their whole life inside a sentence someone else wrote.

Sefa set down the pen. Pressed her palms flat against the desk. Outside the newly opened window, evening sounds drifted up from the street—a knife-grinder's wheel, someone laughing, the particular wet slap of rain beginning on cobbles.

Then she picked up the pen again, and began.

* * *

She wrote the way she had been taught—precisely, dated, sourced—but she wrote the truth, and the truth required a new grammar.

*In the hundredth year of the Court Beneath the Mountain,* she wrote, *a prophecy was fulfilled in exactly none of its particulars.*

That was not strictly true, and she crossed it out, because precision mattered even—or especially—in amendment. Some particulars had come true. The date. The return. The fire failing. Prophecies are like broken clocks in that regard; even a fraud is right twice a century if he aims carefully enough. What had failed was everything that mattered. No blood-balancing. No inheritance claimed. No chosen vessel walking willingly into the court's long table to take up the seller's chair.

She wrote instead:

*What actually happened is as follows.*

And then she wrote it all. She wrote for six hours the first night, and every night after for eleven days, and what emerged was not a prophecy but a testimony. She wrote about Nim Ashvael, who had come to the guild asking for fuel for a dying fire and had ended up carrying the last ember of the last dragon out into the dawn. She wrote about Ombre, guildmaster, who had bought back half a stolen soul years ago and hidden the debt out of shame, and who had ridden through the night with the guild's oldest contracts clutched to her chest like a shield made of guilt. She wrote about Emberless, who had spoken at the end with a voice like a landslide remembering it used to be a river, and whose fire had never been a cage but a dam, and whose death had nearly been the court's feast day.

She wrote about the servants. This part she wrote slowest, and rewrote most. The riot of the unpaid deserved better than a paragraph; it deserved a chapter, and then two, because Sefa had spent the months since the mountain fell interviewing survivors—servants, mostly, the people the court had called staff and treated as stock—and their stories were the true text of what happened below. The court had paid them in their own siphoned life and called it wages. When the containment failed and the hungry nobles turned on the nearest souls, it was the servants who understood first what was happening, because they had always understood what was happening; they had simply never before had company in the knowing. Marisol of the candle-vaults had organized the retreat through the glass-root tunnels. A footman named Pell, who had served the same noble family for sixty years without aging a day past thirty, had stood in the great hall and read his own employment contract aloud in a voice that cracked the marble. Sefa took down all of it. Names, dates, wages owed, wages returned. If the freed were to be documented—and they would be, all of them, every single one, that catalogue was already half-finished in the drawer beside her knee—then the ones who fought deserved their own volume.

But the heart of the new codex, the reason for the psalter-paper and the open window and the eleven nights, was the old book itself.

* * *

On the twelfth day, she brought the grey codex to the guild's common room, where the whole strange family of them had gathered at her request: Ombre behind the long table she was learning to sit beside rather than behind; Nim with singed eyebrows grown back patchy and an ember-shaped scar on one palm that never warmed and never cooled; Lior, quiet as ever, but a different quiet now—the quiet of a held breath rather than an empty room.

"I need witnesses," Sefa said. "This has to be done properly. If we're going to strike a prophecy, we can't just burn it. Burning proves nothing except that we were afraid of it."

"And striking it proves?" Ombre asked, though her eyes said she already knew. Ombre had always known things; her sin had been knowing them alone.

"Authority," Sefa said. "Authority lives in the record. So the record itself must renounce it." She laid the codex on the table, open to the final leaf of prophecy, the one bearing tonight's date from a century ago, the one with a name inscribed beneath it in faded iron-gall ink. "By the rites of authentication—which I am qualified to perform, which I have performed too many times in service of lies—I hereby certify the following finding: this document is a forgery of foresight. Its author was not a prophet. He was the previous soul-seller, and he wrote not what would happen but what he had contracted to make happen. This is not prediction. It is an invoice."

She had prepared the words in advance, rehearsed them, and still her voice shook on *invoice*, because the word was so small for so much horror. A person, invoiced. An infant, entered under *goods received*. Her whole profession's vocabulary laid bare as the language of the thing itself.

"The court enforced this text for a hundred years," she went on. "Every scribe who copied it, every archivist who shelved it, every scholar who cited it—including me—lent it weight it never earned. Tonight the Gilded Ledger strikes that weight. Not the pages. The claim."

She took up the pen—not her fine archival pen but a blunt one, loaded with red ink, the color of correction, the color emendators had used for a thousand years to mark error in sacred texts—and she drew a single line through the prophecy. One stroke, corner to corner, clean and unhurried. Then, beneath it, in her small exact hand, she wrote the formula older than any guild, older than any temple:

*Struck as false. Witnessed.*

Ombre signed. Nim signed. And then everyone looked at Lior.

Lior looked at the struck-through line for a long moment. Sefa watched their face do the thing it had begun doing lately, the thing she privately thought of as weather returning to a landscape—small movements, flickers, a face learning again that it was allowed to have expressions. Their eyes were bright.

"You could write more than your name," Sefa said gently. "It's your ending. You're allowed to author it."

Lior took the pen. Wrote four words beneath the struck line, in a hand that trembled only slightly:

*I chose otherwise. —L.O.*

Then they signed their full name, and Sefa felt something in the room shift, some pressure that had sat on all of them for a century finally sliding off. Nim laughed—one short, startled bark, the laugh of someone setting down something heavy. Ombre pressed her knuckles briefly to her mouth.

"That's it?" Nim asked. "A hundred years of dread, and it ends with four words?"

"It ends with four words and a witness list," Sefa said. "That's how all the important things end. Everything else is decoration."

* * *

The copying took the rest of the season.

She had expected resistance—from the guild's traditionalists, from the temples, from the simple gravity of habit—but the world, it turned out, was hungry for amendments. Word of the mountain's fall had traveled the trade roads ahead of any courier, distorted into a dozen shapes: the dragon had risen; the god had died; a thief had stolen a city. Merchants arrived asking which version was true, and Sefa began keeping copies of the new codex behind the counter the way the guild had once kept bottled grief, and people bought them—paid real coin, or traded honest things, memories given freely, letters of introduction, one memorable afternoon an entire barrel of salted plums—and carried them outward along every road.

Some copies came back annotated. That was her favorite part. A reader in the port city of Vell had added a note in the margin of the chapter on the riot: *my sister was a servant there. she came home aged nineteen years in one night and cries at sunlight. thank you for writing her down.* A temple scribe had written *this contradicts the Canon of Foresight* and beneath it, in a second hand, *good.* Corrections arriving like migrating birds, coming home to roost in her margins.

She struck nothing else. That was the discipline of it. Where the old codex lied, she did not erase—she recorded the lie and the truth side by side, let them stand together like a before-and-after, because erasure was the court's method, erasure was how you turned people into ledger lines, and the whole point, the entire point, was that nothing would ever be unlabeled again. Even the soul-seller's tomb got its entry. Even the masked noble who remembered Lior's mother got hers, though Sefa had needed three cups of tea and a walk around the block after writing that chapter.

Tonight, near midwinter, she finished.

The last page. She sat with the pen poised, the lamp low, the window cracked to let in the smell of coming snow, and considered what belonged at the end of a book that existed to end a book.

An index? She was an archivist; the temptation was real. A colophon, naming materials and methods? Proper. A curse upon future forgers? Deeply satisfying, and she had drafted one—it began *may your ink freeze in the pot*—but curses were just prophecies with worse manners, and she struck it from her draft with genuine pleasure.

No. The book needed one thing only. It needed the name.

Because here was the finding she had come to, ninety days of reading ago, and confirmed in every interview since: the old prophecy had named Lior Oyelaran once, and that single naming had been a hook set in the deep water of a life, and everything that followed—the guild, the invisibility, the numbness, the mountain—had been the line reeling taut. A name in a prophecy is a claim of ownership. Whoever writes your name into fate has told you what you are before you've had the chance to say.

So the new codex would name Lior Oyelaran once also. But differently.

She wrote the closing lines slowly, feeling each word settle into the heavy cream paper like a stone settling into still water:

*Let it be recorded, in place of all prophecy, that no one's ending was written before they lived it. The codex that claimed otherwise is struck, witnessed, and superseded. In these pages, Lior Oyelaran appears a single time, and not as a subject of fate.*

*They appear as its author.*

She blotted the line. Set down the pen. Her hand ached; her back ached; the candle had burned down to a thumb's width. Through the window, the first snow was starting, fat and slow, catching in the light of the streetlamps like the ash of some gentler fire.

There was a knock at the archive door—two soft raps, the rhythm she knew. She said come in without turning around, because she was smiling too widely to want it seen, and archivists are permitted dignity even at their happiest.

Lior entered, snow in their dark hair, stamping cold from their boots. Behind them, visible through the doorway, Nim waited in the hall with a lantern and the particular expression of someone up to something. Sefa had learned to read that expression over the past months. It usually preceded either trouble or a garden.

"You're done," Lior said. Not a question. They could probably feel it, Sefa thought—the way a debt feels when it's paid, the way silence feels when it ends.

"As of one minute ago." She turned the codex so they could see the final page. Watched them read their own name in its single, sovereign occurrence.

Lior was quiet for a moment. Then: "Veth will want a copy."

"Veth gets the first copy. I've already wrapped it." She nodded at a parcel on the shelf, tied in plain brown string, labeled in her neatest hand: *For the God Who Keeps Accounts, with thanks for the word.* "Nim says the little shrine by the well gate accepts deliveries now. Apparently the god takes notes."

"Nim says a lot of things," Lior said, but they were smiling, that new unpracticed smile, and they took the parcel carefully, the way you carry something alive.

At the door they paused. "Come with us tomorrow," they said. "To the mountain's mouth. There's ground there that's never grown anything, and Nim's been hoarding seeds all winter like a dragon with a grudge, and—" they glanced back at the finished codex, the struck prophecy, the whole amended year, "—I thought someone should record what gets planted. First honest crop the place has ever seen."

Sefa looked at her desk—at the old codex, grey and struck and finally, blessedly, just a book—and at the drawer where the Catalogue of the Freed waited for spring, and at the window letting the cold in on purpose.

"Someone should," she agreed, and reached for a fresh quire.
