## 40. Veil and Garden

The mountain's mouth had been a wound for a season, and now it was a garden.

Lior knelt in the black soil with their sleeves rolled to the elbow and their hands full of winter seeds—small, bitter things that would not bloom until the thaw—and thought about how strange it was that healing looked so much like ordinary work. No incantation. No ledger line. Just dirt under the nails and the ache of knees on cold ground and the long slow labor of covering over what had been uncovered.

Behind them, where the court had stood for a hundred years beneath its veil of glass roots and candlelight, there was only a valley now. The collapse had been gentle, as collapses went—the mountain had seemed almost relieved to let go, folding inward like an old man lowering himself into a chair he had earned the right to sit in. Where the Long Table had been, there was a meadow of pale grass already coming up through the rubble, greedy for light after a century of darkness. Where the soul-vault had been, with its thousands of jars labeled like accounting entries, there was a spring, cold and clear, that Sefa swore had not existed on any map.

Lior did not plant over the vault. They planted *around* it, leaving the spring open to the sky, because some things should be marked by absence rather than monument.

"You're doing it wrong," said Veth, at their elbow.

Lior did not startle anymore. It had taken months, but they had learned the god's particular quality of presence—not a sound, not a weight, but a kind of attention, like being read aloud to. "I'm doing it exactly as Nim showed me."

"Nim plants for dragons. Dragons like chaos." A pause, and Lior could feel the god crouching beside them, small and tired and faintly gray around the edges, the way an accountant looks at the end of a fiscal year. "These are grief-seeds. You bury them shallow. Grief doesn't grow deep, Lior. It grows wide."

Lior looked down at the seeds in their palm. Small, bitter things. Of course Veth would know. Of course the god who kept accounts would know the proper depth at which to bury sorrow.

They buried them shallow.

* * *

It had been eleven months since the shattering.

Eleven months since Lior had stood before the master ledger in the heart of the collapsing court, with Veth's voice still ringing in the stone—*Ledger*, one word, nine years of silence broken by a single command—and chosen. Not the inheritance. Not the power. Not the elegant, terrible arithmetic of erasing their own sale and walking out of the mountain as something more than human, something owed, something *paid*.

They had shattered the book instead. And the sound it made—

People asked about the sound sometimes. Sefa had written three essays trying to describe it. Nim said it sounded like ice going out of a river in spring. Ombre, who had arrived too late to help but just in time to hold the door, said it sounded like every debt in the world being forgiven at once, which was either poetry or the most accurate description anyone had managed.

To Lior it had sounded like a page turning. Just that. The soft whisper of a page turning, and then the mountain exhaled, and ten thousand souls went home to bodies that had been waiting for them like coats left in a closet, and the nobility of the Court Beneath the Mountain woke up mortal—old, suddenly, and frightened, and ordinary—in the ruins of their beautiful trap.

Some of them wept. Some of them raged. One of them, the masked noble who remembered Lior's mother, had sat down in the wreckage of her perfect chair and laughed until she cried, and then simply cried, and Lior had left her there, because vengeance was a ledger entry and Lior was done keeping those.

The souls had gone home. All of them. Even the servants who had been paid in their own siphoned life—even the ones whose bodies had crumbled decades ago, who rose out of the spring water as bright motes and drifted upward through the collapsed throat of the mountain into whatever waits above the sky. Sefa catalogued every single one. Four thousand two hundred and six names, entered into a new book with clean pages, each name written once, owned by no one, owed to no one.

Including, on the last line, in Sefa's careful archivist's hand: *Lior Oyelaran — returned — whole.*

Whole. That word had taken the longest to believe.

* * *

The trouble with getting half your soul back from a guildmaster who bought it secretly and hid the debt for twenty years, and the other half back from a shattered ledger, was that you spent a great deal of time afterward discovering what feeling actually was.

Lior had cried at a fruit stall in the spring. Over pears. Perfectly good pears, nothing wrong with them, and they had stood there holding one while the vendor watched with mounting alarm, weeping because the pear smelled like the orchard behind Ombre's old house, the one memory they had paid at the Tollhouse of Small Mercies and never gotten back. They still hadn't gotten it back. Some tolls were not refunded; the tollkeeper had been very clear about that, even as the rest of the court's machinery came apart around him. But the grief for the memory had come back, and grief, it turned out, was its own kind of remembering. You could mourn a thing you couldn't see. You could love a lesson you'd forgotten learning.

Ombre had taught them letters. They knew this now the way you know a story someone told you: Ombre, younger, ink-stained, sitting a terrified child at the guild's back counter and tracing letters in spilled sugar because paper was too precious to waste on mistakes. *This is your name. Learn it well. No one gets to write it but you.*

No one gets to write it but you.

Lior pressed another seed into the soil and covered it gently, and let the thought go where thoughts go when the hands are busy.

* * *

Nim found them at midday, coming up the switchback path with a basket and the particular walk of someone carrying something alive. Emberless's ember rode in a lantern at her hip, wrapped in wool, glowing a soft stubborn orange—the last coal of the last dragon, freed at last from a century of duty it had never chosen, and choosing anyway, in its small way, to keep burning.

"Your rows are crooked," Nim announced, setting down the basket.

"They're honest."

"They're crooked *and* honest. Like you." She sat down beside Lior in the dirt without ceremony, which was one of the thousand things Lior loved about her and had only recently learned to call love, having previously filed it under *unclassified asset*. She pulled the lantern close and unwrapped a corner of the wool so the ember could see—or whatever embers did. "She wanted to come up. She likes watching things grow. Says it's the opposite of what she was made to do."

"What was she made to do?"

"Contain." Nim's jaw tightened, the way it always did. Her whole life had been containment—feeding a fire that fed on her family's generations, keeping a hunger locked down that should never have been allowed to get hungry. "But she says fire was never meant to be a cage. Fire is meant to be a hearth. So." She gestured at the garden, the crooked rows, the spring. "Hearth."

From the lantern came a flicker, warm against Lior's cheek, like a cat leaning in. Lior had stopped questioning these things months ago.

"Sefa's coming tomorrow," Nim said. "With the catalogue. Final printing."

"I know. I can feel her fussing from here."

"And Ombre?"

"Ombre arrives when Ombre arrives. She's bringing—" Lior hesitated, then smiled, because smiling was still new enough to be a choice rather than a reflex, and they chose it. "She's bringing the tea set. From the back counter. She wants to have the first trade."

"The cup-of-tea trade?"

"The cup-of-tea trade."

Nim laughed and lay back in the grass, and the ember glowed contentedly between them, and above the valley the veil—that shimmering lie of altitude and distance that had hidden the court from the world for a hundred years—was simply gone. Travelers on the high road could see the mountain's broken crown now. Could see the green coming up. Pilgrims had started arriving, at first in ones and twos and now in small processions, drawn by rumors of the place where the masks cracked and the book broke. They left offerings at the spring: buttons, ribbons, coins, letters to dead relatives whose souls had gone home or gone on. Lior had decided not to police this. Gardens, Veth had observed, always attract grievers. That was half of what gardens were for.

* * *

Veth stayed while Nim dozed in the afternoon sun, and together—they had developed a rhythm for this, god and accountant, though who kept accounts for whom had become pleasantly unclear—they worked the rows.

It was during the third row that Lior finally asked the question they had been carrying since the vault. Since the tithe-jars. Since nine years of prayers poured out like offerings into a silence that turned out to be theft.

"Why 'ledger'?" they asked. "When you answered. After everything. Nine years I prayed to you—properly, with tithes, with discipline—and when you finally spoke, the word you chose was *ledger*. I've wondered if it was a joke."

Veth was quiet for a while. The god's silences were different now than they used to be—no longer absence, but consideration. Lior had learned to tell the difference, and considered this among the great achievements of their life.

"When the court took my voice," Veth said at last, "they took it in jars. Do you remember what was written on the jars?"

"*Veth — tithe — received.*"

"Mm. Received. As though my voice were payment. As though prayer were a transaction and I were a merchant collecting dues." The god's small gray hands smoothed soil over a seed with surprising tenderness. "For nine years, every prayer anyone offered me anywhere was logged in that vault as income. And I could not correct the entry. Do you understand? I am a god of accounts, Lior. To be unable to correct an entry—to watch myself misrecorded, nine years, ten thousand lines—it was not merely imprisonment. It was blasphemy against my own nature."

"So when you spoke—"

"When I spoke, I spoke the correction. The whole correction. Everything the court had done, everything the guild had done, everything the world does when it turns the living into entries—" Veth looked up, and Lior met eyes the color of ink, and felt no fear at all. "*Ledger.* Meaning: this ends. Meaning: I see the books, and I am calling them false. Meaning: you, Lior Oyelaran, sold as an infant, numbered, filed, prophesied—you are not an entry. You never were. The word was not a joke. It was the truest thing I knew how to say."

Lior's hands stilled in the soil.

Nine years of unanswered prayers. And the answer, all along, had been waiting in a jar in a vault, and when it finally came it was not comfort or absolution or even a name. It was a correction. An audit. A god saying *the books are lies*, in the only language it had.

"I think," Lior said slowly, "that might be the kindest thing anyone has ever said to me."

"It was four thousand pages of fraud reduction," said Veth. "But yes. Also that."

And Lior laughed—really laughed, the kind that hurt a little, the kind the numb years had made impossible—and the sound went up out of the garden into the open air where anyone could hear it, unpriced, ungifted, freely given to no one and everyone.

* * *

They finished the rows as the light went long and gold. Grief-seeds shallow, hope-seeds deep—hope, Veth explained, was the opposite of grief in every respect including horticulture. Nim slept. The ember breathed its slow orange breath. Down in the valley the spring murmured over the place where the jars had been, saying nothing, needing to say nothing, which was its own kind of blessing.

At dusk Lior walked to the garden's edge, where the ground fell away toward the old road, and stood looking at the mountain's broken silhouette against the first stars.

There was no prophecy anymore. Sefa had seen to that—had written the new codex with the old one's authority struck through, line by line, until the future was nothing but unwritten pages again. There was no debt. The guild traded in cups of tea and freely given memories now, and Ombre had wept at the reorganization meeting, and the guild family Lior had never trusted themselves to have had turned out to be real, which was the single entry in all the world's books that Lior had been most afraid to audit and most glad to find true.

Their mother wrote every month now. Short letters, careful letters, letters from a woman still learning how to be a mother to a grown stranger, and Lior answered every one, and neither of them pretended the dinner had fixed everything, because it hadn't, because forgiveness without pretense meant forgiving the awkwardness too, the distance, the decades. But the letters came. That was enough. That was a great deal.

Lior took out the small notebook they carried now—not a ledger, Veth had been very clear on the distinction, more of a field journal—and made the day's entry in their own hand, the hand Ombre had taught in spilled sugar, the hand no one got to write with but them:

*Day 334. Planted seven rows. Nim crooked, Veth precise. Ember warm. Spring loud. Pilgrims left forty-one offerings, all refused, all respected. Balance: owing nothing, owed nothing, rich regardless.*

They closed the book. And at their elbow, punctual as sunset, the god of accounts opened their own much older book, licked the tip of a pencil that had no business existing in a divine hand, and began, as they did every evening, to take notes.

"What are you writing tonight?" Lior asked.

Veth considered. Below them the garden darkened; above them the veilless sky filled up with stars that had always been there, waiting for someone to look.

"An inventory," the god said. "Of everything that was stolen and returned. It's a very short list now." A pause, and the pencil scratched on, and the god's voice went soft as turned earth. "Mostly it's a record of what grew in the space the thefts left behind. It's going to take several volumes."

"Will I be in it?"

"Lior." Veth did not look up from the page. "You are the margins."

And somewhere below, in the black soil, in the shallow graves where grief was planted to grow wide, the first seed—too impatient, too stubborn, too much like everyone who had ever tended this ground—cracked its coat and put down root, and reached, in the dark, toward a spring it had never seen, and began, against all reasonable expectation, to rise.
