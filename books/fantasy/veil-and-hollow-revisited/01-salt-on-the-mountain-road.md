## 1. Salt on the Mountain Road

There are three rules to surviving in the Thornfells, and I learned all of them the hard way, which is the only way anyone learns anything worth knowing.

Rule one: never walk the high passes after the first frost without salt in your boots. Rule two: never trust a man who smiles with his whole face. Rule three—and this is the one that matters—never, under any circumstance, let anyone see what you can do.

I had kept rule three for six years. Six years of biting my tongue while children coughed themselves hollow in the warrens below Greymoss. Six years of watching fevers burn through the smugglers' camps like fire through dry bracken and doing nothing but brewing willow bark and honey, the way any herb-wife might, the way a woman with no gift at all might. Six years of being nobody, which is a full-time occupation, and I was very good at it.

Then came the stranger on the Widow's Stair, and I broke my own rule without even meaning to.

* * *

The morning it happened I was hauling contraband up the switchbacks above the Ashen Vein—feverfew, valerian, a dozen little woven charms stitched with rowan twigs that I'd charmed exactly not at all, because a charm that actually works leaves a residue, and residue is how hunters find you. My pack weighed forty pounds and my boots were stuffed with rock salt against the cold-things that live in the scree, and I was thinking about nothing more profound than whether Corvin would short me again on the delivery fee. He always shorted me. It was practically a sacrament between us.

The fog had come down thick off the peaks, the kind of fog that swallows sound, so at first I thought the moaning was wind in the crags. Then I saw him: a man slumped against the trailside cairn, half-frozen, his breath coming in wet rattles that I could hear from twenty paces. A drover by his coat, gone gray in the face, sweat freezing on his brow in the cold. Lung-fever, and a bad one. Left alone he'd be dead by dark, and the crows would have him picked clean by the time anyone thought to look.

Now. Here is what a sensible woman does. A sensible woman steps around him. A sensible woman has spent years building a life out of invisibility and knows that the dying are dangerous—not because they'll hurt you, but because saving them means touching them, and touching them means being seen, and being seen is the first domino in a line that ends with you burning in a village square while respectable people bring their children to watch.

I stepped around him. I got maybe ten paces.

The rattle in his chest turned into something worse—a wet, tearing sound, like cloth ripping—and then silence, and his head lolled, and I stood there in the fog hating myself with a thoroughness that would have impressed my old mistress. Maren used to say my gift sat in me like a coal under ash: banked, but never out. She'd say it gently, the way you tell someone they're carrying something that will eventually burn them.

"Damn you," I told the stranger, who could not hear me, "and damn me too."

I knelt. I put two fingers to his throat—the pulse was there, thin as thread—and I meant to do what any traveling herb-wife does: loosen his collar, get a bitter tonic down him, drag him toward shelter. That was the plan. The plan did not include my gift.

But the fever reached for me. That's the only way I can describe it, though I've never said it aloud to anyone. Sickness has a kind of hunger, and when my skin touched his skin, the coal under the ash flared before I could stop it, and the heat poured *out* of him and *into* me like water finding its level. His fever became my fever. His sickness became mine to carry, and my blood burned it away the way a forge burns off rust.

It took perhaps four heartbeats. It felt like swallowing a handful of live embers and holding them in your chest.

When I could see straight again, the drover was breathing easy. Color had come back into his face. He stirred, blinked up at me through the fog, and mumbled something about his mules, and I told him he'd caught a chill and should get down to the Wayhouse at Greymoss before nightfall, and he thanked me blearily, believing—as I intended—that some passing herb-wife had dosed him with willow bark. He staggered off down the trail toward the village, alive, oblivious, and I sat down hard on the cold stone and shook.

Because here's the thing about carrying a fever out of a man: it doesn't vanish. You burn it off, yes, but the burning leaves marks. My hands trembled for an hour afterward. And somewhere behind us on that trail, in the fog—

Someone cleared their throat.

* * *

I was on my feet with a knife in my hand before my mind caught up to my body, which is the advantage of a misspent youth running from things. The figure detached itself from the rocks maybe thirty paces back along the trail: tall, wrapped in a hooded cloak the color of wet slate, moving with a stillness I recognized and hated on sight. Hunters move like that. So do soldiers. So do certain other things I'd rather not name.

"You dropped this," the figure said, and held up—of all things—a sprig of dried feverfew that had fallen from my pack when I knelt. Held it out at arm's length, the way you'd offer a treat to a dog you weren't sure was friendly.

"I don't want it," I said.

"No," the figure agreed. "I imagine you don't." The hood tilted. Underneath was a woman's face, weathered and sharp, forty or fifty years old, with eyes the pale gray of ice over a stream. "That man had lung-fever. The black-rattle kind. I've seen it kill in a day. And now he's walking downhill humming a tune."

"People recover."

"People recover from lung-fever the way stones recover from drowning." She tucked the feverfew into her belt, unhurried, which frightened me more than a drawn blade would have. "You carried it out of him. I watched your hands shake after. I know what that costs."

My knife hand stayed steady, which I counted as a professional triumph. "You watched nothing. You watched an herb-wife give a sick man a tonic. If you say otherwise, you're the one spreading tales about witchcraft on crown roads, and I'd think twice about where that leads, these days."

Something moved across her face—something that wasn't fear and wasn't quite respect. "These days," she repeated softly. "Yes. You'd know about 'these days,' wouldn't you, hedge-witch? Word coming down the passes. Proclamations going up in every village from Greymoss to Thornfell's foot. Registration rolls. Hunt lists." She smiled, and it didn't reach anywhere near her eyes. "They say the lists have names on them already. Names gathered from places no crown spy ought to be able to reach."

The fog pressed in. Somewhere above us, a rock cracked with the cold, sharp as a snapped bone.

"I don't know what you're talking about," I said, "and I have a delivery."

"Of course you do." She stepped aside, opening the trail, and made a small courteous gesture that somehow managed to feel like a threat. "Run along, then. But I'll tell you a thing for free, since you gave that drover his life for free." Her voice dropped, and the friendliness went out of it entirely, leaving something old and tired underneath. "Whatever you buried, whoever you were before you came to these mountains—it's not buried deep enough anymore. The hunt isn't guessing. The hunt is being *told*. And the ones telling aren't men."

She turned and walked back into the fog, and I let her go, because chasing her would have been worse than losing her, and because my legs had gone strange and watery, and because some cowardly animal part of me still whispered that if I couldn't see her, maybe none of it had happened.

But the feverfew was gone from my belt where I'd stowed it that morning—I checked twice—and the space where the drover's fever had passed through me still ached like a bruise in the marrow.

* * *

I made the delivery to Corvin's people at the Hollow Stair cache by late afternoon, took my shorted pay without argument, and asked—one question too casually, I'm sure—whether anything new had come up the passes. The fence, a stooped old man called Tally whose eyes were quicker than his back, went still in the way that careful people go still.

"You haven't heard?" he said. "Crown riders came through Greymoss two nights past. Nailed proclamations to every door. All persons of gifted blood to present themselves for registration within the month." He spat over the edge of the stair, into the dark. "Refusal's punishable. harboring's punishable. And there's a new seal on the papers, black iron, not the king's gold. Nobody knows whose mark it is. Somebody high, though. Somebody who wants folk found."

Black iron. I turned that over in my mind all the way back up the mountain, and it fit together with the woman in the fog into a shape I didn't like at all. Because Maren had taught me one more thing besides rules, back when I was young and stupid and she was still speaking to me: that the old stories were true, and the old stories said the fae kept bargains with kings, and bargains cut both ways.

Informants from the Hollow. Being *told*, not guessed.

And if the lists already had names—and if the Hollow was feeding them—then the first name on those lists wouldn't be a stranger on a mountain road. It would be the woman who'd taught me everything, including how to hide, six years ago, when she'd sent me away with salt in my boots and a lie on my lips: *stay invisible, Paloma, and stay away from me.*

I stood at the mouth of my cave above the Ashen Vein and looked down at the lights of Greymoss scattered in the valley like fallen stars, and I understood, with the particular clarity that arrives only when it's too late, that my six years of invisibility had just ended—not with a shout, but with a stranger's fever and a pair of shaking hands witnessed in the fog.

Maren was a day's hard walk south. The proclamation said a month.

I packed in the dark.
