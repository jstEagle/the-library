## 2. The Crown's Long Winter

The poster was nailed to the chapel door in Millbrook with four iron nails, which told me everything before I'd read a word. Iron. Somebody in the capital had done their homework.

*By order of the Crown: all persons possessed of unlicensed gifts shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal their nature shall be hunted. Those who resist shall be burned.*

Burned. Not hanged, not imprisoned—burned. Whoever drafted it wanted the smell of it in people's noses. Fear works better when it stinks.

I stood in the market crowd with my pack of contraband feverfew and charm-salt on my shoulder and read it three times, because the first two times my eyes kept sliding off the word *gifted* the way they'd been trained to slide off anything dangerous. Six years of practice, that. Six years of being nobody, of selling herbs that were only herbs and charms that were mostly superstition with good branding, of keeping my hands in my pockets when someone bled near me. And now some clerk with a seal had written my death warrant in language plain enough for a miller's wife to read aloud.

Around me, the crowd did what crowds do. A woman pulled her child closer. Two farmers exchanged a look too long to be innocent. Old Tam Brackle, who bought sleep-draughts from me every darkmoon, suddenly found something fascinating about his own boots. That was the clever cruelty of it—the proclamation didn't need hunters yet. It just needed neighbors. It turned every village into a net, and every kindness into a confession waiting to happen.

I left by the back lane, took the goat track north, and didn't stop walking until Millbrook was a smudge behind me and my lungs were burning worse than my throat.

Because here's the thing about running six years: you get fast at it. You learn which roads flood, which innkeepers ask no questions, how to read a room for the one face that's watching too closely. But you also get slow in one particular way—you stop checking on the people you loved enough to leave. Checking on them is how you get caught. Love is a road that runs both directions, and I'd spent six years pretending mine dead-ended at Maren's gate.

Maren. Who taught me that magic isn't a thing you have but a thing you owe. Who buried my name so deep even I half forgot it. Who stayed behind in her cottage under the rowan with her bees and her bitter tea and her terrible opinions about the crown, because she said hiding was a discipline and she'd practiced longer than I'd been alive.

She was eighty-one years old and she was on whatever list that proclamation came from. I'd have bet my life on it. In fact, I suppose I was.

* * *

I traveled the high roads by night and slept in shepherd huts by day, and everywhere I stopped, the posters were there before me. Nailed to the tollhouse at Stony Ford. Pasted over the shrine at Hallowmere, right across the old blessing carved in the lintel, which felt less like policy and more like a threat with ambitions. At the crossroads shrine above Vetch Hollow, someone had already scrawled beneath the proclamation: *We know one.* Three words, and I stood in the dark reading them over and over, feeling the net draw tight around a village I hadn't entered in two years.

That was the worst of it, if you want the truth. Not the burning—that was far away and easy to disbelieve, the way your own death always is. The worst was *we know one*. Because every village had one. Every village had an herb-wife whose teas worked too well, a smith who could hear metal sing, a child who talked to crows and got answers. I'd been one of them once, in a village exactly like this one, and Maren had come for me before the fear did.

Now the fear was coming for her, and I was all she had, and I had spent six years being deliberately nowhere.

On the fourth night I crested Thornfell's lower shoulder and saw the valley where I'd learned everything worth knowing. The cottage sat where it had always sat, under the rowan, smoke-thread thin against the dusk. My heart did something stupid and hopeful at the sight of the smoke.

Then I got closer, and my heart learned better.

The gate hung by one hinge. Not broken—unhinged, laid carefully against the fence post, which was somehow worse. Careful work. Work done by men who expected to come back or who wanted the place to look untouched to casual eyes. The bees were silent. Maren's bees had never once been silent in living memory; she claimed they sang the weather three days ahead. I stood at the ruined gate and listened to an absence of bees and felt cold walk up my spine wearing boots.

* * *

The door wasn't locked. It wasn't anything. It leaned inward like a drunk against a wall.

Inside, the cottage had been turned inside out—not smashed, *searched*, which is its own special violence. Herbs dumped from their jars and sifted through, the floor still dusted with dried yarrow. Floorboards pried up and set back crooked. Her loom cut apart looking for things woven into the weave. Her books gone—every one, even the ones in cipher, even the ones I'd have sworn looked like cookery. Someone had known exactly what to take and had taken it with the tenderness of a collector.

And in the middle of the swept hearthstone, where a fire should have been, someone had placed a sigil of black iron the size of my palm: a closed eye inside a crown of thorns, worked so fine it might have been jewelry if it weren't so obviously meant to be found. Left behind. Displayed. A signature.

I knew it without knowing it. Some part of me had seen that mark before—in a dream, maybe, or in the margins of one of Maren's stolen books—and my skin remembered it the way skin remembers frostbite. It wasn't a crown sigil. Crowns stamp themselves on everything; this was quieter, more personal. This was the mark of a man who ran the crown's fear the way a spider runs a web, and who wanted other spiders to know whose web it was.

I picked it up anyway. Foolish. But my hands have never taken direction well—it's why I'm in this mess—and they closed around the iron and squeezed until it bit.

"Where is she?" I asked the empty room, and the empty room declined to answer, and I heard my own voice crack on the second word like I was twelve again and she was teaching me my first binding and I'd gotten it wrong and she'd laughed, actually laughed, and said *again, little wren, magic forgives nothing but it forgets less.*

There was blood on the hearthstone. Just a smear, old, browned to rust—but beside it, pressed into the ash-ghost, the print of a hand much too small to be hers. A child's hand. Or a hand that only looked like one. I crouched over it for a long time, telling myself fae-blooded folk ran small, telling myself anything at all.

Under the loose third floorboard—she'd have flayed me for finding it so easily; she'd have flayed me for looking at all—I found what they'd missed. A scrap of oilcloth, and inside it a single page in her cramped hand, written fast, the ink blotted where her pen had stabbed down:

*If you are reading this, then you came home, which means I failed to teach you patience, which means I love you. Listen. They did not find what they sought. The covenant sleeps under the rowan and it must stay sleeping until a keeper wakes it proper. Do not dig. Do not grieve me ahead of schedule, it's wasteful. Go to Corvin Ash at the Sluice Gate and say the debt is called. He'll grumble. He always grumbles. Trust the huntsman who is dead—he's better company alive. And Paloma—*

The page tore off there, ragged, as if the rest had been taken. Or as if she'd torn it herself, because some lessons shouldn't travel whole.

I sat on Maren's crooked floor among her scattered herbs and did not cry, mostly because crying is loud and I've had six years of practice at quiet. What I did instead was worse. I made a list. Of every visit I hadn't paid. Every festival I'd watched from the ridge instead of coming down. Every time I'd told myself *next season, next season,* while the seasons stacked up like unpaid debts. I had thought distance was protection. I had thought love, kept at arm's length, couldn't be used as a lever against either of us.

But they'd taken her anyway, and they'd left the sigil like a note pinned to a door: *we know who taught you. we know what you are. come and see.*

Maybe it wasn't a message for me. Maybe I was flattering myself. Maybe old women got taken every day now, all across the mountains, and none of them had runaway apprentices creeping home to make it about themselves.

But the page said *the covenant sleeps under the rowan,* and the rowan's roots filled this floor's foundations, and somewhere between those two facts my whole careful invisible life quietly died.

Outside, full dark had come down, and with it a wind that smelled wrong—too cold for the season, carrying something underneath the pine and stone that I could only call *winter with intentions*. On the ridge opposite, a light moved. Not a lantern. Lanterns don't move in straight lines down impossible slopes, and lanterns don't pause, precisely where I would pause, precisely when I paused.

Six years of practice told me to run. Six years of loneliness asked me who, exactly, I was running toward anymore.

I wrapped the sigil in the oilcloth with Maren's page, tucked both against my ribs, and slipped out the back into the dark, taking the goat track down toward the river and the Sluice Gate, where a smuggler named Corvin Ash owed me a life and was about to discover how I planned to collect.

Behind me, on the ridge, the light followed—patient as winter, and just as certain it would win.
