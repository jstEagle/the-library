## 3. A Smuggler's Debt

Corvin Ash owed me his life, and like most men who owe you something large and unpayable, he'd spent six years pretending the debt was smaller than it was.

I found him where I always found him—The Drowned Lantern, a tavern in Hollowbridge that smelled of tallow smoke, wet wool, and the particular desperation of men who made their living in the spaces between laws. He was holding court at the corner table, telling some story about a customs officer and a mule, and when he saw me come through the door he did the thing he always did: went very still, then very loud.

"Paloma Delacroix!" He spread his arms as if I were a long-lost sister instead of a walking reminder that he had once, memorably, died for about four minutes on a riverbank. "And here I thought you'd forgotten us."

"I forget lots of things," I said, sitting down across from him without being invited. "Debts aren't one of them."

The table went quiet in the way tables do when someone says the word *debt* in a smuggler's tavern. Corvin waved off his audience with the practiced grace of a man who'd been waving off audiences since before he could grow a proper beard, and leaned in.

"That was six years ago," he said, low. "You said it didn't need repaying."

"It didn't. Then." I kept my voice equally low, equally pleasant, which anyone who knew me would have recognized as the most dangerous register I owned. "Things have changed, Corvin."

"Changed how?"

I let the silence do some work. Around us the Lantern carried on—a dice game collapsing into argument, someone's wife threatening someone's brother with a ladle—but our corner had developed its own weather.

"You've seen the posters," I said finally.

His jaw tightened. Everyone had seen the posters. *By order of the Crown: all persons possessed of unlawful gifts shall present themselves for registration within thirty days. Those who conceal themselves will be hunted. Those who resist will be burned.* The ink was still wet on half of them; the gallows scaffolding going up in the market square was not.

"Aye," he said. "Ugly business."

"It's about to get uglier, and I need to be somewhere the ugliness can't find me. I need passage, shelter, and your discretion. In that order."

"And what exactly are you running from?" His eyes narrowed—not suspicious, exactly. Corvin wasn't smart enough for suspicion. But he was shrewd in the way of men who survived by reading rooms, and he was reading mine now. "You're careful, Delacroix. Carefuler than anyone I know. You don't run unless something's got your scent."

*Someone saw.* I hadn't told a soul about the fevered stranger on the mountain road, the heat that had poured out of my hands before I could stop it, the look in the man's eyes as the fever broke—not gratitude, but recognition. And behind him, at the treeline, the flicker of movement I'd refused to think about for three days.

"Let's say I'm being prudent," I said.

"Let's say," Corvin agreed, "that prudent people don't call in life-debts." He drummed his fingers on the table, once, twice. Then he sighed the sigh of a man surrendering to a battle he'd already lost. "Passage and shelter, aye. Discretion—" he grimaced, "—you'll have it, but I'll tell you now it costs me. My crew asks questions."

"Then give them answers."

"What answers?"

"Any that aren't true."

That got a laugh out of him, short and reluctant, and just like that I was aboard. It's remarkable, the economy of guilt between people who've seen each other at their worst. He'd been dying of adder venom and river rot, and I'd knelt in the mud and done what I swore never to do, and neither of us had ever spoken of it since. That silence was worth more than gold. I was spending it now, and we both knew it, and he let me spend it without haggling. Whatever else Corvin Ash was—and he was many things, most of them mildly criminal—he was not a man who forgot what he owed.

* * *

The crew was twelve souls and a mule named Bishop who bit everyone except me, which I chose to take as a compliment. We moved up the old drover roads by night, through passes the crown's soldiers couldn't find on a map because the mapmakers had never asked a smuggler.

Corvin gave them the story I'd suggested: I was a herb-wife from over the ridge, paying for protection from the registration men because my sister had been taken in Lowmarsh. It was close enough to true to be comfortable, which is the secret of all good lies. The crew accepted it with the shrugging fatalism of people whose livelihoods depended on not knowing things too precisely.

All except one.

Wren was maybe ten years old, small even for ten, with a shock of white-blond hair that no dye would take and eyes the color of shallow water over green stone. She belonged to no one on the crew and everyone on it. They fed her first, clothed her in castoffs fought over rather than given, and when she had nightmares—which was often—one of them always ended up sitting up with her, grumbling, until she slept. Nobody could tell me where she'd come from. Found on a doorstep in Thornhollow. Left by traders. Won in a card game, though nobody could agree who'd won or lost.

"She's odd," the cook, Brammel, told me, in the tone of a man describing weather. "Not right odd. Just—things happen near her. Candles gutter when she cries. Once she got angry at Tollo for cheating at cards and every card in his hand turned blank. We don't talk about it much."

"We don't," said Tollo, from across the fire, with feeling.

I looked at the girl. She was sitting apart from the others, as she always sat, turning a smooth grey pebble over in her fingers—and the pebble, I noticed, was glowing. Faintly. A soft pulse of light, like a heartbeat under skin, brightening when she turned it toward the fire and dimming when she hid it in her palm.

She caught me watching. Most children would have hidden it. Wren held my gaze instead, defiant and frightened at once, the way only a child can be who has learned that being seen is dangerous and has decided to be seen anyway.

"You saw," she said.

"I see lots of things."

"Not like that." She came closer, dropping her voice to a whisper that wouldn't carry past the firelight. "The stone does it when I'm scared. I can't make it stop. Are you going to tell?"

The question landed harder than she could have known. *Are you going to tell?* Six years I'd asked myself a version of it every single day. Six years of burying my gift so deep I sometimes convinced myself it wasn't there, of wearing gloves in summer, of letting fevers run and bones knit crooked because healing was the first sin and hiding was the second and I'd committed both so long they'd grown into each other like tree roots.

Here was a child doing it wrong in every way I'd learned to do it right. Glowing stones. Guttering candles. Blank playing cards. A walking beacon in a country that had just started building pyres.

"No," I said. "I'm not going to tell."

"But you're going to leave." She said it flatly, without accusation, which somehow made it worse. "Everyone leaves. Brammel says you're just passing through."

"Brammel's usually right about practical matters."

"He's wrong about this one." She put the pebble in my hand before I could stop her. It was warm, pulsing against my palm like a second pulse, answering something in me that I had spent six years pretending was dead. My gift rose toward it the way water rises toward a crack in a wall—involuntary, patient, inevitable. I closed my fist and smothered it, and the stone went dark, and Wren's eyes went wide.

"You felt it," she breathed. "You felt it too."

"I don't know what you mean."

"Liar," she said, with the serene confidence of a ten-year-old, and walked back to her place by the fire.

I sat there holding a warm rock and understanding, with the slow horror of a woman watching an avalanche begin, that I was in trouble.

* * *

Three nights later, in the shelter of a hollow beneath a fallen rowan, I taught her the first lesson. I told myself I wasn't going to. I told myself it for three days while I watched her fail to hide the glow, watched the crew's fond bafflement curdle slowly toward unease, watched her grow quieter and the stone grow brighter, and on the third night I heard two of the men murmuring by the horses—*maybe the registration men would know what to do with her*—and that was the end of my telling myself anything.

"Your gift isn't a fire you put out," I whispered, crouched beside her in the dark while the camp slept. "It's a river. You don't kill a river. You dig channels for it."

"How do you know?"

"Because I've got one too."

Her breath caught. For a moment she just stared at me, and I watched something kindle in her face that I recognized with terrible clarity, because I'd worn it myself once, in another life, in Maren's kitchen: hope. Raw, unguarded, catastrophic hope.

"Show me," she whispered.

So I did. I took her hands and showed her how to breathe the power down instead of clamping it, how to let it move through her like tide instead of damming it until it burst. I showed her how to touch the stone and ask it, politely, to sleep. It took until well past midnight, and when the pebble finally went quiet in her cupped palms—not dead, just resting—she made a sound I'll hear until I die. Small. Wondering. Like a door opening in a house that had been shut for years.

"Why are you helping me?" she asked. "You don't even like people."

"I like some people."

"You don't like *me*. You look at me like I'm a problem."

"I look at everyone like they're a problem. It's a character flaw." I hesitated. The truth was there, sitting in my chest, heavy as a swallowed stone, and I found—to my considerable annoyance—that I meant to say it. "Because someone helped me. When I was your age. And it saved my life, and I ran away from her anyway, and now she's gone, and I—" My voice did something embarrassing. I stopped talking.

Wren considered this with the gravity of a magistrate. Then she leaned her head against my shoulder, which no one had done in six years, and which I permitted for approximately eleven seconds before remembering myself.

"You should help more people," she said. "It makes your face nicer."

"Go to sleep, Wren."

But I sat awake long after she drifted off, her breathing slow and even, the dark stone warm between us, and admitted the thing I'd been refusing to admit since the mountain road: my invisibility was gone. Not broken—spent. Spent on a fevered stranger, spent on a debt called in, spent on a child with a glowing rock. Every kindness was a coin off the pile, and the pile was nearly empty, and somewhere behind us in the dark the crown's hunt was moving through the mountains like frost through soil.

I felt it before I heard it—the horses lifting their heads as one, Bishop's ears swiveling toward the pass. Then hooves. Many hooves, moving at a walk, unhurried, the way men move when they believe nothing ahead of them can hurt them.

I shook Wren awake and pressed my hand over her mouth before the scream could start.

"Don't make a sound," I breathed. "They've found us."
