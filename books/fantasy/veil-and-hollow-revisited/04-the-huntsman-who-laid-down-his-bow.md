## 4. The Huntsman Who Laid Down His Bow

I have been dead for eleven months, and I'll tell you the worst part of it: the paperwork.

Dain Voss, Royal Huntsman of the Third Order, drowned in the Whitemere crossing. There was a cairn. There was a widow's pension paid out to a woman in Corrow who was never my wife but was very good at grieving on command. There was even a ballad, eventually—huntsmen get ballads; it's one of the few compensations of the trade—and I heard a peddler singing it in a lowland tavern last month while I sat three stools away drinking ale under a name I'd invented in a bathhouse. The ballad had me fighting the river to save a child. I never fought the river. I walked into it with my boots on and swam out the far side, because the alternative was the list.

You haven't seen the list. I hope you never do. It's not a proclamation—that's the pretty version they nail to church doors, all flourishes about *registration* and *the crown's care for its gifted subjects*. The list is what comes after registration fails. Names in columns. Dispositions in the margin. *Burn*, mostly. Sometimes *hold*. The holds were what kept me up at night, back when I still slept. A burn is over quickly and means something—a fear, a cruelty, at least a shape you can hate. A hold means someone wants the person intact. In thirty years of hunting wolves and worse, I learned that anything that wants its quarry alive wants it for a reason, and none of those reasons are kind.

So I died, and I grew a beard, and I became a man named Aldous Fen who buys furs badly and drinks alone. And then, three weeks ago, in that same lowland tavern, I saw a courier's satchel come open on the floor, and I saw the top sheet of paper inside, and the name at the top of it was *Delacroix, Paloma — hedge-craft suspected — HOLD.*

I'd been tracking her ever since. Not to take her. To warn her. Though I'll admit, sitting here in the cold above the Thornfell smuggler roads with my teeth chattering like a card-sharp's dice, that distinction felt increasingly academic. She was very hard to find, which was exactly what I'd expect from a woman worth holding, and exactly what made me certain I was right to be freezing on her account.

The trouble with warning people is that they tend to shoot the messenger. Especially messengers wearing the boots of a royal huntsman, which these were, because I'd been dead too long to afford new ones.

* * *

Paloma:

There was a man on the ridge above the salt road, and he had been there since noon, and he was either the worst hunter in the Thornfells or he wanted me to see him.

I crouched by the stream where I'd stopped to refill the water skins, and I let my eyes do the work instead of my gift, because my gift stayed buried, buried, buried, six years of dirt on top of it, thank you very much. The man was big through the shoulders, grey at the temples, dressed like a fur-trader who'd lost an argument with his own wardrobe. His boots were wrong. Everything else about him said *poor and harmless*, but his boots said *crown-issue, resoled twice, gaiter straps cut for stirrups*. I had sold contraband to enough soldiers' mistresses to know a huntsman's boots when I saw them.

Corvin would have told me to walk away. Find another route, lose him in the switchbacks, be smoke. That was the rule of invisibility, my rule, the one that had kept me alive since I was nineteen years old: nobody sees Paloma Delacroix, and certainly nobody sees whatever it is she carries under her skin like a swallowed coal.

But Maren was gone. Maren was gone, and there was an iron sigil on her hearthstone shaped like a closed eye, and I had spent two days telling myself that a man following me was a coincidence, and I had run out of days.

So instead of walking away, I filled the water skins slowly, and I said, loud enough to carry uphill: "If you're going to follow me down the salt road, you could at least carry something. Nobody travels the warrens empty-handed without looking like tax collectors."

A long pause. Then, from the ridge, a voice like gravel settling: "That obvious?"

"Your boots."

He came down the slope with his hands visible, which I appreciated, and stopped ten feet away, which I appreciated more. Up close he looked tired in the way stone looks tired—worn smooth, still standing. "Dain Voss," he said.

"I know," I said, before I could stop myself. "You're dead. You drowned at Whitemere. There's a ballad."

His face did something complicated. "The child part isn't true," he said.

"Shocking." I shouldered the water skins. "Why is a dead man following a herb-seller through the mountains?"

"Because you're not a herb-seller," he said, "and because your name is on a list I've spent eleven months making sure no one I love ends up on. And because what I have to tell you can't be shouted across a valley." He glanced at the sky, at the light going thin and iron-colored between the peaks. "And because we're being watched, and not by me. Can we walk?"

We walked. I kept him on my left, downhill side, where a stumble would cost him more than me. Old habits. He noticed—I could tell he noticed—and something in his expression suggested approval, which was somehow more unsettling than hostility.

"The hunt lists," he said, as the path narrowed between two walls of frost-cracked rock. "The names. Everyone assumes the crown finds the gifted through neighbors informing on neighbors. Some of that happens. But the lists are too good, Delacroix. They find women like your mistress who've hidden for decades. They find children whose gifts haven't even bloomed yet. Neighbors don't know those things." He paused at a bend, checking our backtrail with a fluidity that made my chest tighten with old recognition—he moved like Maren's stories of the king's hunters, all economy, no waste. "The informants aren't human."

I stopped walking. "Say that again."

"They come from the Hollow." He said it flatly, the way you'd say *the well's gone dry*, no drama, which was the only reason I believed him. "Fae. Something broke between the realms a generation ago—the old folk mutter about a covenant, though nobody remembers the terms—and now the Hollow sells us to the crown. Names, locations, bloodlines. Your mistress wasn't betrayed by a neighbor, Delacroix. She was sold."

The ravens went quiet somewhere above us. I stood very still and felt the ground tilt under everything I thought I knew. Maren had served the Hollow once—I'd found the traces, the offerings, the old marks on her doorframe I'd always taken for superstition. If the fae had given her up—

"Why?" My voice came out smaller than I wanted. "What does the crown pay them?"

"Nothing you'd accept." He started walking again, and after a moment I followed. "Which brings me to the second thing, and I need you to hear all of it before you decide whether to put a knife in me. Lord Cassel—the spymaster, the architect of the whole hunt—isn't burning everyone on those lists. The ones marked *hold* go somewhere. A fortress below the winter palace. I've seen the requisitions. Chains of cold-iron. Wardens who don't talk. Carts going in and nothing coming out." He looked back at me, and his eyes were the flattest, most frightened things I'd seen in years. "Cassel isn't exterminating magic, Delacroix. He's collecting it. And I don't know what for, but I know that a man who gathers power doesn't gather it to admire it."

* * *

Dain:

She took it better than I expected. Most people, told their world is upside down, grab for the nearest solid thing and find it's you, and then blame you when you turn out to be as unstable as everything else. Delacroix just went quiet and walked, and I watched her think, and I thought: *this is why she's lasted six years.* Not luck. Discipline. Somewhere under the smuggler's swagger was a woman who had taught herself to be invisible so thoroughly that she'd nearly disappeared to herself, and I recognized that, God help me, because I'd done the same thing with a different costume.

We made camp in a hollow beneath a leaning rowan—her choice, and she chose it fast, like the tree meant something to her—and she finally spoke over the small fire she permitted herself.

"You said 'no one I love.' On the list." She didn't look at me. "Who?"

"My sister's boy. Shows sparks when he cries. Seven years old." I fed a twig to the flames. "He's registered. Registration is supposed to mean protection. I read the margin notes on his district's page before I ran. It says *hold*."

She was quiet a moment. "So you faked your death to save him."

"I faked my death because they ordered me to lead a hold-team and I said no, and the saying-no would have ended with both of us in chains if I'd stayed alive to say it." I shrugged. "The drowning was convenient. I've always been a strong swimmer. First useful thing the crown ever taught me."

"And now you track strangers through the mountains to warn them." Her tone was wry, but there was something underneath it, some careful weighing. "Why me specifically? There are other names."

"There are. But yours had a note next to it. *HOLD — connection to keeper?*" I watched her face carefully as I said it. "Do you know what a keeper is?"

I have hunted things that wear human faces. I have seen men lie with every muscle they own. So I am qualified to report that whatever passed over Paloma Delacroix's face in that firelight was not performance. It was recognition—of a word she'd seen recently, somewhere, and hadn't understood until this moment. Her hand went to the collar of her coat, to something hidden against her skin.

"No," she said, which was a lie, but a useful one, because it told me she intended to keep her secrets, and I decided in that instant that I could work with a woman who kept secrets properly. Amateurs hide things. Professionals hide that they're hiding things, and she'd almost managed it.

"Fine," I said. "Keep it. But here's my price for the warning, and it's not coin." I leaned forward. "Somewhere in the next weeks, you're going to do something about all this. People like you always are—it's why you survived, you can't help reaching toward the fire. When you do, I want in. Not because I'm noble. Because I'm dead, and dead men make excellent allies. We have nothing left to lose and no one left to betray."

She studied me across the fire for a long time. I let her. Trust is a door you can't open for another person; you can only stand in the doorway and look harmless.

"You move like a huntsman," she said finally. "You talk like a soldier. And you're asking a stranger with a price on her head to trust you because you're *conveniently deceased*." A ghost of a smile. "My old mistress always said the crown sends its kindest faces first."

"She sounds like she was usually right."

"She was always right. It was infuriating." The smile faded, and something harder came up beneath it, bedrock under topsoil. "All right, Huntsman. Here's how this works. You walk with me as far as the warrens. You carry nothing, you ask nothing, and if anyone from Corvin's crew looks at you sideways, you're a fur-buyer named Aldous who got lost. And if you betray me—" she tilted her head, and for half a heartbeat the firelight caught something in her eyes that was not entirely ordinary, a banked coal-glow, there and gone, "—I promise you the ballads will get the drowning wrong a second time."

"Noted," I said, and meant it.

She banked the fire and settled against the rowan's roots, and I took first watch, because I always would, because watching was the only prayer I knew. Above us the peaks wore their winter crowns, and somewhere beyond them, past the veil, something old and hungry was selling names to the crown—one of which, I now understood, mattered to her far more than her own.

*Connection to keeper,* the list had said. And she'd touched whatever hung at her throat like a wound.

Whatever Maren Under-the-Rowan had been, her apprentice was going to find out. And I was beginning to suspect that when she did, the hunt, the crown, and quite possibly both worlds would find out too.
