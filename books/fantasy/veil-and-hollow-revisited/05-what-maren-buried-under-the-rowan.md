## 5. What Maren Buried Under the Rowan

Maren had always said the rowan tree was older than the cottage. I'd assumed it was one of her jokes, the kind she told with a straight face while you tried to work out whether the kettle was actually enchanted or just badly made. Six years of running, and it turned out she'd been telling me the truth the whole time, in the way she told all her truths: buried, sideways, and only visible if you already knew to look.

I knew to look now.

The cottage was still ransacked when I came back—of course it was; crowns don't tidy up after themselves—but I'd spent two days watching it from the treeline before I let myself cross the yard. Dain had wanted to come. I'd told him no, and he'd done that thing where he agreed out loud and then simply appeared beside me anyway, which I was learning was his version of a conversation.

"You're going to dig up your dead teacher's garden," he'd said.

"I'm going to *read* her garden."

"Same thing, with hedge-witches."

He wasn't wrong. That was the annoying part about Dain: he kept being right in a flat, unbothered voice, like a man reporting weather.

* * *

The notes weren't under the rowan. That was the first trick. They were *in* it—or rather, they were what the rowan was for. Maren had taught me that old magic doesn't hide things behind wards, because wards are walls and walls invite siege. Old magic hides things by making them part of something living, so that finding them requires not force but *relationship*. You couldn't break into Maren's secrets. You could only be let in.

So I sat down in the frozen mud at the base of the tree, put my bare hand against the bark despite the cold, and did the thing I'd sworn off doing for six years: I used my gift on purpose, out in the open, under the sky, where anyone might see.

The mountain felt it before I finished the first breath. The fever-warmth rose in my palms, that familiar tide of heat that had gotten a stranger healed and a name onto a hunt list, and instead of pushing it down I let it run—down my arm, into the bark, into the roots. Not giving. Asking. There's a difference, and Maren had drilled the difference into me until I dreamed it: *giving is pride, asking is courtesy.* The tree considered me for a long moment. Trees are slow thinkers, but this one had been thinking for two hundred years, and I got the distinct impression my question had been anticipated.

The bark warmed under my palm. Then it opened—not split, *opened*, like a door deciding to be a door—and there, in a hollow that no woodworm had made, lay an iron box no bigger than a bread loaf, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff with age.

Dain crouched beside me, bow across his knees, watching the treeline out of habit. "Well?"

"She's letting me in." My voice came out strange. "She's been letting me in since before I was born, probably."

I unwrapped the box inside the cottage, on the hearthstone, with the shutters closed and a salt line poured because some habits keep you alive. Inside: a bundle of letters tied with red thread, a leather-bound book swollen with pressed leaves and marginalia, and a small object wrapped separately in linen that I didn't touch yet, because even through three layers of cloth I could feel it humming against my gift like a struck bell.

The letters were addressed to no one. Or rather—they were addressed to *the next keeper*, which is a way of addressing someone without ever having met them, and reading them felt less like reading mail than like being spoken to across a table by a woman who had known, for decades, exactly who would sit down in her chair someday.

*If you are reading this,* the first letter began, *then the hunt has found me, and you have found the tree, and we have both run out of time. Sit down. Pour something warm. This will take longer than you want it to.*

I sat down. I poured something warm. I am nothing if not obedient to a dead woman's stage directions.

*You know me as your mistress. What I never told you—what I swore an oath not to tell any apprentice until the oath itself passed to her—is that I am the last Keeper of the Thornfell Covenant. And so, unless the world has grown very strange indeed, are you.*

I read that sentence four times. The fire popped. Somewhere outside, Dain shifted his weight, patient as furniture.

Here is what the letters said, as best I can give it to you straight, though straightness feels like a betrayal of how it actually landed—which was sideways, like everything else Maren ever taught me.

There had been a covenant. Not a treaty between kings—Maren was scathing on this point, underlined twice: *not a treaty, treaties are between powers, and the covenant was never about power*—but a binding between the mountain and the Hollow, between the human folk of Thornfell and the fae beyond the veil. Sealed in blood, yes, but not blood spilled. Blood *given*. A keeper from each side, human and fae, tying their lives into the agreement so that neither realm could raise hand or hedge or winter against the other without wounding its own keeper first. Peace enforced not by armies but by anatomy. It was, Maren wrote, *the only kind of peace that has ever held anywhere, because it makes war expensive in the only coin anyone truly counts.*

And it had held. For centuries, apparently—the long quiet generations when the mountain folk left cream on their doorsteps without knowing why, when the veil thinned politely at midsummer and thickened courteously at midwinter, when gifted children were oddities rather than criminals. All of it covenant-work. All of it maintained by two keepers whose names never appeared in any chronicle because keeping, done properly, is invisible. I thought of Maren's endless small lessons: the exact courtesy owed to a stream, the way you greet a crow, why you never thank a fae aloud. I had thought I was learning superstition. I had been learning *diplomacy*.

Then, a generation ago—it. The break. Maren's handwriting changed here, went small and hard, letters pressed deep enough to tear three pages beneath.

*The crown sent men. Of course it did. A king cannot abide a peace he does not control, and a spymaster least of all. They came for the Hollow's keeper, and they took her, and they broke her—not killed, broken, there is a difference and Cassel knows the difference, it is his whole art—and the covenant snapped like a tendon cut. The Hollow has been bleeding rage into our world ever since. Every informant, every list, every gifted child named to the crown's hunters—that venom comes through the wound the crown itself made. The fae do not betray us out of malice. They betray us because we betrayed them first, and grief curdles.*

*Cassel. Remember the name. Lord Aldous Cassel, the king's spymaster. He was there at the breaking. He is not a man who fears magic. He is a man who intends to be the last one holding any.*

I set the letter down. My hands were steady, which surprised me, because the rest of me felt like the yard after the ransacking—everything pulled out and turned over and none of it back where it belonged.

Six years. Six years of invisibility, of salt lines and false names and healing strangers only by accident, and the whole time I'd been a piece on a board I hadn't known existed—a piece, worse, a *load-bearing* piece, the way you don't notice a wall is structural until it cracks. Maren hadn't taken me as an apprentice out of charity. She'd been training her successor. Every lesson, every drill, every tedious hour of learning the courtesies owed to crows—that had been statecraft. Secret, unregistered, blood-deep statecraft, and I had run away from it at nineteen because I thought the burden was just *being gifted in a country that burns gifted people*.

It had never occurred to me that the burden had a shape, a history, a counterpart on the other side of the veil, and a broken half lying somewhere in the dark, bleeding.

* * *

Dain found me at dawn, still at the hearthstone, the letters spread around me like the petals of a very grim flower.

"You didn't sleep."

"Brilliant deduction. They must miss you at court."

He ignored that, which was also becoming a habit, and crouched to read a letter upside down, which was pointless, but I think he wanted something to do with his hands. "What is it?"

"It's a war," I said. "A war I've been losing my whole life without knowing I was fighting. The hunt lists—the informants—they're coming from the Hollow. From the *break*. Someone shattered a peace-treaty a generation ago and the shrapnel is still flying, and the man who shattered it runs the king's spies." I laughed, and it came out wrong. "Maren was the last keeper. Which makes me—" I stopped, because saying it made it real, and part of me was still hoping the letters would turn out to be one of her jokes.

"Makes you what?" Dain asked quietly.

"The fix," I said. "Or the next casualty. Apparently those are the same job."

He was silent a while. Then he said, "The lists. I told you they came from the Hollow. I thought it was treason." He picked up the letter with Cassel's name in it and read it twice. "It's worse. It's *policy*. Someone built the fear on purpose."

"Yes."

"And your teacher knew, and hid, and trained you, and never once told you why."

"She was protecting me."

"Was she?" Dain asked, not cruelly, just honestly, the way he does everything. "Or was she protecting the covenant? Keepers make hard accountants, I'd guess."

I wanted to throw something at him. Instead I looked at the last item in the box, the one I still hadn't unwrapped, and unwrapped it now because anger makes me reckless and recklessness is at least a form of movement.

Inside the linen was a knife. Small, ceremonial, absurdly plain—no jewels, no sigil, just old iron and a handle worn smooth by generations of hands. But when my fingers closed around it, the hum I'd felt through the wrapping resolved into something with words in it, almost. Something patient. Something that had been waiting a long time to be held by the right blood.

The blade's edge caught the firelight, and along it, faint as breath on glass, I saw writing that wasn't there when I looked directly—old script, the kind Maren used for her most serious charms. I tilted the knife. The letters swam, then settled:

*Given, not spilled.*

Blood-giver's steel. The sealing-knife. The thing the original keepers had used to tie themselves into the covenant, and the thing, presumably, that would be needed to tie anyone in again.

"Well," I said, to the knife, to the ashes, to Maren wherever she was. "You could have mentioned this. Any time in twelve years. Literally any time."

The fire popped. Dead women are terrible correspondents.

But underneath the vertigo—underneath the sick, tilting feeling of learning that your whole life was a load-bearing wall in someone else's architecture—something else was settling into place, cold and clear as the mountain air. Because the letters had ended with instructions, and Maren's instructions were never vague, and the last line of the last letter read:

*The gate the smugglers use, below the old watch-stone at Grayhollow Pass, opens both ways for fae-blood. If the covenant is to be reforged, it must be reforged from the Hollow's side. Take the knife. Take the debt you're owed. Take the child—she is more important than she knows. And Paloma: whatever price they ask, remember that the covenant binds the keeper, not the crown. Read that twice.*

I read it twice.

Then I went to find Corvin, because we were going to need passage, and Wren, because apparently a half-fae child I'd known for three days was a piece of my inheritance, and Dain, because—

Because, honestly, at that point, why not him too.

Outside, the snow had begun again, fine and dry and wrong, falling straight down through windless air, the way snow falls when something enormous and cold is breathing on the world from the other side of a wound. The unnatural winter. The Hollow's grief, leaking through.

We were going to have to go in and answer for it.
