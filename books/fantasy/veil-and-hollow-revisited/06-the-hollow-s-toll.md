## 6. The Hollow's Toll

The gate was a crack in a rock face that didn't look like anything at all.

I want to be clear about that, because everyone in Corvin's crew had spent three days telling me stories about the smuggler's gate — how it breathed, how it sang, how it ate a man named Tolly back before I was born. And then we got there and it was a crack in a rock face about as wide as my hand, up behind a waterfall that was more rumor than water this time of year.

"That's it?" Dain said. He said it the way he said most things, like he was disappointed in the world and expected better of it.

"That's it," Paloma said. "To you." She looked at me. Everyone looked at me. I'd been getting looked at a lot since the rowan tree on the mountain road had leaned down to say hello to me, which is not something trees do for people without fae blood, and Paloma had gone very quiet in a way that meant she'd figured out what I was before I'd finished figuring out how to say it.

"Only the fae-blooded can open it," she said. "That's what Maren's notes say. The gate knows its own."

"I don't know how," I said.

"You do. It's just been waiting for you to stop pretending you don't."

Which felt unfair, because I'd only known I was pretending for about four days, and before that I'd thought I was just strange. Which I still was. But it turned out there was a word for it, and the word came with a gate.

I put my hand in the crack. The rock was cold, and then it wasn't, and then it wasn't rock at all — it was skin, or bark, or something that had been both once and couldn't decide. Something on the other side pressed back against my palm, gentle as a cat bumping your hand, asking to be let in.

So I let it.

The crack opened like an eye.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about stepping into another world: it smells like home.

Not *my* home. I didn't have one of those, unless you counted the back of a smuggler's wagon, which smelled like pipe smoke and Corvin's terrible cheese. But the air on the other side of the gate went into my lungs and every part of me that had ever been hungry sat up and said *oh, there you are.*

The Hollow was green the way nothing in Thornfell was green. Green like the color had been invented here and everything else was a copy. Trees with silver bark stood so tall their tops disappeared into a sky that wasn't quite sky — more like the memory of sky, gold at the edges where it met the branches. Little lights moved through the undergrowth, low and slow, like bees made of dusk. Somewhere far off, water was falling upward.

And underneath all of it, under the green and the gold and the falling-upward water, was a cold. A wrong cold. Frost on the ferns in a world that should never have frost. The little lights flickered when they crossed it, like moths near a dead flame.

"It's dying," Paloma said softly. She'd come through behind me, and Dain after her, and Corvin last, swearing under his breath in three languages because he'd promised himself he'd never come back here and here he was. "The winter's reached into the Hollow too."

"The Hollow's leaking," said a voice from the trees. "Like everything else that was broken."

She stepped out from between two silver trunks like she'd always been part of them and had simply decided to walk. Tall, the way the fae are tall — not bigger than a person, exactly, but *more*, as if someone had taken a person and stretched the idea of her. Her hair was the white of birch bark. Her eyes were the exact green of the Hollow, which meant they were also starting to go gray at the edges, like frost creeping over glass.

She looked at me and stopped walking entirely.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you poor thing. Half and half."

"I'm not a poor thing," I said.

"No," she agreed, and something in her face did a complicated thing. "You're not. You're a door that walks. Do you know how rare— " She caught herself, glanced at Paloma, and changed course like a river changing course, smooth and total. "I am Vess. Ward of the Autumn Court, such as it still is. You were expected."

"We weren't coming to the court," Paloma said.

"You were coming to the Hollow, and the Hollow comes to the court, these days. There isn't much of it left to come to." Vess turned and walked, and the trees got out of her way, and after a moment we followed, because what else do you do when a door opens in front of you except walk through it?

* * *

The Autumn Court was a ruin wearing its own ghost.

The hall had been grand once — I could tell by the bones of it, the great ribs of silver wood holding up a roof that was mostly open to the gray-gold sky. Ivy grew through the throne dais. Frost grew through everything else. Maybe forty fae remained, and they moved through the ruined hall the way people move through a house where someone has recently died: carefully, quietly, touching nothing.

They looked at us the whole way across the floor. At Dain they looked with hatred, and I understood that even before anyone said the word *huntsman*. At Corvin they looked with old amusement, like a cat remembering a dog it used to know. At Paloma they looked with hunger, and I liked that least of all.

At me they looked with something worse than any of those. They looked at me like I was a letter they'd been waiting years to receive.

Vess brought us to a fire that burned without heat — blue-white, shivering, the same sick color as the frost outside. Beside it, on a chair that had clearly been a throne until someone decided thrones were bad luck now, sat a fae so old his skin had gone the color of ash. His crown was a circle of rowan twigs, brown and brittle.

"Keeper's girl," he said to Paloma. His voice sounded like wind through a dead orchard. "Maren's girl. We smelled her coming. Blood carries."

"She's not my—" Paloma started, and stopped, because apparently that was complicated too. Everything was complicated. I was starting to think complicated was the family trait of everyone I'd fallen in with.

"Maren kept the covenant," the old fae said. "The covenant broke. You know this?"

"I know it broke a generation ago," Paloma said. "I know that's why your people started selling names to Cassel's informants. Why the hunt lists come out of the Hollow."

The hall went very quiet. Forty fae, and you could have heard a moth land.

"When the covenant broke," the old fae said, "the road between worlds went dark. No trade. No passage. No protection. My people starved in a realm going gray, and yours burned in a kingdom going mad, and both because one keeper's line failed to keep." He leaned forward. Frost crackled along the arm of his chair. "We sold names because hunger makes merchants of us all. I will not apologize for it. But I will end it, if the price is paid."

"What price?" Paloma asked, and I knew — I knew the way you know a storm is coming, in your teeth, in the roots of your hair — that she already knew, and was asking anyway because hope is stupid like that.

"The covenant was sealed in keeper's blood," the old fae said. "It can only be reforged in keeper's blood. Maren is dying in your king's dungeons; her blood would be thin and late besides. But her line did not end with her." His eyes, gray-frosted, settled on Paloma like weights settling onto a scale. "Yours is strong. Yours is close. Reforge the covenant with your own blood, keeper's girl, and Maren walks free. The gates open. The winter lifts. Both realms breathe again."

"And if I don't?"

"Then the Hollow finishes dying, the winter finishes eating Thornfell, and Cassel gets exactly the world he is building. One with no magic left anywhere but his."

Nobody said anything. The fire burned cold. Somewhere above us, through the broken roof, the gray-gold sky was doing something that might have been weather.

Paloma looked at the old fae for a long moment. Then she laughed — one short, hard note, no humor in it at all.

"You're asking me to die."

"I'm asking you to pay," the old fae said. "Death is one shape payment can take. The covenant has others. None of them gentle. None of them small." He spread his ash-colored hands. "You wanted Maren back. Here is her price. I did not set it. The covenant did, a thousand years before either of us was born, and neither of us is big enough to argue with it."

* * *

They gave us a room to argue in, which was a hollowed-out chamber in the wall of the hall with a curtain of woven silver moss for a door. Privacy, fae-style, which means everyone can still hear you if they want to.

Corvin paced. Dain stood by the entrance with his arms crossed, being furniture. Paloma sat on the floor with Maren's notes in her lap, not reading them, just holding them, and I sat next to her because sitting next to people is mostly what I'm for, apparently.

"He's lying about something," Dain said finally. "Fae don't offer bargains straight. There's a hook in it somewhere."

"There's a hook in everything," Corvin said. "Question is whether the fish cares, drowning as it is."

"He's not lying about the price," Paloma said quietly. "I can feel it. Under my skin. Since we crossed the gate, I could feel the covenant — like a snapped wire, still humming. It wants to be whole. It doesn't care who pays. It just wants *a* keeper." She looked down at her hands. "It's been waiting for me my whole life. That's what Maren was training me for. That's why she hid me. Not from the hunt." Her voice cracked, just slightly. "From this."

"So don't pay it," I said.

Everyone looked at me. I was getting used to that.

"Find another way," I said. "You always find another way. You found one for Corvin. You found one for me. You're the finding-another-way person."

"Wren—"

"I mean it." My voice did the wobbly thing. I hated the wobbly thing. "You said the covenant binds the keeper. You haven't even read all of Maren's notes yet. Maybe there's a page that says *actually, don't*. Maybe the old tree-man is wrong. Maybe—"

"Wren." Paloma took my hand. Her fingers were cold, colder than they should have been this deep in a world that was supposed to be warm. "Thank you." And she smiled at me, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was the smile you give someone when you've already decided and you're just being kind about it.

Outside the moss curtain, soft as snowfall, Vess's voice drifted in — not to us, to someone else, some other fae in the corridor beyond:

"—the half-blood child? A door that walks? The Court will want her kept, whatever the keeper decides. Doors that walk are worth ten covenants—"

Paloma's hand tightened around mine.

Dain had gone very still by the curtain. Corvin had stopped pacing.

None of us said anything. But I saw Paloma look at each of us in turn — Corvin, Dain, me — and I watched her do the arithmetic behind her eyes, the same arithmetic she must have been doing since the gate: what the Hollow was offering, what it was charging, and what it planned to take anyway.

"They're not letting us leave," Dain murmured. "Are they."

Down the ruined hall, the cold fire flared, and the old fae's voice rose to fill the silence, dry as dead leaves:

"Keeper's girl. Come and give us your answer."
