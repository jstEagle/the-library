## 7. Cassel's Confession

They took my bow first, which was expected. Then they took my boots, which was insulting. By the time they marched me down into the dark beneath the Thornfell garrison in nothing but stockinged feet, I had catalogued eleven ways to kill a man with a belt buckle and was mourning the loss of all of them.

The cell they gave me was better than most inns I'd slept in. That was the first wrong thing. Huntsmen who fake their own deaths get a hole in the ground and a bucket, not a feather bed and a basin of clean water. Someone wanted me comfortable. Comfort, in my experience, is what powerful men offer right before they ask you to do something that will keep you up at night for the rest of your life.

I'd been caught on purpose, so I couldn't complain too loudly about the accommodations. Three days I'd let myself be tracked through the high passes—sloppy on purpose, loud on purpose, leaving sign a first-year apprentice could read—until Captain Vess's riders closed around me like a purse string. What I hadn't planned on was how much it would sting to be good at being bad at something.

* * *

They came for me at dusk. Not soldiers—a servant in grey livery, soft-voiced, who said only, "His lordship will see you now," as if I were late for dinner rather than a dead man walking.

Lord Cassel received me in a room with no windows, deep under the mountain, lit by a fire that burned too blue. He sat behind a desk of black oak, writing, and did not look up for the better part of a minute. I knew the trick. Make them wait. Let them study the room instead of you. I'd used it myself on poachers and worse. So I studied him instead: silver at the temples, hands ink-stained and precise, a man built like a well-made lock—nothing wasted, everything fitted.

"Dain Ashford," he said at last, setting down his pen. "Dead these fourteen months. Buried with honors, I'm told. Your cenotaph is very handsome. Your mother wept beautifully."

"My mother died when I was nine."

"Then someone wept beautifully." He smiled without warmth. "Sit."

I sat. The chair was positioned lower than his, another old trick, and I made a show of settling into it like a king taking a throne. If he noticed, he didn't say.

"You've been busy," Cassel continued. "Warning witches. Burning hunt lists. Stealing prisoners from the waystation at Coldbrook—" he turned a page of paper toward me, and there I was in charcoal sketch, hood up, looking exactly as guilty as I felt, "—which was, incidentally, where we were keeping three gifted children whose names had come to us from very reliable sources."

"Your reliable sources are fae," I said. "You're buying your lists out of the Hollow."

Something flickered across his face—not surprise. Disappointment, maybe, that I already knew. "And how does a dead huntsman know that?"

"Because I followed the lists backward. Every name, every village. The informants leave tracks no human leaves. Frost in summer. No footprints in mud. And one other thing." I leaned forward. "Every informant's mark on the crown's ledger is written in silver ink that doesn't smudge. I checked. I stole a page."

For a long moment the only sound was the blue fire eating its fuel. Then Cassel laughed—a short, genuine bark of amusement that frightened me more than anything else in that windowless room.

"You always were the best tracker I ever trained," he said. "Which is why this conversation is going to go differently than you think."

He rose, crossed to the fire, and held out his hand over the flames. I braced for a demonstration—the usual spymaster theatrics, a hidden blade, a vial of poison. Instead, the fire bent. It rose off the hearth like a hound called to heel, coiling around his wrist, licking at his sleeve without burning it, and settled into his palm as a small blue-white star.

I stopped breathing. I know because when I started again it hurt.

"You see the problem," Cassel said quietly, watching the flame turn in his hand like a jeweler examining a flawed stone. "Fourteen years I have run the crown's intelligence service. Fourteen years of hunting people like me. Do you know what that requires, Dain? Discipline. Absolute, total discipline. The gift is a hunger, and I have starved mine every day of my life since I was twelve years old and watched a hedge-witch burn in the market square at Aldenmere while the crowd cheered." He closed his fist and the star went out. "She was my mother."

The room tilted slightly. I gripped the arms of the chair.

"So when you ask why the lists come from the Hollow, why the fae sell us their own renegades and our kingdom's stray witches besides—it isn't treachery. It's tidiness. The covenant between Thornfell and the Hollow broke a generation ago, and everything since has been leakage. Magic seeping into the world uncontrolled, ungoverned, dangerous. Witches healing fevers with a touch. Children starting fires in their sleep. My hunters find them, my registers record them, and my fires—" he glanced at the hearth, "—dispose of them. All of it, every step, controlled. Except the source. Except the Hollow itself, which sits behind its veil doing whatever it pleases, feeding on fear, selling information to whichever crown pays best. That is the last ungoverned power in the world. And I intend to close it permanently."

"The veil can't be closed," I said. "It's older than the crown."

"The veil is a door," Cassel said, "and doors are built by someone, for a reason, with a mechanism. We found the mechanism. Or rather, an old woman buried under a rowan tree kept the notes, and my people kept the old woman." He said it flatly, without pride, and somehow that was worse. "Maren Delacroix. The last keeper of the covenant. She's been very cooperative, once she understood that cooperation was the only thing standing between her gift and the pyre."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept falling. Maren. Which meant Paloma's teacher, which meant the road Paloma was following led straight to a woman in Cassel's cells—and Cassel watched my face do exactly what it did, because he was Lord Cassel, and reading faces was half his trade.

"There she is," he said softly. "There's the loyalty. I wondered when we'd get to her."

"I don't know any—"

"Dain." He said my name the way you'd set down a heavy object. "You faked your death rather than hunt the gifted. You've spent a year warning them, moving them, hiding them. You crossed half the mountain range to find a runaway herb-smuggler named Paloma Delacroix, because you believed—I've read your letters to her, don't look surprised, we intercepted the courier at Millbrook—that the hunt was wrong and she could stop it. You are many things, but you are not a liar. It's your single greatest flaw." He sat back down, folded his hands. "So I won't insult you with lies. I'll offer you the truth instead, and then I'll offer you a choice."

* * *

The truth, as he laid it out, was elegant and horrible.

The covenant, he explained, had never been a treaty between equals. It was a chain—one end fastened to the Hollow, the other to a keeper's blood, generation after generation, binding both realms into a balance neither could break alone. It had kept the peace. It had also kept magic caged, metered out in thimbles, hoarded by whoever held the keeper's leash. Kings, mostly. Priests, sometimes. Never the gifted themselves.

"When the covenant broke, magic went feral," Cassel said. "Scattered into the bloodlines like spilled seed. That's why there are more gifted born now than in a century. That's why the hunt exists—because the world filled up with power nobody governs, and ungoverned power terrifies kings." He spread his hands. "I am offering governance. Reforge the covenant—but this time, hold the chain yourself. Not a keeper chosen by fae riddles and rowan trees. The crown. Me. Magic becomes an instrument of state, licensed, registered, directed. No more burnings—why burn what you can employ? No more hunts. The gifted become what they should always have been: a resource."

"And the ones who refuse?"

"Refuse what? A life without fear?" He almost looked sorry for me. "Dain, I am the most powerful man in this kingdom and I have spent thirty years pretending to be nothing. I know precisely what fear costs. I am offering to end it for everyone. All it requires is order."

"It requires a cage," I said. "A prettier cage, with your name on the door."

"A cage keeps wolves out as well as in." His voice cooled several degrees. "But let's come to your part in this, since we both know why you're really here."

He slid a paper across the desk. On it, in his precise hand, was a list of charges that would have hanged me four times over—desertion, treason, arson at Coldbrook, abduction of crown prisoners. And beneath it, a second document: full pardon, restoration of rank, reinstatement to the Royal Huntsmen with seniority backdated to my supposed death. My old life, pressed flat and offered up like a flower in a book.

"Paloma Delacroix is heading for the Hollow," Cassel said. "Through a smuggler's gate in the eastern passes—one of perhaps three crossings left that the fae-blooded can open. When she reaches it, she intends to reforge the covenant herself. Bind it to her own blood. Become the new keeper." He watched me carefully. "If she does that, nothing changes. A new chain, a new jailer, and in twenty years some clever spymaster—perhaps me, perhaps my successor—hunts her down and starts all this again. But if the gate stays shut. If she arrives at the crossing to find it sealed, and comes to us instead, angry and alive and willing to negotiate—then we reforge the covenant properly. Under the crown. With her teacher freed as a gesture of good faith. Everyone lives, Dain. Even the ones you love."

"And all you want from me," I said, "is the gate."

"All I want is the gate."

I looked at the pardon a long time. That was the trap of it, and he knew it, and I knew it: it wasn't the rank or the name or even the safety. It was the *world*. Fourteen months of sleeping in ditches, of being a ghost, of flinching at my own name spoken aloud. Fourteen months of watching people I'd sworn to protect burn anyway, because one repentant huntsman with a bow couldn't stop a machine built by a kingdom. Cassel was offering me back to myself. Warm beds. My mother's grave—someone else's mother's grave—to visit. An ending to the running.

And all it cost was a girl who healed strangers' fevers with her bare hands, and a half-fae child who deserved better than either realm, and a smuggler who paid his debts, and every witch yet unborn who would spend their lives as inventory in Lord Cassel's ledger.

"You're hesitating," Cassel observed. "That's natural. Take the night. There's a bed upstairs, real food, a bath. Tomorrow at dawn, give me the gate—or give me nothing, and I'll send you to Coldbrook's replacement facility, which is considerably less pleasant than this room." He returned to his papers, dismissal complete. At the door, the grey servant waiting to escort me, I paused.

"For what it's worth," I said, "your mother would be proud of how well you hide."

The pen stopped moving. Just for a heartbeat. Then it resumed, steady as a metronome, and I was marched back down the corridor to my comfortable cell, my borrowed boots, my beautiful trap.

I sat on the feather bed until the blue fire burned low, turning the choice over like a stone with two sharp edges. Give him the gate, and live. Refuse, and die uselessly in a hole, having saved no one. Those were the options he'd built for me, and they were good options, airtight, a well-made lock.

But I had spent a year learning from a woman who survived by being invisible, and she'd taught me the thing Cassel had never learned in all his disciplined, starving years: a lock only holds if nobody knows the third option.

Because here was what Lord Cassel didn't know, what no intercept could have told him: Paloma wasn't heading for the eastern gate. She was heading for the smuggler's crossing at Ravenmere—and Corvin's crew had gone ahead two days ago to open it.

And I was the only soul in this mountain who knew that the gate had *already been opened*.

At dawn, when they came for my answer, I intended to give Lord Cassel exactly what he asked for.

Just not the truth he thought it carried.
