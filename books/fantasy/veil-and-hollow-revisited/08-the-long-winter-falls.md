## 8. The Long Winter Falls

The frost came for the Hollow the way grief comes for a house—first to the edges, then all at once.

I felt it before I saw it. We had made camp in the lee of a standing stone that hummed faintly when Wren touched it, and I woke in the grey hour with my breath hanging in the air like something I'd said and couldn't take back. The Hollow did not have winters. That was the whole point of it. Maren had told me once, when I was small enough to sit at her feet and believe the world was larger than it was cruel: *the Hollow keeps its own weather, child. It owes nothing to any sky.*

Now the sky had come calling anyway, and it was collecting debts.

Frost climbed the standing stone in white ferns. The moss beneath us had gone stiff as old bread. And far off, past the black line of the veil-trees, I could hear it—a sound like a thousand small bells dying, which is what ice sounds like when it takes a river by the throat.

"They've sealed the gates," Dain said. He'd been awake for hours; huntsmen sleep like cats, one ear always open. "All of them. I counted four crossings on the way in. Cassel's men are walled up behind iron and salt."

"Salt," Corvin repeated, spitting into the frozen bracken. "That's churchyard work. You don't salt a gate unless you mean never to open it again."

"He doesn't mean to open them," I said. My voice came out flat, and I was grateful for it. Flat was better than what was underneath. "He means to let the Hollow die with everyone inside it. Then he walks into Thornfell and tells the crown the fae realm was always a story, and every gifted person left alive is a criminal with no country to flee to."

Wren had gone very still. She'd been still since dawn, actually, sitting with her knees pulled up and her strange half-fae eyes fixed on the treeline, where the trees were doing something trees should not do: whitening from the crown down, like candles burning in reverse.

"It's cold here," she said, in the small voice she used when she was trying not to be afraid. "It's not supposed to be cold here, is it?"

"No, love." Corvin put a hand on her head, rough and gentle all at once. "It's not."

* * *

We reached the court by midday, if the Hollow still bothered with midday, which I doubted. The light had gone thin and sideways, the way light gets through dirty ice, and the fae who met us on the processional road looked like their own portraits left out in weather. Beautiful, and fading. A lady whose skin had been opaline the last time I'd seen her—through Maren's stories, I mean, painted in words—now had cracks at her cheekbones like dropped porcelain.

The court received us in a hall of living wood that was no longer entirely living. Frost furred the pillars. Somewhere above us, a branch groaned and did not spring back.

The Queen—or whatever passed for one now; the Hollow's politics shifted like water and I'd stopped trying to chart them—looked at me with eyes the color of thawing ponds.

"You bring winter into our house, hedge-child."

"I bring nothing," I said. "Your gates are sealed by mortal iron. The winter came from outside, same as it came to Thornfell. Same frost, same hand." I made myself say the next part plainly, because plainly was the only coin they respected. "Cassel. The crown's spymaster. He's breaking the covenant so the gift dies everywhere he can't hold it."

A murmur went through the court like wind through wheat. The pond-eyed Queen tilted her head.

"The covenant is broken already. It broke a generation ago, when your mistress's mistress let it lapse. What remains is the price, and the price has been named." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I'd ever seen, because it was kind. "Maren's freedom for the keeper's blood. You know this. You crossed half a world knowing it."

"I know it."

"Then why do you hesitate? The Hollow offers you everything. Belonging. Power that needs no hiding. A home for that fire you've spent six years smothering under smuggler's rags." Her voice softened into something almost tender, and that was the trap, wasn't it—the tenderness. Every fae bargain I'd ever heard of was baited with tenderness. "Stay. Be what you are, loudly. No hunt will ever reach you here."

And oh, it would have been so easy to say yes. Six years of flinching at my own hands. Six years of healing strangers in the dark and running before they could thank me. Here was a door marked *home*, and all it cost was everything.

I thought about Wren, who was half of both worlds and welcome in neither. About Corvin, who owed me a life and paid it in loyalty instead. About Dain, who had shot his own legend dead rather than become Cassel's hound.

About Maren, dying somewhere below us in a cell of frost and iron.

"I'll pay the price," I said. "But not the way you've priced it."

The hall went quiet enough that I heard the ice growing.

* * *

They took me down to her after that. Whether out of respect or cruelty I couldn't say; with the fae it's usually both wearing each other's coats.

Her cell was carved into the root of the palace-tree itself, and the frost had gotten in there too, silvering the walls, creeping over Maren's blanket, her hair, her eyelashes. She was smaller than I remembered. Teachers always are, once you stop being afraid of them.

"Paloma." She said my name the way she used to say it when I'd botched a charm—half scolding, half proud anyway. "You look terrible."

"You look worse."

"Sixty years of practice." She coughed, and the sound had ice in it. I knelt and took her hands, and they were cold in a way that had nothing to do with the cell. "You shouldn't have come."

"You taught me to keep promises. This one's yours. I'm just delivering it back."

Something moved across her face—grief, maybe, or the particular pain of a teacher watching a student walk into the exact lesson she'd hoped to spare her. "Then listen, because I haven't got the strength to say it twice, and the fae listening at that wall have been waiting sixty years for me to get it wrong." She squeezed my fingers, hard, surprising strength in it. "Everyone thinks the covenant binds the Hollow to Thornfell. Peace for peace, passage for passage. Even the court believes it. Even Cassel believes it, gods rot him, and that's why he thinks he can break it."

"But it doesn't?"

"The covenant binds the *keeper*." Her eyes held mine, and in them I saw the girl she must have been, and the woman before her, and the whole long chain of women passing down one terrible secret like a hot coal. "It was never chains for the fae, child. It was a leash for us. For whoever kept it. The covenant lives in the keeper's blood—that's why it needs blood to reforge, why it weakens when the line thins, why it broke when my mother's line faltered. The keeper carries the peace between worlds inside her own body. Every gift on either side of the veil flows through her, and she holds it steady, or it floods."

My mouth had gone dry. "Hold it how long?"

"For life." She said it simply, the way you'd say *for supper*. "No crossing the veil again—not into the Hollow, not freely into Thornfell's wild places. No passing the gift to children without passing the burden too. You stand at the hinge, Paloma, and hinges don't get to be doors. You belong to neither side. That's the price. Not death. Something the court finds far more interesting." Her cracked lips curved. "Exile. Forever. From both realms at once."

The cold in the cell seemed to press closer, listening.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then the winter finishes what Cassel started, the Hollow dies, and every gifted soul in Thornfell burns or kneels. Including that half-fae child you've been pretending not to love." She paused. "You've been doing a poor job of pretending, by the way. Since she was born, I'd wager."

I laughed. It came out wrong, wet and cracked, but it came out. "You're dying, Maren."

"I'm dying *slowly*. There's a difference, and the difference is you." She lifted my hands, pressed them against her frost-silvered cheeks. "I was supposed to carry it. I failed—I ran, after my mother died, I hid in a cottage and taught herbs to smugglers' daughters because I couldn't bear the weight. This is my penance arriving late. But you—" her voice caught, and for the first time since I'd known her, Maren sounded afraid, "you don't have to fail first. You can choose it clean. That's worth more than you know. A covenant chosen freely holds twice as strong."

I closed my eyes. Behind them I saw the whole shape of it: the road out of Thornfell, the gate, the years ahead. No home. No Hollow. Standing forever on the threshold while everyone I loved lived on one side or the other.

Wren's face. Corvin's grudging grin. Dain's careful, wounded honor.

"All right," I said. "But hear me, all of you—" I raised my voice toward the frost-rimed walls, toward the court listening, toward the winter itself. "I accept the blood-price. But I set the terms. The covenant binds to *my* blood alone—not to any crown, not to any court, not to any queen's permission. When I die, it passes to whoever chooses it freely, the way I'm choosing now. No more inherited chains. No more mothers handing daughters a leash and calling it duty."

Silence. Then, from beyond the door, a sound like the whole Hollow exhaling.

Maren smiled, and some of the frost on her face seemed, for a moment, to melt. "There she is," she whispered. "There's my girl."

* * *

They brought me to the heart of the Hollow at dusk-that-wasn't-dusk, to a circle of rowan trees older than the mountains, their leaves hanging frozen like green glass. The court stood at the edge, pale and watching. Beyond them, far off, I could hear the sealed gates groaning under Cassel's siege-iron, and beneath that, closer, a new sound: boots. Many. Coming down through the veil-trees with torches and salt and certainty.

Dain reached me first, moving like the huntsman he'd stopped being. "They're in. Cassel's vanguard—fifty men, maybe more. Ten minutes, perhaps less."

"Then hold the gate for ten minutes."

Corvin was already uncoiling rope, wearing the expression of a man doing arithmetic with his own life and finding the sum insulting. "Ten minutes against fifty crown soldiers. With a half-fae child and a dead man. Wonderful. Paloma—if this goes badly, I want you to know the debt's paid and I regret nothing except every decision that led me to know you."

"Liar," Wren said.

"Worst kind," he agreed, and kissed the top of her head, and turned to face the dark.

Maren's voice, in my memory, steady as a hearth: *a covenant chosen freely holds twice as strong.*

I drew the knife. The rowans leaned in. The winter held its breath.

And the first torchlight broke through the trees.
