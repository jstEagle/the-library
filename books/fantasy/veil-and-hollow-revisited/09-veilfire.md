## 9. Veilfire

The heart of the Hollow was a garden that had forgotten it was one.

I stood in the center of what Maren's notes called the Covenant Ring—a circle of white stones tall as doorways, each carved with script older than any crown, any kingdom, any name I knew. Frost had climbed the stones like ivy and killed everything green. Beyond them, the fae court watched from a distance that was not distance: faces in the frozen mist, beautiful and terrible and hungry, waiting to see if I would pay.

The cold here wasn't weather. It was absence. It had eaten the Hollow from the inside for a generation, ever since the covenant broke, and now it was eating Thornfell too, and Cassel wanted to let it finish the meal.

My hands were shaking. I want to be honest about that, because the songs will say otherwise if anyone ever sings them. My hands were shaking so badly I could barely hold the rowan knife Maren had buried under her tree twenty years ago, wrapped in oilcloth, waiting for a keeper who turned out to be me.

"Paloma." Wren's voice came through the gate behind me—small, strained. "It's opening."

Of course it was. Nothing in my life had ever waited politely for me to finish.

* * *

Let me back up an hour, because the gate deserves its due.

The smuggler's gate into the Hollow sat in a hollow of black stone beneath the Thornfell root-system, a door that only opened for fae blood. Wren had pressed both palms to it and sung three notes that no human throat should have been able to find, and the stone had unfolded like a moth leaving a chrysalis.

Corvin had looked at that unfolding with the expression of a man watching his entire livelihood become a war crime. "Right," he'd said. "So we're doing this."

"You could stay," I told him. "The debt's paid. It's been paid since the fever took your crew and I pulled you all back."

"Debt's got nothing to do with it." He checked his knives the way other men check their consciences—quickly, and without liking what they find. "You're walking into a frozen fairy hell to bleed yourself dry on a rock. Someone has to carry you out after."

"That's not how the price works."

"Then someone has to argue with you about it. I'm very good at arguing."

Dain said nothing at all. He'd been quiet since Cassel's men first appeared on our trail two days ago—quiet the way deep water is quiet. Now he unslung his bow, the one he'd laid down when he faked his death rather than hunt people like me, and strung it again.

"I swore off this," he said, mostly to himself. "Bows. Hunting. All of it." He tested the string. "But there's hunting and there's holding a line. Different animal entirely."

Wren took my hand. Her fingers were cold as the gate-stone. "If you die in there," she said, with the flat pragmatism of a nine-year-old who has already lost too much, "who teaches me?"

"Nobody," I said. "You'll teach yourself. You'll do it badly and scare yourself half to death and then one day you'll do it right, and it'll be yours instead of mine. That's better."

"It sounds worse."

"It usually is."

* * *

Now the gate was opening behind me, and I didn't need to turn around to know what came through. I'd felt Cassel's gift before, back in the warrens—a cold, grasping thing, magic worn like a stolen coat, and underneath it the man himself: brilliant, patient, and so terrified of anyone else's power that he'd decided to burn the world's down rather than share the air with it.

Hunters poured through the veil-gate like spilled ink. Six of them, then eight, in grey leathers with iron sigils at their throats, and behind them, unhurried, Lord Cassel himself, dressed for court as though he'd strolled in from a drawing room instead of a dying realm.

He looked at the Covenant Ring. He looked at me, kneeling among the stones with a knife in my hand. And he smiled the smile of a man who has read every book in the library and found nothing in any of them that frightens him.

"The last keeper," he said. "Maren trained you poorly if she let you come here alone."

"She didn't let me do anything," I said. "That was rather the point of her."

"Girl." He walked closer, boots crunching frost that screamed faintly underfoot—the Hollow itself flinching away from him. "You don't understand what you're standing over. The covenant is a leash. Whoever holds it holds every gifted creature in two realms. Maren held it and did nothing with it. The old kings held it and did nothing with it. I am offering you something neither of them imagined: put the covenant in my keeping, and no hunt will ever touch you or yours. Registration becomes protection. Power becomes order."

"And everyone who won't register burns," I said. "I've read your posters, my lord. Your handwriting is very neat."

His smile thinned. Behind him, his hunters raised their crossbows—not at me.

At the gate.

* * *

Here is what I will remember until I die, however long that turns out to be:

Corvin stepping into the gateway with nothing but knives and a smuggler's grin, planting himself in the breach like he owned it, because in his mind he did—he'd run contraband through that pass for fifteen years and he'd be damned if he'd let crown men use it for free. "Gate's closed," he announced cheerfully, as six crossbows leveled at him. "Toll's gone up."

Dain beside him, bow drawn, choosing—for the second time in his life, and this time knowing exactly what it would cost—to stand between the crown and the gifted. "Last chance to walk away," he told the hunters quietly. He'd trained half of them. Two of them lowered their weapons. The rest did not.

And Wren—Wren, who should have been hiding behind them, who I had personally instructed to hide—stepping forward instead and singing those three impossible notes, and the gate-stone beginning to grind closed with the hunters still inside the threshold.

Cassel spun toward the noise, and in that half-second of divided attention, I cut my palm open on the rowan knife and pressed my bleeding hand to the nearest stone.

* * *

I have bled before. Everyone has. What I had never done was mean it.

The covenant woke like a beast under snow. The script on the stones filled with red light—not my light, older than me, patient as roots—and I felt it reach through me looking for the thing it was made to bind. Looking for a keeper's blood. Looking for a crown to serve.

Every keeper before me had given it a master. That was the flaw Maren had finally understood, dying in her cell: the covenant was never meant to bind the fae. It was meant to bind whoever held it. Give it a king and it makes the king absolute. Give it a spymaster and it makes him a god.

So I gave it nothing.

"No crown," I said, and the words came out of me in a language I'd never learned, rising up through my blood like water finding its level. "No throne. No keeper's house, no lineage, no name. Bind to the blood freely given and to nothing else. Let the magic belong to itself."

The covenant considered this. I felt it considering, vast and slow and ancient, turning my offer over the way a jeweler turns a stone.

And then Cassel understood what I was doing, and he screamed.

Not a command. Not a spell. Just a scream, raw and human, because he saw in one blinding instant that if the covenant answered me, power would exist in the world that no office could claim, no title could control, no amount of registration or iron or fire could ever own—and everything he had built, every hanging and every hunt and every secret he'd kept even from himself, would have been for nothing.

He threw his gift at me. His stolen, hoarded, hidden gift—all of it at once, a winter given shape, and it hit the covenant's rising light and simply—unraveled. Because that was the covenant's oldest function, the one even Maren hadn't known: it kept the balance. It kept magic from pooling in any one pair of hands. Cassel had spent thirty years damming a river, and I had just broken the dam.

The light went through him like dawn through fog. When I could see again, he was on his knees in the frost, and the cold around him was just cold, and his face was the face of a man waking from a long dream to find his hands empty.

The hunters at the gate stopped fighting. I don't know how else to describe it—they stopped, all at once, the way a storm stops, and Dain later told me they all felt the same thing: the leash going slack. Whatever Cassel had used to bind their loyalty, their oaths, their fear—it was gone. They were just men with crossbows, standing in a fairy realm, wondering how they'd gotten there.

The winter broke.

I don't have grander words than that. The frost on the white stones slid off like a shed skin, and underneath, impossibly, the grass was green. Somewhere in the frozen mist, the fae court made a sound—not cheering, nothing so warm—a long exhalation, like a forest after rain. A generation of slow death, ending.

And the cost arrived, as costs do, politely and on time.

I felt the exile settle over me before I understood it. The Hollow's beauty didn't dim—I simply stopped being able to see all of it. Colors went slightly flat. The music under the wind dropped below hearing. A door closed somewhere in me, gently, forever, and I knew that no gate, no song, no drop of fae blood would ever open it again. I had asked the covenant to belong to itself, and it had agreed, and the price of setting something free is that you can't go where it lives anymore.

I knelt in the wet grass with my cut hand and laughed until it hurt, because it had worked, and because it had cost exactly what I'd known it would, and because grief and triumph, it turns out, live in the same room and share a bed.

"Paloma." Wren crouched next to me, gate-frost melting in her hair. She looked at my face and then at the greening Hollow around us, and being nine, she asked the only question that mattered. "Can you still teach me? Here, I mean. Or does the magic not work on your side now?"

I flexed my bandaged hand. Felt, faint and steady and new, the pulse of the covenant running free beneath everything—under Thornfell, under the mountains, under the whole tired world—belonging to no one, which meant belonging to anyone willing to learn it honestly.

"Oh, it works," I said. "It works better than it ever has. Come on. We've got a mountain full of people to un-register."

Behind us, Corvin was already complaining about the walk home.
