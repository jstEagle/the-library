## 10. Hollow and Home

The veil let me go the way a mother lets go of a child's hand at a schoolhouse door—reluctantly, with a little push, as if to say *go on, then, and don't look back*, knowing full well I would.

I looked back. Of course I looked back. The Hollow shimmered behind me like heat off summer stone, all its terrible beauty folding itself away, and I felt the covenant settle into my blood like a stone dropped into a well—deep, cold, permanent. Mine now. All of it. Every gate, every whisper of fae wind through mountain pines, every debt owed between worlds: mine to carry until I died, and then, presumably, someone else's problem, though Maren had been frustratingly vague on the mechanics of succession.

*You'll figure it out,* she'd said, which is what she always said when she hadn't the faintest idea either. Six years of apprenticeship and that was her great pedagogical legacy: confidence in place of answers.

The Thornfell side of the gate was snowed under, but it was ordinary snow—the honest, dumb kind that fell from clouds instead of seeping up out of a dying realm's grief. I stood in the drifts for a long moment, breathing air that tasted of pine and woodsmoke and nothing else, and waited for the exile to hurt.

It did. But not the way I expected. It wasn't a door slamming. It was more like the ache in a hand after you've finally set down something you've carried too long—the blood rushing back in, painful precisely because it was relief.

I had saved the world, more or less. Both worlds. And both worlds had agreed, in their separate and characteristic ways, that I should never come back to one of them and never be hunted in the other. A fair trade, if you didn't look too closely. So naturally I looked too closely the whole way down the mountain.

* * *

Corvin's camp smelled like burnt porridge and wet wool, which is to say it smelled like home, which is to say I clearly needed to recalibrate my standards.

He spotted me coming through the trees and didn't run over or shout or do any of the things heroes do in ballads. He just nodded, the way you nod at someone who went out for firewood and came back, and said, "Porridge's burnt."

"I can smell it from here."

"Good. Saves telling you." He handed me a bowl anyway, and we ate standing up in the snow like a pair of crows, and that was the whole homecoming ceremony. Six years of running, a war between realms, a spymaster unmade—and welcomed back with burnt porridge. I wouldn't have traded it for a coronation.

Wren was less restrained. She hit me at knee height going approximately the speed of an avalanche, and I sat down in a snowdrift with a half-fae child wrapped around my middle, laughing in a way I hadn't laughed since before the hunt lists, since before Maren's ransacked cottage, maybe since before the fever-stricken stranger whose healing touch had started all of this.

"You're glowing," Wren announced, pulling back to squint at me. "Did you know you're glowing? It's very faint. Like a candle behind a curtain."

"That's the covenant," I said. "Try not to point it out to strangers."

"You're the strangest stranger there is." She considered this. "Maren says you'll teach us now. Properly. Not the shouting way."

"There was a shouting way?"

"Maren shouts when people nearly die. You shout when people nearly die *wrongly*." She said it with the solemn authority of a nine-year-old delivering a verdict, and Corvin snorted into his porridge, and I decided that some truths could stand unchallenged.

Dain came out of the treeline last, bow slung across his back out of habit rather than need—he hadn't loosed an arrow at anything living since he'd faked his own death, and I suspected he never would again. He looked at me the way he'd looked at me the day he'd chosen to track me down and warn me instead of capture me: like a man still deciding whether to trust his own eyes.

"The hunt lists are burned," he said. "All of them. Cassel's network came apart when his gift did. Half his informants were fae-bound; when the covenant flared, the bindings snapped. The other half were just frightened people, and frightened people scatter when the fear lifts." He paused. "There's no hunt anymore, Paloma. There hasn't been since the winter broke."

"I know."

"Do you?" He said it gently. "You've been running for six years. Sometimes the running outlasts the thing you ran from."

I wanted to argue. Instead I looked down at my hands—my healer's hands, my smuggler's hands, the hands that had signed a covenant in blood beneath a frozen sky—and admitted that the huntsman who laid down his bow might know a thing or two about laying things down.

* * *

Maren's cottage had been rebuilt by the time I reached it, which surprised me until I saw how.

She hadn't rebuilt it alone. There were perhaps thirty people in the clearing—mountain folk, hollow-cheeked and wary-eyed, the kind of people who'd spent a generation learning to hide whatever made them different. An old shepherd was showing three children how to read weather in the way smoke bent over a fire, except the smoke was bending because he asked it to. A woman with a miller's forearms was coaxing a garden up through the snow—not forcing it, just *suggesting*, the way you'd suggest to a stubborn mule that it really did want to come inside. And at the center of it all, wrapped in blankets on a bench outside her own front door, sat Maren, looking smaller than I'd ever seen her and entirely, infuriatingly content.

"You're late," she said.

"You were in a cell."

"A cell with excellent acoustics. I got a great deal of thinking done." She held out her arms, and I knelt and let her hold my face in her thin hands, and her eyes went over me the way they used to go over my knotwork—looking for flaws, finding them, deciding they'd hold anyway. "You look terrible."

"I carried a covenant through a frozen hell. What's your excuse?"

"Old age and dramatic circumstances." Her laugh turned into a cough, and I felt the frailty of her then, the way the Hollow's toll had taken payment from her too, years of it, and I had to look away for a moment at the grief of it—that she'd survived everything only to be spent, like a candle burned at both ends to light someone else's way.

But her hands were steady. "Sit," she said. "Watch. Then tell me what I'm doing wrong, because you will anyway."

So I sat, and I watched, and what I saw undid something in me that six years of hiding had built.

They weren't hiding anymore. None of them. The shepherd taught weather-sense openly; the miller's wife grew green things in white snow; a boy no older than Wren was learning to mend a cracked bowl with a touch and a whispered word. No registration papers. No crown sigils. No burning. Just magic, passed hand to hand like bread at a table, unregistered and unashamed, rooted in the covenant I carried and belonging to no one—no crown, no court, no keeper's bloodline. Belonging to everyone who chose it.

"This is what you wanted," I said quietly. "This is why you kept the covenant alive all those years."

"This is what the covenant *wanted*," Maren corrected. "I just kept the paperwork." She patted the bench beside her. "The old way bound magic to crowns and courts, and look where that got us—a spymaster hoarding power, a court selling its own children, a generation of gifted folk burned or buried. Your way binds it to choice. Anyone who wants to learn, learns. Anyone who wants to walk away, walks away. No lists. No hunts. No keepers chained to it either." She glanced at me sideways, sly as ever. "Well. One keeper. But she's free to be terrible at it."

"I'm already terrible at it. I don't even know how the gates work."

"You'll learn. I did. Badly, at first." She leaned her head against my shoulder, suddenly tired. "Stay, Paloma. Not because you owe anyone. Because you want to. That's the whole lesson. That's the only lesson. Everything else is commentary."

* * *

I planted the rowan three days later, on the south side of the cottage where the morning sun would find it first.

Maren supervised from her blanket with the air of a general reviewing troops. "Two feet further left. The roots want room to argue with the stream."

"Rowans don't argue with streams."

"These do. I knew its grandmother." She coughed, waved off my alarm. "Plant it, girl. Some trees remember things, and I'd like this one to remember being planted properly."

So I dug, and I planted, and when I pressed the soil down around the sapling's roots I did it the old way—one drop of blood, one spoken word, the smallest possible working, barely magic at all. Just a wish with structure. *Grow. Shelter. Remember.*

The rowan shivered, though there was no wind, and put out one absurdly optimistic bud in the middle of winter, and Maren laughed until she cried, and I decided that counted as success.

That night there was a gathering in the cottage—too many people for the space, everyone talking over everyone, Corvin arguing with the miller's wife about whether charm-work counted as smuggling ("Everything counts as smuggling if you move it across a line," he insisted, "that's the definition"), Dain quietly teaching two teenagers how to fall without hurting themselves, Wren asleep against my side with her fae-bright dreams flickering behind her eyelids like fish under ice. Maren sat in the best chair like a queen holding court, frail as paper and twice as sharp.

And I stood in the middle of it, in the noise and the warmth and the smell of burnt porridge someone had made again, deliberately, as tradition, and realized that the dread was gone.

Six years I had lived by one rule: *be invisible, be unwitnessed, be no one.* And here I stood, witnessed by thirty-odd people who knew exactly what I was—keeper, hedge-witch, exiled from the Hollow, terrible at gate-craft—and not one of them wanted me registered, hunted, or burned. They just wanted me to pass the bread.

I stopped running somewhere between the second cup of nettle wine and Wren waking up to demand a story. I couldn't tell you the exact moment. Things like that don't happen dramatically; they happen the way frost melts, so gradually you only notice afterward, when the ground has gone soft enough to plant in.

Later, past midnight, when the others had gone and Maren slept and the cottage breathed around me in the dark, I stepped outside one last time. The rowan stood black against the stars, its single bud closed tight, waiting for spring with a patience I was only beginning to learn.

I pressed my palm to its bark. Beneath it, faint as a candle behind a curtain, the covenant hummed in my blood—and far away, beyond the veil I could no longer cross, something hummed back. Not a summons. Not a warning. Just acknowledgment, the way one lighthouse acknowledges another across dark water.

*Still there,* it said.

*Still here,* I answered.

And for the first time in six years, that was enough.
