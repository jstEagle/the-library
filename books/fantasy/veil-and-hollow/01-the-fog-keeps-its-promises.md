## 1. The Fog Keeps Its Promises

The fog came up the valley every morning like a tide that had forgotten the sea, and Noor Zhou had learned, over nine years, to read it the way other people read weather. When it lay thick and low over the rooftops of Millbrook, pressing white against the windows, the day would be a day for staying in—mending, mulling cider, keeping small. When it lifted into ragged ribbons by midmorning, the light came down strange and silvered, and you could see all the way to the shrine on the hill, which was a thing most people in the Hollow preferred not to do.

Today the fog held low and stubborn. Good. A day for roofs.

Noor stood on the slates of Widow Ashka's cottage with her sleeves rolled to the elbow, her palms flat against shingles gone soft with moss, and let the work quiet her the way it always did. There was a rhythm to repair: pry the rotten shingle, seat the new one, nail it home. Four beats, over and over, until her hands forgot everything except the task. Her hands were the problem, most days. They wanted to remember something else.

She flexed her fingers against the slate. There—a flicker, faint as heat-shimmer, running under her skin like lightning trapped in amber. The tremor. It had been with her since the night she'd arrived at the valley's edge nine years ago, feverish and empty-headed, found half-drowned in the shallows of the border stream with no name she could recall and no memory worth the word. Maren had pulled her from the water, wrapped her in wool, fed her willow-bark tea, and asked no questions, because in the Hollow nobody asked questions. That was the first law of the place, unwritten but iron: *you came here to be no one, so be no one well.*

The tremor lived in her hands anyway. On dry nights it crackled faintly when she touched iron. In storms—and the Hollow had few, the fog kept the sky pressed flat—it sang along, a low hum in her knuckles, an itch behind her sternum like a word she couldn't quite pronounce. She had spent nine years learning to hold it still. Nine years of breathing exercises Maren taught her without ever naming what they were for. Nine years of never touching a fence post during rain.

Below her, Widow Ashka's voice floated up through the fog. "You've missed a spot, girl."

"I never miss spots," Noor called back.

"You missed the one over the chimney. I can hear the drip already. Drip, drip, drip. Like the house is crying about something." Ashka appeared at the gate below, a small round woman wrapped in three shawls despite the mild air, squinting up into the mist. "Houses cry when they're not loved properly. That's why mine leaks. Nobody's loved it since my Havel died."

Noor smiled despite herself and moved toward the chimney. This was the whole of her life now, and some mornings—the good ones—it felt like enough. Shingles and soup and Ashka's complaints and Maren's garden and Tam chasing chickens through the lane. Smallness, deliberately chosen. She had learned to call it peace.

Most mornings.

Some mornings she woke with her heart hammering and the taste of ozone in her mouth and no idea why, and those mornings the smallness felt less like peace and more like a coat two sizes too small, buttoned to the throat.

She pried loose the shingle above the chimney, worked the new one into place, and drove the nails home—four beats, steady—and told herself, as she told herself every day, that this was enough. That hiding was a kind of living. That the fog kept its promises: it hid you, and it asked nothing back except that you stay hidden.

The fog, as it turned out, was about to break its word.

* * *

She finished Ashka's roof by late morning and walked down through the village with her tool bag over her shoulder, nodding to the faces she knew. Old Piet mending his nets even though the Hollow's streams held nothing worth netting—he fished because his father had fished, and that was reason enough here. The twins Dela and Sorrel arguing over a loom. Little Tam sitting on Maren's step, as he always was, drawing in the dirt with a stick.

Tam drew cities. He didn't know why; he said they just came out of his hand. Towers with brass doors, streets laid out like the veins of a leaf, a river running through the middle of it all. Maren said every child drew strange things, and mostly Noor believed her.

"Miss Noor!" He looked up, dirt-smudged, gap-toothed, seven years old and certain the world was legible if you only studied it hard enough. "Did you fix the widow's roof?"

"Every last shingle."

"She'll still say it drips."

"She'll still say it drips," Noor agreed gravely, and ruffled his hair, and felt the familiar ache underneath the fondness—the ache of loving a child while knowing your life was built on a floor that might not be a floor. She had no past to give him. She had no stories of before. If he ever asked where she came from, really came from, she would have to say *I don't know*, and she had been dreading that question for nine years.

He didn't ask. Children in the Hollow learned early not to ask.

Maren was in the garden, tying back tomato vines, her gray hair pinned up with what appeared to be a pencil and half a spoon. She glanced up as Noor passed the gate, and her eyes did their usual quick inventory—hands, face, the set of her shoulders. Checking the tremor without asking about it. She'd been doing it for nine years.

"Storm coming," Maren said.

"There's no storm coming. Fog's flat as a griddle."

"Fog lies." Maren went back to her vines. "Your hands been quiet?"

"Quiet as church mice."

"Mm." Which meant: *I don't believe you, and I'm not going to say so.* Maren communicated almost entirely in that register. Noor loved her helplessly for it.

She ate lunch standing in the kitchen—bread, sharp cheese, an apple from the cellar—and was halfway through the apple when the first wrongness arrived. Not a sound. A silence. The kind of hush that falls when birds decide, all at once and without discussion, to stop singing.

Noor lowered the apple. Her hands began to hum.

Not the usual flicker. Something deeper, a chord struck low in her bones, and the hair on her arms lifted, and outside the window the fog—she could see it clearly from where she stood—began to move wrong. It drifted toward the eastern edge of the valley, all of it together, drawn like smoke toward a draft under a door. Toward the border. Toward the stream where she had been found.

Toward the shrine hill.

She set down the apple. She picked up her tool bag instead of anything sensible, because her hands wanted a weight in them, and she walked out into the lane, and then she ran.

* * *

The shrine sat on the hill above the eastern border, a small stone circle older than anyone could account for, and Noor had avoided it for nine years with the discipline of the truly frightened. The villagers left offerings there—bread, buttons, a child's carved horse—but Noor had never once climbed the path. Whatever had happened to her had happened near that place. Her body remembered even if her mind refused to.

Now she crested the hill with her lungs burning and stopped dead.

A man lay at the edge of the stone circle, crumpled like a dropped marionette, and the fog around him was behaving impossibly—spiraling inward, slow and deliberate, as though the valley itself were leaning in to look.

He was an outsider. That was clear at a glance, and the clarity of it made her stomach drop. His clothes were road-worn but fine once—good leather boots, a traveler's coat cut in a style Noor had never seen except in her own inexplicable dreams. And he was bleeding. But not blood.

Light. Thin threads of pale gold seeped from wounds across his chest and side, glowing against the dark stone, and where each drop fell the stone beneath it *faded*, going gray and translucent, like cloth worn thin.

"No," Noor heard herself say. "No, no—" She dropped to her knees beside him, and her hands, traitorous things, flared with the tremor so hard that sparks crawled visibly between her fingers, and the man's eyes opened.

They were gray eyes, sharp even now, and they fixed on her face with an expression that cracked something open in her chest: recognition. Not of her—of *what she was*. He saw the sparks. Anyone else in the Hollow would have seen a trick of the fog. He saw it and he wasn't afraid, and Noor realized in one lurching moment that she had never once in nine years been seen, truly seen, by anyone.

"You're inside," he rasped. His voice was ruined, wind-scoured. "Good. Inside is—inside matters."

"You're hurt. I need to—I have to get help, there's an herbalist, she can—"

"No time." His hand shot out and caught her wrist with shocking strength, and where his fingers touched her skin the tremor in her veins went silent—not suppressed, *answered*, like a struck bell meeting its twin. His breath hitched. "Storm-caller. Here. Of all the—" A wet, bright laugh. "The map chose better than I dared hope."

"What map? What are you talking about?"

He was fumbling at his coat, at an inner pocket, and Noor helped him because there was nothing else to do, and together they drew out a roll of heavy paper bound in waxed cord. Even unopened, it hummed. She could feel it against her palms, a vibration twin to her own, and her vision swam with sudden vertigo, the hill tilting, the fog spiraling closer.

"The Hollow," the man whispered. "Do you know what this place is? Do any of you know?"

"It's a valley. Refuge. People come here to disappear—"

"It's a country." His grip tightened. "A country that was erased. Cut out of the world like a—like a bad tooth. And the cutting is failing, do you understand me? The wound is opening. Everything inside it is starting to come *unmade*—" He coughed, and the cough was terrible, and more light spilled between his lips than blood. "There's a Door. At the heart of the old capital. The map knows the way. The map always knows—"

"Stop. Stop talking, save your breath—"

"Listen to me." He dragged her close, close enough that she could see the fog reflected in his fading eyes, and his voice dropped to something urgent and intimate, a secret handed over like a flame. "Nine years ago, someone called a storm over this valley. A veil-storm. It saved everyone inside it. Do you know who calls storms, girl?"

Her heart stopped. Started again, wrong. "I don't remember anything," she said. "I don't know who I was."

"I know," he said gently, terribly. "I counted on it."

And then his hand fell away from her wrist, and he sagged back against the stones, and the light beneath his skin dimmed like a lamp running out of oil. Noor pressed her hands to the worst of his wounds and the tremor poured out of her unbidden—she couldn't have stopped it if she'd tried—and for one blazing second the fading slowed, the gold threads steadying, and his eyes widened with something like wonder.

Then it guttered. She hadn't the strength. She had never had the strength; she had spent nine years teaching herself to be small.

"The map," he breathed. "Take it. Don't let them burn it. They'll want to burn it—they always burn first and grieve after. Follow the landmarks. The shrine—you're kneeling on the first one—"

Noor looked down. Beneath her knees, half-hidden by moss, a mark was carved into the old stone: a spiral, worn nearly smooth, exactly the shape of the flicker in her hands.

"The Door," the man whispered. His voice was almost gone now, a thread of sound. "Before the last thread frays. Promise me. Promise—"

"I promise," Noor said, because there was nothing else in the world to say.

He smiled then—a real smile, brief and crooked, the smile of a man setting down a weight carried too far. "Ilya," he said. "My name was Ilya. Someone should know it, at the end. Someone should—"

The light went out of him all at once, like a snuffed candle, and the fog closed over the hilltop, soft and total, and the Hollow kept its promise and hid everything again: the body, the blood of light, the spiral under the moss, and Noor Zhou kneeling in the ruins of the only life she had understood, holding a humming map against her chest with hands that would not, would not, stop trembling.

Down in the village, far below, the evening bells rang. They sounded, for the first time in nine years, slightly out of tune.
