## 3. The Knight Who Fell Off Her Own Monument

The stranger came up the valley road two days after they buried Ilya, and Noor knew she was trouble before she knew her name.

Trouble had a shape in the Hollow. It was the way the fog thinned where it shouldn't, pulling back from a figure like a crowd making room. It was the dogs going silent all at once, every yard along the lane, as if something had pressed a finger to their throats. And it was this: when the woman walked past Old Bram's fence, the warding knot he'd tied there—twine and iron and nine years of quiet superstition—unraveled with a sound like a sigh.

Noor was on a ladder at the Fenwick house, patching slate, when she saw her come around the bend by the millrace. She nearly dropped her hammer.

The woman wore armor, or what remained of armor—a cuirass of dark steel gone dull at the edges, a half-cloak the color of wet ash, riding leathers beneath that had been good once and were now mostly dust. A sword hung at her hip in a scabbard scarred white with old burns. Her hair was cropped short and unevenly, the cut of someone who'd done it herself with a knife and no mirror, and there was a bruise flowering along her jaw like spilled ink. She walked like a soldier, but a particular kind of soldier—the kind who had stopped believing in the war and kept marching anyway out of spite.

And above the fog line, impossibly, impossibly far away, Noor felt something *stir*.

It happened before she could stop it. The tremor that lived in her hands—the one she'd spent nine years teaching to sit still, to be nothing more than static in her fingertips on cold mornings—rose toward the surface of her skin like a fish rising toward light. The air over the valley road shivered. Somewhere behind the mist, thunder cleared its throat.

The woman on the road looked up directly at her. Not at the roof. At *her*, forty feet up a ladder, small against the grey sky.

Noor's magic reached for her—and broke.

Not slid off. Not fizzled harmlessly into the fog, the way it did when she lost her temper at a stubborn gate or a frozen pump. It *broke*, the way a wave breaks against a sea wall, and for one vertiginous second Noor felt the shape of whatever stood down there: a wall of silence, deliberate and total, wrapped around the stranger like a second skin. Magic couldn't touch her because magic wasn't allowed to know she existed.

Noor had lived in the Hollow for nine years. In nine years, nothing here had ever refused her.

She climbed down the ladder too fast and made herself walk, not run, toward the well in the center of the village green, because that was where everyone would gather, and if a storm was coming she intended to be standing in the middle of it looking bored.

* * *

By the time the stranger reached the green, half of Hollow's End had assembled in the loose, wary crescent that villagers form when they want to look casual and fail completely. Maren stood at the front with Tam's collar gripped in one fist, though Tam had squirmed halfway out of it to see better. Old Bram held his pitchfork the way he held everything, as if it might apologize for him.

"You're not from here," Bram said, which was the sort of observation the Hollow produced instead of welcome.

"No," the woman agreed. Her voice was low and roughened, a voice used to being obeyed in rooms larger than this one. "I'm looking for a man. Ilya Petrov. Cartographer. He'd have been bleeding."

A murmur went through the crescent. Noor watched Maren's face do something complicated and settle on blankness.

"Don't know him," said Doss the cooper.

"He came through two days ago," said Tam.

Maren's grip tightened. The stranger's eyes—grey, flat, patient—moved to the boy and stayed there a moment too long.

"Did he," she said.

"He was hurt," Tam went on, with the terrible honesty of twelve-year-olds everywhere. "He died. We buried him by the alder tree. His blood glowed."

"*Tam.*"

"What? It did."

The stranger crossed the green. She moved without hurry, but each step ate distance the way a blade eats whetstone, and the villagers parted around her the way water parts around a keel. She stopped an arm's length from Noor—which was strange, because Noor had said nothing, done nothing, given no sign, and yet the woman looked at her as if Noor were the only person on the green worth the trouble of looking at.

"You're the one who buried him," the stranger said.

It wasn't a question, and Noor didn't know how to answer it, so she answered the wrong question instead, which was often the safest thing. "We bury everyone by the alder tree. It's not a distinction."

"His wounds bled light." The woman's gaze didn't waver. "That's null-blade work. Chancery work. Someone cut him down with a weapon meant to erase things, and he crawled through a veil that hasn't let anything through in nine years to die in your valley. That's not a distinction either. That's a message." She paused. "You're the one who found him. You've got his ink on your hands."

Noor looked down. There was indeed a stain on her right hand, faint and silverish, that three washings hadn't touched. Ilya's blood, or whatever passed for blood in a man whose veins ran with cartography and dying light.

"I'm Sera Vane," the woman said. "Formerly of the Order of the Sundered Oath. Currently of nowhere in particular. And you're going to tell me what he gave you before he died."

"And if I don't?"

"Then I'll stand in your village until you do." Sera Vane glanced around at the fog-bound rooftops, the shuttered windows, the faces peering from doorways with the particular suspicion of people who had chosen obscurity and defended it fiercely. "I've slept in worse places. I once spent a winter in a drainage culvert. Your fog is practically hospitable."

Behind her, someone whispered, "Is that—" and someone else whispered back, "It can't be," and Noor turned to find Old Bram staring at the stranger's cuirass with his mouth open.

"It is," Bram breathed. "Gods below. That's the Vane crest. That's—" He pointed with the pitchfork, which undermined the gravity of the gesture considerably. "There's a statue of her in Corvale. Twenty feet tall. She *died* at Redford Bridge. There's a song."

Something flickered across Sera Vane's face—something that was almost a flinch, quickly murdered. "The statue's wrong," she said. "The song's worse. And I'd thank you not to sing it."

* * *

They put her in the meetinghouse, because even unwelcome guests got four walls in the Hollow—that was the kind of place it was—and then the council argued about what to do with her for three hours while Noor sat at home staring at the floorboard under her bed where the map was hidden.

She could still see Ilya's hands pressing it toward her, those last minutes by the alder tree, when the others had gone to fetch Maren and it was just the two of them and the light leaking out of him like the last of a candle. *The Hollow is a country mid-unmaking,* he'd said. *Do you understand? Not hidden. Unmade. It was a country once, whole, with a capital and a river and bells, and they erased it, and the erasure is coming apart, and when it finishes coming apart everything inside goes with it.* His fingers had left silver on the parchment. *The Door. Find the Door. Before the thread runs out.*

She hadn't told the council about the Door. She'd told them about the map, because Tam had seen it and secrets were heavier than children could carry, and the council had voted—six to two—to burn it. *Nothing comes in,* Doss had said, striking the table. *Nothing goes out. That's the covenant. That's why we're alive.* And Maren had sat with her hands folded and said nothing at all, which from Maren was louder than voting.

The vote was tomorrow night. Tonight the map lay under her floorboards like a heart she'd smuggled out of a dying man's chest.

A knock at her door, at midnight, was therefore not entirely a surprise.

Sera Vane stood on her step with her helmet under her arm—she'd apparently recovered it from wherever she'd stashed it—and rain in her hair, though Noor hadn't heard rain start. Behind her, the fog had pulled back from the doorway again, that same courteous, frightened retreat.

"You knew I'd come," Sera said.

"You're the first outsider in nine years. Where else would you go?"

"A fair point." She didn't ask to come in; she simply waited, which was somehow ruder, until Noor stepped aside. Inside, she took stock of the single room with a soldier's economy—the herb bundles drying from the beam, the mended shutters, the iron pot, the bed, the floorboard near the bed that sat a half-finger proud of its neighbors. Her eyes rested on it exactly long enough.

"You're not sleeping," Noor said. "You're casing my house."

"I'm assessing threats. It's a habit. You're the largest one in this valley and I'd very much like to know why." Sera set her helmet on the table. Up close, without the armor's authority, she looked tired in a way that went all the way through. "When I walked up your road today, the air *moved*. Something reached for me. Do you know what that was?"

"No."

"Liar," Sera said, without heat. "But a committed one. Fine. Here's what I know, and you can check it against whatever you're not telling me. Nine years ago, a valley that does not appear on any map acquired a veil around it. Fog on the outside, quiet on the inside. Nothing gets in. I've spent two years trying, because Ilya Petrov sent me a letter that said *they've found it again, they're going to finish it, come to the Hollow,* and then nothing, and then I found his trail and the trail of the men hunting him, and the trail ended at your fog like a wall." She leaned forward. "Walls don't happen by accident, Noor Zhou. Veils don't happen by accident. Somebody built yours, and somebody paid for it, and I think—I *think*—that somebody is sitting in this room pretending to mend roofs."

The tremor rose in Noor's hands before she could stop it. The lamp flame stretched tall and blue. Rain lashed the window, sudden and furious, though the sky had been merely damp a moment ago.

Sera Vane looked at the storm gathering inside a one-room house and did not reach for her sword. That was the thing Noor remembered afterward—not the accusation, but the stillness. Most people, faced with weather indoors, would have done *something*. Sera just watched, grey-eyed, with the expression of a woman confirming a suspicion she'd been carrying like a stone.

"There it is," she said softly. "Storm-calling. I've only ever read about it. They purged the last of your kind out of the Chancery provinces before I was knighted. 'Weather-wrights.' As if a name makes it tidy." She sat back. "You called the veil. Or part of it. Or you're tied to it somehow—I don't care which, honestly, I care about the Door."

The word landed in the room like a dropped glass.

"I never said—"

"You didn't have to. Ilya drew maps the way other men pray. If he came here dying, he came here because of the Door, and if he gave anyone anything before the end, it was the way to find it." Sera's voice dropped, and for the first time the roughness in it cracked and Noor heard what was underneath: two years of grief, worn smooth by handling. "They killed him with null-blades, Noor. Do you know what the Chancery uses those for? Erasure. Not killing—killing leaves a body, a grave, mourners. Erasure leaves *nothing*. Ilya didn't die because he was in the way. He died because he was the last man alive who could draw the road back to a country they deleted from the world, and he was about to draw it."

Outside, the storm found its rhythm and settled, sullen. Noor became aware that she was gripping the edge of the table hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that Sera was watching her hands, cataloguing them, the way she'd catalogued the floorboard.

"The council votes tomorrow to burn the map," Noor said.

"I know. I listened through the wall of the meetinghouse for three hours. Your cooper snores between arguments." Sera shrugged. "So here is my offer, and I'll make it once. Give me the map. I'll take it to the Door myself—you can stay here with your fog and your covenants and your excellent alder tree."

"And why would you want that? What's in it for you?"

For a moment Sera said nothing, and the lamplight moved on her face, and Noor watched her decide—watched the exact moment the truth got weighed and found too heavy, and something else was picked up in its place.

"I made an oath once," Sera said. "To hold the line against the Chancery's arithmetic. I broke it, or it broke me, depending on the day. Ilya was the last person who believed I hadn't broken it all the way through." She met Noor's eyes. "Let me finish his errand and maybe the math balances. That's all. That's the whole of it."

Every word was true. Noor could hear that. And every word was also a wall, smooth and load-bearing, with something bricked up behind it—and Noor, who had spent nine years building walls of her own, knew the craftsmanship on sight.

"If I give you the map," Noor said slowly, "you'll die on the road. Whatever killed Ilya will kill you, and it'll have the map, and then it's finished. The Hollow, the Door, all of it." She heard herself say the next part before she'd decided to say it, which was how the truest things usually arrived. "You need someone the fog doesn't stop. You need someone Ilya trusted enough to die reaching. And whether you like it or not, knight, that's me."

Sera studied her for a long moment. Rain ticked against the shutters. Somewhere across the village, a dog finally remembered how to bark.

"That's not the bargain I offered," Sera said.

"No. It's better. Take it."

"You understand I'll be watching you the entire way. Every mile. You lie to me once—"

"I've had nine years of practice lying," Noor said. "You'll never catch me at it. But I won't lie to you about the Door. That much I'll swear."

"On what?"

Noor looked down at her own hands, at the faint silver stain of a dead man's ink, at the tremor underneath the skin that had built a wall around a country and then forgotten why. "On the storm," she said. "It's the only thing I own."

Sera Vane held out her hand—bare, scarred across the palm, a knight's hand despite everything—and Noor shook it, and the moment their skin touched the silence around Sera flexed, and Noor felt her own magic recoil like a hand pulled back from a stove.

"One condition," Sera added, retrieving her helmet. "We leave before the vote. Tomorrow's council meeting becomes a search party, and I've run from searches before. Dawn. Bring the map and bring nothing else you'd grieve losing."

She let herself out into the rain. Noor stood a long time in the empty room, listening to the storm slowly remember it was only weather, and thought about the word *we*, which Sera had used twice now without appearing to notice.

Under the floorboard, the map waited, its silver roads faintly luminous in the dark, and Noor noticed—for the first time, with a coldness that started in her stomach and climbed—that the first landmark marked along the route to the Door was drawn in Ilya's careful hand beside a little alder tree.

The shrine. The place where she had been found, feverish and nameless, nine years ago.

The road to the Door began where her life in the Hollow had begun, and she had a feeling that was not a coincidence at all.
