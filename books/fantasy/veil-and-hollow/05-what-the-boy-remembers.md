## 5. What the Boy Remembers

Tam knew the city before he knew the word for it.

He was seven when he first tried to describe it to Gran Maren, standing on a kitchen stool so he could reach her elbows while she crushed feverfew in the mortar, and the words had come out of him in a rush like water from a tipped bucket: *There's a river with bells in it, Gran. Not bells hanging—bells floating, all different sizes, and when the wind comes off the water they ring in order, lowest first, and everyone stops walking when they hear it because that's how you know what time it is. There's no clocks. The bells are the clocks.*

Maren's hands had gone still around the pestle. Just for a moment. Then she'd said, *That's a fine dream, love,* and given him a honeyed spoonful of crushed herb for his cough, and Tam had learned something important without knowing he'd learned it: some dreams made grown-ups go quiet, and quiet meant *stop talking.*

So he'd stopped. Mostly.

But the city didn't stop coming. It came every night, patient as rain, and by now — eleven years old, nearly twelve, tall enough to reach the top shelf where Gran kept the willow bark — Tam knew it better than he knew the Hollow itself. He knew the way the brass doors along the Canal of Small Mercies were polished by centuries of palms, each one dented differently, so that if you closed your eyes and ran your fingers down them you could tell which street you stood on. He knew the smell of the tanneries at dawn and the way the lamplighters rowed their little boats down the flooded avenues at dusk, lighting wicks that burned underwater somehow, green-gold and steady. He knew the name of the capital — *Vessengard* — though he had never once heard anyone say it aloud, and when he'd whispered it to himself under the blankets it had felt less like a word than like a key turning in a lock he couldn't see.

And he knew the map.

He wasn't supposed to know the map. It lived in Noor's cottage now, wrapped in oilcloth inside the bread box, which was the cleverest hiding place in the village because nobody robbed a baker's widow's bread box twice. But Tam delivered eggs to Noor every third morning, and Noor was the worst hider in the history of hiding things — she always put things exactly where she herself would look for them, and Tam had simply watched where her eyes went. So one morning while she was out mending the Hendersons' roof, he'd lifted the oilcloth and unrolled Ilya's dying map across her table, and his whole body had gone cold and hot at once, the way it did when thunder cracked too close.

There was Vessengard. Drawn in ink that shimmered faintly, like fish scales, like the skin of a drowned thing: the canal streets, the brass doors, the little marks for the lamplighters' boats. And there, running through the heart of it in a long silver curve, the river.

The mapmaker had labeled it in a small, careful hand: *the River of Bells.*

Tam had stood in Noor's kitchen with his heart hammering and understood, with the terrible clarity children are sometimes granted, two things at once. First: he was not making it up. Second: everyone else was.

* * *

He thought about it for four days. Four days of carrying eggs and stacking wood and sitting through supper while Old Piet told the story about the bear and the beehive for the eleventh time, and everyone laughed at the same places, and Tam smiled at the same places, and underneath the smiling he was doing arithmetic of a different kind. If the city was real, then it had been real once. If it had been real once, then someone had taken it away. And if someone had taken it away — why could he remember? Why him, alone, out of everyone?

Unless he wasn't alone.

On the fifth day he climbed the path behind the shrine — not to the shrine itself, never quite to the shrine, because even Tam felt the wrongness up there, a hush like held breath — but to the flat grey rock below it where Noor sat sometimes when she thought no one was watching. She was there now, knees drawn up, staring into the fog that filled the valley floor like milk poured into a bowl. From up here the Hollow looked small. Cozy, almost. A handful of rooftops, thread-smoke rising from chimneys, the fields stitched in green and gold. You could pretend it was the whole world.

Tam sat down beside her, leaving a careful arm's length of rock between them. Noor didn't startle; she never startled. She just glanced sideways at him with those storm-grey eyes and said, "You're supposed to be splitting kindling."

"I split it."

"All of it?"

"Most of it. The rest is Gran's problem." He pulled his knees up to match hers. For a while neither of them spoke. Below, the fog moved in slow spirals, and somewhere in it a cart creaked along the border road, invisible.

"Noor," he said finally. "Do you dream?"

She gave him a look. "Everyone dreams."

"Do you dream about a city?"

Something happened in her face then — very fast, very small, like a fish flashing under ice. "Why do you ask?"

"Because I do." He made himself keep going, made himself say the words he'd been holding behind his teeth for five days and, honestly, for years. "Every night. Brass doors. A river full of bells. Lamplighters in boats. There's a bridge called the Weeping Span because the stone sweats water even in summer, and there's a woman who sells roasted chestnuts outside the chandlery and she has six fingers on her left hand and she gives me the burnt ones free because she says the burnt ones are the honest ones." His voice was going faster now, tumbling. "I know the names of the streets. Marrow Lane. The Canal of Small Mercies. There's a fountain shaped like a whale and the water comes out of the spout and it's always slightly warm and nobody knows why. I've never been there, Noor. I've never been anywhere. But I remember it."

Noor had turned fully toward him. The fog-light made her face pale and unreadable, but her hands — her hands had that flicker again, the tremor she thought nobody saw, tiny sparks crawling between her knuckles like static before a summer storm.

"Say the name," she said quietly. "The city's name."

"Vessengard." And this time saying it out loud, in daylight, to another person, it didn't feel like a secret breaking. It felt like a bell ringing. "It's called Vessengard."

Noor closed her eyes. When she opened them, they were wet, which frightened Tam more than anything else she might have done, because Noor did not cry, Noor was the person who fixed roofs in the rain and carried Ilya's body herself and never once let her face do anything she hadn't chosen.

"Tam," she said. "Show me. On the map. Everything you can remember."

* * *

They spread the map on the flat rock, weighting the corners with stones, and Tam knelt over it and began, hesitantly at first and then with gathering speed, to point.

"The whale fountain — here, see, this mark, that's it, that's the square with the chestnut seller. The Weeping Span crosses here. The chandlery is—" he stopped, frowning, tracing a street with his finger. "This is wrong. This street doesn't bend here. It bends after the second bridge. Whoever drew this drew it from memory too, and they got it wrong, or—" He looked up. "Or the city changed after they left."

"Or the city is changing now," Noor said softly. "Fraying. Like everything else in here."

"What do you mean, in here?"

She didn't answer that. Instead she asked, "When did the dreams start?"

"Always. Gran says I talked about the bells before I could talk properly. She says my first word was *ring.*" He picked at a corner of the map where the ink had begun to flake. "But it got worse lately. Since the stranger came. Now I don't just see it at night. I see it when I'm awake. Yesterday I was carrying water past the well and for a second the well was a different well — rounder, older, with a brass rim — and there were buildings around it that aren't there, and people, Noor, people walking through our village like it was the city, and then it was gone." He swallowed. "And last night I dreamed the bells stopped. All of them. The whole river went silent. And in the dream everybody started crying, even me, even though I didn't understand why, and I woke up and my face was wet and I haven't been able to stop thinking about it."

Noor was silent for a long moment. The fog below them rolled and folded.

"You're the only one," she said at last. It wasn't a question.

"I asked Old Piet once if he'd ever heard of a place called Vessengard. He laughed and said it sounded like something out of a storybook. I asked Gran. She went quiet like she does, and changed the subject, and later I found her sitting alone looking at nothing." He hugged his knees tighter. "Nobody remembers. That's the thing, Noor. It's not that they forgot. It's like there's a hole where remembering should be, and they walk right past it every day and never fall in. But I fell in. I fell in when I was a baby and I've been living down there ever since, in a city that isn't anywhere, and I'm the only one who mourns it because I'm the only one who knows it's dead." His voice cracked on the last word and he hated it, hated being eleven, hated the way his eyes stung. "How do you mourn a whole country by yourself?"

Noor reached over and put her hand on the back of his neck, the way Gran did, firm and warm. Her fingers were trembling. He pretended not to notice.

"You don't have to anymore," she said.

* * *

That night Tam did not sleep. He lay in his loft bed under the eaves listening to Gran move below — the clink of the kettle, the creak of her chair, the small domestic music of a woman who had raised him on goat's milk and stubbornness — and he rehearsed what he would say. Because he had decided. Somewhere on the flat grey rock below the shrine, watching Noor's eyes go wet at the name of a city, he had decided.

At dawn he came down the ladder and found her already at the hearth, and he said it before his courage could evaporate:

"I'm going with them."

Gran's hands paused over the fire irons. She didn't turn around. "Going with who, love?"

"Noor. And the knight. To find the Door." He had heard Sera and Noor arguing in the lane two days ago, low and fierce, and he had excellent hearing and no shame whatsoever. "To Vessengard. To whatever's left of it."

Now she turned. Gran Maren had a face like winter bark, lined and weathered and impossible to read unless you'd spent your whole life learning it, and Tam had, and so he saw what crossed it: not surprise. Grief, arriving early, getting comfortable, unpacking its bags.

"Tam—"

"You're going to say I'm a child."

"You are a child."

"I'm the only one who *remembers,* Gran." He heard his own voice rise and didn't care. "Don't you understand? Everyone else looks at that map and sees drawings. I look at it and I see home. I can name the streets. I can tell them where the Door is — maybe — because I've walked there in my sleep a thousand times, I've stood in front of it, I know the sound it makes, it hums, Gran, it hums like the bells but lower, like the bells' grandmother—" His throat closed. He pushed through it. "You taught me that you don't leave family behind. Well, Vessengard is family too. It's my family. It's the only part of me nobody else in this whole valley has, and it's dying — I can feel it dying, the dreams are going grey at the edges, the chestnut seller's stall was empty last night, the lamplighters' boats were overturned — and if I don't go, if I stay here and split kindling until it's gone, then I'll be the last one who ever knew it existed and I'll have done *nothing.*"

The silence after that was enormous. The kettle ticked as it cooled. Outside, the fog pressed against the windows like something listening.

Gran looked at him for a long, long time. And then she did something he had never seen her do in his entire life: she sat down heavily in her chair, all at once, like a puppet with cut strings, and covered her mouth with her hand.

"Gran?"

"I knew a man from Vessengard," she said, through her fingers. Her voice sounded strange, rusted, like a gate opening after years shut. "Before. Before the Hollow, before your mother, before any of it. He had a laugh like a cart going downhill and he smelled of brass polish and he used to say—" She stopped. Started again. "I used to say I'd take him to meet my family someday. And then one day there was no him. Not dead — there was no *him.* No grave, no letter, no memory in anyone else's head. Just an ache in me shaped exactly like a man, and no name to put on it." She lowered her hand. Her eyes were bright and terrible. "I spent forty years thinking I'd gone mad, Tam. Forty years. And then you were born, and before you could walk you were humming a tune I'd only ever heard in that ache, and I thought — either the madness is inherited, or my boy is carrying something the rest of us lost."

Tam stood very still. "You knew. You knew all along."

"I suspected. Hoping's not knowing." She rose, and crossed to him, and took his face in both rough hands. "If I say no, will you sneak out anyway?"

"...Probably."

"Yes, you will. You're your mother's son." Something moved through her face then, quick and private, a door opening onto a room he'd never been allowed in. "Then listen to me. You go. You find that Door. And you come back." Her grip tightened. "Whatever happens out there — whatever the fog does, whatever the knight does, whatever *she* does — you come back to me. Swear it on the bells, since you seem to think they're listening."

Tam laughed, wet-eyed, and swore it on the bells.

Gran kissed his forehead, hard, the way she'd done when he was small enough to carry, and then pushed him toward the door because in their family tenderness was administered in doses and then immediately followed by chores. "Go on, then. Tell them. And Tam—" He paused in the doorway, morning fog silvering the frame. "If you find a chestnut seller with six fingers," Gran said, and her voice only shook a little, "you buy the honest ones. And you bring me some."

He was halfway down the lane, running, when he heard the bell.

Not a dream-bell. A real one — the old chapel bell at the village center, tolling slow and heavy, the sound it made only for councils and funerals. Tam stopped in the middle of the road, breath smoking in the cold, and watched figures converge on the meeting hall through the thinning mist: Old Piet, the Hendersons, the miller, faces grim, moving with the particular urgency of people who have decided something terrible and want it done before anyone can argue.

They were voting today. On the map. On sealing the valley.

Tam turned and ran faster.
