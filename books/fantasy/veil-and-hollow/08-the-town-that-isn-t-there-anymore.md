## 8. The Town That Isn't There Anymore

The map said *Vessany Cross*, and the world said nothing at all.

Noor stood at the edge of a field gone to seed and looked at the place where a town should have been. The grass grew in straight lines. That was the first wrong thing—grass does not grow in straight lines unless something taught it to, unless streets once ran beneath it like veins under skin. She could see them if she let her eyes unfocus: the ghost-geometry of a market square, the pale rectangles where foundations had been, the sunken trench of what must have been the high road running east toward mountains she had never crossed.

"Three roads met here," Tam said quietly. "The Bell Road, the Salt Road, and the Pilgrim's Way. Grandmother Maren used to sing about it. *Where three roads kiss, Vessany keeps its well.*"

"You never told me you knew songs like that."

"I didn't know I did." He frowned, pressing his knuckles against his mouth the way he did when he was trying not to cry. "It just came out."

Sera crouched at the field's edge, one hand flat on the earth, reading it the way she read everything—like a soldier, like a woman who had learned that ground could lie to you. "No ruins," she said. "No rubble, no burned beams, no bones. A town doesn't leave this clean. Even when the Chancery razes a place, they leave the stones. It's cheaper." Her jaw tightened around the word *Chancery* as though it were gristle she couldn't quite swallow.

"So where did the stones go?" Noor asked.

"That," Sera said, "is the question I've been afraid of since the fog line."

They walked in through the ghost-streets because there was nowhere else to go. The grass came up to Tam's ribs and parted for him strangely, almost courteously, and Noor noticed that he walked without looking down—as if his feet remembered paving that no longer existed. Twice he stepped over things that weren't there. Once he ducked.

"What did you duck under?" she asked.

He looked up at her with an expression of pure confusion, then touched the air beside his ear. "A sign," he said. "The cooper's sign. It squeaked in the wind. It always—" He stopped. His hand closed slowly into a fist. "It always squeaked," he finished, very small.

Noor's chest did something painful and complicated. Nine years in the Hollow had taught her every flavor of loss: the slow kind, the bitter kind, the kind you carried so long it stopped feeling like a wound and started feeling like a bone. But this was a new flavor—a child carrying a whole country inside him, and the country leaking out through songs he didn't know he'd learned.

"It's all right," she said, which was a lie, and took his hand, which was not.

* * *

They found the well at the center of everything, exactly where three lines of dead road converged, and it was the only thing left standing in Vessany Cross.

It was beautiful, in the way that abandoned things are beautiful—the way a held breath is beautiful. Dry stone, older than any masonry Noor had seen in the Hollow, its rim worn smooth by generations of hands and rope. Moss climbed it in careful spirals. And hanging above it, from a frame of black iron that should have rusted away centuries ago, hung a bell.

Not a large bell. A hand-bell, almost—no bigger than a loaf of bread—cast in some metal that wasn't bronze and wasn't silver and caught the afternoon light and held it, refusing to give it back.

"Don't touch it," Sera said immediately.

"I wasn't going to touch it."

"You were absolutely going to touch it. You have the face of a person about to touch a bell."

Noor realized, with some embarrassment, that this was true. She put her hands behind her back like a scolded child. Sera almost smiled—the small, crooked thing that passed for a smile on her—and turned to make camp in the lee of the well, and in that moment of her turned back, Noor leaned forward and rang it.

She didn't mean to. That was the truth she would tell herself later, kneeling in the grass with her ears full of sound: she hadn't meant to. Her hand had simply risen, drawn on a string she couldn't see, and her fingertip had brushed the bell's cold lip, and the bell had spoken.

It did not ring so much as *remember*. One note, low and round and impossibly deep for so small a thing, and inside the note were other sounds—the way a seed is inside an apple. Voices. Hundreds of voices, layered, calling across a market square in a language Noor did not know and understood perfectly: *fresh bread, two coppers; mind the cart; Liss, come away from the well; evening bells at dusk, don't be late.* The smell of woodsmoke went through her like an arrow. The feeling of a crowd, warm and close and utterly unafraid.

Then it stopped, and the silence afterward was enormous, and Tam was staring at her with tears running freely down his face.

"That's them," he whispered. "That's *them*. That's the market. That's—" His voice cracked. "That's my mother's voice. Buying bread."

Noor's blood went cold. "Tam—your mother died before you were born. Maren told me. Your mother was born in the Hollow."

"She was born *here*." He wiped his face with his sleeve, fierce and unashamed. "Everyone says she was born in the Hollow because nobody remembers anything else. But I remember. I remember her hands. I remember this bell. I remember—" He pointed at the empty street leading south, and his finger trembled. "I remember the lamplighter. He had a dog named Turnip. Nobody remembers a dog named Turnip except me."

Sera had come around the well fast, sword half-drawn out of habit, and now she stood looking between the two of them with an expression Noor couldn't read—hunger and horror in equal measure. "The bell holds echoes," she said slowly. "I've heard of relic-bells. Chantry work. They store... impressions. Sound. Feeling." She paused. "They don't store mothers' voices. Not clearly. Not *named dogs*. Something is feeding that memory back to him."

"Or something is letting him remember," Noor said.

"Those aren't different things."

"They might be here."

* * *

They made camp in the shell of what Tam insisted had been the inn, though there was nothing left of it but a foundation and a hearthstone, and the hearthstone was warm.

That was the second wrong thing, and it was worse than the first. Sera put her palm flat against it and held it there for a long time, and her face did the thing it did when she was deciding how much truth a situation could bear. "Stone holds heat for hours," she said finally. "Maybe a day."

"Sera."

"Maybe a day," she repeated, and began unpacking the rice.

But Noor had grown up in the Hollow learning to read weather and wood and the small honest language of things, and stone does not hold heat for a day in open grass under a cooling sky. The hearthstone was warm the way a sleeping animal is warm. As if the inn had only just emptied. As if the fire had only just been banked, and someone would be down shortly to check the kettle.

She sat near it while the light failed, turning Ilya's map over in her hands. In the dying ink, Vessany Cross was marked with a tiny bell-symbol and a word in the same script that threaded through the whole map—that script she couldn't read awake and yet had begun, horribly, to understand asleep.

Because that was the third wrong thing, and it was hers.

For six nights since the fog line, Noor had dreamed in the language of the map. Not dreamed *about* it—dreamed *in* it, fluently, the way you dream in your mother tongue. She spoke with people whose faces she couldn't hold onto. She argued with a harbor-master about tariffs. She recited something long and formal, standing in a great hall while brass doors taller than trees stood shut before her, and everyone in the hall had bowed to her, and she had known, in the dream, that this was right and ordinary and hers.

Last night the dream had changed. Last night she had stood in the hall alone, and the doors had been open, and beyond them was a storm—the same storm, she knew without knowing how she knew, that lived coiled and dormant in her hands—and a voice from everywhere had said, gently, in the dreaming language:

*You sealed us in. You can seal us out. Choose again.*

She had woken with her fingers crackling with blue light and had spent an hour in the dark making sure it faded, while Sera pretended to sleep ten feet away and did not fool her for a moment.

Now, watching the fire, she flexed her hands beneath the blanket. The tremor was there, faint as a heartbeat under ice. Nine years of stillness, nine years of being nobody, of hauling roof-tiles and grinding herbs and keeping her palms wrapped when the storms came so no one would see the sparks—and outside the veil it was all waking up, all of it, like a body remembering how to bleed.

"Noor." Tam's voice, soft, from across the fire. He'd been quiet since the bell. "Can I ask you something strange?"

"You've asked me stranger."

"When you touched the bell. What did you feel? Really."

She considered lying to him. She was getting frighteningly good at lying to the people she loved—it seemed to be the year's chief accomplishment. But Tam looked at her the way no one else did, with the eyes of someone who also carried a secret country, and lies curdled in front of those eyes.

"I felt like I'd come home," she said. "To a home I've never had."

He nodded slowly, unsurprised, and that frightened her more than anything else that day. "That's what I feel all the time," he said. "Everywhere. Like I'm homesick for a place I'm standing in." He poked the fire, and sparks rose, and for a moment—one terrible, luminous moment—they rose in the shape of the script from the map, a dozen glowing characters climbing into the dark before the wind tore them apart.

Neither of them mentioned it. Some silences are a kindness. Some are a door closing.

Across the fire, Sera cleaned her sword and watched them both, and said nothing either, and Noor understood that the knight was collecting these moments—banking them, the way the bell banked voices—for some reckoning she hadn't announced yet.

* * *

Noor woke in the grey hour before dawn to the sound of the bell ringing.

Not the note she had drawn from it—this was faster, urgent, a peal, the alarm-rhythm of a town with a fire in it or an army coming down the Bell Road. She was on her feet with storm-light already crawling her wrists before her mind fully woke, and Sera was up too, sword out, scanning the empty streets, and Tam stood at the well with his arms wrapped around himself, weeping without any noise at all.

"There's no one ringing it," Sera said. "There's no rope. There's no *ringer*."

"I know," Tam said. "It rings at dawn. It always rings at dawn. The bellringer's name was Odell and he was lazy so they made the bell enchanted to ring itself and he got to sleep late and everyone complained about it and—" his voice broke apart— "and everyone *complained about it*, that's such a small thing to be the last of."

The bell rang and rang over the empty town, calling a congregation that had been unwritten from the world, and the sound did not echo, because there was nothing left to echo off of. It just went up into the fog and stopped, cut clean, like a thread snipped.

And Noor felt the town *shift*.

There was no better word for it. The ground under her moved—not physically, not an earthquake, but the way a floor moves when a ship takes a swell. For one vertiginous instant she saw Vessany Cross whole: streets and roofs and washing-lines, the lamplighter's dog trotting past, smoke rising from forty chimneys, a living town wearing the dawn like a coat—and then it was gone, and she was standing in grass again, and her nose was bleeding, and both Sera and Tam were shouting her name.

"I saw it," she gasped, wiping her lip. "I saw the whole town. Just now. For a second."

"The veil is thinning," Sera said grimly. "This whole region is thinning. What happened at the fog line, what Ilya was running from—the erasure isn't holding anymore. Places are flickering." She sheathed her sword and gripped Noor's shoulder hard enough to bruise. "Listen to me. If this town can flicker back for a heartbeat, then the Hollow isn't being erased. It's being *suppressed*. There's a difference, and the difference is that suppressed things can be released—or can break loose. And if the Hollow breaks loose instead of being released—"

"Then what?"

Sera looked at the well, at the bell, at the boy crying beside it, and Noor watched her decide, again, how much truth the moment could carry.

"Then everyone inside it goes with it," she said. "We need to move. Today. Faster than today."

They broke camp quickly. But at the edge of the ghost-town, where the three dead roads diverged, Noor stopped and looked back once.

The bell hung silent in the grey morning. And beneath it, in the dry well, something answered her looking—a faint warmth, a faint attention, the sense of a town holding its breath behind a wall of forgetting, waiting to see whether anyone coming down the road would remember to say its name.

"*Vessany Cross*," Noor said aloud, to the empty air, feeling foolish and certain at once. "Your name is Vessany Cross. I'll carry it."

Far behind them, soft as a heartbeat, the bell rang once more.

And in her pocket, unrolled against her hip, Ilya's map grew warm—and the ink of the road ahead, which had been fading for days, darkened by a single fresh line, as if somewhere a dying man's hand were still drawing them onward.
