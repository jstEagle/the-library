## 9. Hunters of the Erased

The bell hung from a crooked iron post at the crossroads, green with age, its mouth stuffed with a bird's nest. Sera saw it first and stopped so abruptly that Noor walked into her shoulder.

"What is it?"

"That bell." Sera kept her voice low, though there was nothing to be low *for*—the road was empty in both directions, a pale seam of dust stitched through brown hills. "It's Chancery work. Survey bells. They ring them when they erase a place, to mark the boundary of what's being taken." She touched the post without thinking and pulled her fingers back as if scalded. "I've stood beside a hundred of these. I've never seen one left standing."

"Maybe they missed it," Tam said.

"They don't miss." Sera circled the post slowly, one hand on her sword hilt out of habit rather than fear. The nest inside the bell was old, weathered gray, woven from grasses that didn't grow here anymore. A survey bell was supposed to be removed once the erasure was complete—carried back to the Chancery archives like a trophy tooth. This one had been forgotten, or abandoned, or—

Or someone had refused to take it down.

She looked up at it again, really looked, and felt something turn over in her chest. The green patina. The nest. Years of weather on an object that should have been archived within the season. Which meant the erasure hadn't finished cleanly. Which meant the Hollow had been fighting back for a long time, in small stubborn ways, and no one had ever come to finish the job.

Which meant, possibly, that no one had ever needed to.

Until Ilya's map started moving through the world like a lit fuse.

"We shouldn't linger," she said. "This is a landmark. It's on the map. If anyone is looking for us—"

"If anyone is looking for us," Noor said quietly, "they've been looking since before we left."

Sera couldn't argue with that. She had been arguing with herself about it for two days, ever since she'd found the first sign: a hoofprint in soft ground by the river ford, shod in the Chancery pattern—square-toed, weighted for a rider who expected to dismount and fight. She hadn't told Noor. She hadn't told Tam. She'd told herself it might have been a drover, a pilgrim, anyone, and had spent every night since sleeping in her armor with her sword unsheathed across her knees.

She was tired of lying by omission. It was, she was discovering, a skill that atrophied fast when you spent time around people who deserved better.

"Water first," she said instead. "There's a spring behind the rise. We drink, we fill the skins, and we're gone before midday."

Tam was already scrambling up the slope, eager, his satchel bouncing. He'd been like this all morning—bright, quick, reciting street names under his breath. *Copperlane. The Weighhouse Steps. Bellringer's Row.* He thought he was keeping the city alive by saying its streets aloud. Sera had watched him do it and said nothing, because the truth was that every name he spoke seemed to cost him something—a flicker at the edge of his eyes, a half-second pause where certainty used to be—and she didn't know how to tell him that.

Noor lingered at the bell. Sera watched her lay her palm flat against the metal, and watched the fog that lived in Noor's hands—the faint gray shimmer she still tried to hide, though she'd stopped pretending it wasn't there—curl toward the bronze like a cat greeting a familiar lap.

"It's warm," Noor said.

"Bells don't hold heat."

"This one does." Noor turned, and her face had gone strange. "Sera. It's ringing. Not out loud. Inside. Can't you feel it?"

Sera could, actually. She'd been feeling it since the ridge—a pressure behind her teeth, a hum in the fillings of her molars, the particular wrongness of standing inside a held note. She'd told herself it was the wind through the post. She was running out of things to tell herself.

*They know where you are,* said the part of her mind that had survived eleven years of Chancery service by being right at exactly the worst moments. *Survey bells remember their last note. And somewhere, someone has the twin of this bell, and is listening.*

* * *

They came at dusk, which was correct procedure, and Sera hated them a little for how correct they were.

She heard the horses first—or rather, she heard the absence of birdsong along the eastern hedgerow, and understood what the silence meant, and had maybe four seconds. Four seconds to grab Tam by the collar and haul him off the road into the ditch; four seconds to see Noor's head come up like a deer's; four seconds to draw steel and know, with cold professional clarity, that steel was not going to be enough.

Six riders came over the rise in a wedge, and the light bent around them. That was the thing outsiders never understood about Chancery wardens—they didn't glow, they didn't blaze, they simply made the world around them slightly less real. Colors dulled at their edges. Sound fell into their hoofprints. Their armor was plain gray steel, unadorned, and each of them carried the long curved blade called a null-sword, which did not cut flesh so much as cut the fact of flesh, leaving wounds that bled light and closed into smooth, memoryless scars.

Sera knew every one of those blades. She had trained with them. She had, for six years, been one of the people carrying them.

The wedge halted twenty paces out, and the lead rider dismounted, unhurried, and pushed back her hood.

Commander Ilse Marchetti looked older than Sera remembered. There were new lines around her mouth, and her hair had gone iron-gray at the temples, but her eyes were the same—pale, patient, the eyes of a woman who had never once raised her voice because she had never once needed to. She had taught Sera how to hold a null-blade. She had taught her that mercy was a form of arithmetic, and that a good warden learned to carry the sums without flinching.

"Sera Vane," she said, and her voice carried in the dead air like a stone dropped down a well. "Eleven years, and this is where I find you. Guarding a map for a dead cartographer, in a country that doesn't exist."

"The country exists," Sera said. "That's rather the point of my current employment."

A few of the wardens shifted. Marchetti didn't smile, but something moved near the corner of her mouth, brief as a fish under ice. "You always did have your father's mouth. Come home, Sera. Bring me the map, and the testimony you've been sitting on, and I'll reopen your case myself. Full reinstatement. Your name off the disgrace rolls. You could die a knight of the Order instead of a fugitive babysitting ghosts."

"You mean I could go back to helping you erase them."

"I mean," Marchetti said, with the terrible gentleness Sera remembered from a hundred lectures, "that the alternative is that I do my job, and you watch me do it, and then I write a report that says the disgraced knight Sera Vane resisted arrest and died of her wounds. Or—" and here her gaze slid past Sera's shoulder, to where Tam crouched in the ditch, "—didn't."

The word landed in Sera's stomach like a stone in still water.

"There's a boy," Marchetti continued, conversational now, awful now. "Veil-born, by the look of him. Remembers things he shouldn't. Do you understand what that makes him, Sera? Not a person. An *anomaly*. Anomalies are evidence, and evidence gets collected." She let that sit. "Give me the map. Walk away. That's the whole offer, and it expires when I get tired of standing here."

Sera became aware that Noor had moved to stand at her shoulder. She could feel the other woman's magic gathering—not blazing, just gathering, like weather deciding—and part of her wanted to shout at her to stop, because she'd seen what happened when storm-magic met null-steel, and the arithmetic of it favored the steel.

But another part of her, a newer part, the part that had grown in three weeks of bad roads and shared fires, looked at Marchetti's patient gray eyes and felt something she hadn't felt in eleven years.

She felt done.

"No," Sera said.

Marchetti sighed. "I was afraid you'd say that. Take them."

What followed lasted ninety seconds and felt like a year.

Sera got her blade up in time to catch the first warden's descending cut, and the impact sang up her arm wrong—not the shock of steel on steel but a kind of *subtraction*, her own strength dimming where the blades kissed. She gave ground, gave more, turned the fight downhill toward the ditch where Tam was, because that was the only direction that mattered. Behind her she heard Noor shout a word in a language none of them should have known, and the sky answered—clouds boiling in from a clear horizon, wind flattening the grass, the first fat drops of a rain that tasted of ozone and old grief.

Lightning took the lead rider's horse out from under him. It should have taken the rider too, but the wardens' armor drank the strike, dulling it, spreading it, and the man rolled to his feet already drawing.

Sera reached Tam. He was frozen, staring at the storm with an expression she couldn't read—wonder, or recognition, or both. She grabbed him, shoved him ahead of her toward the hedgerow gap, and that was when the third warden came out of the rain.

Sera turned to meet him. She was fast enough. She was almost fast enough.

The null-blade never touched her. It touched Tam.

He had turned back—turned *back*, the idiot, the brave stupid child—to call a warning to Noor, and the warden's cut meant for Sera's throat caught him across the shoulder instead, a glancing blow, barely a touch, the blade whispering through cloth and skin and something deeper than either.

Tam made a small sound. Not a scream. More like surprise.

Then the world went white and screaming, because Noor arrived.

Sera had seen storms. She had grown up on the coast; she knew storms the way farmers know weather. What Noor did in the next three seconds was not weather. The rain reversed itself. The lightning came down in a sheet, a wall, a curtain of white fire between the wardens and the three of them, and the air filled with a sound like a cathedral organ played by an angry god, and Commander Ilse Marchetti—who had never once raised her voice—shouted a single word that Sera didn't catch, and the wardens broke off and withdrew into the rain like ink dissolving in water.

Gone. Just gone. Leaving hoofprints that filled with light and faded.

Sera stood in the downpour, chest heaving, sword shaking in her grip, and turned to check on Tam.

He was sitting in the mud, holding his shoulder. The wound was small—a thin line, hardly bleeding. But where the blood should have been dark, it shimmered, faintly luminous, washing away in the rain like watered milk.

And he was looking at his own hand with an expression Sera recognized, because she had worn it once herself, years ago, the day she'd realized what the Order actually was.

"Tam," she said gently. "Let me see."

"It's fine," he said. Then, after a pause that was a half-second too long: "It's fine. It's—it's fine." He frowned, as if listening to something far away. "We were going somewhere. Weren't we? There was a—we were following the..."

His face went blank. Openly, horribly blank, the way a page goes blank.

"The river," Sera said quickly, kneeling in the mud beside him, taking his small cold hand. "The dry riverbed. With the bells underground. You were going to show us. You *remember*."

"I..." Tam blinked, and rain ran down his face, and for a moment Sera saw him grasping, actually grasping, at the air where his memories had been. "Bellringer's Row," he said at last, uncertain, testing the words like ice on a pond. "Copperlane. The... the Weighhouse..." His voice cracked. "Why can't I—there were more. There were *so many more.*"

Noor dropped to her knees on his other side, and her hands were glowing, that gray storm-light, and she pressed them to the wound without asking. The shimmering cut hissed. Tam gasped—but the light in the blood dimmed, slowed, didn't stop.

"It's in him," Noor said, and her voice was terrible and calm, the calm of someone reading a sentence they wished they couldn't parse. "The null. It's in him. It's eating the memories. The ones of the city first, because those are the ones that are—" She stopped. Swallowed. "Because those are the ones that are *him*."

Sera looked up through the rain at the empty road, at the place where the wardens had been, and felt the full weight of what she'd done settle onto her shoulders. She had brought a child into this. She had told herself the map was worth it, the Door was worth it, that the ends would justify the marching. And now a nine-year-old boy sat in the mud losing his home one street name at a time, and the woman who had offered Sera redemption was riding back to report that the anomaly had been acquired.

No. Not acquired. *Marked.*

Because Sera knew null-wounds. She knew what a graze did. It didn't kill. It hollowed—slowly, from the edges inward, starting with whatever the victim held dearest, because the blade cut the fact of a thing and facts unraveled best from the center. Tam had days. Maybe a week. And at the end of it he wouldn't be dead; he'd be a boy with no past, a blank page the Chancery could write anything on.

Unless they reached the Door first. Unless the Door could do what Ilya's notes claimed it could.

Unless.

"Can you walk?" she asked Tam.

He nodded. Then, in a smaller voice: "Will I forget you too?"

The question went through Sera like a blade of its own. She thought about lying. She opened her mouth to do it, and found she couldn't—not anymore, not to him, not after watching Marchetti make kindness into a weapon for fifteen years.

"I don't know," she said. "But here's what I know. Even if you forget every street, every bell, every name—you're learning new ones right now. Mine. Noor's. Maren's. Those are yours too, and the null can't tell the difference. So we'll just keep giving you more. Faster than it can take them away. Do you understand? We'll drown it in names."

Tam looked at her for a long moment. Rain dripped off his chin. And then he nodded, once, the way soldiers nod, and Sera's heart broke a little and hardened a little in the same instant.

Behind them, thunder rolled away eastward, chasing the wardens' retreat. Noor rose slowly to her feet, and Sera saw her staring at her own glowing hands with an expression that had nothing to do with power and everything to do with fear—as if she'd finally understood what she was, and what it was going to cost.

"We move tonight," Sera said, hauling Tam up, steadying him. "No more camps, no more landmarks. We follow the riverbed in the dark if we have to." She looked east, toward the hills, toward the dry channel that Tam had promised was lined with buried bells. "Because they know where we are now. And worse—" she glanced at Tam's bandaged shoulder, at the faint light still seeping through the cloth, "—so does the wound."

Tam swayed, caught himself against Noor's side, and murmured something under his breath. Sera leaned close to hear it.

"Copperlane," the boy whispered, clinging to the word like a rope. "Copperlane. Copperlane."

Three weeks ago, Sera Vane had traded her oath for a map and told herself it was the last thing she'd ever give up.

Standing in the rain, with a forgetting child between them and a storm-woman staring at her own hands, she understood that she had been wrong. It was only the first.

* * *

They walked through the night.

The rain followed them for an hour and then gave up, sulking off toward the mountains, and the clouds tore open to show a scatter of indifferent stars. The land east of the crossroads grew stranger as they went—hills that repeated themselves, a fence that ran straight for a mile and ended in open field as if the farmer had simply stopped existing midway through mending it, a milestone whose inscription had been scraped smooth by nothing at all. The Hollow's borderlands, Sera thought. The scar tissue where the erased country had been cut away. Reality here was a poorly mended seam, and everything near it pulled at the thread.

Tam named the stars to keep himself awake. Not their proper names—street names. *That one's Copperlane. That bright one's the Weighhouse. That cluster is Bellringer's Row.* Noor walked beside him and supplied names when his faltered, guessing, inventing, and Sera realized after a while that Noor wasn't guessing at all. The names came out of her smooth and certain, in a cadence that matched Tam's, as if some buried part of her had always known the city too.

She filed that away. There was no time to ask, and she suspected Noor didn't have an answer yet anyway.

Near dawn they found the riverbed.

It opened before them out of the dark like a wound in the hills—a broad dry channel of pale stones, curving away north under a sky going gray. No water had run here in a long time. But when Sera dropped down onto the stones, she felt it immediately: a vibration, deep and rhythmic, rising through her boot soles. Faint. Patient. Like a heartbeat heard through a wall.

"Do you feel that?" Noor whispered.

Tam was already walking ahead, arms out slightly from his sides, following the vibration the way a blind man follows a handrail. His lips were moving. Sera caught fragments—names again, but slower now, more careful, a man counting coins he couldn't afford to lose.

And beneath their feet, faint and far down and unmistakable, a bell rang.

Just once. A single deep note, muffled by earth and distance, rolling up through the stones and dying away into silence.

Tam stopped walking. He turned around, and in the pre-dawn gray his face was wet, and he was smiling, and it was the most heartbreaking thing Sera had seen in eleven years of heartbreaking things.

"That's the first one," he said. "I told you. The river of bells." He put his palm flat against a pale stone, and the light from his wound pulsed once, in time, Sera noticed, with something far below. "They're all still down there. All of them. They never stopped ringing."

Noor knelt beside him, laid her hand next to his on the stone, and hummed—three notes, low, matching the fading echo perfectly.

Sera stood in the dry riverbed with her sword loose in her hand and watched the two of them, the storm and the remembering boy, listening to a drowned city sing, and she thought: *Marchetti will come again. And next time she won't offer anything.*

Far to the south, so faint it might have been imagination, a second bell answered the first.

Tam's smile widened. But his hand, Sera saw, had begun to tremble—and when he opened his mouth to name the note, the name wouldn't come.
