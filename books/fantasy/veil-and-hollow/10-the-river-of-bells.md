## 10. The River of Bells

Tam had never led anyone anywhere before. He was the smallest thing in the Hollow, Maren always said, the last seed off a plant nobody remembered planting, and for nine years he had been content to be small—content to be the boy who ground herbs and fetched water and listened, always listening, to the dreams that came up out of him like water from a well no one had dug.

But now he walked at the front of three people, with a map folded against his chest where his heartbeat could keep it warm, and the fog opened before him like it knew his name.

"It's this way," he said, for the fourth time since dawn.

"You've said," Sera Vane said behind him. Her voice was dry as old leather, but she hadn't argued once, which Tam understood—without being able to say how he understood—was its own kind of courtesy. Knights, in his dream-city, had walked like her: like the ground owed them money and they intended to collect.

Noor said nothing at all. She had barely spoken since the bell town, since the well with no town around it, since Tam had stood on its lip and told them about the market square and the tanner's row and the woman who sold honey-cakes shaped like moons, and Noor's face had gone strange and still, like someone listening to a song they'd sworn they'd never heard before.

Tam kept walking. The riverbed was under his boots—he could feel it through the soles, a long smooth channel worn into the earth by water that had not flowed here in his lifetime or anyone's. In the city of his dreams the river had never been water anyway. That was the first thing he tried to explain, back when he was six and Maren had asked him why he woke up crying about bells.

*It's a river of bells,* he'd told her. *The whole river. Not water. Bells.*

Maren had checked him for fever and made him willow-bark tea, and after that Tam learned to keep the city behind his teeth. But he had never stopped walking it in his sleep. He knew its districts the way other children knew their own hands: the Brasswork, where the doors were cast and tuned; the Chimeyards, where the bells were born; the Long Stair down to the river itself, where the water rang as it moved, every drop a note, the whole river one enormous slow song that took a year to play from source to sea.

The map agreed with him. That was the terrible, wonderful thing. Ilya's dying ink showed the river's course exactly as Tam dreamed it, and where the map marked its second landmark—a circle with lines radiating out, like a bell seen from above—the dream-city sat in Tam's memory at precisely that spot.

"The confluence," he said now, stopping. Ahead, the dry riverbed forked around a hill of pale stone. "The Bellwater meets the Graywash here. There was a bridge. The Singing Bridge. You could feel it hum in your teeth if you stood in the middle."

"There's no bridge," Sera said.

"Not yet," Tam said, and then, hearing himself: "I mean—it's under. Everything's under. It didn't get destroyed, it got *buried*. Like—" He searched for it. "Like a coin dropped in flour. It's all still there. It's just that the world got poured over the top of it."

Sera looked at Noor. Noor looked at the riverbed. And Tam, who had spent his whole life being the only person who remembered, felt the strange vertigo of being *believed*—and found that it hurt worse than disbelief ever had. Because if they believed him, then the city was real. And if the city was real, then everyone in it—

He didn't finish the thought. He was getting better at not finishing thoughts. It was a skill the Hollow taught you early.

"Down here," he said instead, and turned left at the fork, into the channel of the Bellwater.

* * *

The fog thickened as they went, but it was a different fog than the valley's. Tam noticed it the way you notice a stranger's handwriting: same letters, wrong hand. The Hollow's fog was soft, apologetic, a blanket pulled over a sleeping thing. This fog had edges. It moved in currents, like it was following the old course of the river, pouring downhill along channels only it remembered.

And beneath their feet, faint as a pulse, something was ringing.

Tam stopped. Held up a hand—he'd seen Sera do it, and it felt magnificent—and crouched, pressing his palm flat to the dry stones.

"Do you hear that?" he whispered.

Noor knelt beside him. She put her hand down too, and Tam watched her face do the thing it did when she was trying very hard not to feel something: everything draining out of it, leaving only the eyes, wide and dark and working hard.

"Bells," she said.

"Underground." Tam's voice cracked and he hated it. "They're buried. All of them. The whole river is bells, I told you, and when the country got—when it got *folded away*, somebody must have—" His throat closed. He tried again. "Somebody buried the river. They buried the *river*, and it's still ringing. It never stopped. Nine years, or a hundred years, however long—we've been walking on top of a song this whole time and nobody—"

His voice broke properly then, and he let it, because Sera had crouched down on his other side and put one gauntleted hand—not unkindly—on his shoulder, and because some things are too heavy to carry quietly.

"They're ringing underground," he wept, "and nobody came to listen."

* * *

They followed the sound.

It grew as they walked, the way a voice grows when it stops calling and starts singing. By midday Tam could pick out individual bells—there, a deep one, tolling so slowly it seemed less a sound than a change in the weather; there, a cluster of small bright ones, quick as rain. He named them as he recognized them, because naming was the only prayer he knew.

"That's the Widow of Lowmarket. She rings for funerals. She has a crack, right *there*—" he pointed at empty air over the stones, "—so she sounds like she's crying while she works. And those little ones, that's the Chimeyard brood, they ring when the tide of the song comes through, every—every hour, it was every hour—"

"And that?" Noor asked softly.

She was pointing ahead, where the fog gathered densest, and Tam realized he could hear it too: a bell bigger than all the others, so vast that its single note wasn't a note at all but a kind of standing silence, a held breath underneath the world.

He knew it the way you know your own name.

"The Motherbell," he said. "She's at the heart of the city. Under the Door." He glanced up at Noor, suddenly shy. "The map says the Door is real. So she's real. Everything's real. It's all just—underneath."

Noor had gone very pale. Tam watched her press her hand harder against the stones, and saw—though he couldn't have said what he was seeing—that her fingers were trembling in rhythm. Not shaking. *Ringing.* As if the bells below had found something in her to resonate with, some hollow chamber in her chest tuned to the same pitch.

"Noor?" he said.

"I'm fine," she said, in the voice of someone who is not fine and knows that everyone knows.

Then she began to hum.

Tam stopped dead.

It was a melody he had known all his life—the river-song, the great slow song of the Bellwater, the one that took a year to play and that he had heard in every dream since before he could talk. He had never once heard it outside his own head. He had certainly never heard another human being hum it, because there was no one left to hum it, because everyone who had ever learned it was buried under the world along with their bells.

But Noor was humming it. Perfectly. Every interval, every strange quarter-tone where the song bent like water around a stone, every rest—she even rested in the right places, in the long pauses where the Motherbell's silence filled the measure.

"Sera," Tam said. His mouth had gone dry. "*Sera.*"

"I hear her," Sera said quietly. She had her hand on her sword, though there was nothing to fight. "Noor. Where did you learn that?"

Noor's humming faltered, caught, resumed—as if the song were carrying her whether she willed it or not. When she spoke, her voice sounded far away, like it was coming up through the ground.

"I don't—I'm not doing it. It's just *there*. It's been there all day, since the well, since—" She pressed the heel of her hand against her forehead. "Tam, when you named the bells. The Widow of Lowmarket. I could *see* her. I could see the crack in her. I've never seen her. I've never seen any of this. Why do I know it?"

The fog curled around them, patient, attentive.

"Because you're from here," Tam said simply.

"No." Too fast. Too flat. "I'm from nowhere. I came over the border nine years ago with nothing. No name I could find, no past. Maren gave me my name."

"Okay," Tam said, because arguing with grown-ups about memory was like arguing with weather. But he kept walking, and he kept the map open, and he let the truth sit between them like a third traveler. "But the song doesn't care where you think you're from. The song knows."

* * *

They made camp that night in the lee of the hill at the confluence, where the two ghost-rivers met and the buried bells rang loudest. Sera built a fire small enough to hide, and they ate hard bread and dried apples, and the fog breathed around them like something sleeping.

Tam couldn't sleep. He lay with his ear against the ground, the way he had lain against Maren's chest as a small child, listening to the big slow drum of a heart much older than his own.

The bells were singing the evening turn of the song. He knew this part. *Vespers,* the dream-city called it, when the Chimeyard brood rang first and the Widow answered and the whole river picked it up, district by district, carrying the tune down toward the Motherbell the way a valley carries a shout.

He started to recite the names. He didn't decide to; they simply came up out of him, the way they came in his dreams, a litany, a roll call, a way of keeping the dead counted.

"Lowmarket. Tanner's Row. The Brasswork. Mooncake Lane. The Chimeyards. The Long Stair. Graywash Gate. The Weepers' Bridge. Candlemakers' Close. The House of Small Hours. The Singing Bridge. The Motherbell."

He said them softly, over and over, rearranging them, adding the streets and the squares and the names of bells, because a city is not buildings—a city is names, and as long as someone says the names, the city is somewhere.

Somewhere in the middle of the litany he realized Noor was sitting up, watching him across the fire with an expression he had no word for. And that she was mouthing the names. Silently. In time with him. *Mooncake Lane. The House of Small Hours.* Names she claimed she'd never heard.

"How," she whispered. It wasn't a question anymore. It was a wound.

Tam sat up. He was nine years old and he was the only mourner at the funeral of an entire country, and suddenly he understood something so large and so sad that it settled over him like the fog itself.

"Maybe you loved it too," he said. "Before. Maybe everybody who loved it got their remembering taken away, except me, because I was little and I hid it in my dreams where nobody thought to look." He hugged his knees. "That's what I think happened. Somebody stole the grief. All of it. Everybody's. Because a whole country dying and nobody crying—that's not natural. That's not how leaving works. Leaving is supposed to *hurt*."

The fire popped. Somewhere below them, the Widow of Lowmarket tolled her cracked, weeping note, patient as sorrow.

"But it didn't hurt," Tam went on, and his own tears came now, quiet ones, the kind he'd gotten good at. "Nobody in the village cries about the city because nobody knows to. Except me. And now maybe you. And it hurts so much, Noor, it hurts like a toothache in my whole body, and I don't want it to stop, because if it stops then I've forgotten her, and she deserves better than that. She deserves somebody crying."

Noor crossed the fire and sat down beside him and put her arm around his shoulders, and for a while neither of them said anything, and the bells sang underneath, and the fog kept its distance, almost respectfully, like a crowd at a graveside.

"She," Noor said finally. "You called the city *she*."

"Of course she's a she." Tam wiped his nose on his sleeve. "You can hear it in the bells. Listen."

They listened. And Noor—Tam heard it, clear as clear—hummed the vespers turn again, low, under her breath, and this time she didn't stop herself, and this time she wasn't afraid of it.

"The Motherbell's note," Tam murmured, drowsy now, the litany and the bells and the warmth of Noor's arm pulling him down toward sleep. "When we find her. When we find the Door. You'll know her when you hear her. Everyone does. She rings the note your heart is already making. That's how you know you're home—she doesn't teach you the song. She just reminds you."

His eyes closed. The last thing he heard, half-dreaming, was Noor's voice joining the underground river, saying the names with him now, aloud, both of them keeping the count together:

"*Lowmarket. Tanner's Row. The Brasswork. Mooncake Lane—*"

* * *

He woke to Sera's hand clamped over his mouth.

Fog everywhere, gray-black, the fire dead. Sera's eyes above her fingers were flat and bright as coins. She tilted her head: *listen.*

Tam listened.

The bells had changed.

All night they had sung vespers, the evening turn, slow and grieving and steady as they had sung it for nine years, for a hundred years. Now the song was stumbling. Skipping. The Widow tolled twice where she should have tolled once; the Chimeyard brood fell silent mid-phrase, then scrambled to catch up, ringing fast and frightened, like children running late.

And underneath it all, rising up through the stones, through Tam's spine, through his milk-teeth, came a new sound. A wrongness. A grinding, dragging nullity, like a great blade being drawn across the face of the world.

The fog to the east was lighting up—not with dawn. With lanterns. Many lanterns, arranged in a line, moving down the old course of the Graywash with the unhurried precision of people searching a grid.

Sera leaned close, her lips nearly touching Tam's ear, and her whisper was the quietest, worst thing he had ever heard:

"Wardens. They've picked up our trail—and they're following the river. Same as us." Her grip tightened on his shoulder. "Which means, boy, that whatever's at the end of this song—they want it too."
