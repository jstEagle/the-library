## 11. Storms Have Long Memories

The watchtower had been a watchtower once, in the way that a skeleton had been a man. Its upper rooms had slumped into one another like tired dancers, and the stairwell gaped open to the sky on its northern face, so that climbing it felt like climbing inside a broken ribcage. But the lower chamber still held a roof of slate and stone, and the walls were thick enough to swallow the wind, and after three days on roads that were never paved—roads that unspooled behind them like thread from a cut spool—it was shelter enough.

Noor built the fire small. Small fires were a habit nine years deep; a small fire could be smothered in a breath, explained away, denied. She crouched over it now with her palms out, watching the flames lean toward her hands the way flowers lean toward light, and she told herself, as she always told herself, that it was nothing. That fire leaned toward everyone.

Behind her, Tam was already asleep, curled against Sera's bedroll with the map folded under his cheek like a pillow he refused to give up. He'd walked eleven miles that day reciting names under his breath—*Vessamy Row, the Tallowmarket, Saint Ondine of the Nine Fountains*—and each name had come out steadier than the last, as though speaking them fed him somehow, as though the city lived in his mouth even while it died everywhere else. He had fallen asleep mid-name. *The Bridge of Small Mercies*, and then nothing but breathing.

Sera sat by the eastern window with her sword across her knees, whetting it in slow, patient strokes. She had barely spoken since the bell at the crossroads town, since the wardens came out of the fog with their null-blades and their tidy voices, since Tam had cried out when the blade grazed his arm and afterward couldn't remember the color of Maren's kitchen. Noor kept catching her looking at Tam's arm—at the thin white line where the wound should have been red—and then looking away with an expression Noor couldn't read. Guilt, maybe. Or arithmetic.

"You should sleep," Noor said.

"I'll take first watch."

"You took first watch last night."

"And you talked in your sleep." Sera didn't look up from the blade. The whetstone whispered along the steel. "You should know that about yourself, if you're going to argue about watches."

Heat climbed Noor's neck. "What did I say?"

"Nothing I understood." A pause, exactly long enough to be a lie or a mercy; Noor couldn't tell which. "Words in another language. And underneath them—" the whetstone stopped, "—thunder."

The fire popped. Outside, the real wind moved through the ruined upper floors of the tower, moaning through the broken stairwell, and Noor found herself listening to it the way she listened to all wind now: with her whole skin, cataloguing it, the way a mother in a crowd catalogs a child's cry. Southwesterly. Carrying rain from somewhere far off. Harmless.

She was so tired of harmless.

"I'll take second watch," she said, and lay down on the cold stone with her back to the fire, and did not sleep, and then did.

* * *

The dream began, as it always began, with rain.

But this was not the soft gray drizzle of the Hollow, the fog-rain that fell without conviction. This was the rain of before—the rain she had spent nine years not remembering, which is not the same as forgetting. It came down in ropes. It came down like the sky had been opened with a knife. And she was standing in the middle of it, in the dream, on a hill above a city of brass doors and ringing bridges, and her arms were raised, and her hands—

Her hands were full of lightning.

Not holding it. Being it. The storm moved through her the way blood moves through a body, and every bolt that tore the dark was hers, hers, hers, called up out of the wet bones of the world because something was coming, something with engines and ledgers and null-blades, something that meant to erase ten thousand lives with a signature and a stamp, and she had stood on that hill and made the only bargain left to her—

*Take my memory,* she heard herself say, in the dream, in a voice that was her own and older than her own. *Take everything. Take the country out of the world and take me out of the country. Only let them live.*

And the storm said yes. Storms always keep their promises. That was the terrible thing about them, the thing no one understood who had never been one: they were not cruel, they were *faithful*, and faithfulness at that scale looked exactly like catastrophe.

In the dream, the veil came down over the valley like a lid coming down over a lantern, and the city went quiet beneath it, and Noor felt herself being scooped hollow—memories lifting out of her like embers on an updraft, her mother's kitchen, her teacher's voice, the name of the street she grew up on, her own name in her own mouth, all of it rising and scattering into the storm until there was nothing left but a woman standing in the rain with empty hands, and the rain saying, gently, in the language she would spend nine years dreaming in: *Sleep now. You paid. You may sleep.*

"Noor."

She surfaced. The tower chamber was dark, the fire burned to orange coals, and the air—

The air was wrong.

It was full of ozone, sharp as a struck flint, and the coals in the hearth were standing up straight, little tongues of flame pointing at the ceiling like compass needles, and outside the broken windows the night was flickering. Not lightning. Something slower, deeper—a pulse in the dark, a heartbeat of blue-white light rolling through the fog beyond the walls, once, twice, in time with her own hammering chest.

Sera stood three feet away with her sword drawn, and she was not looking at the sky.

She was looking at Noor's hands.

They were lifted off the bedroll, hovering, and around them the air shimmered the way air shimmers above summer stone, and threads of pale light crawled between her fingers like the legs of something waking. Static crackled in Noor's hair. Every hair on her arms stood on end. And through the floor, through the stone, through the roots of the mountain itself, she could feel the veil—the great gray lid over the Hollow—trembling like a sheet in wind, trembling *because of her*, answering her nightmare the way a bell answers a struck tuning fork.

"Noor." Sera's voice was very level. Level the way ice is level. "What are you?"

* * *

They stood on opposite sides of the dead fire, and Tam slept on, mercifully, deeply, though Noor noticed—with a lurch—that the white line on his arm had faded another shade paler since evening. The forgetting was still working in him, slow as rot, and here she was making storms in her sleep while a child lost his city one memory at a time.

"You knew," Noor said. Her voice came out scraped. "At the well, in the town that isn't there. When my magic stirred and you went white as bone—you weren't afraid of what I might be. You were afraid of what I *was*. You've known since the beginning."

Sera didn't deny it. She set the sword down on the stone, deliberately, the way a person disarms themselves on purpose, and Noor hated how much that gesture cost her to trust.

"I suspected," Sera said. "Then tonight I stopped suspecting." She nodded at Noor's hands, which still sparked faintly, traitorously. "I served twelve years in the Order of the Vigil. Four of them on the Chancery's border archives. There's a record, Noor. An incident report, sealed at the highest level, dated nine years ago. A storm appeared over a valley in the northern reaches—a storm that came out of a clear sky and never left. It closed like a curtain. And the valley behind it stopped appearing on maps. Roads led around it. Surveyors forgot their instruments pointed at it. The Chancery logged it as an anomaly." Her jaw tightened. "Anomalies don't have heartbeats. Tonight I watched the veil beat in time with yours."

"Say it." Noor's hands curled shut, crushing the light. "Say what you think I am."

"The storm didn't make the veil." Sera's eyes held hers across the coals, steady and awful. "You did. You're not caught inside the Hollow, Noor. You're the reason it exists."

The words landed the way a landslide lands—not all at once, but in stages, each one finding a place in her to break. And yet, underneath the breaking, something else. A horrible rightness. Like a key turning in a lock she'd walked past for nine years without seeing. The shrine where she'd been found, feverish and empty, at the exact center of the map's first landmark. The way magic died at the border but flickered in *her* hands, homesick, trying to get back to its source. The way the fog had always, always been gentle with her. Keeping its promises.

"I don't remember," she whispered. "I swear to you, Sera, whatever I was—I don't remember choosing any of it."

"I believe you." And then, quieter, with something that wasn't quite kindness and wasn't quite accusation: "That's the part that frightens me. Because I've read what the Chancery does to people who stand in front of their engines, and none of them walk away. None of them get to forget. Someone *paid* for that valley, Noor. Paid in the only coin the Door accepts." She paused. "I just spent three days lying to you about knowing why your magic scares me. So ask me what I'm afraid of, and I'll answer honestly, and then you owe me the same."

Noor's throat worked. "Ask."

"What did you give up?" Sera said. "Or better—what did the storm take? Because if you made that veil, you didn't make it free."

And Noor opened her mouth to say *nothing, I remember nothing, there's nothing to tell*—and the lie stuck, physically stuck, like a fishbone in her throat, because the dream was still in her, still dripping, and the dream had *words* in it. Her words. Spoken in a voice she recognized the way you recognize your reflection in dark glass.

Slowly, she held out her hand, palm up, over the coals. And she let go.

It came back in shards. That was the only word for it—not a flood but a *shattering in reverse*, fragments flying together out of the dark: a hill above a city of brass doors. Rain like ropes. A ledger on a warden's desk, and a line item, and a number, and the number was people. Her own voice, younger and harder: *take my memory, take everything.* The sensation of being scooped hollow, ember by ember. A mother's hands kneading dough—gone. A teacher's voice naming constellations—gone. A street name, a sister's laugh, the smell of a harbor—gone, gone, gone, each fragment flaring bright as it returned only to show her the shape of its own absence, griefs for things she hadn't known she'd lost, arriving nine years late, all in the same terrible hour.

She was on her knees. She didn't remember falling. The coals had gone out entirely, snuffed by the pressure in the air, and frost was forming on the stones in July, and Sera had crossed the room and was gripping her shoulders, saying her name, saying it again, anchoring her the way you anchor anything in a gale.

"I chose it," Noor heard herself say. Her voice sounded like someone else's, someone older. "The Chancery was going to erase the Hollow anyway—erase it the way they erase everything, with paper, so that no one would ever even mourn it. And I—I was the only one who could stop them, and the price was—" She laughed, and it came out cracked down the middle. "The price was me. My memories. My magic. They sealed it all into the veil. I hid the country and then I hid myself inside it, and I made myself a woman who repairs roofs and avoids shrines, and I thought—" her breath hitched, "—I thought the emptiness was exile. It wasn't exile. It was the *payment*. I've been spending myself for nine years to keep ten thousand people alive, and I didn't even know, and now Tam is forgetting, and the veil is fraying, and the payment is running out—"

"Stop." Sera's hands tightened. "Breathe. Storms breathe. I've seen you do it."

And absurdly, that helped. Noor dragged air into her lungs, and the frost on the stones retreated, and the pressure eased by one degree, and then another.

They stayed like that a long moment—kneeling in the dark of a dead tower, the knight's hands on the storm's shoulders—until Noor could speak again.

"You lied to me," she said. "On the road. Three days, Sera. Tam got hurt while you were deciding whether I could be trusted with the truth about myself."

"Yes." No flinch, no excuse. "Because I've seen what the Chancery does with people like you, and I thought—if she doesn't know, they can't make her weaponize herself. I told myself the lie was armor." Her voice roughened. "It was cowardice wearing armor's coat. I'm sorry. You deserved the truth sooner, and Tam deserved it sooner, and I will spend the rest of this road earning back the difference if you let me."

Noor searched her face. In the dark it was mostly eyes and honesty, and the honesty was raw enough to be new-made, and Noor—who had spent nine years being lied to by her own self—found that she believed it.

"Then we're even," she said quietly. "We've both been keeping secrets to protect each other. It's a stupid way to travel."

A ghost of a smile crossed Sera's mouth. "It's the only way anyone travels."

Above them, through the broken stairwell, the sky had gone still. But Noor could feel the veil overhead like a ceiling of old ice, and somewhere far off—south, toward the capital that drowned, toward the Door—she felt a pull, faint and insistent, like a hook set behind her sternum. The fraying was accelerating. She could feel the edges of the Hollow going soft, the way a dream goes soft toward morning.

And threaded through the pull, almost too faint to catch, came a sound. A bell. Deep underground, impossibly far, ringing once—and in the half-second before it faded, Noor realized she knew its note. Knew it the way she knew her own heartbeat.

She also knew, with equal certainty, that they were not the only ones who had felt it.

"Sera," she said. "Wake Tam. We ride at first light—and we ride fast. I think the Door just rang for us."

Sera was already reaching for her sword. "Rang for us—or rang to tell the Chancery where we are?"

Noor looked at her hands, still faintly luminous, still hers, and for the first time in nine years she didn't try to hide them.

"Both," she said. "It's always both. Let's get there first."
