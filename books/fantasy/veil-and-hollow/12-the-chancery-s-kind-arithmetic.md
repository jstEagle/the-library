## 12. The Chancery's Kind Arithmetic

They took her at the ford.

Sera had heard the wardens coming for a full minute before they showed themselves—the particular silence of trained men moving through wet grass, the absence of birdsong that always preceded the Chancery's work. She'd had time to warn Noor, to shove Tam into the reeds, to draw her sword and make them pay for every step onto the bank.

She did none of those things. She turned and walked toward the silence with her hands open, because she had counted their footsteps and knew there were too many, and because Noor's magic still stuttered like a lamp in wind, and Tam was small, and someone had to be the thing the wolves chased while the others ran.

"Vane." Commander Aldric Voss stepped out of the mist as though he had been standing there all along, waiting for the world to arrange itself around him. He looked exactly as she remembered: grey at the temples now, but the same flat eyes, the same voice like a ledger being read aloud. "Nine years, and you still walk toward trouble like it owes you money."

"Aldric." She kept her hands loose at her sides. Six wardens fanned out behind him, null-blades sheathed but close. "You've come a long way to lose me again."

"I never lost you. I filed you." He gestured, almost gently, and two of the wardens took her arms—not rough, which was worse. The Chancery never needed to be rough. It simply made everything else impossible. "You'll want to hear what we have to say before you decide how much this costs you."

"It already cost me my oath."

"No," Voss said, turning to lead her up the bank. "It cost you your pension. Your oath is still on our books. That's rather the point of oaths—they outlive the people who swear them."

* * *

They held her in a tent that wasn't a tent.

She understood that within minutes of waking inside it. The canvas walls were canvas, the cot was a cot, the lantern burned ordinary oil—but when she pressed her palm against the fabric, it gave back nothing. Not resistance. Nothing. Her hand passed through into cold grey fog and came back unchanged, unmarked, and the wall sealed behind it like water closing over a stone. Somewhere beyond, the Hollow went on existing. In here, it did not.

A pocket of unmade country. They had brought a piece of the erasure with them and folded it into a room.

"You're awake." A warden sat at the folding table across from her, a woman with ink-stained fingers and the patient look of an accountant. Not Voss—someone lower, someone whose job was the talking part. She had a ledger open before her, and a second book beside it, older, bound in leather gone soft as cloth. "I'm Auditor Cress. I'm sorry about the accommodations. Null-space is disorienting at first. Most people stop noticing after an hour."

"What do you want?"

"The map, eventually. But first—" Cress turned the old book toward her, and Sera saw that its pages were filled not with writing but with names, thousands of them, columns of them, each struck through with a single clean line. "I want to show you something no one outside this order has ever seen. You've earned it, in a way. You were there at the beginning."

"I was a child."

"You were a recruit at the Purge of Veil-Born, which followed, which was the mopping-up. The beginning was nine years before that." Cress's finger moved down a column of names and stopped. "Do you know why the Chancery exists, Serene? Not the story we tell recruits. The reason."

"To contain wild magic. To keep the veils."

"To keep accounts." Cress said it without shame, without pride—the tone of a woman reciting a recipe. "Every working of great magic has a cost. Every veil must be paid for. The question is only ever: who pays?"

Sera said nothing. The lantern hissed. Somewhere far away, muffled by the nothing-walls, she thought she heard bells—or imagined she did, the way one imagines a name called in a crowd.

"Nine years ago, the Concord War ended. You'll have been taught it ended at the field of Marrow Bridge, with a treaty. What you weren't taught is that the treaty required payment. The southern alliance had unleashed something at Marrow Bridge—a working so vast it cracked the sky, and the crack had to be closed, and closing it required a veil of continental scale. A veil needs an anchor. An anchor needs a wound." Cress tapped the ledger. "The Chancery's task was to select the wound."

"Select," Sera repeated. The word sat in her mouth like a coin.

"There were eleven candidate sites. We scored them." Cress turned pages with terrible gentleness, showing him tables, columns, weighted criteria. Population density. Strategic value. Remembrance coefficient—that phrase again, *remembrance coefficient*, as if grief could be graphed. "The Hollow scored lowest on every measure. Isolated. Self-sufficient. Its people had already begun forgetting the old roads; the valley had been fading from maps for a generation before we ever touched it. And critically—" she looked up, and her eyes were kind, which was the most obscene thing in the room— "no one would miss it. No army would march for it. No treaty would break over it. It was, by every metric we possess, the country least worth keeping."

"You erased a nation because it was cheap."

"We erased a nation because the alternative was a war that would have killed four million people within the decade. The Hollow held perhaps twelve thousand souls." Cress closed the ledger softly. "Twelve thousand against four million. That is the arithmetic, Serene. We did not invent it. We only refused to look away from it."

"And the people inside?" Sera's voice came out low and even, which surprised her; she had expected it to shake. "The children? Did they appear in your arithmetic?"

"As rounding error." Cress didn't flinch. "The veil doesn't kill, whatever you've heard. It unmakes slowly, at the edges first. Most of the Hollow will dissolve peacefully over another decade or two. They'll fade in their sleep, in their gardens, among people they love. There are worse deaths. I've audited many of them."

Something in Sera's chest went very quiet and very cold, the way it used to go before battles, before the moment when thinking stopped and only motion remained. But she was tied to a chair in a room made of nothing, and motion would have to wait.

"You said the Door can restore it."

Cress paused. For the first time, something flickered behind the accountant's calm—not guilt, Sera thought, but caution. The look of a woman checking her figures twice.

"You've talked to Ilya."

"He's dead."

"Then whoever he gave the map to has talked to you." Cress folded her hands. "Yes. The Door is real. It was built by the Hollow's own magicians in the last days, before the erasure finished—a failsafe, a seam left in the closing of the wound. It can be opened in two ways, and only two. Restored: the country returns, whole, every soul that remains inside it preserved, the veil reversed. Or released: the country unmade entirely, its people poured out into the world, its memory scattered into everyone who ever loved it. Both are possible. Both are final." She met Sera's eyes. "But not both. One or the other. Restore or release. That is the Door's whole grammar."

"And the Chancery wants it restored? Wouldn't that undo your tidy arithmetic?"

"The Chancery wants it *closed*. Sealed permanently, with the key destroyed, so that no one—not us, not the Hollow's ghosts, not some storm-calling girl with a stolen map—can ever open it again." Cress rose, gathering her ledgers. "Because here is what the arithmetic cannot survive, Serene: exceptions. The moment one erased country comes back, every other debt becomes negotiable. Every other wound demands its Door. The peace of the world rests on payments staying paid." At the tent flap she stopped, half-turned. "Commander Voss has offered you reinstatement. Full honors. The map, and the girl delivered to us alive, and you walk out of this with your name restored. I'm told you want that. I'm told you've wanted it for nine years."

"Who told you that?"

"You did. Every time you wrote to the Order asking to be readmitted. Eleven letters." Cress smiled faintly. "We keep accounts, Serene. Even of longing."

* * *

They left her alone after that, and Sera discovered that a room made of nothing is an excellent place to take apart your own life.

Eleven letters. She had written them drunk, mostly, in the years after the purge—after she had stood in the courtyard at Caer Duloc and watched the veil-born children marched out under white banners, and said nothing, and then finally, too late, said one thing too many and lost everything worth losing. She had written begging to be taken back. She had told herself it was about the Order, about the oath, about the good the Order might still do with her inside it. Sitting in the null-dark with her wrists raw from testing the ropes, she let herself know the truth at last: she had wanted her name back because a name was easier to carry than guilt. Reinstatement was just forgiveness with better tailoring.

And they were offering it to her in exchange for Noor Zhou.

Noor, who flinched at loud noises and apologized to doors she bumped into. Noor, whose magic leaked out of her hands like light through shutters, who had crossed a dying country on the strength of a stranger's map because someone had to. Noor, who was, if Cress's figures were right—and they were right, Sera had seen the ledgers, the Chancery's sin was never being wrong—no more than a rounding error herself. A storm-calling girl from nowhere, worth less than twelve thousand souls, worth less than four million, worth nothing at all except to the two people currently hiding in the fog hoping she'd come back.

*Restore or release.* One or the other. The Door's whole grammar.

Except that wasn't quite true either, was it. Cress had said the Chancery wanted the Door sealed. Which meant the choice wasn't really between restoring and releasing. The choice was between the Hollow deciding its own ending, and the Chancery deciding it had no ending at all—just a long fade, an audit completed, a line drawn through twelve thousand names.

Sera tested the ropes again. Then she stopped testing them and started listening.

Null-space had a texture. That was the thing they didn't teach recruits, the thing you only learned if you'd stood at the edge of an erasure and felt it eat the sound. Nothingness wasn't silent—it was hungry. It pulled. And anything real caught inside it pulled back, the way a drowning man pulls at the water. The tent walls were nothing, but the stakes that held them were iron, driven into actual earth somewhere outside the fold, and iron remembers where it's planted. She could feel it through the floor: a faint stubborn hum, north-northwest, the direction of the bell at the crossroads town. The direction Noor and Tam had fled.

She worked the rope against the cot frame for what felt like hours, and it might have been—the null-light never changed, the lantern flame burning without consuming, time going soft and strange as everything else in here. When the last strand parted she sat still for a hundred heartbeats, letting the ache drain out of her shoulders, and then she walked to the wall and put her hand through the nothing and found, three inches deep, the iron stake.

Iron remembers. Iron also burns.

She gripped it anyway, and hauled, and the stake groaned out of the earth, and the wall above it shivered like a struck drumhead. The null-space was anchored to its stakes—all six of them, she realized, feeling outward along the hum. Pull enough stakes and the fold collapses. Pull all six and—

The second stake came free with a sound like a gasp. The walls began to ripple. Outside, distantly, she heard shouting, Voss's voice cutting flat and clear through the panic: *"She's collapsing the fold—stop her—"*

The third stake. The fourth. The tent was dissolving around her now, canvas unraveling into grey, the cot going soft as smoke beneath her feet, and through the widening tears she could see the real world rushing in—fog, grass, the black shapes of wardens running, lanterns swinging. The fifth stake tore loose and the whole structure screamed, a high thin note like a bell cracking, and Sera threw herself at the sixth stake with both hands and ripped it from the ground as the nothing closed over her head—

—and spat her out into wet grass under open sky, rolling, coming up on her knees with her palms blistered and smoking, ten paces from a ring of wardens with drawn null-blades and Aldric Voss standing behind them with his sword still sheathed, because Aldric Voss never drew until the arithmetic favored it.

"Sera." His voice was almost gentle. "Come back inside. We'll call it a misunderstanding."

"You filed me," she said. She got her feet under her. Behind the wardens, the collapsed fold was still settling—a shimmer in the air, a place where the fog moved wrong—and beyond it, downhill, the faint bronze voice of the crossroads bell, ringing and ringing, calling her home the way bells call everything. "You audited me. You put twelve thousand people in a ledger and drew lines through their names and called it peace." She backed toward the shimmer, toward the tear, toward the thin place where the world hadn't finished deciding what it was. "Here's my arithmetic, Aldric. Two children and a storm-caller against your entire tidy empire. I know which side pays."

"Serene—"

"My name," she said, stepping backward into the shimmer, feeling it take her like cold water, like a door opening, "is Sera Vane. Look it up."

The world folded. The wardens' shouts cut off mid-word. And then she was stumbling out the other side into ordinary fog, ordinary night, her hands burned and bleeding and shaking, with the bell tolling somewhere ahead of her and the whole monstrous ledger of the Chancery burning behind her eyes.

She ran toward the bell. She ran toward Noor and Tam, and the map, and the Door with its terrible grammar—*restore or release, one or the other*—and she ran fastest of all toward the moment she would have to tell them what she'd learned: that the country they were trying to save had been spent, deliberately, cheaply, and that the price of saving it might be the girl leading them there.

Behind her, far off in the fog, a horn sounded once—the Chancery, mobilizing.

Ahead, the bell rang on, and beneath its bronze voice, faintly, impossibly, Sera began to hear another sound: a child's voice, high and clear, singing names into the dark.
