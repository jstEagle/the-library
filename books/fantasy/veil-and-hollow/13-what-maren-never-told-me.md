## 13. What Maren Never Told Me

The peddler came out of the fog the way all things came out of the fog in the Hollow—slowly, half-suggested, as if the world had to be talked into admitting he existed.

Noor saw him first from the ridge above the watchtower, where she'd gone to be alone with the map and the ache of everything it meant. He was a thin figure leading a mule that seemed more memory than animal, its coat patched with colors that didn't quite agree with one another. Bells hung from the mule's harness—small ones, tin-bright—and they rang with a sound that made Tam's stories rise unbidden in her chest: *a river of bells, a city with brass doors.*

She told herself not to read meaning into a mule's harness. She was getting worse at telling herself things.

By the time she reached the ruined tower where Sera stood watch, the peddler had already arrived, and Sera had already put a knife to his throat, because that was how Sera greeted the world now.

"Easy," the man said, hands raised, palms out. He had a face like crumpled paper and eyes the color of weak tea, and he looked at the blade the way a man looks at weather—unimpressed, faintly tired. "If I were one of them, girl, I'd have brought more than a mule."

"Chancery wardens don't need an army," Sera said. "They need one good knife."

"And yet here I am, unarmed, selling buttons." He smiled with too many teeth. "I'm called Odd Wexler. I trade the fringes of the veil—the places the world forgot to finish. And I've come a long way through some very unfriendly country to deliver a letter."

"A letter," Noor repeated.

"To Noor Zhou." His tea-colored eyes found her, and something in them sharpened, quick and knowing, before going soft again. "From Maren Ashwell, herbalist, of the village in the Hollow. Who said—" he closed his eyes, reciting, "—*'Tell her it's from home. She'll know what I mean.'*"

The word *home* went through her like a splinter finding old wood.

Sera hadn't lowered her knife. "How does a fringe-peddler get letters out of a valley that's being erased?"

"The same way I get letters into it." Wexler patted the mule's neck, and the little bells chimed their almost-familiar chime. "The veil has seams, knight. Always has. The Chancery sewed this one shut, but thread wears. And there are those of us who've spent our lives walking the worn places." He turned back to Noor, and his voice gentled into something almost kind. "She paid me in dried feverfew and a warning. Said you'd want to read it alone. Said also—" the smile flickered, "—that you'd argue about reading it alone, so I'm to tell you that arguing wastes daylight, and daylight is shorter than you think."

*Daylight is shorter than you think.* The words settled over Noor like cold water.

"I'll read it here," she said.

"Noor," Sera started.

"She said alone."

"She also said you'd say that."

"Then we're both doing what Maren expected," Noor said, "which should tell you something about how well she knows me, and how little I know her."

That landed harder than she meant it to. Sera studied her for a long moment—the knight's gray eyes doing their quiet arithmetic—and then she stepped back, sheathing the knife with a small, deliberate click. "Wexler. You'll tell me what routes you walked, and when, and who else is walking them. That's the price of your throat staying whole."

"Reasonable," Wexler agreed cheerfully, and led the mule toward the tower's far side, already talking, his voice dropping into the cadence of a man who had traded information so long it had replaced conversation entirely.

Noor took the letter.

It was wrapped in oilcloth, tied with the green twine Maren used for bundling herbs, and the knot was the one Maren tied on jars of willowbark tincture—the double loop, the little tail tucked under. Nine years of muscle memory lived in Noor's fingers, and they knew that knot the way they knew the latch on Maren's door, the weight of the good kettle, the exact pressure of the old woman's hand on her forehead when fever came.

Her hands shook. She made them stop. They shook anyway.

She broke the twine.

* * *

Maren's handwriting was exactly as Noor remembered it: small, slanted, unhurried, the letters of a woman who wrote prescriptions by candlelight and could not afford ambiguity. But the first line stopped her breath.

*You'll be angry with me. Read to the end anyway. You never could stay angry past the third paragraph, even at nine years old with a broken arm I probably shouldn't have set myself.*

*I knew who you were before you woke up.*

Noor sat down on a fallen lintel without deciding to. The stone was cold through her clothes. Somewhere behind her, Wexler's voice murmured on, and the mule's bells chimed softly, and none of it seemed quite real against the black ink marching across the page.

*The night the fog brought you, I was awake because the shrine bell rang without wind. Old women don't sleep well, but we wake for reasons. I went out expecting a storm. What I found was a girl facedown in the bellflower bed with no name in her and a storm still living under her skin—I could feel it, child, the way you feel heat off a banked fire. It wasn't dying. It was waiting.*

*I had seen that storm once before. Thirty years ago, when I was young and foolish and traveled farther than old women are supposed to remember. I saw it over the capital, the night the sky tore open and the Chancery's null-engines burned themselves hollow trying to swallow it. Everyone who saw that storm died or fled or was made to forget. I fled. I have been ashamed of it for thirty years, which is a very efficient way to stay alive and a very poor way to sleep.*

*So when the fog gave you to my garden, I knew two things. I knew you were the Stormcaller. And I knew that if I ever told anyone—if I ever let the council know, or let a single word slip past my teeth—the Chancery would come for you, and they would not stop at you. They would take the whole valley apart looking for what made you.*

*I chose to keep you. Not out of pity. Don't go thinking it was pity; you'd hate that, and you'd be right. I kept you because I looked at you lying in my flowers and I thought: here is someone who did a terrible enormous thing and paid for it with herself, and everyone else who ever met her ran. Someone in this world ought to stay.*

Then a gap, a smear where something had been blotted—a tear, or rain, or ink thinned deliberately—and then:

*Tam's dreams started when he was four. Brass doors. A river of bells. Names of streets no map in the Hollow holds. I knew what that meant too, though I prayed I was wrong: the veil was thinner than I'd hoped, and it leaks both ways. The erased leak into us. We leak into them. I taught him lullabies from a country I'd only seen burning, and he hummed them back to me like he'd written them, and I sat by his bed some nights and cried into my apron where he couldn't see, because I understood that the world outside had done something so thorough, so *tidy*, that a whole nation survived only as a child's bad dreams.*

*And then you came humming the same tune in your fever, and I thought: fate has a sense of humor, and it is cruel, and I will laugh at it anyway because the alternative is despair.*

Noor pressed the page flat against her knee. Her vision blurred and cleared, blurred and cleared.

She remembered the fevers. The first year, especially—waking soaked and screaming in a bed that smelled of yarrow and beeswax, with Maren's hand cool on her brow and Maren's voice low and steady, saying nothing, asking nothing, just *there*, hour after hour. She had assumed the old woman was simply tireless. Kind in the undemanding way of healers.

She had never once wondered why Maren asked no questions. Why the whole household asked no questions. Why a valley of exiles, each with their own wounds and silences, had simply folded a nameless storm-touched stranger into their lives and never once, in nine years, demanded her story.

Because Maren had known the story. Had carried it alone, all this time, so that no one else would ever have to carry it—or use it.

*Here is the part you'll be angry about,* the letter went on, and Noor laughed—a cracked, wet sound—involuntary, because of course Maren knew, of course she'd mapped Noor's anger the way she mapped dosage. *When the council voted to seal the valley, I voted with them. I told them the map was dangerous. I helped draft the order to burn it. I did it because I believed it—that keeping you hidden was keeping you safe. I was wrong, and I was wrong for nine years, and hiding you was just another way of erasing you, and I of all people should have known better.*

*The Hollow is coming apart, love. I can see it from my garden. The fence posts at the east field go transparent at dawn. The Hendersons' boy walked through the market square last week and three people couldn't see him until noon. Whatever you're doing out there—whatever that knight and that map are pulling you toward—do it fast, and then come home, or don't come home, but whatever you choose, choose it as yourself. Not as the girl I decided you had to be to survive. As Noor. Whoever she turns out to be, I already love her. I've had nine years of practice.*

*P.S. There's a jar of feverfew salve in the left pocket of your pack that I put there the night you left, which you haven't found yet because you're hopeless about pockets. Your shoulder will ache on the road north. Use it.*

*P.P.S. Tell Tam the bells under the dry river are tuned to A. He'll know what it means. He always knew more than any of us.*

*M.A.*

Noor read it again. Then a third time, slower, the way you drink something precious when you suspect it's the last of its kind.

The sun had moved. Wexler had stopped talking; she heard him rummaging among his wares, the small commerce sounds of a man giving her the room she needed without pretending he wasn't aware of her. Sera stood at the tower's broken parapet, facing outward, pointedly watching the fog line, giving Noor her back—which from Sera Vane was nearly a tenderness.

*Chosen twice.* The phrase arrived in her mind fully formed, the way important things do, wearing no explanation. Once by fate—the storm, the fog, the bellflower bed, the fever that dumped her at the feet of the one woman in the valley who recognized what slept beneath her skin. And once by love—Maren's deliberate, costly, thirty-years-guilty decision to lie to a whole community, to carry a secret heavier than any herb basket, to fold a weapon of national destruction into her household and call her *love* across the breakfast table.

Noor had spent nine years believing she had been taken in out of charity. Charity was bearable. Charity was a debt you could repay with roof repairs and firewood and silence. But this—this was different. This meant every kindness had been a choice made with open eyes. Every night Maren sat with her through the fevers, she had known she was nursing the storm that had once torn the sky over a capital city. Every time she'd said *child* in that dry, fond voice, she'd been speaking to the most dangerous person in the world, and choosing, again, each morning, each evening, each ordinary day, not to be afraid.

Being truly known, Noor discovered, felt less like being seen and more like being set down. Like a pack taken off shoulders she'd forgotten were aching.

She was crying. She let it happen, briefly, privately, the tears running hot into the collar of her shirt while the fog breathed around the ruin and the mule's bells chimed their almost-remembered chime. Then she wiped her face on her sleeve, folded the letter along Maren's creases, and tucked it inside her shirt, against her sternum, where it could sit like a second heartbeat.

"Pockets," she muttered, and dug into the left pocket of her pack.

The salve jar was there. Of course it was there. Small, wax-sealed, labeled in Maren's slanted hand: *for the road north — apply generously, you never do.* Noor held it in both hands and laughed again, helplessly, and this time the laugh didn't crack.

"You're smiling," Sera observed, without turning around. "Either the letter was good news or you've finally lost your mind. Given the week, I genuinely can't guess."

"Maren knew." Noor stood, tucking the jar away properly this time. "Who I was. From the first night. Before I woke up. She knew, and she took me in anyway, and she lied to the whole valley for nine years to keep the Chancery from ever hearing my name." She looked down at her hands—at the faint tremor that lived in them now, the storm's residue, the thing she had hated and hidden since the day she understood it existed. "She touched these hands every day for nine years, Sera. She bandaged them. She knew what they'd done."

Sera turned then. The knight's face did its usual work of revealing nothing, but her eyes—her eyes had that stillness they got when something struck close to bone. "And?" she said quietly. "What do you do with that?"

"I don't know yet." Noor looked north, where the fog lay thick over the road they'd have to take at dawn—the road toward the drowned capital, toward the Door, toward whatever waited at the center of a country mid-unmaking. "But I know she was wrong about one thing. She said hiding me was another way of erasing me. Maybe. But she also kept me *real*. Nine years of soup and scolding and setting broken bones—that's not erasure. That's a life. She built me a life inside the lie, and she did it alone, and she never once asked me to thank her or explain myself." Noor swallowed. "I owe her a country, Sera. Or at least the attempt at one."

"That's a heavy debt to service on an empty stomach," said Wexler, emerging around the tower's base with the mule in tow. He was watching her with those sharp-then-soft eyes, and now he reached into his coat and produced, with theatrical reluctance, a second object. "Which brings me to the other thing she paid me to carry. Not a letter. A message. Verbal." He cleared his throat, and when he spoke again, the trader's patter had fallen away entirely, leaving something older and graver underneath. "She said: *Tell her the fence posts aren't the worst of it. Tell her the fog is starting to forget which side of the veil it belongs to. Tell her the edges go first, and then the names, and then the people. Tell her she has days, not weeks. And tell her—*" he paused, and for a moment the crumpled-paper face looked almost pained, "*—tell her I'm sorry I waited so long to trust her with the truth. Tell her a mother's job is to make the world safe for her children, and I got it backwards, and I hope she finds it in herself to forgive an old woman who was afraid.*"

The wind moved through the ruined tower, and somewhere far off—farther than sound should travel, deeper than air—Noor heard a bell ring. One note. Low. Patient. The same note she'd been humming in her sleep for three nights without knowing it.

Tam's city, calling.

"Days," Sera repeated flatly. "Not weeks." She was already moving, already buckling her sword-belt tighter, the knight reassembling herself around a new urgency. "Then we leave within the hour. Wexler—you'll guide us to the seam you used coming in. If the veil is failing, the Chancery will know it too, and they'll be moving on the Door."

"They're already moving," Wexler said simply. "I passed their outriders two nights ago. Null-engines on wagons. A lot of them." He glanced at Noor, and something in his tea-colored eyes was almost apologetic. "They weren't heading for the Door, mind. They were heading for the village. For the people. Seems the Chancery's arithmetic doesn't tolerate loose ends—even ones mid-unmaking."

The cold that went through Noor then had nothing to do with the fog.

Maren. The Hendersons' invisible boy. The whole flickering, fraying, beloved valley full of people who had chosen her twice without knowing it—

"We're not going around them," Noor heard herself say. Her voice came out level, and underneath the levelness, deep as the buried bells, something vast turned over and began to wake. "We're going *through*."

Sera looked at her. Looked at her hands.

For the first time since the watchtower, the knight smiled—and it was not a comforting smile. "Good," she said. "I was hoping you'd say that."
