## 14. The Fraying

Tam woke to the sound of the world forgetting itself.

It was not a loud sound. That was the worst part. It was a softness at the edges of everything, like a page left out in rain—the ink of the morning blurring, the letters of familiar things running together. He lay in his narrow bed under the eaves of Maren's house and listened to the rooster crow, except the crow came a half-second after the rooster opened its beak, as if the valley had to think for a moment before it remembered what a rooster was supposed to sound like.

He got up and pressed his palm against the window glass. Outside, the fog had come down into the village.

Not *near* the village. Not curling at the borders where it belonged, where it had always stayed, a gray wall holding the Hollow in its cupped hands. This fog was in the lanes. It pooled in the wheel ruts like spilled milk. It hung in the branches of the plum tree by the well, and when Tam breathed on the window and wiped a circle clear, he saw Mrs. Aldery standing in her doorway with a broom, sweeping at the mist as though she could push it back down her front step.

The fog did not move for her. But it moved for him—or rather, it *hesitated*, the way a dog hesitates at a door it has been beaten behind. Tam had noticed that about himself lately. The veil-stuff knew him. He had been born on the far side of it, in a country that no longer existed, and some crumb of that country still lived in him like a seed in winter ground, and the fog recognized the shape of it.

"Tam!" Maren's voice from below, thinner than usual. "Come help me with the shutters."

He went down. Maren stood in the kitchen with her sleeves rolled, and Tam saw—because he had learned to see it now, the way you learn to see the water level dropping in a well—that her outline flickered. Just slightly. Just at the edges, where her gray hair met the dim air behind her. For half a heartbeat she was not quite there, and the cupboard behind her showed through the place where her shoulder should have been, and then she was solid again and scowling at him as if he were slow.

"Don't stare," she said. "Fetch the hammer."

"The fog's inside the fence," he said.

"I know what the fog is doing." She took the hammer herself, which told him how frightened she was; Maren never did work that could be given away. "I've known since the night your grandmother's grave went soft."

Tam stopped on the stairs. "Went soft?"

"Went *unwritten*." She said it the way you'd say a diagnosis. "The stone is still there. The hole is still there. But the grass over it—" Her jaw worked. "The grass has forgotten which way is up. It grows sideways. I cut it yesterday and this morning it lies flat again, all of it pointing east, toward the border, like iron filings near a lodestone. Like it's trying to leave."

* * *

By noon the whole village had seen it, and by afternoon the whole village had stopped pretending they hadn't.

It started small, the way rot starts small. The easternmost house—the old Cooper place, empty nine years—simply wasn't there anymore. Not fallen, not burned, not collapsed. *Absent.* Tam walked out with a crowd of others to look at the space where it had been, and the space was worse than ruins would have been. Ruins have a story. This was a story torn out of a book, leaving only the smooth stub of the binding. There was a rectangle of paler ground where the foundations had kept the sun off the soil, and a fence post standing with nothing to fence, and everyone who looked at it felt their eyes slide off, the way eyes slide off a word misspelled too many times.

"It's the veil," said Old Hesk, the ferryman, and spat. "It's coming apart at the seams. Nine years it held, and now—"

"Now the one who made it is walking away from it," someone said, and every head turned toward the road south, the road Noor had taken with the knight and with Tam's own secret riding beside her like a bird in his chest.

Tam said nothing. He had promised. He had sworn on the map itself, on Ilya's dying ink, that he would keep what he knew until Noor came back or sent word. But keeping the secret had gotten heavier lately, the way a bucket gets heavier the longer you carry it, and the reason it was heavy was this:

He was forgetting.

It happened in small thefts. He had known the city the way you know your own hands—brass doors gone green at the seams, the river that ran through it ringing with bells sunk along its bed so that the water itself made music, the names of the districts recited by children jumping rope. *Ashfall, Bellrun, the Lantern Quarter, the Weeping Stair.* He had taught them to Noor on the road, sung them to her in Sera's grudging campfire light, and she had listened with an expression he couldn't read then but understood now: hunger. The hunger of someone who had lost something and didn't know what.

And last week, jumping rope alone in the yard because there was no one left his age who wanted to play the old games, he had come to the third district and found nothing there. A hole in the rhyme. *Ashfall, Bellrun, ———, the Weeping Stair.* He had stood with the rope hanging dead in his hands and felt the hole like a missing tooth, his tongue going again and again to the gap.

This morning it was the river. He could remember that the river rang. He could remember *that* he remembered the sound of it. But the sound itself was gone, scooped out of him like the flesh of a fruit, and when he tried to hum it all that came out was the melody Noor had caught herself humming on the road—which was close, which was nearly it, but was a copy of a copy, and getting fainter.

* * *

The peddler came on the fourth day.

No one could explain how he'd gotten in; the borders had been impassable for years, and now they were apparently porous as wet paper, which was its own kind of terror. He was a stooped man with a handcart full of ribbons and tin whistles and dried fish, and he walked up the high street calling his wares in a voice worn smooth as river stone, and when he reached Maren's gate he stopped and drew out a letter sealed with plain tallow instead of wax.

"For the herbalist," he said. "From a girl on the road. Storm-colored eyes. She paid me in silver that wasn't minted anywhere I know of, and I'm not asking questions, because the world's gone strange enough that asking seems rude."

Maren read the letter at the kitchen table with Tam hovering, and he watched her face do several things in quick succession—soften, sharpen, go pale, go grim—and then she held it out to him without a word.

*Tam,* the letter began, in Noor's careful, cramped hand, the hand of someone who had learned to write late and never trusted it. *If the fog is in the streets, don't wait for us. Get everyone to the shrine at the valley's heart—Ilya's map says the old capital's roads all pass beneath it, and the Door will open from either side. I'm coming. Sera says three days, maybe four. Tell them the country isn't dying. Tell them it's being let go, and there's a difference, and the difference is us.*

*Tell Tam I'm carrying his city. Every name. Make him teach me the ones I'll get wrong.*

*M.—N.*

Tam read it four times. On the fourth reading his eyes snagged on the signature, two letters tangled together, and something in his chest cracked open—not painfully, exactly. More like ice cracking on a river in spring: the sound of a thing that had been frozen deciding to move.

"She remembers," he said. His voice came out strange. "She's remembering."

"She's remembering *for* you, by the look of it." Maren folded the letter with the tenderness she usually spent only on herbs. "That girl is carrying more than we ever asked her to."

"We didn't ask her anything. She just—" Tam stopped. Swallowed. "She just picked us up. All of us. Like picking up something dropped in the road."

"That's what some people are for," Maren said. "Now pack. You heard her. The shrine."

* * *

They gathered at dusk, the whole village, in the square by the well, and Tam stood on the well's lip because he was small and light and it was the highest thing handy, and he told them. Not everything—he kept Ilya's death and the wardens and the null-blade that had grazed his arm for later, or maybe forever—but the shape of it. A country erased. A Door beneath their feet. A girl walking back toward them through the unraveling world with their whole history held in her two scarred hands.

"They'll want proof," said Old Hesk.

"The fog is proof," said Mrs. Aldery. "My house is proof. My *garden* is proof—it's forgotten how to grow beans, Hesk, beans, the stupidest plant there is—"

"And even if it weren't," said a voice from the back, and Tam's heart lurched, because it was Jory the smith, who had never once in Tam's life agreed with anyone about anything, "even if it weren't, the boy remembers a city none of us can name, and I've watched him try. I've watched the trying hurt him. That's proof enough of something."

So they went. A ragged procession up the darkening lanes, carrying what could be carried, leaving behind houses that might not exist by morning, and Tam walked at the front beside Maren and tried very hard not to think about the rope rhyme, and failed, and found that now two districts were gone. *Ashfall, ———, ———, the Weeping Stair.*

"Maren," he said quietly. "If I forget it all before she comes—"

"You won't."

"But if I do." He made himself say it. "Then it's only in her head. One person. One person holding a whole country, and she doesn't even know why it matters to her yet. That's not fair. That's—" His voice wobbled and he hated it. "That's too heavy for anybody."

Maren stopped walking. Around them the procession flowed past like a river around a stone, and she took his face in both her herb-cracked hands and looked at him the way she looked at a fever she intended to break.

"Listen to me, Tam of the Bellrun district." The name landed in him like a struck bell, and he realized she had been saving it, hoarding it, feeding it back to him drop by drop for weeks. "You are not the only vessel this country has. Do you understand? It's in the stones of the shrine. It's in the graves. It's in the way Hesk still poles his ferry across dry water every morning because his hands remember a river. Countries don't live in one head. They live in *habits*. And habits are stubborn." She kissed his forehead, once, hard. "Now walk."

* * *

The shrine, when they reached it near midnight, was glowing.

Faintly. Wrongly. The old stones that Noor had been found against nine years ago gave off a light like moonlight remembered rather than seen, and the fog stood around the clearing in a perfect ring, not entering, not retreating—waiting. Watching. Tam felt the hair rise on his arms, and felt, beneath the fear, something else: recognition. The fog knew this place too. Whatever the shrine was, it was a knot in the veil, a stitch in the wound, and the wound was pulling at its stitches now, and everyone in the clearing could feel the tug.

They settled in around fires that burned with flames that flickered a half-second out of time, and Tam sat with his knees drawn up and watched the ring of fog, and somewhere past midnight he felt it happen.

A name, slipping loose.

He grabbed for it the way you grab for a dream on waking, and caught only the shape of where it had been. The fifth district. Or the sixth. A name that meant something about water, or about birds, or about both. Gone. He sat very still in the firelight and counted what remained, the way a miser counts coins in a failing light, and when he finished counting he got up and crossed the clearing to where Maren sat sleepless, and knelt, and said:

"When she comes—when Noor comes—I want to give it to her. All of it. What's left."

"She wouldn't take it as a burden, Tam."

"I know." He looked at his hands, at the faint pale line on his forearm where the null-blade had grazed him, a scar that itched whenever he almost-remembered. "That's why it's safe with her. She already carries things she doesn't understand and doesn't put them down. I've watched her." He swallowed. "But I want to say it while I still know what *it* is. I want to hand it over myself. Not have it fall out of me like—like the Cooper house. Not be *absent*. Given. Given is different."

Maren was quiet a long moment. When she spoke, her voice was rough.

"That's the bravest thing I've heard anyone say in nine years of brave people," she said. "And I include the girl with the storm in her hands."

* * *

She came at dawn on the third day, and Tam knew before anyone shouted, because the fog ring shivered.

It parted like a curtain drawn by a careful hand, and Noor walked through it with Sera Vane at her shoulder and exhaustion written all over her like a second skin, and Tam ran. He didn't decide to run; his body simply committed treason against dignity and went, across the trampled grass, and crashed into her hard enough to stagger her, and she caught him and held on, and over her shoulder he saw Sera look away with the awkward tenderness of soldiers everywhere.

"You're smaller than I remembered," Noor said into his hair, "and I saw you four days ago."

"You're *more*," he said, because it was true; something in her had filled in, some hollowed place bricked over, and when she pulled back he saw it in her eyes—she knew things now. Terrible, necessary things. "Noor. Before anything else. Before the Door, before all of it." He made his voice steady. He had practiced. "I want to give you my city. While I still have it to give. All the names, all of it, everything that's left in me—you carry it, and I'll carry whatever you need carried, and that way it's not lost, it's *handed*. Given is different."

Noor went very still. Then she knelt, right there in the mud and the ash of the dying fires, so that their eyes were level, and she took both his hands in hers, and he felt it—the storm in her, quiet as a held breath, leaning close to listen.

"Teach me," she said. "Every wrong note included."

And Tam opened his mouth to begin—to begin the recitation that was also a farewell, the prayer that was also a handing-over—and got no further. Because behind him, across the clearing, the shrine stones flared white as a struck match, and the ring of fog screamed—a soundless scream, felt in the teeth rather than heard—and beyond it, far off down the southern road, came a sound Tam had heard only once before, on the worst night of his life, and had prayed never to hear again.

Null-engines. Many of them. And they were no longer following.

They had arrived.
