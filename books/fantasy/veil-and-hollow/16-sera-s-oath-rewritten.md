## 16. Sera's Oath, Rewritten

The Chancery came at dawn, and they came the way the Chancery did everything: in perfect, terrible order.

Sera heard them before she saw them—the rhythmic toll of null-engines being wheeled across the drowned plain, each strike a small amputation of sound. The river of bells went quiet around them, note by note, as though the water itself were being taught silence. She stood at the mouth of the flooded avenue with her sword drawn and her back to the brass Door, and she counted.

Twelve wardens in grey lacquer. Two null-engines on iron wheels, their barrels wrapped in chains of cold sigils. And at their head, riding a horse too fine for the mud, Commander Elara Voss—her commander, once; the woman who had pinned the silver oath-clasp to Sera's throat when she was nineteen and foolish and so desperately hungry to matter.

"Vane." Voss's voice carried across the water without effort, the voice of a woman who had never once needed to raise it. "You're standing in the wrong place."

"I'm standing exactly where I should be," Sera said. "That's rather the point."

Behind her, she could feel Noor at the Door. She didn't turn—she wouldn't give Voss the satisfaction—but she could sense her there, a pressure like the air before lightning, the storm-magic that had been bleeding out of Noor all night as the Door pried her memories loose one by one. Tam was there too. She could hear him, faintly, still singing the city's names under his breath, his voice cracked and thin but stubborn as a weed through pavement. *Aldermere. The Brass Quarter. The Street of Nine Fountains.* A child holding up a country with nothing but a song.

"You were the best of us," Voss said, and there it was—the kindness, deployed like a blade laid flat against skin. "I wrote your commendation after Corvane. I argued for you when the tribunal wanted you broken for refusing the purge order. Do you remember what I told them? I said: *Vane is not soft. Vane is precise. She'll come to understand that mercy is just arithmetic done badly.*"

"I remember," Sera said. "I also remember what was in the wagons we were ordered to burn."

The words landed. She watched them land—a flicker in Voss's jaw, quickly mastered. Good. Let it cost her something, even a flicker.

"The Hollow is dying," Voss said, gesturing with one gauntleted hand at the dissolving skyline, the buildings going translucent at their edges like breath fading from glass. "It has been dying for nine years. The girl can hold the veil, restore the country, seal the wound—and the Chancery will honor it. Full restoration. Recognition. The maps redrawn." She smiled, and it was almost warm. "All she has to do is become the Door. You know this. Ilya told her. So why are you standing between us and the only mercy on offer?"

"Because I've read your arithmetic before," Sera said. "And there's always a remainder."

* * *

She had learned arithmetic from Voss, that was the bitter thing. Learned it in the marsh-forts along the Chancery's southern border, where the veil-born were catalogued and contained. Learned that every act of cruelty could be made to sound like bookkeeping if you kept your voice level enough. *Three hundred contained, no casualties. The purge was necessary. The alternative was worse.* Sera had recited those numbers herself, once, in a report she still dreamed about. Then came the order that had ended her—the purge of the Hollow's border villages, the one she'd refused, the one that got her oath struck from the rolls and her name from the monuments.

She had thought, for nine years, that she'd left the Chancery behind. Standing here now, she understood she had simply been walking ahead of it.

"Here's the sum, then," Voss said, and now she stopped pretending. Her hand rose, and the wardens' null-blades came free of their scabbards in unison, a sound like teeth. "The girl restores the Hollow. The Hollow stands again. And in five years, or ten, or fifty—when the Chancery's war requires it, when some new threat needs paying for—we come back, and we finish what we started. Because that country was erased for a reason, Vane. It was the price of peace, and prices don't stop being owed because the debtor finds a sword."

There it was. The cruelty added, folded into the bargain like arsenic into bread.

"And you're telling her this," Sera said quietly, "because you want me to hear it."

"I'm telling you this because you're the last honest person she knows. Convince her to take the bargain. Save her from becoming a door." Voss's eyes, pale and patient, moved past Sera's shoulder toward the Door itself. "Or stand there until we cut you down, and we do it anyway. Those are the terms. I've always been fair with you."

"Fair," Sera repeated. The word tasted like ash and old blood. "You erased a country, Elara. An entire country. Children. Gardens. A river full of bells." Her voice didn't shake. Nine years of exile had burned the shaking out of her. "And you call it fairness because you filed the paperwork."

"The alternative was a war that would have killed millions."

"Yes," Sera said. "Someone always says that. That's how I know it's true—that someone always says it."

* * *

The first engine fired without warning.

Sera felt it as a hole opening in the world—a lance of pure negation that turned the rain to dust midair and struck the Door's threshold three feet to her left, close enough that the null-light licked across her cheek and left the skin numb, dead, briefly *unwritten*. She threw herself sideways, rolled through shallow water, came up swinging.

The wardens advanced in their perfect line, blades low, boots finding the drowned cobbles without a single misstep. Twelve of them. She'd fought six once, in the marshes, and it had nearly killed her. Behind her, Tam's song faltered—and the whole fraying city shuddered, buildings flickering like guttering candles—and she heard Noor shout his name, and the song picked up again, ragged but alive.

So Sera did the thing she had spent nine years not doing.

She turned her back on the Chancery—not away from them, but *on* them, deliberately, insolently, the way you turn your back on something that no longer has power over you—and she raised her sword, and she said, loud enough for every warden to hear, loud enough for the record:

"My name is Sera Vane. Knight-errant, formerly of the Order of the Silver Oath. I took an oath to protect the helpless from precisely this." She pointed her blade down the line of grey lacquer, at Voss, at the engines, at everything the Chancery was. "You struck my name from your rolls years ago. Fine. Consider this my resignation from yours."

Then she moved.

The first warden died surprised. The second died angry. The third and fourth came together, blades crossing, null-light screaming off the steel, and Sera gave ground step by step through the floodwater, giving nothing else. They had trained her. That was the tragedy and the advantage both—she knew their forms the way she knew her own hands, knew that the fifth form always overcommitted on the left, knew that wardens drilled against knights but never against *ex*-knights who had nine years of desperation to sharpen them.

A blade caught her shoulder. Null-fire bit through mail and flesh alike, and her whole arm went cold and far away, and she killed the man who'd done it anyway, because love is not always gentle and duty, she was learning, had never once been kind.

"VANE!" Voss's voice cracked across the battlefield like a whip. "Last chance! Stand down and be restored! Your rank, your honor, your *name*—"

Sera laughed. It hurt to laugh; something in her ribs was probably broken. She stood in the drowning light of the dissolving city, blood diluting pink in the floodwater, an old scarred woman with a borrowed sword, and she laughed.

"My name," she called back, "was never yours to give."

* * *

They might have taken her, in the end. Twelve was twelve, and her arm was failing, and the null-wound was spreading its numbness toward her heart. She had backed herself to the Door's threshold, buying seconds, buying Tam's song another verse, another line of names—*the Weavers' Stair, the Lantern Bridge, Aldermere, Aldermere*—and the engines were reloading, and Voss was raising her hand for the final volley—

And the sky answered.

Not thunder. Something older than thunder. The clouds above the drowned capital tore open along a seam of gold, and Noor Zhou walked out of the Door with nine years of stolen storm burning in her hands, and every hair on Sera's body stood on end, and the floodwater around her ankles began, impossibly, to rise upward.

"Noor—" Sera started, meaning *don't*, meaning *you don't have to*, meaning a hundred things a knight has no right to say.

But Noor wasn't looking at the Chancery. She was looking at Sera—at the blood, the ruined shoulder, the sword still raised—and something passed across her face that Sera would spend the rest of her life trying to name. Not pity. Not gratitude. *Recognition*, maybe. One person who had paid everything seeing another who had.

"Get him behind me," Noor said.

Sera looked at Voss one last time. Her commander stood amid her perfect formation, and for the first time in all the years Sera had known her, Elara Voss looked uncertain—an arithmetic problem that refused to resolve.

"Sera," Voss said, and her voice was almost gentle. "Don't do this. Whatever you think she is—whatever you think *you* are—it isn't worth—"

"It's worth everything," Sera said. "That's the whole lesson, Elara. It was always worth everything. You just never learned to count that high."

She turned her back on the Order of the Silver Oath for the second and final time, and limped to where Tam stood singing, and put her good hand on the boy's shoulder, and together they watched Noor Zhou raise her hands against the engines, against the wardens, against nine years of tidy monstrous arithmetic—

—and behind them all, deep beneath the water, the river of bells began to ring.
