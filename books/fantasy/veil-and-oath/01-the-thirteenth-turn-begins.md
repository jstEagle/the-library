## 1. The Thirteenth Turn Begins

The moon-iron sang in Ansel's pack, the way it always did on the high passes—a thin, fretful ringing just below hearing, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. Seven years of carrying it had taught Ansel to ignore the sound. Tonight, for the first time since Mother Hessel's cottage had shrunk behind them into the pines, the singing changed key.

Ansel stopped on the switchback above Greywater Saddle and listened.

Below, the Thornfell mountains unrolled themselves in folds of blue-black shadow, the passes stitched through them like veins of silver where the last light caught the frost. Somewhere down there was Lowmarsh, and somewhere beyond that, a hundred villages that had never heard Ansel Vane's name and never would. That was the whole architecture of their life: to be a rumor with legs, a weight on the road that left no print. The smugglers called people like Ansel *dustwalkers*—those who moved through the mountain's blind spots, known only by what went missing from one valley and turned up in another.

Ansel had been very good at being no one. It was the first thing they'd taught themselves after running, before even learning which mushrooms killed and which merely blinded: *be forgettable, be useful, be gone.*

The moon-iron rang again, higher now, urgent as a struck bell.

"Quiet," Ansel murmured, shifting the pack's straps across shoulders stiff with three days of climbing. "You'll wake something."

It was a joke. It had been a joke for seven years.

The pass narrowed ahead, walls of granite leaning together like conspirators, and Ansel slipped into the gap with the ease of long habit—left foot on the flat stone, right hand finding the cold iron ring bolted there by some forgotten mason, three steps down and around the shoulder of rock where the wind couldn't follow. The smuggler roads were full of such courtesies, small kindnesses carved by generations of people who needed the mountain to keep their secrets. Ansel knew all of them. Knew which cairns marked safe water and which marked graves; knew that the third boulder past Widow's Rest hummed if you touched it with an open palm; knew that you never, ever prayed out loud on the high roads.

That rule had been old when Ansel was young. Older than the trade routes. Older, the oldest smugglers said, than the forgetting itself.

Because the gods of the Thornfell were dead—or as good as dead—and dead things heard prayers the way drowned men hear voices calling across water. You whispered a wish on a mountain road, and something might turn over in its long sleep and listen. And then you'd have its attention. And attention, on the Thornfell, was debt.

Ansel was halfway through the narrows when the traveler appeared below.

He came up the switchbacks slowly, a drover by his coat, leading a mule whose breath steamed in the deepening cold. Ordinary. The kind of man Ansel passed a dozen times a season without a second glance—the kind who never looked up at ledges, never noticed the extra weight of a pack that shouldn't exist. Ansel pressed back against the granite out of pure reflex anyway, letting the mountain's shadow fold over them, and watched him come.

The drover stopped at the little shrine.

Every pass in the Thornfell had one: a niche cut into the rock, holding a figure so weathered that rain and lichen had eaten away everything except a suggestion of shoulders and a tilted head. Nobody remembered which god it had been. That was rather the point. The shrines were like milestones marking the distance to a country that no longer existed. Travelers touched them for luck without knowing why, the way you might pat the flank of a horse that had carried your grandfather.

The drover set down his mule's lead. He took off his hat. And then he knelt.

Ansel frowned. That was already wrong—grown men didn't kneel at the way shrines, not anymore, not in thirty years. But it was what came next that made the cold crawl up Ansel's spine and settle at the base of the skull like a hand.

The man whispered.

Ansel couldn't hear the words. The wind took them, or the rock did—but the shape of them rose anyway, soft and pleading, a child's cadence in a grown man's mouth. *Please. Please. She's all I have.* A prayer. A real one, offered with the naked desperation people used to carry to temples, back when temples still answered.

And the shrine lit.

Not flared—*lit*, the way an ember lights when breathed upon. A thread of pale silver woke inside the weathered stone, tracing the worn lines of the figure's face, its shoulders, the tilt of its head, until the whole niche glowed like a window into somewhere else. Frost bloomed outward from it across the granite in feathered whorls. The air filled with a smell Ansel hadn't encountered in seven years and had never once missed: crushed yarrow and cold iron and something underneath like snow about to fall.

Mother Hessel's workroom had smelled like that.

The moon-iron in Ansel's pack screamed—a clear, bright note this time, audible, undeniable—and the drover's head snapped up, searching the rocks for the source. Ansel held absolutely still, heart hammering against the pack frame, invisible by seven years' discipline and suddenly unsure, for the first time, whether invisibility would hold.

The god-light pulsed once. Twice. Like something breathing.

Then it guttered and died, and the shrine was only a shrine again, worn and lichen-eaten and blind, and the drover stood there shaking, hat clutched to his chest, looking at the stone the way a man looks at a door he swears was locked.

"Old man's tales," he said aloud, to nobody, to the mountain. His voice cracked on it. He jammed the hat back on, hauled the mule's lead, and climbed on past the narrows without ever seeing the dustwalker pressed into the shadow ten feet above his head.

Ansel didn't move for a long time.

* * *

The singing followed them all night.

By the time Ansel reached the shelter cave at Hollow Tor—a smuggler's rest, half dug into the hillside, its entrance screened by a curtain of hanging roots—they'd catalogued four more lights on the slopes below. Little flickers, scattered across the dark valleys like fireflies rising off graves. Each one meant a prayer. Each prayer meant something waking.

Inside the cave, someone had gotten there first.

Ansel read the signs before stepping fully in: boot prints in the frost-damp, two sets, days old; a fire pit swept clean but still smelling faintly of juniper smoke. Smugglers' etiquette said leave the rest as you found it, take nothing, tell nothing. Ansel banked a small fire anyway, far enough back that its glow wouldn't reach the mouth, and sat chewing dried plums while the moon-iron went on murmuring in its pack like a sleeper talking in her sleep.

Around midnight, the stones started speaking too.

It began as a tick-tick-tick from the cave wall, like ice settling. Then words, or nearly—syllables ground out syllable by syllable, deep under the rock, in a language Ansel's bones recognized before their mind did. Hessel had muttered it over her cauldron. Hessel had written it in the margins of her books, in that cramped sideways script she'd thought her apprentice never studied.

*The roads remember,* the stone said, or something near enough. *The roads remember. The roads—*

"Remember what?" Ansel asked the wall.

The ticking stopped. In the silence after, Ansel became aware of how loud their own breathing was, how loud their heartbeat, how much space their body took up in a world that had spent seven years agreeing to overlook it. The mountain had never spoken to them before. The mountain had never needed to. Dustwalkers were beneath notice—that had been the covenant, unwritten but absolute. The hidden stayed hidden, and the old things slept, and everyone kept their side of it.

But the old things weren't sleeping anymore. And Ansel had a sudden, sickening intuition—no more solid than the smell of yarrow, no more arguable—that the covenant had been broken from the other end first. That the gods waking meant debts waking. That every bargain ever struck in the dark centuries when the shrines still burned was coming due, with interest, all at once.

And Ansel Vane had grown up in the house of a woman who bargained for a living.

They pulled the pack close and drew out one bar of moon-iron, turning it in the firelight. It was beautiful stuff—pale as bone, faintly luminous, cool to the touch even now. The temple-metal of the old faith, mined from veins the gods had slept beside, worth more per ounce than a farmer earned in a year. Ansel had carried it for years without wondering, really wondering, why the waking gods' metal was the one commodity that never lost its price. Why the buyers down-country paid in gold for a thing that hummed.

Tonight the bar felt less like cargo and more like a message. *Carry us,* it seemed to say. *Carry us home.*

"You're ore," Ansel told it firmly. "You're a payday. Don't get ideas."

The iron rang softly, once, like laughter.

Sleep didn't come. Around the coldest hour, Ansel gave up, snuffed the fire, and stepped outside to look at the sky—and stopped dead on the threshold.

The stars over the Thornfell were wrong.

Not gone, not dimmed—*rearranged*. Constellations Ansel could have navigated by blindfolded had drifted, subtly, the way furniture shifts in a dream. The Shepherd leaned where the Miller should stand. The Waning Lady hung low over peaks she had never touched, her crescent tilted like a listening ear. And along the whole spine of the mountains, from Greywater Saddle to the black mass of Thornfell Crown itself, faint silver threads had begun to rise from the valleys—prayer-smoke, or god-light, or roads lighting up vein by vein, Ansel couldn't tell. Couldn't tell, and suspected it didn't matter, because all three were the same thing here.

The mountain was remembering itself. And everything built on its forgetting—the smuggler roads, the dustwalker trade, Ansel's entire careful, nameless life—was built on top of that forgetting like a house on a frozen lake.

Somewhere down in the southern valleys, a bell began to ring. Then another. Then a dozen, scattered and arrhythmic, village bells rung at the wrong hour for no reason anyone would be able to explain come morning.

Ansel stood in the root-hung doorway with the cold biting through their coat and did the arithmetic of fear. Three days to Lowmarsh with the delivery. Two days back up-country to check on Wren—because if the old bargains were stirring, and if Hessel's name was anywhere in those old books, then the witch's other obligations might be stirring too, and Wren was the closest thing Ansel had ever let themselves love since running. After that—

After that, Ansel didn't know. That was the trouble. Seven years of certainty, and now every road on the mountain was a question mark.

The nearest bell fell silent mid-peal, as though a hand had closed around it.

In the ringing quiet that followed, Ansel heard—clearly, unmistakably, from the direction of the deep passes where no one lived—the sound of many feet walking in step. Not animals. Not wind. Feet, and beneath them, faintly, a voice like frost forming on glass, saying a word over and over in the old tongue.

Hessel's margins had given Ansel enough to translate it, years ago, out of boredom and disobedience.

*Coming. Coming. Coming.*

Ansel shouldered the pack. The moon-iron sang. And the dustwalker of the Thornfell, who had survived seven years by trusting no one and nothing—not the roads, not the gods, and least of all the dead—turned south toward Lowmarsh and began, for the first time in their life, to run.
