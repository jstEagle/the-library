## 2. Wren's Fever

The roads into Lowmarsh had changed.

Ansel felt it before they saw it—a wrongness in the way the path bent toward the village instead of past it, as though the mountain itself had decided Ansel's business there and would not be argued with. For seven years they had moved through Thornfell like smoke through a keyhole: present everywhere, seen nowhere. The smuggler's first law was *be nothing to no one*. But the road beneath their boots had opinions now, and it herded them down the goat-track toward the marsh-lights of the village like a shepherd who had grown tired of waiting.

They came in at dusk, when the peat-smoke hung low over the rooftops and the reeds along the mere whispered against each other in voices that sounded almost like speech. Almost. Everything on the mountain sounded almost like speech these days.

Wren's window was dark.

That was the first wrong thing. The child kept a candle stub burning most nights—Ansel had watched its small flame gutter and relight across three winters, a heartbeat of light they checked on the way to wherever they were going that week. A ritual, though neither of them had ever named it. Ansel never entered the house. Never spoke to the boy except by accident, arranged to look like accident: a dropped apple on the lane, a coin in the mud where small hands might find it. *Be nothing to no one.* But be something, just barely, to one child.

The window was dark, and behind the shuttered glass there was a sound like wet cloth being wrung out, over and over, and Ansel understood it was breathing.

They went over the wall at the back of the tanner's yard, up the drainpipe whose rust they knew by touch, and let themselves in through the loft gap the way they had done perhaps six times in seven years, always in emergencies, always hating themselves for the risk and more for the relief.

Wren lay in the narrow bed beneath a quilt gone grey with washing, and he was burning.

Not fever-burning—not the ordinary fire of a child's body fighting for itself. This was a light under the skin, a glow like coals banked in ash, visible in the hollows of his throat and temples and the thin places of his wrists. His eyes were open. They were fixed on the rafters, and they were not seeing the rafters.

"Ansel," he said. His voice was a dry leaf. "I heard you come over the wall."

"You heard the wind." Ansel knelt by the bed and pressed the back of their hand to the boy's forehead. Heat poured off him like a hearth left untended. "Saints' teeth, Wren. How long?"

"Since the moon went thin." He turned his head, slow as something underwater, and looked at them, and smiled, and the smile was the worst thing yet because there was too much peace in it. "It doesn't hurt anymore. That's how I know it's bad. Mam says fevers hurt. This one's just... bright."

Ansel had carried willowbark and feverfew and a dozen other remedies in the lining of their coat since before they'd known why. They brewed what could be brewed on the cottage's cold hearth, crumbled yarrow into water gone lukewarm, sponged the boy's arms and chest while his mother, Marta, hovered in the doorway with a face like wet paper. She did not ask how Ansel had gotten in. Seven years of dropped apples and found coins had bought a silence between them that was almost a language.

"Nothing works," she said finally, flatly, the way people say things they have said until the words are worn smooth. "Priest Bram's oils. The herbwoman from Harrow Ford. Nothing. He burns and he stares and sometimes he talks in a voice that isn't—" She stopped. Her jaw worked. "Isn't his."

"What does he say?"

"I don't write those words down," Marta said, "and I won't say them aloud."

* * *

Priest Bram received Ansel in the vestry of the low stone chapel, among dust and beeswax and the smell of old tallow. He was an old man made mostly of stoop and shadow, and he looked at Ansel for a long moment before speaking, as if deciding whether they were real.

"You're the one who watches the boy," he said.

"I don't know what you mean."

"Of course you don't." He lowered himself onto a bench with the care of a man whose joints had opinions. "Then you'll not know what I'm about to tell you either, and we can both pretend this conversation happened to someone else. Sit."

Ansel sat. It was that kind of night.

"There is a bargain," Bram said. "Older than the chapel. Older than the parish rolls, which go back two hundred years and call it *the standing debt*, so we know it was old even then. When the first families cut Lowmarsh out of the fen, the land didn't want to give. Children died every winter—chill-fever, white-lung, the drowning sickness. So the founders went down." He tapped the floor twice with one knuckle. "Down. To the court beneath the mountain. And they asked for their children's lives."

"And got them."

"And got them. At a price written in a hand none of us can read, on a page none of us has ever seen. Every generation since, the fever comes to one child. One only. And the fever takes them down, or—" he shrugged, a terrible small motion, "—it doesn't, and the family counts itself lucky and never asks why. We are a village built on a payment plan, smuggler. We do not ask about the principal."

"Which child this generation?"

"Wren Coalhouse. Chosen at his birth, by signs I will not describe." Bram's eyes were steady and very tired. "In my grandfather's time, the chosen child was given willingly. There were rites. Comforts. Now we simply... wait, and call it illness, and bury our ignorance with the body."

"The fever doesn't kill him," Ansel said. "He's not dying. He's *leaving*."

"Yes." The priest folded his hands. "And here is the part I should not tell you, and will, because you have the look of someone who breaks rules professionally. The debt is not owed by Wren. Debts pass down the blood, but they are *held* somewhere. Someone must carry the account. In every generation, the holder is marked—the gods know their own bookkeeping—and when the fever comes, the holder can stand for the child. Pay in their place." He looked at Ansel with an expression that was not quite pity and not quite warning. "I don't know who holds Wren's account. But whatever brought you over that wall tonight, I'd think hard on whether it was your idea."

* * *

Ansel did not sleep. They sat in the loft above Wren's bed with a knife across their knees and watched the boy burn gently in the dark, and they thought about Bram's words the way one worries a splinter: uselessly, compulsively, drawing blood.

*Whatever brought you over that wall tonight.*

The road had bent toward the village. The road had *herded* them. And before that—seven years of watching a child they had no reason to watch, no memory of choosing to watch, only a certainty in the chest like a second heartbeat: *Wren matters. Keep Wren alive.* They had called it conscience. They had called it the last good thing Mother Hessel ever taught them, back when they still believed she'd taught them anything good.

Outside, the waning moon slid behind the fells, and the room went very dark, and the dark acquired a shape.

It came to the window without opening it. That was the detail Ansel's mind snagged on afterward, the detail that would keep them company down all the long roads ahead: the shutters stayed closed, the glass stayed whole, and yet the figure stood at the window as though the window were a suggestion it had politely declined. It was tall in the way that shadows are tall—longer than the space allowed, folding slightly to fit, the way a heron folds to stand in shallow water. Antlers rose from its head, black and branching, hung with threads of pale light like a tree strung with the last leaves of autumn.

Wren sat up in bed. He was smiling again, that terrible peaceful smile.

"You came," the boy said.

*I came,* said the figure, and its voice was not a sound but a pressure, felt in the teeth and the breastbone, in the same register as the river-voices and the whispering reeds. *As the account requires.*

"He's a child," Ansel said, and was on their feet with the knife before knowing they'd moved. "Whatever was promised, whoever promised it—he wasn't born yet. You can't hold a soul to a signature it never made."

The antlered head turned. Where a face should have been there was a darkness with depth to it, and in that depth, briefly, Ansel saw something that made their stomach drop: shelves. Rows upon rows of small glass jars, each glowing faintly, each labeled in a silver hand.

*The court honors every debt,* the figure said. *Every clause. Every mercy. Do you imagine we came to take?* It extended one long hand—not toward Wren, but palm-up, open, in the ancient gesture of a creditor presenting a bill. *We came to confirm receipt. The price was paid long ago. Paid in full, in a name freely given, by one with authority to give it. The child's passage is merely the delivery of goods already sold.*

"Paid by whom?"

*By the hand that signed.* The figure's head tilted, and the pale threads on its antlers chimed together, a sound like distant bells heard through water. *You may read the ledger yourself, if you descend. The thirteenth turn opens the door. Three nights remain.* Its attention settled on Ansel then, fully, and the weight of it was physical, a thumb pressing on the soul. *Though I confess curiosity. The signer's name appears twice in our books, keeper. Once as debtor.*

The figure began to thin, unraveling at the edges like smoke deciding to be honest.

"*Keeper?*" Ansel stepped forward. "Whose name? Whose name is on the page?"

*Yours,* said the court beneath the mountain, in the voice of shadow and antler, in the voice of a bargain already closed. *It has always been yours.*

And it was gone, and the shutters were unbroken, and Wren lay back down into his gentle burning, murmuring a word in a language that predated the village, the chapel, and every road Ansel had ever trusted.

Ansel stood in the dark with the knife shaking in their hand and understood, with the cold clarity of a trap springing shut, that there was only one place left to go. The witch's cottage on the high fell. The wards that still hummed after seven years. The papers of Mother Hessel—who had taught them everything, lied about everything, and signed something in their name before they were old enough to know their own name was worth signing.

Three nights. Thirteen turns. One door.

Ansel sheathed the knife, touched Wren's burning brow once—lightly, a promise made of skin—and went out over the wall into the dark, toward the past they had run from, running toward it now faster than they had ever run from anything.
