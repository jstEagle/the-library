## 4. The Oath-Keeper

Serah Ironmark had learned to read snow the way other people read scripture.

The mountain wrote in it constantly—confessions of passage, small sins of weight and hurry. A hare's stitch-work. A goat's careless scrawl along the scree. And there, cutting up the eastern flank of the Cindermaw pass where no honest traveler had business going: a narrow stride, long between steps, the left boot dragging just slightly at the heel. Someone carrying a heavy pack on one shoulder. Someone who knew the passes well enough not to waste movement, but not so well that the mountain had stopped noticing them.

Seven days she had followed that signature through the high country. Seven days since the fever-village of Ashen Hollow, since she had stood with her sword drawn—not against the villagers, never against them—and refused the order that would have made her an executioner.

*"Burn it," Commander Vael had said, and his voice had been very calm, the way it always was when he was asking her to do something unforgivable. "The god-touched burn cleanest."*

She had looked at the village instead—at the shrines springing up in doorways like mushrooms after rain, at the old woman setting a candle before a stone that hadn't been worshipped in three hundred years, at the children laughing because the hearth-fire had turned silver and none of them were afraid—and she had said the only word left in her.

"No."

They had taken her oath-plate with tongs, as though it were diseased. They had struck her name from the rolls of the Vigilant with the same ceremony they used for the dead. And then they had sent her south with nothing but her sword, her armor, and the knowledge that the thing waking beneath the Thornfell was older than her order, hungrier than her order, and far better at keeping its promises.

* * *

She caught sight of the smuggler on the third evening of the seventh day, at the place where the pass narrowed to a throat of black stone and the wind sang through it in a single low note, like a monk holding breath.

The figure stood at the mouth of a cleft in the rock—a door, if you knew how doors worked on this mountain, which most people didn't—which was exactly why Serah had chosen this place to make her approach. You could not run from a conversation held at a threshold. Thresholds had rules.

"Ansel Vane," she said.

The figure went still in the particular way of wild things—not frozen, but gathered, every muscle reorganized around the possibility of violence or flight. When they turned, the last light caught a face younger than their reputation, sharper than their years, with eyes the grey-green of ice over deep water.

"I don't know you."

"No." Serah kept her hands visible, open, away from her hilt. She had learned that gesture in Ashen Hollow too—learned that the difference between a threat and an offer was often nothing but the angle of your fingers. "You know the plate I used to wear, though. Everyone on this mountain knows the Vigilant."

Something moved behind those eyes. Not fear. Calculation—the fast, cold arithmetic of someone who had survived seven years alone by pricing every stranger correctly.

"The Vigilant burns villages," Ansel said.

"The Vigilant burned one village. I refused to help." She let that sit. "Now there is no Vigilant for me. There is only what's coming, and the fact that we are both walking toward it."

"And what's coming?"

Serah tilted her head back. Above the pass, through the ragged tear of cloud, the waning moon hung thin as a paring of bone. Thirteen nights of it left. Thirteen turns, if you counted the way the old counts counted—each night a step down a stair that ended somewhere beneath the world.

"You've seen the shrines lighting," she said. "Don't insult us both by pretending otherwise. The dead gods are waking, and when they wake fully, the court beneath the mountain will have to answer for everything it's taken. Or take more. And there's a child in Lowmarsh whose name is already written in the court's ledger. Wren. Fevered past any herb's reach. You've been watching that child for two years—I've watched you watching."

Ansel's hand had drifted, without apparent decision, to the knife at their belt. "You've been following me."

"Since the Cistern road. Your left boot drags. It's very distinctive."

"What do you want?"

"The truth?" Serah laughed, short and without much humor in it. "I want my oath back. I want the order I served to be worth having served. But wanting is a luxury down there, and I've given up luxuries." She stepped closer, boots crunching on frost-brittle stone, and let her voice drop into the register she'd once used to swear oaths—low, level, impossible to mistake for anything but what it was. "What I *need* is a guide through roads that remember being walked. And you need someone who knows the way into the court beneath the mountain. Because I do. And because the thirteenth turn of the moon is the only door, and there are eleven nights left."

* * *

They did not make camp together. That was Ansel's condition, delivered flatly while already turning away toward the cleft-door, and Serah accepted it because accepting things was cheaper than arguing them. She built her fire fifty paces off, in the lee of a fallen pillar, and sat with her sword across her knees and watched the small, stubborn spark of Ansel's own fire gutter in the dark like something trying to decide whether to exist.

She understood that fire. She had spent three weeks now being a person who had lost the shape of her life and not yet found the next one. The Vigilant had not merely stripped her rank; it had stripped the architecture of her days—the bells, the vigils, the litany of watchwords passed from mouth to mouth down the centuries. *We keep the wall. We keep the faith. We keep the names.* Without them she was just a woman in good armor sitting alone on a cold mountain, and some nights the armor felt less like protection than like a costume she no longer had permission to wear.

But she had seen what came out of the ground at Ashen Hollow, in the hour before dawn on her last night there. She had seen the shrine-stone open its carved eyes—eyes worn featureless by three hundred years of weather, opening anyway—and look at her. Not hungrily. *Assessingly.* The way a creditor looks across a table.

The old gods were not kind. She had known that even as a girl, before the order taught her their sanitized catechism. Kindness was a human invention, young and fragile as a candleflame. What the old gods had was *accounting*. And the court beneath the mountain had been cooking its books for a thousand years.

Somewhere below them, right now, a ledger held the name of a fevered child. Somewhere below them, a bargain signed generations ago in good faith or bad was coming due, and the court beneath the mountain honored every debt—it was famous for honoring debts, that was the horror of it, it never cheated, it never forgave, it simply collected with the patience of stone.

And somewhere on this mountainside, a smuggler who trusted no one was lying awake deciding whether to trust her.

Serah fed a branch to the fire and watched it catch. Trust was not what she was asking for, actually. She had thought about this during the long climbing days, turning the problem over the way you'd turn a lockpick. Trust was a gift, and gifts could be withheld forever. What she was offering was simpler and harder to refuse: *necessity*. Two people, one door, eleven nights. Neither of them could carry the other's part.

She just had to survive long enough for Ansel to do the arithmetic.

* * *

Near midnight, she heard footsteps in the frost and did not reach for her sword, because reaching for it would have been an answer, and she wanted to hear the question first.

Ansel materialized at the edge of the firelight the way they seemed to do everything—without announcement, without wasted motion, wrapped in a hooded cloak the color of wet slate. Up close, in the firelight, they looked tired in a way that had nothing to do with the climb. The tiredness went all the way down.

"If I say no," Ansel said, "you'll follow me anyway."

"Yes."

"If I say yes, you'll want to lead."

"I'll want to advise. You'll lead. You know these roads; I know the destination. That division will keep us both honest."

"Honest." Ansel said the word like they were tasting something spoiled. They crouched at the edge of the light, close enough for the fire to find the sharp planes of their face, and studied her with the unhurried thoroughness of someone appraising goods of uncertain provenance. "Oath-keepers don't come down from the Vigilant halls. They're carried. So either you're dead and remarkably animated, or you did something they couldn't forgive."

"I refused to burn a village full of people who'd done nothing worse than pray to the wrong gods."

"Mm." No softening in the grey-green eyes. "And now you hunt the court beneath the mountain out of—what? Justice? Redemption?"

"Out of arithmetic," Serah said, and saw the faintest flicker of surprise—there and gone, quick as a fish under ice. "The court has been taking children from these valleys for a thousand years, Vane. Quietly. Lawfully. Every contract sealed, every debt honored, every grief properly invoiced. And my order knew. Do you understand? We *knew*. There are records in the Vigilant archive older than the order itself, and half of them are receipts." Her hands had closed into fists without her noticing; she opened them deliberately, finger by finger. "I spent twelve years keeping a wall that was built to protect the people on the wrong side of it. I'm done keeping walls. I'm going under the mountain to break the ledger, and I need someone who can get me to the door."

Silence, except the wind singing its one low note through the pass, and the fire eating its branch.

"Why me?" Ansel said finally. "There are other guides."

"There are other guides for other roads. But you've been carrying moon-iron through these passes for years—sacred metal, old prayers folded into ore—and you never once asked what it was *for*. You think the waking gods haven't noticed the one courier who's been moving their bones around the mountain like a faithful fool?" Serah leaned forward, and the firelight made her shadow loom huge against the rock behind her, a giant with a sword. "The mountain knows your name, Ansel Vane. It's known it for longer than you'd like. Which means the court beneath it knows your name too. And I'd wager—" she paused, weighing whether to spend this coin, and decided she had no choice, "—I'd wager that whatever's happening to the child in Lowmarsh, it isn't random. Nothing on this mountain is random anymore. The dice stopped being fair the week the shrines lit."

Something changed in Ansel's face then—not softening, nothing so generous—but a settling, like a blade finding its groove. Fear, maybe. Or the shape fear wears when it stops being deniable.

"Wren's fever started nine days ago," Ansel said quietly. "The night the first shrine lit."

"I know."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was in Lowmarsh four days ago, and I read the priest's face, and priests' faces are the only honest ledgers left in the Thornfell." Serah rose, brushing frost from her knees, and stood at her full height with the moon at her back—a silhouette in broken armor, offering the only thing she had left. "Eleven nights, Vane. Come morning, we walk or we don't. Decide by then."

Ansel stood too, slow, and for a moment the two of them faced each other across the fire like figures on a card—one upright and armored, one hooded and hollow-eyed, and between them a small flame that neither owned.

"I don't trust you," Ansel said.

"Good," said Serah. "Trust is for people with time. We have eleven nights."

Ansel stared at her a moment longer—then turned and walked back toward their own cold camp, and just before the dark took them, threw the words over their shoulder like a coin tossed to a beggar:

"Be ready at first light. And Ironmark—if you betray me down there, the mountain won't need to punish you. I will."

Serah watched the dark swallow them, and allowed herself, for the first time in three weeks, the ghost of a smile.

It wasn't trust. It was better than trust. It was terms.

She banked her fire and lay back against her pack with her sword in reach, and above her the moon thinned another hair's breadth, and far beneath the stone, in a hall lit by no sun, something vast and patient turned a page in its ledger and waited for the door to open.
