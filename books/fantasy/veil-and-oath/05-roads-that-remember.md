## 5. Roads That Remember

The deep roads had always been Ansel's to read the way other folk read scripture. Seven years of walking them had taught every bend and blind mouth of stone: where the pass above Hollowmere narrowed enough that a mule's load could be lifted over by rope, where the old cairn at Wolfjaw marked a turn invisible from below, which scree slopes sang warning before they slid. The Thornfell was a country of secrets, and Ansel had made themselves one more secret moving through it, unremarked, unwritten, safe.

By the second day of travel with Serah Ironmark, the roads stopped making sense.

It began small. A cairn that should have stood on their left stood on their right, though neither of them had crossed it. Then the path itself—Ansel knew this stretch, knew the way it climbed in three switchbacks past a spring that never froze—went straight instead, rising smooth as a stair into country Ansel did not recognize. Grey fells, yes, and wind-scoured stone, but no spring. No switchbacks. When Ansel looked back the way they'd come, the road behind them was not the road they had walked.

"The gods are waking," Serah said, when Ansel finally stopped and said so aloud. She said it the way she said most things: flat, unhurried, a blade laid on a table rather than drawn. "I told you. The roads feel it first. They were god-roads before they were smuggler-roads."

"They were *my* roads." The words came out sharper than Ansel intended. "Seven years I've walked them. Nothing on this mountain has moved without my knowing."

"Nothing on this mountain was *awake* before." Serah crouched, pressed her palm flat against the stone. Whatever she felt there made her jaw tighten. "It remembers being holy ground, Ansel. And it's remembering who walked it and why."

They walked on because there was nowhere else to go. Behind them, somewhere in the passes, Wren lay burning in Lowmarsh with a fever no herb could touch, and a debt with Ansel's own name signed at its foot waited beneath the mountain for collection. Ahead, if Serah spoke true, a door would open on the thirteenth turn of the waning moon—and the moon was thinning like a coin rubbed between fingers.

* * *

The stones began speaking on the third day.

Not all at once. First just a murmur under the wind, the way a house settles at night. Ansel told themselves it was the wind until they passed a boulder split by frost and heard, distinctly, from inside the crack, a woman's voice reciting names. Not threats. Names. A long patient litany, each one spoken with the care of someone setting a candle in a window.

Serah heard it too. Ansel watched her hand drift to the oath-blade at her hip—an old habit, Ansel guessed, from before the order stripped her—and then fall away, deliberately.

"Don't answer them," she said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were. Everyone wants to. That's how the old tolls catch you—a question asked in a voice you know, and you answer before you think." She glanced sideways. "Whose voice was it?"

Ansel hadn't wanted to say. But the stones had already taken the choice of silence away, because the next boulder they passed spoke in a voice Ansel had spent seven years not hearing: dry, sharp-edged, amused even in instruction. *Mind the loose step, child. You never did watch your feet.*

Mother Hessel's voice, worn into the mountain like water wears a channel.

Ansel stopped walking. Stood very still. "She's dead," they said, to no one, to the stone. "I left her. She died alone in that cottage, and whatever is speaking with her mouth isn't her."

"No," Serah agreed quietly. "But the mountain knows what voices live in you. It's waking up hungry, and hunger starts with what you carry." She looked at Ansel then, really looked, and something in her grey eyes was almost gentle. "What did she teach you? Before you ran?"

"That mercy has a price tag. That every gift is a hook." Ansel started walking again, faster. "That's why I'm still alive."

"And yet here you are," Serah said, falling into step, "walking into the court beneath the mountain to buy back a child's soul. Seems she taught you something else, too, whether she meant to or not."

Ansel didn't answer. The road ahead looped once, gently, impossibly, back past the split boulder, and the woman's voice inside it smiled around the next name in its litany.

*Wren.*

* * *

They came down to the river on the fifth evening, and Ansel knew at once that this was no river any map remembered.

It ran through a gorge that hadn't been there before—or rather, the gorge had always been there, sleeping, and now it had opened its eye. The water was black and slow and utterly silent; sound went into it and did not come out. Even the wind stopped at its banks. On the far side, the road continued up toward the high shoulder of the mountain where, Serah said, the moon-door would open. There was no bridge. There had never been a bridge.

There was a ferryman.

He stood in a coracle of woven bone-grey wicker, and he might have been carved from the same material—tall, gaunt, faceless beneath a hood of river-mist. He did not pole the boat. The boat simply drifted to their bank, as if summoned by their arriving.

"Toll," he said. His voice came from everywhere along the water at once, quiet as silt settling.

"We have coin," Serah said, and reached for her purse.

The ferryman did not move. "Coin is for merchants. This is a god-road. The toll is a memory. One, freely given, freely paid. The river keeps it. You cross lighter than you came."

Ansel's stomach dropped. They thought of the stones speaking with Hessel's voice, of the litany of names, of everything the waking mountain was gathering up as it opened its eyes. Memories were not coin. Coin could be earned again.

"Which memory?" Serah asked. Her voice stayed level, but Ansel saw her knuckles whiten on her sword-belt. "Does the traveler choose?"

"The river chooses what it will take. The traveler chooses whether to pay." The mist where his face should be tilted toward Ansel. "Or turn back. The door opens in four days regardless. The river does not care."

Serah stepped forward first. "Take mine, then. I've memories I can spare."

The mist-face turned to her, considered, and Ansel saw the water shiver along fifty yards of black bank. Then it turned away. "No. Yours are spoken for. Another claim holds them." Something that might have been regret softened the voice. "Yours will be collected elsewhere, oath-keeper. By another creditor."

Serah went pale. Ansel didn't ask. Some debts were too private to name aloud, and they were not yet allies enough for that.

Which left Ansel standing at the bank, and the river waiting, and behind them four days of looping road and speaking stone, and ahead of them Wren's soul in a jar beneath the Moon-Throne.

"Will it hurt?" Ansel asked.

"Nothing freely given hurts," the ferryman said. "It's the keeping that hurts. The river will hold your memory kindly. Kinder than you hold it yourself."

And Ansel understood, with a strange floating clarity, which memory the river wanted. It had been circling it for days—the stones murmuring in Hessel's voice, the road bending toward things half-remembered. The mountain was stripping them down to what could not be traded. And there was one memory Ansel had carried like a coal in the chest for seven years, taken out only at night, held only briefly, because holding it too long burned.

Their mother's face.

They had her hands in memory—flour-dusted, quick, turning Ansel's childhood name into a song while she worked. They had her voice. But the face had begun to blur years ago, edges softening like wet ink, and Ansel had guarded what remained of it jealously, refusing even to look at it directly in their mind lest looking wear it further. It was the last thing they owned that no one had ever used against them. No bargain. No ledger. No name signed at the bottom.

"Ansel." Serah's hand closed on their arm. "Don't. We'll find another crossing. There's always another way—I've spent my life learning the courts' tricks, there's—"

"There isn't, and you know it." Ansel's voice came out steady, which surprised them. "Four days, Serah. Wren doesn't have four days plus however long it takes to outsmart a river."

"You don't owe the mountain your mother."

"No," Ansel said. "But I owe Wren a crossing. And the mountain knows the difference."

They stepped past Serah onto the bank. The black water rose slightly, attentive, courteous. Somewhere beneath its silence Ansel felt the vast patience of the thing they were about to feed.

"My mother's face," Ansel said aloud. "The memory of it. All of it. Freely given."

The river took a breath—or the gorge did, or the world did—and the ferryman extended one wicker-dark hand.

Paying it did not feel like loss. That was the terrible part. It felt like opening a fist that had been clenched for seven years. The memory rose in Ansel gently, the way a bird rises, and for one last moment they saw her whole: the crooked smile, the scar through one eyebrow, the way lamplight caught the grey in her hair, her eyes crinkling with some joke she was saving for later—

—and then it was gone, and Ansel stood on the bank unable to remember what color her eyes had been, or whether the scar was real, or whether the smile had been hers or a thing they'd invented out of longing. Grief hit a moment later, huge and clean and cold, like plunge-water. They heard themselves make a sound. They didn't recognize it as their own voice.

Then the coracle touched the bank, and the ferryman waited, and Ansel got in because the alternative was drowning in place.

Serah crossed beside them without being invited, which Ansel suspected the ferryman permitted out of some obscure courtesy. The black water bore them in perfect silence. Halfway across, Serah spoke, low, so the ferryman wouldn't hear—or perhaps so the river wouldn't.

"I saw," she said. "What you gave. What it took."

"I know."

"I'm sorry." A pause. The oarless boat drifted. "My order taught us that payment freely made is sacred. That the giver stands taller after. I never believed it until now." Her hand found Ansel's shoulder—brief, hard, gone again. "You gave the truest thing you had. For a child who'll never know. That's the only kind of oath that ever mattered, and I broke mine trying to learn it."

Ansel stared at the far bank coming closer and found, unexpectedly, that they believed her. Not because of the words. Because she had offered her own memory first, and meant it, and had been refused—and had looked afraid, afterward, of whatever creditor waited to collect what was "spoken for."

"What holds your memories?" Ansel asked quietly.

Serah was silent a long moment. The far bank slid nearer, grey stone rising into dusk.

"The order I belonged to kept an archive," she said at last. "Every oath-keeper's memories, sworn into vaults, returned only when the oath was fulfilled. Mine were sealed when they stripped me. If I take Wren's place as the court offers—if I serve as steward—they give them back. Every one." Her laugh was short and had no humor in it. "Redemption, itemized."

"And if we do this your way instead?"

"Then I cross that river someday and find out what the water takes from me." She looked at Ansel, and in the last light her face was younger and rawer than her voice. "So. We're agreed. Your way. The stupid way. The way where nobody buys anything with somebody else's soul."

The coracle grounded on the far bank. Ansel stepped out onto stone that hummed faintly underfoot, awake and aware, and found that the grief was still there but it had changed shape—no longer a coal clenched tight, but an open hollow, and somehow easier to breathe around.

Above them, against the deepening violet of the sky, the waning moon hung like a paring of nail. Four days. Three, now, by the time they made the high shoulder.

Serah climbed up beside them, then stopped, head cocked, listening to something in the stone.

"Ansel," she said slowly. "The mountain's singing. Do you hear it?"

Ansel listened. Beneath the wind, beneath the speaking stones, a thread of melody rose from the deep places—from the direction of the court beneath the mountain. A lullaby. Old, and patient, and sung in a voice Ansel almost recognized.

Almost. The way one almost recognizes a face one can no longer see.

"It's counting down," Serah whispered. "Turn by turn. And Ansel—it's singing your name."
