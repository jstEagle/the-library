## 6. The Court Beneath

The moon-door did not open so much as admit them, the way a mouth admits a word it has been holding all night.

Serah Ironmark went first, because that was what oath-keepers did—had done, once, when she had still been one—and because she did not trust Ansel Vane to walk into the dark without trying to vanish sideways down some smuggler's cut. The passage beyond the door was not stone. It was something older and more patient: walls of packed earth threaded with roots that pulsed faintly, like veins in a sleeping wrist. The light came from nowhere Serah could name. It pooled around their boots without casting shadows, and every step they took sounded less like footfalls than like coins being counted.

"Stay behind me," she said.

"You stay behind *me*," Ansel answered. "You don't know these roads."

"There are no roads here," Serah said. "That's rather the point."

Ansel said nothing to that, but Serah felt them fall half a pace back anyway—the grudging concession of someone who had survived seven years alone by knowing when a fight wasn't worth the blood. The smuggler moved like water finding its level, weight always on the balls of the feet, eyes never resting on any one thing long enough for the thing to notice being looked at. Serah had watched that habit for three days now, across the passes and through the shifting roads, and she had come to understand it as a kind of prayer: *do not see me, do not see me, do not see me.*

It was going to fail them both down here. She could feel it already, the way you feel weather coming in your teeth.

They descended for hours, or for what felt like hours; time below the mountain moved like syrup off a cold spoon. The air thickened. It took on flavors—loam, candlewax, old honey, and beneath it all something metallic and sweet that Serah's training named before her mind would accept it. Blood, aged in oak. The smell of a bargain kept too long.

"Serah." Ansel's voice came low, stripped of its usual edge. "The walls are breathing."

"I know."

"Is that supposed to happen?"

"Nothing down here is *supposed* to happen," Serah said. "Everything down here is *permitted*. That's worse."

The roots in the walls had begun to keep time with her pulse. She was almost certain of it. And when she slowed her breathing to test the theory, the roots slowed with her, patient as a creditor.

Then the passage ended, and the court opened beneath them like a held breath finally released.

*

Serah had read descriptions of the Moon-Throne in the order's forbidden archives—three lines, heavily annotated, most of the annotations arguing about whether the place still existed. The archives had not prepared her. Nothing could have prepared her, she thought later, except perhaps standing in a cathedral and realizing the cathedral was looking back.

The cavern rose in tiers, carved or grown—it was impossible to say which—into galleries that spiraled down toward a floor of polished silver stone. Thousands of figures occupied those tiers. They were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, all edges and pale fire, and none of them moved except their heads, which turned in slow unison to follow the two small warm creatures descending the stair. Above them all, at the cavern's crown, hung a moon that was not the moon: a disc of white stone veined with silver, shedding light that fell upward, so that everything in the court cast its shadow toward the sky instead of the earth.

And upon a throne of that same white stone sat the steward.

It wore the shape of a tall figure in grey robes, and it wore the shape the way a hook wears a worm. Its face was smooth and courteous, its hands folded, its posture the very portrait of patience. When Serah reached the bottom stair, it inclined its head with the graciousness of a host greeting guests who were also, technically, inventory.

"Welcome," it said, and its voice was the sound a ledger makes when a page turns. "Travelers. Debtors." A pause, delicate as a scalpel. "Guests."

"We've come for a soul," Ansel said. No preamble. Serah had learned that much about them: when fear got bad enough, the smuggler went flat and direct, all the cunning burned down to a single wick. "Wren Marsh. Child of Lowmarsh. Fevered nine days. Your people came to the window and named a price already paid. I'm here to unpay it."

A ripple went through the galleries—a sound like silk tearing very slowly, which Serah realized with a lurch was laughter.

"The court honors all contracts," the steward said. "It is our pride and our burden. You wish to contest a debt?"

"I wish to buy it out."

"Then let us look at the account." The steward unfolded one hand, and between its fingers appeared a book—not carried, not summoned, simply *present*, the way grief is present. "Wren Marsh. Born under a waning moon. Collateral against obligations incurred by the Marsh line, four generations past, in the year the Lowmarsh mill burned. The debt was assumed and renewed, renewed and assumed, each generation signing afresh in ignorance of what they signed. Marriage contracts. Land deeds. A christening blessing, purchased from a midwife who had her own accounts to settle." The smooth face tilted. "Your villages are built on our paper, traveler. Every hearth in the Thornfell sits atop a signature."

"Then I'll settle it," Ansel said. "Name a price. Moon-iron—I've carried it for years, I know where every seam in this mountain bleeds it. My labor. My memory. My—" a hitch, quickly buried, "—whatever you invoice."

"How generous." The steward turned a page. "But debts are not settled by the willing, only by the bound. Let us confirm the signatory." Its finger traced a line of script that glowed faintly as it passed beneath. "Ah."

One syllable. Serah watched it land on Ansel like a dropped stone.

"Ah?" Ansel said.

"The contract is current," the steward said pleasantly. "Renewed within the last decade. Signed not by a Marsh, but by an agent—one empowered to act in another's name, which the court finds especially binding, as the named party cannot claim ignorance. Only consent." The smooth face lifted, and for the first time Serah saw something behind it, something vast and cold and interested. "The signer gave a name we know well. Mother Hessel, hedge-witch of the Thornfell. Acting"—the page turned with terrible gentleness—"in the name of Ansel Vane."

The silence that followed was not empty. It was full, the way a grave is full.

"That's a lie," Ansel said. Very quietly. Very evenly. Serah had heard soldiers use that voice over bodies.

"The court does not lie," the steward said. "Lying creates ambiguity, and ambiguity voids contracts. We are precise. Here is the signature." The book drifted from its hand, drifting down the tiers until it hung in the air before Ansel's face, open to a page near the end. "Read it. Read your own name. Read the hand that wrote it—you will know the hand. You learned your letters holding it steady, did you not? Seven years ago you fled that cottage. Seven years ago the ink was drying."

Serah watched Ansel read. Watched the color leave their face in stages, like a tide going out. Watched their lips move over the words without sound, and then stop moving altogether.

"She taught me wards," Ansel said at last, and the voice was a stranger's, young and cracked. "She taught me which herbs wake the dead and which put them back to sleep. She told me—she *told* me the court was a story she used to keep children from wandering." Their hand came up and hovered over the page without touching it, the way you hover over a burn. "She signed my name to sell Wren's soul. Before I ever met Wren. Before—" The sentence collapsed.

"Before the child was born, yes," the steward agreed. "Contracts of this quality require foresight. Your teacher was one of our finest correspondents." A pause, and then, with what might have been courtesy in a kinder anatomy: "You were not trapped when you walked in tonight, debtor. You were delivered. The door you came through opens only for those already written in the book. We have been waiting for you the way one waits for a payment scheduled long ago."

Serah's hand found her sword hilt out of pure reflex, and the galleries rippled again—that slow tearing laughter—as if the whole court had noticed the gesture and found it charming. She forced her fingers loose. Steel meant nothing here. She had known that coming in. What she had not known, what no line in the forbidden archives had told her, was that the trap would have her ally's own childhood stitched into its lining.

"Then take me," Ansel said. "Instead of the child. Same ledger, new entry. That's a trade, and trades are your whole religion, isn't it? Honor it."

"Oh, we honor it," the steward said softly. "We honor everything. That is precisely your misfortune." It closed the book, and the sound of it closing echoed up through the tiers like a door in a very large house. "The court will consider your offer. Consideration takes time. Time is measured here in turns of the moon above, and the moon above is nearly spent. You may wait in our hospitality." The grey head turned toward Serah for the first time, and though the face was featureless, she felt it smile. "Both of you. The oath-keeper especially. We have such an interesting file on oath-keepers."

Guards formed out of the galleries—not walking, simply arriving, tall figures of pale fire whose faces were all the same courteous blank. They did not touch Ansel or Serah. They merely stood, and their standing was a wall, and their wall was a sentence being served.

As they were escorted toward the guesting-halls—rooms, Serah would learn, where the beds were soft and the water was clean and every comfort arrived with a small card itemizing its cost—Ansel spoke once, without turning their head.

"Seven years," they said. "I built seven years of not being seen. And the whole time I was already in the book."

Serah had no comfort to offer. Comfort, down here, would have been invoiced.

"Yes," she said instead. "So we stop being careful, and start being dangerous. There's a difference, and the court knows it, and it's afraid of the second thing. Did you see how it closed that book?"

"No."

"It closed it before you could turn the page past your name." Serah kept her voice low, pitched beneath the hearing of the pale-fire guards, if guards with no ears could be said to hear. "There are other names in that ledger, Ansel. Whole pages of them. Children from every village on this mountain. Whatever Hessel did, she didn't do it alone, and whatever the court is hiding, it's hiding it behind your shame. Shame keeps debtors quiet. Fury doesn't."

Ansel was silent a long moment. Ahead of them, the guesting-hall doors opened onto warmth and lamplight and the smell of fresh bread, and every bit of it gleamed faintly, priced and waiting.

"Fury," Ansel repeated, tasting the word like a coin bitten for gold.

Behind them, far above, the false moon shuddered in its setting of stone, and began, imperceptibly, to wane.
