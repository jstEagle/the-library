## 7. Debts of the Hidden Children

The steward's dismissal had the texture of a door closing.

"Return when you have gathered your grief," she had said, her voice like water finding its level in a stone basin. "The court is patient. The court has always been patient." And then she had turned back to her ledgers as though Ansel were a draft to be ignored, and Serah's hand on their elbow had been the only thing that moved them from the Moon-Throne's presence.

Now they walked, because walking was better than standing still, and standing still was better than screaming, and screaming was what Ansel wanted most in all the world beneath the mountain.

*In Ansel Vane's name.*

The words kept turning over like a stone in a streambed, worn smooth with handling. Mother Hessel. The woman who had fed them, taught them, beaten the softness out of them with lessons disguised as kindness. *A hedge-witch learns early that everything costs,* she used to say, stirring her cauldron with hands that smelled of yarrow and iron. *Nothing is given, child. Only lent.* Ansel had thought it wisdom. Seven years running the passes, they had thought it wisdom still.

It had been arithmetic. It had been inventory. Somewhere in that cottage full of drying herbs and crooked shelves, there had been a ledger, and somewhere in that ledger, a page with a child's name on it, and that child had been *them.*

"The vaults are this way," Serah said quietly.

Ansel hadn't told her they were going to the vaults. They weren't sure they'd decided it themselves. But their feet knew—some smuggler's instinct for hidden things, some pull in the blood—and the corridors of the court seemed willing to be known tonight. The walls here were pale stone shot through with veins of moon-iron, the same ore Ansel had carried down these mountains for seven years without ever asking where it came from. Now they knew. It came from here. Everything came from here. The mountain gave up its silver and its children both, and called it fair trade.

Serah walked half a step behind, her hand never far from the broken hilt of her oath-blade. She had not spoken since the throne room. Once, in the corridor of singing doors, she had opened her mouth, and Ansel had said, "Don't," and she hadn't. That was the whole of their alliance so far: a shared talent for knowing when silence was the only mercy available.

The stair down was cut in a spiral, each step worn concave by feet far older than any contract. Cold rose up to meet them, cold with a smell in it—loam and candlewax and something sweeter underneath, like apples going soft in a cellar. Ansel had smelled that sweetness before. In Hessel's cottage, the day they found the papers. They had thought then that it was only rot.

It was not rot.

The vault doors were open.

That was the first wrong thing. A court where every debt was honored, every contract sealed and countersigned—a court like that should have locked its treasures behind iron and oath-fire. Instead the doors stood wide as a welcome, twin slabs of moon-iron carved with thirteen crescents apiece, and beyond them the light was warm as a hearth.

"It wants us to see," Serah said.

"I know."

"It's showing us."

"I said I *know.*" Ansel's voice cracked on the second word, and they hated it, hated the tremor, hated the way Serah looked at them—not with pity, worse, with recognition. "Let it show me, then. Let me see what my name bought."

They went in together.

* * *

The vault was a cathedral built by someone who had heard of cathedrals only in description. The ceiling arched high and vanished into shadow; the pillars were grown, not carved, pale root-stone winding upward like frozen ivy. And the shelves—

The shelves went on forever.

Row upon row upon row, receding into the dark until the lamplight failed, each one lined with jars. Not glass. Something finer, something like blown frost, and inside each jar a light: small, slow, breathing. Some burned white as winter stars. Some flickered amber, or green, or the deep bruised violet of dusk. Thousands of them. Tens of thousands. Each one pulsing gently, in and out, in and out, like sleeping lungs.

Like children breathing in their sleep.

Ansel stopped walking. Their body simply declined to continue, and for a long moment they stood in the aisle between two endless rows while the lights breathed around them, and the sweet apple smell filled their mouth until they could taste it at the back of their throat.

"They're labeled," Serah said. Her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone holding themselves together with discipline alone.

Ansel looked. Of course they were. Small tags of vellum wired to each jar's neck, inked in a hand so fine it might have been done with a single hair. Ansel lifted the nearest one close to their eyes and read:

*Merrow, Tam. Of Lowmarsh. Age six years. Tendered in payment of grain-debt, famine year. Contract sound. Interest accruing.*

Lowmarsh. Ansel knew the Merrows. Knew old Tam Merrow's grandfather, a stooped man who mended nets by the millpond and never spoke above a whisper. Sixty years he'd been whispering, maybe. Sixty years since his brother sold a child for grain.

Ansel put the tag down very carefully, the way one puts down something that might go off, and took the next.

*Aldous, Sef. Of Harrowgate. Age nine. Tendered by father, gaming debts. Contract sound.*

And the next.

*Bray, Ilse. Of Thornhollow. Age four. Tendered by mother, fever-cure for elder daughter. Contract sound. Note: tendered weeping. Mercy discount applied.*

*Mercy discount applied.*

Ansel read it three times. The words did not change. Somewhere in the dark of the vault, ten thousand small lights breathed in and out, and every one of them had a tag, and every tag had a name, and every name was a village Ansel had walked through, traded in, drunk ale in, slept in barns of—and every one of those villages had given up its children, one by one, generation after generation, and no one had ever come down here to count them.

"No wonder the gods are waking," Ansel said. Their voice came out strange, scraped thin. "No wonder the shrines lit up. We've been feeding our children to this place for—" They turned in a slow circle, taking in the shelves, the shelves, the shelves. "How long? How long has this been going on?"

"Longer than my order," Serah said. "Longer than the kingdoms, maybe. The oath-keepers' oldest records call the court *the Quiet Creditor.* We thought it was a metaphor." She laughed, and it was an awful sound, a laugh with nothing in it. "We thought a great many things were metaphors."

Ansel walked. They couldn't help it. The aisle drew them deeper, and they read as they went, tag after tag, village after village. Lowmarsh, Harrowgate, Thornhollow, Millbeck, the high shepherding steads of Crag Fell, the river towns, the pass towns—every settlement in the Thornfell was represented, some by a single jar, some by dozens. Children sold by the desperate, and the greedy, and—the ones that made Ansel's hands shake worst—the well-meaning. Mothers who traded one child to save three. Fathers who signed away a sickly son so the rest would eat. Grandmothers who genuinely believed the stories, that the court beneath was kind, that the children went to a better country under the hill.

Maybe they believed it. Maybe the believing made the ink dry faster.

Here was a jar tagged in a different hand, older, the letters looped and formal: *Hessel, Ansel. Of the witch's cottage, Thornfell verge. Age eleven years. Tendered by guardian in satisfaction of a debt incurred before the named party's birth. Contract sound. Deferred collection.*

*Deferred.*

Ansel stood before their own name for a long time. Eleven years old. The age they'd been when they ran. All those years thinking they'd escaped, thinking the mountain roads were theirs because they'd earned them, because they were clever, because they trusted no one—all those years of invisibility, and it had never been invisibility at all. It had been *deferral.* The court had let them run the way a farmer lets a pig fatten.

Or the way a fisherman leaves bait in the water.

The thought arrived whole, cold as the vault air, and Ansel went very still.

"Wren's jar isn't here."

Serah looked up from a shelf nearby. "What?"

Ansel was already moving, scanning the tags faster now, the H's, the W's, the shelf where a *Wren* of Lowmarsh ought to be. Row after row. Nothing. They checked again, slower, refusing to hope. Nothing. "Every village. Every name. But not Wren." They turned to Serah, and the fury that had been building in their chest all this while finally found its shape, its edge, its purpose. "The steward said the price was already paid. Said the contract was sound. If Wren's soul were shelved here like the others, they'd have shown us the jar and been done with it. One more transaction closed."

"But they didn't," Serah said slowly.

"They held it back." Ansel's heart was hammering now, but it wasn't fear anymore, or not only fear. It was the hot clean feeling of a road making sense at last. "They're keeping it in the throne room. Where we saw it. Where the steward made sure we saw it. Wren isn't collateral, Serah. Wren is *bait.*"

"For you."

"For me. For the deferred contract. For the name Mother Hessel signed." Ansel laughed, and the laugh came out hard and bright and dangerous, and they found they liked how it sounded. "Seven years I survived by trusting no one and hiding from everything. And the whole time, the trap wasn't on the road or in the pass or in the next village over. The trap was my own name, sitting in a ledger, waiting for me to want something badly enough to come claim it."

They started back up the aisle, past the breathing lights, past the tags and the names and the mercy discounts, and this time they did not look at the jars. Looking was what the vault wanted. Grief was what the vault wanted, grief gathered and carried back to the throne room like an offering, so that Ansel would bargain soft and grateful and desperate.

Well. Let the court learn what kind of smuggler it had invited in.

At the vault doors Ansel stopped, and pressed one palm flat against the moon-iron, and felt the faint pulse of the ore answering the waning moon overhead, thirteen turns nearly spent.

"Serah. When we go back up there—I'm not going to bargain."

She studied them, firelight and jarlight moving on her scarred face. "No?"

"I'm going to break something."

* * *

The way back to the throne room was shorter than the way out, which was itself an answer of sorts. The court was done hiding its intentions. The corridors of singing doors had gone silent, the stones no longer murmured their tolls, and the pale veins of moon-iron in the walls glowed brighter with every step, lighting their way like a path of lamps laid down for honored guests.

Honored guests. Baited hooks. The same thing, down here.

As they climbed, Ansel became aware of a weight against their chest—the smuggler's satchel they had carried through every pass in the Thornfell, the one that had never once left their body in seven years. Inside it, wrapped in oilcloth: the last of the moon-iron shipment, the ore they'd been hauling south when the shrine flickered alight and the roads began to shift. Sacred metal. Praying metal. Ore that the old gods had once blessed, back when anyone bothered to ask them.

All those years, Ansel realized, they had been carrying prayers up and down the mountain and never known it. Every ingot a whispered plea. Every delivery a devotion.

The waking gods knew their name now. The court knew their name. Soon enough, so would every village whose children slept in jars below.

Ansel tightened the satchel strap across their shoulder and climbed toward the Moon-Throne, and the fury in their chest had hardened into something with weight and edges, something that could be carried, something that could be *used.*

Above them, at the top of the spiral stair, the doors to the throne room stood open, and the light within was the color of a held breath.

The final turn of the moon had begun.
