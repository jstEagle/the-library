## 8. The Price of Mercy

They came for Serah in the hour the court called morning, though no sun had ever reached this deep.

She knew it was coming. In the seven days since they had descended through the moon-door, she had learned the rhythm of the place: the way the pale lamps along the vaulted corridors brightened and dimmed like a slow breath, the way the bone-china cups arrived unbidden with tea that tasted of whatever you most missed, the way the servants—smooth-faced, sexless, their livery stitched from moth-wing grey—appeared exactly when wanted and vanished before thanks could be offered. Nothing here was free. Even courtesy accrued interest.

So when three stewards-in-waiting glided into her guest chamber and bowed, and the tallest said, *The Court requests the honor of your presence before the Moon-Throne*, Serah set down the cup she had not drunk from, straightened her ruined surcoat—the Ironmark sigil scratched out but still ghosting beneath the gouges—and went.

Ansel was already there.

They stood at the base of the dais steps in their road-worn leathers, arms crossed, jaw tight, looking for all the mountain's depths like someone being asked to pay a bill they'd already disputed twice. The sight of them steadied Serah more than she wanted to admit. Seven days ago she would have said nothing about a smuggler could steady her. Seven days ago she had still believed her order might take her back.

The Moon-Throne rose above them both, a crescent of white stone grown—not carved, *grown*—from the living rock, its horns curving down like tusks over the seat where the steward sat. The steward itself was a tall figure robed in ledger-leather, its face a smooth oval of shadow except when it turned, and then for one disorienting instant you saw pages. Columns of names. Debts entered and discharged. Serah had made the mistake of meeting its gaze on the first day and had dreamed in bookkeeping ever since.

"Serah Ironmark," the steward said. Its voice was dry paper turning. "Formerly Oath-Keeper of the Third Vigil. Formerly Shield of the Vale of Corryn. Formerly."

The word hung in the cold air like a blade mid-fall.

"You were stripped of your oath for refusing to burn the god-touched village of Hollowmere." It did not ask; it recited. Somewhere in that robe of leather and vellum, she understood, her whole life was written out in fair hand. "You knelt in the ash-road while your brothers and sisters of the order passed you by, and you watched the fire take a chapel where an old god had merely been *remembered*. Tell us, Ironmark. Do you regret it?"

"I regret that I was slow," Serah said. "I should have stood in front of the chapel door sooner."

Something moved behind the steward's shadow-face. She chose to believe it was not amusement.

"We offer restoration," it said.

The words fell into the throne room like stones into a well, and Serah felt the ripples of them go through her chest before her mind could catch up. Restoration. The word she had not let herself think in two years, because thinking it was like pressing on a bruise to prove it still hurt. Her oath. Her name spoken clean again. The grey wall of the Vigil-hall at dawn, a hundred voices saying the words of binding, and hers among them, *hers*—

"The order cast you out for mercy," the steward continued, silk over gravel. "We do not make that error. Mercy is honored here. Mercy is *priced* here, which is better than being praised for it and starved. Take up the office of Steward-in-Waiting. Keep our ledgers. Hold our keys. Wear the moth-grey, and beneath it, wear your own name again, whole and unsullied. Your oath will be re-sworn before this throne, and no power above or below the Thornfell will be able to say you are fallen."

The lamps dimmed and brightened. Somewhere far off, water counted drops against stone.

"And the price?" Serah asked, because in this place there was always a price, and asking after it was the only honest prayer left to her.

"The smuggler." The steward's page-face turned toward Ansel. "Leave them to the dark between turns. Walk away, and the child Wren's soul passes to your keeping—free, clear, no lien, no interest. You will carry it up the mountain yourself and give it back to its body with our blessing. And Ansel Vane remains below, as the contract requires. A soul was sold. A soul must stay. The arithmetic does not care whose hand held the pen."

Serah looked at Ansel.

She had expected fury. What she saw was worse: a kind of tired recognition, as if Ansel had done this arithmetic themselves a hundred times in the dark of some pass, running numbers that always came out the same. *One child saved. One smuggler spent. Fair trade.* Seven years of trusting no one, and here was the proof they'd been right to.

"Well," Ansel said quietly. "There it is."

"No," Serah said.

The steward's head tilted. "You have not heard the full terms."

"I heard enough. You're offering me back my name if I sell someone else's soul to buy it." She stepped forward, boots loud on the pale stone, louder than she'd meant to be. "That's not restoration. That's just the fire at Hollowmere with better bookkeeping. My order burned a village to keep a ledger of purity, and I refused, and I have eaten dirt and regret for two years, and I would eat twenty more rather than sign my name under yours."

"Your name is *already signed*," the steward said, and now the dry voice gathered weight, became the sound of a closing cover, a sealing wax, a door. "It has been signed since the day you knelt in the ash-road and swore, alone, unwitnessed, that no power would ever again purchase your mercy. Do you imagine oaths expire? We hold every vow spoken beneath the moon, Ironmark. Yours is the most interesting entry in a century. You swore yourself *against* us. Which means we may claim you—or release you—at our pleasure. Refuse the office, and we exercise the former."

The lamps went low.

And Serah understood, with the cold clarity of a blade finally finding the gap in armor, that the offer had never been an offer. It had been an invoice. The court did not want her service. It wanted her *consent*—wanted the woman who had defied a pyre to kneel willingly in the moth-grey, because a broken saint was worth ten thousand souls in collateral, and they had been patient.

Her hand found the hilt of her sword out of pure habit. Useless here, and she knew it. But her grandmother had put that sword in her hands with a saying Serah had carried through two years of ash and hunger: *A mark is not what's cut into the steel. It's what the steel refuses to become.*

"I refuse," she said. "Not the mercy. The terms. I'll walk out of here with Ansel and the child's soul, or I'll walk out of here with nothing, but I will not walk out of here wearing your livery and calling it honor."

For a long moment the throne room was silent as a sealed tomb.

Then the steward laughed. It was a terrible sound, dry leaves and turning pages, and every lamp in the vast chamber guttered with it.

"Oh, *good*," it said. "We had hoped. A refusal freely given is worth so much more than a surrender bought. Steward—" and it gestured, and the smooth-faced servants moved, "—enter it into the record. The Ironmark declines redemption. Let the dark between turns receive her, and the smuggler with her. Let them learn what the thirteenth turn costs those who stand outside the arithmetic."

The floor opened like a book.

* * *

Falling was brief. Landing was not.

Serah hit wet stone in the dark, shoulder-first, and rolled, and came up with her sword already drawn in a blackness so complete it seemed to press on her eyes. Somewhere close, stone clattered—Ansel, landing harder, swearing in the flat cadence of the high passes.

"Serah." A pause, breath. "Tell me you didn't just do what I think you did."

"Depends what you think I did."

"Threw away your whole life for a smuggler who's spent seven years making sure nobody had a reason to throw anything away for them."

She sheathed the sword because there was nothing to fight yet, only the dark, and the dark deserved no courtesies. "I threw away a life I'd already lost. This one I'm keeping."

Silence. Then, very quietly, Ansel laughed—a short, cracked sound, half wonder, half grief. "Mother Hessel used to say the court always offers you the thing you want most, priced in the thing you can least spare. I thought she meant coin."

"She meant exactly this."

Above them, impossibly far, a seam of pale light showed where the floor had closed. And across it, faint as breath on glass, the light began to change—reddening, tightening, narrowing, as somewhere overhead the waning moon slid into its final turn. Serah felt it in her teeth the way she'd once felt storms coming off the Corryn vale. Hours. Perhaps fewer.

"Ansel." She groped along the wall until her fingers found wet stone, then found Ansel's sleeve. "The old gods. The moon-iron you've carried all these years—you told me the shrines started burning the night you crossed the shrine at the pass. You've been carrying their prayers like embers in a pack. If any part of that is true—if any spark of it is left in you—"

"It's true." Ansel's voice had gone strange, distant, the voice of someone listening to something very far away. "Serah. The dark down here—it isn't empty. Can't you hear it?"

And Serah, oathless, disgraced, refused and thrown down, listened—and beneath the drip of water and the groan of settling stone, she heard it too. A murmur. A gathering. Like a hundred whispered prayers rising through the roots of the mountain, all of them saying the same two words, over and over, patient as stone:

*Answer us. Answer us.*

The last turn had begun.
