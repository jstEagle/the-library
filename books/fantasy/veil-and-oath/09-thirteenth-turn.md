## 9. Thirteenth Turn

The dark beneath the mountain had a heartbeat, and Ansel could feel it slowing.

They crouched in the wreckage of the steward's vault, where the shelves of soul-jars gleamed like a harvest of drowned stars. Hours. That was all the moon's last turn allowed them—hours before the thirteenth door sealed for another age, and everything they'd found here, every name on every shelf, would be folded back into silence.

Ansel lifted Wren's jar from its cradle of black velvet and felt it tremble against their palms, warm as a sleeping bird. Inside, a light no bigger than a candleflame turned slowly, stubbornly, the way Wren used to turn in sleep, one fist curled under her chin.

*Hold on,* Ansel thought at it. *I'm coming. I was always coming.*

Behind them, Serah Ironmark stood in the shattered doorway with her broken oath-blade drawn, its edge still singing faintly from where she'd reforged it against the truth of her own disgrace. The steward's host filled the corridor beyond—a press of courtiers whose faces were beautiful the way frost on a window is beautiful, all of them smiling, none of them blinking.

"You cannot carry that out," the steward said. Its voice came from everywhere, patient as water wearing down stone. "The contract is signed. The name upon it is *yours*, Ansel Vane. You are not Wren's rescuer. You are her collateral."

"I know." Ansel's voice came out steadier than they felt. Seven years of invisibility, seven years of being no one on every road—and now their name rang through this court like a bell struck too hard. "I've known since I read the ledger. Hessel sold me first. Wren was just the interest."

"Then you understand there is nothing to steal."

"No." Ansel rose, the jar cradled against their chest. "I understand there's something to *void*."

* * *

They ran for the throne room through corridors that had begun, subtly, to resist them—the walls leaning closer, the torchlight guttering blue, the floor tilting by degrees so subtle it might have been imagination. But Ansel had spent seven years learning how mountains moved when they didn't want to be crossed, and these were only the small shivers of a thing deciding whether to wake.

Because that was the secret the smugglers whispered and never wrote down: moon-iron was not metal. It was *prayer*. Ore pulled from the high passes where the old shrines stood buried, heavy with centuries of whispered wishes from people who no longer remembered why they climbed there, only that someone should be listening. Every shipment Ansel had ever carried had been a cargo of unanswered voices.

And the gods were waking now. They'd felt it in chapter after chapter of their life on the mountain—in the shrine that flickered alight, in the roads that looped and demanded memory as toll, in the river that had taken their mother's face and left them grieving something they could no longer picture. The old gods were rising through the stone like sap through a tree, and they knew the name of the one who had carried their prayers longest.

Ansel skidded into the throne room and stopped.

The Moon-Throne dominated the cavern like a wound in the world—white stone veined with silver, and above it, suspended in chains of woven shadow, hung the Ledger. Not a book. A *mass*. Thousands of pages bound in hide that had never belonged to any animal, each page dense with names and terms and signatures, breathing slowly, contracting and expanding like the lung of some vast sleeping animal. Every debt in the Thornfell. Every hidden child. Every bargain struck in desperation across two hundred years, honored to the letter and weaponized in spirit.

"There it is," Serah said softly behind them. Her blade hummed. "Two hundred years of mercy as a trap."

"The contracts—" Ansel began.

"Are voided if the ledger dies. Yes. My order taught me that much before they taught me obedience." She stepped past Ansel, planting herself between them and the doors through which the host would come. Her voice dropped, roughened. "When it breaks, everything breaks. The court will come apart around us. Whatever happens—don't stop moving."

"And you?"

Serah smiled, and it was the first true smile Ansel had seen from her—not the polished courtesy of an oath-keeper but something rawer, older, the expression of a woman who had finally found the fight she'd been exiled for refusing to abandon.

"I spent ten years believing my honor lived in my oath," she said. "Turns out it lives here." She rolled her shoulders, set her feet. "Go. Break your mountain loose."

* * *

The host came through the doors like winter coming through a broken window.

Serah met them alone.

Ansel did not watch—could not afford to watch—but heard it all: the ring of the reforged blade, the wet sound of shadow parting, Serah's voice calling out the old words of her order, not the liturgy of obedience but the *first* oath, the one carved on the founding stones that everyone had forgotten: *We keep faith with the forgotten.* The court shrieked at those words. Frost-beautiful faces cracked like river ice.

Ansel climbed.

The dais steps were cold and each one whispered a name as they passed—debtors long dead, their grandchildren's children still paying. Ansel pressed Wren's jar tighter and kept climbing, and at the top, beneath the breathing mass of the Ledger, they knelt and did the thing Mother Hessel had taught them first, before anything else, before the betrayal, back when they were small and the witch's hands were gentle:

*How do you speak to what has no ears, little wren? You don't. You become the wind.*

Ansel opened the satchel at their hip and poured out the last of the moon-iron they'd been carrying since Lowmarsh—raw ore, unsmelted, heavy with two hundred years of unanswered prayer. It spilled across the dais like a scatter of grey stars, and Ansel laid both hands flat upon it and spoke aloud, into the stone, into the roots of the mountain, into the dark where old things were stirring:

"You know me. I carried you. Every load, every pass, every year—I carried your prayers up and down this mountain because no one else would touch them. I didn't know that's what I was doing. But you knew. You *knew me* when I was trying to be no one."

The ore began to glow. Faintly, then truly—a light like moonrise happening underground, silver bleeding up through the veins of the white throne.

"So I'm asking. Not bargaining. Asking. Wake up. All the way. And look at what's been done in the dark with your names."

The mountain answered.

It began as a sound below hearing—a deep, tectonic groan, the sound of stone remembering it was once alive. Then the veins of the Moon-Throne flared silver-white, and the Ledger *screamed*, every page convulsing at once, and the whole cavern shuddered as though a great animal trapped beneath the rock had turned over in its sleep.

Ansel seized the nearest chain of woven shadow and hauled themselves up into the breathing mass of pages. Names slid beneath their fingers—thousands of them, generations of them, children from Lowmarsh and Hollowmere and every village clinging to the Thornfell's skirts. Somewhere below, Serah was roaring defiance, and the doors were splintering, and there was no more time.

Ansel gripped the binding-hide with both hands, planted a foot against the throne's crown, and *pulled*.

The Ledger tore.

Not all at once—it came apart the way ice comes off a lake in spring, in groaning sheets, pages ripping free and dissolving into moths of silver light even as they fell. Contracts unraveled mid-word. Signatures faded like breath off glass. And with every name released, the mountain shook harder, dust cascading from the ceiling, the beautiful corridors beyond cracking open to show the raw stone they had always been.

The steward appeared at the base of the dais, and for the first time its face was not beautiful. It was *old*—old and furious and afraid, frost melting off features that had worn beauty like a mask for centuries.

"You don't understand what you're unmaking," it said. "Every debt honored. Every promise kept. Without us, the mountain forgets its bargains. People will suffer. People will *die* with their wishes unanswered—"

"People have been dying with their souls in jars," Ansel said, "while you invoiced their grief."

They wrenched free the final binding, and the Ledger came apart entirely—a blizzard of silver moths spiraling upward through the cracking ceiling, toward a crack of genuine night sky opening far above, toward the moon completing its turn. In the storm of released light, Ansel saw names rise: *Wren. Wren. Wren.* And others. Hundreds of others, streaming home through the stone.

The court began to fall.

* * *

They ran.

Serah caught up with them at the collapsing colonnade, bloodied, singed, grinning like a woman reborn, and together they sprinted through a kingdom eating itself—walls folding, gardens of pale mushrooms withering to dust, the river of memory bursting its banks and giving back, in one great silver flood, every toll it had ever taken. For half a heartbeat Ansel's mother's face flashed before them, warm and laughing and *theirs again*, and they nearly stumbled weeping, and Serah caught their arm and hauled them on.

Wren's jar burned steady against Ansel's chest, brighter now, impatient.

The thirteenth door stood ahead, already widening, moonlight pouring through it like water through a breached dam. Behind them the Moon-Throne split down the middle with a sound like the end of the world, and the steward's voice rose one last time over the ruin—not cursing, which would have been easier, but *promising*, soft and terrible:

"A debt this size does not vanish, Ansel Vane. It *transfers*. Something always collects."

Then the night took them, and the mountain closed its mouth, and the moon finished turning.

* * *

They surfaced on the high shoulder of the Thornfell beneath a sky ablaze with falling light—souls streaming home across the peaks like a migration of stars, seeking the villages, the cradles, the graves where they'd been mourned. Far below, in Lowmarsh, lights were kindling in windows. On every hilltop, the buried shrines were burning with cold silver fire, and Ansel could hear, faintly, the sound of people waking and walking out of their houses to look at them.

Serah sank down onto a boulder, laughing breathlessly at the ruined sky. Ansel knelt, cracked the wax seal, and opened the jar.

Wren's soul rose like a spark off a bonfire, hovered—one moment, two, turning slowly the way she turned in sleep—and dove into the mountain earth, southward, toward a fevered child in a village bed who would, within the hour, open her eyes and ask for supper.

Ansel stayed kneeling a long time, hands empty, shaking.

"You heard it," Serah said quietly. "What the steward said. Debts transfer."

"I heard it." Ansel looked up. Across the peaks, the burning shrines were all facing them—all of them, every flame bent like a compass needle toward the place where they knelt. The old gods knew their name now. So did the mountain. So, soon, would every soul in the Thornfell, because the released dead were carrying the story home along with the light.

Seven years invisible. Over in a single night.

Ansel stood, legs unsteady, and watched the fires burn, and felt the strangest thing rising in their chest—not fear, though fear was there too, but something underneath it, green and stubborn as spring growth through rubble.

Purpose.

"We need to get down to Lowmarsh," they said. "Before the story arrives without us."

Serah raised an eyebrow. "And when it does? When every village on this mountain knows the name Ansel Vane?"

Ansel flexed their empty hands, feeling the ghost-weight of the jar, the ghost-light of the ledger-moths, the weight of a promise they hadn't yet spoken aloud.

"Then they'll find me ready to answer for it," they said, and started down the mountain as the shrines burned brighter behind them—and somewhere far below, in a fever-dark room in Lowmarsh, a child sat bolt upright in bed, wide awake, saying a name she'd never learned: *Ansel. Ansel. Ansel.*
