## 10. Visible

The last of the moon's light went out above them as they climbed, and the mountain did not go dark.

That was the first wrongness—or the first rightness, Ansel could not yet decide. For seven years they had moved through the Thornfell by starlight and cunning, reading the passes the way other folk read scripture, and never once had the stone itself offered light. Now it did. Veins of pale silver ran through the rock like marrow, pulsing faintly in time with something vast and slow beneath them, and where Ansel set their hand to steady themselves on a boulder, the silver brightened under their palm, as if the mountain recognized a touch it had been waiting for.

Behind them, far below, the court was dying with a sound like a cathedral of ice settling into a summer lake. Ansel did not look back. They carried Wren against their chest, wrapped in Serah's oath-cloak, and the child's fever had broken somewhere in the ninth turn—broken all at once, like a knot slipping free—and now Wren slept the deep, untroubled sleep of the truly safe, breath fogging warm against Ansel's throat.

"You're limping," Serah said from behind. Her voice was raw; she had been shouting the steward's host back for the better part of an hour, and her oath-blade was notched like a saw.

"I'm walking."

"You're limping *and* walking. There's a difference, and I'll be carrying you if you pretend otherwise."

Ansel almost smiled. It felt strange on their face, that motion, like a door long rusted shut grinding open a finger's width. "Save your chivalry, Ironmark. We're not out yet."

But they were nearly out. Ansel knew these heights blindfolded, knew the smell of juniper at the tree line and the particular cold that pooled in the high hollows like water in a cup, and when they came up over the shoulder of the pass where the thirteenth turn ended, the sky opened above them—vast, black, and burning.

Not with stars. With shrines.

Every ridge across the Thornfell wore a crown of flame. The little way-shrines, the moss-eaten cairns, the forgotten altars tucked into cliff-crooks where smugglers left offerings without knowing why—all of them alight, all of them burning with that same silver-white fire that did not consume, and between them, across the valleys, Ansel could see smaller lights moving. Lanterns. Villagers, climbing out of their houses in the dead of night, drawn by dreams or by the pull in their own blood, standing before the burning shrines with their faces turned upward.

"They're answering," Serah said quietly. "All of them. The gods called, and the people are answering."

"The gods didn't call them." Ansel shifted Wren's weight. "I did. When I broke the ledger, every prayer I ever carried—" They stopped. Swallowed. Seven years of smuggling moon-iron down the hidden roads, seven years of sacred weight against their spine, and they had told themselves it was only cargo. "Every prayer I carried went through me. I was the road. And when the ledger shattered, everything I'd been carrying poured out through every village I'd ever passed."

Serah looked at them for a long moment. In the shrine-light her face was stripped of its usual iron; what remained underneath was younger, and kinder, and more afraid. "Then they know your name," she said. Not a question.

"They've always known my name." Ansel's voice came out flat. "That was the whole point. That was Hessel's bargain—I would be invisible, unclaimed, unwritten, so that no god and no court could find me. And she sold the space she made anyway. She sold my name to buy her own quiet death." The fury rose again, hot and familiar, and then—strangely—it crested and broke, and what was left underneath was only grief, old and tired. "Seven years I thought I was hiding. I was just inventory."

* * *

They came down into Lowmarsh at grey dawn, and the village was waiting.

Ansel had planned, in the hours of the climb, for every possibility except this: not torches, not pitchforks, not the priest with his warding signs. Just—the village. Standing in the lane. Old Marta with her shawl pinned crooked because she'd dressed in the dark. The miller's boys, barefoot in the frost. Father Aldous without his vestments, looking smaller than his robes, holding his hat in his hands like a man come to apologize.

And behind them, in the dooryard of the tanner's cottage, Wren's mother—alive, weeping, hands pressed to her mouth.

Wren woke as Ansel crossed the threshold of the village, stirred against their chest, and opened eyes that were clear and brown and utterly their own. No shadow behind them. No antlered reflection. Just a child waking in the cold morning, confused, and then seeing their mother, and then crying out—a small, cracked, human sound that undid something in Ansel's chest that had been clenched since the night at the window.

Ansel handed Wren down into their mother's arms and stepped back, and the crowd parted around the reunion the way water parts around a stone, and then closed again—with Ansel inside it.

That was the thing. That was the unbearable, impossible thing. Nobody stepped away from them. Nobody crossed themselves. Old Marta looked at them with wet eyes and said, "You're Hessel's girl, aren't you. I knew your face. I always knew your face," and Ansel, who had spent seven years being no one's anything, found they had no answer at all.

"I'm not—" they started, and stopped. *I'm not hers anymore. I'm not anyone's.* But that wasn't true either, was it. They were the mountain's now. The gods' own courier, whether they'd asked for the post or not.

"Vane," Serah supplied, at their elbow, dry as flint. "Their name is Vane. And they're the reason your children will stop disappearing, if any of you have the sense to thank them instead of staring."

Father Aldous cleared his throat. "We owe you more than thanks," he began, in his pulpit voice, and Ansel held up a hand.

"Don't. Please." Their voice cracked on the word and they hated it. "Don't owe me anything. Debts are how this started. Debts are what the court *was*." They looked around at the faces, all of them open, all of them watching, and felt the old panic rise—the panic of being seen, of being known, of having a shape that others could grab hold of. Seven years of training screamed at them to slip backward, to find the hedgerow, the shadow, the road.

Instead they stood still.

*Be seen,* the mountain seemed to say, in its slow silver pulse. *And answer.*

* * *

They rebuilt the shrine at Lowmarsh that same day, because there was nothing else any of them could think to do with their hands.

It had been a heap of mossy stones at the edge of the barley field for as long as anyone remembered—older than the village, older than the parish, dedicated to a god whose name had worn off the standing stone generations ago. Now the central slab bore fresh fire, patient and white, and around it the villagers worked in the strange golden mood of people who have survived something they don't yet understand. The miller's boys hauled stones. Marta directed everyone and thanked no one. Serah, who had sworn off holy work when they stripped her order from her, knelt in the mud re-setting the foundation stones with a mason's precision, and if her eyes were red-rimmed, no one was fool enough to mention it.

Wren sat on the standing stone itself, legs swinging, fever-bright no longer but luminous in some quieter way, watching everything. At some point the child slid down and came to stand beside Ansel at the edge of the work, close enough that their shoulders touched.

"You went under the mountain for me," Wren said. Not a question. Children, Ansel was learning, had no use for questions when they already knew the answers.

"Someone had to."

"Marta says the old stories say the court keeps what it's given. Forever." Wren's chin lifted, stubborn. "But you got me back."

"The court kept what it was *owed*. It turns out it was owed nothing." Ansel looked down at the child—at the thin wrists, the frostbitten ears, the terrible ordinary aliveness of them. "Your ancestors' bargain died with the ledger. All of them did. Every contract on the mountain is ash now. Whatever the priests told your grandmother's grandmother, whatever price your family paid—" They paused, choosing honesty over comfort, because Wren deserved honesty; everyone on this mountain had paid too much for comfortable lies. "You were stolen, Wren. Not sold. Stolen. Remember that, when the stories get told. Make sure they tell it right."

Wren nodded solemnly, the way children nod at burdens too large to lift, and then ruined the gravity entirely by asking, "Is the oath-keeper lady going to stay?"

Ansel glanced across the shrine-yard to where Serah was arguing with two farmers about load-bearing angles, blade-less, orderless, and entirely in her element. "Ask her yourself. She'll lie about it, but ask."

By dusk the shrine stood whole. The standing stone had been scrubbed of moss, and in the last light Ansel finally read what generations of weather had failed to erase entirely: a name, half-legible, in letters older than any alphabet taught in the lowland schools. The god whose fire burned on the slab. The god whose prayers Ansel had carried on their back for seven years without once speaking aloud.

When Ansel spoke it now, quietly, the flame leaned toward them like a listener.

The villagers gathered. Not summoned—gathered, the way water gathers, the way the mountain's silver light had gathered under their palm. Marta and the miller and the tanner and Wren's mother with Wren's hand in hers, and Father Aldous standing awkwardly among them, a shepherd suddenly aware his flock had found a different fold. Serah came to stand at Ansel's left, close enough that their sleeves brushed, and said nothing, which from Serah was a whole speech.

Marta spoke first. "The shrines are lit all over the Thornfell. Every village. And every one of them, the fire looks at whoever comes near—looks *through* them. Except here." She nodded at Ansel. "Here it looks at you, and waits. So we're asking, since you're the one who knows the way of things now. What does it want?"

Ansel looked at the fire. The fire looked back, patient as bedrock, and Ansel understood that it wanted nothing—that wanting was the court's grammar, debts and prices and ledgers, and all of that was ash. This was different. This was older. This was simply a door, standing open, waiting to see who would walk through it.

"It doesn't want," Ansel said. "It asks. It's asking all of us the same question it asked me under the mountain, in the dark, when I was carrying its name in my mouth and didn't know it." They drew a breath, and the cold air went into them like clean water. "It asks: *will you be seen?* Not hidden. Not bargained. Not owned. Seen—and answering. That's the whole covenant. That's all it ever was, before we let the courts and the ledgers build walls over it."

"And what do we answer?" Wren asked, in the ringing silence.

Ansel knelt—not to the fire, but to the child, eye to eye—and heard themselves say, in a voice steadier than they had any right to expect:

"We answer yes."

They laid their palm on the standing stone, over the old name, and the silver light ran up their arm like dawn running along a ridge-line, and Ansel Vane—who had spent seven years perfecting the art of passing unseen, who had trusted no one and nothing, who had lost a mother's face to a river and a childhood to a witch's ledger—felt the last wall inside them come down, quietly, like snow sliding from a branch.

Serah put her hand beside theirs on the stone. Then Wren's small one. Then Marta's, gnarled and unashamed. One by one, the whole village, and beyond the village—Ansel could feel it, could *hear* it now, a murmur moving ridge to ridge across the Thornfell like wind through barley—hand after hand after hand, on stone after stone after stone, the whole mountain answering.

The fire on the shrine rose tall and white and joyous, and somewhere far below, in the deep places where a court had crumbled into honest rubble, the mountain's slow silver pulse quickened—like a heart remembering its rhythm.

Only afterward, when the crowd had thinned to embers and laughter and the smell of Marta's stew, did Serah speak, low, at Ansel's shoulder.

"You know they'll come now. Pilgrims. Petitioners. People with problems no priest can solve, looking for the one who walked into the dark and walked back out with a child." A pause. "People like we used to be."

Ansel watched the shrine-fire bend in the evening wind, steady, unhidden, lighting the road down into Lowmarsh for anyone who might need to walk it in the night.

"Then they'll know where to find me," Ansel said. "That's rather the point."
