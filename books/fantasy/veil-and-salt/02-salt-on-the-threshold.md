## 2. Salt on the Threshold

Edmund did not sleep.

He told himself it was the damp, the way Greyhaven's autumn fog crept under doors and settled in the bones like a tenant who paid no rent. He told himself it was the luck-knot, that he was simply running the knot's weave over in his mind the way a mason checks mortar, looking for cracks. He told himself a great many things between midnight and the grey hour before dawn, and by the time the gulls began their quarrels over the fish-market gutters, he had told himself so much that the truth had been buried alive somewhere beneath all of it, still breathing, waiting to be dug up.

The truth was this: he had felt the prayer land.

Fifteen years of selling charms that were nothing—blessed thread, knotted rope, salt in folded paper, small comforts with no more power than a mother's kiss—and in fifteen years not one of them had ever *answered*. That was the whole architecture of his safety. Harmless. Deniable. A charm-seller whose charms did nothing was a fraud, and frauds were fined, not burned. The Edict of Ash had teeth, but teeth only closed on the guilty.

And last night, in the dark behind his shuttered stall, he had felt Tam Wicker's grief go out through the knot like heat through iron—and something on the far side of the world had turned over, slow as a whale sounding, and *listened*.

Edmund dressed in the dark. Wool shirt, oilskin coat, boots with the heels worn crooked from fifteen years of walking the same streets in the same careful way. He took up his satchel—the one with the false bottom, the one every charm-seller in Greyhaven carried, though most of them only hid tax-evasion ledgers in theirs—and let himself out into the fog.

* * *

The Saltrow district woke slowly, the way old men wake: reluctantly, painfully, with complaints about the weather. Fish-carts rattled over cobbles slick with brine. A woman beat a rug from a second-floor window with the rhythm of someone punishing it for personal reasons. Smoke rose from a hundred chimneys and was immediately swallowed by the sea-haze, so that the whole city seemed to be exhaling into a pillow.

Tam Wicker's house stood at the end of Saltrow Lane where it bent toward the harbor wall—a narrow, salt-stained thing with three windows and a door painted the blue of bad weather. Edmund had sold charms within sight of that door for years. He knew its history without wanting to: the wife, Ilse, lost to the fever two winters back; the son, Jory, taken by the sea last month when the *Kindly Fortune* went down in a squall with five hands and no survivors. Tam had come to the night market with his grief worn smooth as sea-glass, the way dockworkers wear theirs, and had bought a luck-knot for a drowned man's door because there was nothing else left to buy.

Edmund stopped ten paces from the threshold and stood very still.

There was salt on the ground.

Not scattered, not spilled, not tracked in from the harbor road the way salt got everywhere in Greyhaven until it seemed the city itself might dissolve. This salt lay in a ring—a perfect, unbroken circle around the doorway, fine as flour, white as bone, each grain sitting exactly where it sat as if it had been *placed*. And even from ten paces, even through the fog, Edmund could see that it was wrong in a way that made his skin try to crawl off his body.

Because it was burning.

Not with flame. With light. A low, deep luminescence, like coals seen through ash, like the last ember of a fire that had been enormous once. The grains glimmered and pulsed, dimmed and brightened, and if you watched long enough—and Edmund watched, hating himself for watching—you could see that the pulsing had a rhythm. A cadence. It rose and fell the way language rises and falls.

It wasn't burning like a fire. It was burning like scripture.

Edmund had seen scripture burn once. Only once, and he had been young, and it had been in another country entirely—one that no longer appeared on any map, including his own. He had watched a page of the *Liturgy of Names* consumed in a brazier while an old priest wept, and the flames had moved the way these salt-grains pulsed now: syllable, syllable, rest. Syllable, syllable, rest. As if the words refused to stop being words just because they were being destroyed.

He crouched at the edge of the ring, careful not to touch it, and looked closer. Within the circle of salt, the doorstep itself was dry—not merely dry but *parched*, the stone bleached pale, as though something had drunk the moisture out of it down past the water table, down past patience. And on the door's blue paint, faint as breath on glass, was a mark. A spiral. Turning inward, turning down, the way water turns when it finds a drain.

Maren's mark. The tide-goddess's sign, drawn the way she had been drawn in temples before Edmund was born—in rings, in spirals, in the patient geometry of water finding its level.

"No," Edmund said quietly, to no one, to himself, to the fog. "No, no. I sealed you."

His voice cracked on the last word, and he pressed his lips together hard enough to hurt.

Because that was the other truth, the one beneath the truth, the one he kept in the deepest room of himself where even he rarely visited: he hadn't merely witnessed the silencing of the gods. He had helped build it. His maps—his beautiful, meticulous, god-realm-spanning maps—had been the scaffolding. Every seal laid over every divine name in Greyhaven and beyond had been drawn from his cartography, measured against his lines, anchored to his coordinates. The Edict of Ash had needed a mapmaker the way a hanging needs a rope.

And Edmund Coldharbour had been very, very good at maps.

* * *

"Master Charm-seller?"

He came up out of the crouch too fast, heart slamming, one hand already inside his coat where he kept—nothing, actually, nothing but a stub of chalk and a folded prayer he'd never had the courage to burn or keep. Fifteen years of caution, and still his first instinct was the old one: reach for power. Some habits were carved deeper than the Edict.

A child stood in the lane. A girl, perhaps eight, wrapped in a man's coat cut down badly at the sleeves, her hair stuck to her forehead with fog-damp. She had the look of the Saltrow children—sharp-eyed, underfed, old in the ways that mattered—but there was something else in her face this morning, something that made Edmund's stomach drop before she said a word.

She was looking at the ring of salt. She was looking at it the way people used to look at shrines.

"You're the charm-man," she said. "From the night market. You sold my neighbor's da a knot."

"I sell thread," Edmund said automatically. "Knots are just thread, arranged."

"It worked." Her voice dropped to the fierce whisper children use when they have discovered something enormous and know the adults will ruin it. "It *worked*, master. Da prayed at the door last night, after you left, and the water—" She stopped, swallowed, started again. "The harbor gave him back a boot. Jory's boot. It came up on the tide and knocked at our door like it was knocking to come home. Nobody's boot comes back off the *Fortune*'s wreck. The wreckers dive it every spring and find nothing but nails." Her chin trembled, and she mastered it the way her father must have taught her, jaw set, eyes fierce. "Something answered him. Something *old* answered him."

Edmund looked at the boot.

It sat on the doorstep, inside the ring of salt, upright, laced, filled to the brim with clean seawater that did not spill. It gleamed. It was impossible. And it was warm—he could feel the warmth of it from here, a hearth-warmth, a welcome-warmth, radiating off the leather like the memory of a body.

"That's tide-scum and coincidence," Edmund heard himself say, and his voice came out flat and cold and cruel, crueler than he intended, crueler than the moment deserved. "Boots wash ashore. Grief makes patterns out of nothing. Go home, girl."

The child flinched as if struck.

And then—because children are honest in the way that adults spend decades learning not to be—she stepped closer instead of away, close enough that he could see the salt-light reflected in her eyes, two tiny burning circles, and held out her hand.

In her palm: a copper bit, worn thin, probably everything she had.

"I want a charm that really works," she said. "Not thread. Not knots. One that really works, like his did. My mam's sick and the physickers want coin I don't have, and if something answered Tam Wicker then something can answer me, and I'll pay, I've got it right here, please—"

"*No.*"

The word came out of him like a slap. Louder than he meant. Harder. The girl recoiled, and a fishwife two doors down turned to look, and Edmund felt the whole street's attention begin to swing toward him like a searchlight, and he hated it, hated her, hated the copper bit shining in her small palm, hated the word *works* sitting in the open air between them where anyone could hear it, anyone, an Inquisitor, a neighbor, a spy for the Council's tithe-collectors who paid silver for the names of charm-sellers whose charms did anything at all.

"Magic is outlawed," he said, quieter now, each word placed like a stone. "By the Edict of Ash. Fifteen years ago. Anyone found practicing it is burned, and anyone found *buying* it is flogged, and I will not be party to either. Take your coin and buy bread. Bread works."

The girl closed her fist around the copper. For a moment she looked at him with an expression no eight-year-old should have had to learn—that flat, measuring look, the look of someone revising an estimate downward—and then she spat, neatly, into the gutter at his feet, and walked away down Saltrow Lane with her father's coat flapping around her stick-thin legs.

Edmund stood alone on the cobbles with the impossible boot glowing gently behind him, and felt the guilt arrive the way the fog arrived: everywhere at once, from every direction, already inside.

*That really works.*

As if he had ever sold anything else. As if the whole gray machinery of his survival—the harmless knots, the deniable thread, the fraud's life built grain by grain over fifteen years—had ever been anything but a betrayal conducted one customer at a time. He thought of Tam Wicker's face in the lamplight, the grief worn smooth, the hope held carefully like something already broken. He thought of the girl's copper bit. He thought of a page of scripture burning in a brazier thirty years ago, refusing to stop being words.

He had sealed the gods. He had helped do it, gladly, gratefully, in exchange for his own skin and a quiet life in a city that never learned his name. And now the seals were leaking, and the leak had begun at the door of the first grieving man to buy one of his charms in years, and a child had asked him for help and he had spat cruelty at her because cruelty was cheaper than courage and always had been.

"Damn you," Edmund said—to Maren, to the dragon he would not yet let himself name, to the younger man he had been, to all of them equally. "Damn every one of you."

The salt-ring pulsed. Syllable, syllable, rest.

* * *

He spent the morning doing what he always did when the world tilted: work. He opened his stall in the night market's daytime ghost—a shuttered awning near the ropewalk where he repaired nets and sold thread and performed, publicly and boringly, the trade of a man with no secrets. He re-spliced a trawl line for the widow Brann. He argued amiably about the price of hemp cord. He was halfway through convincing a deckhand that no, luck-knots were decorative, purely decorative, when he saw the Inquisitor's boat put in at the customs stairs.

Greyhaven had three Inquisitors under the Edict, and everyone knew them the way sheep know dogs. Veyra was the youngest and the worst, or the best, depending on which side of her ledger you expected to appear. She wore the ash-grey coat rather than the scarlet, which meant she did her own walking rather than sending bailiffs, and she had a habit—famous in the low wards—of stopping in markets and asking questions in a voice so tired and reasonable that people answered before they remembered they should lie.

Today she was not alone. Today she had a clerk, and the clerk had a ledger, and they were going stall to stall along the ropewalk, and the market had gone quiet in that particular way markets go quiet when everyone is listening without appearing to listen.

Edmund kept splicing. His hands were steady. His hands were always steady; that was the first thing they had trained out of him and the last thing that remained. But his mind was moving fast and low, a fox through hedgerows. *Salt on a threshold. A boot returned. A goddess's spiral on a door in the poorest ward of the city, visible to any passerby.* How long before Veyra heard? Hours. Less. And when she traced the miracle backward—and she would, she was thorough, it was her single virtue and his coming doom—where would the trail lead?

To a luck-knot. Sold by a charm-seller. At the night market.

To him.

The deckhand bought his decorative knot and went away happy. Edmund tied off the splice, wiped his hands, and allowed himself one long breath, in through the nose, out through the mouth, the breathing of a man standing very still in deep water.

Fifteen years ago, the Edict of Ash had come down like a slammed door, and Edmund Coldharbour had slipped through it in the same season, arriving in Greyhaven with salt spray on his coat and a forged journeyman's papers and nothing in his satchel but chalk. Everyone in the city knew those two facts separately. No one had ever been permitted to know them together. *The year magic died was the year the mapmaker came.* If Veyra ever laid those facts side by side on her ledger's clean pages, the sum would be his life.

Across the market, the ash-grey coat drew nearer, stall by stall. The clerk's pen scratched. Somewhere behind Edmund's ribs, older than fear, older than guilt, something that had slept for fifteen years shifted its weight and began, slowly, to wake.

Veyra stopped three stalls away. Looked up. Their eyes met across the morning crowd, hers the color of a winter harbor, and she did not smile, and she did not look away, and she reached into her coat and drew out a folded piece of paper to check against something—

A torn page. Creased, water-stained, ragged along one edge where it had been ripped from a larger sheet.

Edmund knew the paper. He knew the weight of it, the faint cream of its sizing, the particular way it took ink.

He had made it himself, twenty years ago, in a country that no longer existed.

The Inquisitor folded the page away, tucked it into her coat, and walked toward his stall with the unhurried tread of a woman who had all day and all the law behind her, and Edmund Coldharbour picked up his splicing needle and prepared, once again, to lie for his life.
