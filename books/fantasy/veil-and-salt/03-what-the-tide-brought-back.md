## 3. What the Tide Brought Back

The bells of Greyhaven rang wrong that morning.

Edmund heard it from his stall in the night market's shuttered edge, where he was counting out dried kelp and knotwork charms for a morning that had not yet decided to be one. The harbor bells rang the hour, and then they rang it again, and then they rang a third time with no pattern any bell-ringer alive would admit to. Fifteen years of careful listening had taught him the city's every voice—the fishwives' dawn argument, the gull-wars over the fishmongers' roofs, the groan of the tide gates—and none of them sounded like this.

This sounded like alarm wearing the skin of routine.

He closed his stall early. He told himself it was because of his hip, which ached in damp weather, and the weather was certainly damp enough. He told himself a great many things on the walk down Saltrow, past the charm-shops with their legal disclaimers painted in cheerful red letters—*NO EFFECT GUARANTEED. DECORATIVE PURPOSES ONLY*—and he did not believe a single one of them.

The crowd at the harbor mouth was thick and silent in the way crowds are silent when they are afraid to hope. Fishermen stood with their caps in their hands. Women held children up on their shoulders so they could see. And everyone, everyone, was looking at the water.

Edmund shouldered through to the front, and there he saw the drowned man walking.

He came up out of the shallows by the breakwater with the slow, deliberate tread of someone who has been walking a very long way underwater and has not yet remembered how to stop. His clothes were soaked through, heavy with harbor silt. Barnacles clung to his boots. Water poured from his sleeves and the hem of his coat in steady ropes, as though he were a vessel that had been filled past its capacity and would go on emptying forever. His face was gray. His lips were the color of things found at the bottom of nets.

Edmund knew him. Torv Anselm, third boat on the left of the winter moorings, a widower who had gone out two days ago into a squall the harbormaster had sworn at him for ignoring. They had dragged the harbor for him yesterday. Edmund had watched them do it from his window, and had thought, with the cold professional detachment of a man who sells luck-knots to the grieving: *another one the knots didn't save.*

Torv Anselm walked out of the sea and stopped at the tideline, and when he opened his mouth, his voice was not his own.

It was a woman's voice. It was a voice like water finding the lowest place, patient and inevitable, a voice that had been speaking since before there were ears to hear it. It came out of the dead man's throat with the gentle authority of a tide coming in.

"I have been asleep," said the voice of Maren, tide-goddess of the old pantheon, through the mouth of a drowned fisherman, "and you have been calling me."

No one moved. A gull screamed somewhere overhead and was ignored.

"Little ones," the voice said, and there was something in it that might have been tenderness or might have been hunger, and after fifteen years Edmund could no longer tell the difference where gods were concerned, "I hear you now. I am sorry it took so long."

An old woman near the front dropped to her knees so hard Edmund heard her kneecaps crack against the stone. Someone began to weep. Someone else began to pray, aloud, in words Edmund recognized from a liturgy that had been illegal for fifteen years, and no one stopped them, and no one arrested anyone, and the whole harbor stood in the rain while a dead man spoke with the voice of the sea.

Edmund turned and walked away as fast as his bad hip allowed.

Because here is what the crowd did not know, could not know: the seals that silenced Maren had not simply failed. Seals did not fail. Seals were drawn by hands—by *his* hands, among others, in another life, in a tower above a world that no longer appeared on any map—and they held until the hand that anchored them grew weak, or died, or in this case lay coiled beneath the city's foundations doing exactly what dying things do, which is letting go.

Edmund had helped draw those seals. He had drawn the third ring himself, the one that bound Maren's name into the deep water, and he had done it believing—he had *needed* to believe—that silence was mercy. That gods unanswered hurt less than gods answered wrongly. That a world without miracles was a world where no one got eaten by their own prayers.

And now a drowned man was standing in the shallows reciting the tide-goddess's apology, and every hour she stayed awake, the seal-memory in Edmund's own blood burned a little brighter, like a signature being traced by an unseen finger.

Someone would come asking questions. Someone always did.

* * *

Sela Anselm had prayed to the sea the way other girls talked to themselves.

She had never told anyone. It wasn't prayer, exactly—not the kneeling, candle-burning kind the Edict had burned out of the city before she was old enough to remember it. It was just talk. She'd stand on the breakwater with the wind pulling her hair sideways and tell the water about her day, about the boats, about her father's stubbornness, about how the luck-knots sold in the market were pretty but empty, she could feel it, they were like songs with the words taken out.

"Come back," she had told the water, two nights ago, when the harbormaster's men had called off the dragging. "You took him. You bring him back. I don't care what the law says your name is—you know what you are. Come *back.*"

She hadn't expected anything. That was the whole point of talking to the sea; the sea didn't answer, and that made it safe, the way writing letters you never send is safe.

Then her father had walked out of the water.

Now she sat beside his bed in the room above the net-loft, watching him sleep—or whatever it was he did, because his chest rose and fell too slowly, and seawater still beaded on his skin no matter how many times she wiped it away, and when he breathed the whole room smelled of low tide. His eyes, when they opened briefly, were the wrong color. Not the brown she'd inherited from him. Something green-gray and vast, something that looked at her the way the harbor looks at a boat it has decided, for now, to spare.

"You called me," her father's mouth had said, in that voice that was not his voice, when she'd first run to him on the tideline. "You, little one. Out of all the small voices, yours was the one that reached down where I lay."

"What do you want?" Sela had whispered, because her father had taught her never to accept a gift without knowing its price.

"To remember," the voice had said. "To be remembered. It is the only food we have."

Now her father slept, and Sela sat with her back against the bed frame, and she was afraid in a way she had never been afraid before. Not of the god. Not even of the Edict, though the Edict's shadow lay across everything—she was fourteen, and she had grown up learning to lower her voice around certain words, to call the old stories "fairy tales" when soldiers were nearby, to watch the Inquisitors' gray coats move through the market and feel her stomach tighten around nothing at all.

She was afraid because for the first time in her life, the thing she wanted most in the world had happened, and it was real, and real things could be taken.

They had taken the shrines before she was born. They had taken the names—scrubbed from doorposts, chiseled off the harbor stones, cut out of songs until the songs were nonsense syllables. They had taken her grandmother, who had taught her the first words of the tide-liturgy in a whisper under blankets, and her grandmother had gone to the Inquisitors' house on Cordwainer Street and had not come back, and the family had been told she had died of fever, and no one had ever been permitted to bury anything.

And now the god was *back*, in her father's body, drinking in the sound of her voice like a starving thing, and Sela understood with a child's terrible clarity that this could not last. Wonder this large always woke the wolves.

There was a knock at the net-loft door. Three soft knocks, unhurried.

Sela went still. Through the floorboards she could hear the harbor crowd dispersing below, the ordinary sounds of ordinary evening. She looked at her father's sleeping face, at the water still beading on his brow like sweat, or tears.

The knock came again.

She crossed the room on silent feet, took up the gutting knife from the table—it was for fish, it was all she had—and opened the door a hand's width.

A man stood in the stairwell's dim light. Middle-aged, unremarkable, with ink-stained fingers and the tired eyes of someone who had spent a long time being careful. He held his hat in his hands like a man at a funeral.

"I sell charms," he said quietly. "Knots, mostly. Harmless ones." His gaze flicked past her, to the bed, to the slow rise and fall of the drowned man's chest, and something moved behind his eyes—recognition, and grief, and fear layered over fear like sediment. "I wanted to see if the family needed anything."

"We're fine," Sela said, and started to close the door.

"Wait." He said it softly, but the word landed like a hand on her shoulder. He glanced once down the stairwell, listening, then leaned closer. When he spoke again his voice had changed—lower, older, carrying a weight that did not belong to a charm-seller. "The voice that speaks through him. Does it say its name?"

Sela's grip tightened on the knife. "Why?"

"Because if it does," the man said, and now she saw that his hands were trembling, just slightly, the ink-stained fingers shaking around the brim of his hat, "then the seals are breaking, and everything your grandmother whispered to you under the blankets is about to become either salvation or a death sentence—for you, for your father, and for every soul in this city who remembers. And I need to know which one we have time to prepare for."

The word *grandmother* hit her like cold water. Her grandmother, dead of "fever," whose whispered liturgies Sela had never repeated to a living soul.

She stared at him. He stared back, and in his tired face she saw something she had never seen in an adult's face before: he was more frightened than she was.

"The voice doesn't say its name," Sela said slowly. "But last night, when I asked it what it was called—" her throat tightened, remembering, "—it said, *ask the man who drew my cage.*"

Above the net-loft, out in the darkening harbor, the tide began to rise.

It rose higher than the charts said it should, higher than it had risen in fifteen years, sliding up the breakwater steps and licking at the harbor stones with a patience that felt deliberate, that felt like attention, that felt like something vast turning over in its sleep and fixing one green-gray eye—

—on the charm-seller standing in a fisherman's stairwell with his hat in his trembling hands.

Edmund Coldharbour closed his eyes. Somewhere beneath the city, something old was dying, and it was whispering his name to the water.
