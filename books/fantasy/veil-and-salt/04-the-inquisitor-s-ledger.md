## 4. The Inquisitor's Ledger

The summons came at dawn, folded into the crack of Edmund's shutter by a boy who ran off before he could be paid. The paper was grey, official, smelling faintly of ash and lamp-oil: *All licensed charm-sellers of the Lower Wards will present themselves for interview before Inquisitor Veyra, Salt Court annex, third bell. Failure to appear will be construed as confession.*

Edmund read it twice, then made tea.

This was the discipline he had taught himself over fifteen years: never let the fear move first. Fear moved first in the young ones, the ones who burned their stock and fled and were caught at the gates with singed fingers and guilty eyes. Fear was a scent, and Veyra would know it the way a hound knows rain coming. So Edmund drank his tea, ate his bread, wound his grey scarf twice around his neck, and looked at himself in the cracked mirror above the basin until the man looking back was nobody at all—a tired tradesman with salt-stiff hair and ink stains on his knuckles that any honest person might have, if they kept accounts.

*Any honest person.* He almost smiled. Fifteen years of being no one had taught him exactly how much honesty a lie required to stand up straight.

The walk to the Salt Court annex took him through the morning market, where the night's gossip had already curdled into something with teeth. A fishmonger was telling anyone who'd listen about the drowned man—Corvin Hale, walked out of the harbor three days dead, dry as a sermon. Two women crossed themselves the old way, thumb to brow to breastbone, a gesture the Edict had supposedly scrubbed from the city's hands and hadn't. A Council crier stood on the fountain steps reading the official position: *anomalous tidal event, under investigation, report all rumors of sorcery to your ward officer.*

*Rumors of sorcery.* As if the sea itself hadn't stood up and spoken a goddess's name in front of forty witnesses.

Edmund kept his head down and his pace ordinary, and all the while he felt it again—the tremor under the cobblestones, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall. Something vast turning over in its sleep beneath Greyhaven. He had felt it first when the dockworker's luck-knot had answered a prayer it should never have been able to hear, and he had felt it every hour since, growing a little stronger, a little less deniable.

He knew what slept under the city. He had helped tuck it there.

* * *

The annex had once been a counting-house; now its ledgers were people. A clerk with ink to his elbows took Edmund's name—his false one, Edmun Carrow, charm-seller, license 4471—and directed him to a bench where six other sellers sat in a row like suspects at a lineup, which, Edmund reflected, was precisely what they were. A hedge-witch named Osla who sold fever-charms and talked too much when nervous. Twin brothers who dealt in love-knots and couldn't stop glancing at each other. An old man whose entire trade was buttons blessed against rheumatism. And a girl, nineteen maybe, who had set up her tray of woven protections only last spring and was visibly calculating whether she could make the door before anyone stopped her.

Nobody spoke. The silence had weight, the particular gravity of people waiting to find out how much trouble they were in.

Third bell rang. A side door opened, and Inquisitor Veyra came out carrying a ledger the size of a paving stone.

Edmund had prepared himself for many kinds of Inquisitor. The zealot, flushed with certainty. The bureaucrat, bored and therefore dangerous in the way weather is dangerous. The sadist, savoring. What he had not prepared for—and what unsettled him more than any of them—was a woman who looked *tired*. Not lazy-tired. Tired the way a lighthouse keeper is tired: someone who had watched the water too long to believe it gentle, and stayed at her post anyway.

She was perhaps forty, grey threading her dark hair at the temples, dressed in the plain charcoal of her office with no ornament except the silver chain of office and, hooked at her belt, a leather folio that did not match the rest of her equipment. She wore it the way soldiers wear a letter from home—close, private, not for display.

"Osla Fenwick." She didn't look up from the ledger. "In."

The hedge-witch went through the door like a woman walking into cold surf. Through the thin wall, Edmund could hear fragments: questions, flat and precise. *Where were you on the night of the ninth. Who bought a charm from you that week. Did you tell them it would work. Define work.*

Twenty minutes. Osla came out grey-faced, dabbing her eyes, alive. The old button-man went in and came out faster, bewildered, apparently having bored even an Inquisitor. The twins went in together and came out separately, which seemed designed to teach them something.

Then: "Carrow."

Edmund rose, arranged his face into polite apprehension—the correct amount, he'd decided, for a small tradesman summoned somewhere intimidating—and followed her in.

* * *

The room beyond was spare: a desk, two chairs, a high window through which grey light fell like sifted flour. On the desk, beside the great ledger, lay a single sheet of paper covered in cramped handwriting. She gestured him to sit and remained standing, which put her between him and the window so that he had to squint slightly to see her face. Deliberate, he guessed. Everything about her seemed deliberate without seeming cruel.

"You are Edmun Carrow," she said. "Charm-seller, Lower Wards, eleven years licensed."

"Fifteen in the city," Edmund said mildly. "Eleven licensed. It took me a while to learn the paperwork."

Something flickered at the corner of her mouth—not quite amusement. "You find this funny?"

"I find it true. Truth's funnier than most things, if you live long enough."

"Do you." She opened the ledger. "Let's talk about truth, then. Master Carrow. Three nights ago, Corvin Hale drowned in the harbor. His body was recovered by the harbor watch, tagged, and stored in the mortuary house. Two nights ago, Corvin Hale walked out of the harbor, dry, alive, speaking in a voice the witnesses describe as—" she consulted the page, though Edmund suspected she knew it by heart— "*'a woman's voice, but big, like the sea talking through a keyhole.'* You sell charms near the docks. You would have known Hale."

"I knew him to nod to," Edmund said. "Big man, quiet. Bought a luck-knot from me some months back."

"A luck-knot." Her pen scratched. "What does a luck-knot do?"

"It's rope and red thread and good wishes," Edmund said. "It does nothing. That's what I tell every customer. I'm careful to say it."

"And yet." She looked up, and her eyes were the pale grey of a winter harbor, and they did not blink enough. "Half this city believes their charms work. Half this city prays over knots and buttons and braided thread because the Edict took away everything else they were allowed to hope with. And now something in this city has started *answering*, Master Carrow, and my job—which I did not choose, and do not enjoy—is to write down names until I understand why."

"Names of charm-sellers?"

"Names of everyone." For a moment the weariness in her face showed all the way through, like a flaw in glaze. "Do you know how many miracles I've logged since the Hale incident? Eleven. Eleven in four days. A fever broken in Ashlane that the physicians swear was past breaking. A well in Tanner's Row that came up sweet after thirty years of brine. A child pulled from a collapsed chimney without a scratch. Small things. *Small mercies,* the people are calling them." She closed the ledger. "Miracles come in flocks, Master Carrow. Like gulls. When you see one, there's a reason, and the reason is usually food. Someone is feeding them. Someone in this city knows something."

"And you think it's a charm-seller."

"I think charm-sellers traffic in belief," she said. "And belief is the only currency these miracles seem to accept." She leaned forward slightly. "So. Tell me about belief, Master Carrow. You've spent fifteen years among believers. What do they want?"

It was a trap laid casually, the best kind. Answer cynically and he confirmed he thought the miracles were manipulation. Answer devoutly and he became a suspect of faith, which under the Edict was nearly as bad. Edmund let a small silence pass, the silence of a man thinking honestly.

"They want to be remembered," he said. "Mostly. People can survive hunger, cold, bad years. What eats them is the sense that no one is keeping track—that they suffer and the world doesn't notice. A charm is a way of saying *the world noticed*. Even a fake one. Especially a fake one, sometimes."

Veyra studied him for a long moment. Then she wrote something in the ledger, and Edmund's pulse ticked up half a beat before he mastered it.

"That's a better answer than most," she said. "Most of your colleagues gave me either piety or contempt. You gave me grief." She turned the ledger toward him briefly—he caught a glimpse of columns, dates, locations, small neat annotations—and then closed it. "One more thing. Routine. I ask everyone."

"Of course."

"Where were you on the night of the ninth? Between the bells of nine and midnight."

"The ninth." Edmund frowned, genuinely performing the search. "That was the night of the storm. I closed early—no foot traffic in that weather. I was home. Mending a net, actually. Charm-sellers on the docks learn to mend nets; it keeps the neighbors friendly."

"Anyone confirm that?"

"I live alone, Inquisitor. That's rather the point of living alone."

"Hm." She made another note. "You'd be surprised how many people in this city live alone, Master Carrow. It's practically a civic virtue since the Edict. Everyone alone, everyone minding their own business, everyone *careful*." She rose, and he understood the interview was over, and he also understood—with the cold certainty of a man who has survived by reading rooms—that it was not over at all. "Your papers check out. Your answers are reasonable. You may go."

Edmund stood, inclined his head, and had reached the door when she said, without turning:

"One more thing. Curiosity, not accusation." Now she did turn, and her hand rested lightly on the leather folio at her belt—the one that didn't belong. "You're a mapmaker's sort of man, aren't you? Ink on the knuckles. The way you look at corners. I've noticed charm-sellers don't usually look at corners."

"Corners keep the rain out," Edmund said. "I appreciate them professionally."

"Mm." She unhooked the folio, opened it just far enough to slide out a single page, and held it up between two fingers.

The floor of the world tilted very slightly under Edmund's feet.

It was a fragment of a map—or the ghost of one. The vellum had been torn raggedly along its edges, and whatever the original had shown, most of it was gone. But what remained was unmistakable to the man who had drawn it: a coastline rendered in fine stippled line, a bay shaped like a cupped hand, and along the margin, in a script tight and precise and fifteen years younger than his current hand, a column of annotations. Not place-names. *Names.* Names written small and dense, the way one writes things meant to be hidden inside decoration.

His own hand. His own secret cipher, the one he had invented at twenty-three to carry the gods' true names disguised as marginalia, believing no one would ever read them because no one else knew the key.

He had not seen that handwriting in fifteen years. He had convinced himself the atlas was destroyed, scattered, lost. And here was a piece of it, in the hand of the Inquisitor, worn soft at the folds as if carried often.

"Found it in the Edict archives," Veyra said, watching him with those winter-grey eyes. "Filed under confiscations, year one. No provenance. No artist credited. Just this." She turned the page slightly, and the light caught the faded ink. "I've been carrying it for months. There's something about it I can't put down. It doesn't look like a map of anywhere, and yet it looks *truer* than any map I've ever seen. Like whoever drew it wasn't recording a place. Like they were *keeping* one."

Every instinct Edmund owned screamed at him to shrug. To say *pretty work, strange, no idea*. It would cost nothing. She had no proof, the cipher was unreadable without the key, and the key lived in exactly one skull in all the world.

But something else in him—something he had starved carefully for fifteen years, the cartographer, the man who had once loved the god-realm enough to betray heaven for it—looked at his own young handwriting and ached.

"Fine stippling," he said quietly. "Coastline's wrong for anywhere real. Bay like a cupped hand." He met her eyes. "I've seen work like it once. Years ago. Before the Edict. There was a cartographer who used to say maps weren't pictures of places—they were *sentences about them*. That if you wrote the sentence right, the place obeyed." He shrugged, a small tired motion. "Everyone said he was mad. Then the Edict came, and everyone said he'd been right, which was worse."

Veyra's gaze sharpened by a degree. "You knew him."

"Everyone knew *of* him." Edmund smiled, and it cost him more than any lie he had told in fifteen years. "He vanished the year the Edict passed. They say he burned his own atlas. They say a lot of things."

"Yes," Veyra said softly. "They do." She slid the page back into the folio, and her fingers lingered on it a moment longer than efficiency required—an intimacy Edmund recognized and feared, because he knew what it was to carry a page you couldn't put down. "Thank you for your time, Master Carrow. You'll hear from the annex if we have further questions."

"We rarely do," Edmund said, "when the answers are reasonable."

"No," she agreed. "We rarely do. That's what makes the unreasonable ones worth waiting for."

* * *

He made it three streets before he had to stop, in the shadow of a fish-curing shed, and press his scarred palm flat against the wet boards until his breathing steadied.

*A page of the atlas. In the Edict archives. Filed year one.*

Which meant the atlas had not burned. Which meant someone had taken it—taken *him* apart, page by page, in the chaos of the Edict's first days—and built something out of it. And there was only one thing in all the world that could be built from an atlas of the god-realm containing the true names of every god in creation, written in cipher along its margins.

Seals. The seals that had silenced Maren and her kin. The seals that were failing now, one answered prayer at a time.

They had stolen his life's work and used it as a cage. And the cage was rusting, and the prisoner was waking, and the woman hunting the mystery was carrying a piece of the lock in a leather folio against her heart.

Under his feet, faint but unmistakable, the city shivered again—that vast slow turn of something dreaming beneath the foundations. Stronger today. Closer.

Edmund pushed off from the wall and walked home through the grey drizzle, composing his face into nobody's, while behind him in the annex a weary Inquisitor opened her ledger and wrote, in small precise letters, beside the name *Carrow*: *Watch this one. He looks at corners.*
