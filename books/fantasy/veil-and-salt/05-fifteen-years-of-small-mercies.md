## 5. Fifteen Years of Small Mercies

The god-realm smelled of rain that had never fallen anywhere.

Edmund remembered that first—before the light, before the impossible geometry of it all—the smell. He was twenty-three years old and he had crossed over through a door in the roots of a mountain that no longer existed, carrying nothing but a leather satchel of pens, a roll of treated vellum, and the arrogant certainty that anything could be mapped if a man was stubborn enough.

He had been right. That was the tragedy of it. He had been right about everything.

* * *

The realm opened above him like a cathedral with no floor and no ceiling, only distances. Islands hung in a golden haze, some no larger than a garden, others wide enough to hold weather. Rivers ran upward without complaint. And everywhere, everywhere, the gods—or rather, the evidence of them, for they were shy of being seen whole: footprints the size of lakes filling slowly with wildflowers; a voice moving through the haze like weather, singing in a language that made his teeth ache and his eyes water; a warmth passing over him that he would later understand was attention.

He unrolled his first sheet of vellum with hands that shook, and began.

For six years he mapped it. He learned its tides—which were not water-tides but attention-tides, hours when the realm's gaze turned elsewhere and a mortal could walk unwatched. He charted the Court of Salt where Maren kept her drowned treasures, each one a prayer someone had thrown into the sea believing no one heard. He walked the ash-gardens of the nameless god who tended them and left an offering of bread on every visit, until one morning the bread was gone and a single white flower stood in its place, and he wept like a child at the courtesy of it.

He gave them names on his maps. Not their true names—he was not fool enough to write those—but the names mortals had given them across centuries, gathered from drowned prayers and cracked bells and the lullabies of grandmothers. Maren of the Turning Tide. Emberveil, who slept beneath the world's foundations and whose breathing was the reason volcanoes were warm. The Ashen One, who kept the gardens. Nine great names, and beneath them hundreds of lesser ones, spirits of thresholds and hearths and the last hour of the workday.

He did not know yet that a map is a cage drawn in loving detail. He did not know that to name a thing precisely enough is to build a door to it—and that doors can be locked from either side.

He only knew that he loved it there, and that when he came home to Greyhaven with his atlas nearly complete, men in grey coats were waiting at the dock, and they looked at his satchel the way wolves look at a wound.

* * *

They took him not to a prison but to a tower office that smelled of ink and cold iron, where three founders of the new Council sat behind a table, and between them lay a copy of his atlas—not his copy, a stolen draft, the one that had vanished from his lodgings two months prior.

"You've done remarkable work," said the woman in the center. Her name, he would learn too late, was Hesse—grandmother to the chancellor who ruled now. "Remarkable. Do you understand what you've made?"

"A map," Edmund said.

"A key," she said. "Every true name you've recorded, every location, every relationship between divinity and place—you've built us a lock for every door in heaven." She turned a page with one finger, delicately, as if the paper might bite. "Magic is going to be outlawed, Master Coldharbour. It has already been decided. The Edict of Ash will be signed within the year. The question is only whether the gods are destroyed outright or merely... silenced."

"Silenced," he repeated. His mouth had gone dry. "You can't silence a god."

"With this," she said, tapping the atlas, "we can do anything. A seal is only a map read backward, young man. You've written the instructions yourself."

He told them no. Of course he told them no. He remembers saying it—remembers the word coming out of him flat and certain, *no*, a stone dropped into still water.

And then they showed him what happened to cartographers who refused. Not to him—to the records. His family name struck from the guild rolls. His mother's shop license revoked, her debts called in full. His younger brother conscripted into a border war that had been quietly arranged to need more bodies that season. They did not threaten him. They simply read aloud, in calm voices, the future they had already purchased, and let him understand that every line of it was negotiable except the Edict itself.

That night he did not sleep. He sat in his lodgings with the finished atlas under his hands and felt it breathing under his palms like something alive, because in a way it was—it held nine true names and the geography of heaven, and it was the most dangerous object in the world, and he had made it out of love.

It was near dawn when he felt the summons. Not heard—felt, a pull behind his sternum, gentle and immense, like the tide taking hold of a moored boat.

He went down through the city, past the sleeping wards, down stairs that were not on any map because he had erased them from every map, into the undercity where Greyhaven's oldest foundations sank into rock older than the city's name. And there, coiled around the roots of the world, he found the dragon.

* * *

Emberveil was dying.

Edmund saw it at once, the way you see a fire guttering in a grate—no single flame extinguished, but the whole of it lower than it should be, the gold gone amber, the amber gone to ember. The dragon was vast beyond sense, scales like roof-slates the size of church doors, and half-buried in stone so that it seemed less a creature resting than a mountain that had dreamed briefly of being alive.

"You are the mapmaker," the dragon said. Its voice did not come through air. It came up through the soles of his boots, through the bones of his legs, resonant and slow as continental drift.

"I am."

"You have been writing down our names." A pause, long as a season. "I felt each one. Like small hands on my skin."

"I'm sorry," Edmund said, because he understood suddenly that it must have been like being catalogued by mice.

"Do not be sorry. Be useful." The great head shifted, grinding stone against stone, and one eye found him—an eye like a well of banked fire, deep and tired and terribly old. "The grey-coats have your book."

"Yes."

"They will build their seals. They will lock the doors you drew. And we—" the eye dimmed, brightened, dimmed again, "—we are already fading, little cartographer. Did you know? The worship has been thinning for a century. Fewer prayers. Smaller ones. Prayers for luck at cards instead of rain for the fields. We starve politely while your kind forgets us course by course." Firelight moved in the dragon's throat. "The Edict will only finish what indifference began."

"Then help me stop them."

"I am dying, mapmaker. I have perhaps fifteen years—fifteen of your years, which are nothing to me and everything to you. When I die, my fire goes out, and my fire is the last anchor. Every seal they build will be nailed to my sleep, because I am the oldest thing your species ever named, and old names hold nails well." The eye closed, opened. "So here is my counter-offer, since you came down here offering nothing and asking everything."

Edmund waited. The dark breathed around him.

"Hide us," Emberveil said. "Take your atlas and hide our true names inside it—in the margins, in the flourishes, in the curve of a coastline or the shading of a hill. Bury the names so deep in your artistry that even the grey-coats cannot find them, even using the book itself. Let them build their seals on the shells of our names, empty words, dead letters. And keep the living names somewhere no inquisitor will ever think to look."

"Where?"

"In the one place that cannot be seized, burned, or stolen." The dragon's breath stirred Edmund's hair, warm as a hearth. "In your memory. You will carry us, mapmaker. Every true name, perfectly, for as long as you live. And in exchange—"

"In exchange for what?"

"For your life, which will be very long, and very safe, and very lonely." The words came without cruelty, which somehow made them worse. "No one hunts a man who does not exist. So you will not exist. You will fold yourself away like a map no one asks to see, and you will keep our names, and you will wait, because all things wait, and someday the seals will weaken—as all things weaken—and on that day someone will need to know where every door in heaven stands."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then they take the atlas intact, the seals bind us truly, and when I die the names tear free all at once, raw and starving, into a world that has forgotten how to receive them. There will be a war, or a madness, or both. Or perhaps simply silence forever, which would be worse." The eye regarded him. "You are the only mortal who has walked among us in four hundred years, Edmund Coldharbour. The only one who drew us with love. Love is the only ink that holds."

He wanted to say no again. He reached for the word and found it had dissolved.

"What happens to me?" he asked instead. "If I take the names in?"

"You become a vessel. Vessels do not age quickly. Vessels do not die easily. Vessels remember everything, whether they wish to or not." The dragon's voice softened, or seemed to; perhaps it was only the stone absorbing less. "You will watch everyone you love grow old at the proper speed. You will bury them. You will move to a new city and become small and careful, and you will sell trinkets to people who cannot see what you are, and you will tell yourself it is enough. For fifteen years, it will be enough."

"How do you know all that?"

"Because," said Emberveil, and the fire in its eye turned almost tender, "someone made the same bargain for me, a very long time ago, and I have been keeping their names ever since. We are all of us vessels, little cartographer. Some of us are merely larger than others."

He knelt then, on the stone at the edge of the dragon's coil, and he swore it—not in the language of contracts, which the grey-coats owned, but in the language of maps, which belonged to no one. He swore by scale and margin, by north and the honesty of north, by every coastline he had ever traced with love.

Emberveil exhaled once, a long gold ribbon of fire that did not burn him but passed *through* him, and he felt the names settle into his memory like coins into a well—nine great names, hundreds of small ones, each one ringing as it fell.

When he climbed back up into the grey dawn of Greyhaven, he walked to the Council tower and surrendered the atlas himself, voluntarily, and watched the founders' faces bloom with triumph, and said nothing, and was paid thirty crowns and a pardon, and bought a stall in the night market with eleven of them.

The charm-selling was his own idea. Small mercies, harmless and deniable—a knot for luck, a pinch of salt for grief. He told himself it was camouflage. He told himself it was penance. He told himself many things, in fifteen years, and got very good at telling them.

* * *

Present-day Edmund woke at his worktable with his cheek stuck to a half-finished luck-knot and his heart hammering like a fist on a door.

The lamp had burned low. Outside the shuttered window, Greyhaven murmured its midnight murmur—gulls, rigging, the far complaint of the tide. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. He sat up, rubbing the lamp-oil from his cheek, and tried to tell himself the dream had been only a dream.

But his hands knew better. His hands had gone cold in the specific way they went cold when a memory surfaced that he had spent years teaching himself not to surface—the way a scar aches before rain.

*Fifteen years,* Emberveil had said. *I have perhaps fifteen of your years.*

He counted without wanting to. It was a short count. Fifteen years since the bargain. Fifteen years since he had come down the mountain with his satchel and his arrogance and found the grey-coats waiting. Fifteen years since a drowned fisherman had been impossible, since salt had burned on a doorstep like scripture, since a tide-goddess had spoken through a dead man's mouth in a voice Edmund had helped to seal away.

Fifteen years, and the miracles had started *now*.

"No," he said aloud, to the empty room, in the voice he used on customers who haggled past dignity. "No, no."

But the arithmetic did not care for his tone. The seals were nailed to Emberveil's sleep, and Emberveil was dying, had been dying for fifteen years exactly, and the dragon's deathbed was the world's loosening. Every answered prayer in Greyhaven was not a miracle returning. It was a nail coming out. And when the last nail came—

*Every sealed name will tear free at once,* the dragon had warned him, in the dream that was not a dream, in the memory he had buried so deep he'd mistaken it for sleep. *Raw and starving, into a world that has forgotten how to receive them.*

Edmund stood. His legs were unsteady. He crossed to the window and put his scarred palm—for it had been scarred even then, though he no longer remembered why, one of the small prices the bargain had collected quietly along the way—against the cold shutter, as if he could feel through the wood, through the street, through forty feet of stone and clay, the vast slow heartbeat beneath the city.

Once, it had been strong as surf. Now he fancied he could feel the arrhythmia in it, the stutter and fade, a fire guttering in a grate.

Somewhere below, wrapped around the roots of Greyhaven, the oldest thing his species had ever named was dying. And when it died, everything he had hidden—every name he had carried like stones in his belly for fifteen years—would come loose at once, unless someone went down into the dark and did something about it.

He thought of Sela, the fisherman's daughter, with her hungry eyes and her dangerous questions. He thought of Inquisitor Veyra's ledger, and the torn page of map he had glimpsed in her coat—his own linework, unmistakable, in his own hand. Someone had been selling pieces of the old atlas. Someone was piecing together what he had spent fifteen years scattering.

And he thought of the charm he had sold the dockworker, the harmless luck-knot, and the vast old thing that had answered anyway, and how the world had shuddered under his feet like a table struck from beneath.

Edmund dressed in the dark. He took down his satchel—the same satchel, absurdly, after all these years—and put into it a stub of pencil, a folding knife, and the last white flower he had ever been given, pressed flat between two pages of a book of common prayers, kept fifteen years and never once explained to himself.

At the door he paused, hand on the latch, and spoke to the floorboards as if the thing beneath could hear him. Perhaps it could. Perhaps it had always been able to hear him; perhaps that was the loneliest part of the bargain.

"Hold on," he said. "I'm coming down."

Far beneath the salt-city, in the dark where the foundations met the roots of the world, something ancient and guttering heard him—and, hearing, let go of one more nail.
