## 6. The Congregation of Ash

The cellar smelled of tallow and wet stone, of salt leaching up through the foundations the way it did everywhere in Greyhaven, as if the city itself were weeping through its skin. Edmund stood at the top of the stairs with his hood drawn low and his hand resting on the doorframe, telling himself he had come only to watch.

That was a lie, and he knew it for one, and he went down anyway.

Twenty-three people had gathered in the undercroft of a collapsed chandlery on Brine Street—a number he counted without meaning to, because counting was what he did when he was afraid. Fishwives with their hands still smelling of the day's catch. A lamplighter with a burn-scarred jaw. Two soldiers out of uniform, their boots giving them away. And Sela, standing before them on an overturned fish crate, holding a candle she had not yet lit, looking younger than her seventeen years and older than the sea.

Edmund took a place against the far wall, in the shadow beneath a shelf of ruined candles, and made himself small. Fifteen years of practice. He could have taught a masterclass in it: how to stand so that eyes slid past you, how to breathe quietly, how to arrange your face into the particular blankness of a man thinking about nothing at all. He had worn invisibility so long he sometimes wondered if there was anything left underneath it.

Sela struck her match, and the candle caught, and twenty-four faces turned toward the flame like flowers toward a sun they had been told no longer existed.

"I prayed," she said simply. "And something answered."

A murmur ran through the room, fear and hunger braided together. Edmund knew both textures intimately. Hunger he remembered from the god-realm, from the first time he had stood before a divinity and felt himself known down to the marrow. Fear he had learned later, much later, the night the Edict of Ash came down and the names began to be scrubbed from the world.

"My father drowned eleven days ago," Sela said. "The *Kestrel's Mercy*, lost off the Teeth with all hands. I prayed to Maren—my grandmother's goddess, the one the priests say is only a story now—and I asked her to give him back or give me back my heart." Her voice did not waver. That was the frightening part, Edmund thought. Not the miracle itself but the girl's absolute steadiness, the way she held the candle as if it were a thing she had been carrying all her life. "Three days ago my father walked out of the harbor. Alive. Speaking words that weren't his."

"Or it wasn't him," said the lamplighter, whose name Edmund did not know. "Or it was something wearing him."

"Yes," Sela agreed, and Edmund saw the crowd flinch at her honesty. She was not selling comfort. That was either innocence or something rarer. "Maybe it wasn't him. Maybe it was the tide wearing my father like a coat. But it *knew* me. It called me by a name nobody uses anymore—the name my grandmother gave me, that isn't written anywhere, that I've never spoken aloud in this city." She let that settle over them. "So either the old stories are true, or something in the deep water has been reading our grandmothers' letters. I know which one I believe."

Edmund closed his eyes briefly. *Cartographer's secret,* he thought. *Names are doors.* The girl had stumbled onto a truth he had spent fifteen years burying, and she didn't even know she'd found it. She thought prayer had done it. Prayer was only the knocking; the name was the key, and somewhere in her grief she must have spoken her own hidden name into the water, and the sea—who forgets nothing, who has never once been silenced in her bones, only at her surface—had heard.

He should leave. Every instinct honed across a decade and a half of survival said *leave now, report nothing, sell charms tomorrow as though this cellar did not exist.* If Veyra found this gathering—and she would; the woman had a nose for devotion like a shark has a nose for blood—everyone here would face the ash-priests and the question rooms. His presence made it worse, not better. His presence was a lit fuse walking around in a hood.

He stayed.

*Because you're monitoring the danger,* he told himself. *Because you need to know what they know, who they'll tell, whether any of them carry names that matter.*

All true. None of it the reason.

* * *

They spoke in turns after that, the way frightened people do when they've finally found a room where speaking is allowed. An old woman described her mother's lullabies, songs to a hearth-god whose name Edmund had once inked onto vellum in letters of gold leaf—*Hestavar, Who Keeps the Flame*—before the Edict burned every copy but the ones inside his head. A young man confessed he still set out a bowl of cream on the solstice, had done it every year since he was six, couldn't stop, didn't want to. A soldier, the taller of the two, said nothing at all for a long time and then said, very quietly, "I was at the burning of the Temple Row. I held the torch line. I've been trying to pray for forgiveness ever since, but I don't know who's left to receive it."

Silence followed that, the good kind, the kind that holds a wound gently.

Edmund found that his hands had curled into fists inside his sleeves. He uncurled them slowly, finger by finger, the way you calm a spooked animal.

*This is what it was for,* he thought, and the thought arrived like a knife between ribs. *The bargain. The seals. All of it was supposed to keep this from happening.* Because he had seen what happened when gods walked openly among the desperate—he had mapped the wars of the Godtongue centuries, the cities that tore themselves apart over which name to shout loudest, the temples fat while the streets starved. Emberveil had shown him all of it, dying and patient, and young Edmund had believed he was choosing mercy. Silence as a kindness. Forgetting as anesthesia.

But standing in this cellar, watching a soldier weep over a torch he'd held fifteen years ago, watching an old woman mouth a lullaby she'd been forbidden to sing since childhood—watching them *hunger*—he understood that anesthesia was not the same as healing. You could numb a limb for so long that it died. Greyhaven had been dying at the surface for fifteen years, polite and gray and godless, and everyone had called it peace.

"They're coming back," whispered the old woman, and it was not triumph in her voice. It was terror, and joy, and the two sounded almost identical at that pitch. "Whether we want them or not. They're coming back."

*Yes,* Edmund thought. *Because the dragon is dying, and everything I built is failing, and I am the only person alive who knows why.* He looked at Sela—at the scale she wore on a cord around her neck, which he had noticed the moment he entered and had been carefully not-noticing since. It was the size of his palm, dark as cooled tar, with a faint ember-light moving in its depths like a coal remembering fire. He knew what it was before she touched it. He had pressed his hand against scales just like it once, a lifetime ago, sealing a bargain in blood and ink.

She must have found it washed up, or been given it—no, he realized, watching the way her thumb moved over it, rhythmic, habitual, comforting. Given it. Someone had given it to her. Which meant someone else knew. Which meant the secret was already leaking through more cracks than he could count.

The candle guttered. Somewhere above them, faint through the floorboards, came the sound of boots.

* * *

Veyra came down the stairs with six wardens and no announcement, because Inquisitors of the Ash did not announce; announcing was for people who expected resistance to matter.

Edmund had perhaps four seconds. He used three of them.

In the first, he assessed: two exits, the stair behind the wardens and a coal-chute in the north wall half-hidden by shelving, too narrow for grown men, adequate for a girl. In the second, he noted that the wardens carried nets weighted with salt-iron and that Veyra herself carried only her ledger and a lantern, which meant she wanted testimony, not corpses—or wanted corpses to look accidental. In the third, he did the thing he would spend the rest of the night, and possibly the rest of his life, trying to decide whether it was wisdom or betrayal: he shifted along the wall, put his body between the coal-chute and the nearest warden's line of sight, and waited.

"Inquisitor Veyra," Sela said. Of course she knew her. Everyone in Greyhaven knew the Inquisitor's face now; it was chalked on walls next to warnings the way plague-houses used to be marked. "Come to pray?"

"Prayer's legal," Veyra said. Her voice was flat, tired, precise—the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Unlicensed congregation isn't. Possession of pre-Edict relics—" her lantern swung, caught the scale at Sela's throat, and stopped, "—considerably less so."

"It's a rock," Sela said.

"It's a rock that glows," Veyra said. "Down on your knees. All of you. Names taken at the door, statements by morning, and anyone who runs will be assumed guilty of whatever we haven't thought to charge them with yet."

They knelt. Most of them. The old woman knelt weeping. The soldier knelt like a man returning to a posture he deserved. The lamplighter hesitated, glanced at the chute, and Edmund gave him the smallest shake of the head—not yet, not you—and the man sank down, hating him for it, and Edmund accepted the hatred like a tithe.

Only Sela stayed standing.

"Sela," Edmund said. Just her name, quiet, pitched to carry no further than her ears. *Don't.* He watched her hear it. He watched her weigh it. He watched her—this was the moment, this was the hinge the whole night turned on—watch her decide that she had spent enough of her life being told to kneel by people holding torches.

She blew out the candle.

Darkness, total, the kind the undercity made when it wanted to. Shouting, boots, the crash of a shelf going over, salt-iron nets whistling through black air. Edmund moved through it the way he had moved through the god-realm once, by memory rather than sight—three steps left, hand on the chute's iron cover, wrenching it wide. Cold air breathed up from below, tasting of coal and old sea.

"Girl!" Veyra's voice, cutting through the chaos, closer than it should have been. "The relic! Give me the relic and the rest of this becomes paperwork!"

Hands found Edmund's sleeve in the dark—small hands, strong, shaking. He pushed the scale-cord back under Sela's collar where it belonged, shoved her toward the open chute, and felt her hesitate for one heartbeat, feeling in the dark for his face, finding instead the edge of his hood.

"Who are you?" she breathed.

"Nobody," he said, and it was the oldest lie he owned, worn smooth as river glass. "Go. Down the shaft, left at the second junction, follow the smell of brine until you reach the cistern hall. Don't stop for anything that calls your name—it won't be me."

She went. The grate clanged shut behind her a half-second before a lantern-flame bloomed at the top of the stairs.

* * *

Afterward, kneeling among the others with his hood torn back and his face deliberately blank, Edmund endured the taking of names. He gave the one he'd lived under for fifteen years—Edmund Marsh, charm-seller, no fixed address worth recording—and watched Veyra write it down in her ledger without any flicker of recognition, and felt the strange double sensation he always felt at that: relief, and beneath the relief, something colder and more shameful. Disappointment. As if some buried part of him kept waiting to be recognized, and kept being spared, and could not decide which hurt more.

The wardens marched the congregation out into the drizzle. Veyra lingered, sweeping her lantern across the undercroft, across the overturned crate, across the wax puddle where the candle had died. Then, as Edmund rose to shuffle out with the rest, she spoke without turning around.

"The chute was opened from inside," she said. "Someone knew it was there. Someone knew where it led." Now she turned, and her eyes—gray, exhausted, missing nothing—found his in the lantern light. "You're the charm-seller from Gullmarket. The one who sells luck that never quite works."

"Luck's a delicate commodity, Inquisitor. Overwork it and it sours."

"Mm." She studied him for a long moment, and Edmund held her gaze with the ease of long practice while his heart performed maneuvers he hadn't authorized. "Funny thing about tonight. Twenty-two people kneeling, one girl running, and one man standing exactly where the dark was deepest." She closed the ledger. "I'm not charging you, Master Marsh. Consider that a receipt. I find I collect debts more often than payments these days."

She climbed the stairs. Edmund stood alone in the dead cellar, in the smell of extinguished wax, and admitted to the darkness what he had refused to admit to himself on the way down:

He had not monitored the danger. He had joined it. He had opened a door in the dark for a girl carrying a piece of a dying god, and he had done it not for strategy, not for the city, not even for the dragon—but because when she had stood there refusing to kneel, something in his chest that he'd sealed shut fifteen years ago had knocked, once, from the inside.

Belonging, it turned out, was not a thing you could map your way around. It found the cracks. It always found the cracks.

He pulled his hood up and went out into the rain, and did not go home. He went toward the harbor, following the smell of brine, because there was one place in Greyhaven where a runaway girl with a dragon's scale might run, and one person alive who needed to reach her before Veyra worked out the same thing.

By morning, he knew, the Inquisitor would have the chute mapped, the junctions searched, the cistern hall staked out. By morning it might be too late.

Which meant that tonight—for the first time in fifteen years—Edmund Coldharbour had to move faster than his own caution, carrying the most dangerous cargo in the world: the truth about where the last dragon slept, and the growing, terrible certainty that he was about to teach it to a girl who would use it.
