## 8. Ashes of the Edict

The proclamation went up at dawn, nailed to the customs house in letters tall enough to read from the far side of the harbor square, and Edmund read it twice before the ink had finished drying.

BY ORDER OF THE COUNCIL OF GREYHAVEN, it began, and then descended into language that had been old when he was young. FOREIGN SORCERY. AGENTS OF THE TIDE-CULTS OF THE SOUTHERN ISLES. THE FLOODING OF THE LOWER WARDS WAS AN ACT OF WAR.

He stood among the early crowd—fishwives with their baskets, a boy sweeping ash from the steps—and listened to them read it aloud to one another in voices that did not believe it. That was the strange mercy of Greyhaven: its people were too practical for easy lies. A woman who had pulled her neighbor's son from the floodwater stood squinting at the word *sorcery* and said, flatly, "The water glowed. I saw it glow. Sorcery doesn't glow like that. Sorcery burns."

"Then what does glow like that?" asked the boy with the broom.

Nobody answered him. But nobody looked away from the proclamation either, and Edmund understood that the Council had made an error older than any of its chancellors: they had told a frightened city a story the city could see was false, and now every person in the square was holding two truths at once—the official one and the one in their own eyes—and a city that holds two truths begins, quietly, to choose.

By noon the purges had doubled. He watched the gray-cloaked wardens move through the night market in daylight, which was itself a violation of custom, tearing down charm-stalls with axes while their owners stood in the wreckage counting what was left. Old Nettie's luck-knots went into a brazier. The candle-seller from Candle Row was made to kneel on wet cobbles while a warden read her inventory aloud as though each item were a confession. And through all of it the wardens worked with a new urgency, a new fear, because the Council had given them one too: *the gods are not returning; anyone who says otherwise is an enemy.*

Edmund kept his stall closed. He sat in his rooms above the shop with the shutters drawn, cataloguing everything he had lost and everything he still might lose, and found that the second list was longer than the first and growing by the hour.

They would come for charm-sellers first. Then for witnesses. Then for a girl carrying a dragon's scale, if they had not already come.

And somewhere in that widening net, sooner or later, would come the Inquisitor Veyra—who had stood in his shop three days before the flood with a torn page of a map in her coat, asking questions she already knew the shapes of.

He should run. Fifteen years of instinct said so, said it clearly, said it in the voice of the younger man who had once walked out of a burning archive with nothing but his own name and a bargain he barely understood. Run north along the salt road, past the watchtowers, into the hills where names meant nothing.

But the sea had chosen, and the sea had flooded the lower wards, and somewhere down there a child was laughing at glowing foam, and Edmund Coldharbour—cartographer, coward, keeper of dead names—found that his feet would not carry him toward the hills.

* * *

She came at dusk, alone, without the escort of gray cloaks that announced an Inquisitor's official business. He heard her boots on the stairs—unhurried, deliberate, the tread of someone who wanted to be heard coming—and he did not reach for the knife under the counter or the packet of ash-powder behind the shutter. There would have been no point. Veyra was not a woman you surprised.

She let herself in the way officials do, without quite being invited, and stood in the middle of his shop looking at the empty shelves where the charms used to be.

"You closed early," she said.

"The Council frowns on commerce this week."

"The Council frowns on breathing this week." She turned, and in the low lamplight he saw what three days and a flood had done to her: shadows under her eyes deep enough to hold water, a fresh tear in her coat sleeve crudely stitched, and something else—something he had not seen in her before, because he had never seen her doubt. "I've logged forty-one miracles since the tide came in. Forty-one sworn statements. A man whose drowned brother walked out of the harbor. A woman whose blight-killed orchard flowered overnight. Children in the lower wards scooping light out of the water in jars." She paused. "My ledger says these are impossible. My eyes say otherwise. Do you know what that discrepancy does to a woman who has spent eleven years believing her ledger?"

"It makes her honest," Edmund said, "or it breaks her."

"I'm deciding which." She crossed to the counter and set both hands flat upon it, and looked at him the way she had looked at him during their first interview—except that now there was no performance in it, no cat-and-mouse, only a tired woman asking a direct question. "Show me your hands."

He showed her. Chalk-white fingertips, salt-crusted knuckles, the ordinary hands of an ordinary charm-seller. She studied them for a long moment, then nodded once, almost to herself.

"When the flood came," she said, "every warden in the lower wards reported the same thing. The water didn't behave like water. It rose against the slope. It gathered around certain doorways and left others dry. It glowed." Her voice dropped. "And in the middle of it, standing on a rooftop above the harbor, witnesses describe a man and a girl. The man spoke words no one recognized. The girl held something that caught the light like a coin the size of a hand. Afterward the water receded—but slowly, reluctantly, the way a dog leaves a table."

"Witnesses," Edmund said, "are generous with detail after a catastrophe."

"Yes," Veyra agreed. "They are. Especially when the detail is wrong. And yet every single witness agrees on one thing: the man on the roof was calm. Not brave. Calm. The way a man is calm when he has seen the thing before." She reached into her coat, and Edmund's heart went still in his chest, because he knew what was coming before her fingers closed on it. "You told me, the last time we spoke, that you'd never seen anything like what's happening in this city. You lied to me. I've decided not to arrest you for it yet, which means I've decided to ask again, properly, and hope you'll do me the courtesy of a better answer."

She laid the page on the counter between them.

It was the corner of a map. His map. He knew it the way a man knows his own handwriting in a stranger's letter—the coastlines drawn in his young hand, the careful hatching of depth-soundings, and along the margin, small and precise, the notation system he had invented himself at twenty-three years old: a script of dots and strokes that the world had taken for decoration and that was, in truth, a catalogue of names.

Not just any names.

*Their* names.

He kept his face still. Fifteen years of practice held it still. But Veyra was watching for exactly the thing he was hiding, and he saw her see it—not the recognition itself but the effort of suppressing it, the half-second delay before his eyebrows lifted in polite puzzlement.

"Interesting workmanship," he said. "Where did you get it?"

"That's my question to you." She tapped the margin script with one finger. "I've spent four days in the Council archives trying to identify this notation. It appears nowhere in the cartographic record. No guild uses it. No academy teaches it. But yesterday I found its twin—in the sealed vault beneath the Salt Court, in the founding documents of the Edict of Ash." Her eyes came up and fixed on his. "The seals that silenced the gods fifteen years ago were built from a map. Whoever drew this map drew the architecture of the Edict. And whoever drew this map—" she turned the page toward him, slowly, deliberately, "—wrote like you write. I compared it to the ledgers of this shop. Your shipping manifests. Your price lists. The way you cross your sevens."

The lamp flickered. Somewhere outside, a patrol passed, boots ringing on stone, and neither of them moved until the sound faded.

"I'm going to tell you a story," Edmund said at last, "and it will be mostly true, and you will know which parts aren't, and I'm asking you to accept that trade."

Veyra's jaw tightened. But she nodded.

"Fifteen years ago," he said, "a cartographer worked in the god-realm. He charted it because charting was his gift and the realm permitted it—or seemed to permit it. When the Edict came, that cartographer ran. He came here, to Greyhaven, because Greyhaven forgets people better than any city in the world. He sold charms that didn't work, because working charms were dangerous and useless ones were safe. And he told himself, every year, that survival was enough. That staying small was a kind of virtue." He met her eyes. "That cartographer is dead, Inquisitor. What's left of him sells luck-knots."

"And the map?"

"Gone. Burned, scattered—I don't know. If fragments survive—" he gestured at the page between them, "—then whoever holds them holds pieces of something they don't understand. The notation isn't decoration. It's a binding script. In the wrong configuration, those pages don't just record the old names. They can *use* them."

Something shifted in Veyra's face—not fear exactly, but the grim settling of a suspicion confirmed. "Use them how?"

"To seal. To silence. To build cages out of language." He laid his scarred-free hand gently on the edge of the page, not touching the script. "You said you found its twin in the founding documents of the Edict. Think about what that means. The men who outlawed magic didn't defeat the gods, Inquisitor. They *mapped* them. Someone gave them the instrument, and they built the Edict out of it, and they have been sitting on the rest of that atlas for fifteen years in a vault under their own court."

Veyra was quiet for a long time. Outside, the tide was coming in, and even here, three streets up, they could hear it—louder than it should have been, insistent, like a knock at a door repeated patiently forever.

"If that's true," she said finally, "then the Council hasn't been protecting the city from the gods. They've been holding them prisoner. And last week the prison started failing." She straightened, and when she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman reading a sentence aloud that she wished she hadn't written. "There's more. I wasn't supposed to see it, and I'm telling you because I think you're the only person in this city who'll understand what it means. Three days ago the Chancellor's office requisitioned the garrison. Not the wardens—the *garrison*. Two thousand soldiers, redeployed from the border forts into the city proper. Officially it's flood relief. Unofficially, the order of operations reads: secure the temples, suspend the wards' assemblies, and prepare for the declaration of martial law upon confirmation of 'sustained divine incursion.'"

"Sustained divine incursion," Edmund repeated.

"They want the gods back," Veyra said. "Or rather—they need them back, briefly, visibly, terrifyingly. Enough to justify iron rule. Enough to make every citizen beg the Council for protection from the very things the Council imprisoned. And then, I suspect, the gods will be put down again—permanently this time—with whatever remains of that atlas." She picked up the torn page and held it a moment, looking at it with something close to grief. "I've spent eleven years serving the law, Coldharbour. I believed the Edict was a wall between the city and the fire. Tonight I'm looking at the wall and wondering who built it, and why, and what they bricked up inside it."

"And so you come to a charm-seller."

"So I come to the only man in Greyhaven who flinched at my map fragment." A ghost of her old sharpness returned, one degree warm. "Don't mistake me. I haven't decided what you are. Traitor, coward, survivor, fool—all four, possibly. But you know what this notation does, and the Council doesn't, and right now that difference is the only thing standing between this city and a very carefully engineered apocalypse."

"What is it you want from me?"

"Nothing tonight." She tucked the page back inside her coat, and paused at the door, her hand on the frame, not turning around. "But tomorrow the wardens begin house-to-house searches of the lower wards, and the day after, they start on the market streets. When they come here—and they will come here—you should know that Chancellor Hesse has authorized field executions for anyone found in possession of 'god-relics.' Scales. Bones. Anything that burns cold." She glanced back over her shoulder, and for a moment the Inquisitor's mask slipped entirely, and what looked out at him was simply a tired, decent woman standing on the edge of treason. "There's a girl, Coldharbour. Dark hair, sixteen, father drowned and returned. She was seen on the roof with you. My superiors want her name. I have written in my ledger that the witness was of indeterminate age and unremarkable appearance, and that I am pursuing several leads."

Edmund felt something loosen in his chest that he had not known was clenched.

"Why?" he asked. "Why lie for her? Why warn me?"

Veyra stood in the doorway a moment longer, lamplight catching the gray at her temples, the stitched tear in her sleeve, the ledger-shaped bulge in her coat where a torn god lived folded against her heart.

"Because I took an oath to protect this city," she said, "and I have finally worked out that the city and the Council are not the same thing. Good night, charm-seller. Burn nothing. Change nothing. If they search, let them find exactly what I found—an honest shop and a dishonest man. We may yet make use of both."

Her boots went down the stairs, unhurried, deliberate, wanting to be heard.

Edmund stood alone in his shop with the lamp burning low and the tide knocking patiently at the walls of the world, and understood that something had ended tonight that had lasted fifteen years. He was still hunted. He was still lying. But for the first time since he had walked out of the burning archive, he was not lying *alone*—and somewhere across the dark city, a woman was committing treason one falsified ledger line at a time, and a girl with a dragon's scale was hiding in the lower wards, and beneath it all, vast and guttering, something ancient coiled around the foundations of Greyhaven, waiting to learn what its oldest debtor intended to do.

He snuffed the lamp and sat in the dark, and began, very quietly, to plan.
