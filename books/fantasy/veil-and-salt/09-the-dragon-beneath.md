## 9. The Dragon Beneath

The scale did not glow. Edmund had expected it to—some soft lambence, some holy shimmer, the way relics behaved in the old stories he had once helped make true. Instead it sat in Sela's cupped palms like a chip of slate, dark and dull, and it was warm. Warm in a way that had nothing to do with fire. Warm the way a sleeping animal is warm, the way a hand you have held for years is warm.

"It's heavier than it looks," Sela said, for the fourth time since they'd left the cellar shrine. She said it the way people repeat things when they are afraid and need the world to keep being ordinary. "It's heavier than it should be."

"Everything true is," Edmund said, and immediately regretted it. It sounded like a charm-seller's patter, and he was tired of his own voice sounding like that. Fifteen years of harmless lies had worn grooves in him; sometimes the truth came out polished by accident.

They went down where no one was supposed to go down anymore. The Edict of Ash had sealed the undercity's mouths with iron grates and poured salt into the old cisterns—not the blessed salt of Greyhaven's thresholds but the other kind, the kind that killed memory—and yet the city was old, older than the Council, older than the Edict, older perhaps than the names it had outlawed, and old cities do not close their doors so much as forget which ones they opened. Edmund knew the forgotten ones. He had mapped them all, in another life, with another name, when mapping had been a sacred act and not a crime.

He led her through a gap behind the tannery where the wall had settled away from its foundation, down a stair that smelled of wet stone and old tallow, through a cistern whose water stood black and still as ink. The scale grew warmer with every step. By the time they reached the third level down—a place where the architecture stopped making sense, where arches sprang from walls at angles no mason would choose—it was almost too hot to hold, and Sela carried it wrapped in her shawl like an infant.

"How far?" she whispered. Her whisper was unnecessary. Nothing lived down here. That was what she believed. Edmund knew better, and the knowing sat in his chest like a swallowed coal.

"Not far," he said. "Farther than distance."

She gave him a look—the look young people give old men who speak in riddles—but she kept walking. She had been keeping walking since the harbor, since her father had walked out of the grey water alive with a goddess in his mouth, since the tide had risen at a name and drowned three streets. She was sixteen and she had already outlived the version of herself who thought the world was small.

* * *

The passage narrowed, then opened, then narrowed again, and somewhere in the narrowing Edmund began to feel it: a pressure, gentle and enormous, like standing near the sea and feeling it know you. The air changed temperature. Not colder. Warmer. The stone underfoot lost its chill.

Sela stopped. "Do you hear that?"

He did. A sound below hearing, or beneath it—a slow, vast rhythm, like breathing conducted by mountains. In. Out. In. Each breath took nearly a minute. Each exhale stirred the dust on the floor in a faint, patient sigh.

Edmund's knees nearly failed him. Not from fear. From recognition. He had heard that breathing once before, fifteen years ago, in a place that was not a place, while he pressed a quill to vellum and made the worst decision of his long, cowardly life.

"She's here," he said. His voice came out strange, flattened, the voice of a man reading his own death warrant. "Under everything. Under the whole city."

"What is?"

"The reason your prayers work." He looked at the girl, at her chapped hands and her stubborn chin, and found he could not soften it. "And the reason they stopped working fifteen years ago. Come on."

The last corridor sloped downward into a dark that was not empty. As their eyes adjusted—as the warmth grew until it was like walking into a held breath—they saw that the walls were not walls. Or rather, the walls were walls, and something else was also there, occupying the same space the way a word occupies a page. Curves of shadow within shadow. Ridges like hillsides. And everywhere, faintly, a dull red light pulsing in time with that mountainous breath, guttering, guttering, like a lamp running out of oil.

Sela unwrapped the scale. It rose from her hands. It did not float, exactly—it simply declined to obey gravity any longer, drifting forward into the dark like a compass needle finding north, and they followed it because there was nothing else in the world to follow.

The chamber, when they finally stood inside it, was not a chamber. It was a hollow in the dragon herself, a space between coils vast as cathedral naves, and Emberveil filled the world.

Edmund had seen her last young—or what passed for young in a creature older than the coastline—and even then she had been enormous beyond sense, her wings folded like storm fronts, her eyes like wells of patient gold. Now she was ruin. Her scales, each one large as a door, had gone grey and brittle, cracked along their seams, and through the cracks showed not flesh but embers—dim, dying, sinking coals. Her breathing shook the floor. One eye opened as they entered, an eye the size of a millpond, and its gold had gone the color of old brass, clouded, exhausted, and unbearably kind.

"*Little cartographer,*" she said.

Her voice did not come through the air. It came up through the stone, through their boots, through their bones, as if the city itself had learned speech. Sela made a sound like a struck bell and grabbed Edmund's arm.

"You know me," Edmund said. His voice sounded very small in the vastness, a mouse squeaking in a granary.

"*I have known you since before you had a name worth keeping.*" The great eye turned to Sela, and the girl went rigid under its regard. "*And this is the one who knocked. Fifteen years I have held the door shut, and this child put her ear to it and asked, and I was too tired to pretend I wasn't home.*"

"I don't understand," Sela said. "I prayed. For my da. He was drowned, and I prayed, and Maren answered—"

"*You prayed to a name that was locked away,*" Emberveil said, "*and the lock is me, little one, and I am failing. Every miracle your city has seen these past weeks has been a seam splitting. And when I die—*" The breath that followed was long, and rattled like a landslide deciding whether to fall. "*When I die, every seal breaks at once. Every name tears free together. Maren and her brothers and sisters, the bright ones and the hungry ones, all of them, all at once, into a city that has spent fifteen years learning to forget them.*"

The words fell into the dark and lay there.

"That can't happen," Edmund said. It came out flat, automatic, the reflex of a man who had spent fifteen years refusing to think about exactly this. "There has to be a way to release them slowly. One at a time. There has to be—"

"*There was.*" The eye came back to him, and now the kindness in it was terrible, because kindness that knows too much always is. "*There was a map, little cartographer. Drawn by a man who loved the gods enough to learn every one of their true names, and feared them enough to write those names where only he could find them. The Council stole his atlas and built their seals upon it, name by name, page by page. But the seals needed an anchor. Something living. Something that could hold the weight of nine hundred gods' names without breaking.*"

"No," Edmund said.

"*So the man made a bargain. With a dying dragon, in a place outside the world. His safety, his hiding, his fifteen quiet years—in exchange for carrying the lock inside himself. In exchange for being the door.*"

"I said no." But his voice cracked on the second word, and Sela turned to stare at him, and the heat in the chamber seemed suddenly to come from inside his own skin.

He remembered. God help him, he remembered—he had paid for perfect memory with everything else, and it had never once let him forget. The god-realm unspooling around him like a storm given geometry. Emberveil's ember-light dimming even then, her wings heavy with age. His own young voice, arrogant and terrified: *I want to survive this. I want them to never find me. Whatever it costs.*

And hers: *Then carry it. Be the hinge. And pray you are never asked to open yourself.*

"You used me," he whispered. The words tasted like ash and like relief, because at last, at last, the shapeless dread of fifteen years had a shape. "You let me believe I'd just—hidden. Run away. All this time I thought I was a coward, and I was—what? Furniture? A lock?"

"*A hinge,*" Emberveil said gently. "*And hinges do not get to choose when the door swings. You wanted safety more than truth. This is what safety cost. Not nothing. Never nothing.*"

Sela's grip on his arm had gone tight enough to bruise. "Edmund," she said carefully, "is that your real name?"

He couldn't answer. He was looking at the dragon and seeing, superimposed over her ruined magnificence, a younger self kneeling on vellum-bright ground, quill in hand, trading away a future he hadn't understood. He wanted to scream at that boy. He wanted to kneel beside him. He wanted, more than anything, to ask him the question he had avoided for fifteen years: *Did you know? Did some part of you know what you were buying, and buy it anyway?*

Because the boy had smiled, at the end. He remembered that now, the way you remember things you've spent years not remembering. The boy had smiled like a man setting down a burden, and said, *At least it will be quiet.*

Quiet. Fifteen years of quiet. Charms that didn't work. Prayers that bounced off a locked sky. A whole city's soul in escrow, and him the vault it was stored in, walking through the night market selling luck-knots to grieving dockworkers and calling it survival.

"I'm sorry," he said aloud. He didn't know who he was saying it to. The dragon. The boy. Sela. The city overhead, ten thousand sleepers dreaming on top of a dying lock. "I didn't—I didn't know what I—"

"*You knew,*" Emberveil said, not cruelly. "*That is the mercy I cannot give you. You knew, and you chose, and now the choosing comes due.*" The ember-light in her cracks pulsed, guttered, steadied. Slower than before. "*I do not tell you this to wound you, little cartographer. I tell you because time is short now. Days, perhaps. When my heart goes out, the seals go with it—all of them, all at once, and the gods will not arrive. They will detonate. Nine hundred divinities, fifteen years of pent worship, and a city standing on the fault line.*"

"Then we take the seals apart," Sela said fiercely. She stepped forward, still holding the scale, which hovered between them now like a small dark moon. "One at a time. You said there was a way—one at a time. Show us. He drew the map, he can read it backward, right? He can undo it the way it was done?"

Emberveil was silent for the space of three long breaths. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped, become almost private, meant for Edmund alone though Sela heard every word.

"*There is a way. There has always been a way. But it is not a key, little one. It is a flame.*" The great eye closed, then opened, and Edmund saw in its depths something that made his blood run cold: tenderness. Real tenderness, the kind that precedes farewell. "*One true flame. The last of mine. Enough to unmake the seals gently, name by name, the way they should have been freed—if someone carries it into the heart of the lock and lets it burn. And the lock, little cartographer, is you.*"

The silence that followed was absolute. Even the mountain-breath seemed to hold itself.

"And if no one does?" Edmund asked. His voice was steady. He was distantly proud of that, and distantly horrified.

"*Then the door bursts off its hinges, and Greyhaven learns what nine hundred gods look like when they arrive angry.*"

Sela was crying. He realized it without looking at her—heard it in the way her breathing had gone ragged, felt it in the tremor of the hand gripping his sleeve. "No," she was saying. "No, there's another way, there's always—people say that, that there's always another way—"

"People say that," Edmund agreed quietly, "because mostly it's true."

He looked up at the dragon—at the dying god-engine coiled around his city's bones, holding back a flood with the last of her strength because a frightened young man fifteen years ago had asked her to help him hide. And the strangest thing happened, then. The fear did not leave him, but it moved over, made room, and something else sat down beside it. Something that felt, horribly and wonderfully, like relief.

Fifteen years he had been waiting for this. He understood that now. Every charm sold, every name withheld, every night he'd lain awake listening for boots on his stairs—he had been waiting for the bill to come due, and here it was, itemized at last, and there was a terrible comfort in knowing the amount.

"There's time," he said. "You said days. Then I have days to finish what the boy started. To do it properly this time." He met the vast brass eye. "Not to hide the names. To give them back."

"*Little cartographer,*" Emberveil breathed, and the whole chamber sighed with her, dust rising like incense, "*you could not even give them back to yourself.*"

But above them—far above, through ten fathoms of stone and street and salt—bells had begun to ring. Not the harbor bells, not the curfew bells. All of them. Every bell in Greyhaven at once, ringing in no pattern any ringer had chosen, and through the stone, faint but unmistakable, came a sound that froze the blood of everyone in the dark:

Screaming. Thousands of voices, screaming in joy or terror, and beneath them, rising through the foundations, through the dragon's failing body, through Edmund's own scarred and mortal-seeming chest—

a second heartbeat, where no heartbeat should be. Quickening. Awake.

Something had torn loose early.
