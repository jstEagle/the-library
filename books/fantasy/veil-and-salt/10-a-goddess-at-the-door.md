## 10. A Goddess at the Door

The tide came in wrong that night.

Edmund noticed it from his window above the charm-shop, the way a man notices a familiar face gone strange—same features, same rhythm, but something behind the eyes that did not belong. The water should have pulled back by now, should have left the harbor mud glistening and stinking under the moon. Instead it stood high, unnaturally still, holding its breath. The fishing boats rocked without reason, their lines slack, as if the sea had forgotten them entirely.

He had not slept properly in days. Emberveil's revelation sat in him like swallowed stone: *every sealed name will tear free at once*. He had spent three nights with his old instruments spread across the floor, recalculating the seals' decay, and every calculation ended the same way—in catastrophe, or in the atlas burning on his own hearth, which was its own kind of catastrophe. He had built his immortality out of that map. He had built his prison out of it too.

A knock came at the door below. Three knocks, unhurried, spaced like waves against a hull.

Edmund went down the stairs with a candle and no small measure of dread. Past midnight, after everything that had happened—the flood in the lower wards, Veyra's questions, the painted names blooming across the city walls like lichen—he had learned to fear polite knocking more than pounding. Pounding was soldiers. Politeness was something else.

He opened the door.

A woman stood on his threshold, dressed in oilskins the color of deep water, her hair dark and heavy with salt spray though no rain had fallen. She was ordinary in every particular—a fishwife's face, wind-chapped, middle-aged, the kind of woman who mended nets and argued prices and buried husbands lost to weather—and she was utterly, catastrophically not ordinary at all. The harbor behind her had gone silent. Not quiet: *silent*, the silence of held breath, of a congregation waiting for the hymn to begin. And her eyes, when they lifted to his, were the exact green-grey of the tide over drowned things.

"Edmund Coldharbour," she said, and his name in her mouth sounded like a bell rung underwater. "You've kept my seals well. I wanted to see the hand that bound me."

His candle flame bent toward her, the way flames bend toward an open door.

"Maren," he said. His voice came out steady, which surprised him. Fifteen years of practice at steadiness. "You shouldn't be here. Speaking through a drowned man is one thing. Wearing flesh in the open—" He glanced past her at the empty street. "If Veyra's people see—"

"Veyra's people are asleep," the goddess said. "I asked the night politely. The night remembers me." She tilted her head, and there was something almost amused in the gesture, something ancient wearing patience like a coat. "May I come in, cartographer? Or do you make your guests stand in salt?"

*Salt.* He looked down. Without deciding to, without noticing, he had scattered a line of it across his threshold sometime in the last three days—old habit, old fear. It lay there between them, plain and pathetic, a ward drawn by a man who had once warded the doors of heaven.

"It won't stop you," he said.

"No," Maren agreed gently. "But it tells me what you're afraid of. That's worth more than stopping."

She stepped over the line as if stepping over a child's chalk drawing, and Edmund closed the door behind her because leaving it open felt worse.

* * *

She would not sit. She moved through his shop the way the tide moves through a sea cave—touching everything, claiming nothing. Her fingers trailed across the shelf of luck-knots, the jars of salt charmed into harmlessness, the little brass compasses he sold to sailors who didn't know they were buying pieces of a dead world. At the back wall she stopped, before the framed chart of Greyhaven's harbor that hung there for customers, and studied it with an expression he could not read.

"You drew this," she said. Not a question.

"I draw all my charts."

"You know what I mean." She turned. In the low light her face shifted—not changing shape, but changing *weight*, the way the sea changes weight before a storm. "Fifteen years ago, a young man came to the edge of the god-realm with ink-stained fingers and a hunger in him bigger than his body. He mapped my halls, Edmund. My tides, my moods, the names I answer to when mortals call. He drew me so precisely that when the Edict's founders needed to cage me, they used his lines as the bars."

"The Edict wasn't mine."

"No. But the map was. And you were paid for it, weren't you? Safety. Silence. A long life with no one looking too closely." She picked up a luck-knot from the shelf, turned it over in her fingers. "Do you know what fifteen years underwater feels like, when you are the water? Like being a word no one is allowed to say. Like drowning in yourself."

Edmund's hands had gone cold. "Why are you here? To punish me? Take your place in the queue behind the Council, the Inquisitor, and my own conscience. It's a crowded dock."

Something flickered across her face—hurt, quickly mastered, and that hurt undid him more than anger would have. Gods, he thought, were supposed to be above such things. But Maren had always been the closest of the old pantheon to mortal grief; she took the drowned into her halls and sang to them, the fishermen said, so they wouldn't be frightened in the dark.

"I'm not here to punish," she said. "I'm here because I remember what you were, before the bargain. And because I'm honest, Edmund, in a way that mortals rarely let each other be. So I'll be honest now."

She set the luck-knot down and faced him fully, and the shop seemed suddenly very small around her, the way a rowboat is small on open water.

"You are starving," she said. "Don't argue. I can smell it on you the way sharks smell blood—fifteen years of it. You sold charms that don't work because working charms would mean mattering. You refused a child because saying yes would mean being seen. You walk through this city like a ghost haunting its own life. You have safety, Edmund. You have nothing else. No name anyone speaks. No hand that holds yours. You lie awake doing arithmetic about your own death." Her voice softened, and somehow that was crueler than any accusation. "I know hunger when I see it. The sea is full of hungry men who traded everything for survival and called the trade wisdom."

"And you're offering to feed me." His mouth was dry. "What does it cost?"

"Nothing you haven't already paid once." She stepped closer, and the smell of her was brine and cold stone and, beneath it, something older—rain on the first morning of the world. "Give me the complete atlas. Every page, every seal-line, every name you hid in your cartography. With it, I can unmake the Edict's work myself—free my brothers and sisters without waiting for the dragon to die and tear us loose in fire and ruin. And you—" she reached out, and did not touch him, but he felt the touch anyway, cool as water closing over the ears, "—you would have back what you surrendered to make the bargain. Your divinity. Your place. You walked among us once as more than a mapper, Edmund. You've forgotten, but I haven't. You could walk again. Belong again. Be *known*—by beings who never forget, who never leave, who will love you for as long as the tide turns."

The words went into him like warmth into frozen hands.

That was the terrible thing, he thought distantly. Not that she threatened. Not that she lied. But that every word was true. He *was* starving. He lay awake at night cataloguing the shapes of his loneliness the way he'd once catalogued coastlines. Fifteen years of small harmless charms and careful lies and a name spoken by no one. And here was the sea itself, offering him a place in the choir.

"What would I be?" he heard himself ask. "Among you. Exactly."

"Honored," Maren said. "Remembered. Worshipped, if you wished it—you earned worship once; the god-realm still has altars with your sigil on them, dusty but unbroken." She smiled, and it was a kind smile, and that was the cruelest thing yet. "You would be one of us. Never alone again. Never hiding. Never afraid."

*Never afraid.*

He closed his eyes. Behind them he saw it all laid out: the halls he had mapped, lit again; the pantheon turning toward him as he entered, recognition dawning on faces older than mountains; himself, whole, restored, *belonging*—

And beneath the vision, faint but persistent, another image. A cellar shrine full of frightened people singing badly. A girl with salt-cracked hands holding a dragon's scale like it was the most precious thing in the world. Sela's voice: *they answered. Someone finally answered.*

"Sela prays to you," he said quietly, eyes still closed. "Did you offer her divinity?"

Maren was silent a moment. "Sela wants nothing. That's what makes her prayers clean."

"And mine are dirty. Because I want."

"Because you're *hungry*," Maren said, and there was no judgment in it, only the vast patient sadness of the tide. "Hunger isn't sin, Edmund. It's just hunger. But I promised honesty, so here is the rest of it: gods do not love the way mortals love. We love the way the sea loves the shore—constantly, and without ever asking the shore what it wants. If you come to us, you will be adored forever, and you will never again be *met*. Do you understand the difference? Mortals meet each other. They argue, and disappoint, and choose each other daily despite it. Worship has no choosing in it. It simply flows, like water, and asks nothing, and gives nothing back that you can hold."

Her honesty was a knife offered handle-first. That was the worst of it. She was letting him see the trap clearly, trusting him to walk in anyway, because she believed—perhaps rightly—that his hunger was stronger than his sight.

"That's not belonging," he said slowly. "That's being a lighthouse. Loved by everyone. Touched by no one."

"Now you understand why I came at night, alone, instead of in glory." Her voice dropped. "Take the offer, Edmund. I hope you do—I want my freedom, and yours is the key, and I will not pretend otherwise. But take it with your eyes open. I refuse to win by lying. The old gods made enough cages."

His hand trembled at his side. The scarred palm—the one that had caught the axe, the one that ached in rain—throbbed now as if remembering. He thought of the atlas in its hiding place beneath the floorboards, wrapped in oilcloth, fifteen years of his soul folded flat. One page of it torn away and sitting in Veyra's possession. All he had to do was kneel, dig, and hand over the rest.

No more arithmetic. No more hiding. No more being a ghost.

He took one step toward the floorboards.

* * *

The door banged open.

Sela stood in the doorway, breathing hard, lantern in one hand and the dragon's scale clutched in the other, her hair plastered to her forehead with harbor fog. She must have run the whole way from the lower wards. Behind her the night was waking—somewhere a dog barked, somewhere a shutter creaked, the polite sleep Maren had asked for fraying at its edges.

"You're doing it," Sela said. Not a question either. She looked from Edmund to the goddess, and her chin came up the way it did when the harbormaster tried to cheat the widows' fund. "You're doing exactly what you did to the drowned man. Walking into someone's house and making the drowning sound like a gift."

"Sela." Maren's voice was warm, and Edmund heard the undertow in it now, the way he hadn't before. "Child, I sang your father's body home to you. I owe you gentleness."

"You owe me truth." Sela's knuckles whitened around the scale. "My da told me about offers like yours. He said the sea gives with one hand and keeps the ledger with the other. What did you promise him?" She jabbed a finger at Edmund. "What did she promise you?"

"Divinity," Edmund said. His voice sounded far away to himself. "Belonging. An end to being alone."

Sela stared at him. And then—worse than anger, worse than scorn—her face crumpled with something like pity.

"Mister Coldharbour. You sell charms to people who are lonely. Half your customers buy luck-knots just so someone will talk to them while they tie it. You *know* what it is to be alone." She stepped into the shop, between him and the goddess, small and soaked and absolutely unmovable. "And you'd trade the whole city for a seat at a table where nobody ever argues with you? Where nobody ever *chooses* you, because they can't do anything else?" She shook her head. "That's not a family. That's a mirror that says yes."

The words landed in him like a hook set deep.

Maren's expression had gone still, the stillness of deep water. "You speak boldly to a goddess, girl."

"My da spoke boldly to the sea every day of his life," Sela said. "It's how fishermen pray. Somebody has to tell you when you're being unfair." She lifted the scale, and it caught the candlelight and threw it back ember-red. "Emberveil is dying so we can go free *gently*. That's the plan. That's the sacrifice. And you want to shortcut it because waiting is hard—even for a god, even for fifteen years." Her voice cracked, and she pushed through it. "You said you refuse to win by lying. Then here's the truth: if he gives you that map, you won't free your family. You'll just trade one cage for another, and you'll be the jailer this time. Is that the tide-goddess my da loved? Because he loved a wilder one than that."

Silence. The candle flame stood perfectly straight, listening.

And Edmund saw it—saw the moment Maren's honesty met Sela's, and found itself outmatched. Because the goddess had been honest about the cost to *him*, but the girl was honest about the cost to *everyone*, and that was a larger arithmetic than any he had ever drawn.

Maren looked at Edmund for a long moment. Something moved behind the green-grey eyes—regret, perhaps, or relief, or the exhaustion of a prisoner offered freedom by the wrong means.

"He nearly said yes," she told Sela, as if reporting the weather. "Remember that, both of you. Remember that the offer was real, and the hunger was real, and the choice was still his." She turned toward the door, then paused, her hand on the frame. "Edmund. When you burn the atlas—and you will, I think, because you looked at her when you decided, not at me—know that some part of the sea will grieve the version of you that said yes tonight. He would have been magnificent. And miserable. We are often both."

She stepped over the threshold, over the useless line of salt, and paused once more.

"For what it's worth," Maren said, without turning around, "the child is right. I loved a wilder sea than the one I've become. Perhaps that's the cage I chose." And she was gone—not walking away, but going the way a wave goes, leaving the street wet and the night ordinary again, the harbor sighing back into its old rhythms behind her.

* * *

For a long time neither of them moved.

Then Sela set the scale down on the counter, very carefully, as if it might startle, and looked at him with her father's stubborn eyes.

"You were going to give it to her."

"Yes."

"Why did you stop?"

Edmund looked at his hands—the scarred one, the steady one—and found he couldn't answer honestly, because the honest answer was *you*, and he wasn't ready to say it. A girl he'd known a handful of weeks, standing between him and eternity, armed with nothing but a dead fisherman's common sense.

"Because you reminded me what the map was for," he said instead. "Or what it should have been for. Maps aren't cages, Sela. They're invitations. I forgot that for fifteen years."

She nodded slowly, accepting maybe half of it. Then her gaze dropped to the floorboards—to the spot where, God help him, her eyes had gone with unerring instinct—and back up to his face.

"So," she said. "When do we burn it?"

Edmund opened his mouth to answer—and stopped. Because outside, in the wet street where the goddess had stood, a figure detached itself from the shadows by the chandlery opposite. Tall, cloaked, moving with the weary precision he would have known anywhere. Inquisitor Veyra raised one hand—not a threat, not yet—and in the lamplight he could see she was holding something up for him to see.

A page. Torn from a map. And beside her, half-hidden in the doorway of the chandlery, a second figure in the grey and gold of the Salt Court guard—who had seen everything, heard everything, and was already turning to run for the Chancellery.

Veyra's eyes met his across the street, and she mouthed two words, clear as a bell:

*They know.*
