## 13. Emberveil's Last Question

The dark beneath Greyhaven was not empty. Edmund had known that for fifteen years in the way a man knows a weight he has stopped feeling—the way a shoulder knows an old scar, the way a tongue knows a missing tooth. But knowing and standing inside it were different things.

They had followed the scale's warmth down through the drowned cisterns and the bone-dry aqueducts, through corridors where the salt in the walls glittered like frost that refused to melt, until the passages opened without warning into a vastness that made Edmund's breath catch and hold.

Emberveil filled the cavern the way night fills a valley.

The dragon lay coiled around the city's foundations—around them literally, Edmund realized, around the great root-stones and pilings that had held Greyhaven above the tide since before the Edict, before the Council, before anyone now living could remember why the first stones were laid in the shape they were. The dragon's body was the shape beneath the shape. Every street above, every ward and stairway, had grown around this sleeping coil like ivy around a fallen tree.

And Emberveil was dying. There was no mistaking it, no pretending otherwise. The scales that had once burned bronze and gold were gone grey as ash, cracked along their seams, and from the cracks came a light—not fire, not anymore, but the memory of fire, guttering in slow pulses like a heart deciding whether to keep beating. Each pulse lit the cavern and let it fall dark again, and in the falling dark Edmund heard the sound the dragon made: a long, low exhalation, less a breath than a tide going out.

"Edmund." Sela's voice beside him was very small. She had the scale cupped in both hands, and it glowed in answer to the pulses, brightening and dimming, brightening and dimming, a child's lantern calling across a dark field to its mother. "It knows we're here."

"It has always known we're here," Edmund said. "It has known everything for fifteen years."

He stepped forward, and his footsteps rang strangely on the stone—not hollow, as he'd expected, but resonant, as though the floor itself were a drumhead stretched over something alive. Which, he understood now, it was. He had walked these streets for fifteen years believing he hid beneath the city. He had been walking on its keeper the whole time.

The eye opened when he was still twenty paces away.

It was the size of a chapel window, and it did not gleam the way reptile eyes gleamed in stories. It was deep and patient and terribly tired, gold shot through with grey, and when it fixed on Edmund, he felt the full weight of what he had done settle onto his shoulders like a wet cloak. Fifteen years ago he had stood before this eye and made his bargain. He had told himself a hundred times since that there had been no other way. Standing here again, older and no wiser, he found the telling did not come so easily.

"You came back," Emberveil said.

The voice was not a voice. It arrived through the stone, through the soles of Edmund's boots, through the water beaded on the walls—a vibration more felt than heard, old as the mountain the city stood on. Sela swayed and put her hand against the wall to steady herself.

"I didn't know if you'd want me to," Edmund said.

"Want." The dragon's nostrils flared, and a thread of true smoke curled up into the dark, the first real smoke Edmund had seen from it, thin as incense. "Want is a young creature's word. I have passed beyond wanting. I am at the place past it, where there is only what is owed, and what is chosen, and how little time remains to choose." The great head shifted, scales grinding like millstones, and the eye came closer, close enough that Edmund could see himself reflected in it, small and bent and holding a lantern he no longer needed. "You brought the girl."

"She brought me," Edmund said. "She always does."

Sela stepped up beside him. She was soaked to the knees from the cistern crossing, her hair plastered to her face, and she looked up at the dying god-beast with none of the terror Edmund had expected and all of the plain, unguarded wonder that had gotten them both into this. "You're Emberveil," she said. "You're the last one."

"I am the last one," the dragon agreed. "Which means I am also the first one, now, and the only one, and soon I will be none at all. Language was not built for what I am becoming." A pause, another guttering pulse of light. "But you did not climb down through the drowned places to hear an old beast complain. You came because the seals are failing. You came because Maren walks your harbor in a dead man's skin. You came because the names are tearing loose, one by one, and no one above remembers why."

"The bargain," Edmund said. His mouth was dry. "Tell me what happens when you die. All of it. No more half-truths between us—I've carried yours for fifteen years. Carry mine a moment longer if you must, but give me the whole of yours."

* * *

Emberveil told him.

It took hours. The dragon spoke slowly, in the manner of creatures for whom time is measured in centuries, and Edmund sat on the warm stone at the edge of the great coil and listened while the story he had spent fifteen years refusing to tell himself was told back to him in full.

The seals were not spells, not exactly. They were knots. When the Edict of Ash had come down and the Council's founders demanded a world with no gods in it, they had needed something to do with the gods—they could not kill divinity, only fold it small—and so they had taken the atlas. Edmund's atlas. His maps of the god-realm, drawn in the years when he had walked those shining provinces and set down every river of light and every name spoken at the world's beginning. Names are doors: he had learned that truth before he learned to shave. And the founders of the Edict had used his doors to build their prison, folding each god into the map-province where its name lived, sealing the pages, burying the atlas in law and ritual and forgetting.

Except the atlas had not stayed buried. Because Edmund had made a second bargain, before the first, with a dying dragon.

"I was already failing then," Emberveil said. "The last of my kind does not die of wounds or years. We die of loneliness. There is a fire in us that burns for company, and when there is no company, it turns inward." The pulse of light dimmed as it spoke, and Edmund understood suddenly, horribly, that the dragon's fading and the seals' weakening were the same event seen from two sides. "I could not save myself. But a young cartographer came to me with a mad plan—to hide the gods' true names inside his own memory, woven into his maps, so that even if the Edict's founders seized his work, the deepest names would be somewhere they could never look. Inside him. Inside the man who drew the doors."

"And you agreed to hide him," Edmund said. "To make the hiding permanent. To make me—" He stopped. Started again. "You made me forget. Not just the realm. Everything. My name, my life, the reason I ran. You folded me the way the Edict folded the gods."

"I gave you a mortal life," Emberveil said, "which is what you asked for. You said, *let me be no one, let me be safe, let me sell small charms and never be found.* I granted it. I have kept it for fifteen years, and keeping it has cost me—" the light stuttered, and for a moment the whole cavern blazed, every crack in every scale shining, and Sela cried out and covered her eyes— "everything I had left. Your forgetting was a seal too, Edmund Coldharbour. The strongest one. And I am the lock it hangs upon. When I die, every seal dies with me. The gods' names will tear free of the atlas all at once, in a single hour, and they will come back to a world that outlawed them, furious and unforgiven, and there will be war such as the god-realm itself never saw."

The silence afterward was enormous. Water dripped somewhere far off, counting seconds.

"There must be another way," Sela said. Her voice shook but did not break. "There's always—people say there isn't, but there always is, if you look hard enough—"

"People say that," Emberveil said gently, "because they have not yet looked hard enough to find the place where it isn't true." The eye turned back to Edmund. "But there is one way. One. And it is why I let you live, all these years, when a kinder death might have served us both."

* * *

The dragon raised its head.

It was a labor Edmund could see costing the creature everything—muscles like continents shifting under ash-grey scale, the neck unfolding by degrees, joint by ancient joint, until the vast skull hung above him and the heat of its breath washed over his face like a door opening onto summer. In the breath he smelled things he had not smelled in fifteen years: cedar and lightning, the green smell of the god-realm's first morning, the salt-sweet air off Maren's shore. His eyes stung. He did not wipe them.

"Before I give you anything," Emberveil said, "you will answer me one question. It is the question I should have asked you fifteen years ago, and did not, because I was dying and you were afraid and we were both in a hurry to be finished with honesty." The eye, so close now, filled Edmund's whole vision, gold and grey and bottomless. "When you drew the atlas—when you mapped the god-realm, province by province, name by name, when you wrote down the words that are doors—what was it *for*, Edmund? Did you draw the map to own the gods? Or to free them?"

Edmund opened his mouth.

Nothing came out.

He had thought about many things in fifteen years of selling luck-knots and denial-charms in the night market. He had thought about the taste of bread, the price of lamp oil, the faces of customers whose prayers he pretended not to hear. He had thought about Veyra's sharp grey eyes and Sela's stubborn chin and the exact blue of the harbor at dawn. He had thought, in the worst hours, about whether the gods dreamed in their folding, and whether they hated him, and which of them would come for him first.

He had never once thought about what the atlas was *for*. Because to think about it was to stand at the center of himself, and he had spent fifteen years building a life shaped like a hole where his center used to be.

"I don't know," he said.

The words fell into the dark and the dark held them.

"I don't know," he said again, and his voice cracked on it, and he found he did not care. "Everyone believes—I believed—that I was saving them. Hiding the names so the Edict couldn't burn them out of the world entirely. That was the story I told the dragon who found me weeping over my own drawings. But that's not the same thing as knowing. Maybe I wanted to be the one who held them. Maybe I liked being the man who knew the doors. Maybe I drew the map because I couldn't bear for the god-realm to belong to anyone but me." He laughed, and it came out wrong, wet and short. "Fifteen years. I've had fifteen years to decide, and I never decided, because deciding meant looking, and looking meant—"

"Meant this," Emberveil said. "Meant sitting in the dark under the city you hid in, admitting you don't know your own heart." The great head lowered, until the dragon's brow nearly touched the stone before Edmund. "Good. That is the only honest answer you could have given. A liar would have said *to free them*. A tyrant would have said *to own them*. Only a man who truly never knew would sit silent this long." A rumble moved through the coils, and Edmund realized with a start that it was laughter—or the ghost of laughter, the shape laughter leaves behind. "You have not changed as much as you think, cartographer. You are still the man who would rather carry a question for fifteen years than answer it badly."

"Is that a compliment?"

"It is a diagnosis." The light in the scales gathered, steadied, burned brighter than it had since they arrived. "Now listen, because I will not have strength to repeat it. There is one way to unmake the seals without tearing them. Fire. Not my ordinary fire—that went out with my kind. But a dragon keeps one flame in reserve, deeper than the lungs, older than the body. The first flame. The one we are kindled from. It can burn a knot open without breaking the rope. It could unmake every seal in your atlas gently, page by page, name by name, and let the gods return the way rain returns—unwelcome perhaps, but natural, and necessary, and impossible to fight."

Sela made a small sound of hope, and Edmund's heart leapt toward it before he caught himself, because Emberveil was still speaking, and the dragon's voice had gone quiet in the way that quiet things go before avalanches.

"But the first flame cannot be thrown. It cannot be breathed. It must be *carried*. Held in a living hand, fed by a living will, walked step by step to whatever must be unmade. And it burns what carries it. Not quickly. Not mercifully. From the first moment it touches you, it is unmaking you, as it will unmake the seals—gently, thoroughly, completely. Whoever takes it will have time to finish the work. They will not have time for anything after."

The cavern was very still. Even the dripping water seemed to wait.

"That is the gift," Embervevel said. "That is the only gift I have left to give. One true flame, enough to free every god in creation—and a death attached to it, plain as a price tag, no fine print, no hidden clause. I promised you once that my bargains were honest. This is the last one, and it is the most honest of all." The eye closed, then opened, and in it Edmund saw reflected not himself now but the city above—every rooftop, every lamplit window, every soul asleep tonight above a dying god who had held the ceiling up for them out of nothing but an old promise to a frightened man. "Take it freely, knowing what it costs, or leave it, and take your chances with the tearing. Those are the only roads. I will not choose for you. Choosing for you is what started all of this."

Edmund looked at his hands.

His left hand, the cartographer's hand, the one that had drawn ten thousand miles of shining country and every door-name in creation. It was trembling. He watched it tremble with a strange detachment, as though it belonged to the young man in the flashback of his own fragmented memory—the one kneeling before a dying dragon, saying *let me be no one, let me be safe.*

Safe. He had traded everything for safe, and safe had turned out to be a room with no windows in a house with no name, and he had lived in it fifteen years, and it had never once felt like living.

"Edmund." Sela's hand on his arm. Her eyes were wet and fierce. "Don't. Don't you dare decide right now, don't you dare do what you always do, carrying the terrible thing alone so no one else has to—we'll find another way, we'll—"

"Sela." He said it gently, and she stopped, because she had heard the tone in it, the tone of a man who has stopped arguing with himself. "There is no other way. She looked. You always look hard enough. Look now, and tell me what you see."

She looked—at the dragon, at the failing light, at the cracked and guttering scales—and he watched her arrive at the same country he had just crossed, watched her shoulders fall.

"It's not fair," she whispered.

"No," Edmund agreed. "It rarely is. That's the first thing they should teach children, instead of geography." He rose. His knees ached. He was fifty-one years old and mortal-made and tired down to the marrow, and he found, examining himself honestly for perhaps the first time in his life, that he was not afraid. Or rather—he was afraid, thoroughly and completely, the way any man is afraid of burning—but the fear sat alongside something else, something that had been growing in him since the night a dockworker's prayer was answered, since a drowned fisherman walked out of the harbor speaking with the tide's voice, since a girl begged him for a charm that really works and he had refused her too harshly and hated himself for it ever after.

The something else was certainty. Plain, unglamorous, earned.

"Yes," he said to the dragon. "I'll carry it."

Emberveil regarded him for a long moment. Then, slowly, the great jaws parted—not wide, just enough—and from somewhere deeper than the throat, deeper than the body, deeper than the world, a light began to rise. It was white at the core and gold at the rim and it did not flicker, and looking into it Edmund felt every sealed name in his forgotten atlas stir at once, like birds waking in a single tree.

"One condition," the dragon said, and the light paused, hovering between them, impossibly patient. "Not mine. Yours. Name it. Every carrier of the first flame may ask one thing of the fire, and the fire will honor it, because the fire is honest and expects honesty in return."

Edmund thought of the atlas buried in his memory. Thought of the gods waiting behind their doors. Thought of Maren's offer, still ringing in him like a struck bell—*restoration, belonging, everything you lost.* Thought of Veyra's torn map page, and the Council's purges, and the curfew bells he could almost hear beginning to ring above.

Thought of Sela, watching him with her whole heart in her face.

"When it's done," Edmund said, "when the last seal is open and the gods are free—let me remember why I did it. Not the god-realm. Not the maps. Just—" his voice caught— "just the reason. Let me keep the reason."

The first flame drifted closer, warm as a hearth, gentle as a held breath.

"Asking to remember," Emberveil murmured, "instead of asking to forget. Fifteen years, cartographer. You did learn something after all."

And that was when the stone overhead shuddered—from far above, from the world of salt and lamplight, came a sound Edmund knew in his bones though he had never heard it before: the great bell of the Salt Court, rung not for the hour but for judgment. Three strokes, a pause, three more. The toll reserved, by ancient law, for one announcement only.

An execution.

Sela was already running for the passage, the scale blazing in her fist like a falling star, and Edmund stood alone in the dragon's gathering light with the flame waiting before him and the bell tolling above, and understood that the choice he had just made was no longer the only one the day would demand.
