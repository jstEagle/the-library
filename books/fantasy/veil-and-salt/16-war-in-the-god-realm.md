## 16. War in the God-Realm

The rupture did not announce itself with thunder. It announced itself with silence—the particular silence of a held breath, of a city's ten thousand hearths guttering at once, of Sela's voice cut off mid-word as she reached for Edmund's scarred hand in the undercity dark.

Then the world turned inside out.

He had felt it before, once, fifteen years ago: the sensation of being *unmapped*, of every coordinate he occupied dissolving like sugar in rain. But then he had crossed willingly, carrying his atlas and his arrogance. Now he was torn through, dragged backward through a door that should not have opened for another three days, and he came out screaming into a country made of weather.

The god-realm.

It was worse than he remembered. It was better than he feared. Both things were true, and truth here had teeth.

Edmund knelt on nothing—a plain of light that rippled like water struck by wind—and looked up into a sky that was not a sky but an argument. Storms moved there with intention. Clouds coiled and uncoiled in shapes that hurt to follow, because they were not shapes but *opinions*, vast and colliding, and every collision shook the ground beneath him even though the ground was only a courtesy the realm extended to visitors.

*The seals have broken,* he thought, and then corrected himself. *No. The seals are breaking. I am inside the breaking.*

He could feel them still—shreds of the old bindings hanging in the air like torn fishing nets, each strand a line from his own atlas, inked in Emberveil's blood and his own certainty. They flared and failed around him, one after another, each collapse releasing something that had been folded small for fifteen years.

Something unfolded above him now. He felt it the way a man feels a mountain lean over him.

"Cartographer."

The voice came from everywhere, which meant it came from Maren, or from what Maren was when she was not wearing a drowned fisherman. The tide-goddess uncoiled out of the storm-light, and she was not the patient woman-shape who had stood at his door offering divinity. She was the thing beneath that shape: a mile of moving water given hunger and memory, her eyes two abysses, her voice the sound of a harbor drowning.

"You are early," she said. "We are all early. Your dragon is dying faster than you promised."

"I know." His voice sounded absurdly small. He was grateful, distantly, that it did not shake. "Emberveil gave me the flame. I was going to do this gently. There was a way to—"

"Gently." The word rolled through the plain of light, and somewhere far off, other voices stirred at it, waking. "Fifteen years, cartographer. Fifteen years we were pressed flat as flowers in your book, and you speak to us of *gentle*."

"I didn't press you. I hid you. There's a difference."

"Is there?" And now other shapes were rising out of the storm-argument, drawn by the scent of grievance. He recognized some of them, because he had mapped them—he had *named* them, that was the wound of it, he had written their true names in his own hand and those names had become their cages. A figure of ash and antlers. A woman whose body was a field of wheat burning in slow motion. Something with too many wings that might once have been called merciful. "Tell us the difference, Edmund Coldharbour. We have had fifteen years of silence to consider the question."

* * *

He ran.

Not because running would save him—nothing here could harm him, probably, though *probably* was a thin rope to hang from—but because running was how he thought. Fifteen years of walking Greyhaven's streets at night, letting his feet find the pattern while his mind worked. So he ran across the plain of light, and the god-realm unspooled ahead of him according to its old grammar, and that was the only mercy he found: the realm still obeyed the map. His map. Even unraveling, even at war with itself, it remembered the lines he had drawn.

That was the secret he carried, the one thing no god could take from him: he was the only living being who knew this place. Not as scripture, not as fear, not as longing. As *geography*. He knew where the river of first-light bent around the mountain of unanswered vows. He knew the ford across the Sea of Small Prayers where the water ran shallow enough to walk. He knew which storms were borders and which were merely moods.

And so when the ash-and-antler god sent a hunting wind shrieking after him, Edmund did not flee it. He read it. He saw the way it curled leftward, always leftward, circling the place where the Vow-Mountain's shadow fell, and he stepped sideways into that shadow, and the wind passed over him like a hand over a page, finding nothing written there.

Old trick. Cartographer's trick. You cannot hide from gods in their own realm—unless you know the places where the realm forgets to look.

He climbed. The Vow-Mountain rose out of the light-plain like a throat of black glass, and its slopes were studded with the prayers of centuries, each one a small cold star embedded in the rock. He had mapped this mountain in another life. He had sat on its shoulder for what felt like a year, sketching, while Emberveil's warmth pulsed against his back and the dragon asked him questions he had never answered honestly.

*What is it for, little mapper? The map. What is it for?*

He had said: to keep them safe.

He had meant: to keep myself safe.

The summit, when he reached it, gave him the whole realm at once, and he wished it hadn't.

The god-realm was tearing along the seams of his atlas. He could see it plainly from up here, the cartographer's curse of seeing everything whole: great wounds in the fabric of the place where his ink had been scraped away or overwritten, and through the wounds bled raw possibility, unformed, screaming. Rivers ran uphill toward arguments. Constellations had come loose and drifted like lanterns in a flood. And everywhere, everywhere, the gods were gathering—not as a host, not as an army, but as a mob. A congregation of the silenced, and mobs and congregations are separated only by what happens next.

Below him, at the foot of the mountain, they had begun to speak among themselves. The sound carried the way grief carries, too large to be contained by distance.

* * *

"—fifteen years is nothing," the wheat-burning woman was saying, and her voice was harvest and arson together. "I have slept longer between empires. Let them keep their little city. Let them forget us again. Forgetting is only a long sleep, and I am *tired*, sisters, I am tired of being needed—"

"Needed?" That voice was new to him—or old, older, a voice he had heard only secondhand through Emberveil's stories. It came from a shape at the edge of the gathering, a figure of gray stone and patient erosion. "We were not needed. We were *used*. Do not confuse the two. This Coldharbour drew our names into his book and sold the book's shadow to mortal men, and mortal men built cages from it, and you call that being needed?"

"They built cages from his map," said the many-winged one, and its voice was almost gentle, which was worse. "But he built the map from love. I remember. I was watching when he walked our roads with his ink-stained fingers and his hungry eyes. He loved us the way mortals love—possessively, ignorantly, completely. Is that a crime? It is only mortality, worn as ambition."

"And the cages?"

"The cages were men's work. Men will always cage what they fear. The question is not what he did." The winged shape turned, slowly, and Edmund felt its attention pass over the mountainside like a searchlight, and he pressed himself flat against the black glass and stopped breathing, though breathing was optional here and hiding was not. "The question is what we do. There is a city down there, under the salt. A million small lives, praying again, badly, hopefully. Do they deserve us?"

Silence. Even the storms leaned in to hear.

"They deserve what they have earned," said the stone-and-erosion god. "Which is nothing. Which is ash. They outlawed wonder. They burned the shrines. They let an Inquisition grind prayer into powder and sell it as safety. Let them have their safety. Let them choke on it."

"Maren thinks otherwise."

"Maren is sentimental. Maren has been walking their streets in borrowed corpses and calling it wisdom."

"Maren has been *listening*. Which is more than any of you have done since the seals closed."

Edmund lay against the cold glass of the Vow-Mountain and listened too, and the loneliness of it broke over him like the tide he had once named. This was the center of everything. Every prayer ever prayed passed through this place; every name he had ever written echoed in these arguments; and he was the only living soul present, the sole mortal witness to the war council of heaven, and none of them knew he was here, and if they knew, most of them would want him dead, and the ones who wouldn't want him dead wanted something worse—they wanted him *useful*.

Fifteen years invisible in Greyhaven, and he had told himself that invisibility was the price of survival. He had never admitted it was also the punishment. Now, lying unseen at the roof of the god-realm while divinities debated whether humanity deserved to hope, he understood that he had spent his whole immortality practicing for this exact moment: alone at the center of everything, holding knowledge no one else had, unable to share it without dying.

Except.

Except that dying was already on the table. Emberveil's flame sat banked in his chest like a coal wrapped in cloth—he could feel it, warm and finite, one fire, one use. He had planned to spend it on the atlas, gently, in the undercity, with Sela's hand in his and the seals loosening like knots soaked in oil.

The seals had not waited. The rupture had come early, ragged, violent, and now the choice was different: the flame could still unmake the atlas, but it would happen *here*, in front of all of them, in the heart of the argument. Or he could climb back down, find the thin places, slip home to Greyhaven, and let the gods tear free on their own schedule—which was to say, at war, in storm-form, over a city full of people who had only just remembered how to pray.

Sela was down there. Veyra was down there. The dockworker with his luck-knot and his answered prayer was down there, and the child he had refused too harshly, and ten thousand strangers who had never done anything to the gods except be afraid of them.

*Do they deserve us?*

Edmund realized his hands were shaking, and made them stop, because a cartographer's hands must be steady, and got out the tools of his old trade from the pockets of a coat that had crossed between worlds with him because the coat, at least, was loyal.

Ink. Pen. The last clean page of his memory, which was the only paper the god-realm could not burn.

If he could not stop the argument, he could redraw the room it happened in.

* * *

There was a place he remembered. Of course there was; he remembered everything, that was the bargain's cruel gift, perfect recall as payment for perfect isolation. On the far side of the Sea of Small Prayers there had been a hall—not a building, nothing so humble, but a *confluence*, a place where the realm's arguments had once been spoken aloud instead of hurled as weather. The gods had used it in the old days, before mortals, before maps. A floor of settled light. Acoustics made of honesty.

The atlas had recorded it. Therefore the realm still honored it. Therefore, if he could reach it and speak its name—

Its name. His own handwriting. The last door he held a key to.

Edmund went down the Vow-Mountain's blind side, where the prayers embedded in the glass could not see him, and crossed the plain of light at a dead run while the storm-argument raged behind him, and the hunting wind came back twice and lost him twice among the shadows of borders he himself had surveyed. Once, passing the shore of the Sea of Small Prayers, he saw his own reflection looking up at him from water that held no reflections—young Edmund, twenty-six years old, ink to the elbows, eyes bright with the sin of ambition—and the reflection smiled at him, and he did not smile back, and he kept running.

The confluence was exactly where he had left it, because nothing in the god-realm moved except by will or wound, and no one had willed this forgotten hall anywhere in fifteen years. A floor of pale gold light, ringed by pillars of quiet. Above it, the storms of the gathering gods churned like a sky deciding whether to end the world.

Edmund stood in the center of the floor, opened his memory to the page he needed, and spoke the hall's true name aloud.

The name was seven syllables long and tasted like the moment before dawn. When he finished speaking it, the confluence woke, and the acoustics of honesty seized the entire realm the way a bell seizes a room: every voice, every storm, every grievance in the god-realm was suddenly, forcibly, *audible*—not louder, but clearer, stripped of thunder, reduced to meaning.

The effect on the gathering was immediate and enormous. The gods had been shouting in weather for hours. Now they heard themselves thinking.

"—let them choke—" became, in the honest air, *I am afraid they will not need me.*

"—tired of being needed—" became *I am afraid of being wanted only for what I can burn.*

"—men will always cage what they fear—" became *I do not know how to be free and loved at the same time, and I never learned, because the last time I tried, they built the cages.*

The storms slowed. The mob became a congregation again. And in the ringing silence of the confluence, Edmund Coldharbour stood alone on a floor of light with a pen made of memory in his hand, having bought his city perhaps an hour, perhaps a day, with nothing but a word he had stolen from heaven fifteen years ago.

They would notice him soon. The acoustics worked both ways; the hall amplified whatever stood upon it, and he was standing on it, and he was the only mortal heartbeat in a realm of eternity. Already the stone-and-erosion god had gone still at the edge of the gathering, head tilting, listening for the small warm noise that had no business existing here.

Already the many-winged one was turning, and its face, when Edmund finally saw it, was the face of someone recognizing an old debt.

"Cartographer," it said, and the honest air carried the word to every corner of heaven. "Come down and be counted. The vote is beginning—and you are the only one among us who has ever lived in the country we are deciding about."

Far below, through the thinning veil between worlds, Edmund felt Greyhaven like a bruise: the Salt Court in uproar, the tide climbing the harbor stairs, Sela's fear flickering like a candle in wind, and Veyra's ledger-code burning its single line into the dark. One hour. Perhaps one day.

He put the pen away. He stepped forward to the edge of the light, where all of heaven could see him.

"My name is Edmund Coldharbour," he said, "and I have come to testify against myself."
