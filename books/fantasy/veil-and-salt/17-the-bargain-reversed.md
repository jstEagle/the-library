## 17. The Bargain Reversed

The god-realm did not storm around Edmund so much as *remember* him.

He stood on a road that had no business existing—a cartographer's road, ink-dark and ruled with faint gridlines, running through a country made of weather. To his left, a mountain range exhaled thunder and folded itself away like laundry. To his right, an ocean hung in the sky, upside down, its waves breaking against nothing and falling upward into rain. Somewhere behind him, the seals he had helped draw were tearing loose one by one, and each rupture rang through this place like a bell struck inside his skull.

But the road was calm. The road was *his*.

He knew it the way a man knows his own handwriting. Every contour of it, every deliberate curve where the path bent to avoid a grief he had once refused to cross. He had drawn this road thirty years ago, kneeling in Emberveil's smoldering hollow with a quill cut from his own fingerbone, because ordinary ink would not hold in a place where names were load-bearing.

And at the end of the road, waiting with the patience of paper, was himself.

Not a reflection. Not a ghost. A young man of perhaps five-and-twenty, standing in the posture Edmund remembered from mirrors he no longer owned—weight on the left foot, chin lifted, that insufferable certainty that comes from never having lost anything yet. The young man wore the traveling coat Edmund had burned fifteen years ago. His hands were stained to the wrist with silver ink.

"You're late," the young man said. "I've been holding the door."

"You're not real," Edmund said. "You're the map."

"I'm what you put in the map." The young man spread his arms, and behind him the horizon unrolled like a scroll, revealing the whole of the god-realm rendered in Edmund's own hand: the nine courts of the pantheon, the tide-halls of Maren, the ash-gardens where Emberveil had been young, the thousand small shrines of forgotten divinities whose names existed nowhere else but here, in these lines, in this memory given permanence against his will. "Every place you ever charted. Every name you ever copied down to keep it safe. You didn't just map the god-realm, Edmund. You became its only door. And doors get bargained with."

Edmund walked toward himself. It was the strangest journey of his life, approaching his own face and watching it age in reverse as the distance closed—as if the space between them were all the years he had spent hiding.

"The bargain," Edmund said. "Tell me exactly what I agreed to. I've spent fifteen years not remembering, and I need—" His voice caught. "I need to know if I chose this or if I was tricked."

"You were never tricked," the young man said gently. "That's the worst of it. You were *thorough*."

* * *

They sat, absurdly, at a table the young man conjured from a page—the same table, Edmund realized, where the bargain had been struck. Emberveil's presence filled the memory around them: vast, dim, the heat of her coming off her body like a dying hearth. In the recollection she was already wounded, her left wing a ruin, her breath short. The Edict's first armies had found her before they found anything else. Dragons could not be outlawed; they could only be killed slowly.

"Say it again," the young Edmund said to the dark, and the darkness answered in a voice like coals settling.

*"You will carry my name and the names of my kin out of the reach of ash and iron. You will hide them so well that even the gods cannot find themselves. And when the last seal is drawn, you will forget that you drew it."*

"And in exchange?"

*"In exchange, you will not die. Not by age, not by blade, not by grief. You will walk out of this burning world and keep walking until the world is ready for you to stop."*

The young Edmund at the table had laughed—one short, disbelieving sound. "Immortality. For a library job."

*"For a prison job,"* Emberveil had corrected. *"There is a difference, and you will feel it every day."*

Present-day Edmund watched his younger self sign. Watched him press his fingerbone-quill to the atlas and write the sealing words in a script that looked, even now, like beautiful weather about to turn. Watched him believe—he could see it in the set of the shoulders—that he was saving the gods by hiding them, that silence was a kind of shelter, that the Edict's fires would burn out and someday he would simply open the atlas again and let everyone out, forgiven and intact.

"Fifteen years," Edmund said aloud, to the memory, to himself. "Fifteen years and I never opened it."

"You couldn't," the young man beside him said. "Forgetting was part of the seal. The gods couldn't find their names because *you* couldn't remember where you'd put them. That was the lock's true teeth, Edmund. Not magic. Amnesia. You hid the key inside a man who would never know he was carrying it." He smiled, and it was a terrible smile, full of everything the young Edmund had not yet suffered. "It worked beautifully. Until I started dying."

"What?"

"Emberveil." The young man's face flickered—just for a moment, just at the edges, like a candle guttering—and Edmund saw beneath it: parchment. Lines. The faint gray ghost of coastlines moving under skin. "Her fire is what keeps the seals supple. Her memory is the ink's fixative. She has been dying for years, and now she is nearly done, and when she goes, every name I hold tears free at once. Nine courts' worth of divinity, released unbound, into a city full of people who have spent fifteen years learning to fear them." He looked up, and his eyes were Edmund's eyes, older than his face. "Maren wants her tide back. The ash-gods want their gardens. Some of them want apologies. Some want more than apologies. Unless someone chooses how they come back, they will choose for themselves, and Greyhaven will be the anvil."

"So I unmake the atlas." Edmund said it flatly, because he had known since Emberveil asked her question, since the flame was offered, that it would come to this. "I burn it. The seals open gently. The gods return bound by gratitude instead of chains."

"Yes. And."

"And I die."

"Worse." The young man leaned forward. "You burn. That's the price nobody wrote down because *I* didn't understand it when I signed. The atlas isn't a book, Edmund. It's your memory of the god-realm, externalized. Every perfect thing you've ever seen is in those pages. Burn it, and you don't lose the realm—you lose the *resolution*. Everything softens. Faces blur. You'll keep the shape of your life and lose the texture of it." He paused, and for the first time the young man's voice wavered, and it sounded suddenly very much like an old man's. "Sela's face. Veyra's voice saying your name. The weight of Sela asleep against your shoulder in the cellar shrine while the congregation sang. The exact blue of Maren's drowned eyes. You'll know you loved them. You won't remember loving them. You'll have to do it all again from scratch, like a stranger reading letters about someone else's family."

The road around them went quiet. Even the rupturing seals seemed to hold their ringing.

"That's not death," Edmund said slowly. "Death would be easier."

"No," the young man agreed. "This is worse than death. This is *grief*, chosen in advance, with your eyes open. Which is why almost no one ever does it. Which is why it might actually work." He stood, and held out his hand—the silver-stained hand, the hand that had signed. "The bargain reversed. You free them; you pay me. Or rather, I pay you. We've always been the same debt, you and I."

Edmund did not take the hand. Not yet.

* * *

Instead, he walked.

The young man let him. The god-realm unrolled around Edmund in all its terrible beauty, and he made himself look at everything, because looking was the last time. He walked the shore of Maren's tide-hall, where the water sang in chords and the drowned danced on the seafloor with their hair drifting like banners. He climbed the ash-gardens and touched a tree that grew embers for fruit. He passed a shrine no taller than a dog, built by some mortal dead three thousand years for a god of thresholds and small kindnesses, and he knelt there a long moment, because that god had no court, no cult, no power worth stealing—only a name, and the name deserved to survive.

*This is what I was,* he thought. *A keeper of names too small to matter and too precious to lose.*

He thought about Sela. He made himself think about her properly—not the idea of her but the specifics: the salt-cracked skin of her hands, the way she said *really works* as though wonder were a currency she'd been cheated of, the ferocious tenderness with which she'd carried Emberveil's scale like a sleeping bird. If he burned the atlas, he would keep none of it. He would meet her tomorrow and know only that this fierce girl mattered to him, without knowing why, and he would have to earn the answer all over again.

Perhaps that was fitting. Perhaps love that must be re-earned daily was the only honest kind. He had spent fifteen years hoarding his memories like charms in a locked drawer, safe and useless, and it had kept nothing warm.

He thought about Veyra, who had burned her ledger and crossed a burning city to stand beside a man she'd spent weeks suspecting. He thought about the dockworker with the luck-knot. He thought about Emberveil, guttering beneath the streets, who had traded her last flame for a question she trusted him to answer honestly.

*What was the map for?*

"To free them," he said aloud, to the empty shore, to the young man waiting at the end of the road. "It was always supposed to be to free them. I just didn't have the courage to mean it until now."

When he returned, the young man was waiting exactly where he'd been, and something in his expression had changed—softened, the certainty cracked open into relief, the way a sentry's face changes when the watch finally ends.

"Well?" the young man asked.

"Burn it," Edmund said. "All of it. Don't spare me a single page."

The young man nodded once, formally, like a colleague closing a contract. Then, quieter: "There's one thing I can give you. One thing the fire can't touch, because it was never written down—it was lived. Do you want it?"

"What?"

"The reason you signed. Not the bargain's terms. The moment before. When Emberveil asked why a mortal cartographer would risk everything for gods he'd never met." The young man's eyes shone. "You told her: *Because someone should remember them kindly.* That's not in the atlas, Edmund. That's in you. Fire doesn't reach it. Whatever else burns, that stays."

Edmund closed his eyes. *Someone should remember them kindly.* Yes. That was the boy he had been, before the bargain calcified into duty and duty calcified into hiding. That sentence was the seed the whole atlas had grown from, and no flame could kill a seed already planted in living ground.

"Then let's finish it," he said.

* * *

They walked together to the center of the map—to the place that was also, somehow, the center of Edmund, where the atlas lay open on a lectern of dragon-bone, its pages stirring in a wind that came from nowhere because everywhere here was wind. Beyond it, visible through the unraveling sky, Emberveil's final flame waited in his cupped hands, small and white and patient, hotter than the sun that had never risen over this country.

The young man stood beside him. Their reflections overlapped in the atlas's gilded edges—one face, two ages, the same debt.

"For what it's worth," the young man said, "I'm sorry. I made you live fifteen years invisible to buy the gods time, and then I made you watch them start to come back while knowing what it would cost you to finish it."

"We're the same person," Edmund said. "Apologize to yourself."

"I have." The young man smiled, sad and young and finally at peace. "For fifteen years. Every night. You thought that ache in your chest was guilt. It was me, saying goodnight."

Edmund lifted the flame.

The atlas did not resist. That was the strangest mercy of it—it opened toward the fire like a flower toward light, as though it had been waiting all along to be finished rather than preserved. The first page caught, and the fire ran through the god-realm like dawn through a valley: the nine courts brightening, the tide-halls filling, the little shrine-god of thresholds stepping out of his lines and stretching, astonished, into being. Names tore free—but gently now, gently, each one loosening like a knot untied by a friend rather than ripped by a hook.

And with every page, Edmund felt himself simplify. The perfect memory of Maren's tide-song blurred to a rumor of music. The precise geography of Sela's face softened into warmth without features. He gripped the lectern and kept burning pages anyway, feeding the flame, weeping without noticing he wept, grieving in advance and choosing it, choosing it, choosing it—

The last page was blank. Of course it was. It had always been blank. It was the page where the next map would go, the one he would never draw, the one Sela might.

He set the flame to it anyway.

Light took everything. The young man was smiling as he came apart into ink and morning. Somewhere impossibly far below, a dragon sighed out her last breath as fire, and the fire did not die but scattered—into hearths, into lamps, into the salt-lamps of Greyhaven, which bloomed alight all at once across the waking city like a constellation answering a question.

And Edmund Coldharbour fell out of the god-realm, mortal, lighter, emptied of everything except one sentence he could no longer source and would never lose:

*Someone should remember them kindly.*

He hit the stone floor of the undercity on his knees, in the dark, beside a dying dragon's cooling ashes. Above him, muffled through miles of rock, the bells of Greyhaven began to ring—and not the alarm bells. The old bells, the ones that hadn't sounded since the Edict, ringing the hours of a day that belonged to everyone again.

Then the dark around him shifted, and a voice he knew better than any scripture said, wet and vast and gentle:

"You're late, cartographer. I've been holding the door."
