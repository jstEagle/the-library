## 18. The Unmapping

The god-realm was coming apart around him like a book dropped in water.

Edmund stood on a road that had been the Silver Causeway when he drew it, forty years and one lifetime ago—a road of hammered light that once ran straight as a vow from the Gate of Morning to the Court of Tides. Now it buckled beneath his feet in slow waves, its surface rippling like the skin of a struck bell. Above him, where the sky should have held the slow procession of the Hours, there was only storm: clouds of unspent prayer, purple-black and gravid, turning on axes that no longer agreed with one another.

He could feel every seal failing at once. Not as sound—there was no sound here, not really, only pressure and meaning—but as a great loosening, like fifteen years of held breath finally leaving a body. Each name he had hidden in his cartography was tearing free of its page, and each tearing left a wound in the world's grammar.

And in the center of it all, coiled around the roots of Greyhaven itself, Emberveil was dying.

Edmund walked toward her. There was no other direction anymore. The god-realm had contracted until she was its whole geography—her body the mountains, her breath the weather, her heartbeat the drumbeat under everything. He crossed a plain that had been the Field of Small Kindnesses and found it strewn with the shed scales of her youth, each one the size of a barn door, each one still faintly warm. He crossed a river whose waters were made of forgotten lullabies, and they parted for him without being asked, because everything here still remembered what he had been.

That was the cruelest part. The god-realm remembered him perfectly. It was only the mortal world that had learned to forget.

* * *

Emberveil lay at the center, and she was small now.

That was what none of the old stories said about dragons—that at the end, they fold inward. She had been vast enough once to circle the foundations of a city and have room left over for grief. Now she was scarcely larger than the chapel where Sela had first gathered her frightened congregation, her hide gone the grey of banked coals, her wings folded tight against a body that had become mostly hollow space. Her eye, when she opened it, was still the same: molten gold shot through with veins of green, an eye that had watched the first temples rise and the last ones fall.

*Cartographer,* she said. Her voice did not come through the air. It came up through him, through the soles of his feet, through the marrow the way an earthquake comes. *You are late.*

"I stopped believing I was allowed to be early," Edmund said. "It seemed safer."

*Everything you did seemed safer.* A plume of smoke escaped her nostrils, grey and thin, more sigh than flame. *That was always your gift. You could make cowardice look like prudence, even to yourself.*

He knelt beside her head. This close, he could see the truth of her dying—not decay but dispersal, her edges going soft, her outline losing its argument with the air. She was becoming weather. She was becoming the thing she had spent fifteen years holding shut.

"The seals are breaking," he said. "All of them. When you go—"

*Every name tears free at once. Yes. I told you.* Her eye turned to fix on him, and in it he saw reflected a tiny, perfect image of himself kneeling, and behind that image, faint as watermark, the ghost of another Edmund—younger, arrogant, standing in this same realm with ink-stained fingers and a bargain already forming in his mouth. *I have held them gently for fifteen years. Gently is over. What comes next will not be gentle unless someone makes it so.*

"That's why I'm here." He reached into his coat and drew out the atlas.

Even here, in the realm it had been made to describe, the book looked wrong—too plain, too mortal, a battered folio bound in salt-stained leather with brass corners worn to brown. But the god-realm flinched from it. He felt the flinch run through the ground, through the storm overhead, through Emberveil herself. Fifteen years ago, the Council's founders had taken his life's work and used it as a cage. Every god's true name, written into the margins of a map; every map, folded into a book; every book, sealed under oath and ash. They had called it the Edict of Ash, as if the burning had been the point. It hadn't. The burning was theater. The binding was the book.

"You gave me a flame," Edmund said. "One true flame. Enough to unmake the seals gently—if someone carried it knowing they would burn."

*I did.*

"I've thought about what burns." He turned the atlas over in his hands. "My immortality. My memory. Everything I am that isn't—" He stopped. Started again. "Everything I am that I hid behind."

*Not everything,* Emberveil said. *The scar will stay. Scars are how flesh remembers what the mind forgives itself for forgetting.*

"Then let it stay." He looked up at her, and found that his eyes were wet, which surprised him, because he had not wept in fifteen years and had assumed the capacity had been traded away with everything else. "Emberveil. Before I do this. Tell me what happens to you."

The great eye closed, then opened again, slower. *I pass into the hearths,* she said. *Into every fire lit in Greyhaven tonight and every fire lit after. I will be the warmth in a baker's oven and the ember in a child's brazier and the candle a widow lights because the dark has gotten too honest. Dragons do not end, cartographer. We change jobs.* Something moved at the corner of her mouth—if dragons can be said to smile, it was that. *You taught me that joke. Forty years ago. You were funnier before you learned to be careful.*

"I know," Edmund said. "I miss him too."

*Then burn him,* said Emberveil. *Burn all of him. And give the rest back.*

* * *

He opened the atlas across her foreleg, across scales gone cool as river stones.

The pages fell open to the map of the god-realm itself—the master map, the one from which all others derived—and for a moment he simply looked at it. His own hand, younger and surer, had drawn the Silver Causeway in silver ink that still gleamed. The Court of Tides sat at the bottom margin rendered as a spiral shell, and inside the shell, in letters so small they were nearly prayer, was written: *Maren, who is the tide's long patience, whose name is a door and the sea beyond it.*

Fifteen years ago, men in grey coats had copied these names into their seals and locked the doors and thrown away the keys—or believed they had. But a map is not a cage. A map is a promise that a place exists. And every promise, Edmund understood now, kneels eventually to its maker.

"I'm sorry," he said—to the book, or to the boy who'd made it, or to the gods whose names he had lent to their jailers. Perhaps all three. Perhaps apology was just another kind of cartography: drawing the shape of a wrong so it could be found again.

He cupped his hands together, and between them, Emberveil set down her last flame.

It was very small. That was the first thing. After all the terror of it, after the dragon's warning and the scale's hot insistence and the long descent through the undercity—it was the size of a moth, a curl of gold-white light that weighed nothing and warmed everything. It did not flicker. It simply was, with the terrible simplicity of things that are entirely themselves.

*Carry it knowing,* Emberveil whispered. Her voice was thinner now, stretched like the last note of a bell. *Knowing is the fuel. Knowing is the whole sacrifice. Anyone can burn a book. Only you can burn what the book holds.*

"I know," Edmund said, and meant all of it: the centuries he would never have, the faces he would lose—the dockworker's gratitude, Veyra's wary respect, Sela's fierce young certainty, and stranger, older loves, a woman in the god-realm who had poured him tea made of dawn, a student whose name he had already begun to surrender without noticing. He knew. He lifted the flame anyway.

It did not hurt the way fire hurts.

It hurt the way truth hurts—cleanly, totally, without any possibility of pretending otherwise. The flame touched the first page and the page did not char; it *remembered*, and then it forgot, and the forgetting spread outward like frost in reverse. The Silver Causeway dissolved into ordinary white paper. The Court of Tides became blank vellum. Name by name, door by door, the atlas emptied itself—not destroyed but *released*, each true name lifting off the page like heat-shimmer and rising into the storm above, where the clouds of unspent prayer began, at last, to open.

And Edmund burned with it. He felt his own edges go soft. The perfect memory that had been his curse and his armor—the face of every person he'd ever failed, filed and cross-referenced and kept forever—began to blur at the borders, gentling, going soft the way a photograph does in a damp room. He clung to nothing. That was the discipline of it, the real burning: to hold still while yourself departed.

Somewhere far below, in the mortal city, fires leapt up in ten thousand hearths at once. Bakers gasped as their ovens roared to life unlit. Candles that had guttered stood upright and blazed. In the Salt Court, in the cellar shrines, in the hovels of the lower wards where the floodwater still stood ankle-deep and cold—warmth, everywhere warmth, a city suddenly full of the sense of being tended.

Emberveil sighed. It was the longest sigh in the history of the world, and it smelled of cedar smoke and rain on hot stone.

*Oh,* she said. *Oh, that's better.*

And she came apart the way a constellation comes apart—not into ruin but into arrangement, her body loosening into light, into heat, into the slow gold sediment of embers, pouring downward through the roots of the city, into every grate and chimney and kitchen range, until the last of her was everywhere at once and nowhere in particular, and the god-realm, unheld by anything now except its own returning divinities, shuddered once—

—and settled.

* * *

The storm broke upward.

That was how it looked from inside: the clouds of unspent prayer parting like curtains, and through them, descending, the gods. Not the furious storm-wrack presences Edmund had navigated during the rupture—those had been gods mid-birth, half-remembered, screaming themselves into shape. These were something else. These arrived the way morning arrives: gradually, then completely, then as if they had never been away.

The Hours resumed their procession. The Field of Small Kindnesses greened over. Somewhere a river of lullabies found its banks again. And walking down the mended Silver Causeway, unhurried, barefoot on hammered light, came Maren.

She wore the drowned fisherman's face still, but transfigured—no longer borrowed but chosen, the way a coat becomes yours after enough winters. Her hair moved as if underwater though there was no water. When she reached Edmund, she stopped, and looked at the empty atlas in his scorched hands, and at the man holding it, who was no longer quite the man who had picked it up.

"You gave them back," she said. Her voice was the voice of the harbor at slack tide, patient, immense, kind. "Not freed like prisoners released. Given back, like something returned to its owner. Do you understand the difference?"

"I'm starting to," Edmund said.

"Good." She extended her hand, palm up, and in it rested a small blue flame that was also a wave that was also a key. "Because I owe you a debt, cartographer, and my kind pays debts in the only coin we have. Your divinity. It was never destroyed—only shelved, the way you shelve everything you cannot bear to look at. I can restore it. All of it. You would walk out of this place knowing every name again, seeing every current of prayer, belonging to the realm you mapped. You would never be lonely again. Never be *small* again."

And oh, it was tender, the offer. That was the trap of it, if trap it was—that she meant it, wholly, without cruelty. He could feel the truth of it radiating off her like heat off summer stone. He would never be lonely. He would stand at the center of everything, and everything would know his name.

He thought of fifteen years of the night market. Of selling luck-knots that worked only because people needed them to. Of eating alone above his shop while the city sang songs about gods it wasn't allowed to name. Of Sela's face when the tide answered her—not power, not glory, just the unbearable relief of being heard.

"No," Edmund said.

Maren tilted her head. Around her, the other gods had gathered at a respectful distance—shapes of light and weather and animal attention, watching the first mortal refusal in living memory the way sailors watch a rare bird.

"Explain," she said, and it was not a command. It was curiosity, genuine and vast.

"Because I'd hoard it," he said. "I know myself. I spent forty years learning that knowledge kept safe turns to poison, and I only unlearned it by giving the last of mine away. If I took it back—" He laughed, short and wet. "I'd start mapping again. Drawing doors. Deciding who deserved to walk through them. I'd become the Edict with better manners."

"And if we bound you?" Maren asked softly. "If we held you to your better self, as you held us?"

"Then I'd be a pet," Edmund said. "And you'd be jailers again. We've both done our time in that story." He closed the empty atlas. It weighed almost nothing now, which was fitting. "Let me be small. Let me forget. Let me wake up tomorrow in a city full of gods I'll have to take on faith, like everyone else. That's not a punishment, Maren. That's the whole point of you."

For a long moment the tide-goddess said nothing. Then she closed her hand around the blue flame, and it went out—or rather, went home—and she smiled, and it was the smile of the sea after a storm, which is to say: exhausted, enormous, and glad.

"So be it," she said. "Bound by gratitude, then. Ours to you. May it weigh less than chains." She leaned close, and her breath smelled of salt and kelp and the deep cold places where old ships sleep, and she pressed her lips to his forehead like a mother marking a child for sleep. "Forget us, Edmund Coldharbour. Forget us *completely*. And live long enough to be surprised when we say hello."

The god-realm folded around him like a page being turned.

* * *

He woke on stone.

Real stone—undercity stone, slick with harbor water, smelling of salt and lamp oil and the particular iron tang of a city that had recently been through something. Above him, impossibly far above, Greyhaven's sky showed through a broken grating, and it was evening, and the evening was full of light: every window lit, every lantern burning high and gold, as if the whole city had decided, unanimously and without discussion, to leave its lamps on.

Sela was there. She was kneeling beside him with Emberveil's scale pressed to his chest, and her eyes were red-rimmed and furious with relief, and when he opened his mouth to speak she said, "Don't. Don't talk yet. Just—your name. Say your name. Everyone keeps asking and I want to hear you say it first."

"Edmund," he said. And then, testing it like a step onto ice: "Edmund Coldharbour."

Something in his chest ached when he said it—an ache like a scar remembering rain—but the name fit. It fit the way an old coat fits, worn smooth, missing buttons, warm anyway.

He tried to remember the god-realm and found only softness. A sense of having been somewhere vast. Gold light. A smell of cedar smoke. Someone pressing lips to his forehead. The rest was weather. The rest was gone, and the going of it felt less like loss than like setting down something heavy at the top of a staircase and hearing it roll away behind him, and being too tired, and too glad, to chase it.

"Is she—" he began.

"Gone," Sela said. "Or everywhere. People keep lighting candles and they won't go out. Veyra says the whole harbor ran warm as bathwater at sundown. The fish are coming back, Edmund. The *fish*." She laughed, and it cracked in the middle. "There are people painting names on the Custom House right now and the soldiers are helping them. Helping. I don't—none of us knows what happens tomorrow."

"Nobody ever does," Edmund said. "That's what makes it a life instead of a map."

He got one arm under himself and sat up, and the empty atlas slid from his lap to the stone—blank pages, ruined binding, worth exactly nothing to anyone in the world. Except that when Sela picked it up, carefully, the way you pick up a sleeping animal, she frowned.

"There's writing," she said slowly. "On the last page. It wasn't there before—I'd swear it wasn't—"

She turned the book toward him. And there, in ink that gleamed faintly silver, in a hand that was his and yet not his—younger, freer, the hand of the boy he had burned—one line remained, the single sentence the atlas had kept when everything else was given away:

*A map is a promise that a place exists. Go and see.*

Edmund read it twice. Then he looked up at the broken grating, at the lamplit city breathing above them, at the first stars coming out over a harbor that remembered its goddess—and somewhere far out past the sea-wall, deep enough that only the water heard it, something vast exhaled.

"Well," he said, and his voice was only a man's voice, mortal and slightly hoarse, and it had never sounded more like his own. "I suppose we'd better go and see."
