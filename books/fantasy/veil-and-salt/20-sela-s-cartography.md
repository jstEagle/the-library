## 20. Sela's Cartography

The shop on Lantern Row had been a charm-seller's stall for fifteen years, and it did not know how to be anything else. Edmund spent the first week of spring trying to teach it. He moved the luck-knots to a lower shelf, out of reach of impulse. He cleared the counter of the little glass vials of salt and ash that had once been his livelihood—harmless things, deniable things, each one a small act of hiding—and replaced them with paper, ink, compasses, and a single jar of Emberveil's ash, which he kept not for sale but because some part of him could not yet let the dragon go entirely.

The scarred hand ached in the damp mornings. It always would, Veyra's physicians said, though they said it with the particular reverence Greyhaven now reserved for wounds earned in service of something larger than oneself. Edmund flexed it as he worked, feeling the ridged skin pull across his knuckles like a second glove, and thought—as he thought every morning—that pain was a fair price for a world where gods walked openly and no one had to whisper.

The bell above the door rang at half past eight, which meant Sela was early again.

She came in smelling of harbor wind and lamp oil, her hair tied back with the red cord she still wore from the cellar shrine days, when belief had been a crime and she had carried a dragon's scale against her heart like contraband. Now the scale hung openly at her throat, polished by two years of touching, and the city's first licensed wonder-worker wore it the way other officials wore their seals of office.

"You're early," Edmund said.

"You're surprised," Sela said, hanging her coat on the peg. "Every morning. You're surprised every morning, and every morning I'm early. At what point does this become knowledge instead of surprise?"

He smiled despite himself. "When I stop being an old man set in his ways."

"You're not old."

"I am older than the city's oldest wall, in every way that matters."

She took her seat at the worktable—the one by the window, where the light came in clean off the harbor—and opened her portfolio. Two years now she had been coming to this table, first as a petitioner asking to learn, then as a student, and now as something Edmund had no better word for than *heir*, though neither of them said it aloud. The word sat between them sometimes, in the pauses, patient as tide.

"Show me yesterday's exercise," he said.

She laid out three sheets. On each, she had drawn the same thing: the lower wards, the flooded streets from the night Maren's name had been spoken over the harbor, the night everything changed. But each map rendered the flood differently. The first was pure cartography—streets, waterlines, depths, precise as a surveyor's dream. The second was pure testimony—handwritten accounts from survivors, arranged in the shape of the streets they'd stood in. The third was something else entirely, something Edmund had only begun to teach and could not have learned from anyone: the streets drawn as they had *felt*, grief and wonder marked in colors that existed on no official palette, the water rising highest where the most prayers had been spoken.

"Which is true?" Edmund asked. It was the question he asked every time, and it was never rhetorical.

Sela considered. She always considered; that was why she was good. "All of them," she said finally. "And none of them alone." She tapped the third sheet. "This one is closest to what happened. But it's useless if you need to rebuild a seawall." She tapped the first. "And this one is useless if you need to understand why people stood in the water singing instead of running."

Edmund looked at the maps for a long moment. Outside, a gull cried over the harbor, and somewhere in the city a temple bell rang the hour—not the Council's hour, but the new one, the one the returned gods had asked for, nine strokes for the nine who had come back gently, bound by gratitude rather than seals.

"When I was young," Edmund said, "I believed a map should tell you everything. That was the whole sickness of it. I mapped the god-realm down to its roots, every name, every door, every current of divine attention. I thought completeness was the same as truth." He turned his scarred hand palm-up on the table. "The Edict's founders stole my atlas and used it to build their seals. And I let them, because I told myself the knowledge was safer hoarded. Safer locked away. Safer in the hands of one careful man than scattered among careless ones."

"And now?"

"Now I think a map should tell you what it knows and admit what it doesn't. A map is a conversation, Sela. Not a cage."

* * *

They walked the lower wards that afternoon, as they did twice a week. This was the other half of the apprenticeship: the walking, the listening, the standing in places where something had happened until the place gave up its story.

The flood-streets had been rebuilt, but the city remembered. New stone laid over old foundations, and beneath both, the faint salt-line where the tide had reached—a stain the masons had given up trying to scrub, which the neighborhood had instead painted over in patterns of waves and fish and the long blue-green figure of Maren walking upright through shallow water with her skirts full of drowned sailors' names, giving them back.

A woman was chalking prayers on the wave-wall when they passed. Not begging—thanking. That was the change Edmund still caught himself marveling at. For fifteen years prayer in Greyhaven had been either criminal or desperate, and now a fishwife stood in afternoon light adding a line of gratitude to a wall already dense with it, unhurried, unafraid.

"She'll wear the wall down," Sela murmured, sketching.

"The wall will hold. Walls here are older than the Council." Edmund watched the woman finish, tuck her chalk away, and go. "What do you see?"

Sela had learned to take these questions seriously. She lowered her portfolio, studied the street, the wall, the light. "I see a neighborhood that decided the flood was a gift before the Council finished deciding it was a crime. I see—" she paused, tilting her head—"I see that the prayers cluster thickest near the drain-grate on Corver Street. Where the child almost drowned, during the flood. The one Maren pulled back."

"Yes."

"So if I were mapping belief, the center of this district isn't the shrine. It's a drain-grate."

Edmund felt the particular pride that had become, over two years, both familiar and dangerous—the pride of watching someone surpass the lesson you'd prepared. "That," he said, "is exactly the kind of thing I spent fifteen years refusing to write down."

"Why?"

"Because whoever held the map held the center of the district's faith. Because I had seen what was done with my atlas when it fell into ambitious hands." He touched the scarred hand absently. "Because hoarding felt like safety."

Sela was quiet a moment. Then, carefully: "You know it wasn't, though. Right? Hoarding didn't keep the founders from stealing your work. It just kept everyone else from knowing enough to stop them."

It was the oldest wound, and she had found it without looking, the way she found everything—by paying attention. Edmund looked out at the harbor, at the far horizon where, some evenings, something vast and warm-breathed passed along the edge of sight, and admitted what he had been admitting slowly for two years, in pieces, like a man learning to unclench a fist.

"No," he said. "It didn't."

* * *

In the third week of spring, the Licensing Assembly sent its inspector.

The office of licensed wonder-worker was Sela's own invention, argued into existence over eighteen months of hearings—Veyra's new order of witnesses had testified on her behalf, and Maren herself had appeared before the Assembly in a vessel of harbor-water and spoken four sentences that ended the debate. But inventions attract inspectors, and so a narrow man named Ostrell came to Lantern Row with a clipboard of regulations and a manner of profound suspicion, to determine whether Sela's methods were "sufficiently rigorous for public practice."

Edmund sat in the back of the shop, ostensibly doing inventory, actually listening, while Ostrell examined Sela's portfolio.

"This map," Ostrell said, holding up the third rendering of the flood—the one drawn in grief-colors. "How do you verify it?"

"You don't," Sela said. "You compare it. Against the survey, against the testimonies, against the god's own account if she consents to give one. It's not evidence. It's a lens."

"A lens that shows things no instrument can measure. How is that rigorous?"

"Because I show my work." Sela laid out all three sheets side by side. "Every map I make comes with its sources, its limits, and its failures. Here's where the testimony contradicts the survey. Here's where I don't know. Here's where the belief-data and the physical data can't be reconciled, and here's my best guess at why, clearly labeled as a guess." She met the inspector's eyes. "Master Coldharbour taught me that a map admits what it doesn't know. The Council's old surveys never did. They just left the blank spaces empty and pretended the emptiness was authority."

Ostrell wrote for a long time. When he left, he said only that the Assembly would respond in due course, and Edmund, watching from the shadows, recognized the posture—he had worn it himself for fifteen years, the careful neutrality of a man deciding whether you were safe.

"He'll approve it," Edmund said, after the door closed. "Grudgingly. In six weeks."

"How do you know?"

"Because grudging approval is how institutions learn to say thank you." He came forward and gathered her scattered sheets, squaring their edges. "Your defense was better than mine ever was. I never once showed my work. I showed finished things. Perfect things. Things designed to make questions unnecessary."

"That sounds lonely."

"It was." He handed her the portfolio. "Don't be perfect, Sela. Be honest. Perfection invites theft. Honesty invites company."

* * *

On the last evening of the month, they stayed late in the shop, and Sela asked the question she had been circling since winter.

"What happened to the atlas? The original. After you burned it."

Edmund was cleaning his compasses, an occupation that had replaced prayer in his mortal years and served much the same function. "Ash. Emberveil's flame doesn't leave residue—it leaves *absence*. There's nothing to find."

"But you remember making it."

"Some of it." He set down the compass. "The unmapping took the perfect memory too. That was the price. I remembered loving people; I lost the precision of them. My mother's face is a warm blur now. The god-realm is—" he searched for it—"a room I know I stood in, with the furniture gone soft. Some nights I grieve it. Most nights I don't. Precision was never the same as love. I just hadn't learned that yet when I made the bargain."

Sela turned her chair toward him fully. "Then teach me what's left. Not the god-realm—I don't need doors to places I can't responsibly open. Teach me the *method*. How you saw. How you drew. Because there are things in this city that need mapping and no one alive knows how anymore. The spaces where Maren touches the tide-line. The paths the dead take through the Memory Gardens. The shape of a congregation's courage on the night of a raid. Nobody maps those. Everybody pretends they aren't real."

"They are real," Edmund said quietly. "They were always real. That was never the question. The question was who gets to hold the knowing."

"Everyone," Sela said. "That's the whole point of me. Of the license. Of the shop." She gestured at the shelves, the cleared counter, the jar of dragon-ash catching lamplight. "You spent fifteen years being the only one who knew, and it nearly killed the city. So the answer isn't another hidden cartographer. It's a school."

Edmund looked at her—at the red cord, the scale, the ink stains on her fingers that mirrored his own—and felt the last knot of the old hoarder's fear loosen somewhere deep in his chest, not breaking, just slackening, the way a mooring line slacks when the tide finally turns.

"A school," he repeated.

"Small. Three students next year, maybe five. We start with the flood-maps. We walk the wards. We show our work." She smiled, and it was the smile of the girl from the cellar shrine, terrified and certain, translated into a woman with a license and a calling. "And we put your name on the door."

"My name is already on the ledger. Veyra read it aloud to the whole Assembly."

"On the *door*, Edmund. Not the record. The door. Master Coldharbour, Cartographer. Where children walking past can see it and know that this is a thing a person can be."

He turned to the window. Down on Lantern Row, the lamps were being lit one by one, and a pair of children chased each other past the shop, and above the rooftops the evening star stood clear over the harbor where, far out, something vast and patient breathed along the edge of the world.

"I'll want to approve the curriculum," he said, which was, in the grammar of Edmund Coldharbour, a yes.

* * *

He began the letter that night, after Sela had gone home to the harbor district—to the little house near the drain-grate on Corver Street, because of course she had chosen to live there—and the letter was to the Assembly, proposing the school, listing the texts, admitting freely and in writing every limit of the discipline he intended to teach.

Halfway through, he stopped.

There was a knock at the door. Three knocks, spaced evenly, in the old ledger code that Veyra had invented and burned and reinvented twice since founding the Witnesses. Edmund knew it the way he knew his own heartbeat.

But when he opened the door, Veyra was not standing there.

A boy was—young, ten perhaps, breathless, harbor-dressed, with salt drying white at his temples in a pattern Edmund recognized with a lurch he hadn't felt in two years. The mark of a message carried through Maren's own element. The goddess did not send errands lightly.

"Master Coldharbour?" the boy panted. "I'm supposed to say it exact. The Lady of the Tide says—" he screwed his eyes shut, reciting— "*The sea has found a page. Torn from an atlas. It should have burned, but fire cannot drown. Come and see what the water kept.*"

Edmund stood very still in his doorway, the scarred hand beginning, faintly, to ache.

Somewhere out in the dark harbor, under all that forgiving water, a piece of the unmapped map was waiting—and it had not burned after all.
