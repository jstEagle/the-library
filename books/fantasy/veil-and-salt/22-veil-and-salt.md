## 22. Veil and Salt

The stall had not moved in eleven years, but everything around it had.

Greyhaven's night market still smelled of brine and frying fish and the sweet smoke of chestnut braziers, but now the lanterns burned with a faint blue undertone on certain nights—Maren's nights, the dockworkers called them—and the salt-crusted cobbles of the Lantern Row hummed, very softly, like a struck bell that never quite finished ringing. Edmund Coldharbour had learned to sleep through it. He had learned to sleep through most things, at his age. Seventy-one years sat on him like a well-worn coat: heavy in the shoulders, warm at the back, impossible to take off.

His stall was a plank table on trestle legs, draped in oilcloth gone soft as chamois from decades of hands. Upon it he arranged his wares each evening with the same ceremony he'd once given to inking coastlines: luck-knots braided from grey harbor-grass; small clay bells that rang only when someone you loved was thinking of you; vials of tide-glass sand that glowed faintly for an hour after sunset, "just enough light," the label said in his careful cartographer's hand, "to find your way home by." Each charm bore its true name and its true working, written plainly on a scrap of paper tied with twine. No more harmless deniability. No more lies folded into knots. The city had outlawed lying about magic before it had ever thought to outlaw magic itself—Veyra's new order of Witnesses had insisted on that clause personally, and Edmund had wept with laughter when she told him, and then wept again for other reasons.

"Evening, grandfather."

The voice belonged to Pip Ondrell, eight years old, gap-toothed, perpetually salt-sticky, and the self-appointed terror of Lantern Row. She arrived every market night like weather, planting herself at the front of his stall with her elbows on the oilcloth and her chin in her hands, as though she were the customer and he the curiosity.

"Evening, smuggler," Edmund said. "What are you stealing tonight?"

"Nothing. Yet." She surveyed his wares with the gravity of a harbormaster assessing cargo. "Grandmother says your bells are real now. She says before, they were just—" she screwed up her face, hunting for the grown-up word, "—sentimental."

"Your grandmother is right. Before, they were sentimental. Now they're honest sentiment. It's a different trade entirely."

"How can you tell if one's working?"

Edmund picked up one of the clay bells and held it between them. It hung silent in the evening air. "You listen," he said. "And mostly, you wait. That's the whole secret, Pip. Everything I know about charms, gods, maps, and living, reduced to two words: listen, and wait."

"That's three words."

"Cartographers count differently."

She snorted, unconvinced, and stole a chestnut from the brazier man behind them with the fluid ease of long practice. Edmund watched her go and felt the old ache in his scarred left hand—the one that ached in rain and in cold and, inexplicably, whenever he was happy, which Sela said meant the scar remembered something his head had let go.

He flexed the fingers. They worked. They always worked. That was more than he'd expected, that morning twenty-two years ago when he'd woken mortal on the floor of a cellar shrine with Sela shaking his shoulder and no memory at all of what the god-realm looked like—only the feeling of it, the way you remember a dream of flying: the sensation without the geography.

* * *

Sela came by at moonrise, as she did on Maren's nights, carrying two cups of mulled wine and the particular walk of a woman who had spent the day being argued with by the Guild of Licensed Wonder-Workers, of which she was chair, founder, and sole original member.

"They want to certify the tide-glass," she announced, setting down the wine. "Formally. With stamps."

"It's sand that glows, Sela."

"It's sand that glows *because* Maren likes you, and the Guild wants that in triplicate." She dropped onto the stool beside him, older now too—thirty-something and broad-shouldered from hauling nets in her youth, with her father's drowned-and-restored eyes. "I told them wonder doesn't stamp well. I told them you'd say the same."

"I would have said it more rudely."

"You would have said it while charging them double." She smiled, then let it fade into the quieter thing underneath. "Pip asked me today why you don't teach anymore. Properly, I mean. Apprentices and all."

Edmund looked out over the market—the lanterns, the gulls arguing over fish heads, the far dark line of the harbor where the water breathed against the seawall. "Because everything worth teaching, I taught you already. And because the rest—" he tapped his temple, "—went soft. You can't apprentice someone to a feeling."

"You remember the bargain."

"Some of it. Enough. I remember a dragon's voice like a hearth the size of a cathedral. I remember being young and frightened and certain that safety was the highest good a man could purchase." He turned the cup in his scarred hand. "I don't remember my mother's face anymore. Or the god-realm's eastern gates. Or the exact words Emberveil used. The fire took what the fire took, and what grew back grew back changed." He shrugged. "It's a fair price. I've stopped auditing it."

Sela leaned her head briefly against his shoulder—the gesture of a daughter who had long ago stopped asking whether he wanted the affection and simply issued it—and together they watched the market do what markets do: persist.

* * *

Later, near midnight, when the stalls were folding and the braziers banking their coals, the child came back.

Not Pip. A smaller one, a boy Edmund didn't know, maybe six, in a coat cut down from a man's, standing at the edge of the lamplight with the terrible directness of the very young. His mother hovered behind him, apologetic, holding coins she clearly couldn't spare.

"He won't sleep," the mother said. "Since the storms last month. He keeps asking—"

"Mama says the gods live under the sea now," the boy said, talking over her, because six-year-olds have never met a rhetorical situation they couldn't charge. "In the deep parts. Is that true?"

"Parts of it," Edmund said. "Maren keeps her halls below the harbor trench. The others went where they liked. Gods are like cats that way."

The boy absorbed this with enormous seriousness. Then: "Do they still listen? If you talk to them?"

The question landed in Edmund's chest the way such questions always did—not painfully, not anymore, but with weight, like a stone set down gently on old scar tissue. He thought of a night twenty-two years ago, a cellar shrine full of frightened people, a girl with her father's eyes praying to a name she'd found scratched on a relic. He thought of a blade falling toward that girl's neck and his own hand rising, burning, choosing. He thought of a dragon's last breath passing into ten thousand hearths.

He crouched down, joints complaining, until his eyes were level with the boy's.

"They always listened," he said. "That's the truth of it, and it's the saddest and gladdest truth I know. They listened when nobody believed in them. They listened through all the silent years, through the Edict, through the purges—they listened the way the sea listens to a stone thrown in it. We're the ones who stopped asking. We got scared, or proud, or tired, and we stopped. But asking—" he tapped the boy's chest, lightly, right over the heart, "—asking costs nothing, and it's never once been refused. Not here. Not in this city. Go home and ask for a good dream. See what happens."

The boy's mother tried to pay him. Edmund waved her off and sold her a tide-glass vial for half price instead, because a man could be generous and solvent both, if he was careful.

They went off into the dark, and Edmund stood alone at his stall a while longer, listening to the harbor breathe.

* * *

He was closing up—folding the oilcloth, counting the night's modest coin—when he felt it.

Not heard. Felt. A change in the pressure of the air, the way the room changes when a very large animal enters it quietly. The lanterns along Lantern Row flickered blue, all together, all at once. The salt-hum in the cobbles rose half a tone. Down at the harbor, the water went strangely, utterly flat, from the seawall to the horizon, as though the sea had drawn a breath and was holding it.

Edmund set down his coins. He walked to the end of the pier, slowly, his bad hand resting on the rail, and looked east, where the water met the dark.

At first there was nothing. Then there was a swell—a single, vast, unhurried swell, moving against the wind, moving with intention—and beneath it, glimpsed rather than seen, something passed. Something long. Something old. The water over it glowed faintly, warmly, the color of banked coals, of hearths, of a fire that had once been a dragon and had become, by choice, the warmth in ten thousand homes.

It did not surface. It did not need to. As it passed—far out, past the shipping lanes, past the deep-water buoys, heading east toward the open sea and whatever waits there—Edmund felt the warmth of it on his face like a hand laid briefly on a bowed head. And in his scarred hand, the ache flared bright and then eased, eased completely, for the first time in twenty-two years, as though something had finally been forgiven all the way through.

The swell subsided. The sea resumed its ordinary breathing. The lanterns returned to gold.

But on the water, where the great shape had passed, a single line of phosphorescence lingered, glowing softly, curving away eastward like a stroke of ink across a chart—a coastline drawn by no human hand, marking a shore that no map had ever named.

Edmund stood at the rail a long time, smiling, his heart going quiet and full.

Then he turned back up the row, whistling, to fetch his chalk—because a cartographer's work, he had learned at last, is never finished, and somewhere between belief and proof there was a new stretch of country waiting to be mapped.
