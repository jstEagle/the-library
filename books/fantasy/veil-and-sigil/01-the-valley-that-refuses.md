## 1. The Valley That Refuses

The map on Astrid Joubert's table had never been seen by anyone who lived there.

It was a city called Vell, three hundred miles east and one mountain range and half a lifetime away, and he was drawing it from the testimony of a wool merchant's letters, a pilgrim's marginalia, and the secondhand memory of a peddler who had once gotten drunk in Vell's lower quarter and remembered almost nothing. The merchant wrote that the harbor curved like a cupped hand. The pilgrim noted that the temple district sat higher than the markets, so that incense smoke drifted down over the fish stalls at dawn. The peddler remembered only that the streets smelled of tar and oranges.

Astrid took these fragments the way a mason takes rubble—sorting, weighing, fitting—and built from them a city he would never walk through. He gave Vell its cupped harbor and its descending incense. He gave it a hill of temples and a warren of fish stalls, a ring road where the walls had once stood before the city outgrew them, because cities always outgrew their first walls and the scar of the old gate always remained as a boulevard or a market square. He could not have said how he knew this. He simply knew it, the way he knew that the river through his own valley ran faster on the outside of every bend, the way he knew that the miller's house leaned two degrees west of true and always would.

He knew places the way other men knew faces. It was not magic—or rather, it was not *only* magic, though here in the valley of Merrow's Ford the word itself had gone soft with disuse, worn smooth like an old coin passed through too many hands until no one could read what was stamped on it. Here, Astrid's gift was just a knack. A talent for maps. Something a man could have without anyone looking at him twice.

That was the whole point of the valley.

He dipped his pen, touched it to the vellum, and drew the long curve of Vell's eastern quay. Outside the window of the mapmaker's cottage, autumn was doing its slow work on the birches along the lane, turning them to coins of yellow light. Somewhere down the slope, Odile's rooster was announcing noon to an audience that had stopped listening hours ago. The kettle on the hearth ticked as it cooled. Everything was ordinary. Everything was exactly as it should be.

Astrid worked until the light shifted, then set down his pen and flexed his fingers, ink-stained to the second knuckle. He studied the finished sheet with the particular dissatisfaction of a craftsman who can see only what is missing. The quay was right. The temple hill was right. But there was a street missing somewhere in the tanners' quarter—he could feel the absence the way one feels a gap in a row of teeth—a street that ran crooked where all the others ran straight, because it followed the line of some vanished stream. He would write to the merchant and ask. The merchant would think him mad for caring about a tanners' street in a city he'd never seen, but the merchant thought him mad anyway, and answered his letters regardless, because Astrid paid well and asked better questions than anyone else in the trade.

He rolled the map, tied it with twine, and added it to the chest beneath the window: forty-one maps of forty-one places, each rendered in his careful hand, each destined for buyers in towns he would never visit. The chest was his life's work and also, if he was honest with himself in the small hours, his life's apology. He mapped the world he refused to enter. He charted distances the way a man counts the steps between himself and a door he will not open.

Below the cottage, past the terraced gardens and the low stone walls, the valley floor spread out in its patchwork of barley and flax, and beyond that, at the far edge of everything, the mountains rose up in their gray rampart—the shoulders of the world, the folk called them, because they seemed to hold the sky in place. And in the notch of the western pass, where the road came down from the high country, the ward lay across the land like a held breath.

Astrid could not see the ward. No one in living memory had seen it. You knew it the way you knew the inside of your own mouth: by absence. Beyond the pass, travelers said, the world was full of wonders and horrors both—witches in the fens, spirits in the deep woods, kings whose armies marched behind storm-calls and blood-omens. Within the valley, none of it. Milk soured at the ordinary speed of milk. Crops failed for honest reasons. Children were born plain-faced and giftless, and grew up to be farmers and weavers and, in one memorable case, a tax collector of unusual cruelty, which everyone agreed required no magic at all.

The founders had bought that ordinariness at some forgotten price, centuries ago, and the valley had been paying it ever since in the coin of smallness. Nothing remarkable entered. Nothing remarkable left. The maps Astrid sold to the outside world were the one exception, and even those traveled under another man's seal, printed in Lowmarsh by a firm that believed its reclusive author was a retired surveyor with a prodigious memory. Let them believe it. Belief was a kind of wall too.

He ate bread and hard cheese standing at the counter, the way he always did when a survey day waited, and he was shrugging into his coat when he heard Odile's knock—two raps and a pause, her signature, though she owned a key and used it whenever she pleased.

"Door's open," he called.

Odile came in smelling of woodsmoke and the bitter herbs she chewed against her rheumatism. She was seventy if she was a day, small and dry as a wren, with eyes that had gone pale as frost but missed nothing. She had taught him his letters, his inks, his proportions; she had taught him, above all, the discipline of the unremarkable. *Never be the best at anything visible*, she liked to say. *Be the second-best mapmaker in the valley. Be the third.* He had never quite managed it. There was no one else.

"You're going up to the boundary stones," she said. Not a question.

"The autumn survey. I do it every year."

"Mm." She lowered herself into his chair without asking, which was also her signature, and looked at the empty table, the drying pens, the evidence of a morning's work. "Every year you go up and measure the same rocks and come back and tell me the same nothing. Forty years of nothing."

"Thirty-two."

"Don't correct me, boy, I taught you arithmetic too." She turned her frost-pale eyes on him. "Take the long way back. By the ford. I've heard things."

"What things?"

"Things I don't repeat indoors." She rose, creaking, and paused at the door. "And Astrid. If the hum's changed—you'll tell me. Won't you?"

There it was. The word no one said plainly, laid out on the table between them like a fish gutted open. *The hum.* Every child in the valley learned it before they learned their prayers: that if you pressed your ear to the boundary stones on a still night, you might catch a sound like a struck bell that never finished ringing, fainter than hearing, more felt than heard. The voice of the ward. The valley's oldest lullaby.

"It hasn't changed in four hundred years," Astrid said. "It won't change today."

"No," Odile agreed. "It won't." And she left, taking the certainty with her.

* * *

The path to the western boundary climbed through birch and then pine, and then above the pines there was only rock and wind and the thin gold grass that survived on stubbornness. Astrid walked it with the ease of thirty-two autumns, his survey kit on his back, his thoughts arranged in the comfortable grooves they always found on this climb. The merchant's letter. The missing street in the tanners' quarter. Whether he dared ask the Lowmarsh firm for a commission on the northern coast, which would mean corresponding with sailors, who were worse gossips than peddlers and twice as unreliable.

By mid-afternoon he reached the first boundary stone.

It was a squat pillar of granite older than any record, carved with symbols so worn that scholars—who did not come here, no scholars ever came here—would have argued over them for decades. Astrid knelt beside it, unpacked his instruments, and began the ritual his mentor's mentor had prescribed: measure the stone, measure the shadow, note the weather, note the hour, confirm that nothing has changed, and write *nothing has changed* in the ledger in your neatest hand.

He measured the stone. He measured the shadow. He noted the weather—high cloud, wind from the northwest, cold enough to promise the first frost within the week. Then he did the thing he never skipped, the thing that made this survey different from any other work he did: he pressed his palm flat against the granite and listened.

The hum was there. Of course it was there. It was always there, the great slow bell-note of the ward, running through the rock and the ground and, he sometimes suspected, through the bones of everyone born in the valley, so that they carried it with them wherever they went and wondered why the rest of the world felt hollow.

But today—

Today it stuttered.

Astrid went very still, palm flat on the stone, breath stopped in his chest. It was such a small thing. A flicker, a hesitation, the way a candle flame leans when a door opens in another room. One heartbeat the hum was beneath his hand, vast and steady as the tide; the next it was—not gone, never gone, but *thinned*, stretched like a drumskin pulled too tight, and in that stretched instant he felt something on the other side of it. Not a presence, exactly. More like pressure. Like the sea leaning against a seawall, testing, patient, indifferent to whether the wall held.

Then it passed. The hum resumed its full depth. The wind moved the gold grass. A hawk turned a slow circle over the pass, riding air that was only air.

Astrid stayed kneeling for a long time.

He told himself it was the cold. Instruments misread; ears rang; a man alone on a mountain at the edge of winter could imagine anything. He told himself that four hundred years of silence did not break in an afternoon, that wards did not flicker, that the founders' work did not wear out like a roof or a plowshare. He told himself these things in the tone of a man reading a legal document aloud in a burning house.

Then he walked the line of stones, north along the ridge, checking each one, pressing his palm to each, and at the fourth stone it happened again—that lean, that thinning, that sense of something vast shifting its weight on the far side of a door. At the seventh stone it happened twice. By the ninth stone, near the old ford where the boundary road crossed down toward the lowlands, the flickers were coming often enough that he stopped pretending to take measurements and simply stood with his hand on the rock, counting.

*Something is leaning on the other side.*

Not breaking through. Not yet. But the ward had never flickered—not in the ledgers, not in the oldest widow's oldest story, not in the four hundred years of *nothing has changed* written in thirty-two hands in the survey books gathering dust in the meetinghouse. Whatever pressed against it now was new, or newly strong, or newly numerous.

And Astrid, kneeling in the wind with his instruments useless in their case, understood something else, something that made the cold go deeper than the season could account for. When the hum thinned, in those stretched instants, he had *felt* the shape of the ward. Not heard it—felt it, the way he felt the missing street in Vell's tanners' quarter, the way he felt the lie in a badly drawn coastline. For four hundred years the ward had muted everything, including the gift it was made to mute. Now, as it strained, it was letting things through. Slivers of the true shape of the world, leaking past a weakening seal.

His gift was waking up. Which meant the ward was failing. The two facts arrived together, inseparable, like thunder and lightning seen from opposite ends of a valley.

He packed his instruments with hands that were almost steady. He walked back down through the pines and the birches in the long slanting light, and at the bottom of the track he did not turn toward his cottage but toward the meetinghouse, where the survey ledgers slept in their cedar chest, because whatever he decided to say or not say, the record would need to be kept, and keeping records was the one form of courage he had ever practiced.

At the bend in the lane he stopped. Below him, the valley lay in its evening peace—lamplight kindling window by window, smoke rising straight from a dozen chimneys, the barley stubble silvered with frost. His whole life, contained in one bowl of sheltered land. Every person he loved asleep tonight inside the hum, inside the lullaby, inside the lie of safety that had held for four hundred years and might, he now knew, be holding for considerably less.

From the direction of the western pass, faint on the wind, came a sound he had never heard in thirty-two autumns of walking that road.

Bells. Many bells, far off, ringing in the high country beyond the ward—traveling bells, the kind peddlers stitched to their packs, the kind refugees tied to their children so they could find each other in the dark.

Someone was coming down out of the mountains. And the ward, for the first time in living memory, did not seem entirely sure it wanted to stop them.
