## 2. Ink and Ordinary Days

The morning Odile caught him drawing a room he had never entered, Astrid was seventeen and certain he would be ruined.

He had been working at the long table by the window of her map-shop, the one with the warped shutter that let in a blade of light sharp enough to cut paper, when his hand simply went on without him. He had meant to finish the survey of the miller's new sluice—honest work, paid work, work that required nothing but a measuring chain and patience—but somewhere between the third contour line and the fourth, the pen had drifted. It drew walls. A hearth. A table set for two, though the chairs were pushed apart as if the people who sat there had stopped speaking to each other. A window looking east, its glass fogged from the inside.

Odile's shadow fell across the page before he heard her footsteps. She had always walked quietly for a woman built like a dock crane.

"That's the Harrow house," she said. "On Cindermoss Lane."

Astrid's mouth went dry. "I don't know any Harrow house."

"No," Odile agreed. "You don't." She picked up the sheet, held it to the light, and studied it the way she studied everything: without hurry, without mercy. "Old Harrow died in the winter. His daughter came back from Vell to settle the estate. They haven't spoken in nine years. The house has been shut since midsummer."

"I made it up," Astrid said. "People make things up."

"People make up dragons," Odile said. "They do not make up the exact number of panes in a window they've never seen." She set the paper down, and for a long moment the only sound in the shop was the tick of the clock she kept deliberately slow so that customers would leave before closing. "How long?"

He could have lied. He had been lying, in small careful increments, for most of his life—the way you sand down a board until it fits a frame it was never cut for. When the schoolmaster asked how he'd known the old road ran beneath the barley field, he said he'd guessed. When he told the ferryman his rope was about to part and the ferryman asked how he knew, he said he'd heard it creak. Small lies, sanded smooth, fitted into an ordinary life.

"Since I was small," he said instead, because Odile had a way of waiting that made lying feel like a waste of good silence.

She nodded once, as if he had confirmed something she'd suspected for years, and then she did the last thing he expected. She took the strange drawing, folded it into quarters, and fed it to the stove.

"The valley keeps us safe," she said, watching it curl. "But safety is a habit, Astrid, not a guarantee. You will never draw what you see again where anyone can watch your hand. Do you understand me? Not a windowpane. Not a fencepost. If the ward ever so much as hiccups, I want no evidence on this table that you are anything but the best plain mapmaker in three counties."

"Why?" he asked. "What happens if the ward hiccups?"

Odile looked at him then with an expression he would spend the next ten years learning to read, and failed.

"Nothing," she said. "Nothing happens. That's rather the point."

* * *

Ten years later, Astrid Joubert was thirty-one years old and the best plain mapmaker in three counties, and he had gotten very good at nothing happening.

His days had the pleasing regularity of a well-drawn grid. He rose before the light, because the first hour belonged to him alone—to ink, to the smell of linseed and cold tea, to the particular silence of a valley that had not yet remembered it had people in it. He ate bread and honey standing up. He walked the boundary path as far as the Third Stone, where the ward lived in the air like heat above a summer road, and he stood there a moment each morning feeling it hum against his skin, faint and constant, the way you might rest a hand on a sleeping animal's flank. Then he went to work.

Work was the tithe maps, the estate surveys, the occasional commission from the wool factors downriver who wanted to know where their flocks could graze without quarreling with their neighbors' flocks. Work was also, though only Odile knew it, the letters. They came by peddler and pilgrim, by tinker and occasionally by pigeon, from people in the lowlands and beyond who had heard—no one could say how—that there was a mapmaker in the valley who could chart places he had never visited. A merchant in Marrowgate whose caravan route had stopped making sense. A widow whose late husband's mine had gone strange after the flood year. A scholar who wanted the shape of a coastline no ship had rounded in a century. Astrid would sit with their letters spread around him like tarot cards, close his eyes, and let the true shape of the place rise in him—not seeing, exactly, but *remembering*, as if every place in the world had once been known to him and he needed only to be reminded. Then he would draw it, check it twice against anything verifiable, burn his first drafts, and send the finished map south with instructions that it be copied in another hand.

It was, he understood, a strange way to live: a man who mapped the world while refusing to touch it. But the valley was soft and green and utterly unremarkable, and the ward hummed at its borders, and inside that hum his gift lay quiet as a stone at the bottom of a well. He had a mentor who pretended not to worry about him. He had a corner table at the Drowned Bell. He had, most nights, the deep ordinary pleasure of falling asleep knowing exactly where every wall in his house stood.

He should have known better than to trust it. Places, he would learn, are never still; they only seem so to people who aren't paying attention.

* * *

The Drowned Bell was not actually drowned—it had been named for a bell salvaged from a flooded church upriver, which hung now above the bar and rang only when the landlord's cat, a vast gray tyrant named Duchess, chose to leap against it—and it was where the valley did its thinking out loud.

Thursday nights were debate nights, which meant the same six arguments rotated through the room like weather. Tonight's weather was tariffs on river wool, and Astrid sat in his corner with a cider he was letting go flat, half-listening, sketching idly on a napkin: not a map, just lines, the way other men whittled.

"—all I'm saying," Hesk the cooper was declaring, "is that Aldric sits on a throne he poured for himself, and now he wants our wool taxed to pay for soldiers to keep him on it, and we're meant to call that a kingdom."

"It *is* a kingdom," said Marta Ostweather, who owned the mill and enjoyed disagreeing with Hesk the way other people enjoyed dancing. "That's the whole trouble with it."

"My cousin saw the edict posted in Marrowgate," offered young Tam, the farrier's son, leaning in with the relish of a boy delivering news he didn't yet understand. "Proclamation, sealed with the boar. My cousin says it's about the gifted. Says the king's declared a census. Every man, woman, and child with the knack is to be registered. For their own protection, the posting says."

"For their own protection," Hesk repeated, and spat with great precision into the spittoon by the door. "That's what they wrote before the last purge too. Protection. Like a wolf protecting a lamb right down to the marrow."

The room laughed, but it was the thin kind of laughter, and Astrid noticed that Marta didn't join it. Neither did the landlord, Wren, who had gone very still behind the bar with a cloth in his hands, polishing a mug that was already dry.

"They'll not find many gifted here," Tam said, shrugging. "Ward sees to that, doesn't it? Nothing odd gets born in the valley. Or if it does—" he waggled his fingers mysteriously, "—it stays quiet-like. Everyone knows that."

Everyone knows that. Astrid turned his napkin over and began drawing the same lines on the blank side, slowly, so that no one would notice his hand had changed. Everyone knows that the valley is safe. Everyone knows the ward holds. Everyone knows that nothing happens here.

"Registering them's one thing," Marta said. "My question is what comes after registering. You don't count sheep unless you mean to shear them or eat them."

"Or sell them," said a voice from the shadows by the hearth.

It was Odile. She rarely came to the Bell anymore—her knees objected to the benches, she claimed, though Astrid suspected the truth was that she disliked watching people think out loud when she could not correct them—but there she was in the corner seat, small and square and iron-gray, with a cup of hot wine she had clearly been nursing for some time.

Nobody laughed this time.

"You've seen it before," Wren said quietly. It wasn't quite a question. Odile was old enough that everyone assumed she had seen everything, and everyone was nearly right.

"I've seen proclamations," Odile said. "I've seen censuses. And I've seen what follows them, in the years when the borders weren't so quiet as ours." She drank her wine. "A list is a tool. In gentle hands it's a ledger. In others, it's a hunting map." Her eyes crossed the room and rested, for half a heartbeat, on Astrid in his corner—so briefly that no one else could have caught it, so precisely that he felt it like a fingertip laid on the back of his neck. "Best thing a clever person can do, in my experience, is be boring. Be so boring the census-takers fall asleep writing your name."

General grumbling agreement, and the argument rolled on toward tariffs again, and the evening resumed its usual shape. But Astrid stayed in his corner long after his cider died, turning his napkin into ribbons, and when he finally walked home under a sky salted with stars, he found himself doing the thing he did when he was frightened: reading the valley. The true shape of it rose around him unbidden—the roots of the hills braided under the fields like knuckles under skin, the cold thread of the aquifer, the ward itself arcing overhead and around, a shell of old intention, humming its one long note.

And beneath the note, so faint he might have imagined it, a second note. Slightly flat. Slightly wrong.

Like a bell with a crack in it, ringing anyway.

* * *

The peddler came on the last day of the month, as he always did—a round, cheerful man called Petch with a mule named Philosophy and a cart that smelled of cinnamon and cheap tinware. He was a fixture of valley life, arriving with the seasons' gossip the way other men arrived with cloth and needles, and his arrival was usually the pleasantest interruption Astrid's week contained.

This time, Petch came straight to the map-shop, which was unusual. Unusual still, he came without his smile.

"Astrid Joubert." He set a wrapped parcel on the counter—paper, twine, the ordinary architecture of commerce—and then left his hand on it, as if it might escape. "Got something for you. Two somethings, really. One's a letter from your Marrowgate scholar, wants a chart of the drowned coast, pays double, usual business." He paused. "Other one's not business."

"Petch."

"There's talk on the roads." The peddler glanced at the door, then at the window, then lowered his voice as though the ward itself might be listening. Probably, Astrid thought, it was. "Talk from travelers coming up out of the east. Villages emptied, whole valleys of folk walking. And the reason they're walking is the king's men have started taking names off the census lists and doing more than writing them down. There's a word going round the fires at night. *Tithe.* They're calling the gifted the king's tithe. Like grain. Like something owed."

Astrid found that his hands had gone cold around the parcel. "Taken where?"

Petch shrugged, and the shrug was worse than any answer, because it was honest. "East," he said. "Everybody says east. Toward the mountain. Nobody says what happens there, and the ones who'd know aren't talking." He pushed the parcel across the counter at last, and Astrid saw that beneath the wrapping paper, the shape of it was wrong for a book. Too flat. Too wide. "Found this nailed to a waystone two days north of the ford. Thought maybe you'd want to see it before anyone else did. Being as you're the one who notices things."

Inside the paper was a printed broadsheet, the cheap kind, ink already smudging at the folds. At the top, the boar's head of King Aldric, crowned. Beneath it, in letters chosen for legibility at a distance:

BY ROYAL EDICT: LET EVERY SOUL POSSESSED OF THE UNNATURAL GIFT PRESENT THEMSELVES FOR REGISTRATION WITHIN SIXTY DAYS. THOSE WHO CONCEAL THE GIFT ARE ENEMIES OF THE REALM AND SHALL BE HELD ACCORDINGLY. INFORMERS WILL BE REWARDED. THE KING PROTECTS HIS PEOPLE.

Below the text, a woodcut illustration: a benevolent-looking figure extending a hand toward a kneeling family. The artist had drawn the king's shadow falling across all of them, long and total, though the sun in the picture stood behind the viewer.

Astrid read it twice. Sixty days. Informers rewarded. Held accordingly. The words were ordinary words, the grammar of taxes and harvest quotas, and that was the horror of it—dread dressed in the clothing of paperwork, arriving gently, disguised as gossip, sitting on his counter between a roll of vellum and a jar of gum arabic as if it belonged there.

"Sixty days," Petch said. "From when it was posted. Which was, I'd guess, three weeks back." He rubbed his nose. "Roads being what they are."

Forty days, then. Perhaps less.

"Petch," Astrid said, and was surprised at how steady his voice came out. "These travelers walking out of the east. How many?"

"Hundreds. Thousands, maybe. More every week." The peddler shifted his weight. "And here's the thing, Joubert, the thing I keep chewing on. Where's a body to walk *to*? Everywhere's the king's country now, near enough. Except—" he tipped his head toward the window, toward the green and humming valley, "—places like this. Quiet places. Old wards. If I were running from the boar's men, I know where I'd head."

Astrid looked at the broadsheet. He thought of the second note beneath the ward's hum, the flat tone, the crack. He thought of Odile folding his impossible drawing into the stove ten years ago, and of her saying *safety is a habit, not a guarantee,* and of how he had spent a decade building his life inside that guarantee anyway, brick by careful brick, telling himself the ward would hold because it had always held.

"Thank you, Petch," he said. "For bringing it to me first."

"Well." The peddler gathered up his cheer and put it back on like a coat. "You pay your bills on time. Rare quality." At the door he paused, one hand on the frame, and his face did something complicated. "You take care of yourself, mapmaker. Strange times coming up the road. I can feel it in the mule, and Philosophy's never wrong about weather or trouble."

He left. Astrid stood alone in the shop with the edict in his hands, and outside the window the valley went on being beautiful—smoke rising straight from the chimneys, children shouting in the lane, the mill wheel turning its slow silver circle—as if none of it were already changing.

He carried the broadsheet to Odile's that evening without deciding to. His feet decided. She opened the door before he knocked, which meant she had seen him coming, which meant she had probably known since Thursday night that he would come.

She read the edict standing in her doorway, by the light of her lamp, and when she finished she did not gasp or curse. She simply folded it along its existing creases, very neatly, the way she folded everything, and said:

"How long since it was posted?"

"Three weeks, maybe. Forty days left. Perhaps fewer."

Odile looked past him, down the darkening street, toward the invisible southern border where the ford lay and the ward hummed its cracked and faithful note. When she spoke again, her voice was quiet in the way that frost is quiet.

"Then we haven't much time," she said, "before the first of them start crossing the water."

Astrid followed her gaze south, and though he could see nothing in the dark but the shapes of hills, he reached out—with the sense he had spent his whole life hiding, the sense that was now, apparently, a thing worth hunting—and felt, far away at the edge of everything, the ward flex like a muscle under a growing weight.

Something was already coming.
