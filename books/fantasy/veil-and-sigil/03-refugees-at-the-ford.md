## 3. Refugees at the Ford

The ward woke him before the horn did.

Astrid had lived his whole life beneath its hum the way a fish lives in water—unnoticed, unexamined, essential. It was a sound below sound, a pressure behind the ears, a faint sweetness in the air like rain that never quite arrived. He had slept inside it for twenty-six years without once thinking of it as anything other than silence.

So when it *changed*, he came awake all at once, heart hammering, hand already reaching for the lamp.

The hum had developed a seam. That was the only word he could find for it, lying in the dark of his room above the map shop: a place where the smooth fabric of the valley's protection caught and pulled, the way fine cloth catches on a nail. He lay still and listened to it with whatever part of him listened—the part he never named, the part Odile pretended not to notice when he described a village three valleys over down to the crookedness of its bell tower.

*Something is pressing against us,* he thought. *Something is leaning on the door.*

Then the horn sounded from the ford watchtower, one long note and two short, and the meaning arrived plain as ink: travelers at the border. Many travelers.

He dressed quickly, took up his satchel out of habit more than purpose, and went down into the street where the night was still holding its breath. Lamps were kindling in windows along Tallow Row. Somewhere ahead, near the water, voices were gathering like starlings.

---

The ford was a half-mile east of the town proper, where the Vaelin shallowed over pale stones and the valley's borders ran closest to the outside world. Astrid had mapped it a hundred times—not from life but from the survey reports the watch filed each season, cross-checked against letters, against a peddler's offhand remark about the depth of the water in spring melt. He knew the ford the way he knew most of the world: secondhand, stitched together, imagined.

What he saw when he crested the last rise was nothing his maps had prepared him for.

There were people in the river.

Dozens of them, wading the shallows in the gray hour before dawn, their belongings bound in bundles above their heads, children carried on shoulders, an old man leading a mule whose eyes rolled white at the cold. They came out of the dark on the far bank like a procession out of a story—the dispossessed, the hunted, the ones who had run far enough and long enough to reach the one place in the realm where King Aldric's writ was said to run thin.

And they were *lit*.

That was the thing Astrid could not look away from. Every one of them glowed—or rather, every one of them was *visible* to him in a way people had never been visible before. Not with light. With shape. With structure. He could see the old man's grief the way he saw contour lines: a deep fold running through him, north to south, worn by repetition. He could see the mother clutching her child and read her fear as a tautness in her outline, a thinning at the edges, as if she were being stretched between the country she'd fled and the country she'd entered.

He staggered, put a hand against the fence post, and breathed.

*This is what I am,* he thought, and the thought terrified him even as something in his chest unfurled like a fist opening after years clenched. *This is what the ward has been smothering.*

For as long as he could remember, his gift had been a candle burning under a blanket—a warmth, a glow, a danger of fire, but no illumination. In the valley, he perceived places the way everyone else did: flatly, ordinarily, through his eyes alone. His true sight worked only at a distance, on places beyond the ward's reach, fed by letters and rumor and the stubborn labor of inference. He had built a celebrated career on that distance. *The Valley Cartographer*, the traders called him, half in admiration and half in pity. The man who mapped the world he could not touch.

Now the world had come to touch him instead, and the ward—strained, flickering, its hum seamed like torn cloth—could no longer hold his gift down.

Every refugee crossing the ford was a revelation. A boy of perhaps ten whose outline shimmered with a strange double edge, as if he existed slightly out of step with the ground he walked on. A woman whose shape was *hollowed*, scooped out like a gourd, and Astrid felt his stomach turn with an instinctive horror he didn't yet have words for. A family of five walking so close together they nearly merged into a single form, their shared terror binding them into one creature with ten legs and five hearts.

"Master Joubert." Watch-captain Denna Lorre appeared at his elbow, breathless, her spear still shouldered from the tower. "You felt it too? The ward?"

"I heard it change," Astrid said. His voice sounded distant to himself. "How many?"

"Ninety-odd at the ford. More coming down the far road—we can see their fires." She squinted across the water, where dawn was beginning to bruise the sky. "They say Aldric's men burned Hallowmere. They say there's a hunt now, a real hunt, with rewards. Master Joubert—" she lowered her voice, "—some of them are saying the gifted ones are being taken to the mountain. To the court."

Astrid looked at the hollowed woman again and understood, suddenly and horribly, what the scooping-out meant.

"They've already been taken," he said quietly. "Some of them. Something's been taken *out*."

Denna gave him a strange look—the look people gave him when he said things he shouldn't be able to know—but the ford demanded her attention, and she let it go. Astrid was grateful. He did not want to explain that he could see the wounds people carried the way he saw rivers: plainly, unavoidably, in true shape.

---

By midmorning the meadow above the ford had become a camp. Blankets on wet grass. Cookfires smoking. The valley's relief committee—Odile foremost among them, sleeves rolled, voice hoarse from directing—moved among the arrivals with bread and broth and questions asked gently.

Astrid stayed at the edges. He told himself it was to stay out of the way. He knew, privately, that it was because every step closer to the crowd was another step deeper into the flood of perception, and he had not yet learned how to close his eyes to it. The gift did not ask permission. It simply showed him: the boy with the doubled edge, who turned out to be able to walk through locked doors; the mule-driver whose spine curved with decades of burden; the whole ragged congregation of the displaced rendered in lines and pressures and folds, a living topographic survey of suffering.

It was beautiful. That was the worst part. It was beautiful the way a storm is beautiful, and he hated himself for finding it beautiful.

"You're staring," Odile said, materializing beside him with two bowls of barley soup. She pressed one into his hands. "Staring is rude. Drink your soup and stare less obviously."

"I can't help it." He kept his voice low. "Odile—I can *see* them. All of them. Inside the ward. It's supposed to suppress this—it suppressed me my whole life—and now—"

"Now the ward is straining, and whatever it suppressed is leaking." She blew on her own soup, unhurried, though he saw her eyes flick toward the treeline, toward the borders, calculating. "Your grandmother's generation said the ward was woven to keep magic *out*. Perhaps no one considered what would happen if it had to keep magic *down* at the same time."

"Is it going to fail?"

Odile was quiet for a moment. Beyond her shoulder, a refugee child laughed at something—bright, incongruous, startling the crows from the meadow grass.

"Everything fails eventually," she said. "The question is whether we're holding the thread when it does." She nodded toward the far end of the camp. "There's a woman down there the watch can't get a word out of. Won't give a name, won't take soup, sits facing the water like she's expecting the river to follow her here. Go sit with her. You're good at sitting with people. And you're the least threatening man in this valley, which is a compliment."

"It wasn't, was it."

"No," Odile agreed, "but it's useful today."

---

She sat apart from the others, on a flat stone near the water's edge, exactly as Odile had described: facing the ford, spine straight, hands empty in her lap. Younger than Astrid expected—mid-twenties, maybe—with dark hair cut short and practical, the kind of cut you give yourself with a knife. Her clothes were road-worn past the point of style into mere fact: patched, stained, mended badly. She had the stillness of someone who had learned that stillness was safer than motion.

But it was her shape that stopped him three paces away.

Everyone else in the camp wore their damage on the outside of their outlines—folds, tautnesses, thinnings. This woman was different. There was a *void* in her, low in the center of her being, a place where something vast had been removed and the edges had begun, slowly, to curl inward around the absence. Like a map with a country cut out of it. Like a page with a name scraped away, leaving only the ghost of the ink.

She was missing something enormous. And she was holding herself around the hole so carefully, so precisely, that Astrid understood she knew exactly what was gone—even if she couldn't name it.

"Soup's free," he said, because it seemed like the least terrible opening available. "No questions attached. Well. One question. Are you warm enough?"

She didn't look at him. "You're the cartographer."

Word traveled fast in a frightened camp. "I'm Astrid."

"I know your maps." Now she glanced at him, and her eyes were the color of shallow water over slate—gray-green, watchful, exhausted. "My father traded for one. The northern passes. He used to spread it on the table and trace the roads with his finger and tell us where we'd go if we ever had to leave." Her mouth twisted. "We left. Different roads than the ones on your map. The king's men closed the passes."

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be sorry. Be quiet for a minute." But there was no venom in it—only exhaustion, and something underneath the exhaustion that might have been surprise at her own rudeness, or at the fact that he didn't flinch from it.

He sat down on the grass beside her stone, not too close, and drank his soup, and watched the ford with her. The water ran silver-bright in the full morning. On the far bank, watch-fires marked where more refugees waited for the ferry barge, because the ford itself was closed while the council argued about numbers.

"The ward flickered last night," she said at last, still watching the water. "Did you feel it? Everyone in the camp felt it. Like a draft under a door. Some of them wanted to turn back." A short, hard laugh. "Turn back. As if there's a back to turn to."

"My whole life, that ward has been the same sound," Astrid said. "Last night it changed. I don't know what it means."

"It means the walls are coming down everywhere." She said it flatly, without drama, the way you report weather. "The king's inquisitors found the gifted in my city by listening. There are people who can hear magic the way you'd hear a bell. They walk the streets ringing themselves, and anyone who rings back gets taken." Her hands, in her lap, had gone white-knuckled. "They took my neighbor's son. Fourteen years old. He could make flowers open early. That was all. Flowers. They took him anyway."

Astrid's throat was tight. "And you? Do you ring?"

She turned and looked at him fully then, and for a moment—just a moment—the careful guard dropped, and he saw beneath it: terror, vast and specific, and beneath the terror a grief so large it had its own weather.

"No," Sera said. "I don't ring. I sold the bell."

The words landed strangely, and he felt his gift stir unbidden, reading her again despite himself: the void in her center, the curled edges, the absence shaped like—

Like a soul. Like the exact place a soul should be, hollowed clean.

"What did you—" he began, and stopped, because she had already closed. The guard was back up, slate-gray and total, and she stood, brushing off her clothes with sharp economical motions.

"Forget it," she said. "Forget I said anything. I'm tired and I talk when I'm tired, and you're a stranger with kind eyes, and kind eyes are dangerous right now." She took a step away, then paused, not turning around. "Your maps are good, cartographer. My father trusted them. Don't make me regret telling you that."

She walked back toward the camp, small and straight-spined against the noise and smoke, holding herself carefully around the hole in her, and Astrid sat alone by the silver water with his cooling soup and the terrible new clarity of his waking gift.

Because he had seen it. In the moment before she closed, his sight had gone *through* her, deeper than courtesy allowed, and he had seen the ledger-line written into her absence—the mark of ownership, thin and precise as a surveyor's stroke, running from the hollow of her all the way east, all the way to the mountain, like a road only he could see.

A road. Leading to the court beneath the mountain.

Astrid looked down at his own hands, ink-stained, steady, made for drawing safe distances—and understood that the distance was gone. The world had crossed the ford and come to him, wounded and glowing and lit with true shape, and somewhere in the meadow behind him a woman was forgetting her own name one day at a time while a line as fine as a hair tied her to the mountain's heart.

The ward hummed overhead, seamed and straining.

He thought: *I am going to have to leave the map.*

He thought: *God help me, I think I already have.*
