## 6. King's Men at the Border

The ward woke him three nights before the king's men came.

Astrid had learned to sleep through its hum the way one sleeps through a river's murmur—so constant it became silence. But that night, in the small hours, something in the sound *stuttered*. A skipped beat, like a heart missing its rhythm, and he was upright in his cold room with his hand pressed flat against the plaster wall before he was fully awake, listening with more than his ears.

Through the wall, through the floor, through the sleeping bones of the valley itself, he felt it: a thin place. Somewhere along the western arc of the border, where the ward ran oldest and thinnest over the old ford road, the great woven silence that had held this valley safe for four hundred years had developed a flaw no larger than a needle's eye.

And through that needle's eye, faint as starlight through smoke, something on the other side was *looking*.

He dressed without lighting a lamp. He knew every step between his bed and his workroom by heart, which was its own kind of irony—a man who could see the true shape of places, navigating blind by habit because seeing openly had never been safe. But the habit felt different now. Since the refugees had come, since Sera had arrived hollow-eyed at the ford, the gift had been waking in him like a limb gone numb beginning to prickle back to life. The ward still pressed down on it, still muffled it to a whisper—but the whisper was growing louder each day, and Astrid was learning, guiltily, greedily, how much he had been missing.

He spread his latest draft across the worktable: the valley, rendered in his careful hand, all false distances and invented contours, the map of a place he'd drawn a hundred times from letters and rumor and other people's eyes. He never mapped the valley truly. That would have been madness—the true shape of the ward itself was the one thing he had trained himself never to look at directly, the way one does not stare at the sun.

But tonight he let himself look.

It came slowly, the way color returns to a world after snow-blindness. First the room's true geometry asserted itself—the worktable not quite where his eyes insisted it was, the ceiling a hand's breadth lower than it appeared, the whole house leaning almost imperceptibly toward the river the way old people lean into a wind they've stopped fighting. Then beyond the walls: the village, its lanes knotted together like thread in a seamstress's abandoned work, and beyond that the fields, and beyond the fields—

The ward.

He had expected a wall. Everyone imagined the valley's protection as a wall, high and gray and absolute. What he saw instead nearly undid him. It was not a wall but a *song*—a vast slow chord sustained across the entire rim of the valley, woven from thousands of interlocking threads of intent, each one anchored to a stone or a spring or a boundary oak, each one humming the same note in slightly different voices. It was the most beautiful thing he had ever perceived, and it was *dying*. Not quickly. Not dramatically. But here and there the threads had frayed to gossamer, and along the western arc, over the ford road, the weave had worn so thin that the note faltered—and through the gap in the music, patient and cold, something was listening for the melody's name.

Astrid sat down hard on his stool. His hands were shaking. Four hundred years, Odile had always said, the ward has held for four hundred years, and she had said it the way one says *the mountains are old*, meaning *forever*. Nothing was forever. The ward was an artifact, tended once by keepers whose order had dwindled to nothing, and artifacts wear, and somewhere out in the dark lands beyond the ford, someone had finally heard the skip in the song.

He did not sleep again. At dawn he went to find Odile.

* * *

She was already awake, of course. She was always awake before him, though she was seventy if she was a day, sitting in her kitchen with tea going cold beside a half-mended net and her old brass compass open on the table like a sleeping bird.

"You felt it too," she said, before he'd gotten two words out.

"The western arc. Near the ford."

"Near the ford," she agreed, "where our new guests crossed." Her eyes, pale and sharp as winter light, held his. "You're telling me you felt it. In words. Out loud." She set down her mending. "Fourteen years I've known you, boy. Fourteen years you've looked at my maps and corrected them in your head and said nothing. Now you're telling me what the ward sounds like."

"I don't know what else to do." His voice came out smaller than he wanted. "Someone is probing it, Odile. Something on the other side is testing the thin places, night after night. And last night I—" He stopped. Started again. "I think I can mend it. The frayed parts. If I can see them, I think I can... hold them together. Temporarily."

Odile was quiet for a long moment. Outside her window, the village was waking—the smith's hammer, the lowing of cattle, ordinary sounds, blessedly ordinary. Then she said, quietly: "Do you understand what you're offering? Every time you touch the ward with your gift, you leave fingerprints. The ward is a working of magic, Astrid. Magic answered with magic leaves traces. You'd be a candle lit inside a dark house."

"And if I do nothing?"

"Then perhaps the needle's eye stays a needle's eye. Perhaps whoever is out there grows bored. Perhaps—" She stopped herself, and he watched her refuse to finish the sentence, because they both knew how it ended: *perhaps Aldric's inquisitors find the flaw and pour themselves through it like water.*

"Perhaps," Astrid said, "isn't shelter anymore. We've been living in perhaps for four hundred years and calling it safety."

The old woman looked at him for a long time. Then she closed the compass gently, the way one closes a book one isn't finished reading, and pushed it aside, and said, "Show me where."

* * *

They went up on the third morning, before light, taking the shepherd's track above the terraces until the village was a scatter of roofs below and the ford road wound away westward into the hills. Odile walked slowly, leaning on her stick, but she walked, and she talked the whole way—about the keepers who had once tended the ward, about her grandmother who remembered their last ceremonies, about everything that had been forgotten on purpose because forgetting had seemed safer than remembering.

At the crest of the ridge, where a boundary oak split the skyline, Astrid stopped and let himself see.

The true shape of the place struck him like cold water. The ridge was not a ridge; it was a *seam*, one of the anchor-points where the ward's oldest threads were tied off around the oak's roots, and the knot there was beautiful and ancient and fraying, fibers parting one by one like the last strands of a rope. Beyond it, downslope, the ford glittered in the early light, innocent as any river. And beyond the ford—

He flinched. There were shapes in the trees on the far bank. Not men, exactly. Not yet. More like the *idea* of men, folded into the landscape the way a blade folds into a coat—six of them, maybe seven, arranged in a crescent, utterly still, and around each one the air had a wrongness he had no better word for than *iron*. They had been there for days, he realized. Probing. Listening. Sending out thin threads of attention against the ward's worn places, the way a locksmith sends picks against a lock, feeling for the tumblers.

One of those threads of attention was touching the frayed knot beneath the boundary oak *right now*.

"Odile," he breathed. "They're here. They're at the ford. They've found the flaw."

Her face went gray, but her voice stayed level. "Can they get through?"

"Not today. Not through this knot. But they're patient, and the knot isn't." He crouched, laid his palm against the earth among the oak's roots, and made the choice he had been dreading since the night the ward first stuttered. "Hold my shoulder. If I start to fall, shake me."

"Astrid—"

"If I start to *drift*," he corrected, "shake me. Hard."

Then he closed his eyes and reached into the knot with his gift.

It was like putting his hand into a running loom. The ward's threads were not matter; they were *intent*, centuries of it, braided by dead hands into a single sustained act of will, and when he grasped the fraying strands and began to draw them back together, they resisted—not because they rejected him, but because they were tired, the way old muscles resist, and he had to coax them, sing to them in whatever wordless language his gift spoke, gathering fiber to fiber, rebuilding the weave strand by strand while sweat broke cold on his neck and the iron-wrongness of the watchers pressed against his work like fingers testing a bandage.

He felt them notice.

There was no mistaking it. The probing threads went still—all six, all seven—and then converged, delicate and hungry, on the point where his intent touched the ward's. For one endless moment he was aware of being *examined*: turned over, weighed, read like a page. He felt their attention cataloguing him—the shape of his gift, its texture, its reach—and he poured everything he had into the knot, into finishing, into closing the needle's eye before they could widen it, and the last strands came together with a sensation like a bone setting, and the chord of the ward rang whole again along the western arc, and he tore his hand back and fell sideways into Odile's arms, gasping, the world swimming.

"Boy. *Boy.* Look at me. Are you drift—"

"No." He coughed. "No. I'm here. I'm—" He laughed, shaky and appalled. "Odile, they saw me. Whatever I just did, they *felt* it. They know there's someone inside the valley who can work the ward."

"Yes," said a voice behind them, dry and unhurried. "We do."

* * *

He came up the track from the ford alone, walking easily, hands visible and empty—a lean man in travel-gray clothes with no badge and no weapon showing, followed at ten paces by two others built like doorframes. The sun was fully up now, and it gave the scene a terrible ordinariness: three travelers on a shepherd's path, an old woman with a stick, a young man on his knees in the grass.

"Inquisitor Vael," the man said, stopping a courteous six paces away. He had a long, pleasant, forgettable face, the kind of face that made you realize afterward you couldn't describe it. "His Majesty's service. You'll forgive the intrusion—we're surveying roads." His smile suggested he found the phrase funny. "Roads are rather the king's passion these days."

"This is private valley land," Odile said. "Under charter older than your king's family."

"Charters." Vael tasted the word and smiled again. "Paper burns so readily, madam." His gaze moved past her, unhurried, and settled on Astrid with the mild weight of a hand on a scale. "Your companion looks unwell. Kneeling in the wet grass at his age. Or is it exertion? One works up such a sweat, tending things."

Astrid got to his feet slowly. Every instinct screamed at him to look away, to be nothing, to be the dull-eyed mapmaker he had played his whole life—but the gift was still half-awake in him, ringing with the ward's restored chord, and through it he could see what the man's forgettable face was built to hide: Vael was *threaded*. Fine filaments of the court's iron magic ran through him like wire through wax, anchoring him to something far away beneath a mountain, and where the filaments entered his eyes they had left marks like frost ferns.

Vael saw him seeing. Something flickered behind the frost.

"There was a rockslide on the path," Astrid said. His voice sounded steady. He was almost proud of that. "I stumbled. My grandmother helped me up."

"Grandmother," Vael repeated warmly. "Family. How precious. Family is what His Majesty's edict is all about, you know—keeping families *whole*. Whole families, whole realm, whole maps." He took a small notebook from his coat, and Astrid's blood went cold, because he recognized the gesture: it was the gesture of a man making a *record*. "I'll want names, of course. Routine. Everyone within a day's walk of the ford gives names—it helps us help you."

"We don't require help," Odile said.

"No one ever does." Vael wrote anyway, unhurried, and then he did the thing Astrid would remember for the rest of his life, the thing that would wake him sweating in years to come. He stepped closer—two paces, breaking courtesy deliberately—and looked at Astrid, really looked, with the frost-ferned eyes, and said softly:

"When you mended the knot under the oak just now—you gathered the north strand first. Before the south. Before the east." He tilted his head, pleasant as a schoolmaster. "Only the weaver knows the order of the weave. Only the maker knows where the making begins." He tucked the notebook away. "I don't have your name yet. But I have your *face*, and faces are only names that haven't been read properly. I'm very good at reading."

He touched two fingers to his brow—a parody of respect—and turned back down the track toward the ford, the doorframe men falling in behind him. At the bend in the path he paused, without turning, and called back:

"Mend your roads well, cartographer. His Majesty will be needing them soon. All of them."

Then the trees took him, and he was gone.

* * *

They did not speak until the village was in sight below them, smoke rising from the morning fires, ordinary and doomed-looking all at once.

"He'll come back," Astrid said. "With more. With a warrant, or an army, or worse—with paperwork. He marked me, Odile. He *read* me. I could see the filaments in him reaching for my face like—like hooks."

"Then we have days," Odile said. "Perhaps less." She was quiet a moment, looking down at the valley she had spent her whole life defending with stubbornness and tea and mended nets. When she spoke again her voice had changed—flatter, harder, the voice of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "You mended the ward. Good. It will hold against probes. It will not hold against siege, and now they know there's a weaver inside worth sieging for." She turned to him. "And there's a girl at the ford settlement whose soul is written in a ledger under a mountain, and a dying clerk who knows what's written there, and a company of strays who look at you lately as if you might be a door out of this place."

Astrid said nothing. The wind moved through the boundary oak above them, and deep beneath the roots, the knot he had rebuilt hummed its restored note—whole, for now, and temporary as a held breath.

"Go home," Odile said gently. "Sleep. Eat. And then go and sit with Sera, because I think you already know what you've decided, and I think she's going to need to hear it before Vael reads her face next."

Below them, on the far bank of the ford, half-hidden in the trees, something glinted once—brief, cold, deliberate—like a signal, or a salute, or the turning of a page in a notebook.

Astrid turned toward the village, and began to walk down into the last ordinary morning the valley would ever have.
