## 7. The Ledger Beneath

The dying man had been carried into the mill on a door taken from the cooper's house, and Sera could not stop looking at his hands.

They were clerk's hands. She knew them the way she knew her own mother's—ink-stained at the cuticles, the middle finger of the right hand calloused into a ridge where a pen had rested for thirty years. Even now, even gray-lipped and shivering under three blankets that Odile had fetched without being asked, his fingers kept making small motions against the blanket's weave, as though he were still writing. Still recording.

Sera stood in the doorway of the mill loft with a cup of willow-bark tea going cold between her palms and understood, with a certainty that arrived like cold water down the back of her collar, that this man had worked beneath the mountain.

She had known it since they'd carried him up from the ford two days ago, feverish and muttering numbers. Numbers with too many digits. Numbers that sounded like names.

*You could walk away,* said the careful voice inside her, the one that had kept her family alive through three checkpoints and one terrible winter. *He is nothing to you. He is a stranger dying of a lung-rot, and whatever he knows will die with him, and that would be a mercy.*

But her hands did not move from the doorway. Her feet did not carry her back down the stairs to the ordinary noise of the valley—the hammering, the argument at the tavern about rationing, Astrid Joubert bent over his maps with ink to his elbows. Instead she crossed the loft, set the tea down on the barrel beside the door-taken-for-a-bed, and sat.

The man's eyes opened. They were pale as river ice, and they found her face with the terrible accuracy of the very ill, who have no strength left for pretense.

"You're one of them," he rasped. "One of the sold."

Sera's whole body went still, the way a hare goes still—not because stillness helps, but because running has already failed.

"I don't know what you mean," she said, which was a lie so worn it barely qualified as speech anymore.

The man smiled. It was not a cruel smile. That was somehow worse. "I wrote your name," he said. "Or one like it. I wrote four thousand of them, and then I stopped counting, because counting was the thing that was killing me before the lung got its turn." His hand lifted from the blanket, trembling, and made that writing motion again—a small, precise stroke, the way you'd enter a name into a ledger column. "Vell-born? No—you'd be north country. The accent. North-country souls go to the foundries."

The tea in Sera's cup had gone entirely cold, but she felt heat anyway, rising up her throat, behind her eyes. "Foundries," she repeated. Her voice came out flat. Small. "What are you talking about?"

*What are you talking about*, as if she hadn't spent every night since the ford feeling herself thinning like a coat worn through at the elbows. As if she hadn't woken that morning reaching for her mother's face and finding only the shape of a face, a hole in the world shaped like remembering. As if she didn't know, in the marrow-deep way you know weather coming, that something of hers was being *used*.

The man coughed, long and wet, and when it passed he lay panting, and Sera found herself doing the unforgivable thing: she leaned closer. She took his hand. Clerk's hand, ink-ridge and all.

"Tell me," she said. "Please. My name is Sera. If you wrote me—if there's any chance—" She stopped. Started again, quieter. "Tell me what happens to us."

His name was Corvin Hale, and he had been second under-clerk of the Receiving Hall for nineteen years, and he told her everything, because—as he said, with the dark humor of men who have nothing left to protect—dead men make excellent confessors.

"The court keeps a ledger," he said. His voice had found a rhythm now, the rhythm of recitation, which Sera suspected was the only way he could bear to say it. "Not a book. People think ledger, they think book. It's bigger than a book. It's a room. The Master Ledger stands in the Hollow Choir—that's what we called the vault, though it isn't a vault, it's more like... you know how a bell looks from inside, when it's ringing? A room shaped like the inside of a sound." He paused, gathering breath. "Every soul that comes to us is entered. Name, birth-road, the terms of the sale, the price paid. And then the entry is bound."

"Bound how?"

"Ink and iron and older things than either." His fingers twitched against hers. "A soul in a body is like a river in its banks. The entry cuts a new channel. The soul flows where the ledger says. And the ledger says—" He stopped. For the first time since he'd begun, his recitation faltered, and Sera watched something move across his ruined face that she recognized, because she wore it herself every morning: shame.

"The ledger says what?" she asked, gently, because she had learned in the last year that cruelty and gentleness can use the same words, and only the speaker knows which is which.

"Work," Corvin said. "The court doesn't keep souls. It sells them. Has always sold them, but Aldric—" He spat, or tried to; there was nothing left in him to spit with. "Aldric doubled the intake. Tripled it. A soul burns clean, do you understand? Better than coal, better than the black oil from the deep seams. A soul given willingly burns brightest of all, because willing souls remember what they're burning *for*." His ice-pale eyes fixed on her. "Your armies march on it. Your siege engines. There's a foundry-wagon the size of a chapel that needs forty souls a day just to walk, and it walks, madam, it *walks*, and it eats."

The loft seemed to tilt. Sera heard the millwheel turning below, the ordinary groan of water doing honest work, and it sounded obscene.

"Where," she said. Her voice did not sound like her voice. "Where does it go. The foundry."

Corvin looked at her for a long moment, and whatever he saw in her face made him reach past his own dying to answer quickly, the way you hurry to catch someone who is falling.

"It was mustered south when I left. Six weeks ago. Bound for the free cities—the roads through Vell territory, the lowland passes. They mean to have the cities before first snow." He swallowed. "It's called the Anvil-Mother. And if your name is in my columns, north-country, sold within the last two years—" he closed his eyes, and the arithmetic moved visibly behind them, "—then some part of you is walking south right now, at the speed of an army, toward everything you ever loved."

Afterward, Sera did not remember leaving the loft. She remembered the stairs, and the yard, and then the path along the millrace, walking fast, faster, until the valley's sounds fell away and there was only the water's cold argument with itself and her own breath sawing in and out.

She stopped where the path widened, where Astrid had shown her the survey stake with its faded ribbon, and she put her hand against a birch tree because the ground was moving and the tree was not.

*Forty souls a day.*

She thought: *my mother's face.* She thought: *I am forgetting my mother's face because part of me is pulling a war engine through the passes toward her.*

That was the shape of it. That was the joke the mountain court had written in its beautiful terrible ledger: the soul she sold to save her family was marching, in chains of ink and iron, to destroy the place her family had fled toward. She had paid for their passage with the piece of herself that would be spent breaking the cities that might have sheltered them.

Sera slid down the birch trunk until she was sitting in the dead leaves, and she did not cry, exactly. What happened was worse and quieter: she went somewhere very still inside, the stillness of a woman reading her own death warrant and finding the handwriting neat.

*You are fuel,* said the stillness. *You have been fuel since the day you signed. You are burning right now, and you cannot feel the flame because the flame is in Vell, in the free cities, in the faces of people whose names you'll never learn—and they cannot feel it either, and that is the design. That is the whole genius of it. Nobody hears themselves burn.*

A branch cracked behind her. She knew before she turned who it would be. Astrid Joubert had a way of arriving at the edges of things, quiet as a man checking his own margins.

He didn't speak. He came and sat down in the leaves an arm's length away, folding his long frame with the awkward care of someone who had never learned to occupy space unapologetically, and he waited. The millrace murmured. Somewhere above, a hawk rode the wind off the ward-line, and Sera thought about how the ward hummed at the borders of this valley like a held breath, and how held breaths always ended.

"I talked to him," she said finally. "The clerk."

"I know. Odile said you were up there most of the night."

"He wrote my name." She kept her eyes on the water. "Nineteen years, second under-clerk of the Receiving Hall. He remembers mine. Or thinks he does. Four thousand entries a season, and he thinks he remembers mine." Her laugh came out wrong, a dry crack. "Isn't that something. To be memorable to the man who filed you."

Astrid said nothing, but she felt him listening—not the polite waiting kind of listening, but the other kind, the kind that made a space around a person the way a map makes a space around a place. She hated how much she needed it.

"He told me what they do with us." The words came out of her now like water through a split seam, unstoppable, scalding. "There's a ledger. A room-sized ledger, under the mountain, in a place called the Hollow Choir. Every soul entered, bound, channeled. We burn. We burn their engines, we light their halls, we—" Her voice caught on the next part and she forced it through. "There's a foundry. The Anvil-Mother. Forty souls a day. It's marching on the free cities, Astrid. It's marching on *Vell*, and if my name is in those columns—and it is, I know it is, I've felt it going thin since the ford—then I am in that machine. Part of me. Pulling it south. Toward my mother."

The last word broke, and she let it.

For a while there was only the water. Then Astrid spoke, and his voice was low and roughened, the voice of a man who had spent his life describing places he could never touch and had recently begun touching them.

"When I was a boy," he said, "I used to think the worst thing the ward did was hide the world from us. Keep us safe and blind. But it's been sitting here watching me for twenty-six years, and I've started to think the ward's real work wasn't keeping the world out." He picked up a leaf, turned it over. "It was keeping us from knowing what our safety cost. Somebody pays for every border, Sera. Somebody always pays. The valley just never had to look at the bill."

"And now?"

"Now the bill's come due, and it has your name in it." He looked at her then, and his eyes—those strange eyes that saw the true shape of things—held no pity at all, which she realized was the only thing she could have borne. Pity would have drowned her. This was something else. This was recognition. "So the question isn't whether you're burning. The question is what you do with the fire."

Sera stared at him. Something was happening in her chest, in the hollowed-out place where her mother's face used to live—something shifting, settling, taking weight. Not comfort. Nothing so soft. It was closer to what happens to a bowstring when it's drawn: the same tension, reversed into purpose.

"They can't run the foundry without us," she said slowly. The thought assembled itself as she spoke it, piece by piece, like a road appearing underfoot. "Forty souls a day. That's not a detail, that's a *throat*. The whole army drinks through it. If the ledger—" She stopped. Tested the idea the way you test ice. "If anything happened to the ledger. To the entries."

"Then the channel closes," Astrid said quietly. He was ahead of her; of course he was, he was a mapmaker, he thought in connections. "And whatever's flowing through it flows back to where it came from."

"To *me*." Her hands had closed into fists in the dead leaves without her noticing. "My soul comes home, and their engine starves on the road to Vell, and every other name in that room-sized book—" She looked up at the gray sky over the ward-line, at the faint shimmer there that only Astrid could truly see, and felt the horror in her transmute, molecule by molecule, into something with edges. "Four thousand a season. How many seasons, Astrid? How many names are in that choir?"

"Enough," he said, "to win a war. Or to lose one."

Sera rose. Her legs were steady. That surprised her; she had expected to stand and find herself still the thinned-out thing she'd been since the ford, the woman missing pieces. But rage, it turned out, was structural. Rage went all the way down to the parts they hadn't managed to take.

"Then we don't buy anything back," she said. "Buying is their game. Their prices, their ledger, their rules—I've played by their rules once already and it cost me my mother's face." She turned to look down the valley, at the mill, at the smoke of the refugee fires, at the small stubborn lights of people who had chosen to shelter strangers knowing what it might cost. "We take it back. All of it. Every name in that book."

Behind her, Astrid got to his feet more slowly, and when she glanced back at him, his expression had changed—there was fear in it, real fear, but underneath the fear something kindled, the look of a man who has spent his whole life drawing roads and has just been handed a reason to walk one.

"That's not a rescue," he said. "That's a war."

"No," Sera said, and heard her own voice come back to her, whole, unthinned, hers. "It's an audit."

Down in the valley, the millwheel turned. South beyond the ward, beyond the mountains' blue shoulders, something vast and hungry was walking toward the free cities on legs of iron and stolen fire—and for the first time since she had signed her name in a room that smelled of ink and iron, Sera knew exactly where she intended to be standing when it arrived.
