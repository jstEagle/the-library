## 8. Leaving the Map

The map of the mountain road took Astrid three nights to draw, and he hated every line of it.

Not because it was wrong. It was the truest thing he had ever made. He could feel that now, in the strange new way he felt everything since the refugees crossed the ford—the valley's ward no longer a blanket over his senses but a thin sheet of glass, and beyond it, through the glass, the world's actual shape pressing against him like a face at a window. The old trade road wound up out of the valley in a series of switchbacks his grandfather had described as "brutal but honest," and Astrid could see them exactly: each bend, each culvert, each place where the mountain's shoulder had shrugged and dropped a century of scree across the paving stones. He could see where the road remembered being a pilgrim's way, older than the trade route, its buried course running parallel like a vein beneath skin.

He hated it because drawing it was a decision. Every line was a promise to walk beside it.

He sat in Odile's workroom with the finished chart pinned under weights, lamplight pooling on the vellum, and let himself do the arithmetic he'd been avoiding. From the ford to the mountain's foot: eleven days, if the weather held, twelve if it didn't. Through the Gray Stair country after that—cliff roads, single-file paths, places where four people could be ambushed by two. Then the dwarven ruins, then whatever lay beneath. And all of it under the eyes of a king who had declared open season on people like Astrid, guided by a man whose gift would ring like a bell in Astrid's chest every time he used his own.

Sera's soul powered a siege foundry. Bramble had been certain of the timeline: the foundry marched with the spring roads, and the spring roads opened within the month. Whatever they meant to do, they had weeks, not seasons.

Astrid looked at the map and understood, with the clarity of a man reading his own death warrant in a beautiful hand, that he was going to leave the valley.

He had never left the valley.

The thought was so enormous it refused to feel real. He was twenty-six years old and he had never stood outside the ward. He knew the world only the way a man knows a woman from letters—intimately, wrongly, entirely secondhand. He had drawn the coast of the Sundering Sea from a sailor's drunken recollections and three contradictory charts; he had mapped the free city of Vell from a peddler's gossip and a smuggler's grudge. He knew the world's shape the way the blind know faces, by touch alone, by rumor, by love.

And now the world wanted to know him back.

* * *

He told Sera first, because it seemed cowardly to tell anyone else before her.

He found her at the edge of the refugee camp, sitting on the low stone wall above the terraces, watching the evening come down the mountainsides. She did that often—sat at the boundary of things, as if she were still deciding which side of any given line she belonged on. When he climbed up beside her she didn't startle, just glanced at him sideways with those hollowed-out eyes that had begun, lately, to hold something other than emptiness. Wariness, maybe. Or the raw beginning of trust.

"I've finished the map," he said.

"Of the trade road?"

"Of everything between here and the mountain." He unrolled a copy across their knees, weighting the corners with stones. "Eleven days to the foothills. Four more through the Stair country. The ruins after that—I can't chart what I haven't seen, but the old records say the dwarven ways run under the whole massif, and if the court is beneath the mountain, there will be doors."

Sera studied the map for a long time. Her finger traced the route without touching it, hovering a hair's breadth above the ink, the way people touched things they were afraid of wanting.

"You drew this from letters," she said. "From rumor. You've never seen any of it."

"No."

"And you're going to guide us along it anyway."

It wasn't a question, but he answered it. "Yes."

She looked up at him then, and he braced for her to say what any sensible person would say—that he was a mapmaker, not a soldier; that his hands were stained with ink, not anything useful; that sending him into Aldric's realm was like sending a candle to negotiate with a wildfire. Instead she said, quietly:

"Why?"

And because she had earned honesty—the way she'd paid for it, giving him the worst thing about herself in the dark of the infirmary—he gave it back.

"Because I've spent my whole life drawing places I was too afraid to see," he said. "Because the ward kept me safe and small and I called the smallness safety. Because your mother's face is disappearing from you one memory at a time, and there is a ledger under a mountain with your name in it, written down as property, and I have looked at enough maps to know that every border ever drawn was drawn by someone who benefited from it." He stopped, embarrassed by the flood of it, and made himself finish. "And because I think I'm the only person alive who can read the road to get us there."

Sera was silent. Below them, the camp fires were coming alight one by one, little orange constellations in the blue dusk. Somewhere a child was laughing, and the sound carried strangely in the cold air, bright and fragile.

"The foundry marches when the passes open," she said at last. "Bramble's sure?"

"As sure as she can be."

"Then we have maybe five weeks." She folded the map with quick, precise movements, the way she did everything, as though even hope had to be handled efficiently or it might escape. "You'll need more than a map, Astrid Joubert. You'll need people who can fight, people who can lie, and people who know what the inside of that court looks like."

"Bramble knows the court."

"Bramble knows the ledgers. There's a difference." She handed the map back. "I know the checkpoints. Yorick knows how Aldric's men move. The twins know every goat path in three provinces. And I—" her mouth twisted, "—I know what it feels like to be owned by that place. Which may be worth more than all of it."

She stood, brushing stone dust from her skirts, and looked down at him with an expression he couldn't fully read—something between pity and respect, and underneath both, faintly, warmth.

"Ask them tomorrow," she said. "Before you lose your nerve. Courage has a shelf life, mapmaker. Ask me how I know."

* * *

They said yes. That was the thing that undid him—not the danger, not the leaving, but how easily they said yes.

Yorick listened to the plan with his arms crossed and his scarred face unreadable, and when Astrid finished, the big man was quiet for so long that Fen started to fidget. Then Yorick said, "You'll freeze on the Stair. You'll hesitate, and hesitation kills on cliff roads." A pause. "So I'll walk point until you stop hesitating." And that was all. As if volunteering to die on a mountainside for a stranger were a matter of scheduling.

Fen and Ost exchanged one of their twin conversations—a flicker of the eyes, a tilt of the head, entire paragraphs in half a second—and Ost said, "We're in. But we pick the campsites." Fen added, "And if your magic gets us killed, we haunt you specifically." It was impossible to tell if she was joking. With the twins it always was, and never was.

Bramble said yes before he'd finished the first sentence, already pulling a stub of pencil from her sleeve to start a list. "Rations for sixteen days minimum. Oil of wintergreen for the twins' feet. Do you know how many forms of tribute the court accepts? Neither do I, but I'll find out." She wrote *rope* three times, crossed out two, and underlined the third with the ferocity of a woman who had once watched someone die for lack of it.

Only Odile argued.

She didn't argue loudly. Odile never did anything loudly. She waited until the others had gone, until the workroom was just the two of them and the smell of ink and old paper, and then she began clearing his drafting table with slow, deliberate movements, stacking his tools in the order he liked them, which was her way of saying something while refusing to say it.

"Say it," Astrid said.

"Say what?" She set his good pen down with exaggerated care. "That you're my student and I'm proud of you? That this is the bravest and stupidest thing anyone has ever proposed in this valley, and I include the year the miller tried to fly? That the last time I left this valley I was younger than you are now, and I came back with nothing but this?" She touched something at her throat that he'd never noticed before—a cord, and on the cord, an object he had somehow never seen in fifteen years of working beside her.

A compass.

It was old. Old the way the valley was old, its brass case worn smooth and dark at the edges, its glass clouded like a cataract eye. But when Odile lifted it into the lamplight, the needle swung—not toward north, Astrid realized, but toward *him*, trembling, eager, like a dog that had caught a scent after years of forgetting how.

"It doesn't point north," he said.

"It points at true things." Odile pressed it into his palm and closed his fingers over it. Her hands were dry and light as autumn leaves. "My master gave it to me when I left. I asked her what it was for, and she said: *for knowing which way home is, when home stops being a place.*" Her voice caught, just slightly, on the last word. "I never needed it. I came back. I stayed. I let the ward make me comfortable, and I told myself comfort was a choice." She looked at him, and her eyes—mild, nearsighted, ordinary eyes that had read ten thousand maps and believed none of them completely—were wet. "Don't make my mistake twice, boy. Don't confuse the walls with the house."

"Odile—"

"Listen to me." Her grip tightened, sudden strength in the frail fingers. "Outside, your gift will be different. In here it wakes through the ward like light through frosted glass—soft, muffled, half yours. Out there it will be *loud*. You will feel every road, every grave, every wall men ever raised, all of it shouting its history at you at once. It will be a weapon. It will save your life a dozen times." She paused. "And it will be a beacon. Every gifted soul within fifty miles will feel you use it—every inquisitor, every hunter, every hungry thing that serves the court. The moment you reach for the land's true shape, you are ringing a bell that says *here, here, here I am.*"

"So I shouldn't use it."

"So you should choose, every time, *when*." She released his hand. "Power spent carelessly is just noise, Astrid. Power spent precisely—" the ghost of her old dry smile, "—that's cartography."

He laughed, and it came out cracked, and then he wasn't laughing at all, and Odile pulled him down and held him the way she hadn't since he was a child newly orphaned and new to her table, all elbows and grief and hunger for the shapes of far places. He breathed in ink and lavender and the particular dust of old vellum, and thought: *this is what home smells like, and I am about to leave it.*

"When do you go?" she asked into his shoulder.

"Tomorrow. Before dawn. The ford is easiest at low water."

"Then finish your tea," she said, stepping back, brisk again, blinking hard, "and get some sleep. Dead cartographers draw terrible maps."

* * *

He didn't sleep. He spent the night doing what he had always done with unbearable feelings: he worked.

By lamplight he redrew the route three times, refining, annotating, marking water sources and blind camps and the three places where the trade road narrowed enough that two determined men could hold off twenty. He copied the master map twice—one for the company, one wrapped in oilcloth to cache at the valley's edge, so that if they failed, someone might follow. He packed rations Bramble had weighed with monastic precision, rope, flint, the wintergreen oil, a knife he barely knew how to use. Last of all, at the bottom of the pack, wrapped in a shirt that still smelled of home, he placed the compass.

At some hour past midnight he stood at his window and looked out at the valley lying silver and still under a waning moon—the terraces, the roofs, the ward-stones at the borders glowing their faint, patient blue, holding back a world that had finally decided to come in whether they held the door or not. His whole life fit inside that glow. Twenty-six years, and every one of them spent inside a circle drawn by other hands.

*Tomorrow,* he thought, and the word tasted like iron and like honey.

He was terrified. He wanted to name it honestly, at least in the privacy of his own skull: he was terrified the way he imagined a bird must be terrified the first time it falls off the edge of the nest—terrified and, underneath the terror, appalled to discover a wild gladness, a *yes*, beating its wings against the fear. Outside the ward his gift would be loud and dangerous and visible. Outside the ward he would be nobody's mapmaker of safe distances, nobody's quiet keeper of secondhand worlds. He would be exposed, hunted, new.

He found he could not wait.

Dawn came gray and cold. At the valley's edge, where the trade road slipped between the first ward-stones, the company assembled in silence: Yorick checking straps, the twins already scanning the heights with the restless attention of creatures built for horizons, Bramble muttering inventory, Sera standing a little apart, her hood up, her face turned toward the mountain that had taken her name.

Odile walked him to the boundary itself, to the exact line where the ward's hum became audible, a note below hearing, felt in the teeth.

"Your gift will wake properly out there," she said. "It will hurt. It will be the most beautiful thing that has ever happened to you. Both at once, always." She straightened his collar with two tugs, an absurd maternal gesture that undid him more than tears would have. "Come back and tell me what the sea actually looks like."

"If I live that long."

"*When* you live that long," Odile said. "Precision, boy. It's cartography."

He stepped past the ward-stone.

The sound cut off behind him like a door closing—and the world *opened*. It came flooding into him all at once, exactly as she'd promised: the trade road singing its centuries of footsteps, the mountain's bones groaning their slow tectonic patience, the buried pilgrim way pulsing beneath the paving like a heartbeat under floorboards, the graves, the walls, the old burned watchtower remembering its fire. Ten thousand truths shouting at once, gorgeous and unbearable, and Astrid staggered, caught himself on his staff, and laughed out loud—a wild, cracked sound that sent a crow up from the gorse.

"Astrid." Sera's voice, sharp with alarm. "What's wrong?"

"Nothing." He wiped his eyes, still laughing, still half-blinded by the sheer *presence* of the world. "Everything. It's all true out here. All of it, all at once—it's—" He groped for the word and found the only one that mattered. "It's *mapped*. The whole world is mapped, and I can finally read it."

Ahead, the trade road climbed away through the morning mist, switchbacking up the mountain's flank toward the Gray Stair country, toward the ruins, toward the court and the ledger and the foundry marching on borrowed souls. Behind him, the ward hummed its fading note, and Odile stood at the border growing smaller, one hand raised.

Astrid settled his pack, unstoppered the compass, and watched the needle swing—past north, past the road, pointing dead ahead into the mist at something true.

"Eleven days to the foothills," he said. "Keep pace, and stay off the paved sections after rain."

Yorick grunted acknowledgment. The company moved.

None of them saw the figure standing on the ridge above the third switchback—still as a heron, robed in travel-gray, watching the valley's gate with the fixed attention of a man who had marked a face at a border and traveled a long way to see it leave.

But the needle of Odile's compass trembled once, and swung toward the ridge, and Astrid, feeling the land's true shape shift with a stranger's weight upon it, reached instinctively for his gift—

—and felt, faint and far and cold, another gift reaching back.
