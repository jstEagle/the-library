## 9. The Company of Strays

The company assembled itself the way a river assembles a flood—piece by piece, without anyone's permission, until it was simply there and could not be unmade.

Astrid had imagined, in the fevered hours after his decision, something orderly. A muster. Names read from a list, hands raised, oaths spoken over Odile's compass. What he got instead was Bramble arriving two days early with three satchels of stolen stationery and an opinion about everything; Yorick appearing at the ford at dawn with no explanation and a soldier's bedroll worn to thread; and the twins, Fen and Ost, who had not so much joined as *attached*, the way burrs attach to wool, present before anyone thought to ask them to leave.

They gathered on the last evening in the tavern's back room, where Odile had pushed the tables together into one long island of candlelight and ale, and Astrid stood at the head of it feeling less like a captain than like a schoolmaster who had lost control of his classroom.

"So," said Bramble, "the routes."

She was a small woman built like a filing cabinet, all corners and drawers, with ink permanently stained into the creases of her knuckles. She had been a clerk of the mountain court for nineteen years before the dying refugee's confession had sent her running with her conscience and as many records as she could carry. She now spread three maps across the table—*his* maps, Astrid realized, copied without his knowledge—and weighted their corners with tankards.

"The pilgrim road through the Ashen Pass is fastest," she said. "Also most watched. The shepherd tracks south of it are slower but cross open moor for two days where anyone with a spyglass can count your heads. And there is the old dwarven way under the Gray Stair, which doesn't appear on any map made in the last century because everyone who used it stopped needing maps." She looked up. "I want to know which one we're taking before I decide how much paper to burn tonight."

"Burn?" said Fen.

"Records leave trails," Bramble said, as if explaining rain to a duck. "My handwriting leaves trails. There are clerks beneath the mountain who could identify me from a comma. So we decide, and then I burn what we don't need, and then we are four fewer decisions away from being caught."

"Five," said Ost.

Everyone turned to him. He sat beside his sister with the stillness of a heron, and when he spoke it was in the flat, unhurried voice of someone reporting weather.

"You're forgetting the ford. Someone has to tell the valley we went east instead of north, or Aldric's men will pull the thread wrong and hang someone here for it. That's a fifth decision, and it's the one that gets people killed if we botch it."

Fen grinned at her brother, quick and sharp. "What he means is he's already worked out how to do it. He just likes saying the killing part out loud."

"I like accurate accounting," Ost said.

Yorick laughed—a short, ugly bark that surprised everyone including, apparently, himself. He was a big man gone soft at the edges, with the broken-veined face of a drinker who had recently and reluctantly stopped, and he sat with his hands flat on the table as though at any moment someone might order him to stand down. "Accurate accounting," he repeated. "Gods. Nineteen years in Aldric's army and I never once heard those words used honestly."

"And what were you doing in Aldric's army?" Bramble asked, sweetly, terribly.

The room went quiet. Sera, sitting at the far end with her hood half up even indoors, watched Yorick the way one watches a door at night.

"Killing people who ran faster than me," Yorick said. "Mostly. You'll hear the rest eventually or you won't. Can we talk about the Stair?"

"We can't," said Sera.

Her voice was low and rough, like a hinge that hadn't been oiled in years. Everyone looked at her. She didn't seem to enjoy it, but she didn't look away either, which Astrid was beginning to understand was the whole architecture of the woman: every soft thing stripped away and replaced with load-bearing stone.

"The Gray Stair goes under the mountain's skirts," she said. "I know, because I sold my soul to the thing that lives there, and things like that don't sell passage past their own doorstep. If the old road runs through court territory, then using it isn't a route. It's a delivery."

"She's right," Bramble admitted, and began stacking papers with sharp little movements. "Which leaves the moor, which leaves us visible, which leaves the pass, which leaves us dead. Wonderful. I've risked my neck for a company with no good options."

"That's usually what a company is," said Fen cheerfully.

Astrid cleared his throat. Six faces turned to him, and he felt the weight of each one like a hand on his shoulder—the weight he had spent thirty years avoiding by staying in a valley that refused the world.

"We take the moor," he said. "But not straight across. There's a fold in the land north of the standing stones—I've seen it in the letters, an old drover's hollow—and if my gift reads it true, we can move by day inside it and be invisible without being hidden. Hidden draws eyes. Invisible is just... not worth looking at."

"You've never seen this hollow," Ost said. It wasn't quite a challenge. It was worse: it was arithmetic.

"No," Astrid agreed. "I've read forty accounts of it and triangulated three of them. It's either there or it isn't, and if it isn't, we lose a day and I lose the argument forever."

Ost considered this for a long moment. Then he nodded, once, like a ledger closing. "Fair terms."

* * *

Sera left the tavern before the others, as she always did, slipping out through the kitchen where the cook owed her silence and out into the lane where the evening mist came off the river like breath. She had meant to go straight to the loft above the chandlery where she'd been sleeping. Instead her feet took her to the ford.

The water talked to itself in the dark, patient, indifferent. On the far bank the ward hummed its old note, the sound she'd stopped hearing after three days and now heard again constantly, the way you hear a tooth after the dentist names it. Beyond the ward, the world. Beyond the world, the mountain.

*You'll feel yourself thinning,* the dying clerk had told her, gripping her wrist with terrible clerkly precision. *It starts with faces. You'll reach for your mother's and find a shape where she should be. That's the interest, girl. The principal was paid the day you signed.*

Sera crouched at the water's edge and tried, as she did every night, to hold her mother's face. Brown eyes. A scar through the left eyebrow from a fall off a hay wagon. The way she'd hummed when kneading bread—not a song, just a note, one note, over and over, like the ward itself.

The eyes came. The scar came. The humming wouldn't come. She reached for it and found a smooth place, a place where something had been filed away, and she pressed her palm flat against the cold mud and breathed through the panic the way she'd learned to breathe through everything: quietly, completely alone.

"You shouldn't be out here by yourself."

She hadn't heard Astrid come down the path. Of course she hadn't; the man moved like marginalia. He stopped a few paces off, holding two cups of Odile's terrible spiced wine, and offered one without ceremony.

"I'm always by myself," Sera said. "It's the only thing I own outright."

Astrid sat down on the wet grass beside her, which no one in the valley would have done, and drank his wine, and said nothing, which was somehow worse and better.

"The twins think you're going to get us all killed," she said eventually.

"The twins think I might get us all killed. Ost distinguishes between certainty and probability. It's why I trust him."

"Why do any of us trust any of you?" She heard her own voice sharpen and couldn't stop it. "You've known me three weeks. You know what I am. What I sold. And you're spending your first day outside a valley in thirty years—your *whole safe life*—to walk toward the thing that owns me. That's not sense, mapmaker. That's something else, and I'd like to know what it's called so I can refuse it."

Astrid turned his cup slowly in his hands. When he answered, he didn't look at her, and she understood that too was a kindness.

"When I was twelve," he said, "I drew a map of my mother's garden from memory, and it was perfect. Every bed, every stone, the crooked apple tree. And I remember thinking: this is the truest thing I've ever made, and it's of the smallest place I know. I've spent my life making true maps of places I've never seen, Sera, and every single one of them was a guess wearing a beautiful dress. You—" he gestured vaguely at her, at the ford, at the dark, "—you're the first territory I've ever been certain about. I know exactly where you are. I know what's been taken and what remains. A man charts what matters to him. That's all cartography ever was."

Sera stared at the river for a long time.

"That's the worst courting speech I've ever heard," she said finally.

"It wasn't—" Astrid went pink to the ears. "That wasn't courting."

"Mm." She took the second cup of wine from his hand, because her fingers were cold and because she wanted to, and both reasons felt dangerous. "Drink your wine, mapmaker. Dawn comes early, and I intend to watch you fail to find your hollow."

* * *

Yorick did not sleep. He sat up by the banked fire in the tavern's yard and cleaned a sword he had no right to carry anymore, and he told himself—as he told himself every night—that the dreams would stay on their side of the wall.

They didn't. They never did.

In the dreams he was back in the burning village with the writ in his fist and the list of names, and the names were not names, they were *people*, and the people were running, and his orders were clear, and he had followed them, and following them had been so easy, that was the unforgivable part, how easy it was, how the body learned atrocity the way it learned drill—

"You breathe loud when you dream."

Fen dropped onto the bench across the fire from him, knees up, chin on knees, a small sharp shadow. Behind her, at the edge of the firelight, Ost leaned against the wall with his arms crossed, awake too, awake probably always, the two of them keeping watch over nothing in particular because watching was what they were.

"Go back to bed," Yorick said.

"Can't. Beds are for people whose heads stop talking." Fen studied him across the flames with unsettling directness. "You were at Marrowfield."

It wasn't a question. Yorick's hands went still on the sword.

"Who told you that?"

"Nobody. You flinch at the word *east*. You check the horizon at dusk like you're expecting cavalry. And you said 'nineteen years' earlier, which puts your service ending the autumn of the purge." She shrugged. "Ost does dates. I do people. Between us we're better than a spy."

Yorick looked at the sword in his lap. Then, because lying to these two felt like lying to weather, he said: "Yes. Marrowfield. I held the gate."

"And now?"

"Now I carry a bedroll and follow a mapmaker into hell, and if that's penance it's cheap, and if it isn't penance then I don't know what it is, and either way I'll die before I let it happen again. Is that enough for you?"

Fen was quiet for a moment. Then she said, "Ost?"

From the shadows: "He sleeps facing the exits. He gave his rations to the miller's kids at the ford without being asked. Dates and behavior say he's telling the truth." A pause. "Behavior also says he hasn't forgiven himself. That part checks out."

"He's keeping you," Fen translated, and grinned, and was gone into the dark, and Yorick sat alone by the fire with his sword clean and his chest aching in a way he almost remembered how to call hope.

* * *

On the first morning out, the moor received them without comment.

It was gray and vast and stitched with heather going rust-brown, and the wind came off it in long shoves that made conversation impossible and thinking difficult, and Astrid loved it immediately and completely, the way one loves a stranger who resembles no one.

By midday they found the hollow.

He knew it before Ost's pointing finger confirmed it—a long green seam folded into the moor's shoulder, deep enough to swallow the six of them and their mules, walled with old drover's stone, floored with centuries of beaten turf. His gift opened around it like a hand unclenching: he could feel the track that had made it, ten thousand hooves and feet over five hundred years, the groove of it sunk into the world's memory, and he could feel—fainter, stranger—an older road beneath *that*, dwarf-work, running toward the mountain like a buried vein.

"There," he said, and his voice cracked with something he refused to name. "There it is."

Ost walked the length of it, paced it, checked the sightlines from three angles, and came back wearing the closest thing to approval his face permitted. "Invisible," he conceded. "Not hidden. Your word holds."

They made camp early, and it went badly, and it went badly *together*, which Astrid would later understand was the important part. Fen strung the rope for the awning at a height suitable only for herself. Yorick dug the fire pit in the wrong place and redug it without complaint. Bramble organized the packs by a system she explained twice and abandoned when nobody grasped it. Sera cooked, silently, competently, over Yorick's relocated fire, and produced a stew from hard travel-rations that tasted like someone's actual home, and everyone ate too much of it and pretended not to be moved.

And when the light failed and the wind settled into its night-long monotone, Astrid sat apart with his journal on his knees and began, as he had every night of his adult life, to map the day.

The hollow first: its curve, its walls, the sightlines, the buried vein of the old road beneath. Ink moving easily, gift and pen working together like breath. Then the camp—the fire ring, the awning, the mule lines—and here his pen slowed, because the shapes would not resolve into objects.

He drew the fire ring and found himself drawing Yorick's hands, flat on a table, steadying. He drew the mule lines and found Fen's grin, quick as a struck match. He drew the awning line and it became Ost's nod, precise as a ledger closing. The stew-smoke became Sera's profile against the dusk. Bramble's stacked papers became the set of her jaw when she said *wonderful* and meant *terrified*.

Astrid sat back and looked down at the page and understood, with the slow vertigo of a man reading his own sentence, that he was not mapping terrain at all.

He was mapping them. The hollow was just where they happened to keep it.

His gift had always shown him the true shape of places. Nobody had warned him that people were places—that a company of strays, thrown together by edict and grief and one woman's stolen soul, had a shape too, forming already in the dark around a small stubborn fire, awkward and provisional and real.

He inked it anyway. All of it. And at the bottom of the page, where a cartographer writes the map's name, his pen hovered, and then wrote:

*Camp, first night. Population: six.*

Below it, smaller, almost ashamed:

*Home?*

He closed the book quickly, as though the question might escape, and looked up to find Sera watching him across the fire with an expression he couldn't chart at all.

"Astrid," she said quietly. "Before you sleep—you should see what the wind's carrying down from the pass."

He followed her gaze to the northern sky, where—thin and distant and unmistakable, riding the moor wind like a smell of smoke—came the sound of bells.

Bells from a direction where, according to every map he had ever drawn, there were no bells.
