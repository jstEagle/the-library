## 10. Roads That Remember

The land began to speak to Astrid on the third morning out of the valley, and it did not speak in words.

It happened first at a stream crossing, where the company stood arguing about the best fords. Yorick wanted to push north along the ridge line; Fen and Ost insisted the water was too high and they'd lose half a day going around. Astrid crouched at the bank with his boots off, testing the current with a stick, and while he waited for them to exhaust themselves he felt it—a pull, faint as a thread tugged at the back of his skull, running not across the stream but *through* it, angling downstream and then back up again at a place where the water spread wide and shallow over gravel.

He looked. Really looked, the way he had learned to look since the refugees came, since the ward began straining and his gift woke inside him like an eye unsealing after years of sleep.

The ford was there. Not a guess, not an inference from the shape of the banks—he could see it, the way he could see the true shape of everything now: a low shelf of packed stone beneath the water, worn smooth by centuries of cart wheels and cattle hooves, invisible under the surface but utterly plain to him, glowing in his mind's eye like ink held up to lamplight.

"There," he said, pointing downstream. "Forty paces past that split willow. The bottom's stone all the way across."

Ost squinted at him. "You've been here before?"

"No."

"Then how—"

"Mapmaker's instinct," Astrid said, and hated how easily the lie came now.

They crossed dry-shod. Sera went last, as she always did, watching the water instead of her feet, and when she reached the far bank she fell into step beside him and said nothing, which was its own kind of conversation between them now. She knew what he was. She was the only one who knew the whole of it—that the valley's mapmaker could read the land like a page, that every day outside the ward the reading grew clearer, deeper, stranger.

What she didn't know—what Astrid was only beginning to understand himself—was that the land read him back.

* * *

By the fifth day he could no longer pretend it was instinct.

They were climbing through hill country that no map in Odile's collection had ever covered properly—the old surveyors had marked it with the cartographer's confession, *here be broken ground*, and moved on—and Astrid found himself walking with his eyes half-closed, because closed or open made no difference. The true shape of the hills bloomed behind his lids: every fold, every hollow, every buried thing.

And there were so many buried things.

Roads, mostly. That was the revelation that kept him awake at night, staring at the canvas of the sky. Under the bracken and the sheep-cropped turf, under the landslides and the deliberate forgetfulness of centuries, the old roads still ran—king's roads and drover's roads and roads older than any kingdom, paved with stone that remembered the weight of traffic. The realm above ground was a scatter of villages and fortresses connected by wandering tracks. The realm below was a lattice, dense and purposeful, a spiderweb spun by someone who had understood the land intimately and loved it enough to work *with* its grain rather than against it.

Some of those roads led places no living person intended to go. He felt one pass beneath their camp on the fifth night—a broad way, running straight as a ruled line toward the mountain, toward the court beneath it—and where it ran, the ground hummed. Not sound. Something deeper than sound. A resonance in the soles of his feet and the roots of his teeth, like standing near a struck bell that rang too low for ears.

"You're doing it again," Fen said, appearing at his elbow with the silent suddenness the twins shared. She dropped beside him at the fire's edge. "The staring-through-things thing."

"I'm resting my eyes."

"You're listening." She said it flatly, without accusation, the way she reported weather or the position of patrols. "Yorick does the same thing before a fight. Ears you can't see working."

Astrid said nothing. Fen studied him a moment longer, then shrugged and left him to it, which was the twins' particular mercy: they noticed everything and demanded nothing.

But he sat by the fire long after the others slept, one hand flat against the earth, and listened to the road below them humming its low note toward the mountain, and understood that he was not eavesdropping on the land. He was part of its conversation now. And conversations, once joined, could be overheard.

* * *

The checkpoint proved it.

They smelled the smoke before they saw the walls—a thin bitter reek of pitch and wet ash that made Yorick go still on the trail and lift a fist, the old soldier's gesture that stopped the company like a hand on a rein. Ahead, where the valley of the River Liss narrowed between two shoulders of rock, a palisade had grown up around an old tollhouse like fungus around a dead tree. Watch-fires behind the stakes. The glint of helms. A chain stretched across the river itself, hung with bells.

"King's men," Yorick murmured. "Inquisitors' writ, by the black pennon. They're shaking down every traveler between here and Vell. Looking for—" He glanced at Astrid, and didn't finish.

"Looking for us," Sera said quietly.

"Looking for anyone," Yorick corrected. "Which is worse, for our purposes. There'll be a queue. Names taken, papers checked, questions asked by men who get paid by the finding." He chewed his lip. "We could go around. Two days north through the high fells, maybe three."

"Three days we don't have," Bramble said. She'd been counting days against some private calendar ever since the dying clerk's story about the foundry, and her voice had acquired an edge that could shave wood. "The siege train moves when the passes clear. Every day is a day closer to Vell."

"So we go through," Ost said. It wasn't quite a question.

"Through a checkpoint run by Aldric's inquisitors," Yorick said, "with a fugitive soul-seller, a runaway clerk who knows their filing systems better than their mothers do, a deserter whose face is on proclamations from here to the coast, and—" he tipped his chin at Astrid— "whatever *that* is. Yes. Splendid plan."

"It's the only plan," Astrid said.

Everyone looked at him. He realized he'd spoken with a certainty that hadn't come from him—or rather, that had come from a deeper part of him than the part that was afraid. He closed his eyes, and let himself look.

The true shape of the checkpoint unfolded behind his eyelids. Palisade, gate, watch-fires, the tollhouse with its cellar full of impounded goods. But underneath—underneath, the old lattice. A road. One of the ancient ones, deep-buried here, running beneath the river and the palisade both, surfacing again half a mile beyond the checkpoint in a cutting choked with alder. And branching from it, nearer, a smaller way—an old drove-road, sunken so far into the earth that it had become a trench, roofed in places by hawthorn, invisible from ten paces away. It came out inside the trees on the near side of the narrows and rejoined the king's road well past the chain and the bells.

There was more. As he listened, the way a man learns to hear individual instruments in an orchestra, he felt the checkpoint's own small magics: a ward-stone set into the gatepost, tuned to prickle at the passage of the gifted. It had a texture, that stone—a sour, grasping note, like a fishhook of sound. Anyone with a gift would feel it snag them as they passed through the gate.

But the drove-road bypassed the gate entirely. And the hook, Astrid realized, could be muffled—not destroyed, he didn't have that kind of power, but *covered*, the way a hand cupped over a bell's mouth kills its ring. If he could fold the land's own noise over the ward-stone for the few minutes they needed—

"Astrid?" Sera's voice, close. His eyes were open. He didn't remember opening them. His hands were trembling, and the firelight seemed to bend slightly toward him, the flames leaning like grass in wind.

"How fast can everyone walk quietly?" he asked.

* * *

They went at dusk, single file, into a green trench in the earth that the others clearly couldn't see the point of until they were in it and it had swallowed them whole.

The sunken drove-road was a corridor of shadow and hawthorn, the branches meeting overhead so thickly that the last light came down in coins. Old hoof-worn stone underfoot, soft with moss. Astrid led, though he had never been here, because the road told him its turns before they came: a bend left, a fallen oak to climb, a stretch where the trench flooded ankle-deep and they waded without complaint. Behind him he heard Bramble breathing the quick breaths of a woman cataloguing exits, and Yorick's boots moving with a soldier's economy, and the twins making no sound at all, which was somehow louder than sound.

Halfway through, the ward-stone's note rose ahead of them—sour, searching, a fishhook dragging through the water of the world, feeling for gifted flesh.

Astrid stopped. Put his palm against the earthen wall of the trench. And pushed.

Not with muscle. With attention. He gathered the land's own hum—the deep patient note of the buried road, the slow chord of root and stone and water—and drew it up and over the ward-stone like a blanket drawn over a lamp. For a moment the world *thickened*. Sera, just behind him, made a small involuntary sound, and he felt her hand close on the back of his belt, gripping hard, anchoring herself against whatever it was she could feel happening.

The sour note went quiet. Muffled. Blind.

"Move," Astrid whispered, and his voice came out strange, resonant, as if the road itself were speaking through his mouth.

They passed within forty paces of the watch-fires. Through a gap in the hawthorn Astrid glimpsed them: soldiers dicing, an inquisitor in grey reading papers by lantern-light, the black pennon hanging limp in the windless dusk. So close. The nearest sentry turned his head as they passed, frowned at the hedge, at nothing, and turned away. Above them, muffled under Astrid's blanket of silence, the ward-stone slept.

Then the trench bent, climbed, and opened, and they stepped out into ordinary twilight on the far side of the narrows, and Astrid's legs simply declined to hold him anymore.

He sat down hard on the verge of the king's road, gasping, and the world roared back into its ordinary register—no more buried harmonies, no more true shapes, just dusk and road and the small sounds of his companions regrouping around him. His nose was bleeding. He wiped it on his sleeve and found his hands wouldn't stop shaking.

"That," Yorick said, looking back the way they'd come, then at Astrid, then back again, "should not have worked."

"But it did," Sera said. She knelt, took his chin in her fingers, tilted his face to the light, inspected the nosebleed with a professional detachment that reminded him painfully of Odile. "How much did it cost you?"

"A nosebleed."

"Liar."

"A nosebleed," Astrid repeated, "and something else I don't have words for yet." Which was true, and more frightening than the nosebleed. Because in the moment when he had pulled the land's song over the ward-stone, he had felt the song *notice* him. Felt the road beneath their feet attend to his passing the way a dog attends to a whistle. And felt—faint, distant, at the very edge of perception—other whistles answering.

* * *

They made camp two miles on, in a hollow Bramble chose with a clerk's instinct for defensible ground, and Astrid lay awake listening to the aftermath of what he'd done.

Ripples. That was the only word. When he used the gift—really used it, not the passive seeing but the active reaching—it moved through the world like a stone through still water. He could feel his own ripples spreading outward through the lattice of old roads, ringing along them like fingers along harp strings, diminishing with distance but not, he suspected, ever quite dying. And here and there across the darkened map of the realm, other notes stirred in answer. Other gifted folk, feeling the water move. Some merely waking, puzzled. Some, he was sure, paid to notice. Paid to triangulate.

An inquisitor's network, threaded through the land like the roads themselves. Every use of his gift was a shout in a canyon, and somewhere in that canyon were ears trained for exactly this sound.

He should stop using it. That was the rational conclusion, and he arrived at it cleanly, and then he lay in the dark and understood that he wouldn't. Because tomorrow the road forked, and one branch was watched, and the other existed only in the buried lattice that only he could perceive. Because the day after, the ford at Marle would be flooded or it wouldn't, and thirty lives might depend on knowing which. Because Sera was thinning by the mile, forgetting her mother's face a little more with each league toward the mountain, and every hour mattered, and he possessed the only instrument in the world that could buy hours.

Power as intimacy, he thought. The land had opened itself to him the way a lover opens a door—wholly, trustingly, asking only that he not pretend he hadn't seen. And every time he reached back, the whole landscape shivered with the touch. There was no discreet way to love a country. There was no whispering with the world. You either lived at mapmaker's distance, safe and ignorant, or you put your hands on the living earth and let the earth announce it.

He thought of Odile's warning at the valley's edge, her old compass heavy in his pocket: *Outside, your gift will be both weapon and beacon.* He had nodded as if he'd understood. He hadn't. You couldn't understand a beacon until you'd been lit.

Near midnight, footsteps. Sera settling beside him at the cold edge of the fire, wrapped in her cloak, her eyes on the stars.

"I felt it," she said. "When you did it. At the checkpoint." Her voice was careful, the voice of someone handling a memory that might cut. "Like the ground took a breath. Like something enormous rolled over in its sleep." She paused. "And then—farther off. Something answered. Did you feel it?"

"Yes."

"What was it?"

"I don't know. Other gifted people. Or things that listen for gifted people." He turned his head to look at her. In the starlight her face was thinner than it had been in the valley, the cheekbones sharper, the eyes larger, as if she were being slowly edited down to essentials. "I can't use this gift quietly, Sera. Every time I reach for the land, it rings. And someone out there is listening for the bell."

She was silent a while. Then: "So we move faster."

"That's one option."

"And the other?"

"The other is that I keep ringing the bell and we make sure that when they come, we're somewhere of our choosing." He heard himself say it and marveled a little. The man who had spent thirty years mapping places he'd never dared visit was planning ambushes by starlight. "Terrain favors whoever knows it best. No one alive knows it better than I do now."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. "That's either courage or hubris."

"In my experience," Astrid said, "the difference is usually decided afterward, by historians."

Something that was almost a laugh escaped her—brief, rusty, real. She pulled her cloak tighter. "Get some sleep, cartographer. Tomorrow you're going to show us a road that doesn't exist."

"It exists," Astrid said. "It's just been waiting a long time for someone to ask."

He closed his eyes and let the land sing to him, and far away across the dark, patient and numerous, the listeners sang back.
