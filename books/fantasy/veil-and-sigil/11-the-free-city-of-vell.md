## 11. The Free City of Vell

The road to Vell announced itself long before the city appeared—a smell of tallow and hot iron riding the wind, then the sound, a low continuous hum like a hive the size of the horizon. Sera heard it first from the crest of a hill where the old road bent around a shrine to some forgotten river-god, its stone face worn smooth as a thumbprint.

"That's not a city," Fen said beside her, squinting down into the haze. "That's an argument."

"It's a market," Bramble corrected, adjusting her satchel straps with the fussy precision of a woman who had once inventoried souls for a living and had never quite stopped counting things. "The largest between here and the coast. Which means it's also the most watched."

Sera said nothing. She was watching the smoke rise from Vell's ten thousand chimneys—gray threads climbing into a sky the color of unpolished pewter—and she was thinking about checkpoints. About the last time she'd walked toward a walled city with her family's lives folded into her pockets like stolen letters. About what it had cost her then, and what she no longer had left to pay with.

Her hands were steady. That was the strange part. Somewhere in the months since the ford, since she'd spoken aloud the thing she'd sworn never to speak, the trembling had gone out of her. Not because the wound had closed—it hadn't; it wept in her dreams every night, her mother's face dissolving like sugar in rain—but because a wound that is witnessed stops being a secret, and secrets are heavier than wounds.

Astrid came up the hill behind them, sketchbook already open against his forearm, pencil moving. He didn't look at the city the way the others did. He looked *through* it, or under it, his eyes going slightly unfocused in that way she'd learned meant he was seeing the true shape of things—the buried roads, the old foundations, the bones beneath the skin of the world.

"Three gates open," he murmured, half to himself. "The river gate's been bricked up for sixty years but they've reopened it. They're expecting... traffic." His pencil paused. "Or refugees."

"They're expecting taxes," Yorick said from behind, his voice flat as slate. The disgraced soldier stood with his arms crossed, watching the walls the way other men watched wolves. "Vell's council will charge you for the air if they can find a way to weigh it."

Ost laughed, high and bright, and Fen elbowed him quiet. The twins moved like weather, always half a step out of phase with everyone else, and Sera had come to find their strangeness comforting—the way a lighthouse is comforting precisely because it stands where nothing else can.

"We go in small," Astrid said, closing the book. "Sera, Bramble, and me for supplies. Yorick, you keep watch outside with the twins—if anyone follows us off the Gray Stair, I want to know before they know we know."

Yorick's jaw tightened. Since his confession at the fire—*I burned names off lists for Aldric before I burned the lists themselves*—he had taken every task that put distance between himself and the group, as though penance were a ration to be measured out. Sera caught his eye and held it until he nodded. It was not forgiveness. It was something more useful: an agreement to be honest about not being whole.

They went down toward the hum.

* * *

Vell swallowed them the way cities do—not all at once but in increments, gate-wardens and toll-clerks and then the press of bodies, the noise folding over them like water. The market district was a canyon of awnings and shouting: pepper and salt fish, dyed wool the color of bruises, caged birds whose songs had been trained into coin-counting rhythms. A woman sold charms against the King's Men from a tray like sweetmeats, each one a knotted red thread, and did brisk business.

Sera kept her hood up and her eyes moving. This was the skill the road had taught her, the one skill she'd brought out of her burning village intact: how to walk through danger without appearing to notice it. How to be furniture. How to be nobody.

Except she wasn't nobody anymore. That was the problem with honesty—it followed you.

Bramble led them through the crowd with unnerving confidence, turning down alleys Sera would have sworn were dead ends and finding them open, greeting a fishwife by name, haggling in three currencies. The ex-clerk had a past in Vell, it emerged, layer by layer, the way a map emerges under a wash of ink.

"You worked here," Sera said, catching up to her at a stall where a man sold secondhand boots by the pair and the lie by the dozen.

"I *filed* here," Bramble said. "Before the mountain. Before—" She made a small gesture that encompassed everything: the ledger, the theft of herself, the long flight south. "Cities are archives, Sera. Everything that happens gets written down somewhere, by someone who thought it was boring. Boring records are the ones that survive." She bought dried figs, counted change twice. "Which is why we're not just shopping."

Astrid, who had been drifting with his sketchbook half-raised like a pilgrim's token, looked up. "No?"

"No. We're reading." Bramble's eyes, usually so mild, had gone sharp as a clerk's on audit day. "You wanted proof Aldric's armies are coming. Vell is where proof comes to be sold. Follow me, and touch nothing, and don't look at anyone longer than they look at you."

* * *

What Bramble showed them over the next three hours was a city telling on itself.

It was in the granaries first: queues too long, sacks stamped with the seal of the coastal leagues—grain being bought up, hoarded, priced for war rather than winter. It was in the smithies along the river wall, running triple shifts, the ringing of hammers layered so thick it became a single note. It was in the hiring halls, where notices offered silver for barge-men willing to run cargo north at night, and more silver for those who asked no questions. It was in the temple of the Ledger-Saint, where Bramble paused in the doorway and read, upside down and across the nave, the tally-board of recent burials: fever, fever, fever, *accident*, fever—and then, three times in one week, a word Sera didn't know.

"*Conscription fever*," Bramble said quietly. "That's what they're calling it. It isn't fever. It's what happens when the King's provosts come through a village and take every able body between fifteen and fifty, and the ones who resist get a sword-point instead of a shilling." She turned away from the board. "My brother died of conscription fever. Before I ever saw the mountain."

Sera touched her arm. It was all either of them could bear to give or receive, that touch, and it was enough.

But it was in the harbor quarter, at dusk, that the proof stopped being circumstantial and became a wound.

Bramble took them to a tavern called the Drowned Bell, low-ceilinged and loud, where sailors drank and, more importantly, where clerks drank—minor functionaries of the free cities' councils, men and women with ink-stained fingers and grievances, drinking away their days and occasionally, for the price of a bottle, their discretion. Bramble knew which table. Bramble always knew which table.

She came back twenty minutes later with a face like paper and sat down heavily across from Sera and Astrid.

"Well?" Astrid asked.

"The foundry," Bramble said. Her voice was very controlled, the control itself a kind of screaming. "The siege foundry. The one marching north along the old salt road. It's real. It's bigger than the rumors. And it's fast—faster than anything that size should move, because it doesn't rest, doesn't eat, doesn't mutiny. It runs on—" She stopped. Looked at Sera. Looked away.

"On fuel," Sera said.

"On fuel," Bramble agreed softly.

Around them the tavern roared on, oblivious, and Sera sat with the words settling into her like stones into a pond. She had known this, in the abstract. The dying clerk in the valley—old Master Corvin, coughing his life out among strangers—had told them the shape of it: every soul in the ledger bound to an engine, every engine bound to Aldric's ambition. But abstract knowledge is a coat you can take off. This was different. This was the foundry given a name and a road and a schedule, marching north with her mother's face—or the memory of her mother's face, the thinning remnant of it—burning in its belly.

"How far north?" she heard herself ask.

"A week's march from the Vell border. Ten days from the river crossings." Bramble spread her hands flat on the table, steadying them or the table, hard to say. "And here's the part that matters. The free cities have been squabbling for a year about whether to federate, whether to arm, whether Aldric is even their problem yet. Vell's council has the votes to call a defense league—all seven cities, pooled garrisons, sealed the river. But they won't act without certainty. Without *proof*. Politics." She spat the word like a pit. "They'll debate while the foundry walks, and they'll lose."

Astrid was staring into his untouched cup. When he spoke, his voice had the careful evenness he used when his gift was close to the surface. "What would proof look like? To a council?"

"Ledgers," Bramble said. "Always ledgers. Dates, tonnage, troop counts, supply contracts. Something with the mountain's own seals on it, that can't be dismissed as rumor or refugee hysteria." She gave a bleak little laugh. "The court beneath the mountain keeps perfect books. It's the one virtue of evil: it loves documentation."

Sera felt it then—not a thought arriving but a truth, the way you feel a door standing open in a dark house. Cold air on your skin before you understand why.

Her soul was in that ledger.

Her name, her price, her binding—written in the court's own hand, sealed with the court's own authority, feeding the foundry with the court's own confession attached. Every document that proved Aldric's war machine ran on stolen souls would necessarily, inseparably, contain the record of *her*. Of what she'd sold. Of what she'd paid.

The thing she had hidden longest, guarded closest, starved herself to conceal—

It was the weapon. It was the only weapon that couldn't be forged, because it was written in the enemy's own hand.

"Sera?" Astrid's voice, gentle. "You've gone somewhere."

"I've gone north," she said. "To the mountain." She pulled her hood back—deliberately, in the middle of the crowded tavern, feeling the strange lightness of it—and looked at both of them. "You've been planning to steal my soul back. Quietly. A heist, a strike-through, in and out. For me." Her throat tightened and she pushed through it. "But it was never only mine. Every name in that ledger belongs to someone's mother, someone's brother. Corvin. Bramble's—" she glanced at Bramble, who had gone very still, "—everyone. And the same ledger that holds us prisoner holds the proof that could save the free cities. We don't need to choose between saving me and saving them. The ledger is the key to both doors. It always was."

Astrid was looking at her with an expression she was still learning to read—not pity, never pity, thank all the quiet gods, but recognition. Two damaged people mapping each other. "The risk changes," he said. "If we take the ledger, we can't just slip away with it. It has to reach the councils. All of them. Publicly."

"Yes."

"And Aldric will know exactly who struck the entry. Exactly where to send everything he has."

"Yes," Sera said again, and found she was almost smiling. "Good. Let him spend his strength chasing the girl he thinks he owns. I intend to be very expensive to catch."

* * *

They left the Drowned Bell after full dark, when the lamps along the harbor made gold scars on the black water. Astrid walked beside her, silent for three streets, and then said, without preamble:

"You know what you just did."

"Do I?"

"In the tavern. You took the worst thing that ever happened to you and you turned it outward. Made it ammunition." He shook his head slowly. "I've spent my whole life hiding what I am because I thought visibility was death. You just chose it. On purpose. In a room full of strangers."

Sera watched a lantern-barge slide past on the river, its wake unspooling behind it like a thread being drawn from a spool. "I didn't choose it tonight," she said. "I chose it the day I signed. Everything since has just been me catching up to what it cost." She looked at him. "Your turn's coming, cartographer. The mountain's going to ask you for a map of yourself, and it won't accept rumor or approximation. It never does."

Ahead, at the river gate where they'd agreed to regroup, Yorick detached himself from a shadow and fell into step, and behind him the twins materialized the way they always did, grinning, mud-caked, carrying news in their matched voices:

"Riders on the north road," Fen said.

"King's colors," said Ost. "A dozen. Moving slow. Asking questions at the farms."

Asking questions. Looking for someone. The ripples Astrid's gift sent through the world, perhaps—or simply the net drawing tighter everywhere at once, the way nets do.

Sera felt the cold air of that open door again, and this time she understood it completely. There was no path home that avoided the mountain. There was only the path through it, and the ledger waiting at the bottom with all their names in its patient hand.

"Then we leave before dawn," she said, "and we stop being refugees."

She turned her face north, toward the dark shoulder of the world where the mountain stood, and began—to her own astonishment—to plan.
