## 12. Yorick's Confession

The fire was Astrid's doing—not the flame itself, but the hollow he'd chosen for it, a cup of stone in the hillside where the wind couldn't reach and the light wouldn't spill past the first bend in the road. Yorick had watched him do it without touching the ground, without pacing anything off, just standing there with his eyes half-closed like a man listening to a conversation happening under the earth. Then he'd said, *here*, and here had been right.

Yorick had served under men who could read terrain like that. They'd all been hanged by now, or worse.

He sat at the edge of the firelight, sharpening a knife he didn't need to sharpen, and told himself—for the hundredth time since the ford, since the twins, since the moment Bramble had looked at his soldier's hands and asked no questions—that tonight he would say nothing. That silence was a kindness. That the company slept better not knowing what their swordsman had done before he knew them.

Fen and Ost had taken first watch on the ridge, twin silhouettes against the bruised sky, so alike even in stillness that you couldn't tell which one blinked. Bramble was asleep sitting up, her ledger-hand curled around a stick she'd been sketching figures with until her eyes gave out. Sera sat across the fire from Yorick, wrapped in her cloak, feeding the flames twigs one at a time with the patience of someone who had learned that small motions were safer than large ones.

Astrid was awake too, of course. The man barely slept. He lay on his back with Odile's compass open on his chest, watching its needle do something needles weren't supposed to do—drift, hesitate, point sometimes at Sera, sometimes at the dark road behind them, as if the world had more directions than north.

"You're grinding that knife to dust," Sera said.

"It keeps my hands busy."

"Your hands have been busy since we left the valley." She fed another twig to the fire. "You haven't talked once about where you learned to fight the way you fight."

"Sera," Astrid said quietly, a warning or a permission, Yorick couldn't tell which.

"No." She didn't raise her voice. She never raised her voice; that was the thing about her, the thing that made her more frightening than any shouting woman Yorick had ever stood across from. "I sold my soul to buy my family's passage. I told you both because I had to, and because I thought—if they know the worst of me and stay, then maybe the worst isn't everything I am." Her eyes came up, gray and steady across the flames. "You've known mine for weeks. It's your turn, Yorick. Or it's the road's turn, tomorrow, when you walk away from us at the crossroads instead of saying it out loud."

The knife stopped moving.

Yorick looked into the fire and saw, the way he always did, another fire—a much larger one—burning in a market square in a town whose name he had tried for three years to forget and could not, because forgetting was a luxury the purge had taught him nobody earned.

*Tell her,* said something in his chest that might have been conscience and might only have been exhaustion.

"All right," he said. His voice came out rough, like a door long unopened. "All right. But let me start at the beginning, because if I start in the middle you'll put a knife in me, and you'd be right to."

Sera didn't smile. Astrid sat up slowly, the compass forgotten in his lap.

"I was born in Harrowmere," Yorick said. "East of Vell, past the salt marshes. My father was a tanner. Smelled like death and made a living from it, which should have taught me something early about how the world works." He turned the knife over in his hands. "I joined the king's army at sixteen because the alternative was the tannery, and because they gave you a red coat and everyone looks at you differently in a red coat. Even the girls who used to hold their noses."

"That's not the confession," Sera said.

"No. That's the prologue." He swallowed. "When Aldric took the throne—when he *took* it, there's no polite word for what happened—the army got new orders. New officers. Men who wore gray instead of red, and carried books instead of banners. Inquisitors. And the orders were simple enough that even a tanner's son could follow them: lists of names. Houses marked with chalk. Anyone named on the list, anyone found behind a chalked door, was to be taken to the provincial seat for assessment." He heard the word *assessment* come out of his mouth in the officer's flat voice, and hated it. "That's what they called it. Assessment. Like weighing grain."

The fire popped. On the ridge, one of the twins shifted but did not turn around. Yorick wondered if they were listening. He decided he hoped they were.

"For two years I followed those orders," he said. "Two years. I told myself it was just following, that the chalk wasn't my hand, that the dragging wasn't my arms. I was good at telling myself things. It's a soldier's whole education, really—how to tell yourself things." His jaw worked. "There was a town called Miren's Ford. Little place, river town, nothing in it but fishermen and a shrine nobody important ever visited. There was a name on our list. A woman. Gifted, they said—she could find lost things. Keys, rings, children. The town loved her for it."

He stopped. Sera waited. Astrid had gone very still, the way he went still when he was reading something invisible.

"We surrounded the house at dawn," Yorick said. "Standard procedure. And she came out the front door with her hands up, and she was calm, calmer than any of us, and she looked at me—I was the one holding the list—and she said, *you're going to feel this later. I can see it. You're going to carry this.*" He laughed, a short ugly sound with nothing in it. "Gifts. Everyone thinks gifts are lightning and flying. Hers was seeing what people would become. And she wasted it on me, on a gray-coat with a list, and she was *right*, and I still took her."

"What happened to her?" Sera asked. Her voice was gentle, which was somehow worse.

"The assessment," Yorick said. "She didn't pass it. None of them passed it. Do you understand? Nobody passed. Two years, four hundred assessments, and I never once saw anyone walk back out. And every morning I put on the coat again, because the alternative was asking questions, and asking questions in Aldric's army gets you assessed too." He set the knife down at last, deliberately, the way you'd disarm yourself. "Miren's Ford broke me, though. Not the woman—though her, yes—but after. We burned the shrine. Orders. Nothing in it but candles and a wooden saint, and we burned it anyway, and an old man threw himself into the doorway to stop us, and I—" His breath caught. He made himself finish. "I moved him aside. Gently. That was my great act of rebellion. I moved him *gently*. And the shrine burned, and we marched out, and three days later I deserted. Walked off watch, left the coat folded on my bunk like a good soldier, and ran west."

Silence held the little hollow. The fire ate down a log and settled.

"So that's what I am," Yorick said. "Not a deserter. Deserting was the least of it. I'm a man who spent two years being the hand that other men's fear reached through, and I liked the coat, and I told myself stories, and when the stories stopped working I ran instead of turning around. I joined this company—" he gestured at the sleeping clerk, the silent twins, the mapmaker, the hollow-eyed woman who had sold herself to save her family, "—because I heard you were going toward the mountain, and I thought maybe there was a direction a man could walk where the walking paid something back. Penance, if you want the church word. I don't know if I believe in penance. I believe in carrying weight. I know how to do that."

He looked up. Sera was watching him with an expression he couldn't read, and it terrified him more than any tribunal ever had.

"There," he said. "Now you know. If you want me gone at first light, I'll go. I'll even point you down the safe road first—it's the one Astrid won't take, the wide one through the toll-lands, and yes, I know the checkpoints on it, because I helped build two of them." He bared his teeth at himself. "Everything I know is useful and everything I know is filthy. Welcome to me."

Astrid spoke first, softly. "The checkpoints. You could draw them?"

"I could draw them in my sleep. I drew half of them awake."

"Then you're not leaving," Astrid said simply, and turned the compass over in his fingers, and said nothing else, because that was Astrid—all verdict, no ceremony.

But it wasn't Astrid's decision. They all knew whose it was.

Sera rose. She crossed the fire's circle and stood over Yorick, and he made himself look up at her, made himself receive whatever judgment was coming, because he had decided somewhere in the last hour that receiving it was the whole point of having spoken.

"My mother had a saying," she said. "*A debt confessed is a debt you can carry together. A debt hidden carries you.*" She crouched down to his level, forearms on her knees, close enough that he could see the fine tired lines around her eyes, the places where the thinning had begun to show—she was slightly translucent at the edges, these days, like paper held up to light, though only Astrid seemed able to see it. "You took a woman who could see what people would become. What did she see, Yorick? When she looked at you?"

"She saw this," he said. "This. Firelight. Confession. Carrying weight."

"Then she's still finding lost things," Sera said. "She found you a use."

His throat closed. He nodded, because words had failed him entirely.

"But hear me," Sera went on, and her voice hardened into something that belonged on a throne or a battlefield, some register she'd clearly inherited from people tougher than her slight frame suggested. "We are five days from territory where a wrong word at a checkpoint kills all of us. So from tonight, truth is our law. Not comfort—truth. If you remember a checkpoint, you say it. If you remember a face, a name, an order—you say it, even if it shames you, especially if it shames you. Bramble's ledgers, Astrid's maps, your lists—we pool everything, and we let the truth be ugly, because the pretty version is what Aldric counts on. People hiding their worst parts until the worst parts get them killed." She straightened. "Can you live under that law?"

"I've been dying outside it for three years," Yorick said. "Yes."

"Then get some sleep. You're drawing checkpoints at dawn."

She turned back toward her bedroll, and Yorick watched her go, and felt something in his chest that he hadn't felt since Miren's Ford—not absolution, nothing so clean, but a loosening, like a knot worked at by patient fingers. She hadn't forgiven him. She'd *employed* him. Somehow that was worth more.

Astrid lingered by the fire, coaxing it low. As Yorick rolled into his blanket, the mapmaker spoke without looking over.

"The woman from Miren's Ford. The one who saw what people would become." A pause. "Her name would have gone into a ledger too. Somewhere under the mountain."

Yorick stared at the dark. The thought landed in him like a stone dropped down a well—long falling, deep water waiting.

"Her name was Maerin," he said finally. "Maerin of the Ford. If there's a book down there with her in it..."

"Then we'll know where to look," Astrid said. "Sleep, Yorick. Tomorrow you make maps of the enemy."

Across the hollow, Sera lay awake, and none of them saw her hand pressed flat against her sternum, over the hollow place where her soul had been, holding it the way you'd hold a wound—or a promise.

And far to the east, though none of them knew it yet, the first gray riders crossed the border-marks at a trot, following ripples in the land that only the gifted could feel—ripples that had started, five nights ago, around a campfire in a cup of stone, where a man had finally told the truth.

* * *

Dawn came gray and cold. Yorick knelt by the dead fire with a charred stick and a strip of bark-cloth, drawing checkpoints while the others watched, and discovered that the truth, laid out in lines, weighed less than he had feared and cut deeper than he had imagined.

At the third mark he drew, his hand stopped.

"This one," he said slowly. "The garrison at Thornhollow Pass. It shouldn't be manned yet. It wasn't finished when I ran." He looked up at Astrid, and saw the same cold understanding rising in the mapmaker's face. "Someone's been building these ahead of schedule. Someone knew a company like ours was coming."

Behind them, on the ridge, Ost's whistle sounded—one note, sharp, the signal for riders.

Three notes meant many.
