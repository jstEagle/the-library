## 13. Ambush on the Gray Stair

**Fen**

The Gray Stair had been a road once. Fen could feel that much of it through her boots—the old paving sunk under centuries of scree, the ghost of a guardrail worn to nubs of iron, and beneath all of it, faint as a heartbeat heard through a wall, the hum of the way-stones that had lit travelers home in the days when this pass belonged to a kingdom that no longer existed.

She crouched at the lip of the cliff road and let the mountain speak to her the way she'd learned to listen as a child, flat against her grandmother's roof, counting soldiers by the sound of their breathing in the street below.

Ost crouched beside her, mirror-twin still, though three weeks of hard road had put new lines around his eyes that she didn't have yet. He held up four fingers. Then, after a long moment, folded one down and raised it again. *Four. No—five.*

Five riders, then, moving slow along the switchback below, wrapped in gray cloaks the color of the stone they climbed. Not soldiers. Soldiers rode like men who wanted to arrive. These five rode like men who wanted to *find* something, heads tilted, the way you'd walk a house you suspected of hiding a thief.

"They've stopped," Ost breathed. "The lead one is doing something with his hands."

Fen watched. The rider dismounted, knelt, and pressed his palm flat against the old way-stone at the bend of the road. Even from here, even without her brother's sharper senses, Fen saw the stone answer—a pulse of pale light running up the Stair like a shiver up a spine, passing under their very feet, and going on. Going back the way they'd come. Going toward the hollow where the company slept.

Going toward Astrid.

"Ripples," she whispered. The word Bramble used, when she talked about what the gift did to the land—how every time Astrid read a road or eased a slope, it rang like a struck bell, and somewhere in the world there were always ears made for listening. "They're reading his ripples."

Ost was already moving, low and fast along the cliff edge, and Fen went with him because she always went with him, because they were two halves of one instinct and the instinct said: *warn them, run, warn them.*

They made it two hundred paces before the light found them.

It came up out of the way-stone at their feet without warning—a column of white that didn't burn but *named*, that poured over Fen like cold water and knew her, knew the shape of her, knew she was gifted, knew she was *here*. She heard Ost shout her name. She heard the riders below spur their horses. And she heard, clearest of all, a voice like dry paper speaking out of the light itself:

*Found.*

She ran anyway. It was the only prayer she knew.

* * *

**Astrid**

Astrid woke with the map burning behind his eyes.

He'd been dreaming of the valley—of Odile's kitchen, of ink drying in rows, of the ward's low hum that had been the sound of safety his whole life—and then the dream tore open and he was seeing the Gray Stair from above, from everywhere, the way he saw all places now whether he wanted to or not: the switchbacks coiled like dropped rope, the scree fields, the five points of gray climbing, and the light.

He came upright in the dark of the camp and the world came with him, laid out in lines only he could see. Sera sat up across the dead fire, already reaching for her knife. Yorick was on his feet with a hand on his sword hilt, listening. Bramble clutched her satchel of stolen clerk's knowledge to her chest like a child.

"The Stair," Astrid said. His voice came out wrong, thin. "They're on the Stair. They've marked Fen and Ost—they're coming up."

"How long?" Yorick asked, and Astrid hated that he was the one who had to answer, hated the gift that made him a compass and a target in the same breath.

"Minutes. The horses are spent from the climb; the riders will be faster on foot at the top. But—" He closed his eyes and the map sharpened, cruel and beautiful. "There's a second group. Below the first. They fanned out at midnight. We're in the fold of a page, and they're closing the book."

Sera crossed to him in three strides and took his face in her hands, and her eyes—her thinned, half-sold, stubbornly surviving eyes—were furious and afraid in equal measure. "Then we don't take the road. Tell me where there isn't a road."

"There isn't anywhere," he said. "That's what I'm trying to tell you. That's what I've been trying to tell all of you since Vell. Every path out of this fold, they've thought of it first, because they think the way I think. They read ripples. They—" His breath caught. Because he saw it then, in the map behind his eyes, the thing he'd been circling for three weeks and refusing to name: the way his gift bent the land's truth whenever he touched it, the way the bends lingered, the way the inquisitors followed those lingering bends like hounds following blood.

"It's me," he said quietly. "I led them here. Every shortcut, every buried way—I've been writing our trail in letters only they can read."

Yorick spat into the ashes. "Then we split. Some of us make noise east, you take Sera west—"

"No." Sera's hands were still on Astrid's face. She hadn't looked away from him once. "We don't leave anyone. That's the whole point of us. That's the only thing we have that Aldric doesn't." She turned her head, and her voice carried across the camp, across the sleeping mules, across the dark. "Fen and Ost aren't back yet."

No one answered, which was answer enough.

Astrid stood, and the mountain stood with him—all its hidden angles, all its buried bones, the whole true shape of the Gray Stair unfolded in his mind like a letter he'd finally been given permission to open. He had spent his life mapping places from a distance, drawing worlds he'd never touch, safe behind the valley's ward and the valley's silence. The ward was broken. The silence was broken. And Fen was running up a cliff road with the light of an inquisitor's spell pouring off her like water off a duck that had never learned to swim.

"Get everyone to the narrows," he said. "The chokepoint where the road cuts through the spine of the ridge. You can hold two people there, maybe three. I'm going to get the twins."

"You don't even know if they're alive," Yorick said.

"I know exactly where they are," Astrid said, and the terrible thing, the thing he would remember afterward with shame and wonder in equal measure, was that it felt *good* to say it. "Both of them. Their hearts are beating. Move."

* * *

**Sera**

Sera had sold her soul in a room full of candlelight and courtesy, and she had learned something in that room that she carried now like a stone in her shoe: that evil prefers paperwork, that horror wears a clerk's calm, that the worst moments of your life often proceed at the pace of a signature drying.

So she recognized the shape of what was coming up the Gray Stair, because it moved the same way. Unhurried. Certain. Five gray figures ascending the switchbacks at a clerk's pace, because they believed the outcome was already recorded.

She lay flat at the lip of the narrows with Yorick beside her and Bramble behind, and below her, forty feet down the cliff face where the road bent, she could see the first of them come around the rock with a lantern that gave off no warmth—only that naming-light, sweeping the stone, tasting it.

And pinned against the cliff wall twenty feet above the road, wedged into a crack no wider than a coffin, she saw Fen. Alive. One arm hanging wrong. Ost above her, holding her wrist, both of them frozen because any movement sent pebbles rattling down into the lantern's reach.

"Sera." Yorick's voice was barely air. "There's too many. We hold the narrows, we die tired. We run, we die apart. Give me another option."

She had none. She had a knife, a stolen soul, and a strategic understanding of siege logistics, and none of it mattered on a cliff in the dark against men whose weapons were made of names.

Then the lantern-light swept upward, and found Astrid.

He was standing on the road. Standing *in the open*, between the twins' crack in the cliff and the advancing line of gray, with his hands empty and his coat flapping in the wind that funneled up the Stair, and Sera's heart stopped—not with fear, though there was that, but with recognition. She knew that stance. She'd worn it herself, in a candlelit room, the day she signed. It was the stance of someone about to pay a price they've already decided is worth it.

"Inquisitor Vael," Astrid called down, and his voice carried impossibly far, riding the wind like it had been invited to. "You've been following my ripples for three hundred miles. Would you like to see one up close?"

* * *

**Astrid**

Later—much later, feverish, half-drowned in landscape—he would try to describe what he did on the Gray Stair, and he would fail, because the words for it lived in the same country as the words for grief.

Here is what he could say: he had spent his life seeing the true shape of places. And the true shape of the Gray Stair, seen all the way down, past the paving and the scree and the bones of the mountain itself, was not fixed. It was *negotiable*. The mountain remembered being smaller. The cliff remembered being a seam of stone not yet split. The road remembered ten thousand possible routes it might have taken and didn't.

Every gifted cartographer knew the land had a true shape. None of them, so far as Astrid knew, had ever dared to ask it to consider a different one.

He reached out—not with his hands, with whatever it was that he mapped with, the sense that lived behind his eyes and under his ribs—and he took hold of the Gray Star the way he'd take hold of a sheet of paper.

And he folded it.

The world screamed. Not the people—the *world*, a groan of stone deep enough to feel in the teeth, as forty feet of cliff road bent like a page folding shut. The lead inquisitor had time to look up, had time to begin a word that might have been a spell or a prayer, and then the road beneath him rose and met the road above it, and the space between—forty feet of open air where Fen and Ost clung—simply ceased. Cliff became ledge. Chasm became corridor. The lantern fell into a gap that no longer existed and shattered against stone that had been a hundred feet lower a moment ago.

Fen dropped—three inches, onto solid ground that hadn't been there when she started falling. Ost caught her. Both of them stared at Astrid with the expressions of people who had just watched the alphabet rearrange itself.

The inquisitors were less awed. Four of them survived the fold, and they survived it angry, and they were already chanting—Vael's voice rising over the others, the naming-light gathering around his hands like a storm deciding where to strike.

Astrid folded again.

This time he folded *distance*. The narrows were two hundred yards from the bend of the road; he made them twelve steps. Sera felt it happen—felt the ground under her knees compress like a rug pushed across a floor—and then Astrid was in front of her, staggering, bleeding from the nose, catching himself on her shoulder, and the inquisitors were suddenly close enough to touch and also somehow still climbing, their advance stretched into a crawl, each stride eating inches instead of yards, because Astrid had pulled the road out from under their momentum like a tablecloth from under dishes.

"Run," he gasped. "All of you. Now. I can't— I can't hold both folds and—"

He couldn't. That was the truth of it, the cost arriving on schedule: holding the terrain bent was like holding a door closed against a flood. Blood ran freely from his nose now, from his ears. The map behind his eyes was tearing at the edges, places he knew bleeding into places he didn't, and somewhere in the white noise he heard Yorick roar, heard steel ring against the naming-light, heard Bramble screaming coordinates like a battle-hymn—

—and then a crossbow bolt, loosed wild from the crawling fray, took Fen through the side.

* * *

**Ost**

Afterward, Ost would understand that the bolt had been meant for Astrid—that Vael, half-crushed by folded stone and mad with the effort of chanting through a mouthful of broken geography, had simply fired at the brightest ripple in reach, and Fen had stepped into its path because that was what Fen did, had always done, had been born doing: standing where the blow was aimed at someone else.

But in the moment there was no understanding. There was only his sister folding against him with a feathered shaft standing out below her ribs, only the wet heat spreading through his hands, only his own voice making sounds he didn't recognize as words.

The fold collapsed. He felt it go—felt the mountain snap back to its honest shape with a crack like the sky clearing its throat—and the inquisitors came flooding up the restored road, and Astrid was on his knees in the middle of it, empty-handed, spent, a beacon with no light left in it.

Ost knelt over Fen and did the arithmetic of despair: five enemies, one broken cartographer, one soldier, one thief, one clerk, one girl with a stolen soul, and his sister dying under his hands on a road named for the color of ash.

Then the ash moved.

It came up the Stair from below—from the direction of the second group, the closing net—and it wasn't five figures, it was dozens, it was a tide, and Ost's heart broke a second time before his eyes finished the count and told him the truth: these weren't grays. These were ragged, armed, desperate, *free*—refugees out of Vell, out of the hill camps, out of the roads Sera's knowledge had opened, drawn by the ripples just as the inquisitors had been drawn, but drawn the way moths draw other moths. Gifted folk and their kin and their protectors, dozens strong, answering a bell they'd felt ringing for three hundred miles.

The inquisitors heard them coming. Vael, bloodied and swaying in his ruined cloak, made the calculation clerks always make, and sounded the retreat, and the gray tide flowed back down the mountain faster than it had come up.

The free folk crested the Stair into firelight and silence, and found a cartographer kneeling in his own blood beside a girl who was learning to fold the world, and a wounded scout, and a company of strays standing very still around them, waiting to find out what they had become.

* * *

**Sera**

They made camp in the lee of the fold—of the scar, rather, because the place where Astrid had bent the road would never quite lie straight again; the stone there had a new grain to it, like wood that had been bent and released. Sera sat with Fen's head in her lap while Ost worked, with shaking hands and terrifying competence, to pack the wound. The bolt had gone through flesh and not lung, by some mercy neither of them had earned. Fen was grey-lipped and conscious, and kept trying to joke, and kept failing, and kept trying.

Astrid sat apart, at the edge of the firelight, cleaning blood from his face with a scrap of cloth and looking at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger who had done something magnificent and unforgivable.

Sera settled beside him. For a while neither spoke. Below them, the free folk were posting watches, sharing bread, staring at Astrid with reverence he visibly wished would kill him.

"They'll follow you now," she said at last. "All of them. Word will spread by morning—you folded the Gray Stair, you beat Vael, you're the mapmaker who bends the world. They'll want you to lead them."

"I know." His voice was scraped raw. "I felt them coming for miles. I thought—I hoped it was more inquisitors. At least inquisitors, you can run from."

"And instead it's hope." She almost smiled. "Worse, I know."

"Sera." He turned to her, and in the firelight his eyes had the faraway shine of a man still half-lost in the map. "When I folded the road—I saw the court. Under the mountain. I wasn't looking for it, but everything's connected now, everything I touch shows me what it touches, and I saw the ledger. I saw the vault. I saw—" His jaw tightened. "I saw your name. It's written in gold, Sera. They keep the sold ones in gold."

The fire cracked. Down the slope, Fen laughed weakly at something Ost said, and the sound was the best thing Sera had heard in a year.

"Then we know where we're going," she said.

"Yes. But Sera—there's no going back from tonight. Not for any of us. What I did, they'll never stop hunting it. The valley, the old life, the safe distances—it's all burned. I burned it." He looked at his hands again. "I'm sorry."

Sera took one of those hands—still tacky with his own blood—and pressed it flat against the ground, against the scarred, bent, newly-grained stone of the Gray Stair.

"Don't apologize for redrawing the map," she said. "Somebody had to."

Above them, on the highest ridge, a signal fire bloomed—one of the free folk's, answering another far to the south, and another beyond that, a chain of small lights kindling across the dark mountains like a constellation deciding, at last, to arrange itself into a road.

Astrid watched them, and Sera watched him watch, and she saw the moment the fear in his face lost the argument—not to courage, exactly, but to necessity, which is courage's older, harder sister.

"To the mountain, then," he said quietly. "Before the foundry marches. Before they move your name."

The lights burned on, ridge to ridge, all the way toward the black bulk of the peaks where the court beneath the mountain kept its golden ledger—and somewhere in its vault, a siege foundry drank deep from a soul that Sera was coming to take back.
