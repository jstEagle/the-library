## 14. What the Gift Costs

The fever came for him in the shape of a road.

That was how it began—not with heat, not with shivering, but with the Gray Stair unspooling behind his closed eyes, climbing and climbing through the dark of his skull until he could no longer tell whether he was lying on a bedroll in a shepherd's hut or standing on that cliff again, wind screaming, Fen's blood bright on the stone. The road went on. Roads always went on. That was the first law of his gift, older than any map he'd ever drawn: every way leads somewhere, and somewhere is always farther than you think.

He was walking it now. He could feel the gravel under feet that were not moving.

"Astrid."

A voice, far off, like a bell rung underwater. He knew it. He had drawn its owner once without meaning to—the set of her shoulders, the wary angle of her jaw—back on their second night together, when he'd realized with a start that his charcoal had stopped rendering terrain and started rendering people. He tried to answer. His mouth made a sound like wind through a pass.

"Don't you dare," the voice said, closer now, and there was iron in it, the same iron that had faced down Yorick's confession and the twins' bickering and the whole grinding fear of the road. "Don't you go walking off into the ground, Astrid Joubert. I have lost enough of myself to know what losing looks like, and I am not watching you do it."

Hands on his face. Cool hands. He clung to them the way a drowning man clings to a chart of a shore he has never seen.

* * *

Time behaved badly after that. It pooled and drained and pooled again, and in the gaps between its tides he surfaced into fragments:

— A wet cloth on his brow, wrung from snowmelt, smelling faintly of the pine resin Ost chewed when she was nervous.

— Yorick's low voice beyond the curtain of the hut's back room: *How bad?* And Sera: *Bad. His eyes keep tracking things that aren't here.* And Yorick again, quieter, guilt worn smooth as river stone: *He folded a mountain for us. For Fen. Of course it took a piece of him.*

— Fen herself, propped on one elbow across the room, shoulder bound and grey-faced, insisting in a whisper that she was fine, that Astrid should stop apologizing every time he woke, that she had chosen to stand on that stair and would choose it again. He wasn't sure whether he'd heard this or dreamed it. He chose to believe it either way.

— The sensation, worst of all, of being *unmoored*. Once, waking at dusk, he looked at his own hands and could not say where they ended and the floorboards began. The wood grain ran up his fingers like veins. He thought, with a calm that frightened him more than panic would have: *this is what a river feels like, just before it reaches the sea and stops being a river.*

He understood then, dimly, what Odile's warning had meant. *Outside, your gift will be both weapon and beacon,* she'd said, pressing the old compass into his hands at the valley's edge. She had said more than that, too—he remembered now, in the fever's honesty, the thing she'd added quietly, almost unwillingly, the way one mentions a family curse: *And it will spend you, boy. Every map costs ink. You've never had to pay before.*

He had paid on the Gray Stair. He had taken the cliff road—a road with a bend in it, a hard and murderous bend where the inquisitors waited—and he had *folded* it, pulled its two ends together like a sheet, so that the company stepped from one side of the ambush to the other in a single stride while the inquisitors' crossbow bolts buried themselves in stone. He had felt the land resist, felt it groan like an old ship, felt something tear loose inside himself to pay for the passage. Then he had walked them three days further by ways only he could see, because stopping meant dying, and the bill had come due all at once, in a shepherd's hut above a valley whose name he suddenly could not remember.

His own valley. The one he'd been born in. He could not remember its name.

That was when he started to scream, or thought he did. Perhaps it was only in the road, in the fever, in the place where he was still walking.

* * *

Sera sat with him through the worst of it.

She learned the rhythm of it quickly, the way she seemed to learn everything—by watching, by enduring, by refusing to look away. When his eyes went distant and began tracking nothing, she talked to him. Not comfort, exactly. She talked about ordinary things, deliberately ordinary, things with weight and edges: the price of lamp oil in Vell, the exact number of stairs in the temple district (four hundred and eleven; Bramble had counted), the way Yorick snored in two movements, like a saw finishing a cut. She anchored him to particulars because she knew—she alone among them truly knew—what it was to feel yourself dissolving out of the world's inventory.

On the fourth night, or perhaps the fifth, he surfaced long enough to know where he was and who held the cup of willow-bark tea to his lips, and he said, in a voice scraped raw:

"I couldn't remember the valley's name."

Sera didn't flinch. She set the cup down. "I know."

"It's where I was born. I've mapped half the realm from letters and rumor and I couldn't—" His breath caught. "It's gone, Sera. Like a page torn out. What else is gone?"

"I don't know," she said honestly. "We'll find out together. That's the only way I know how to do this." She hesitated, and then, in the tone of someone stepping off a ledge, she added: "Can I tell you what mine feels like? The thinning. While yours is loud. Because mine was quiet, and maybe it helps to hear it said."

He nodded. Outside, wind moved through the pines, and the hut's single lamp bent its flame toward him as if listening too.

"When they took my soul," she said, "it didn't hurt. That's the thing nobody tells you. There's a moment—you sign, or they mark you, whatever the ritual is—and you expect pain, fire, something you can fight. Instead there's just a sort of... settling. Like a house after everyone's left. And afterward you keep waiting to feel different and you don't, not right away, and that's the trap. You think: *I'm fine. It wasn't so much. I got my family through three checkpoints and all it cost was a thing I never used anyway.*" Her mouth twisted. "You tell yourself the soul is like an appendix."

"And then?" His voice was very small.

"Then the forgetting starts. Small things first. My mother's face—I told you. But it wasn't just her. It was the smell of our kitchen. The name of the street I grew up on. My brother's laugh—I could remember that he laughed, that he laughed *easily*, but the sound itself was gone, like a room I'd been locked out of." She wrapped her arms around her knees. "And here's the part I've never said aloud. The scariest part wasn't losing those things. It was realizing I couldn't miss them properly anymore. Grief needs a shape to hold. When the shape goes, you're not sad—you're *smoothed over*. Polished. Like a stone the river's been working. And some part of you thinks, isn't that easier? Isn't it kinder? And that thought is the true theft. They don't just take the soul. They take your ability to want it back."

The lamp flame steadied. Astrid found that his hands had stopped feeling like floorboards. He reached out—slowly, the way you reach toward a wild thing—and found her wrist, and held it.

"That's what the fever is like," he said. "The smoothed-over part. There was a hour—I don't know how long—where I couldn't remember the valley, and worse, I couldn't remember *wanting* to. I lay there thinking, well, places are places, one is much like another, why does it matter—" He stopped. Swallowed. "I've spent my whole life loving places from a distance because the ward wouldn't let me touch them. And in one afternoon of using the gift the way it was always meant to be used, I nearly became a man who doesn't love anything at all."

They sat with that, the two of them, in the small circle of lamplight while the mountain dark stood at the windows like a patient animal.

"You paid for us," Sera said finally. "For Fen. For all of us. Don't you dare apologize for it."

"I'm not apologizing. I'm—" He searched for the word and found it, and it scared him with its accuracy. "I'm learning the exchange rate."

* * *

Recovery, when it came, came the way dawn comes in high country—not all at once but in stages, each one a little less grey than the last.

Bramble appeared at his bedside on the sixth day with ink-stained fingers and a scholar's merciless cheer, and quizzed him like a schoolmistress. Names. Dates. The nine gates of Vell. The tributaries of the Sorrel. He got them all except one—the name of the inn where they'd slept outside Vell—which turned out not to be forgotten but simply never known, since they'd camped in a sheep pasture, and Bramble cackled at him for ten minutes over that.

Yorick brought wood without being asked and split it within earshot of the sickroom, the steady percussion of the axe saying what the ex-soldier could not: *I am here, I am useful, I am not leaving.* Once, when Astrid woke at night, he heard Yorick and Sera talking low by the fire, and Yorick's voice cracked on a sentence Astrid couldn't quite catch—something about men he'd helped hunt, men who had thinned and vanished, and Sera's answer, gentle and absolute: *Then we get there first. Then we burn the ledger before it writes anyone else.*

Fen hobbled in on the seventh day, arm in a sling, and dropped onto the edge of his bed with the total lack of ceremony that was her native language. "Ost says you screamed my name in your sleep for two days straight. Very embarrassing for both of us." Her grin faded into something rarer. "You folded a mountain, Astrid. I've seen gifted soldiers do party tricks their whole lives and never once risk what you risked. So." She pressed something into his good hand: a smooth grey pebble from the Gray Stair, the exact spot where she'd fallen. "So you remember the place cost something. Places always do. We're even now."

And Ost, from the doorway, arms crossed, said only: "Next time, warn us before you bend the world. I hate surprises." But her eyes were wet, and she left before anyone could remark on it.

Through all of it, Sera stayed. She had appointed herself his keeper with the same fierce practicality she brought to everything, and if the others noticed that she slept in the chair beside his bed more often than in her own, none of them were foolish enough to say so.

* * *

On the ninth night, well past midnight, he woke clear-headed for the first time and found her awake too, sitting by the banked fire, turning something over in her fingers. He recognized it with a jolt: the scrap of paper she'd carried since Vell, the one she'd shown no one. A rubbing, he realized now—charcoal on thin paper, lifted from something. Letters swam across it in a clerk's tight hand.

"What is that?" he asked.

She didn't startle. She'd known he was awake; he suspected she always knew. "From the archive under Vell. Bramble taught me how to take a rubbing before we left—I told her it was idle curiosity." She handed it to him. "It's my entry. Or the shadow of it. The ledger itself is under the mountain, but the court keeps duplicate records in the free cities—Bramble says it's how they audit themselves. Name of seller. Name of buyer. Date. And this line here." Her finger touched a column of figures. "Disbursement. That's me. That's what I'm *spent on*, Astrid. A siege foundry, rolling south toward the cities that took us in. Every bolt it fires, every wall it breaks, part of the force comes from—" She stopped. Started again, flatter. "I wanted to see it written down. I thought if I could look at it, it would stop being a nightmare and become a problem. Problems can be solved."

He studied the rubbing. The clerk's hand was neat, indifferent, terrible. Somewhere in that column of figures was a number that was also a woman sitting beside him, also a laugh locked in a room, also a mother's face.

"Sera." He gave the paper back. "When I was in the fever—when I was on the road—I learned something. About my gift, and about what I paid." He flexed his fingers, feeling for the fraying at his edges, the new permanent looseness in his seams. "Folding the stair tore something open. Not just cost—*opened*. I can feel places now the way I used to only see them. Right now, through the floor, through the rock, I can feel the mountain's roots going down and down, and I can feel..." He paused, testing the truth of it against the strange new sense humming in him. "Roads. Old ones. Buried ones. Ways under the mountain that aren't on any map, because no one living has mapped them. The dwarves dug deep, deeper than the court uses, and the deep ways are still there. Empty. Waiting."

Sera had gone very still. "You're saying you can find a way into the court that isn't a gate."

"I'm saying the fever burned off the last of my caution and left the sight behind." He almost smiled. "Odile would box my ears. She always said a mapmaker's first duty is to know what a road costs before he walks it. I know what this one costs, Sera. I've already paid part of it. Which means—" he met her eyes, and the lamplight caught both their faces, hers hollowed by what she'd sold, his sharpened by what he'd spent, two ledgers balanced against each other in the dark— "which means the rest is just distance. And distance is the one thing I have never been afraid of."

Sera looked at him for a long moment. Then she leaned forward and fed a fresh branch to the fire, and the flames rose, and threw their joined shadows tall against the wall—one shadow, almost, where the lamplight made them overlap.

"Bramble will want to see the rubbing," she said softly. "And Yorick will want to argue, and the twins will want to race each other down whatever hole you find, and Fen will pretend her arm doesn't hurt." A ghost of a smile. "And tomorrow, cartographer, you're going to draw us a map of the one place in the world that has spent its whole existence making sure it can't be mapped."

Outside, far below them, beneath leagues of stone, something vast and patient was burning souls to forge a war—and for the first time since the Gray Stair, Astrid Joubert could feel exactly where it was.
