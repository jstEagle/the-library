## 15. The Court Beneath the Mountain

The stairs went down for a day and a half.

They had been cut by hands that did not measure in human spans—each step a forearm deep, each landing wide enough to hold a market square, and every surface carved with script that Astrid's gift read even when his eyes could not. The dwarves had not written their history on the walls. They had *built* it, load-bearing, so that the mountain itself remembered. He walked with one palm trailing the stone and felt the passages of it: the founding, the delving, the long wars, the abandonment—and beneath all of it, running like a vein of ore through the strata, the newer carving. Recent. Human. Wrong.

"Stop touching the walls," Fen whispered.

"I'm reading them."

"You're *glowing*."

He looked down at his hand. She was right. A faint gold light traced the lines of his palm where it met the stone, the way dawnlight finds the seam of a door. He pulled his fingers away and the glow faded slowly, reluctantly, like a child refusing to let go of its mother's hem.

"The whole mountain is a map," he said quietly. "It's been mapped into itself. Every tunnel, every vault, every—" He stopped. Swallowed. "Every place they keep things they don't want found."

Ost, walking behind him with an arrow nocked out of habit rather than fear, said: "Then you're the only man alive who can get lost here on purpose."

"And the only one who can find us again," Sera said.

She had barely spoken since the Gray Stair. Since Fen's blood on the rocks and Astrid folding the world like a letter to slip them through the inquisitors' fingers. Fen walked now with her arm bound and slung, pale but stubbornly upright, refusing Yorick's offered shoulder at every landing. But it was Sera who watched Astrid now with an expression he could not name—as if she were measuring him against something, some cost she knew the price of better than anyone.

The air changed as they descended. Warmer. And sweet—not the sweetness of flowers but of something burning that wanted very much to be smelled as pleasant. Bramble stopped on a landing, pressed her back to the wall, and was sick.

"I clerked here," she said when she could speak. "Four years. I told myself it was accounting. Numbers are clean. Numbers don't scream." She wiped her mouth. "That smell is the rendering hall. They burn what's left over. What doesn't take to the engines properly." Her voice went flat, clerical, terrible. "There's always leftover."

No one answered her. There was nothing to answer with.

* * *

They came up out of the stair into a gallery of pillars, and there Astrid stopped breathing for a moment, because the dwarves had built this place to be seen from above and the court beneath the mountain had repurposed it without shame. Pillars like trees in a stone forest rose toward a ceiling lost in gloom, and between them moved figures—robed, hooded, carrying lanterns of green glass—and the floor itself was engraved, vast spirals of it, channels worn smooth by centuries of feet, all converging toward a distant radiance that was not firelight and not daylight and not any light Astrid had a name for.

"Tribute-bearers keep to the third channel," Bramble murmured. "Left of the great pillar with the broken crown. Walk slow. Look bored. Tribute-bearers are terrified, but they're a *familiar* terror—the stewards' eyes slide off familiar terror like rain off wax." She straightened her coat, tugged her collar up around the scar on her throat. "I'll do the talking. My voice still has the accent. Four years leaves an accent in your bones."

"And if someone recognizes your face?" Yorick asked.

"Then we improvise," Bramble said, "and I'd remind you all that my improvisations historically end in fires."

They joined the third channel. It was like wading into a river of people—all of them moving in the same slow, shuffling current, hooded, bowed, carrying chests and urns and caged birds and, once, a small wagon drawn by two white goats, on which lay a figure covered entirely in linen. Astrid did not let himself think about what was under the linen. Around them the green-lanterned stewards drifted like moths, and the sound of the crowd was strange: everyone whispering, no one speaking, a vast soft susurrus like wind through wheat, or like a congregation mid-prayer.

Sera walked beside him. Under the hood, her face was gray. Not with fear—or not only fear. With *recognition*. This was the smell of the room where her name had been taken from her. Somewhere in this cathedral of ledgers, on some shelf or in some binding, the thing that made her fully herself sat waiting to be weighed and spent.

"Your hand," she said suddenly.

He looked down. His palm was glowing again, brighter now, and threads of gold were crawling up his wrist like ivy finding a trellis. The mountain was reading him back.

"It knows I'm here," he whispered. "The whole structure—it's not stone to me, Sera. It's *ink*. Everything they've built, everything they've hidden, it's all written, and I can—" His breath caught. "There's a room below us. Deep. Ringed in wards older than the court itself, dwarven work they never managed to unpick, so they built *around* it instead. That's the vault. That's where the ledger sleeps."

"How far?"

"Half a mile down. Maybe less, as the mountain folds." He almost smiled. "Distance is negotiable, if you know how to ask."

"Don't," Sera said softly. "Not yet. Not until we have to. You fold anything down here and every gifted soul in this mountain will feel it—including whatever they keep in the engines." She touched his glowing wrist, gently, the way you'd touch a wound. "Save it. Please. Save yourself."

* * *

The rendering hall announced itself before they saw it.

First the heat, rolling up the corridor like breath from a forge. Then the light—a red-gold glare spilling across the flagstones, flickering, alive. And then the sound, which Bramble had promised and which no promise could have prepared them for: a low, continuous chord, dozens of voices braided into one, rising and falling, never quite resolving. Not screaming. Screaming would have been easier. This was *singing*—or something shaped like singing, the way a drowned man's thrashing is shaped like swimming.

The hall opened around them without warning, and the company stopped as one body, because awe is a trap and they fell into it together.

The chamber was a cathedral inverted—a pit, a throat going down and down, terraced in rings, and in the heart of it burned the Light. Not flame. Flame has an edge, a hunger, a direction. This was illumination without source, vast and patient, and it rose in slow columns that fed upward through brass conduits along the walls, up and up, away into the dark, toward the surface, toward the war, toward the foundries and the siege-works and the armies of King Aldric that marched on light like this.

And in the rings around it, on tiered benches of black stone, sat the souls.

Astrid saw them the way his gift showed him true shapes, and he would spend the rest of his life trying to unsee it. They were not ghosts. They were *people*—the essential architecture of people, rendered down to luminous thread, thousands upon thousands of them, seated in rows like a choir awaiting a conductor, each one burning steadily, giving off its light the way a candle gives off its flame: completely, and forever, and without being asked.

Bramble made a sound beside him, small and ruined. "The ledger assigns them their benches," she said. "By value. By... yield. I sat at that desk." She pointed with a hand that shook, at a high lectern overlooking the rings, a small dark shape against the glare. "Third desk from the left. I recorded the assignments. I told myself numbers were clean."

Yorick put his hand on her shoulder. She didn't shrug it off. That, more than anything, told Astrid how far past words they all were.

"They can feel it," Sera said. Her voice was very quiet and very steady, the steadiness of a woman standing at the edge of something and choosing not to fall. "The burning. That's why they sing. It's not worship. It's the only shape left for what they—" She stopped. Closed her eyes. "My mother used to hum when she worked. Bread days. I forgot her face and I kept the humming, and I told myself that was enough of her to survive on." She opened her eyes and looked down into the Light with an expression that had passed through grief and come out the other side as something harder and colder and cleaner. "It isn't. None of this is."

Fen, gray-faced, gripping her bound arm: "Which one is yours?"

"I don't know." Sera's jaw tightened. "That's the worst of it. She could be any light in that room. I sold myself wholesale. No description, no distinguishing marks. Just a name and a price."

Astrid looked at the choir and did the thing he was made for, the thing he had done his whole life from the safety of the valley: he measured. Thousands of lights. Each one a person, a mother's humming, a bread-day, a childhood, a whole country of small ordinary mornings rendered down to fuel. And above them, drifting on the terraces in their green-lantern hoods, the stewards moved among the benches with little brass clippers, trimming the flames that burned too bright, tidying the ones that guttered, keeping the choir neat.

Something in Astrid's chest, which had spent thirty years being careful, being quiet, being safe, closed like a fist.

"We're going to strike her entry," he said. Not a plan. A vow. "And then we're going to open every shelf in that vault, and every soul in this mountain is going to hear its own name read aloud."

* * *

They posed as tribute-bearers delivering a late consignment from the western roads—Bramble's forged writ, sealed with wax she had carried for four years against exactly this need, bought them passage past the first checkpoint with a bored wave of a steward's hand. The second checkpoint was worse: a tollgate of black iron where a steward with a voice like dry paper examined their faces one by one, and Astrid stood in the line with his heart hammering and his palm wrapped in a strip of linen to hide the glow, and thought, absurdly, of Odile's compass in his pocket, its needle trembling toward home.

The steward paused at Sera longest. "You've been here before," he said.

"All tribute-bearers have been here before," Bramble said smoothly, in her flat clerical accent. "That's what makes them bearers. The writ stands. Log it or log my complaint, but either way log it fast—we've a schedule and the western roads close at thaw."

Something in the sheer bureaucratic tedium of it saved them. The steward stamped the writ, waved them through, and did not see the way Sera's knuckles had gone white inside her gloves.

Beyond the checkpoint the crowds thinned. The corridors narrowed, grew older—the dwarven work resuming beneath the court's additions, joints fitted so finely that Astrid's fingertips ached with admiration even now, even here. And the deeper they went, the more his gift woke, gold light bleeding through the linen wrapping his hand, the mountain unfolding in his mind like a book opening: corridors, chambers, shafts, and always, below, the vault—a knot of ancient warding, dense as a clenched heart, with the master ledger coiled at its center like a pearl in a fist.

"There," he breathed, at a junction where six tunnels met under a dome carved with constellations. "Down the eastern passage, then the stair that pretends to be a wall. The wards are dwarven—they key to bloodline and to *name*. They'll part for anyone whose name is written in the ledger." He turned to Sera. "Yours is. Mine isn't. Which means the vault will open for you and bar the rest of us, and the moment it opens for you, the Steward will know."

"Then we go in knowing," Sera said.

But it was Bramble who laughed—a short, wild, disbelieving sound, there under the constellation dome, with the Light of ten thousand burning souls seeping up through the stone beneath their feet.

"Oh," she said. "Oh, you beautiful, doomed mapmaker. You haven't understood yet. The wards key to names in the ledger—but I was a *clerk*. I know the filing system. And the filing system, friends, has a flaw the stewards never fixed because no one has ever been mad enough to look for it." Her eyes were shining. "An entry can be struck. And a struck entry leaves a gap. And a gap in a dwarven ward is a door."

Above them, faint through miles of stone, a bell began to ring.

Not the alarm—not yet. The tribute bell, marking the hour, calling the stewards to the changing of the watch. The corridors emptied around them. And in the sudden silence, Astrid unwound the linen from his hand and let the gold light rise, and pointed down the eastern passage, into the dark, toward the room where every stolen name in the realm waited on its shelf.

"Now," he said. "While the watch changes. Now, while the mountain is blinking."
