## 16. The Price of a Name

The Steward received them in a chamber that was not a chamber.

Sera understood this the way she had come to understand everything beneath the mountain—not with her eyes, which kept insisting on impossible things, but with the hollow place inside her where her soul used to sit. The room was a throat of black stone, ribbed with veins of pale light, and at its center stood a desk. Just a desk, oak and iron-banded, so ordinary it made her teeth ache. Behind the desk sat the Steward, and behind the Steward's shoulders rose shelves upon shelves of ledgers, their spines glowing faintly, like coals banked for a long winter.

She counted them without wanting to. Hundreds. Thousands. Each one a life, filed and burning slow.

*They are not books,* something in her whispered. *They are people.*

"You are the tribute-bearers from Vell," the Steward said. Its voice was dry as a page turning. It did not look up from the ledger open before it, and Sera saw that its hands were wrong—too many joints, fingers like folded paper, moving with the patience of something that had been doing this work since before the mountain had a name. "You are late. The tithe was due at the dark of the moon."

"We came to negotiate," Astrid said.

Sera heard the steadiness in his voice and marveled at it. She knew what it cost him. She had spent three days watching him in the dwarven ruins above, watching him press his palm to walls and close his eyes, reading the mountain's bones the way other men read letters. She had watched him go gray afterward, each time, and say nothing. He was spending himself to get them here, coin by coin, and he thought no one could see the ledger of it running down.

She could see it. She knew exactly what it looked like when someone thinned.

"The court does not negotiate," the Steward said. "The court records."

"Then record this." Astrid stepped forward, and Yorick shifted behind him—the big man's hand drifting toward the axe he wasn't supposed to be carrying past the outer wards. Fen and Ost flanked the doorway with the loose-limbed stillness of scouts pretending to be servants. Bramble stood at the back, hood up, and Sera prayed to every god she'd stopped believing in that the ex-clerk's forged tribute-seal would hold if it came to that.

It would not come to that. Not yet. That was the plan: talk first. Talk because Bramble had sworn there was precedent, that the court had sold souls back before, in the old centuries, when the free cities still sent ambassadors and the mountain still cared about appearances.

"Six years ago," Astrid said, "a woman named Sera Voss—no relation—" Sera caught the flicker of his glance, quick as a fish under ice, "—sold something to this court. A soul, recorded under the name Sera of the Ash Roads. The entry would be in your master ledger. We wish to buy it back."

For the first time, the Steward looked up.

Its face was the worst thing yet. Not monstrous—worse than monstrous. It was the face of an accountant who had been beautiful once, long ago, and had let the beauty be spent the way everything else here was spent: gradually, on installments, without ever quite noticing. Its eyes were the color of tallow.

"Buy it back," the Steward repeated. Something moved across its features that might have been amusement, in the way that frost might be called weather. "How quaint. Yes. There is provision for redemption. There has always been provision for redemption." One paper finger traced down the open page. "The court is not cruel, cartographer. The court is *fair*. Every contract holds within it the seed of its own unmaking—that is the oldest law, older than the mountain. What was sold may be bought. The price is simply... equivalent."

"Name it," Sera said.

Her voice came out harder than she meant it to. Harder than she'd planned. But she found she could not stand behind Astrid one more moment, could not let him do all the standing while she hid in the shape of a servant girl with a covered basket. She stepped forward into the tallow light and pulled back her hood, and watched the Steward's eyes find her—and *recognize* her, or rather recognize the absence in her, the way a clerk recognizes a missing line in a column.

"Ah," the Steward said softly. "The seller herself. You've come in person. How economical."

"I want my soul back."

"It is a fine soul," the Steward said, and turned a page with terrible gentleness. "Grade three. Unspoiled by oath-magic. It has been fueling Foundry Nine for six years—siege-craft, fire-pumps, the great engines that walk. Your mother's face, I believe, was among the first collateral. Souls burn brightest from the inside out; memories go first, then loves, then the sense of being loved. Yours has held remarkably well. There is perhaps a third of you remaining on deposit."

A third. Sera had known, of course. She had known in the way you know a wound is deep without pulling the bandage. But hearing it itemized—hearing herself described as *on deposit*, as grade and remainder—did something to her that fear alone had never managed.

It made her angry.

"Then sell it back," she said. "I'll pay. Gold, service, whatever the court values. I have skills. I can carry messages through Aldric's lines. I know the checkpoints between here and the coast better than any living courier."

"The court does not value gold," the Steward said. "Gold burns poorly. And we do not value services; services end, and the ledger must balance eternally." The paper fingers stilled. "The price of a soul is a soul. Equivalent exchange. This is not cruelty. This is arithmetic."

The silence that followed had weight. Sera felt it settle on her shoulders like snow.

"No," she said.

"You have not heard the terms—"

"There is no one here whose soul you're taking," Sera said. "I came to buy mine back, not to sell someone else's. If that's the only currency, then the negotiation is over, and I'll find another door." She said it loudly, deliberately, knowing the wards would hear, knowing the Steward would hear. Knowing Astrid was standing very still beside her in the way he stood still when he was thinking too fast.

*Don't,* she begged him silently. *Whatever you're thinking. Don't.*

"The terms," the Steward continued, as if she hadn't spoken, "are flexible regarding *whose* soul balances the entry. It need not be yours, obviously. It need not be a companion's." The tallow eyes lifted, and for the first time they left Sera entirely, sliding past her shoulder with the unhurried appetite of something that had been weighing souls for millennia and knew good stock when it saw it. "There is, for instance, the matter of the man who reads places."

Sera's blood went cold.

"You think we did not know?" the Steward said mildly. "The moment you crossed the outer ruins, the mountain felt him. A gift like that comes along once in an age—a soul that perceives the true architecture of the world. Do you understand what such a soul is *worth*? Mapped and bound, it could chart every hidden road, every warded border, every refuge of the gifted from here to the sea. His Majesty King Aldric would pay a kingdom for what that soul could show him. The court would pay more. It is, by our reckoning, worth ten of yours." The paper fingers folded, neatly, like a document being closed. "One gift-soul against one grade-three remainder. The court would even call it generous. You would leave whole, Sera of the Ash Roads. Entirely whole. And he would remain here, on deposit, burning bright for centuries."

"No." Sera's voice cracked on it. She made herself say it again, louder, to the Steward, to the shelves, to the whole cathedral of burning lives. "*No.*"

"Sera." Astrid's hand touched her elbow, light as a pen-stroke. "Let me—"

"*No.*" She turned on him, and the anger that had been building all the way down the mountain—all the way down six years, all the way down since the checkpoint at Marrow Ford when she'd signed her name in blood-ink and told herself it was nothing, a formality, a toll—came out of her at last. "You don't get to do this. You don't get to look at me with that face and start calculating. I know that face. I *wear* that face. I wore it at the ford when I decided my family's lives were worth more than mine, and I will tell you what I learned, cartographer: it doesn't stay noble. It gets smaller every year. It forgets faces. It forgets why it started. You want to trade yourself for me and spend the rest of forever as a line in a ledger, and you think that's love, but it isn't. It's just the same arithmetic the court uses. It's *their* math. Don't you dare bring their math into this."

The words rang off the black ribs of the chamber and died.

Astrid looked at her, and she watched him take it—watched the words land the way arrows land, the way truth lands when it's aimed well. His jaw worked. His eyes, those eyes that saw the true shape of everything, were wet.

"If it were reversed," he said quietly, "you'd pay for me."

"That's different."

"It isn't."

"It *is*," Sera said, "because I'm asking you to let me be selfish. For once. For one hour, in one room, let me be the person who refuses the noble thing. Let me walk out of here thin and forgetting and *mine*, and let that be enough. Because if you pay for me—if you do this—then I'm not saved, I'm just *bought*, and I will spend every day of my whole life knowing what I cost. I already know what that feels like. My family carried it. They looked at me after the ford like I was made of glass and debt. I won't be that again. Not for you. Never for you."

Something shifted in his expression then—something that hurt to watch, because it was hope losing an argument with love, or love losing an argument with hope. She couldn't tell which. She only knew that he nodded, slowly, and said, "Then we find another way," and that she believed him.

That was the cruelest part. She believed him.

"The negotiation is concluded, then," the Steward said, and there was satisfaction in its dry voice, the satisfaction of a trap that has not needed to spring. "The entry stands. Foundry Nine marches at the turn of the season, and your third-of-a-soul marches with it, Sera of the Ash Roads. Unless—" the paper fingers spread, magnanimous, obscene, "—you reconsider. The offer does not expire quickly. Gifts like his keep."

They walked out. All of them, in order, servants leaving the presence of power, heads appropriately bowed. Yorick last, filling the doorway a moment longer than necessary, and Sera saw the old soldier's knuckles white around nothing at all.

Only when they were three galleries away, in a corridor of dwarf-cut stone where the light came from veins in the rock and the air tasted of cold iron, did Astrid stop walking.

He didn't say anything. He took out Odile's compass—the old brass thing, dented, its needle swinging lazily toward nothing a normal compass would care about—and held it flat on his palm. The needle spun once, twice, and settled. Pointing not north.

Pointing *down*. Deeper than down. Toward a place below the Steward's chamber, below the foundries, below anything on any map Astrid had ever drawn or ever admitted to drawing.

"Bramble," he said softly. "You served the court. Tell me about the master ledger. Not the copies. The original. Where it lives."

Bramble's hood came back. Her face, in the vein-light, was the face of a woman doing rapid, dangerous sums. "Below the counting halls," she said slowly. "The Hollow Choir, the clerks called it. Where the first entries were written. It's guarded by—"

"I know where it is," Astrid said, and the needle trembled on his palm, faithful as a heartbeat, pointing at the heart of the mountain. "I've known since the Steward said *equivalent*. Ledgers are places, Bramble. Everything is a place. And I am done letting other people set the price."

Sera caught his sleeve. "Astrid. What are you—"

He smiled at her, and it was the smile he wore over maps of places he'd never been, and it frightened her more than the Steward's tallow eyes ever could.

"I'm going to strike your name," he said.
