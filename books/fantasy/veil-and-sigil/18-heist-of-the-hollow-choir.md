## 18. Heist of the Hollow Choir

**Yorick**

The diversion had to be loud enough to empty a corridor and quiet enough not to wake the whole mountain. Yorick had spent thirty years learning the difference, first as a soldier of Aldric's purge and then as a man running from it, and he had never once imagined those skills would be spent here—beneath the earth, in a cathedral built of paper and screaming souls, on behalf of a mapmaker who flinched at raised voices.

He stood in the Choir now: a vaulted gallery where the court's tribute-bearers waited to be processed, all of them hollow-eyed men and women clutching deeds of sale like rosaries. The ceiling was ribbed bone-white stone, and every arch was carved with mouths—hundreds of them, open, silent, arranged in ranks like a congregation frozen mid-hymn. The Hollow Choir, the clerks called it. Yorick could hear why. When the wind of the mountain's breathing moved through the galleries, the mouths sang. A thin, airless note, like a wet finger circling the rim of a glass. Like grief practiced until it became technique.

He wore the gray livery of a foundry overseer, taken off a dead man whose boots had almost fit. At his hip hung the seal-stamp of the Third Foundry, and in his pocket, folded small as a prayer, was the lie that would buy his friends their hour.

A steward-clerk approached, quill already lifted. "Name and purpose."

"Yorick Vane," he said, and gave the name freely, loudly, the way guilty men do. "Overseer, Third Foundry. I've come to report an irregularity in the fuel intake." He let the word *irregularity* sit there, fat and alarming. "There's a soul in my ledger-line that's burning wrong. It's contaminating the draw on two others. If it isn't reconciled tonight, you'll lose three entries' worth of output before the foundry marches."

The clerk's quill stopped. Souls burning wrong was not heresy down here; it was arithmetic. "Contamination," the clerk repeated, with the particular hunger of a bureaucrat smelling promotion. "Which entries?"

"That's what I need your reconciliation chamber for." Yorick leaned in, lowered his voice, and did the thing he was best at in all the world: he made a frightened man feel important. "And I'll need it done quietly. If the Steward hears the Third Foundry has been feeding him spoiled fuel since midwinter—" He let the silence do the rest.

The clerk went pale in the way only clerks can, pale with visions of demotion, and began walking fast toward the reconciliation chambers, Yorick at his elbow, asking urgent questions about intake seals. Behind them, the Hollow Choir sang its one thin note.

*Two guards from the west gallery,* Yorick thought, counting them as they peeled away to follow the commotion. *Three from the archive stair.* Every eye he drew was an eye not watching a blind cartographer feel his way through the dark.

He had told Astrid once, around a fire, that penance was a debt you paid in installments. Tonight was a large one. He walked faster, talking louder, and made himself magnificent.

* * *

**Fen**

The wards were threads. That was the secret no one told you, Fen thought, pressed flat against the cold stone of the archive stair with Ost's shoulder warm against hers. Everyone imagined wards as walls, as gates, as thunder. But Astrid had read the mountain's structure the way he read everything—by its true shape—and the true shape of the court's protections was a weaving. Threads of intent, anchored at nodes, humming with borrowed power. And a thread, however fine, could be cut.

She could see them now that she knew how to look. Not with Astrid's gift—with the twins' older, humbler one, the knack for stillness that let you notice what moving things missed. The ward-threads crossed the archive landing in a lattice, silver-pale, trembling faintly each time the Hollow Choir sang.

"On three," Ost breathed against her ear.

They moved together, as they had moved together since the womb—two knives drawn in a single motion, each severing a thread at the node where it anchored. Fen felt the cut more than heard it: a sensation like biting foil, a bright wrongness on her teeth. The lattice sagged. Somewhere far above, a bell that had never been rung shivered in its cradle but did not sound.

"Not yet," she whispered, to the bell, to the mountain, to her own hammering heart. "Not yet."

Ost was already threading the gap, small and quick, and Fen followed, and they were through—the first living things in a hundred years to pass the archive stair without the court's permission. Above them, the ledgers waited. Below them, Yorick's voice rose through the stone, magnificently tedious, cataloguing fictional irregularities.

Fen paused at the top of the stair and looked back once. The severed threads were already trying to regrow, fine filaments reaching across the gap like spider silk in rain. They had minutes. Perhaps fewer.

"Hurry," she said, to the darkness ahead, where a blind man was listening to the shape of the world.

* * *

**Astrid**

Blindness, Astrid discovered, was a discipline.

He had bound his eyes with Odile's old compass-silk because the court's corridors lied to sight—murals that shifted when watched, doorways painted onto blank stone, stairs rendered in perspective so perfect they beckoned. But his gift did not need light. His gift needed only attention. So he gave the mountain all of his.

The true shape unfolded around him like a hand opening. Corridors became currents; rooms became pools; the great furnace-shafts of the foundry below ran through the rock like veins of heat, and along them, unbearably, he felt the souls—thousands of small bright knots of *person*, drawn thin into wires, wired into engines. He had mapped coastlines he'd never seen and cities he'd never walked, and nothing had ever hurt like this map. Every entry in every ledger was a place too, he realized. A location where a human being used to be.

"This way," he murmured. Sera's hand was on his sleeve, Bramble behind her with the stolen clerk's ring, and he steered them left where the corridor pretended to go right, down a service passage so narrow the true shape of it seemed embarrassed, hidden between two walls of ceremonial masonry never meant to be found.

"The master ledger is—" Bramble whispered.

"I know. It's the heaviest thing in the mountain." Astrid almost smiled. Weight, to his gift, was just another kind of shape. "It sits at the center of everything, the way a heart does. Everything else is built around it. We're beneath the scriptorium now. There's a shaft—"

He stopped. Ahead, in the true shape, something was wrong. A knot of presence where presence shouldn't be—a guard, or worse, standing precisely in the gap between the service passage and the ledger vault, patient as a held breath.

"Sera," he breathed. "Your knife. There's one. Ten paces. He hasn't seen us—he's watching the corridor we're *supposed* to take."

He felt Sera go very still beside him, felt the moment she understood: the court expected thieves to come by the grand route, and had posted its patience accordingly. It had not expected a man who navigated by truth through a building made of lies.

"Then we aren't thieves coming by the grand route," Sera said softly, and drew her knife without a whisper of steel.

* * *

**Sera**

The guard died quietly, which was more than he deserved and less than she wanted, and Sera tried not to think about how easy it had become, this arithmetic of necessity. One guard against a soul. Her soul. She stepped over him and told herself the ledger would balance later, if there was a later.

Then they were in the vault, and thinking became impossible.

The master ledger was not a book. It was a *room*—a circular chamber walled floor to ceiling in shelved volumes, each spine labeled in gold leaf, and in the center, on a plinth of black glass, the current volume lay open, its pages turning themselves in a wind Sera could not feel. Light came from nowhere and everywhere, the same sourceless glow as the foundry fires. And the singing was louder here. The Hollow Choir's note had followed them down, or perhaps it had always lived here, in the paper, in the ink, in ten thousand names written in a hand so beautiful it looked like devotion.

Bramble made a sound low in her throat, almost a sob. "I shelved some of these," she said. "Gods forgive me. I shelved some of these and went home to supper."

"Find her," Astrid said gently. "Quickly, Bramble. The threads are regrowing."

Sera walked the shelves. She did not know how she would recognize it—there were thousands of volumes—but her body knew. Her body had been missing a piece of itself for a year, and bodies remember. Three rows in, her chest went tight and hot, and she pulled a slim volume from between its neighbors, and there it was.

*SERAPHINE VAIL.*

Her own handwriting could not have made the letters more real. Below the name, columns of notation: date of purchase, terms of sale, transfer history. And at the bottom, in red ink that seemed faintly warm to the touch, the destination her soul had been assigned:

*Third Foundry — Siege Line — Allocated.*

Allocated. Like grain. Like rope.

"They wrote me so neatly," she said. Her voice came out steady, which surprised her. "All this time I imagined monsters. But it's just... good penmanship."

"It's always just penmanship," Bramble said, coming to stand beside her, her clerk's ring already off her finger, fitted to a stylus of black glass. "That's how they get away with it. Strike the entry?"

"Strike the entry."

Bramble set the stylus to the line of Sera's name. Around them the sourceless light dimmed, flickered—the ledger noticing, the mountain noticing—and in the silence before the stroke fell, Sera heard, impossibly, clearly, her mother's voice saying her name the way she'd said it at bedtime, *Seraphine*, both syllables, both syllables loved—

Bramble struck the line through.

* * *

For one heartbeat, triumph.

The red allocation faded. The gold-leaf name dimmed like a lamp going out—not erased, Sera realized, *released*—and warmth flooded up her arm from the book, through her chest, into the hollow place behind her ribs that had ached for a year. She gasped. It felt like surfacing. It felt like her mother's kitchen. It felt like—

The bell rang.

Not the shivering-silent bell of the archive stair. This was the deep bell, the mountain's own heart-note, and it rang once and then did not stop, and every mouth carved into the Hollow Choir above began to sing in earnest, hundreds of voices, one note, rising.

"Threads are back," Fen's voice crackled from the stair, flat with fear. "All of them. Something saw us. Something *felt* it—"

"The entry didn't just leave the ledger," Bramble whispered, white-faced, stylus shaking. "It left the *balance*. The court feels its weight shift. Gods—they'll know exactly which line, exactly whose—"

The pages of the open volume began turning faster, hunting backward through years of purchases, and the light in the vault turned from white to a deep arterial red, and somewhere far above, boots—many boots—began to move in unison, a rhythm like a drum, like a march, like a foundry waking.

Sera closed her hand around the warm ghost of her own name and turned to Astrid.

"Astrid. The way out. Tell me there's a way out."

He was already reading the mountain, eyes bound, head tilted, listening to ten thousand tons of stone rearrange themselves around their crime. When he spoke, his voice was calm in the way of a man standing in a river measuring the flood.

"There's a way out," he said. "But we're going to have company on the road. All of it."

Above them, the bell rang on, and the mountain began, note by note, to sing them shut.
