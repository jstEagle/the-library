## 1. The Ledger of Small Debts

There are three rules to selling luck in a city where luck is illegal, and I had learned all of them the expensive way.

Rule one: never call it magic. Call it a charm, a keepsake, a trinket your grandmother pressed into your hand at the door. Grief makes excellent cover. Nobody interrogates a grieving woman's grandmother.

Rule two: never sell to anyone who can afford you. The rich don't buy charms; they buy wardens, and wardens ask questions with thumbscrews. The poor pay in coppers and gratitude, and gratitude has never once testified against me.

Rule three — and this is the one that cost me two fingers' worth of scar tissue to learn — never keep stock. A scribe with ink-stained fingers is invisible. A scribe with a drawer full of knotted red thread and river-stones carved with the old signs is a confession waiting for a search.

So on the evening the widow Maren came knocking at my door on Candle Lane, I had exactly one charm in my possession, and it was already spoken for, and I was already lying about it.

"Master Ravensworth." She stood in my doorway wringing her hands like she was trying to wring the fear out through her fingertips. She was a big woman, Maren, broad as a door herself, but fear had folded her down small. "They say you can write things. Things that help."

"They say wrong," I said, which is what I always said, and which had never once worked. "I'm a copyist. Deeds, wills, letters to relatives who won't answer. If you want someone threatened in elegant handwriting, I'm your man."

"My son's ship is due Thursday." Her voice dropped to the register people use in Vharen when they're about to commit a crime, which is to say: barely above a whisper, eyes flicking to the shutters. "The *Kestrel*. And the harbor-master's clerk says — he says there's a list. Ships that get held. Inspected. And hers is on it, and if they hold her, the cargo spoils, and Tam's whole season drowns, and he'll—" She stopped. Swallowed. "I have money."

"You have grief," I corrected gently. "It spends better."

She looked at me like I'd spoken a foreign language, which, in a way, I had. The language of men who've done this too long.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about outlawed magic: it didn't go away. It just went indoors. When the Temple of Nine Bells burned the last hedge-witch on Pyre Hill forty years ago, they didn't burn the need along with her. A mother whose son is at sea does not care about doctrine. She cares about Thursday.

What I sold wasn't even properly magic. That was the joke of it, the bitter little joke I kept behind my teeth like a coin. A luck-charm, done right, is mostly paperwork. You take a river-stone, smooth one, and you write on it — not words, exactly, more like the shape of a wish — and then you knot it in red thread while thinking very hard about the person it's for. Nine knots. One for each bell. Temple bells, ironically; I figured if the gods were going to be complicit anyway, they might as well be useful.

Does it work? I genuinely don't know. What I know is that Tam's ship came home the autumn before last, and Maren's roof survived the hailstorm that took her neighbor's, and a fever passed over her street like a polite guest. Maybe the stones did it. Maybe the wishing did it. Maybe a woman who believes her son will come home stands straighter, sleeps better, and makes fewer desperate bargains, and that alone is worth three coppers.

Three coppers. That was the price. Not because that's what a soul's safety is worth, but because three coppers is what a frightened widow can pay without having to explain the missing money to anyone. I priced my sins to be affordable. It's the only ethical position available in my line of work.

Maren put five on the table. I pushed one back. She pushed it forward again, and her chin came up, and for a second she was broad as a door again instead of folded small, and I understood that letting her overpay was its own kind of theft, so I let her.

"There's a warden post on Tallow Street now," she said as I wrapped the stone in brown paper, slow, like wrapping something that could bite. "They stopped Ferro's girl yesterday. Asked her where she got the ribbon in her hair."

"What did she say?"

"That her mother gave it to her." Maren almost smiled. "Which was true."

"Good answer." I tied the string. "The truth, kept small, is the best lie there is. Remember that, and give this to no one, show it to no one, and if a warden ever asks, it's a hearth-stone from your husband's family. Old thing. Sentimental."

"And if they don't believe me?"

"Then you weep." I handed it over. "Wardens hate weeping. It gets on their boots."

* * *

She left, and I sat in the blue hour of my room doing what I do every evening, which is the actual work of my life: the tallying.

On the desk before me, my ledger. Not the charm-ledger — that lives nowhere, exists nowhere, is written in nothing. This ledger is worse. This is the debt-book.

Every citizen of Vharen owes the Temple of Nine Bells. That's not rhetoric; it's arithmetic. When the city fell to the Temple a century back, they forgave the war-debts of the population and replaced them with something neater: a tithe against every birth, every burial, every marriage, every apprenticeship. You are born owing. You die owing. The middle is spent chipping away at it, and the Temple's clerks — men like me, though I flatter myself slightly faster — sit in the counting-halls scratching numbers that decide whether your children eat meat this winter.

My own number, as of that evening: four hundred and eleven marks, fourteen coppers.

Four years ago it had been six hundred. I had ground it down like a man grinding a stone with his forehead — copying contracts by day, wills by dusk, and by night, the stones and the thread and the wishing. Every charm sold was maybe ten marks off the principal after expenses, and every mark was an hour of my life the Temple owned, because here's the trick of the debt: you can pay it in money or in service, and service pays at half rate. Half rate. They literally discount your hours. I once asked old Clerk Hobb why, and he looked at me with genuine puzzlement and said, "Because your time isn't worth full price until the debt is cleared," and I swear to you he saw nothing wrong with the sentence. That's the genius of the Temple. It doesn't need cruelty. It needs clerks who've internalized the arithmetic.

I dipped my pen. *Received, five marks, Maren widow of the Kestrel's quarter.* Then, under expenses: *one river-stone, found free; thread, two bits; candle-end, one bit.*

Net: four marks, six coppers off the principal. At that rate I would clear my debt in roughly nineteen years, assuming I never ate anything but barley, never fell ill, and never once made a mistake, which, since mistakes in the counting-hall are deducted from your wages, meant never being human.

I set down the pen and laughed, quietly, the way you laugh at a funeral. Somewhere in the walls a mouse agreed with me.

And then — because the ledger always leads to the same thought, the thought I keep in the bottom drawer of myself, under the socks — I thought about the year the debt jumped. Three years ago. Six hundred marks became seven hundred and twelve overnight, penalties and interest compounding like rot, the month I got sick and couldn't work and missed a tithe-date. I'd gone to the counting-hall expecting ruin. Instead the clerk had slid the book toward me and my debt was — paid. All of it. Erased. Some benefactor, the clerk said, some anonymous settlement, unusual, very unusual, sign here.

I signed. Of course I signed. I didn't ask questions because the questions were terrifying. Who pays seven hundred marks for a stranger? Nobody does. Nobody buys a stranger out of a debt-hole unless they want something, and I spent that whole first free year waiting for the collector to come collect whatever it was.

Nobody came. The debt stayed dead. And slowly — shamefully, I admit it — I let myself believe in the anonymous benefactor. Let myself build a life on top of the mystery the way you build a house on filled-in ground, telling yourself the settling sounds are just the house.

But debts in Vharen don't die. They migrate. Everyone knows that. Everyone says it like a proverb and nobody follows the proverb to its end, because the end is: *if your debt vanished, it went somewhere.* Debts are like water in this city. They flow downhill. They find the lowest person.

I blew out the candle and lay in the dark listening to Candle Lane settle around me — the cart-ruts, the drunk singing two streets over, the temple bells ringing nine, each toll a small cold hand on the back of my neck. Nine bells. Nine knots. I'd been joking about the gods' complicity for years, and lately the joke had started tasting less like a joke.

One step ahead. That was the whole art of my life. One step ahead of the wardens, one step ahead of the count, one step ahead of the question I wouldn't ask. And the exhausting thing — the truly exhausting thing, worse than the hunger winters, worse than the scarred fingers — is that you can't ever stop moving. Stand still for one moment, one honest moment, and the arithmetic catches up. Stand still and you feel the weight of everything you owe and everything you've dodged and everything you've never once said out loud, pressing down like the mountain itself, like Mount Vhalen sitting on the city's shoulders, patient as a creditor.

Tomorrow I was due at the archive. Cataloguing seized books — the Temple's quiet little graveyard of confiscated print, where forbidden things go to be numbered and forgotten. Easy work. Invisible work. Work for a man who is very good at standing one step ahead.

I remember falling asleep thinking: *just keep moving. Keep the ledger balanced. Keep your head down and your hands clean-ish and your heart locked in the bottom drawer, and in nineteen years you'll be free.*

Nineteen years. I actually believed I had nineteen years.

The next morning, in a century-old book of prophecies, I would find tonight's date circled, and my own name written beneath it.

But that, as they say, is another entry in the ledger.
