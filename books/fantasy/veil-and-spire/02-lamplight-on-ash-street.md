## 2. Lamplight on Ash Street

There are forty-one lamps on my route, and I have named every one of them, because a woman who talks to her lamps is eccentric and a woman who works in silence is lonely, and I would rather be eccentric.

The one at the corner of Ash and Tanner's Row is called Grumpy, because his wick never sits straight no matter how many times I trim him, and he throws light like a man throwing stones — grudgingly, and with bad aim. Tonight he was worse than usual. The wind coming off the mountain had that wet, mineral smell it gets before the first frost, and it kept bending Grumpy's flame sideways until the whole corner looked like something seen through water.

"Stand up straight," I told him, cupping my hands around his glass. "You're embarrassing me in front of the mountain."

The mountain, of course, did not answer. Mount Vhalen never does. It just sat there above the city with its crown of cloud, the way it has sat for as long as anyone remembers, patient as a creditor.

I finished my rounds an hour after dark, which is late even for me, because Widow Marlowe on Cordwainer Lane had stopped me to talk about her son for the better part of twenty minutes and you cannot hurry a woman who is talking about her son. Then old Tam the cooper wanted someone to witness a signature, and then there was a dog, and then there was always a dog. Lamplighting is mostly walking and talking to people who can't sleep, with occasional fire. People think the fire is the job. The fire is the easy part.

By the time I came down Ash Street toward home, my shoulders were up around my ears and my chest had that tight, papery feeling it's been carrying for three weeks now, like a page left too long near a candle. I ignored it. You can ignore most things if you keep your hands busy, and I had thirty-nine lamps behind me and two ahead.

That was when I saw the light in Orsen Ravensworth's window.

Now, a light in Orsen's window is not remarkable. The man writes until his eyes cross; the wardens could set their clocks by the glow of his lamp and the scratch of his pen. But this was past midnight, and the light was moving — swinging, guttering, going out and coming back — and Orsen Ravensworth is a careful man, a *paranoid* man, a man who checks his door latch twice and once made me wait outside while he counted his ink pots. Careful men do not swing their lamps around at midnight unless something has gone wrong.

I let myself into the stairwell without knocking, because knocking is for people with boundaries, and climbed up to his landing.

He was on his knees in the middle of the floor with his coat off, patting along the boards by feel, his lamp sitting crooked on the table and throwing his shadow across the ceiling like a hanged man.

"If you've lost a coin," I said, "it rolled under the stove. If you've lost your mind, I'm afraid that's been gone for years."

He jumped about a foot, which was deeply satisfying. "Sera. *Knock.*"

"I did knock. In my heart." I leaned against the doorframe and got a proper look at him. He was pale — paler than usual, and Orsen's usual is the color of good paper — and there was a smear of soot along his jaw where he'd pushed his hair back with dirty fingers. "What did you spill?"

"Nothing."

"You're searching the floor on your knees at midnight. Either you spilled something or you're praying, and we both know you don't pray."

"I dropped ash," he said, too quickly. "From the hearth. Carrying it out."

"In your coat?"

"My sleeve."

"On your *face?*"

"The ash," said Orsen Ravensworth, the finest liar in the Temple of Nine Bells' employ and possibly the city, "was very enthusiastic."

I looked at him for a long moment. He looked back with that flat, patient expression he does, the one that says *I will outlast your curiosity*, and ordinarily he would, because the man has the endurance of a mule and the moral flexibility of a wet rag. But I was tired, and my chest hurt, and I decided to let him have his enthusiastic ash.

"Fine," I said. "Keep your secrets. I brought bread."

His eyes went immediately to the cloth bundle under my arm, the way they always do, and I felt the small warm mean pleasure of it. Hunger is the one honest thing about Orsen. Everything else about him is filed and cross-referenced, but hunger goes straight from his stomach to his face without stopping at the ledger.

"That's stolen bread," he said.

"It's *liberated* bread. From the ovens on Bellringer's Walk. The baker owes me for three years of lighting his alley lamp at no charge, and tonight I collected interest."

"You light his lamp because it's on your route."

"I light it *slowly*," I said, unwinding the cloth, "which is different. Sit down. You look like a debt notice."

He sat. We ate on his floor, backs against his bed, because he owns exactly one chair and it holds his ledgers, and between us we demolished half the loaf while it was still warm enough to steam. The baker on Bellringer's Walk makes it with a crust like armor and a heart like custard, and for a few minutes neither of us talked, which is the highest compliment two people can pay bread.

"So," I said eventually, wiping my hands. "Three years."

"Don't start."

"Three years this month. Three years since a certain scribe with excellent penmanship and catastrophic judgment signed himself over to the Temple of Nine Bells for a sum I could count on one hand if I folded some of the fingers down, and certain generous parties began quietly paying his installments so the Temple didn't fold *him* down instead."

Orsen closed his eyes. "It was a loan."

"It was a *beating* with extra steps. You signed a debt contract with the Bells, Orsen. Do you know what happens to people who default with the Bells? They don't send letters. They send *wardens*. And the wardens don't collect coin." I tore off another piece of crust. "They collect other things."

"They haven't collected anything from me yet."

"No," I said, "they haven't," and took a bite so I wouldn't have to say anything else for a moment.

Here is what I have never told him: I know exactly what the wardens collect. I've seen it happen, twice, from far enough away to pretend I hadn't. A debtor falls too far behind, and one morning his room is empty and his neighbors remember him vaguely, the way you remember a dream, and somewhere beneath the mountain — so the old stories say, so the *records* say, if you know which records to read — a little flame gets set on a shelf and auctioned off to settle accounts. The Temple calls it *remission*. The lamplighters call it what it is, quietly, among ourselves: the mountain eats.

And here is the other thing I have never told him: three years ago, when Orsen Ravensworth stood in the Temple counting-house with his signature still wet on a contract he was too proud and too stupid to read properly, a certain lamplighter went down the street to a broker she knew and sold fifteen years of her own soul to cover his arrears. Fifteen years. It seemed like nothing at the time. I was twenty-four; fifteen years sounded like a fortune I'd never miss.

My chest has been hurting for three weeks.

But that is a conversation for a night when I am braver than this one, so instead I said, "You owe me approximately everything, you know. By my arithmetic you'll be free of me sometime around your ninetieth birthday, assuming you live that long, which given your diet of ink and anxiety seems unlikely."

"Your arithmetic," Orsen said, "has always been sentimental."

"My arithmetic is *exact*. I keep a ledger. Little book, right here—" I patted my coat pocket, where there was indeed a little book, though not the one he thought. "Every installment. Every date. Every time you tried to pay me back with a charm you 'found' and I had to pretend I believed you, because we both know you made it in your kitchen at two in the morning like a criminal."

"I don't make charms."

"Of course not. And I don't steal bread. And the ash on your face was enthusiastic." I nudged his knee with mine. "Three years, Orsen. Say thank you."

He was quiet for a moment. Outside, the wind worried at the shutters, and somewhere below a cat was making its opinions known about the state of the world. When he spoke, he didn't do the wry thing. That was how I knew it mattered.

"Thank you," he said. "For all of it. I mean to pay you back. Every copper, with interest, I swear it on—"

"Don't swear it on anything." I said it faster than I meant to, sharper, and he blinked at me. I softened it with a shrug. "Swearing on things is how people end up owing the mountain. Just — owe *me*. I'm cheap."

"You are the most expensive friend I have."

"And your only one, so mind your tone."

He laughed — really laughed, the short surprised bark he tries to suppress and never quite manages — and stole the last piece of bread off my side of the cloth, which I allowed because I was full anyway, or because my appetite has been strange lately, small and skittish, like everything else about me.

We sat there while his lamp burned low. This is the part of the night I like best, though I'd sooner swallow my trimming knife than tell him so: the quiet after the rounds, when the city has finally stopped asking things of me and it's just two people on a floor with crumbs and a dying lamp. Friendship, I thought, watching him pick wax off the table with his thumbnail. The one luxury neither of us can afford. Everything costs in Vharen — light costs, warmth costs, staying alive costs monthly — but friendship costs the most, because it's the thing you can't put in a ledger, and what can't be ledgered can't be protected.

"Your cough's back," Orsen said suddenly.

"I don't have a cough."

"You've had it since the frost came. You did it four times while we were eating. You do it quieter when you think I'm not counting."

"Maybe I'm counting too," I said, "and you cough when you lie about ash."

"That was one lie. You've coughed—" he paused, and I realized with genuine alarm that he actually had been counting, "—eleven times since you came in."

"Then I owe you eleven apologies and you owe me three years. We'll call it even." I stood up, brushing crumbs off my skirt, and my chest gave a long dry rasp that I turned into a yawn halfway through, which fooled absolutely no one. "It's the season. Lamp oil, cold air, the mountain breathing on us. Everyone on my route is coughing. Widow Marlowe sounds like a cart on cobbles."

"Widow Marlowe is eighty."

"She's *experienced*."

"Sera."

"*Orsen.*" I picked up my lamp hook and my empty cloth and smiled at him, the particular smile I keep for ending conversations, honed over years of difficult customers and one extremely stubborn scribe. "Go to bed. Stop dropping enthusiastic ash. Trim your own wick for once — you let it burn crooked, it's a wonder you can read your precious ledgers."

He stood too, and for a second he looked like he wanted to say something else — something with weight to it, something that would have ruined the evening — and I watched him decide against it, the way I've watched him decide against things a hundred times. That's Orsen. He files everything. Somewhere in that paper-colored head is a drawer with my name on it, and I'd give a great deal to know what's inside, and a great deal more to make sure the worst of it never comes out.

"Same time tomorrow?" he said.

"I'm always same time tomorrow. I'm a schedule with legs." I let myself out, and paused on the landing with my hand on the rail. "Oh — and Orsen? Whatever you spilled tonight, whatever you were looking for on that floor — if it's trouble, and it's always trouble with you—"

"It's not trouble."

"—then lose it properly. Lose it deep. Because I've only just finished paying for the last trouble you lost, and I'm told my rates are going up."

I heard him laugh softly behind the door as I went down, and I carried that sound with me out onto Ash Street, where Grumpy the lamp was still burning crooked and the mountain still sat above the city, patient as a creditor, and my chest ached in a way that had nothing to do with the cold.

Eleven coughs. He'd counted eleven. The man counts everything — debts, dates, lies, coughs. One day soon he's going to count the wrong thing. One day he's going to go digging through Temple records the way he digs through everything, looking for the shape of what I've hidden, and he is going to find fifteen years missing from a lamplighter's account, and he is going to be *unbearable* about it.

Not yet, though. Not tonight. Tonight the lamps were lit, the bread was eaten, and my best friend was safe behind his crooked-burning lamp, owing me everything and knowing almost none of it.

I trimmed Grumpy one last time, told him to stand up straight, and walked home under the mountain's patient shadow, coughing quietly, keeping count myself.

* * *

It was three nights later that I came around the corner onto Ash Street and saw the wardens' lanterns, six of them, blue-white and wrong, arranged outside Orsen's building like a ring of cold teeth. And standing in their midst, calm as you please, giving them directions to his door, was Pell the junior scribe, holding a purse that had not been in his pocket last week.

I stood in the dark with my lamp unlit in my hand and did the fastest arithmetic of my life.
