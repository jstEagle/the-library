## 3. A Date That Should Not Exist

The Temple of Nine Bells paid me four coppers an hour to touch books that could get me hanged, which is the sort of arithmetic that tells you everything you need to know about Vharen.

Not all of them were dangerous, of course. Most of what came through the seizure room was rubbish — herbals with a few too many footnotes, almanacs printed before the Ban with diagrams of stars nobody was allowed to chart anymore, a truly staggering quantity of love poetry that some warden had flagged because it rhymed *moon* with *tune* and wardens are not famous for their sense of humor. I catalogued it all. Author, subject, date of confiscation, disposition pending. Four coppers an hour, and the only rule that mattered was the one printed on the inside of my wrist in fading ink from my indenture papers: *the scribe does not read.*

I read.

Everyone reads. You cannot catalogue a book without reading at least its spine, and spines are liars. A book bound as *Meditations on Humility* might contain meditations on humility, or it might contain a treatise on sympathetic magic disguised as meditations on humility, because the people who wrote forbidden books were exactly the sort of people who enjoyed hiding things, and the people who seized them were not. So I skimmed. I skimmed the way a man walking past a guardpost skims the guards — quickly, without appearing to look, keeping my face arranged into the mild boredom of a functionary who has seen ten thousand pages and been moved by none of them.

It had been three years since I'd learned to arrange my face like that. Three years since Sera had walked into the Temple's debt office with her lamplighter's wages in a sock and said, in a voice pitched to carry, "Put it against the Ravensworth account," and then looked at me with an expression I still haven't found the right word for. Not pity. Pity would have been easier to repay.

Fifteen years of her life, or so the ledger said, though ledgers round down. The Temple held her soul's paper the way a bank holds a deed to a house someone still lives in. She coughed more than she used to. She said it was the lamp-oil smoke. I let her say it, because the alternative was arithmetic I couldn't bear to do.

So: four coppers an hour, and I read, and I did not feel guilty about reading, because guilt is a luxury item and I had already sold most of mine.

* * *

That evening's crate had come up from the river district, out of the cellar of a dead bookbinder named Corvus Hale whose neighbors had finally noticed he hadn't bought bread in a month. The wardens had gone through his things with their usual delicacy — which is to say they'd taken anything silver and left the rest for the Temple's assessors, who took anything interesting and left the rest for scribes like me to number and shelve until the disposition orders came down, which they rarely did, because dispositions require decisions and decisions require someone to be responsible when the Inquisition asks questions later.

The crate smelled of river damp and tallow. At the top sat forty years of Hale's business ledgers, which I set aside with professional sympathy; every ledger is eventually someone else's evidence. Beneath them, wrapped in oilcloth gone stiff as bark, was a book that did not want to be found.

I don't mean it was hidden. It was hidden badly, actually — tucked under the false bottom of the crate where anyone diligent would find it, which is either very poor craftsmanship or a message. It was small, octavo, bound in grey leather that had once been dyed to look more expensive and had given up sometime around my grandmother's childhood. No title on the spine. No title on the boards. When I opened it, the first page offered me nothing but a date written in a careful clerk's hand:

*Anno Domini 1712. Being a true accounting of certain words spoken by the woman Vessa of the Low Steps, called the Veiled, in the year of her death.*

A century old. Pre-Ban, which meant it predated the law that made its contents criminal, which meant technically — and I have always been a great lover of *technically*, it being the last refuge of men like me — the book itself was legal. Its contents were another matter. Prophecy was explicitly named in the Ban's third clause, right between divination and enchantment, because the legislators of a century ago understood something their successors preferred to forget: a prediction is a kind of power, and power is what the Ban was for. Not safety. Power. The Temple wanted a monopoly.

I should have shelved it. Disposition pending, subject: prophecy, author: deceased, condition: water-damaged. Two lines in the catalogue and four coppers closer to freedom.

Instead I turned the page.

Vessa of the Low Steps had been, according to whoever transcribed her words, one of those unfortunates who speak true things without meaning to — a well-spring, the old texts call them, back when wells and springs and other natural phenomena were permitted to have opinions. Her prophecies ran mostly to weather and the deaths of minor officials, which is why this particular transcription had survived at all. Nobody burns a book to hide a prophecy about rain.

There were perhaps sixty entries. I skimmed them the way I skimmed everything, my pen moving in the margin columns, tallying subjects: *rain, rain, harvest, death of a customs man, fire in the Tanner's Row, rain.* The handwriting of the transcriber deteriorated toward the end of the book, letters leaning like tired men, and I remember thinking — with the smugness that precedes every fall — *here's a scribe who stayed too long at his work.*

Then I reached the entry dated for the year 1812.

This year.

My pen stopped. I'd like to tell you I felt something dramatic — cold, a whisper, the sensation of being watched by the century itself. What I actually felt was the specific irritation of a man whose column of figures has refused to sum. I looked at the date again. I checked it against the almanac page I keep folded in my sleeve, because a debt-bound man knows the calendar the way a sailor knows the tide-tables: tonight's date, written there in my own hand that morning, matched the entry exactly.

And beneath the date, circled — circled in ink so fresh it seemed to still be deciding whether to dry — was a line of Vessa's, transcribed in that same deteriorating hand:

*On this night the thief will find his name, and finding it, will begin.*

Beneath that, in a different ink entirely, brown-black and thin, someone had written a single word:

*Ravensworth.*

* * *

I am going to be honest with you, because honesty is cheap when no one can hear it: my first thought was that Pell had done it. Pell, my fellow scribe, my colleague in the catalogue room, a man whose entire personality was a slow-burning resentment of everyone whose debts were smaller than his. Pell knew my name. Pell knew I worked the seizure crates. Pell had the humor of a man who thinks a scorpion in a boot is funny if the boot isn't his.

I turned the book over looking for glue marks, fresh ink bleed-through, any sign of forgery. There were none. The brown-black word had sunk into the paper the way ink sinks when it has had decades to do its sinking. I held the page near the lamp — carefully, because the Temple's lamps are watched even when their light is not — and the fibers of the paper had drunk the word deep. Whatever else it was, *Ravensworth* had been written a long time ago.

Which raised a question I did not enjoy.

I am not the only Ravensworth in Vharen. There's a family of them in the Weavers' Quarter, prosperous, insufferable, none of whom would be caught dead in a seizure room. There was a Ravensworth who kept a pie stall on Cooper's Lane until he died of his own cooking. A century is a long time. Names drift down through a city like silt through water.

But.

But the circle was drawn around tonight's date, and the circle was fresh, and the word beneath it was old, and somewhere between those two facts a floor I had been standing on for twenty-nine years quietly removed itself.

You know the feeling? Probably not, if your life has been sound. It's not fear, exactly. Fear has a direction — away from something. This was more like vertigo, the moment on a stair when your foot expects one step and finds air. My whole existence was a set of steps I could recite: wake, work, tally the debt, sleep, repeat, and someday — fifteen years, thirty if I was careful — the account would close and I would own my own hands again. Every day of it accounted for. Every hour mortgaged. It was a terrible life but it was *mine*, it was *legible*, and legibility is worth more to a debtor than comfort.

And now a dead woman's borrowed voice, filtered through a dead scribe's tired pen, claimed to know how my story went.

*The thief will find his name.*

I'm not a thief. I want that on the record, wherever records go when no one reads them. I sell charms, yes — luck-knots, salt-wraps, little braided things that work or don't depending on how much you believe in braiding — but selling charms to frightened widows is commerce, not theft, whatever the wardens say. I copy documents accurately. I return overpaid coppers when I notice them, which is often enough to mention. If you asked anyone on Ash Street to name a thief, they'd point at the Temple, and they'd say it loudly, and then they'd lower their voices, because that's Vharen.

So the prophecy was wrong. Obviously. Prophecies are wrong the way stopped clocks are right — twice a day, and only if you're standing in front of them.

Except.

Except that I was standing in front of it. On the night it named. Holding it in a room where no one was supposed to know I read the stock. Having found it in a crate that someone had hidden it in *badly*, or *deliberately*, and I couldn't decide which possibility I liked less.

I sat down on the stool. My legs made the decision before I did.

For a while — I couldn't tell you how long; the temple bells had gone quiet and the lamp burned the same amount of oil whether minutes passed or hours — I just looked at the word. *Ravensworth.* The ink had gone the color of dried blood, which I noted with the detached professionalism of a man who has catalogued enough anatomy texts to know what dried blood looks like on vellum. Someone had written my name in this book before I was born. Before my father was born. And then someone else, recently, recently enough that the paper still remembered it, had drawn a circle around the date and waited for me to come pick the book up.

Books don't wait. People wait. Books just sit there being patient about it.

I thought about Sera. I don't know why — yes I do, I always know why; she is the fixed point my thoughts navigate by, the way sailors use a star they'll never reach. She'd finished her rounds an hour ago. She'd be on Ash Street now, trimming the last lamp outside the widow Maren's door, probably saving half her supper for me out of habit, probably coughing into her sleeve when she thought the street wasn't watching. If I told her about this — and I tell her everything, that's the whole architecture of us — she would laugh first, because she laughs first at everything, and then she would go quiet in that way she goes quiet, the way that means she's already three moves ahead of me and doesn't like the board.

*You look like a man reading his own obituary,* she'd say. *Is it a good one? Do I feature?*

She features. God help me, in every version of every story I've ever been in, she features. Which is precisely why I put the book down.

I put it down, and then I picked it up again, because here is the thing about a man one step ahead of his creditors: he cannot leave a document unread. Unread documents are how debtors die. Somewhere in this book, possibly, was a clause, a condition, a loophole — and loopholes are the only scripture I've ever believed in.

I turned to the next page.

The entry continued. More of Vessa's words, more of the tired scribe's transcription, and I read them standing up because sitting suddenly felt too much like surrender. I won't reproduce them all here — partly because my memory of them has the strange lacunae of things read in shock, and partly because what I remember most clearly is not the words themselves but the sensation of each line arriving like a bill: individually survivable, collectively ruinous.

*A thief shall go down beneath the mountain,* the dead woman said, in the voice of a dead scribe's pen. *He shall steal back what was sold. The court below will call it theft. The city above will call it mercy. Both will be wrong.*

And then, the line that I have since had occasion to memorize against my will, the way you memorize the shape of a knife pointed at you:

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

I closed the book.

The lamp hissed. Outside, Vharen went about its evening — cart wheels, a drunk singing three streets over, the ordinary machinery of a city that did not know it had been mentioned in dispatches. My hands were steady, which surprised me. Inside, nothing was steady; inside was the vertigo, the missing stair, the floor of my ordinary life giving way with the soft, complete silence of a thing that had never really been load-bearing at all.

Because here is what I understood, sitting there in the seizure room with a century-old book and four coppers an hour: the prophecy didn't need me to believe it. Belief was optional. Attendance was not. Tonight's date was circled, and I was in it, and the only question — the question I would spend the next nine days learning to ask properly — was whether a man could be written into a story and still refuse to play his part.

Or whether refusing was also a part.

I did the sensible thing. I catalogued the book — *author: Vessa of the Low Steps (deceased); subject: prophecy; disposition: pending* — and I shelved it in the row of pre-Ban miscellanies where the assessors never look, and I told myself I would report it in the morning, absolutely, first thing, to Master Ellery himself, because that was procedure and procedure keeps debtors alive.

Then I sat back down at my desk, pulled a sheet of scratch paper toward me, and copied the entry out by hand, every word, in my best clerk's fist, the way I copy everything: accurately, completely, and in direct violation of the third clause of the Ban.

A man one step ahead learns early that the rules and the survival instinct are not always pointing the same direction. Tonight they had parted company entirely.

The last bell rang midnight. I blew out the lamp, pocketed my illegal copy, and walked home through a city that felt suddenly like a stage set — solid-looking, familiar, and built by someone with plans for me.

Behind me, in the dark of the archive, a hundred-year-old book sat in its shelf, patient as a trap, waiting for morning to make it someone else's problem.

It would not stay shelved. Some part of me knew that even then — knew it the way you know a cough is more than smoke.

Morning would bring Master Ellery. And Master Ellery, I would learn, had been waiting far longer than the book had.
