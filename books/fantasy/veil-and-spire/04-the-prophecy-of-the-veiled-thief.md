## 4. The Prophecy of the Veiled Thief

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the deep shelves of the Temple archive, and I have come to believe it is not silence at all. It is listening. A thousand years of confiscated books, stacked spine to spine in the dark, all of them waiting for someone foolish enough to pull one down.

Tonight, apparently, that someone was me.

I had told myself I would only look. That's the lie every thief tells, and yes, I include myself in that company now, though at the time I still thought of myself as a scribe with poor judgment. The book sat where I'd left it an hour ago — *Consolidated Seizures, Century Nineteen, Unclassified* — and it sat the way a debt sits. Patiently. Knowing you'll come back.

I checked the aisle both ways. Old habit; new stakes. The archive lamps burned low at this hour, and Master Ellery had gone up to vespers, which meant I had perhaps forty minutes before he came shuffling back down with candle wax on his fingers and that mild, terrible attentiveness of his. Forty minutes to read a book I was not permitted to read, about a date that should not exist, bearing my name.

I opened it to the marked page.

The paper was good stock, older than my grandfather, and someone had treated it against damp and worms, which meant whoever wrote this had expected it to last. Expected it to be found. The ink had gone brown at the edges like old blood, which is a comparison I wish I hadn't made, because once made, it refused to leave.

The heading read: *The Prophecy of the Veiled Thief.*

And beneath it, in a hand steadier than mine has ever been:

*In the hundredth year of the ban, when the bells ring nine and the moon stands debtor to the dark, a thief will descend to the court beneath the mountain.*

*He will carry no weapon but a name not his own.*

*He will carry no light but the light of the one whose soul he comes to steal back.*

*The court will know him. The court will welcome him. For the court has been promised a theft, and the court keeps its promises.*

*But mark this, and mark it well: the thief's hand will be guided by betrayal. He cannot reach the flame except through treachery, and he cannot return except by spending what he loves.*

*What is sold may be stolen. What is stolen may be kept. But nothing beneath the mountain returns without a price, and the price is always named too late.*

I read it twice. Three times. My lips were moving on the fourth pass and I stopped myself, because reading prophecy aloud in a temple archive felt like the sort of thing that gets a man noticed by forces he'd rather invoice from a distance.

Here is what I want you to understand about me, since we've gotten this far together: I do not believe in fate. Fate is what rich people call their luck and poor people call their sentence. I have spent six years living one step ahead of the Temple of Nine Bells, and every step of that dance has been choreographed by very human things — interest rates, informants, the price of lamp oil. When people told me the mountain below Vharen held a court that bought souls, I nodded the way you nod at ghost stories: politely, while checking the exits.

But the date circled on the previous page was tonight's date. And the name written beneath it, in that same steady brown-inked hand, was *Orsen Ravensworth*.

You can disbelieve in fate all you like. It doesn't seem to require your belief to function. Much like the Temple.

I set the book down on the reading ledge and pressed my palms flat against the wood until the grain bit into my skin. Grounding. Sera taught me that — when the coughing fits take her, she presses her hands flat and counts the knots in the lamppost wood until her breath comes back. *The world is still here, Orsen. You can feel it. It's still here.*

The world was still here. It just had a hole in it now, shaped exactly like the next nine days.

Because that was the other thing. The prophecy spoke of "the hundredth year of the ban," and any child in Vharen could tell you the ban entered its hundredth year this winter — there had been speeches, and a commemorative bell-ringing nobody attended, and a special tax everyone did. Nine days from tonight was the solstice. The bells ring nine at solstice. I did the arithmetic three times hoping it would come out differently, because that is also an old habit, though usually I reserve it for ledgers.

*A thief will descend.* Fine. I steal things. Small things, mostly — charms and luck-knots, the petty contraband of frightened people. I had never stolen anything that couldn't be hidden in a sleeve. The idea of stealing a *soul* — of reaching into whatever vault the mountain kept and lifting out something that used to be a person —

Except.

That was the thought that stopped my hands from shaking, or rather redirected the shaking somewhere more useful. *Steal back.* Not steal. Steal *back*. Which meant the soul in question had been taken first, sold, filed away in the court's ledgers like so much seized inventory. And I knew — I knew with the sick certainty of a man who has spent his life reading other people's debts — that somewhere in the Temple's records was a transaction. A sale. Someone had sold years of themselves to pay for something, the way everything in Vharen eventually gets sold to pay for something.

And if the prophecy chose me — if some century-dead hand had written my name under tonight's date — then perhaps it wasn't random. Perhaps the soul belonged to someone whose debts I knew.

Whose debts I *caused*.

I didn't let myself finish that thought. Not yet. There's a discipline to dread: you ration it, or it eats you whole before breakfast.

* * *

Copying it was illegal. Let's be clear about that from the outset, because I want the record to show I knew exactly what I was doing, which is more than most men in Vharen can say about their crimes.

Temple law, Statute the Fourth: no prophecy, augury, or divinatory text may be reproduced outside the archive. The penalty is a fine I couldn't pay in ten lifetimes, or service, and "service" is a word the Temple uses the way the mountain court uses "purchase." Soft word. Sharp teeth.

I copied it anyway.

My hands were steadier than I expected them to be. That surprised me — I'd assumed terror, but what I felt instead was something worse: purpose. Terrible, specific purpose, the kind that arrives uninvited and starts rearranging your furniture. Every stroke of the pen felt less like transcription and more like signing.

I worked small, the way I do everything. Quarter-sheets of scrap vellum, the kind I keep for practice alphabets, tucked inside my ledger so they looked like accounting notes. My own shorthand over the top of it — a scribe's cipher, dots and tails that turned "the thief's hand will be guided by betrayal" into something that looked like a column of sums. If a warden shook me out on Ash Street, they'd find a man anxiously balancing accounts. Which, in fairness, I am. Always. Forever.

But even caged in cipher, the words kept their weight. I could feel them through the disguise, the way you can feel a bruise through a sleeve.

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

There it was again. I'd copied it four times now and it hadn't softened. Betrayal wasn't a risk in this prophecy; it was a component. An ingredient. The text didn't say *beware* betrayal, or *guard against* betrayal. It said the theft *cannot happen* without it. As if treachery were a toll on the road, and the road had no other route.

And I found myself doing the arithmetic I'd sworn off — the other kind, the human kind. Who would I betray? Everyone I had, I realized, was already betrayed-adjacent. Sera, who covered my debts and called it a joke. Ellery, who had either planted this book for me or failed spectacularly to prevent me finding it, and I genuinely did not know which possibility frightened me more. The widow I'd sold the luck-charm to, who trusted me precisely because I seemed like a man who wouldn't sell her out. Pell, who — no. Not Pell. Pell was nothing. Pell was a fellow scribe with ink-stained cuffs and a laugh like a hinge. Surely not Pell.

Funny how the mind, given a list of betrayals to choose from, starts ranking them by convenience. I hated myself a little for how quickly the ranking formed. I hated the prophecy more for asking.

*He cannot return except by spending what he loves.*

Spend. Like coin. Like the years Sera supposedly — no. Still not finishing that thought. Rationing. Discipline.

I blew the copy dry, folded it into my ledger between two pages of genuine Temple invoices, and stood there in the listening silence feeling the shape of the next nine days settle over my shoulders like a coat cut for a bigger man. Somewhere above me, the vespers bells rang seven, muffled by stone, and I counted the strokes out of habit and heard, underneath them, the memory of *nine* — the number the prophecy wanted, the number coming in nine days whether I consented or not.

One question remained, and it was the question I'd been circling the way a tongue circles a broken tooth: why me? Out of all the debtors and smugglers and desperate men in Vharen, why had a century-old book put *my* name under *tonight's* date?

There are only two kinds of answers to that question. Either fate had chosen me — which I refused to believe — or a person had. And a person, unlike fate, can be found, confronted, and asked to explain themselves.

I put the book back exactly as I'd found it, spine aligned, dust undisturbed — though dust, I suspected, was the least of what had been disturbed tonight. Then I took up my lamp and started down the aisle toward the stairs, toward the door, toward the night and the nine days and the name I'd just committed treason to carry.

Halfway there, I stopped.

Because on the reading ledge behind me, where the prophecy book had lain, the air still smelled faintly of brown ink and old blood, and beneath it — fresh, deliberate, unmistakable — the scent of lamp oil. The same oil Sera burned. The same oil I'd been selling in charms for six years.

Someone had handled that book recently. Recently enough to leave it. Recently enough to *place* it.

Master Ellery's footsteps creaked on the stair above me, slow and unhurried, wax and patience descending.

I turned to face the dark at the top of the aisle, and I did not run, and I did not speak, and I understood — with the calm of a man watching his own trap close — that whatever happened next, the prophecy had already accounted for it.

It had, after all, been written by someone who knew exactly how to make a scribe read to the end.
