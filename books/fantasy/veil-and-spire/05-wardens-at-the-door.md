## 5. Wardens at the Door

I smelled the raid before I saw it.

That's not a lamplighter's gift, though we do have good noses — you spend your life breathing smoke and you learn to tell the difference between a chimney's honest soot and something wrong. This was wrong in a specific way: hot iron and sanctified oil, the smell of warden lanterns burning consecrated flame. They say you can't smell magic. You can't. But you can smell what people burn to hunt it.

It was just past dusk on Ash Street, which meant I had eleven lamps left on my round and no time at all. The wardens came up from Bellows Lane four strong, grey coats, grey hoods, their lanterns swinging that particular slow pendulum swing that said *we are not in a hurry because there is nowhere you can run*.

And Orsen Ravensworth had forty-one luck-charms hidden under his floorboards.

Now, before you judge me for knowing that number exactly — a lamplighter knows her street. She knows which shutters stick, which widow waters her step-plants with gin, and which scribe on the corner keeps a stock of illegal charms beneath a loose plank he thinks nobody has noticed him prying up at odd hours for three years. Orsen thought he was subtle. Orsen is many things — clever, kind in the way of men who'd deny it under oath, funny when he forgets to be frightened — but subtle is not among them. He pries up that plank like a man defusing a bomb every single time, sweat on his upper lip, glancing over both shoulders like a heron checking for cats.

I love him like a brother. I have wanted to shake him like a rug since the day I met him.

The wardens stopped at the mouth of Ash Street and did the thing wardens do, which is stand very still and let the street be afraid for them. Fear does half their work. A street that's afraid will hand them whatever they want, point at whoever they need pointed at, and thank them after. Old Man Corvo was already pulling his shutters closed. Mrs. Tallow gathered her children inside with the practiced calm of a woman who has done this arithmetic before — how many heartbeats between seeing grey coats and being somewhere else.

Me, I stood under my own lamp on the corner, pole in hand, and did my own arithmetic.

Forty-one charms. One loose plank. Four wardens. And Orsen — where was Orsen? He should've been home by now. He kept temple hours even when he wasn't working, because debtors keep temple hours the way tides keep the moon. If he was still at the archive, then the charms were alone under the floor, and the only thing standing between four wardens and a lifetime sentence for my best friend was me, a lamp-pole, and whatever lie I could build fast enough to hold weight.

I've built lies before. Lamplighters lie constantly, professionally. *No, mistress, that lamp's been out since sundown, must be a fault in the line.* *Yes, master, I trimmed it, it's the oil that's gone thin.* We lie small and often enough that the big ones come easy. But I'd never lied to a warden's face, and I want to be honest with you, since honesty is about to become scarce in this story: my hands were shaking. Not from cold.

The lead warden peeled off from the others and came toward me, boots ringing on the cobbles, and I made myself look bored. Boredom is armor. Frightened people get searched; bored people get ignored.

"Lamplighter." He had a voice like a ledger closing. "Seen anyone selling charms on this street?"

"Charms?" I said, and gave him the face Mrs. Tallow gives me when I ask about the rent. The face that says *what an odd question, what an odd little man*. "You mean luck-charms? Warden, if I had luck, do you think I'd be lighting lamps for nine coppers a night?"

He didn't smile. Wardens don't, professionally. But one of the others behind him made a sound that might have been a laugh strangled at birth, and I filed that away. There's always one. There's always one who remembers being a person.

"Temple received a tip," the lead said. "Charm-seller operating out of Ash Street. We'll be searching premises."

"Search away," I said. "Mind the Tallow house, the baby's sleeping. And Corvo's got a bad hip, don't let him try to hurry."

This is a trick I learned from watching my mother handle bailiffs: give them more than they asked for. Directions, courtesies, small human details. It makes you a fixture instead of a suspect. Fixtures don't get arrested. Mostly.

They started at the far end. Which was wrong — or right, depending — because Orsen's room was at the near end, above the chandler's, third door, the one with the crooked shutter. Every minute they spent rapping on Corvo's door was a minute bought. I went back to my lamps. What else does a lamplighter do? I lit the next one. My hands were steadier with the flint than they'd been with my mouth, which tells you something about where I keep my courage. It lives in my hands. It always has.

I was trimming the wick outside the chandler's when I heard it — the particular scrape of a window opening above me, soft as a cat's apology. I didn't look up. You never look up. But I knew that scrape, and a moment later something dropped into the hood of my coat, wrapped in a rag that smelled of Orsen's cheap ink.

A note. Of course a note. The man writes notes the way other men breathe.

*Sera — if they're here for the stock, it's under the floor, third plank, DON'T let them take the ledger too, it looks worse than it is. Burn everything. I'm sorry. I'll pay you back. — O*

*I'll pay you back.* Three years of knowing this man and those were the four words I could set my watch by. He owed the Temple of Nine Bells a sum I'd once seen written down and immediately wished I hadn't, and every coin he earned went into that bottomless purse, and every time trouble found him he promised repayment like a man promising the tide won't come in.

Well. Tonight the tide was coming in wearing grey coats, and I decided, standing there under the chandler's sign with a note dissolving in my palm, that some debts run the other direction.

Here is what I did, and I'll tell it plain because I'm proud of it and pride is also scarce:

I finished my lamp. Then I walked around the corner, knocked on Orsen's door the way a neighbor knocks — two raps, casual — and let myself in with the key under the sill that he thinks I don't know about. Inside, the room smelled of him: ink, candle grease, the ghost of yesterday's bread. The floorboard was where it always was. I pried it up — and yes, I do it better than he does, one motion, no heron-glancing — and there they were. Forty-one little charms, each one a scrap of bone or copper or knotted thread with something old and patient coiled inside it, waiting to be sold to frightened people who needed a little luck the way drowning men need air.

Magic is outlawed in Vharen. Has been for a hundred years, ever since the mountain stopped answering and everyone decided the safest thing to do about power was pretend it wasn't there. But outlawing magic doesn't outlaw needing it. That's why scribes with steady hands and no future make charm-stock in their spare rooms. That's why widows buy. That's why wardens have work.

I took the charms to the hearth. Orsen's fire was down to embers, so I fed it the kindling first, got it proper and hungry, and then I began feeding it everything else.

I want to say it was quick. It wasn't. Forty-one charms takes time, because you can't just pitch them all in — some of them *resist*, the way a wet log resists, except a wet log doesn't whisper. I won't tell you what they whispered. Some nights I still hear it, and I've learned not to listen, the way you learn not to touch a bruise.

While the fire ate, I searched for the ledger. Found it under his mattress, because Orsen hides things like a child hides sweets — the first place anyone would look. It was worse than he thought and less than the wardens would hope: names, prices, dates. His whole shadow-business in his own neat scribe's hand. I put it in the fire too, and watched three years of careful bookkeeping curl and go black, and felt — I'll admit it — a savage little joy. Let the Temple chase numbers that no longer exist. Let them audit ash.

Then the knocking started on Orsen's door, and I sat down in his chair, and I became a woman waiting for her friend with tea going cold, which is the oldest and most boring story in the world.

"Open in the name of the Nine Bells."

"It's open," I called, "it's always open, the lock's been broke since winter."

Two wardens came in, then the lead, filling the doorway like the doorway owed him money. Their lantern-light swept the room. Behind them, in the hall, the young one — the almost-laugh one — hung back, and our eyes met for half a second, and I made a bet with myself about him. More on that later, maybe. Maybe not.

"You're not Ravensworth," the lead said.

"No," I agreed. "I'm the lamplighter. Sera Voss. He owes me for the last three rounds of candles, if you're taking collections."

"What are you doing in his rooms?"

"Waiting. He asked me to mind his fire while he worked late at the archive — man's terrified of burning, always has been, sleeps with a bucket by the bed, you can check." I gestured at the hearth with my chin. "As you can see, I've been keeping it lively."

The lead warden crossed to the hearth. I will not lie to you and say I was calm. My heart was going like a drum in a procession. But I'd done my work well: the charms were past identifying, ground into the general ash, and the fire was roaring the way fires do when they've been fed things that burn hot and strange. It threw sparks. It made a sound I've only heard once since — a low singing, like a wire in wind. The warden stared into it for a long moment.

"Smells odd," he said.

"Bought bad coal off a cart-man last week," I said. "Burns green sometimes. I keep telling Ravensworth to stop being cheap about coal but the man counts coppers in his sleep." I leaned forward, conspiratorial, tired, a woman with sore feet and no patience left. "Warden, if you're here about charms, you've wasted a walk. Orsen Ravensworth is the most lawful man on this street. He alphabetizes his sins. The worst thing he does is read after lights-out, and I know because I can see his candle through the shutter."

Something flickered behind the lead warden's eyes. Not belief. Wardens don't believe; they weigh. He was weighing me — my lamp-pole leaning by the door, my worn apron, the tea I'd actually had the presence of mind to pour, the ash, the ordinary clutter of a poor man's room. Weighing it against a tip somebody had sold them.

"Where is Ravensworth?"

"The archive, like I said. Temple keeps him till the ninth bell most nights." I sighed, rubbed my eyes, and played my last card, the one I'd been saving because it cost the most. "Look — if he's in trouble, tell me now, because I've covered for that fool before and I'd rather know what I'm covering."

Ah, that landed. Confession-shaped bait always lands. The lead warden's head tilted a degree. "Covered for him how?"

"He had a book last year he shouldn't have had. Borrowed, he swore. I told the door-warden he was with me." I shrugged, the shrug of long-suffering familiarity. "Men like him don't mean wrong. They just forget the world has teeth."

Silence, except the fire singing its wire-song. Then the lead turned to the young one in the hall. "Run to the archive. Confirm he's at his desk. Don't bring him — report."

The young one went. Boots on stairs, fading. Bought, I thought. Hours, maybe. Maybe only minutes, but hours if the archive clerk on duty was the sleepy one, Brother Anselm, who signs the attendance sheet without looking up.

The lead warden stayed another quarter hour, poking through Orsen's things with a courtesy that was somehow worse than roughness — lifting the mattress (the ledger's absence a hole shaped exactly like guilt), tapping walls, peering into the cold stove upstairs where Orsen *didn't* keep anything because Orsen kept everything under the floor, which was now empty, which was now just a floor. I drank my tea. I commented on the weather. I yawned once, genuinely, and the yawning helped more than any lie, because it made the whole night boring again, and boredom is the best disguise truth ever wears.

At the door, he paused. "If Ravensworth returns tonight, tell him the Temple wishes to speak with him. Tomorrow. Noon."

"I'll tell him," I said. "Noon. Anything else?"

He looked at me a moment longer than he needed to. Wardens have that trick, the long look, trying to see the seam in you. "Your hands are burned," he said.

I looked down. He was right. Along my right palm and fingers, three fresh blisters rising, shiny in the lantern-light — burns from the charms, from pulling them out of the fire when they clung, from feeding them in bare-handed because I hadn't taken time to find a poker and couldn't risk one.

"Catch sparks off a bad lamp once," I said, "you learn respect for flame." I smiled. He didn't. He left.

I stood in Orsen's dark room until their footsteps faded off Ash Street entirely, and then I sat down on the floor — the actual floor, third plank, empty — and laughed until it turned into something else, which I'll call coughing, because that's what I called it then.

Because here's the thing about the coughing. It had been coming for months. Little dry catches at first, ignorable, the kind of thing you blame on coal-smoke and lamplight fumes and Vharen's general talent for fouling its own air. But lately — since midsummer — the catches had grown teeth. Some mornings I woke and my chest felt full of sand. Some nights, lighting the last lamps, my breath whistled faintly through me like wind through a keyhole, and I'd stand there under the flame thinking: *that's not right. That's not smoke. Smoke sits in the throat. This is deeper. This is further down.*

Further down than lungs have any business reaching.

I hadn't told Orsen. Obviously. Tell Orsen what? *I'm ill* becomes *I'm dying* becomes Orsen Ravensworth doing something catastrophic, because that man cannot leave a problem alone, he has to *solve* it, and the problems he can't solve he tries to buy, and the ones he can't buy he goes looking for doors that ought to stay shut. I'd seen him do it with his debts for three years — chasing every scheme, every side-trade, every desperate little economy — and I was not going to hand that man a problem shaped like my own death. He'd sell himself to fix it. He might already have, in ways I hadn't checked, in ways I was afraid to check.

So I coughed into my sleeve, alone, in the room I'd just saved him from, and when it passed I got up, banked the fire, straightened his chair, and put the key back under the sill.

On my way out I paused at the window. Down the street, the wardens were regrouping at the corner, lanterns swaying, and beyond them the city lay under its curfew-hush, ten thousand shuttered windows, and beyond *that*, black against the stars, Mount Vhalen with its buried court that nobody talks about, that everybody pays.

My chest whistled when I breathed. I pressed my hand flat against it, over the burns, and made it a promise, the way you promise a horse before a long road: *Hold. Just hold.*

Orsen came home at the ninth bell, white-faced, having heard from Brother Anselm that wardens had been asking after him. He burst through his own door ready to confess to everything, I think, possibly to crimes he hadn't committed yet, and found me sitting in his chair with my feet on his hearthstone and the last of his tea in my hand.

"They're coming back at noon," I said. "The floor's clean. So is the mattress, which, honestly, Orsen, the *mattress*—"

He stared at the hearth. At the ash. At me. I watched him do the arithmetic — forty-one charms, one fire, one lamplighter with singed fingers — and I watched the exact moment it landed on him, the size of it, what I'd burned and lied and bled to buy him. His eyes went bright and furious and grateful all at once, and he opened his mouth, and I knew — I *knew* — he was about to say *I'll pay you back.*

"Don't," I said. "Don't you dare. Just sit down and help me figure out what you're going to tell them at noon."

But he wasn't listening anymore. He was looking at my bandaged hand, and then at my face, and his expression changed into something I liked much less — the look of a man who has just realized the debt runs the other way, and intends, with that terrible stubbornness of his, to settle it.

"Sera," he said slowly, "how long have you had that cough?"

* * *

Outside, far below the streets, beneath the stone and the roots of the mountain, something old turned over in its sleep — and in the temple archive, a century-old book lay open to a page with tomorrow circled, waiting for a scribe who had just lost his alibi, his stock, and very nearly his freedom, and gained, in exchange, a reason to start asking dangerous questions.

Orsen poured me tea. His hands weren't shaking.

Mine were.
