## 6. The Archivist Who Knew Too Much

I did not sleep.

I want that on the record, in some ledger somewhere, in a hand steadier than mine: on the night I found my own name written beneath a date a century old, I sat at my desk with the illegal copy of a prophecy warming under my shirt like a stolen loaf, and I did not sleep. I drank cold tea instead. I read the words until they stopped looking like words and started looking like teeth.

*On this night shall come the thief.*

*He will descend to the court beneath the mountain and steal back what was sold.*

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

And beneath it, in ink older than my grandfather: *Orsen Ravensworth.*

There are exactly two explanations for a thing like that. Either the universe had been keeping a file on me since before I could hold a pen — which flattered me in a way I found deeply insulting — or someone had put it there. And of those two, only one had hands.

By morning I knew whose.

Master Ellery kept hours like a man who had never once been surprised by dawn. He arrived at the archive before the bells, unlocked the doors with three keys he wore on a cord around his neck, and spent the first hour of every day dusting shelves no one else was permitted to touch. I had catalogued for him four years. In all that time I had never seen him hurry, never seen him sweat, never seen him look at a book the way I now understood he must have looked at one particular book — the way a man looks at a trap he has already baited.

I went in early. Earlier than Ellery. I let myself into the archive with the scribe's key I wasn't strictly supposed to have after hours, and I sat down in his chair, behind his desk, and waited.

It's amazing how much courage you can borrow from someone else's furniture.

The bells rang six. The door opened. Ellery came in carrying his morning bread and a pot of honey, humming — actually humming, a little tuneless hymn, the kind of sound a man makes when his conscience is either perfectly clean or perfectly buried — and stopped when he saw me.

To his credit, he didn't drop the bread.

"Orsen," he said. "You're early."

"You're not surprised."

"No," he agreed, setting the bread down with the care of a man arranging pieces on a board. "I'm not."

"Sit down, Master Archivist."

"It's my chair."

"I know," I said. "That's rather the point."

He looked at me for a long moment. Ellery is not an impressive man to describe — soft around the middle, spectacles forever sliding, ink stains up both forearms like a disease of the sleeves — but I had watched him stare down a warden-captain over a misfiled seizure once, and I knew the trick of him. He simply refused to be moved. It is a kind of power. It is, I would learn that morning, a very old kind.

He sat. Not in his chair — in the visitor's seat across from it, folding himself down with a small grunt, and I realized I'd expected him to argue longer. A man who argues is a man who wants something from the argument. Ellery wanted nothing. That frightened me more than shouting would have.

"How long have you known?" I asked.

"Known what?" he said mildly. "Be specific. Precision matters in an archive."

"The book. *The Veiled Thief.* The date. My name." I pulled the copy from my shirt and laid it on the desk between us, flat, like a card I couldn't afford to bet. "How long have you known?"

He looked at the page without touching it. His eyes moved over the lines the way a man reads a letter he wrote himself.

"Since before you were born," he said.

The room got very quiet. Somewhere above us, the Nine Bells were doing their morning arithmetic, counting out the hour in bronze, and I remember thinking absurdly that the Temple charged the city for every ring, and that even the sound of time passing in Vharen came with a debt attached.

"Explain," I said, "or I walk out of here and straight to the wardens, and we discover together whether they believe prophecies or burn them."

"They believe them," Ellery said. "That's the problem. They believe them so thoroughly they've spent a century making sure none ever comes true where anyone can see." He took off his spectacles, breathed on them, polished them with his sleeve — a stalling gesture, I realized, performed by a man who had rehearsed this conversation for years and still needed a moment. "You won't go to the wardens, Orsen. You've already decided that. If you were going to, you'd have gone last night. Instead you sat up reading it. I'd wager my pension you've memorized it."

I hated him a little, because he'd won the wager.

"Why me?"

"Ah." He put the spectacles back on. "There it is. The real question. Not *what*, not *how* — why me. You're angry about being chosen."

"I'm angry about being *used*."

"Yes," he said. "Those feel the same from inside. They aren't." He leaned forward, and for the first time the mildness dropped away, and underneath it was something tired and old and terribly certain. "Do you know what the Temple of Nine Bells actually does, Orsen? Not the sermons. Not the charity loaves. Do you know what we do?"

"You keep records."

"We keep *accounts*." He said it the way a priest says a holy word. "Every debt in this city passes through these archives. Every loan, every tithe, every fine. And every debt, in the end, flows downhill. Down past the ledger halls, past the vaults, past the foundations—" he tapped the floor twice, softly, "—down to the court beneath Mount Vhalen. You've heard the stories. Souls sold to settle what coin cannot. Everyone hears the stories. Everyone assumes they're stories."

"And they're not."

"They're invoices." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I have ever seen on a kind face. "Thirty years ago, Vharen owed a sum it could not pay. A bad harvest, a war levy, a plague year — the details bore everyone who isn't a historian. The city was going to default, and the court beneath the mountain does not forgive default. It *collects*. Whole streets, Orsen. Names off doorposts. Children born already owing. So a younger archivist than I am now went down the old roads with an offer: the city's safety, in exchange for a steady supply. Debtor souls. The ones nobody would miss. The ones the ledgers had already given up on."

My mouth had gone dry. "You."

"I," he agreed, without drama, without shame, and somehow that was worse than either. "One soul a season, delivered by arrangement, and Vharen keeps its sky. Thirty years. Do the arithmetic — I have. It comes to somewhat fewer than a hundred and twenty people. I can name most of them. I choose not to, as a kindness to myself, which I recognize is not a kindness at all."

I stood up. I don't remember deciding to; my legs simply reported for duty. "You fed people to a hole in the world."

"I kept a city alive," Ellery said, unmoved. "You sell charms to widows, Orsen. Don't stand behind your little counter of morality and pretend you don't understand triage."

"That's different."

"Is it? The widow pays you because the alternative frightens her more. I pay the mountain because the alternative frightens me more. We are both merchants of fear. I'm merely better capitalized."

I walked to the window. There's a window in the archive, high and narrow, barred like everything else in the Temple, and through it you can see Ash Street if you crane — the smoke, the roofs, the thin threads of lamplighters' oil-smell rising even in daylight. I looked at it because I could not look at him.

"The prophecy," I said. "Where does it fit? Why plant it? Why me?"

"Because the arrangement is ending." For the first time, his voice changed — quickened — and I turned despite myself. "The court doesn't want debtor souls anymore. Small coin. It wants — it has grown *particular*. And when the court grows particular, it sends collectors up the old roads, and collectors do not distinguish between the guilty and the convenient. The city pays, Orsen. All of it. Unless—"

"Unless the prophecy."

"Unless the prophecy." He nodded slowly. "The book is older than the arrangement. Older than the Temple's claim on this hill. A century ago someone wrote down what happens next, and the court — I have reason to believe — treats fulfilled prophecy as *binding*. As contract. If the thief descends and steals back a sold soul, the theft itself settles accounts. A debt discharged by its own terms. The books balance. The collectors stay below. The city breathes."

"So you needed a thief."

"I needed a thief who would be believed by the mountain. Prophecies are picky about their casting. I didn't choose you, Orsen. I checked you against what was written, and you fit. There's a difference, and I understand that from where you stand it's a difference without a distinction, but I ask you to believe it matters to me."

"*Fit.*" I laughed, and it came out wrong, jagged. "What does that mean, fit? My name was in the book before I existed. How does a man *fit* a sentence written a hundred years ago?"

Ellery rose, crossed to the shelf he dusted every morning — the shelf no one else was permitted to touch — and drew out a slim volume I hadn't catalogued, wrapped in oiled cloth. He set it beside my copy.

"Because names have weight," he said. "Below, especially. Above too, if you know how to weigh them. Your family name appears in debt records going back two hundred years, always in the same column — the owing column. Ravensworths pay late and pay long. The court knows your blood the way a moneylender knows a face. When I read the prophecy's requirements — a scribe, debt-bound, named Ravensworth, with a soul-connection to a sold flame—" he paused, delicately, "—there was exactly one candidate in Vharen. I didn't build the trap. I just made sure the cheese was reachable."

"Soul-connection," I repeated. The words went through me cold. "What does that mean? What did you write — what does the book say about—"

"I didn't write anything," Ellery said. "I moved the book. Three weeks ago I re-shelved it into the seizure intake, misfiled just badly enough that you — you specifically, you with your habit of reading everything you catalogue, your incurable inability to leave a text alone — would find it. Everything else in that book was written a century ago. I only chose the shelf."

"But the connection. To a sold soul. Whose?"

He looked at me then with something I might have mistaken for pity, if pity weren't so often just cowardice wearing a softer coat.

"You already know," he said gently. "You've known since you read it. Ask yourself why you haven't slept. Ask yourself whose lamp you think of when the word *flame* appears on the page."

Sera. Three years of covered debts. Three years of a cough she laughed off, getting harder to laugh off. Bread shared on a cold step. *You'd do the same for me,* she always said, and I always said yes, and neither of us ever asked what she'd already paid.

I gripped the edge of the desk. The wood was old and smooth and utterly indifferent to me.

"If I refuse," I said. "If I burn the copy and the book and your arrangement with it. What happens?"

"Collectors," Ellery said simply. "First the debtors. Then the shelters. Then, working upward by amount, everyone the ledgers touch — which, in Vharen, is everyone. It will take years. It will be orderly. The mountain is nothing if not orderly." He folded his hands. "You would be trading one woman's freedom for a city's slow bleeding. Some men would call that noble. The mountain would call it Tuesday."

"And if I go?"

"Then you fulfill a century-old contract, steal a soul out of hell, betray someone along the way — the book is quite insistent on that part, and I confess I don't know who or how, only that the court will arrange it, because betrayal is the coin they prefer — and come back owing nothing." He smiled again, that terrible gentle smile. "Owing *nothing*, Orsen. Do you know what that's worth in this city? Neither do I. No one alive does."

I picked up my copy of the prophecy. My hands were steady, which surprised me; I'd have bet on shaking.

"You could have told me," I said. "Before. You could have asked."

"Would you have said yes?"

"No."

"There's your answer." He gathered his bread and his honey, the small domestic sacraments of a monster who liked his mornings ordinary. "For what it's worth — and I know precisely what it's worth, which is nothing — I am sorry. I chose the kindest possible cruelty available to me. Most archivists in my position would have simply arranged for you to owe the wrong person at the right time, and let necessity do the persuading."

"You say that like it's a comfort."

"I say it like an apology," he said. "I'm out of practice."

He went to the door. Stopped. Didn't turn around.

"One more thing, and then you should decide what you're going to do, because the date is nine days out and the veil beneath the temple opens whether you're ready or not." His voice had gone quiet, almost kind, which from Ellery was the loudest warning he knew how to give. "The prophecy says the thief's hand will be guided by betrayal. You're assuming that means you'll be betrayed. Read it again tonight. Grammar matters in an archive, Orsen. *Guided by* is not *victim of*."

The door closed behind him.

I stood alone in the archive with the barred light coming through the high window and the copy trembling in my hand, and I did what he knew I would do. I read it again.

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

Not *the thief will be betrayed.*

Grammar matters.

Which meant the hand that betrayed would be mine — and the question I carried out of the archive, down the stairs, out into the ringing morning, was one I already dreaded the answer to:

Whose?

* * *

I found her at dusk on Ash Street, trimming the third lamp from the corner, her ladder creaking, her cough held behind her teeth like a coin she didn't want to spend. She waved the little brass key at me in greeting.

"You look terrible," Sera called down. "Even for you."

"Sera," I said. "If I asked you something — anything — would you tell me the truth?"

She climbed down one rung and looked at me, and for half a second — half a second, no longer — something moved behind her eyes that looked horribly like guilt.

"Depends on the question," she said lightly.

And I thought: *she knows something too.*

Everyone in my life, it seemed, had been keeping accounts behind my back.
