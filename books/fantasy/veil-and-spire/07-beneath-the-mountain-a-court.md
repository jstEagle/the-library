## 7. Beneath the Mountain, a Court

I have been dead for a very long time, and I have found it agrees with me.

The living imagine death as an ending. The poor among them, at least, do — those who sell their hours by the dozen and their years by the fistful. They think of endings the way they think of debts: something that arrives, that is suffered, that is survived or not. They have never sat where I sit. They have never had eternity in which to learn the truth, which is that nothing ends. Nothing is ever allowed to end. Everything merely changes hands.

And I, if I may boast of my one accomplishment across these long centuries beneath Mount Vhalen, am very good at hands.

* * *

The court wakes when the mountain does, which is to say it never sleeps at all but performs a convincing imitation of rest. The great hall is carved from the mountain's own marrow — black stone veined with something that glows a dull amber, like coals banked for the night. My throne is not a throne, whatever the flatterers call it. It is a fault line. I sit upon the place where the world cracked first, and I have made myself comfortable there, which I consider the whole of statesmanship.

Above me rise the galleries: tier upon tier of seats cut into the rock, and in them sit my courtiers. Do not picture skeletons. Skeletons are honest, and honesty has no place in commerce. My courtiers are the shapes memory makes when it forgets itself — tall, courteous, indistinct at the edges, dressed in robes the color of deep water. Each one was once someone's grandmother, someone's sworn brother, someone's debt. They sold themselves down here piece by piece until what remained was pure function, and function, I have found, dresses beautifully.

Below the galleries, the floor of the hall opens into the auction pit: a ring of glass cases arranged like the petals of some iron flower. And in the cases —

Ah. In the cases.

Come closer. You may look. The living are always afraid to look, which is why they send scribes and clerks and men with ledgers instead of coming themselves. In each case burns a flame. Not fire — fire is only the living world's crude sketch of this. These flames are souls, or the portions of souls that were sold, and they burn with colors the surface has no names for. A blue-white one over there, steady as a surgeon's hand: a midwife from three generations back who sold her calm. A guttering red one: a soldier's courage, bought by a house that wanted its sons brave and got instead sons who wept at loud noises, because courage does not transfer cleanly and I never said it would.

The whispering is the part visitors never expect. Souls talk. Not in words — words are a surface affectation — but in that sound wind makes through wires, through reeds, through the ribs of a drowned ship. If you stand in the pit long enough, you begin to understand it, and then you wish you didn't, and then you stop standing in the pit, and that is why my courtiers rotate their duties.

I never leave the pit. Someone should listen.

* * *

Tonight's session began, as all sessions begin, with the reading of accounts.

My Master of Ledgers — he was a tax collector once, above; he died clutching his books and arrived still clutching them, and I admired the grip so much I gave him a kingdom's worth of accounting to do — descended the stairs from the counting-rooms with tonight's volume balanced on his palms like a sleeping child.

"Debts come due," he announced, in the voice of a man reading weather.

"Debts always come due," I replied. "It is their single virtue."

The hall stirred. Courtiers leaned forward on their benches, and the air took on that particular hush — the hush of appetite pretending to be etiquette. Above us, in Vharen, in the city of bells and bans, the Temple of Nine Bells had spent the week collecting. That is the arrangement, and it has been the arrangement for thirty years now, since a frightened archivist came down the old roads with an offer in his mouth and terror in his eyes. The Temple sends me its debtors' souls — the ones who sell years to clear accounts, the desperate, the loving, the foolish. In exchange, the city keeps its walls standing, its plagues mild, its fires few. A tithe of selves. The Temple calls it mercy. I call it supply.

"The Nine Bells remit fourteen," said the Master of Ledgers. "Fourteen souls, partial and whole, against the quarter's account."

"Fourteen." I let the number sit in the air like a coin tossed to see who will grab. "The city grows prudent. Prudence is poverty wearing a clean shirt."

A ripple of laughter through the galleries — or what passes for laughter down here, a sound like sand settling. My courtiers enjoy my wit. They would enjoy anything I said; that is what I made them for. But even flattery gets stale after a century, and so I treasure the other thing, the rare thing, the thing that actually entertains me:

Complication.

"Also," said the Master of Ledgers, and here he paused, and I confess my attention sharpened the way a cat's does when the mouse stops running, "the roads report movement. A veil was tested. Shallow water only — the toll-bridge at the River of Forgotten Things felt footsteps, four days past. The walker paid in small memories and retreated."

Now that was interesting.

The old roads are mine. Every veil between the world above and the court below is a door in a house I own, and I know when they open the way a man knows when his own front latch lifts. Surface walkers almost never come. When they do, they are usually scholars playing at bravery, and scholars pay their tolls in memories of lectures and go home lighter by nothing that matters. But this one had paid in small memories — a mother's voice, perhaps, a summer rain — and small memories are the currency of the serious. No tourist spends his childhood to take a stroll.

"Describe the walker," I said.

"The bridge could not see him clearly. Veils blur. But it noted the manner of his walking." The Master of Ledgers consulted his book, though he did not need to; the roads speak to him in ink. "Hesitant. Then stubborn. He turned back twice before he crossed."

I smiled. I felt myself do it — a slow thing, rusted at the hinges, all the more terrible for the disuse. Around me the courtiers went still, because they know what my smiling means the way sailors know what a falling barometer means.

"He turned back twice and crossed anyway," I said. "That is not a thief's walk. Thieves walk smooth. That is a man being dragged by something he loves more than his own sense."

"You believe he will return," said the Master of Ledgers. It was not quite a question. He has served me long enough to know I dislike answering questions I have already answered for myself.

"I believe," I said, rising from the fault line that is my throne, "that something is about to be stolen from me."

The word moved through the hall like a draft. *Stolen.* The courtiers whispered it to one another, tasting it. It has been a hundred years since anyone stole from the court. There was a woman once — a widow with her husband's sword and no sense whatsoever — who tried to carry out her son's flame in a lantern. She got as far as the Gate of Names. The gate kept her name, and the roads kept the rest, and I had her flame added to the collection out of respect. It burns green, hers. Spite burns green. It is my favorite color.

"But, Majesty," ventured a courtier from the third gallery, a shapeless courtesy of a creature who had been a lawyer above, "no theft can succeed. The prophecy—"

"Yes," I said pleasantly. "The prophecy."

Ah, the prophecy. Let us speak of it plainly, you and I, since no one down here dares to. A century ago, a seer above — dying, dishonest, and inspired, the best combination — wrote down a set of lines concerning my court. A thief would descend. A soul would be taken back. The thief's hand would be guided by betrayal. The Temple has kept the book locked ever since, turning its pages only when the dates grow near, and they have fed me debtors for thirty years on the theory that a full larder prevents famine.

But here is what the Temple does not understand, what the archivist with his frightened eyes has never grasped, what no one above has understood in a hundred years of careful, cowardly compliance:

Prophecy is not a wall. Prophecy is a script. And I did not survive a century beneath this mountain by obeying scripts written by the living.

Every prophecy wants fulfilling. That is their hunger, their engine, their terrible gravity. Fulfill me, they say, in the voice of your own heartbeat. And every power built on a fulfilled prophecy — as mine is, as my court's claim on the world above is — grows brittle at exactly one point: the moment of fulfillment, when the words become flesh and can therefore, like all flesh, be wounded.

So when a thief comes — and one is coming, I can feel him the way an old scar feels rain — I will not bar the door.

I will open it.

* * *

After the session ended and the courtiers dispersed to their alcoves to practice being forgotten, I walked the pit alone, as I do. The cases glowed around me in their iron flower. Fourteen new flames had been installed that evening, and I stopped before each one and listened, because that is the office of a king: to hear his subjects.

Most were ordinary. Fear, regret, a young man's ambition sold to pay a father's medical debts — ambition burns violet, incidentally, and smells faintly of ink. I passed them the way a miser passes familiar coins.

Then I stopped at the last case, near the petal's edge, where the light was dimmest.

The flame inside was pale gold. Lamplighter's gold — I know the trade's souls when I see them, warm and steady and worn thin at the edges from years of giving light away. This one had been sold in installments, three payments across three years, fifteen years of a life paid out a fistful at a time. It flickered as I watched, and the flicker had a rhythm to it that I recognized, because everything up there eventually comes down here, and rhythms travel: it flickered in time with a cough.

"Well," I said to it softly. "You are the reason, aren't you?"

The flame bent toward my voice the way a candle bends toward a draft. Souls cannot answer me. But they can lean.

Somewhere above, a scribe with hesitant-stubborn feet was reading a book he should not have found — a book I had permitted to be found, through channels I will not describe to you because a king must keep some instruments dark even from his chroniclers. Somewhere above, an exiled inquisitor was sharpening herself on old griefs. Somewhere above, a frightened old man was rehearsing justifications he had polished for thirty years. All the pieces were moving, drawn along grooves cut a century ago, and the grooves all ran downhill to me.

Let them come. Let the thief descend with his forged name and his borrowed courage. Let him stand in my hall and reach for what he loves. I will offer him the bargain every thief dreams of — the easy road, the betrayal as written — and he will take it, because they always take it, because love curdles into arithmetic the moment survival is mentioned, and I have never lost that wager in a hundred years of making it.

And when the prophecy fulfills itself through his willing hand, my claim on the world above will renew for another century, and the bells of Vharen will keep ringing their nine obedient notes, and the larder will stay full.

Unless.

Unless he does the one thing no thief has ever done. Unless, at the final page, faced with the choice between the script and the person, he chooses the person — and breaks the words rather than the friend. I have imagined it, the way I imagine all impossibilities; a king's hobby is contingency. And I will tell you what frightens me, in the privacy of this telling, where no courtier hears and no ledger records:

If a prophecy can be broken, then it can be rewritten. And if it can be rewritten, then everything I have built on the certainty of the old words — the court, the claims, the long patient architecture of debt — stands on paper. And paper burns.

I looked at the lamplighter's flame a while longer. It burned on, thin and gold and stubborn, spending itself for someone who did not yet know he was worth the spending.

"Soon," I told it, and my voice was kind, which from me is the cruelest thing there is. "Your thief is learning to walk."

Then I returned to my fault line and sat, and folded my hands, and waited — as I have waited a hundred years, as I could wait a hundred more — for the sound of footsteps on the old roads, coming down toward me with all the terrible, world-breaking weight of a man who has decided to be good.

Far above, muffled by a mountain's depth of stone, I thought I heard a bell.

It rang ten times.

One too many.

I smiled.
