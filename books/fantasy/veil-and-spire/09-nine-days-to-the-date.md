## 9. Nine Days to the Date

The prophecy allowed nine days.

I want you to appreciate the absurdity of that sentence. A book written a hundred years before my birth — by an author whose name had been scraped off the flyleaf like a debt from a ledger — had circled tonight's date in faded ink and put *Orsen Ravensworth* beneath it, and now it was generous enough to give me nine days to prepare. Not ten. Not a fortnight. Nine. As if fate were a moneylender with a heart of stone and a taste for round numbers.

"You could refuse," Master Ellery said, watching me across his desk in the archive's back room, where the lamps burned low and the dust had opinions about being disturbed.

"I could," I agreed. "And then?"

"Then the city pays." He said it the way a man says *the tide comes in*. No drama. No threat. Just arithmetic.

I had spent three years learning to live one step ahead of my debts, which meant I had also learned to recognize when someone was quoting terms at me. Ellery wasn't threatening me. He was reading me the contract.

"Fine," I said. "Nine days. Teach me."

He looked at me for a long moment, and there was something in his face I didn't have a name for yet — something between pity and apology. I would learn its name later, and I would hate it. But that night it just looked like an old man deciding how much truth a drowning man can carry.

"The old roads," he said, "are not roads. Remember that first. Everything else follows from remembering that."

* * *

We began at midnight, because of course we did. Everything forbidden happens at midnight; even the wardens' manuals assume it.

Ellery led me down past the archive's lowest shelf, past the room where seized books went to be forgotten, to a door I had walked past four hundred times without ever once seeing. It was a door made of not-being-looked-at, if that makes any sense. It doesn't? Good. You're already halfway to understanding veils.

Beyond it was a stairwell that smelled of cold stone and older cold, and at the bottom of the stairwell was a wall.

Not a remarkable wall. Mortared brick, damp at the seams, exactly the sort of wall you'd expect at the bottom of a temple stairwell. Ellery pressed his palm flat against it and said a word.

I won't write the word here. Partly because writing it felt dangerous even on paper, and partly because I've since learned that some words are like keys cut for one lock — mine wouldn't turn it anyway. But the wall heard him, and the wall *agreed*, and where his palm rested the brick went soft as the surface of dark water.

"A veil," he said. "A place where the world forgot to finish itself. The mountain's court lives in the unfinished places. So do the roads to it."

"And I'm supposed to walk through that."

"You're supposed to learn to walk through that. There's a difference, and the difference is usually whether the student survives the lesson." He said it mildly, the way he'd once corrected my penmanship. "Rules first. The veil does not care about your body. It cares about your *shape* — your name, your debts, your intentions. Walk in carrying too much of any of those, and the road will take its toll."

"Toll."

"Everything below is paid for, Orsen. Nothing under the mountain is free. Especially not passage." He turned to me, and lamplight from somewhere that had no lamp caught the deep lines around his eyes. "Tonight you go in one step. One step, no farther, and you come back the moment I pull the cord." He held up a length of red thread tied to his wrist, the other end looped around mine. "Feel that?"

"It's a thread."

"It's a thread *and* your life. If it goes slack, run. If it goes taut, run faster."

"Wonderful. Any other cheerful instructions?"

"Yes." He almost smiled. "Don't think about what you owe."

I stepped through.

* * *

Here is what I remember of my first lesson, reconstructed afterward from fragments and from Ellery's account, because my own memory of it arrived home late and missing pieces:

There was a road. It was not stone and it was not light and it was not dark, but something that behaved like all three depending on where you weren't looking. It stretched ahead into a distance that had no interest in being distant, and behind me the doorway I'd come through hung like a picture frame with nothing in it.

And there were voices.

They came out of the ground, or the air, or the space between my ears where my own thoughts usually lived. They sounded like wind through wires, if wires could grieve. Souls, I understood later. Stray ones. Sold ones. Ones still traveling, or ones that had stopped traveling and simply remained, the way a sound remains in a bell after the ringing stops.

I did the sensible thing. I thought about my debts.

Three years of them. The Temple of Nine Bells and its patient, itemized cruelty. The interest that grew like mold in a wet season. The charm-sales and the forged marginalia and the small dishonesties of a small man keeping his head above water. I thought about all of it, because I am Orsen Ravensworth, and thinking about what I owe is the one discipline I have never once failed at.

The road noticed.

I don't know how else to say it. The road *noticed*, the way a dog notices the smell of blood, or a moneylender notices a signature. The gray under my feet stopped being indifferent and became attentive, and something rose up through it — not hands, exactly, but the *idea* of hands, the way a creditor's letter contains the idea of a bailiff — and it took hold of my coat, my sleeves, the hem of everything I was, and it began to *read* me.

It read fast. It read like Pell skimming a ledger for anything worth stealing. And every line it found, it kept.

My mother's voice saying my name — gone. Not forgotten; I want to be precise about this, because the difference matters and it took me days to understand it. Not forgotten. *Taken.* There was a shape in my memory where her voice had been, smooth-sided and empty, like a coin slot after the coin drops.

The summer it rained for nine days straight and Sera and I sat under the awning of the chandler's shop and invented insults for passersby — taken. I knew we had done it. I knew I had laughed until my ribs hurt. I could not, for the life of me, remember why.

The red thread went taut.

I have been hanged at twice. Not literally — Vharen hasn't hanged anyone in years, they prefer the quieter economies of debt — but I know the feeling of a rope's opinion arriving in your throat. That's what the thread's pull was. Ellery hauled, and I clawed, and the road objected, and somewhere in the middle of that argument I lost something else I've never identified, and then—

Then I was on the archive floor, on my back, gasping like a landed fish, with an old archivist kneeling over me looking like a man who has just watched a student nearly die and is deciding how to phrase the report.

"Rule two," Ellery said quietly. "You thought about what you owed."

"You said—" My voice came out in ribbons. "You said don't think about it. I thought about *not* thinking about it. That's the same as—"

"Yes. Welcome to the old roads." He gave me water from a flask that tasted of iron and peppermint, an combination I have never forgiven. "You lost something."

"Not much."

"More than you think. Less than you fear. The road takes a tithe from everyone who enters unprepared. That's why we practice." He sat back on his heels, and for a moment the lamplight made him look every one of his years and several he hadn't reached yet. "Nine days, Orsen. By the ninth, you need to walk a mile of that road and keep every step of yourself. Tonight you gave away a handful of memories for three feet."

"Efficient of me."

"It's the standard rate." He stood, joints announcing themselves. "Come back tomorrow. Sleep first. Eat something. And—" he paused at the door, and I saw the unnamed thing cross his face again, "—don't tell the girl."

"Sera? Why not?"

"Because she'll try to stop you," he said, "and because she might succeed."

I lay on the cold floor a while longer after he left, taking inventory. Mother's voice: gone. The rain: gone, though the laughter's echo stayed, orphaned. Something else: gone, unidentified, a blank page in a book I'd apparently read.

And underneath all of it, doing its quiet work, a feeling I didn't expect and couldn't argue with.

I was furious. Not at Ellery, not at the road, not even at the century-dead prophet who had circled my name like a butcher circling a cut of meat. I was furious at myself — at the scribe who had spent three years being careful, being small, being one step ahead and no more, who had let a friend sell fifteen years of her soul while he counted coppers and called it survival.

That man had walked three feet into the dark and paid in memories.

The man who got up off that floor intended to walk a mile and pay in nothing.

* * *

Day two, I lasted six steps and came back with a nosebleed and most of my dignity.

Day three, twelve steps, and I learned rule three: the road shows you things. Mine showed me Ash Street at night, every lamp lit, and a figure walking the rounds with a ladder on her shoulder — except the figure had no face, because the road deals in shapes, not people, and it was showing me the *idea* of Sera, and the idea of Sera was flickering. Guttering. Like a lamp running low on oil.

I came back shaking. Ellery pretended not to notice how long I sat with my head between my knees.

"That's her," I said finally. "Out there. On the road."

"Souls sold to the court travel those roads," Ellery said. "Or pieces of them do. What's left below calls to what's left above. Like recognizes like." He hesitated. "It may also have been showing you what you intend. The road is honest that way. Rude, but honest."

"Then it can be rude somewhere else."

Day four, twenty steps. Day five, forty. I learned to walk the way Ellery taught: empty. He called it *traveling light*, and it was the hardest thing I have ever done, harder than hunger, harder than lying to wardens. Traveling light meant walking into that gray nothing with my mind swept clean of debts and names and wants — a scribe with no ledger, a debtor with no debt, a man who was nobody on purpose.

"You are very good at being no one," Ellery observed on day five, and I couldn't tell if it was a compliment or a diagnosis.

"It's the one skill my upbringing provided."

"Then it will save your life below. The court cannot bargain with a man it cannot name. But hear me, Orsen—" and he gripped my shoulder hard enough to leave marks, "—the moment you reach for the flame, you stop being no one. The moment you claim her, you have a name again, and a purpose, and a shape, and everything below will see it. Whatever plan you make, make it for the man you'll be *after* you're seen. Not before."

Day six, I walked a hundred steps and heard the auction hall for the first time — a vast murmuring beyond the road's edge, voices raised in numbers, in offers, in the polite cannibal language of appraisal. Somewhere in that murmur, I was certain, a voice like wind through wires belonged to Sera.

I came back with my hands steady. First time all week.

Ellery watched me set down the flask without spilling it, and something in his expression shifted — the pity thinning, the apology remaining.

"You'll do," he said.

"Don't sound so surprised."

"I'm not surprised. I'm saddened. It's always the careful ones who turn out to be brave." He gathered his thread and his flask and his terrible peppermint water. "Two days left. Tomorrow we rehearse the descent proper — the gate, the names, the case. And Orsen—"

"Yes?"

"When you stand in front of whatever guards that glass, and it asks what you're willing to give — and it *will* ask — decide your answer now. Tonight. In the daylight, with bread in you. Never decide a price in front of the seller."

I told him I would. I even believed it.

But that night I sat in my room above the chandler's shop with Sera's lamp — the little brass one she'd lent me months ago and I'd never returned, trimmed and filled and ready — and I made my list, the way I make all my lists. Things I could pay: memories, small ones, the loose coin of a life. Things I could not: her. Her freedom. The fifteen years she'd signed away in a room I should have been in.

And at the bottom of the list, in the space where honest ledgers record the balance outstanding, I wrote the only entry that mattered:

*Nine days minus two. Whatever it costs, it costs less than her.*

Somewhere below the mountain, I suspect, something ancient and patient felt me write that line, and smiled.

I felt it smile back.
