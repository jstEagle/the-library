## 10. The Exiled Inquisitor

I had been in Vharen for eleven days when I finally caught the thief.

Not the soul-thief — the prophecy kind. The paper kind. A scribe with ink-stained fingers and a walk like a man trying to apologize to the ground for standing on it.

Let me back up. My name is Dagny Holt, and if you'd asked anyone in the Inquisition's Third Circle six years ago, they'd have told you I was the best hound they ever leashed. I found hidden charms in floorboards. I found hexes stitched into wedding veils. I once tracked a hedge-witch through four provinces by the smell of her tallow alone. They gave me a seal of grey iron and a title and told me that what I did was justice.

Then I asked one question too many about where confiscated souls went after the burning, and the leash turned out to run both directions.

Now I am nobody from nowhere, with a face that has learned to be forgettable and hands that have not forgotten anything at all. I came to Vharen because Vharen is where the trail ends. Or begins. With mountains it's hard to tell which.

* * *

The copy surfaced on a Tuesday, which felt appropriate. Tuesdays are the day the Temple of Nine Bells airs its archive linens, and linen-day means loose pages, and loose pages mean a scribe somewhere has been doing something he shouldn't.

I didn't find it the way an inquisitor would have. An inquisitor would have leaned on the under-clerks, bought the laundry mistress, and had three names by supper. I no longer have that machinery. What I have is patience, a rented room above a dye-shop that smells permanently of vinegar, and a habit of sitting in taverns near the temple district listening to clerks complain.

Clerks complain beautifully. It is their one unguarded art.

By the fourth evening I had a shape: someone in the archive had copied something forbidden, and the copying had been sloppy enough to leave a page behind in a returned binding. By the sixth evening I had a name whispered between two junior archivists over watered wine: *Ravensworth*. Debt-bound. Charm-peddler on the side, if you believed the gossip, which I did, because gossip about small crimes is usually accurate and gossip about large ones never is.

And by the seventh evening, I had the page itself, because Ravensworth had done the single most human thing a frightened man can do with dangerous paper: he hadn't destroyed it. He'd hidden it. Under the floorboard of his rented room on Ash Street, third board from the window, beneath a loose nail he probably thought looked natural.

It did not look natural. Loose nails never do. Guilt has a carpentry style all its own.

* * *

I want to be honest about what I expected to feel, crouched in a stranger's room at dusk with his secrets in my hand. I expected the old cold satisfaction. The click of the trap. Six years ago I would have been already composing the report in my head, already weighing whether the man was a heretic or merely a fool, already deciding how much of him would need to burn.

Instead I sat on a stranger's floor and read a dead woman's handwriting, and my hands shook.

The prophecy was a century old — the archivists' gossip had that much right. But the copy was fresh, and fresh in a way that mattered. Someone had circled a date. Tonight's date, as it happened, though I wouldn't learn that until later. And beneath the date, in the same cramped clerk's hand:

*A thief will go down to the court beneath the mountain, and steal back what was sold.*

I read it twice. Three times. The room got darker around me and I let it, because there are some things you should read in the dark, so the dark can't pretend it isn't listening.

A court beneath the mountain. Mount Vhalen. The mountain every child in Vharen is taught to call *the quiet neighbor*, the mountain the Temple calls *holy ground, closed to the faithful for their own protection*.

I had spent six years following rumors of that court up and down the spine of the world. I had interviewed a smuggler in Casterhale who wept into his beer describing toll-bridges made of rope and teeth. I had bought, at ruinous expense, half a ledger from a dead courier — pages listing names, dates, and sums, in a bookkeeping style I recognized instantly, because I had spent ten years enforcing the system that fed it. Confiscated souls. Debtor souls. Heretic souls. All of them flowing downhill, always downhill, toward Vhalen.

And I had a sister who went into that flow thirty-one years ago, when I was nineteen and still believed the Inquisition's arithmetic. Maren. Sold — not executed, *sold*, the tribunal had been very proud of its mercy — for the crime of teaching letters to mill children without a license. I signed the transfer order myself. I was the best hound they ever leashed, and good hounds fetch.

So yes. I sat on a scribe's floorboards and shook, and then I did something no inquisitor would have done.

I put the page back exactly as I'd found it, nail and all, and I waited to meet him.

* * *

You can learn everything about a man from how he sells a luck-charm to a widow.

I watched Orsen Ravensworth work a market stall two days later — I'd taken a room across the lane, which cost me most of what remained of my coin, and I considered it money well spent. He sold the widow a charm against bad dreams, and here is the thing: the charm worked. Not magic — the wardens would have had him inside the hour. It worked because he'd talked to her first, really talked, asked about the dreams, listened to the answer, and then sold her a bit of carved bone and a ritual she could perform herself, a thing to hold in the dark. Placebo, prayer, theater. Call it what you like. The woman left standing straighter than she'd arrived.

He took her coin with visible shame. That was the detail that snagged me. Most charm-sellers pocket the money like a victory. He took it like a confession.

*Debt-bound,* the gossip said. *Temple property, more or less.* And yet here he was, quietly committing fraud against despair, giving frightened people something real wrapped in something fake, and hating himself for the fake part instead of being proud of the real one.

I know that flavor of shame. It's the flavor of a person who was decent before an institution got its hooks in him, and who has not yet stopped noticing the hooks.

That night I followed him home along Ash Street, past the new-lit lamps — a lamplighter was working the row, a thin young woman with a cough that carried further than her ladder — and I stood in the shadow of a cooper's yard while he let himself in, and I knocked on his door before he'd finished lighting his candle.

He opened it on the chain. Wise. His eyes did the arithmetic fast — my build, my boots, my scar, the way I stood like someone accustomed to doors opening *for* her rather than against her — and I watched him arrive at the wrong conclusion, the safe one.

"If this is about the widow's refund," he said, "the charm was honestly described."

"It's about the page under your floorboard," I said. "Third from the window. You'll want a better nail."

I have seen men go white. I have seen men go gray. Orsen Ravensworth went the color of cheap vellum, which I suppose is the occupational hazard, and then — and this is why I'm telling you all this, why I've spent a chapter of my life on this man — he did not slam the door. He stood there on the chain, terrified, and said:

"Are you warden?"

"No."

"Temple?"

"No."

"Inquisition?" His voice dropped on the word, the way people drop their voices near graves.

"I was," I said. "Six years ago. Then I asked what happens to the souls after the burning, and they took my seal and gave me exile." I held up my empty hands, palms out, the old gesture, the surrender sign every trained interrogator knows reads as honesty because it costs something to make. "I'm the only kind of person who can help you, Ravensworth. Someone with nothing left to lose and a great deal of practice losing it."

* * *

He let me in eventually. Not that night — that night he let me in as far as his doorway, and we spoke in the low rapid dialect of two people conducting an illegal negotiation while pretending to discuss the weather. But three nights later, after he'd verified me six ways from Sunday — he was thorough, I'll grant him, he checked my story with a smuggler and a pawnbroker and, gods help me, a fortune-teller — he unbolted the door and showed me the page, and I showed him what I knew.

Which was, admittedly, mostly how to be afraid properly.

"You understand what this says," he said. We were at his table, the prophecy spread between us like a patient on an operating cloth. "The date. It's — it's nine days from now. And my name. My actual name, written down a hundred years before I was born."

"Yes."

"That's not possible."

"No," I agreed. "It isn't. Welcome to the part where the world stops being possible. It doesn't negotiate. You just have to decide what you're going to do inside it."

He laughed — a short, cracked sound, the laugh of a man finding the trapdoor in his own floor. "And what did *you* decide? Inside it?"

I looked at the line again. *Steal back what was sold.*

"I decided thirty-one years ago," I said. "I just didn't know it yet."

He waited. He was good at waiting; scribes are. So I told him. Not everything — I am not a fool, and trust is a currency I spend in small denominations — but the shape of it. A sister. A tribunal that preferred sale to execution because sale turned a profit. A transfer order with my own signature learning itself under my hand. Six years of asking questions afterward, each question a rung down a ladder, until the Inquisition decided I'd climbed too far from the fire and cut the rope.

"They don't exile you for disloyalty," I told him. "They exile you for accuracy."

"And now you're here." He glanced toward his window, toward the mountain that fills half the sky over Vharen whether you look at it or not. "For her. Your sister. You think she's still—" He stopped. He was kind enough to stop.

"Maren's flame may be ash by now. May have been ash for decades." I kept my voice level, which took work; level voices always do. "But the court keeps accounts, Ravensworth. Even burned debts stay in the ledger. If there's a record of her, I want it. If there's a chance of her, I want *that*. And either way, I want the place that did it to stop existing." I tapped the page. "This prophecy is the first door anyone's ever opened in that wall. I intend to walk through it. The only question is whether I walk through it with you or past you."

"And if I say neither? If I burn this and run?"

"Then I'll be sorry," I said, "because you're the thief it named, and thieves who refuse their jobs tend to get replaced by ones who don't. And the replacement won't care who gets hurt." I let that sit. Cruelty delivered gently is still cruelty, but sometimes gentleness is just the wrapping the truth comes in. "Also, you'd break your lamplighter friend's heart, and I've heard her cough from across the street. Whatever she sold to cover your debts, she hasn't got long to collect it back."

His head came up sharply. "What do you know about Sera?"

"Nothing you don't. Less. But I know what thinning souls sound like, and hers sounds like a candle in a doorway." I watched him absorb that, watched it land somewhere below the ribs where such things live. "You didn't know. Of course you didn't. Nobody tells the debt what it cost."

* * *

We argued for two hours. It was, I confess, one of the better evenings of my exile.

He argued like a man drafting a contract — clauses, exceptions, contingencies — and I argued like an inquisitor, which is to say I let him talk himself into corners and then stood in them with him. He wanted guarantees. I told him the truth: there are none. Not below that mountain. The court cannot be beaten, not with steel, not with law, not with any power I've ever seen. It can only be *out-bargained*, and bargains are won by the party who needs less.

"So we need nothing," he said.

"We need exactly one thing each," I corrected. "You need her soul. I need mine back, or its ashes, or its accounting — I'll take whichever the ledger offers. Two needs, cleanly stated, no greed attached. That's the strongest position a thief ever walked in with." I leaned forward. "But hear me, Ravensworth, because this is the part everyone dies skipping: the court will offer you more than you came for. It always does. It will dress the offer in courtesy and reason and it will be *fair*, gods, it will be scrupulously fair, and fairness is how it farms. Whatever it offers you down there — say no first. Say no, and mean it, and only then start counting."

He was quiet a long moment, turning his cup. Then: "Why help me? Truly. You could do this alone. You clearly know more than I do."

"Because the prophecy named *you*, not me." I said it flatly, because flattery would have insulted us both. "Fate is a bureaucracy too, and I've learned not to argue with paperwork. Besides—" and here I nearly didn't say it, and then did, because alliances built on withheld things fall in the exact shape of the withholding— "because I've spent thirty-one years being the hand that sent souls down. I'd like, before I die, to be the hand that takes one back. Even if it's only yours."

That landed. I saw it land. He looked at me the way people look at a door they'd assumed was painted on the wall.

"The archive," he said suddenly. "Master Ellery. The head archivist — I think he *wanted* me to find this. The book was misfiled in a way that wasn't accidental. I've catalogued ten thousand books; accidents have a shape, and this wasn't one."

Something cold walked down my spine wearing my mother's footsteps. An archivist who plants prophecies. A temple that feeds its debtors downhill. A city built on top of an auction house and taught to call it holy silence.

"Then you have a traitor," I said, "and traitors are either your worst enemy or your best map. We find out which." I stood, and took my coat, and paused at his door with my hand on the latch. "Nine days, Ravensworth. Tomorrow we start preparing, and I warn you now — the first lesson will feel like drowning, because it is. Veil-walking is not a skill. It's a permission you beg for."

"From whom?"

"From the road itself." I smiled, and I knew it wasn't a comforting smile; exiles don't have those anymore. "Sleep. Eat something. Kiss your lamplighter's lamp for luck, if that's the shape of things — I don't ask. But tonight, look at your ceiling and memorize it. Men who go beneath mountains come back wanting proof the sky was real."

I stepped out into Ash Street. The lamps were lit, all down the row, steady and gold, and the thin young woman was snuffing the last of them for the morning, coughing into her sleeve.

Nine days. Somewhere under our feet, behind a hundred feet of holy stone, something ancient was keeping books.

I intended to audit it.
