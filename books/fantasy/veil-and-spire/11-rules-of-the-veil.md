## 11. Rules of the Veil

Dagny Holt taught the way wardens break doors: quickly, thoroughly, and with a certain grim satisfaction in watching you fail.

"Again," she said, and dropped me through the floor.

Not literally. That was the problem. The veil between the cellar of the Broken Compass — one of Tessin's smuggling holes, three streets from where I'd sold charms to frightened widows for six years — and the shallow road beneath the world was supposed to open like a door and let me *step*. Instead it opened like a mouth and swallowed me sideways, and I came out on my hands and knees in a place that smelled of cold iron and old rain, with my breakfast somewhere behind my left eye.

I lay there a moment, cheek against stone that was not any stone Vharen had ever quarried. The dark above me wasn't darkness exactly. It was more like the memory of darkness — the idea of it, worn smooth by use. Somewhere far off, something whispered. A thousand somethings. Like wind through wires, like a congregation murmuring through a closed door.

*Souls*, I thought. *Those are souls.* And then I made the mistake of standing up to look.

The road stretched away beneath an arch of roots that had no tree. They were pale as candle-wax and thick as temple columns, and they went up forever into nothing, and I understood with a clarity that felt like falling that they were holding the mountain up. Holding everything up. The whole weight of Vharen — its temples, its debt-ledgers, its wardens, its nine bells that rang the hours of a law that outlawed magic while running on it like a cart runs on grease — all of it balanced on roots, and beneath the roots, a court.

"Up," Dagny's voice said, right beside my ear, and I screamed, which she ignored with the ease of long practice. She hauled me back through the shimmering seam by my collar before I could so much as apologize to the dark. "You looked. Rule one: you don't look at what whispers. Looking is attention. Attention is invitation."

"You said this lesson wouldn't kill me."

"I said it probably wouldn't." She set me down on the cellar's packed earth and crouched in front of me, gray-eyed and gray-haired and built like something the Temple had tried to break and failed. "It didn't. Congratulations. Now we do it again."

We did it again. Eleven more times, over four days, in cellars and cisterns and once — because Tessin had a sense of humor and no respect for my dignity — the back room of a dye-house that smelled so violently of madder root that even the veil seemed offended. Each time, the road beneath tried something new. Once it was longer than it should have been. Once the whispering shaped itself into my mother's voice, calling me home to supper in a house that had burned when I was nine. Once there was nothing at all, which was somehow worse, and I walked blind with my hand on Dagny's shoulder while she counted my steps aloud in a voice flat as a ledger line.

By the sixth day I could cross a shallow veil without vomiting. By the seventh, I could cross it and come back on purpose, which Dagny informed me put me ahead of most first-time smugglers and behind every dead one.

"You learn fast," she said, in the tone other people used for *you have a pleasant face*.

"I learn fast or I die slow," I said. "Scribe's training. We're paid to copy things correctly the first time."

"Mm." She fed the brazier a knot of charcoal. The cellar light moved across her face and found the scar there, the one that ran from her ear to her jaw like a sentence someone had started and abandoned. "Then write this down. Not on paper. In you. Paper can be seized."

I sat up straighter. This was the part I'd been waiting for — the actual rules, the grammar of the place I intended to walk into and walk out of again with a soul under my arm.

"Rule one," she said, holding up one finger, "you already know. Don't look at the whispering. Rule two: below, everything is a bargain. The air you breathe down there is borrowed. The ground holds you up because some ancestor of yours, ten generations back, made a payment nobody remembers. You will be offered things. Food, rest, directions, kindness. Every single one has a price, and the price is never written on the sign. If you cannot see the cost, assume the cost is you."

"That seems—"

"Rule three." Another finger. "Names have power below. Your name is the only thing you own outright, and it is the first thing they'll try to take. Never give your true name. Not to a gatekeeper, not to a beggar, not to a child who asks sweetly. Especially not a child. The court breeds children who ask sweetly; it's cheaper than breeding hounds."

My skin crawled. "What happens if you give it?"

"They own you. Not your body — bodies are cheap, they have warehouses full of those. Your *self*. Everything you've ever meant, filed away in their ledgers, and you spend the rest of your existence being whatever the current entry says you are." She said it without drama, the way you'd describe weather. "I've seen men whose names were taken. They smile a great deal. It's the worst thing I know."

"Rule four," I said, because if I didn't start reciting them myself I was going to be sick again. "Never eat, never drink, never accept a gift."

"Good. Rule five: debts are sacred below. More sacred than life, more sacred than law. The court exists to collect them. Which means—" she leaned forward, and her eyes were very bright and very tired, "—the court cannot be beaten, Orsen. Say it back to me."

"The court cannot be beaten."

"It is older than the mountain. Older than the city. It was collecting debts before anyone thought to write them down, and it will be collecting after Vharen is a rumor told by gulls. You cannot fight it. You cannot trick it, not really — it invented tricks the way the sea invented wet. All you can do is out-bargain it. Find the flaw in the contract. Find the debt *they* owe, because everyone owes, even them, especially them — a court that keeps ledgers keeps accounts against itself whether it likes it or not. And you make them honor their own rules until they choke on them."

"And if there's no flaw?"

She looked at me for a long moment. The brazier popped. Somewhere above us, Vharen went about its evening — carts, bells, Sera lighting lamps along Ash Street with a cough she thought I hadn't noticed getting worse.

"Then you lose," she said simply. "But there's always a flaw. Contracts are written by the proud, and pride is the flaw. Remember that."

* * *

The days took on a rhythm that would have been funny if it hadn't been terrifying. Mornings, I scribed in the archive with Ellery watching me over his spectacles like a man watching a kettle he'd personally placed on the fire. Afternoons, I crossed veils until my nose bled. Evenings, I sat in the Broken Compass while Dagny drilled me in the law beneath the law.

Tessin and Bren joined us when they could — Tessin asking sharp, practical questions about toll-bridges and safe-roads, Bren mostly silent, sharpening a knife he never seemed to finish sharpening. On the third evening Bren finally spoke.

"The tolls," he said. "On the deep roads. What do they take?"

"Memories," Dagny said. "Small ones, if you're lucky. The bridges are old contracts themselves — passage for remembrance. Pay in trivialities and they'll usually take them. Pay in nothing and they'll take what they choose."

"What counts as small?" I asked.

"A stranger's face. The taste of a meal. The color of a day." She turned her cup in her hands. "Pay attention to what you'd miss least. Then miss it on purpose. It's the only way through with anything left."

I went home that night and did something I'm not proud of. I sat at my desk with a candle and made a list. An inventory of my own head, ranked by expendability. The name of my first landlord. The smell of the tannery row. Three years of sermons I'd copied for the Temple and never believed. It read like a eulogy for a very boring man, and I hated how relieved I was that it was long enough.

At the bottom of the list, unranked, unwritten but present, sat the things I would not pay. My mother's voice, singing. Rain on the roof of the house that burned. And Sera — Sera laughing at me over stolen bread, Sera saying *you'd lose your own head if it weren't listed as collateral*, Sera's lamp coming up Ash Street like a small stubborn sun.

I looked at that unwritten line for a long time.

Then I blew out the candle, because some inventories are safer in the dark.

* * *

On the fifth evening, Dagny taught me bargains.

She set three objects on the table between us: a copper coin, a key, and a dead moth in a matchbox. "Three contracts," she said. "The coin buys a lie — anyone below will tell you one truth-shaped falsehood for it. The key opens a door that wants opening, and the door will remember you kindly. The moth—" she paused, "—the moth is a favor owed to you by something that owes favors badly. Don't touch the moth unless you're dying."

"Why do you have these?"

"Because thirty years ago I was very good at my job, and my job was taking things from people below." Her mouth did something that wasn't quite a smile. "Inquisitors don't burn books, Orsen. That's the Temple's vulgar little theater. We *collect*. Seals, tokens, contracts. Half the arsenal of the old order sits in vaults the Temple forgot it has, and I remember every inventory line."

She pushed the coin toward me. "Practice. Hold it and ask me a question. Any question. I'll answer falsely, and you find the false seam. That's the skill — not detecting lies, but finding where the truth was folded to fit the price."

I held the coin. It was warm, which coins shouldn't be, and it hummed faintly against my palm like a trapped fly. "Where is Sera's soul?"

The question came out of me before I'd decided to ask it — the true question, the only question, dressed as practice. Dagny's face didn't change.

"She is happy," I said, in a voice that was mine and not mine, "and she has forgotten you entirely, and it is better this way."

The words were silk and they were poison and I believed them for one entire heartbeat — believed them down in the marrow, felt the terrible peace of them — before the seam caught. There. In the word *forgotten*. Folded wrong. Truth bent around a price I couldn't see yet.

I threw the coin across the cellar like it had bitten me. It landed in the straw and lay there, humming.

"Good," Dagny said quietly. "You found the fold. Now understand what you just learned: below, the lies will be *better* than that. They'll be tailored to you. They'll be the thing you most want to hear, priced at exactly what you can pay. The court doesn't tempt with evil, boy. It tempts with relief." She retrieved the coin with a handkerchief, not touching it bare. "Which brings us to the last rule. Rule six, and it's the one that kills people."

I waited.

"When you make a bargain below, they will offer you terms in words you think you understand. Accept nothing you haven't heard twice. Ask for the price *first*, in writing, in your own hand. And never—" her voice dropped, and for the first time since I'd met her, the exiled inquisitor sounded afraid, "—never let them finish a sentence that begins with *in exchange for everything*."

"In exchange for everything what?"

"That's the point. Nobody knows. Nobody who found out could tell us." She sat back. "The phrase is a hook older than the court itself. Even the King uses it carefully. So when you hear it, you run. You drop the bargain, the flame, the plan, all of it, and you run."

* * *

I walked home late through streets gone quiet, past lamps Sera had lit hours ago, still burning steady in the windless dark. Nine days had become five. Somewhere beneath my feet, past the roots and the roads and the gates, a flame flickered in a glass case with her fifteen years inside it, and a king who collected debts was waiting with the patience of geology for the thief the prophecy promised him.

I should have been afraid. I was afraid. But underneath the fear, growing like mold in a damp wall, was something else — the thing Ellery must have felt thirty years ago, the thing that lets cowards and scribes and exiled inquisitors keep walking toward the mountain.

Certainty. Terrible, borrowed certainty. The belief that because I understood the rules, I had some say in the game.

Dagny's voice followed me home, uninvited: *humility, scribe. Humility before forces older than law.*

I climbed my stairs. I trimmed Sera's spare lamp — the one I kept ready without letting myself ask why — and I slept, and dreamed of bridges, and woke with salt on my lips and no memory of why.

Two days left. And when I reached the archive that morning, Master Ellery was waiting at my desk with a book in his hands and an expression I had never seen on him before.

He was terrified.

"Orsen," he said. "We need to talk about what happens the night after tomorrow."

He wasn't looking at me. He was looking past me, through the archive window, at the mountain.

And the mountain, I would have sworn on any ledger in Vharen, was smoking.
