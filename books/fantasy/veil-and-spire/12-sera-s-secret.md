## 12. Sera's Secret

There are three things a lamplighter learns early: how to climb a ladder in the wind, how to tell a good flame from a dying one, and how to lie with a straight face to people who are watching you die.

I'd gotten good at all three.

The cough came at dawn that morning, the bad kind, the kind that folded me over my own knees on Ash Street while the milk cart rattled past and nobody looked twice because everybody on Ash Street had learned not to look twice at anything. I spat into my rag and checked it by lamplight — my own lamp, the one I kept trimmed for myself instead of the city, vanity of vanities — and there it was again. Not red. Worse than red. A faint grey shimmer, like ash stirred in water.

Soul-thinning. Any hedge-witch could have told me what it was. Any hedge-witch would have charged me a week's wages for the privilege of telling me what I already knew, because three years ago I'd stood in a debt office under the Temple of Nine Bells and sold fifteen years of myself to a court beneath a mountain, and the mountain had accepted, and the mountain does not do refunds.

Fifteen years. It sounded so tidy when the clerk said it. Fifteen years off the back end, I'd thought. I'll be old anyway when they come due; who counts the years after sixty? Nobody tells you the years come off the front. That they take them out of you *now*, a spoonful at a time, like broth sipped by something patient and enormous underneath the world. Nobody tells you your own body starts keeping their ledger for them.

But then, nobody asked me either. I'd signed fast and walked out fast, because Orsen Ravensworth was going to be fed to the debt-collectors inside the month if somebody didn't do arithmetic on his behalf, and Orsen was constitutionally incapable of arithmetic where his own survival was concerned.

Three years I'd kept it from him. Three years of coughing into my sleeve on the far side of the street, of blaming the coal smoke, of laughing at his jokes a half-beat late because the breath for the laugh hadn't arrived yet. He was a scribe. A good one, when he wasn't selling luck-charms out from under the wardens' noses, which is to say he was observant about everything except the one thing I needed him blind to. People see what they can bear to see. Orsen couldn't bear to see me dying, so he didn't. I loved him for it and hated him for it and mostly just got on with lighting lamps.

And then, nine days ago, he stopped sleeping.

* * *

You have to understand: Orsen not sleeping is not an event, it's a condition, like weather. The man treats sleep as a rumor spread by his enemies. But this was different. This was the particular insomnia of a man who has found something in a book. I've watched him read since we were children stealing cinders off the temple scrap-heaps, and I know the difference between Orsen reading for work and Orsen reading for ruin. When he reads for ruin, his lips move.

So I did what any sensible friend does. I snooped.

He keeps his illegal copies in a loose brick behind his stove, which he thinks is clever, and which I have known about since the year of the flood, because I am the one who showed him the loose brick. Behind the brick, wrapped in oilcloth: pages in his own hand, copied from something older. I read them standing in his kitchen with my heart going like a triphammer, and I read them twice, and both times my eyes snagged on the same words like a nail in a stair.

*A thief will descend to the court beneath the mountain.*

*The thief will steal back a soul that was sold.*

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

And beneath it, circled in faded ink a century dead: tonight's date — no. Nine days from now. And a name.

His name.

I put the pages back exactly as I'd found them, went home, sat down on my floor, and laughed until the laughing turned into the cough and the cough turned into something I won't describe, because a lady needs her mysteries. Then I got up, washed my face, and made a plan, because that is the other thing a lamplighter learns: when a flame is guttering, you don't stand around admiring it. You trim the wick, or you cup your hands around it, or you get it out of the wind.

Orsen was going to go down the mountain after my soul. I knew it the way you know rain is coming — not from evidence but from the ache in old scars. And he was going to fail, or worse, succeed, and either way the prophecy said his hand would be guided by betrayal, and I knew my Orsen. Give that man a moral cliff-edge and he wouldn't fall off it. He'd build a little shop on it first and sell charms to the falling.

There was only one person in Vharen who knew enough about prophecies to stop him, and that person was the reason the prophecy had found him in the first place. Which meant I had to go to the Temple of Nine Bells and have a word with Master Ellery.

I hate the Temple. For the record. In case anyone's writing this down.

* * *

The Temple of Nine Bells smells the way debt feels: cold stone, old tallow, and the faint sweetness of candle-wax burned by people praying for money. I'd dressed in my least memorable clothes — the brown coat, the shawl with the mend in it, the whole costume of a woman who is nobody's problem — and I'd brought a lamp, because a lamplighter without her lamp is a fish out of water and everyone knows it, and because the lamp gave me something to do with my hands besides shake.

Ellery received me in the archive annex, among the seized books. He was a dried-up stick of a man with ink-stained fingers and the eyes of someone who has spent forty years reading other people's endings. He looked at me for a long moment before speaking, and I watched him decide what I was. Clerk's widow? Petitioner? Trouble?

"Master Ellery," I said. "You don't know me, but you know my name. Sera Voss. Lamplighter, Ash Street ward."

Something moved behind his face. Small, quickly buried, but I've spent three years burying things behind my own face and I know the neighborhood. "Mistress Voss," he said carefully. "What can the archive do for you?"

"You can stop him."

"I'm afraid I don't—"

"Don't." I set the lamp on his table, gently, the way you set down something you might need to throw. "Don't do the thing where you pretend. You're too old and I'm too tired and we've both got better uses for the hour. You planted that book where he'd find it. You wanted him to read it. Now I want you to un-plant it, un-tell it, un-whatever-it-is, before he does something that gets him eaten by a mountain."

Ellery took off his spectacles, polished them on his sleeve, put them back on. Buying time. Scribes always think time is free because they spend it in such small denominations.

"Mistress Voss," he said at last, "what makes you believe I planted anything?"

"Because Orsen Ravensworth has never once in his life gone looking for a book. Books find *him*. He trips over them. If a century-old prophecy happened to land open in front of his exact name on the exact date, then somebody with access to this archive arranged the trip." I leaned in. "Also, you flinched when I said my name. Before I said anything else. Which means you know what I sold, and to whom, and you know why a lamplighter might care about a thief in a prophecy. So let's skip to the part where you tell me how to make this stop."

For a moment I thought he'd summon the wardens. I'd planned for that — the lamp was temple-issue, bought secondhand through a licensed chandler, nothing incriminating, and my story about petitioning for a deceased aunt's debt record was watertight — but he didn't summon anyone. He just looked tired. Tired the way I felt, which is to say, tired down to the marrow, tired in the place where the years used to be.

"He told you nothing?" Ellery asked.

"He doesn't know I know. And if you value his life, he still won't by the time I leave here."

"That," said Ellery, "is precisely the difficulty." He rose, went to a shelf, and stood with his back to me, hands clasped behind him like a man addressing a jury that had already convicted him. "Do you know what happens if the prophecy goes unfulfilled, Mistress Voss?"

"I know what happens if it *is* fulfilled. My best friend walks into a hole in the world to steal fire from something that eats cities' debts for breakfast, and the book says his hand will be guided by betrayal. I've met his hand. It's a decent hand. It doesn't deserve that."

"The alternative," Ellery said, still facing the shelf, "is that the court beneath the mountain collects differently. Not one thief. Not one soul. The city pays its arrears all at once." He turned. His face had the awful calm of a man reciting terms he'd agreed to long ago. "I have spent thirty years making sure that didn't happen. Thirty years of small payments, quiet arrangements, a steady supply of debtor souls moving down the old roads so the flood never comes up them. The prophecy is the invoice coming due. It must be paid as written, or renegotiated by someone standing in the court itself. Those are the only two doors."

"Then close both doors and board up the wall."

"There is no wall. There has never been a wall. There is only the bill, and the date, and the question of whose name is on it." He came back toward the table, and for the first time he looked at me directly, and I understood that he had already done the arithmetic I'd done, three years ago, in a debt office. "You sold fifteen years for him once. Don't look surprised — the archive sees every contract. So you of all people should understand: sometimes the only mercy available is choosing *who* pays."

"No," I said.

"Mistress Voss—"

"*No.*" The word came out with more force than I'd meant to spend, and the lamp-flame between us leaned toward me like it was listening. I lowered my voice. "You think because I sold years once, I'll stand aside while he sells himself? I didn't sell those years so he could repay me. I sold them so he'd never know the price of anything. That was the whole point. That was the *gift*. And you—" I jabbed a finger at the shelves of seized books, at the whole dusty mausoleum of other people's fates, "—you took my gift and you gift-wrapped it in a prophecy and handed it back to him with his name on it. Do you have any idea what that does to a man like Orsen? He'll go. Of course he'll go. He'll go because some dead fool wrote his name in a circle, and he'll go politely, and he'll apologize to the guards on the way down, and the book says betrayal, and do you know what the easiest betrayal in the world is, Master Ellery?"

He didn't answer. His silence answered.

"It's the truth," I said. "It's telling the truth to someone who trusted you. That's the coin that's always in your pocket. And he will spend it, because you put him in a room where spending it looked like the kind thing to do, and then whatever comes back up that mountain won't be Orsen, and I will have sold fifteen years of my life to protect a man who stopped existing on the date in your book."

The archive was very quiet. Somewhere above us, distantly, one of the nine bells rang the hour, and I hated it, the smugness of bells.

"What would you have me do?" Ellery asked finally, and there was something almost human in it, almost pleading. "Burn the page? The book rewrites itself. Refuse him the roads? He'll find another way, a worse one, alone. I chose him because he is careful, and clever, and because—" he hesitated, "—because he is the sort of man who, given a choice between the prophecy and his friend, will try to find a third door. The court respects a third door. It's the only thing it respects."

"Then help me find the third door first," I said. "Before he's standing in front of a king with no door at all."

Ellery studied me for a long time. I let him. I'd come prepared to beg, and if begging failed, prepared to threaten, and if threatening failed, prepared to do the thing I'd sworn I'd never do, which was tell him exactly how much time I had left and let the arithmetic do its work. But before I had to choose, he sighed — the sigh of a ledger closing — and said:

"There is a version of events where the thief descends and returns, and the betrayal line is satisfied by something smaller than a soul. A technicality. The court loves technicalities; it runs on them. If someone who understood the terms were to... advise the thief beforehand. Prepare him. Arm him with the right technicalities." His eyes flicked to mine. "That person would need to know the terms intimately. Would need, perhaps, to have read every contract the archive holds. Including one signed three years ago by a lamplighter of the Ash Street ward."

My stomach dropped through the floor and kept going. "You want me to help him."

"I want the prophecy fulfilled *correctly*, with the smallest possible payment. You are the only person in Vharen with a stronger motive than his. And you are, I suspect, the only person alive he would actually listen to." Ellery folded his hands. "So. Shall we discuss terms, Mistress Voss? Or shall we continue pretending you came here to ask about your aunt?"

I should have said no. I want that on the record too. I stood in that cold archive with my borrowed hours ticking away in my chest, and every instinct I had — every lesson of the ladder and the wind and the lying-with-a-straight-face — screamed at me to walk out, find Orsen, and put him on a boat to somewhere the mountain's roads don't reach.

But I'd seen his face these last nine days. Sleepless, lit by candle-glow, copying dead words in a dead hand. Men like Orsen don't turn back from a thing like that. They just go more quietly, and more badly, and more alone.

If he was going to walk into the dark, then the dark was going to get both of us whether I liked it or not. The only choice left was whether it got us organized.

"Terms," I said.

* * *

I left the Temple near dusk, when the bells were at their most self-satisfied, and walked home the long way along the river because I needed the wind and didn't want to admit it. My chest ached. It always ached now; I'd stopped noticing the way you stop noticing a stone in your shoe, until moments like this when the stone shifted.

At the corner of Ash Street I saw him. Orsen, at my lamp-post, doing the thing he did sometimes and pretended he didn't: checking that my ladder was latched, that my oil tin wasn't leaking, fussing over my equipment like a mother hen with a gambling problem. He hadn't seen me yet. In the last light he looked young and worried and stubborn, all the things I'd sold fifteen years to keep in the world.

Nine days. Ellery had given the prophecy nine days, and the book had agreed, and now I had nine days to learn the law of the court beneath the mountain well enough to keep my idiot best friend alive inside it — without ever letting him learn that the soul he was coming to steal already knew he was coming.

I coughed once, quietly, into my sleeve, and started toward him with a smile I'd been practicing for three years.

"Sera!" he called, spotting me, relief naked on his face before he could dress it up. "You're late. Your lamps are staging a revolt."

"Are they," I said. "Well. Someone had better talk them down."

And behind us, up the hill, the Temple of Nine Bells began to ring — all nine, out of order, a sound I'd never heard in my life and hoped never to hear again, because Ellery had gone pale at the mere mention of it that afternoon.

Trouble, coming down the hill like weather. And this time, I wasn't sure even I could lie to it.
