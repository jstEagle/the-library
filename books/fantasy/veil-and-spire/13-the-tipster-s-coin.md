## 13. The Tipster's Coin

There is a particular kind of arithmetic that only scribes understand, and it has nothing to do with numbers. It is the arithmetic of favors: who owes whom, how much, and at what interest. The Temple of Nine Bells taught me that arithmetic before it taught me my letters. Debt comes first in Vharen. Everything else — love, faith, the alphabet — is a way of keeping score.

So when the wardens came to Ash Street with a tip about charms sold out of a scribe's back room, I did not ask *who told them*. I asked *what it cost them to tell*, because in this city nobody gives away a name for free.

I started where all bad investigations start: with the paperwork.

* * *

The wardens keep their tip ledger in the Hall of Reckoning, third floor, behind a door with a lock so old it had given up any ambition of actually locking anything. I know this because I have catalogued three hundred and eleven seized books for the Temple archive, and seized books require seizure records, and seizure records are filed by clerks who cannot be bothered to walk downstairs twice in one day.

The trick to reading a document you're not supposed to read is not stealth. Stealth is for burglars and inquisitors, and I am neither. The trick is boredom. You must be so thoroughly, radiantly bored that no one can imagine you doing anything interesting. I have cultivated boredom the way other men cultivate beards — patiently, and with an eye toward how it looks from a distance.

I spent Tuesday morning in the Hall of Reckoning re-sorting a drawer of charm-confiscation forms by date, then by ward, then by ink color, because the supervising clerk, a woman named Mistress Halloway with a face like a closed ledger, needed to believe I had nowhere better to be. By the fourth hour she had stopped watching me entirely. By the fifth she was asleep at her desk with her cheek on the municipal seal, which struck me as either blasphemy or devotion, depending on your theology.

The tip ledger lived in the bottom drawer. It was not even hidden. That was the part that made my teeth ache later — not the secrecy of it, but the casualness. Selling a man's name to the wardens was such ordinary business in Vharen that it got filed between a complaint about a blocked gutter and a request for more lamp oil.

Tips were logged by number, not name. Number forty-one hundred and six, entered nine days ago — two days before the raid on Ash Street — read:

*Informant reports unlicensed charm-crafting, premises behind the chandler's on Ash Street. Stock believed substantial. Informant requests standard consideration.*

*Standard consideration.* In Temple language, that phrase has exactly one meaning: debt relief. Not coin — the Temple doesn't pay for names, it forgives. Which means whoever sold me had owed the Temple something, and had paid with me.

Nine days ago. I cast my mind back over everyone who'd known about the charms. Sera knew, but Sera had stood in front of three wardens and lied to their faces while my stock burned in her brazier — you don't sell a man and then spend your own lungs defending him. Dagny Holt knew, but Dagny had found my illegal copy of a prophecy and responded by offering to help me rob a god's court, which is a strange business model for an informant. Ellery knew, but Ellery had planted the prophecy for me to find; betraying me to the wardens would be like planting a seed and then salting the row.

That left the people who knew without my ever telling them. The people who'd seen me carrying wrapped parcels. Who'd noticed I paid a little too promptly some months. Who'd heard me turn down a drink because I "had inventory."

That list was short. And at the top of it, alphabetically and in every other way, sat Pell.

* * *

Pell Ammasar is what happens when talent marries caution and they raise a child together in poverty. He copies faster than I do. His hand is cleaner. He has never once, in eleven years at the Temple scriptorium, been late, been loud, or been interesting. If virtue were currency, Pell would own the city.

Which is why, when I walked into the scriptorium Wednesday morning and saw him hunched over his desk copying penitential psalms with the particular care of a man being very careful, I felt the floor tilt under me the way it had tilted when I read my own name in a century-old book.

You can tell a lot from how a man holds his pen. Guilt makes the strokes small. Fear makes them fast. Pell's were both — cramped little letters marching across the page like men trying not to be seen crossing a courtyard.

"Orsen." He didn't look up. "You're dripping."

I looked down. My hand was bleeding again — the shallow cuts from the veil lesson, the ones Ellery said would close once I stopped picking at them. I hadn't picked at them. They opened anyway, lately, as if my body had opinions about what I was planning.

"Paper cut," I said. "From a very aggressive hymnal."

He almost smiled. Almost. Then his pen stopped moving, and in the silence I heard him decide something.

"You should be careful," he said quietly. "People are watching you. More than usual."

"People are always watching me. I'm fascinating."

"I'm serious, Orsen." He set the pen down and finally looked at me, and there it was — not guilt exactly. Something worse. Pity. "There was talk. After the raid on Ash Street. Talk about whether the wardens would come to the scriptorium next. Whether they'd find anything if they did."

"And what did you say to that talk?"

His throat moved. "I said the Orsen Ravensworth I know wouldn't be fool enough to keep stock in his rooms after a warning."

"A warning," I repeated. "Funny. I never gave anyone a warning to give."

The silence stretched. Somewhere across the scriptorium, fifty pens scratched on, indifferent, and I understood suddenly why monasteries invented silence — not for holiness, but so that conversations like this one could happen without witnesses hearing the exact moment a friendship dies.

"Pell," I said, gently now, because rage is a fire and you don't wave it around indoors, "nine days ago, someone filed a tip. Number forty-one oh six. *Standard consideration.* Do you know anything about that?"

I watched him do the arithmetic. Watched him realize I'd already done mine. His face went through three expressions in the space of a breath — alarm, calculation, and then a flat, tired honesty that I honestly hadn't expected.

"How much do you owe?" he asked.

"That's not an answer."

"It's the only answer that matters." He rubbed his eyes with the heel of his hand, and for the first time I saw how gray he'd gotten. When had Pell gone gray? He was thirty-two. We were the same age. "How much, Orsen?"

"Two hundred and forty marks," I said. It cost me something to say it aloud. It always does. "To the Temple. Plus whatever the mountain court says the Temple owes it, which nobody will tell me."

"Three hundred and ten," Pell said. "As of last month. Mine."

I stared at him. Three hundred and ten marks. A scribe earns maybe forty a year, and half of that goes to room and ink. Pell had been drowning longer than I'd known, and he'd never said a word, and I had never thought to ask, because I was too busy drowning myself to notice the water rising around my friends.

"So you sold my name," I said.

"I sold a *rumor*," he said, and his voice cracked down the middle. "I told them there might be charms on Ash Street. I didn't tell them it was you. I didn't tell them where you slept. I told them what anyone on that street could have told them — what *everyone* on that street already knew, Orsen, you weren't subtle, you wrapped charm-stones in butcher paper like a man smuggling sausages—"

"They burned everything I had. They'd have taken me if Sera hadn't—"

"They'd have taken you and assessed you," Pell said, and the word landed in the room like a dropped weight. *Assessed.* We both knew what assessment meant. We copied the forms often enough. Value of assets. Value of labor. Value of soul, remainder, in years.

"The Temple doesn't arrest debtors anymore," he went on, quieter. "It transfers them. You know that. Everyone knows that. Where do you think the transferred ones go? There's a road under Mount Vhalen and every quarter a cart of 'assessed' debtors goes down it and none of them come back up, and we *copy the receipts*, Orsen. I've written *received in good order* beside the names of men I drank with." His hands were shaking now. "Eleven years I've written those receipts. And then last month they sent me my own statement of arrears with a note attached. A polite note. A *considerate* note. It said that informants in good standing receive standard consideration."

"They threatened you into selling me."

"They *offered* me," Pell said. "That's worse, isn't it? That's the whole horror of it. Nobody held a knife to my throat. They just showed me the knife lying on the table between us and said, helpfully, that it cuts both ways." He laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all year. "My daughter needs a lung-mender. Fourteen marks a visit. I have a choice between feeding the machine a stranger or watching my girl drown in her own blood, and you want to know the truly obscene part? I'd do it again. I'd do it tomorrow. That's what they've built, Orsen. Not a tyranny. A machine where every cog betrays the next cog and calls it providing for his family."

I should have hit him. Part of me wanted to, wanted it badly enough that I could feel my knuckles rehearsing it. But you can't punch a man for being caught in the same trap you're in, and besides — he'd handed me something worth more than my whole burned stock of charms, though he didn't know it yet.

Because here is what Pell had just confirmed, in his cracked voice, with his shaking hands: the Temple of Nine Bells does not merely tolerate the court beneath the mountain. It *feeds* it. It harvests its debtors, assesses them, carts them down the old roads, and files the receipts in triplicate. And when the supply runs thin, it offers its own scribes a discount on damnation — sell us a name, and we'll lose yours in the shuffle.

The prophecy said the thief's hand would be guided by betrayal. I had assumed it meant mine. But maybe betrayal isn't one act. Maybe in Vharen it's a weather system. Maybe it's the air itself, and we've all been breathing it so long we call it normal.

* * *

I didn't go back to my rooms. I walked. Vharen at dusk is a city pretending to be respectable — shutters closing, bells tolling the hour, lamplighters working their rounds with the unhurried grace of people who have decided not to think about what the lights are for. I passed Sera on Candlemaker's Row. She was up a ladder, trimming a lamp, and she called down something mocking about my face, which apparently wears its thoughts like a dirty shirt. I called back something equally mature. Neither of us mentioned the raid, or the burning, or the cough she still pretended was dust.

I kept walking until I reached the wall where the old notices hang — the ones the Temple posts quarterly, listing debts publicly, as scripture commands. *Let every man's burden be seen, that shame may teach him thrift.* Shame teaches nothing. I've read that wall since I was nine years old. All it ever taught me was how many names there are, and how the list grows every quarter, and how the same families appear year after year like chronic illnesses.

But tonight I read it differently. Tonight I counted.

Two hundred and eleven names on the current posting. At an average assessment of, say, ten years of soul apiece — and the assessments run higher for the old debts — that was two thousand years of human life posted on a wall like a grocery list. Per quarter. In one city. And Vharen was only one bell in the Temple's ring of nine.

I stood there in the cold doing arithmetic that made my hands shake, and I thought about Pell's daughter, and Sera's fifteen years, and the whispering I'd heard through the veil — souls like wind through wires, hundreds of voices, all of them saying the same thing in different words: *we were somebody's friend, once, and it wasn't enough to save us.*

Here is the thing about rage. It gets a bad reputation, but rage is just grief that has decided to stand up. And standing there in front of that wall, I felt something in me finish setting itself, the way a bone sets — painfully, and slightly crooked, and stronger at the break than it was before.

I could not buy justice. Justice in Vharen belongs to the Temple, and the Temple sells it by the pound to the thing under the mountain. I could not appeal to law, because law was the machinery of the sale. But a ledger, now. A ledger is a holy object to people like me. And somewhere in the Hall of Reckoning — or in the archive, or in Ellery's locked cabinet, or in the receipt-books Pell had been copying for eleven years with his careful guilty hand — there was a complete accounting of every soul the Temple had sold. Names. Dates. Prices. Signatures.

Proof.

Not the kind you take to a tribunal — the tribunals were bought. The kind you nail to a door. The kind you read aloud in a square full of people whose own names were on the list. The kind that turns two hundred and eleven entries into two hundred and eleven families asking questions that polite institutions cannot survive.

"Pell," I said to the empty street, testing the shape of it. My informant. My betrayer. My witness. Because who better to expose the machine than a cog who could describe every gear from the inside — and who hated it enough, underneath the fear, to want it broken?

He'd do it, too. Not for me. For the girl with the bad lungs, and for the eleven years of receipts, and because a man who has betrayed once and hated himself for it is the most dangerous ally alive: he has nothing left to protect except the chance to be otherwise.

I turned toward home, and that was when I saw the boy.

He couldn't have been more than twelve, leaning against the chandler's wall with the studied idleness of someone being paid to look idle. City kid, Temple coat a size too big, eyes that tracked me the way pigeons track bread. He'd been on Candlemaker's Row an hour ago. I was nearly sure of it.

We looked at each other for a long moment. Then he pushed off the wall, crossed the street, and held something out to me on his open palm with the gravity of a page bearing a royal decree.

A coin. Not real currency — a token, lead, stamped with the sigil of the Nine Bells on one side and, on the other, a single word in the old script that I recognized from a century-old book I probably shouldn't have read:

*Owed.*

"He said you'd know what it means," the boy said. "And he said to tell you: the ledger you want is in the third vault, and the third vault opens at midnight, and Master Ellery knows you know." The boy shrugged, professionally. "Those were his words. Mostly. Also he said you owe him a copper for the message, but the coin counts, so we're square."

"Who's *he*?"

But the boy was already walking away, hands in pockets, whistling, a child again, employed again, invisible again. And I stood under Sera's freshly trimmed lamps holding a lead token stamped *owed*, understanding that somewhere in this city, someone had decided my investigation was useful — and that in Vharen, nothing is given that isn't owed, and nothing is owed that isn't collected.

Midnight was four hours away.

I put the coin in my pocket, where it lay against my leg like a second heartbeat, and I went to find out who wanted me to open the third vault — and what they expected me to owe them for it.
