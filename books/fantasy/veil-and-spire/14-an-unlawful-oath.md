## 14. An Unlawful Oath

### Orsen

The cellar belonged to a woman named Marette who sold pickled eels by day and pretended not to exist by night, which made her the most honest merchant in Vharen. She rented us the space for three silver and a promise never to mention her name again, and I have kept that promise so well that even now I will only say it was a woman, and there were eels, and one of them is still watching me from somewhere in my memory.

Dagny Holt had chosen the place, naturally. Everything about her was chosen — the way she sat with her back to the wall and her eyes on the stairs, the way she'd laid her inquisitor's seal face-down on the barrel between us like a card she hadn't decided to play yet. The smugglers sat across from me, shoulder to shoulder like bookends that had been dropped once or twice.

Bren was the big one, all forearms and a beard you could lose a ledger in. He'd spent the last ten minutes sharpening a knife he clearly had no intention of using, because sharpening was what his hands did when his mouth wanted to be nervous. The other one — Wren, small, quick-eyed, with a scar through one eyebrow that suggested she'd lost an argument with a door — kept looking at me the way you look at a man who has just told you he intends to rob a god.

"So," Wren said. "Say it plain. We're going under the mountain."

"We're going under the mountain," I agreed.

"To steal a soul."

"One soul. Sera's. Nothing else." I said it firmly, because I had learned in nine days of Ellery's brutal tutoring that specificity was armor down there. Vague intentions were how the veils ate people.

"And then," Dagny said, in the tone of a woman reading charges, "we burn the trade to the ground. Every ledger. Every name. Every temple clerk who's been feeding debtors to the court beneath Mount Vhalen for thirty years."

"That part wasn't in the original plan," Bren rumbled.

"The original plan," I said, "was me going alone with a forged name and a lamp, which Master Ellery described as 'technically possible' in the way that drowning is technically swimming. Dagny improved it. Then we found out Pell sold my name to the wardens for debt relief, and I stopped caring about plans that end quietly."

Wren snorted. "You've got a scribe's temper. All ink and no noise until suddenly there's a fire."

"I contain multitudes," I said. "Mostly debt."

The joke landed the way jokes land in cellars before dangerous things — a small warmth everyone pretended not to feel. But I saw Bren's shoulders come down half an inch, and I counted that as progress.

Dagny turned her seal over. It caught the lamplight — real lamplight, Sera-trimmed quality, though I didn't tell anyone why I'd insisted on bringing my own oil. "Before anyone swears anything," she said, "you should know what this costs. An oath sworn in a court binds your property. An oath sworn here binds *you*. There's no tribunal to appeal to, no fine to pay, no cell to sit in until it blows over. Break this and the only penalty is that you have to keep being the person who broke it. Some men find that worse than hanging."

"Which are you?" Wren asked.

"Me?" Dagny almost smiled. It was a terrible thing to witness, like watching a cathedral attempt a curtsy. "I was hanged already. Professionally speaking. They took my office, my rank, and my name off the rolls. What's left doesn't scare."

I believed her. That was the frightening part. Nine days ago I'd have crossed the street to avoid an inquisitor; tonight I was about to swear blood-oaths with one, and the strangest thing was that it felt less like corruption and more like coming home.

"Then let's be exact about the terms," I said, because if I was going to damn myself I intended to do it in clean handwriting. "First: we go below, and we take back Sera's soul. Her name is on the record as Sera Vane, lamplighter, Ash Street, fifteen years sold three years past. Second: whatever happens below, none of us sells another soul to buy our way out. Not each other's. Not a stranger's. Not ours."

I looked at Bren when I said it. He met my eyes without flinching, which I chose to read as honesty rather than good acting. Time would tell. Time was going to tell me a great many things I didn't want to hear.

"Third," I went on, "the ledgers come up. The Temple's trade, the court's debts, every name — exposed. Publicly. Let Vharen choke on what it's been swallowing."

"And fourth," Dagny added, "no one dies for this who doesn't choose to. We're thieves, not martyrs. Martyrs make bad witnesses."

"Speak for yourself," said Bren. "I'd make a gorgeous martyr."

"You'd make a large one," Wren said. "They'd need two pyres."

And there it was again, that warmth, threading through the fear like lamplight through smoke. I want to be honest about this, since honesty is the whole point of the exercise: I had spent three years keeping everyone at arm's length because arm's length was the only distance at which my debts couldn't reach them. And here, in a pickle-smelling cellar, four strangers were about to tie their fates to mine with nothing but words, and some traitorous organ behind my ribs was whispering *this is what it feels like*.

This is what it feels like when people stay.

Dagny produced a knife — hers, not Bren's, though his looked wounded about it — and a shallow dish. "Old form," she said. "Blood in the dish, oath over the flame, names spoken true. Below the mountain, names have power. Up here, tonight, we borrow a little of that law for ourselves."

"Spoken true," I repeated. My stomach did something complicated. "All of us? Real names?"

"It's not an oath if it's a forgery," Dagny said. "That's the entire difference between an oath and a lie with ceremony."

So we gave them. Wren, whose real name was Odile Ferrin and who looked genuinely pained to surrender it. Bren, who was actually Berengar Tull and who pronounced it like a man setting down a heavy load. Dagny Holt, which needed no correction, because apparently even her birth certificate had been exiled.

And then me. "Orsen Ravensworth."

It sounded strange in my own voice. I realized I hadn't said my full name aloud in years — not since the Temple clerks had stopped using it and started using my account number. Names were currency in Vharen, and I'd been hoarding mine like a miser. Spending it now, over a stolen flame in a borrowed cellar, felt less like losing something and more like finally buying something worth the price.

We cut our palms. We held them over the lamp — my lamp, Sera's spare, trimmed and burning steady — and let four drops fall into the heat, where they hissed and vanished, which I tried very hard not to interpret as an omen.

"By flame and by name," Dagny said, "sworn outside every court, answerable to no law but the one we write: we go down together, we come up together or not at all, we sell no soul, we spend no friend, and we drag the truth into the light if it kills the light."

"If it kills us," Wren corrected.

"That too," Dagny admitted.

We said it together, four voices in a cellar that smelled of brine and old wood, and the words went out of us and did not come back. I don't know what I expected — thunder, a shiver, some sign that the universe had notarized the document. What I got instead was silence, and Bren gripping my cut hand in his enormous one hard enough to rearrange the bones, and Wren laughing shakily, and Dagny looking at the lamp with an expression I couldn't read and wouldn't ask about for another fourteen chapters of my life.

"Well," said Bren, releasing my hand. "Now we're family. Anyone want eels?"

Nobody wanted eels.

But we stayed anyway, all four of us, passing around a bottle of Wren's terrible plum brandy and trading the small histories people trade when they've just promised each other their lives. Bren had a daughter in the lowtown who thought he moved furniture. Wren had run messages for the resistance until the resistance ran out of members. Dagny said nothing about herself except "I had reasons," twice, in a voice that closed doors.

And I told them about Sera. Not the prophecy version — not the soul in the glass case, the flickering lamp-flame, the fifteen years. I told them about the bread she stole and split unevenly in my favor. About how she teased me for counting my debts in my sleep. About the cough she hid behind her sleeve and the grin she hid behind everything else.

"She sounds too good for you," Wren said.

"She is," I said. "That's rather the point."

* * *

### Dagny

I have sworn oaths in cathedrals. I have sworn them in courtrooms with the Nine Bells ringing and the Grand Inquisitor's hand on my shoulder, and every one of those oaths had a hole in it the size of ambition, because institutions draft their promises the way lawyers draft contracts — with exits.

This one had no exits. I checked. I always check.

The boy — I know he's twenty-six, but everything about him reads *boy*, the ink-stained fingers, the flinch when raised voices pass overhead, the way he holds his cup like it might be repossessed — the boy thinks he recruited me. He believes I found his illegal copy of the prophecy and attached myself to his cause out of shared principle. There's a version of events where that's true.

Here is the truer version. Twenty-two years ago I stood in a courtroom and watched a woman named Maren Holt — my sister, my elder by four years, the person who taught me to read — declared *debt-settled* by a tribunal that never told us where she'd gone. Debt-settled. As if she were a chair. Three months later the dreams started, the ones inquisitors are trained to dismiss, and I dismissed them for eleven years because dismissal was my job and I was very good at my job. Then I found the ledger. Then I asked one question too loudly. Then I was hanged, professionally speaking, and set loose into the world with nothing but a seal nobody had thought to confiscate and a grudge the size of a mountain.

A literal mountain, as it turns out.

So no, I didn't join Orsen Ravensworth's crusade out of principle. I joined it because his prophecy-book mentioned the auction hall, and because a man walking into that hall with a stolen ledger might open a door I've been picking at for two decades. The fact that I've grown fond of him — fond of all of them, these ridiculous, brave, doomed children — is an complication I did not plan for and do not intend to report.

The oath sits in me like swallowed coal. Good. Coal burns.

While the others traded stories, I watched the stairs. Habit. Also suspicion, which is habit's uglier sibling. A plan like ours has more holes than a sieve: Ellery knows, the veil-keeper knows, the smugglers' network knows, and somewhere in that chain of knowing is always a Pell — someone small and frightened who trades other people's names for a few weeks of breathing room. I've interrogated enough Pells to know they're not evil. They're just cheaper than courage.

"Your turn," the boy said, catching me watching him instead of the stairs. "Fair's fair. Why does an exiled inquisitor want to burn down the court?"

"Reasons," I said.

"You said that already."

"I'm consistent."

He waited. That's the thing about Orsen — he has the patience of a man used to waiting for ink to dry, and he deploys it like a weapon. I lasted forty seconds, which for me is practically garrulous.

"My sister," I said. "Sold. Twenty-two years ago. If her flame is still lit down there, I mean to find it. If it isn't—" I turned my cup. "Then I mean to make the dark pay rent."

Nobody laughed. Bren refilled my cup without a word, which is the correct response to grief among professionals, and Wren touched my wrist for exactly one second, which is the correct response among friends.

"There's a case for every flame," Orsen said quietly. "If hers is there, we'll find it. That's the fourth term. No one gets left below."

"No one who swears tonight," I corrected. "I know the terms. I wrote half of them."

He smiled at that, tired and crooked, and I thought: *this one would have made a fine inquisitor, and thank every silent god that he won't.*

Later, when the brandy was gone and the others had gone up the stairs into the ordinary criminal night, I stayed a moment longer with the lamp. It burned the way good lamps burn — steady, uncomplaining, trimmed by someone who cared about the work. Lamplighter's oil. Lamplighter's trim. I'd noticed it earlier and said nothing.

Whoever this Sera was, a man had walked into a cellar carrying her light like a relic, and four strangers had sworn on its flame. Whatever happened below the mountain, that lamp had already done its work. It had made us a congregation.

I pinched it out gently, the way you close a book you intend to finish, and followed the others up.

* * *

### Bren

People think smuggling is about nerve. It isn't. Nerve is common — every drunk in Vharen has nerve. Smuggling is about knowing which doors are lies, and tonight I stood in a cellar and swore on a lamp to walk through the biggest lying door in the world, and I want it recorded that I knew better and did it anyway.

My girl, Pip, is seven. She thinks I move furniture. When this is done — if this is done — I'll tell her the truth, or most of it, or the parts that fit in a bedtime story. *Papa went under the mountain to fetch a lady's light home.* She'll like that. She likes lamps.

The scribe worries too much to die. I've watched him these nine days, training with old Ellery until he vomits, studying veil-law until his eyes cross, and underneath all the terror he's got a spine like a ledger's binding — bent, creased, but holding. The inquisitor is ice with a furnace somewhere deep in the middle; I've carried enough contraband past enough checkpoints to know hidden heat when I stand next to it. And little Odile — Wren — laughs at death because she's never once taken anything seriously, including herself, which is either wisdom or a diagnosis.

Me, I take things seriously. That's my whole trick. I take them seriously and I show up anyway.

When we cut our palms I didn't hesitate, and it surprised me, the not-hesitating. I've hesitated at every border crossing of my life. But blood over a lamplighter's flame, swearing to sell no soul — that I could sign without reading the fine print, because for once there wasn't any. Four lines. Clean terms. You could fit the whole oath on a coin.

After the others left, I sat a while in the dark with my cut hand wrapped in my shirt-tail, thinking about the door below. Ellery says the veil opens in six days. Six days to get Pip's winter boots, to settle my accounts with Marette — the eel woman, honest as sin — to write the letter that explains itself badly.

Six days. Then the lying door.

I got up, blew out nothing because the lamp was already out, and went home through streets that felt different now — thinner, somehow, like the city was paper and I'd seen the writing on the back of it.

At my door, on the step, small and deliberate: a warden's chalk mark. The kind they leave on houses they're watching.

Somebody had given us up already. Somebody always does.

I rubbed it out with my thumb, went inside, and started packing for a trip my daughter must never learn about — while upstairs, in the flat above mine, someone's floorboard creaked, once, softly, like a listener settling in.
