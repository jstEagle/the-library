## 15. First Descent

There is a particular kind of fear that arrives not as a scream but as a ledger entry. You look at your life, you tally the columns, and you realize the numbers no longer balance — that somewhere between *scribe* and *prophecy-thief* a debt has accrued that cannot be paid in coin or charm or cleverness.

I felt it on the fourth night of my instruction, standing in the cellar beneath the tannery on Cindermilk Row while Dagny Holt drew a circle around my feet with chalk she kept in a pouch like a hangman keeps rope.

"Rules," she said, for what I judged was the eleventh time. She had a habit of numbering things. Rules, warnings, reasons I should turn back. "One: speak nothing below that isn't true, because lies have weight there and weight is currency. Two: accept nothing freely given, because nothing down there is free. Three—"

"Three: if I see something beautiful, assume it's hungry."

"You're learning." She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth she'd produced in four days, and I treasured it the way one treasures a coin found in a gutter — gratefully, and with suspicion about where it had been.

Ellery stood at the edge of the chalk circle wringing his hands, which was his default posture and, I suspected, had been since roughly the age of six. He'd opened this veil before — he'd admitted as much, in the way guilty men admit things, all at once and then never again — but tonight he was only witness. Tonight the teaching fell to Dagny, who had learned the old roads during her years as an inquisitor, back when learning them was how you hunted people rather than saved them.

"This is a shallow veil," she said. "A crack, not a door. We go through, we stand on the threshold lands, we look, we come back. No farther. If anything speaks to you, you do not answer. If anything offers you anything, you refuse politely. Politeness matters. The court respects manners the way a spider respects a fly that doesn't struggle."

"That's not comforting."

"It isn't meant to be. Comfort is a currency too, down there. Everything is." She crouched and pressed two fingers to the chalk line. "Ready?"

"No," I said honestly.

"Good. The ones who say yes are liars, and liars sink." She spoke a word I didn't catch — a word that seemed to arrive slightly after she said it, like thunder lagging lightning — and the cellar floor opened.

I don't mean it cracked. I mean it *opened*, the way an eye opens, the stone becoming a lid over something that had been waiting underneath Vharen all along, under every street and sewer and grave, patient as interest on a loan. The darkness below wasn't empty. It was *occupied*. And then Dagny took my wrist and we stepped off the world together, and I learned that falling and walking can be the same thing if the road beneath you consents to be walked.

* * *

The threshold lands were gray.

That sounds like a complaint, so let me be precise: they were gray the way a ledger page is gray, the way ash is gray, the way a thing is gray when color has been *repossessed*. A plain stretched out before us made of ground that looked like packed flour and felt, when I dared touch it, like cold velvet. Above us — if above was the right word — hung no sky but a vast dim suggestion of one, a ceiling of fog lit from nowhere, and far away at the edge of seeing stood shapes that might have been mountains or might have been the shoulders of something sleeping.

The air tasted like the inside of a locked room.

"Threshold lands," Dagny murmured. "The court's front garden. Everything past here belongs to Mount Vhalen the way everything inside a temple belongs to its god. Walk where I walk. Step nowhere I don't."

We walked. My legs worked, which surprised me. My heart worked harder. Every few steps the ground gave faintly underfoot, soft as turf, and each time I had the sick certainty that it wasn't ground at all but something breathing very slowly, very deep, and that our footsteps were the only interesting thing happening in its long gray dream.

"How far?" I whispered.

"Far enough. Close enough." She pointed.

At first I thought the light ahead was dawn, which would have been absurd, because I had already understood that dawn did not operate down here. Then I saw it was a hall — a hall built into the flank of the mountain-shape, its doors tall enough to admit grief on horseback, its windows glowing with a gold so warm and human-seeming that my eyes actually stung with relief before my mind caught up and asked the necessary question:

What burns that brightly, underground, forever?

Lamps, said some traitorous part of me. Lamplight. Sera-light. And my feet wanted to run toward it, and I want to tell you I resisted, but the truth is Dagny's grip on my arm was the only reason my next step went forward instead of toward that glow like a moth with a name and a debt.

"There," she said quietly. "The auction hall. That's as close as we come tonight."

We stopped behind a ridge of pale stone — bone-colored, possibly bone — and looked down across a shallow valley at the doors of the court beneath the mountain.

Even from that distance, even through the dead gray air, I heard them.

Souls.

They sounded like wind through wires. You know the sound — the telegraph lines along the Ash Street viaduct on a winter night, that thin keening note that makes dogs bark and children ask questions their parents won't answer. It was that, multiplied by hundreds, by thousands, a chorus of small sustained notes rising from the glowing hall, and each note was different, and each note was *singing*, and I understood with a lurch that none of them knew they were singing. They were simply being what remained of someone, resonating in their cases, the way a struck glass hums without meaning to.

"Sera's in there," I said. My voice came out flat and strange, a stranger's voice.

"If she hasn't been sold onward. Souls get resold. The court trades in futures." Dagny's face, in the gray light, was carved from something less forgiving than stone. "Look carefully. This is the part Ellery couldn't teach you, because Ellery has spent thirty years refusing to look."

So I looked.

Through the great doors, which stood open — open, always open, a mouth that never needed to close because nothing down there had teeth worth fearing — I could see the auction floor. Rows of glass cases on iron stands, arranged like a greenhouse for impossible flowers, and in each case a flame. Not fire exactly. Fire is honest; it consumes and asks nothing. These flames were *lit from within*, each one a small person-shaped brightness folded down to the size of a candle, flickering in rhythms I slowly, horribly recognized as gestures. One turned, endlessly, as if searching a crowd for a face. One rose and fell, rose and fell, like breathing, like a chest, like — I gripped the ridge until my knuckles cracked — like a cough.

Like Sera's cough, three years worsening, the rhythm of it worn into her soul so deeply that the soul still performed it, alone in its case, in the dark, under the mountain.

The wind-through-wires sound swelled, and I realized the souls could feel us watching. Or feel *me* watching. The nearest cases' flames leaned toward the doors, all in the same direction, like grass bending under wind, like flowers turning toward a sun that was a scribe from Ash Street with chalk dust on his boots.

"They know I'm here," I breathed.

"Everything knows you're here. That's the point of prophecy — it's not a prediction, it's an invitation sent ahead of time." Dagny pulled me down lower. "Which means everything down there also knows why you've come. The King will have been told within moments of your crossing. He may be watching you right now."

"Where?"

"Anywhere. Everywhere. He's old, Ravensworth. Older than the mountain's name. When he looks at you, you won't see him looking. You'll just notice, afterward, that you've been thinking about him for some time without knowing when you started."

And that was precisely when I noticed I'd been thinking about him for some time without knowing when I started.

A cold moved through me that had nothing to do with the threshold lands' chill. Because he *was* there, suddenly, in the way a thought is there — vast and courteous and amused, a presence shaped like patience itself, and I felt him consider me the way a moneylender considers a debtor who has finally walked into the office. Not with hunger. With *bookkeeping*. Somewhere beneath the mountain, in a hall full of singing lamps, something ancient had opened a ledger, found my name — both my names, the true one and the forged one Dagny had drilled into me — and written a small satisfied mark beside them.

"He's smiling," I said. "I can't see him and he's smiling."

"Yes."

"Why does that frighten me more than screaming would?"

"Because screaming wants something from you. Smiling has already gotten it." She took my shoulder and turned me away from the glow, and the flames in the cases leaned after me, and the wire-wind keened one note higher, and I swear on whatever I still owned that one of them — one small flame near the front row, guttering, stubborn, rhythmically coughing — flared bright for a single instant as I turned, the way a lamp flares when its wick is trimmed.

The way Sera trimmed hers every night on Ash Street, quick and practiced, before kissing her fingers and touching the glass.

I ran.

I'm not proud of it. Dagny had rules about running — Rule Four, probably, *never show the land your fear, fear is currency* — but rules are for people whose hearts are still in their chests, and mine was back in that valley, leaning against the glass of a case, coughing in the dark. I ran across the breathing ground and the gray plain blurred and the wire-song chased me, not angrily, not hungrily, but *hopefully*, hundreds of small voices harmonizing on my name in a key I'd never heard and would hear in dreams for the rest of my life.

Dagny caught me at the veil-crack. Of course she did. She put one hand flat against my sternum and said the lagging-thunder word backward and the world closed over us like water, and then we were in the tannery cellar, on honest stone, in honest dark that smelled of hides and lamp oil and Vharen, and I was on my knees shaking so hard my teeth chattered.

Ellery hovered. "Was it — did you see —"

"I saw." I couldn't lift my head. My hands wouldn't stop trembling, so I watched them, fascinated, as though they belonged to a braver man having a worse night. "They sing, Ellery. Nobody told me they sing."

"The records describe them as inert—"

"The records were written by men who sold them!" The words tore out of me with a force that surprised us all. I got upright by way of a shelf, which held, barely. "They lean toward the doors. They *watched* me. One of them—" My voice failed, restarted. "One of them coughed."

Silence in the cellar. Even the tannery vats overhead seemed to hold their breath.

Dagny crouched beside me, and for the first time in four days she touched my shoulder not to steer or restrain but the way you steady something you've decided is worth keeping whole. "Now you know," she said. "Not what the court is. What it *does*. Most people go their whole lives knowing neither. You learned it in one night, and you came back, and you're still planning to return. That's rarer than you think."

"I ran."

"You ran *back*. There's a difference, and the court knows it, and now so do you." She stood, brushed chalk from her knees, and looked at me with those inquisitor's eyes that catalogued everything and forgave selectively. "Rest. Eat. Tomorrow we begin the real lessons — bargains, names, the grammar of the veil. Because the trial run is over, Ravensworth, and here is what it bought us: the King has seen you now. Which means he'll make you an offer. They always do. And when he does—"

She paused, and in the pause I heard the wire-song again, faintly, impossibly, as though it had followed me up through the crack in the world and was waiting patiently in the walls of Vharen for me to sleep.

"—when he does," Dagny said, "you'll need to know, before you answer, exactly what he's really selling. Because I promise you it isn't what's written on the tag."

Behind her, unnoticed, the chalk circle she'd drawn lay scorched black — and at its northern edge, where I had stepped back into the world, the burn marks didn't fade into stone.

They faded into letters. Small, neat, patient ones, in a hand I didn't recognize yet would spend the rest of this story learning to dread:

*He came early. Good. The King dislikes waiting.*
