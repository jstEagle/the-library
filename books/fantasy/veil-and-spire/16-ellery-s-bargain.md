## 16. Ellery's Bargain

There is a particular kind of silence that lives in the archive of the Temple of Nine Bells after midnight. It is not the silence of an empty room. It is the silence of a room that is listening.

I have lived inside that silence for thirty years, and I have learned its grammar. It says: *we know what you did.* It says it politely, the way Vharen does everything. The city never accuses you outright. It simply arranges itself around your guilt like furniture, and you learn to walk carefully between the chairs.

Tonight I am walking very carefully indeed.

The boy — Ravensworth — has found the book. Of course he has. I placed it where his hands would find it, in the crate of seized texts marked for cataloguing, third shelf from the top because he is a tall man who stoops when he thinks no one watches, and stooping men look down. He will have read it by now. He will have seen the date circled in ink that dried before either of us was born, and beneath it, in a hand that is not mine and not anyone's, his own name.

*Orsen Ravensworth.*

I wrote that name there myself, eleven years ago, copying it from a page that had shown it to me first. But that is getting ahead of things. If you are to judge me — and you will, everyone does eventually, it is the one tax Vharen collects without exception — then you must begin where I began, thirty years ago, on a night much like this one, with the mountain humming under my feet and a ledger open on my desk that would not stay closed.

* * *

I was thirty-four years old, newly made Master Archivist, and I believed — this is important; I want it recorded somewhere, even if only in the confession I am writing now and will burn by morning — I believed I was a good man doing a difficult job.

The winter of that year killed forty-one people in the Lowquarters. Not dramatically. No plague, no fire. They simply stopped. A lamplighter froze at her post with her lamp still burning. A baker lay down beside his oven and did not get up. The physicians called it wasting fever, and the wardens called it nothing at all, because naming things costs money and Vharen was poor in everything except debts.

But I kept the temple's records, and records are honest even when their keepers are not, and I noticed something the physicians missed. Every one of those forty-one had sold something to the mountain. Years of luck. A talent for music. A grandmother's blessing. Small sales, desperate sales, the kind the debt-brokers arrange in back rooms for people who have run out of everything else. And every one of them had died within a season of the sale, hollowed out like gourds.

The court beneath Mount Vhalen was eating them. Not all at once — the court is patient, the court is *civilized* — but steadily, the way a tide eats a sandcastle while pretending to be merely water.

I did what any archivist would do. I researched. For four months I read everything the temple had seized or suppressed about the undervaults, the old roads, the veils. I learned that the court had once been a kingdom's treasury, that its king had been buried alive with it during some war whose name we no longer remember, and that death had not taught him humility so much as accounting. I learned that the court survived on souls the way Vharen survived on grain imports: continuously, and without gratitude.

And then, on the fourth of Frostmonth, I did what no archivist should ever do.

I opened a veil.

Not a great one. A thread, really, no wider than a needle, tucked behind the archive's false wall where the censers used to be stored. I told myself I only wanted to look. This is the lie every coward tells, and I want to be fair to my younger self: he believed it. He stood in the cold dark with his lantern held high and he looked down the long throat of the old road, and he heard — I still hear it, some nights, when the wind comes off the mountain wrong — he heard the souls whispering. Like wind through wires. Like a choir that had forgotten its own names.

And something came up the road to meet him.

It wore the shape of a clerk. That should tell you everything about the court beneath the mountain. It is not a place of horns and horror. It is a place of *paperwork*. The thing that met me had a face like a folded document and a voice like a stamp coming down on wax, and it said:

"Master Ellery. You have an inquiry."

"I—" My voice cracked like a boy's. "I represent the Temple of Nine Bells."

"The temple does not know you are here."

"No," I admitted.

"Good." The clerk-shape produced, from nowhere and everywhere, a ledger. "Then we may speak frankly. Your city owes us. It has owed us since before your temple had bells. We have been lenient. Leniency ends this year. Forty-one souls last winter was our collection, and it was *improvised*, Master Ellery. Improvisation is vulgar. We prefer contracts."

"What kind of contract?"

The thing smiled. I use the word loosely. Its face creased the way paper creases.

"A steady supply," it said. "Debtors. The ones your temples and brokers have already squeezed dry. You have thousands of them, living in your Lowquarters like coins someone dropped behind a sofa. Give us the ones no one will miss, and we will leave the rest of Vharen alone. No wasting fevers. No winters of stopping. Your city keeps its heart beating."

"And if I refuse?"

"Then we collect as we always have. Widely. Indiscriminately. Perhaps we begin with the temple itself." It tilted its head. "You have a fine voice, Master Ellery. It would be a shame to spend it screaming in a vault."

I want to tell you I refused. I want to tell you I stood there in the dark and said *no, find another archivist,* and was struck down or exalted or anything other than what actually happened, which is that I thought about the forty-one dead, and then I thought about the forty thousand living, and I did the arithmetic that cowards have done since the beginning of cowardice, which always comes out the same way: *fewer, if I agree. Fewer deaths, if I say yes. Fewer, fewer, fewer* — until the word loses all meaning and becomes just a sound you hide behind.

"One condition," I heard myself say. "They must consent. There must be papers. Debts, properly recorded. Nothing taken that wasn't already sold."

The clerk looked almost pleased. "You want the fiction maintained."

"I want the *law* maintained."

"Yes," it said. "That is the same thing. We have a contract."

And that was it. That was the whole of it. Thirty years of Vharen's safety, purchased in a conversation shorter than a sermon, sealed with no handshake at all. The veil closed behind me and I went home and was sick in the gutter outside my own door, and in the morning I washed my face and unlocked the archive and began keeping two sets of books.

That is the profession, really. Everything else — the robes, the title, the respect — is packaging. An archivist is a man who decides which truths survive. I decided that Vharen would survive, and that certain truths would not, and for thirty years the arrangement held. Three or four debtor souls a month, all documented, all technically sold, all funneled down the old roads through veils I opened and closed like a man drawing curtains. The city flourished. The bells rang. Children who would have died of the wasting lived to be tedious adults, and I attended their weddings and funerals and told myself, each time, *this one is here because of me. This one is the interest paid on my sin, and the principal is a city.*

You may call it self-justification. You would be right. But understand: a self-justification is still a justification. It is simply one written by the guilty party, and therefore more honest than most, because at least we know where the bodies are filed.

* * *

Here is what I did not anticipate, in all my careful accounting: compound interest.

The court does not forget a contract. The court does not *close* a contract. Thirty years of souls bought Vharen safety, yes — but the contract had a clause I never saw, because I signed it in the dark with a lantern shaking in my hand, and clauses written in the dark are always the expensive ones. I found the clause three years ago, in a text I had myself seized and suppressed twenty years earlier, a merchant's diary from the last century, and I sat down on the archive floor like a man whose legs had been repossessed.

*"When the supply falters,"* the diary read, *"the debt comes due entire. And the court sends for the contractor."*

The supply was faltering. It had been faltering for years — the debt-brokers were running out of throats to squeeze, the Lowquarters was emptying, and the court's patience, which is vast, is not infinite. I had perhaps two years. Maybe less.

And then, eleven years ago now — I remember because it was the night of the Long Bells — the prophecy book showed me a way out.

I did not find the book. I want that understood too. The book found *me*. It was in the deepest stack, uncatalogued, unseizable, older than the temple's oldest bell, and one night I came around the corner of Stack Nine and it was simply *open* on the reading table, turned to a page I had never seen, though I have since memorized every word.

*A thief will come to the court beneath the mountain, guided by betrayal, and steal back a soul that was sold. On the thief's return, the debt of the city is forgiven entire — the contractor released, the contract burned, the account closed.*

An escape clause. Written a century before I needed it. The court's own law, turned against the court, provided someone could fulfill it.

Provided someone could walk down those roads and steal from a king who has been robbing the dead for a thousand years, and walk back out again.

I am sixty-four years old. I have bad knees and a conscience like a rusted hinge. I am not that someone.

But I am an archivist. I decide which truths survive. And so I began to search the records for a man who might be — and I will admit the selection process was not noble. I needed someone expendable. Someone debt-bound, so desperation would sharpen him. Someone clever with documents, so he could forge a name below. Someone with a reason to hate the trade, so he would not stop halfway. And — this part shames me most, and I set it down anyway — someone alone enough that when he failed, no one would make noise about it.

Orsen Ravensworth. Scribe, second class. Debt to the Temple of Nine Bells: considerable. Friends: one, a lamplighter, which I confess I did not initially count as significant.

I planted the book where he would find it. I watched him find it. I watched his face go through the stages — confusion, disbelief, vertigo, that terrible hunger that purpose puts in young men's eyes — and I felt, for the first time in thirty years, something adjacent to hope.

Adjacent. Not the thing itself. Hope requires believing you are on the side of the story where things end well, and I have known for thirty years exactly which side of the story I am written on. I am the contractor. When the account closes, the contract burns — and the clause does not specify whether the burning stops at the paper.

* * *

Which brings us to tonight.

Ravensworth has the copy. He will have read the whole of it by now, including the line about betrayal, which I left intact because omitting it would be fraud, and whatever else I am, I have never falsified a record. He will be angry. He will feel chosen rather than choosing, and he will be right to feel it, and I will let him feel it, because anger is fuel and he will need every drop for what comes next.

He will come to me. They always come to the archivist eventually; we are where the questions live. And when he does, I will teach him the old roads, and I will open the veils, and I will spend whatever remains of my reputation, my freedom, and quite possibly my life making sure he reaches that court and steals that flame.

Because here is the arithmetic I still believe in, rusted hinge and all: one scribe's risk against forty thousand lives. One stolen soul against a city's whole account. It is the same bargain I struck thirty years ago, inverted — then I fed the few to save the many, and now I arm the one to free them all — and if that inconsistency damns me, well. Consistency is a virtue for people whose hands are clean.

There is a knock at the archive door. Three raps, uneven, impatient. Him.

I close this confession. I do not burn it — not yet; I find I lack the nerve, which is fitting, since nerve has never been among my assets. I slide it behind the false wall, next to the veil-thread, next to thirty years of ledgers, next to the small cold space where my soul would be if I had not spent it in installments.

"Enter," I call, and put on the face I wear for the living: mild, scholarly, faintly apologetic. The face of a good man doing a difficult job.

The door opens. And standing behind Ravensworth, half-hidden in the corridor shadow, is a young woman with a lamplighter's calluses and a cough she is trying very hard not to have — and she is looking at me the way people look at a locked door they intend to get through.

Ah, I think. So the soul in question has opinions of her own.

This, I suspect, is going to complicate everything.
