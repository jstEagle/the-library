## 17. Sera Confronts Orsen

### Sera

I have lit four thousand lamps on Ash Street, give or take the ones the wind murdered before their time, and I know every trick of a flame that doesn't want to be found. So when I came around the corner by the tannery and saw no light at all in Orsen's window — not candle, not lamp, not even the grey ghost-glow of a moon through cheap glass — I knew something was wrong in the specific way that things are wrong with Orsen, which is to say: quietly, and with paperwork.

He'd shuttered the window from the inside. Orsen had never once shuttered his window. He complained about the cold for three years running and called shutters "a coward's architecture."

I let myself in with the key he thinks I don't have. I've had it since the winter his hands shook too badly to turn his own lock, back when I still believed the shaking was just hunger.

The room was a museum of bad decisions. A pack half-filled. The stolen ledger wrapped in oilcloth like a swaddled infant. His good boots — the ones he claimed were "for funerals" — sitting by the door, freshly oiled. And on the table, laid out with the tenderness most men reserve for love letters: a knife, a spool of black thread, a vial of something that smelled like a struck match, and Sera's lamp.

My lamp. The one I'd reported stolen two winters ago so the wardens wouldn't ask why I kept it burning past curfew. He'd had it all along. He'd trimmed the wick. He'd filled it.

That's how I knew this wasn't a scheme. Orsen schemes the way rats swim — frantically, and only when drowning. This was preparation. This was a man packing for a road he didn't expect to come back down.

"Evening," I said.

He jumped like a cat in a bell shop, which was satisfying, and then went pale, which was not, because Orsen pale is Orsen guilty is Orsen about to do something noble, and nobility in that man has always been a symptom rather than a virtue.

"Sera. It's late."

"It's my street after dark. I own it, more or less. The lamps and I have an arrangement." I picked up the lamp. It sat warm in my hands, familiar as my own pulse, which lately was doing less work than it used to. "You were going to leave without saying goodbye."

"I was going to leave a note."

"A note." I turned it over in my mouth like a bad coin. "A note. From the man who once wrote me a four-page letter explaining why he couldn't repay six coppers, complete with footnotes."

"There were extenuating circumstances with those coppers—"

"You were going to descend into the mountain where dead kings auction souls, and your farewell was going to be *a note*." I set the lamp down carefully, because if I set it down carelessly I was going to throw it at him. "Sit down, Orsen."

"You can't forbid me—"

"Sit *down*."

He sat. That's the thing about Orsen Ravensworth that nobody who's only met him drunk understands: he is, underneath the wit and the debt and the flinch, an honest man. Honest men sit when you tell them to, because they haven't yet learned the trick of standing up for themselves without feeling like thieves.

I stood over him and told him what I'd come to tell him, which was: "You're not going."

And he looked up at me with those tired archivist's eyes and said, gently, the way you talk to someone standing on ice, "Yes, I am."

### Orsen

Here is what nobody tells you about being caught: it's almost a relief. For nine days I'd been living a double life, copying veils-lore into the margins of tax ledgers, lying to Pell about my hours, smiling at Ellery across the archive while planning to rob the god beneath the mountain. My face hurt from holding its shape.

Sera's face, when she caught me, did not hold its shape at all. It did everything at once — fury, fear, and underneath both, something older and sadder that I recognized because I'd been wearing it myself since I read her name in the debt records.

Fifteen years. She'd sold fifteen years of herself to erase my ledger, and she'd done it the way she did everything — quietly, competently, and without filing a single footnote.

"You're not going," she said again, as though repetition were jurisdiction.

"The prophecy names me," I said. "The thief. It's been waiting a hundred years for tonight's date and my name under it. If I don't go—"

"If you don't go, the city pays. Yes. Ellery's little sermon. I've heard it." She paced the width of his room, which took four steps, which meant she passed the packed bag twice per circuit, glaring at it each time like it owed her money. "You know what else happens if you go? You die. Or worse — you succeed, and the court comes collecting, and the city pays anyway, and you paid too. There's no version of this where the arithmetic works, Orsen. I've done sums my whole life. This one doesn't balance."

"It balances," I said, "if the thief is worth less than the city."

She stopped pacing. That was a mistake. I should have kept talking, filled the silence with wit, which is my armor and my native language. Instead I said, quieter, "It balances if the thief is me."

"Don't." Her voice cracked on it, just slightly, like a wick catching wrong. "Don't do that thing where you make yourself small enough to spend. I've watched you do it for years. Every time the Temple squeezed, you shrank. Every time the debt grew, you apologized for taking up space. And I let you, because shrinking men are easy to help. But I'm telling you now, as your friend, as the person whose bread you've been stealing for three years—" she jabbed a finger at the cupboard where I hid the heels, "—you are not spending yourself on my account. I forbid it."

"Forbid it," I repeated. "Sera, you light lamps. You don't legislate."

"And you copy other people's words for a living. Doesn't stop you believing them." She pulled out the chair across from me and sat, hard, knees nearly touching mine. Up close I could see what the lamplight always forgave and daylight never did: the greyness under her skin, the way her breath carried a faint rattle like paper in wind. Three years thinner than she should be. Fifteen years thinner, technically. "Tell me why. Not the prophecy. Not Ellery's arithmetic. Why."

And because she'd asked the real question, I gave the real answer.

"Because I read the record," I said. "Three years ago. Entry 4471, Temple of Nine Bells, transfer of soul-years, fifteen, debtor: Ravensworth, O., cleared in full. Creditor of record: a name I didn't recognize until last week." I swallowed. "You sold fifteen years of yourself to pay my debts, and then you bought me bread with the interest, and you teased me about it, and you never said a word. I have been free for three years, Sera. Free off the back of your life. And every night I slept soundly in a bed you purchased with years you'll never get back. So yes. I'm going. Not because a book told me to. Because I owe you, and the court beneath the mountain is the only creditor I can actually reach."

The silence afterward had weight. It pressed on us both.

"That's not why," she said finally.

"What?"

"That's guilt, not reason. Guilt makes terrible currency below — Dagny told you that, I heard her through the cellar door last week, you thought I was asleep." She leaned forward. "You want to know why I did it? Really?"

I braced. "Yes."

"Because watching you drown was worse than drowning." She said it flatly, factually, the way she'd report a broken lamp bracket. "I'd finished my rounds, I had wages, I had years I wasn't using — I was twenty-six, Orsen, nobody uses their twenties well — and there you were, going under, and the Temple offered me a price, and I could pay it. Do you understand? It wasn't sacrifice. Sacrifice is when it costs you something you needed. I gave away surplus." Her hand went briefly to her chest, to the cough she pretended was dust. "The cough started last spring. That's not surplus anymore. But three years ago it was, and I'd do it again tomorrow, and that's what you can't forgive, isn't it? Not that I did it. That I'd do it again."

"Yes," I said, and hated how small my voice came out. "Because if you'd do it again, then everything I am is just a debt you keep choosing to service, and I can't stand being a line item in your ledger, Sera. I won't. I'd rather owe the mountain."

"You'd rather *owe the mountain*."

"I'd rather owe anything I can look in the eye."

### Sera

There it was. The crack in him, the one I'd been papering over for three years with bread and banter. He didn't want my help. He wanted my *respect*, and I'd been so busy saving him that I'd never once asked permission, and now he sat across from me with a packed bag and a stolen ledger and a lamp he'd kept secretly oiled for who knows how long, and he was right, damn him. He was right the way a splinter is right.

"You kept my lamp," I said, because I couldn't argue with him anymore and needed somewhere soft to put my voice.

"You reported it stolen."

"I did. To protect it." I reached over and touched the glass. Warm already, from sitting near him. Everything near Orsen got warm eventually; it was his one unmarketable talent. "Wardens confiscate lamplighters' personal lamps when the lighters fall behind on temple dues. Mine was flagged. So I stole it from myself, filed the theft, and let the case go cold." I laughed, and the laugh turned into the cough, and the cough went on two beats longer than a cough should, and I saw him watch it happen and catalog it, the way he catalogs everything, into evidence.

"You're dying," he said. Not a question. An accusation aimed at himself.

"I'm *thinning*. There's a difference. Dying is fast and dramatic. Thinning is what happens to a soul when someone's been skimming it for three years." I wiped my mouth. "I have maybe — I don't know. The temple physicker says 'months.' Physickers say months the way priests say 'perhaps': it means nothing and costs nothing."

"So I'm right to go."

"You are *not*—" I stopped. Breathed. The old trick: count the lamps. Four thousand, give or take. "Orsen. Listen to me. If you go down there and you win — if you actually steal my flame back and walk out — do you know what you'll have done? You'll have made us even. And we have never been even, and that imbalance is the only honest thing between us. You need me and I need you and neither of us has ever once said it out loud because saying it would mean admitting we can't survive alone, and in Vharen that's a confession the Temple taxes." I stood up. My legs were less steady than I wanted them to be; I put a hand on his table and pretended it was casual. "If you make us even, you'll leave. People leave when ledgers close. I've seen it. Debtors freed after ten years, walking out of the temple gates like strangers to their own lives."

"I would never—"

"You'd never *mean* to. But you'd wake up some morning owing me nothing, and you'd feel the shape of that nothing, and it would feel like freedom, and freedom is a door, Orsen, and doors only open one way." My voice was doing something embarrassing. I let it. "So no. I don't forbid you because I think you'll fail. I forbid you because I'm afraid you'll succeed."

He stared at me. And I watched him — I actually watched it happen — take my fear and turn it over in those clever hands and find the flaw in it, because he always finds the flaw, it's why the Temple pays him and why I'd trust him with my life, which is apparently literal now.

"That's not how it works," he said softly. "Between us. You think I keep a ledger. I burned mine years ago — you know that, you watched me burn it, you brought the wine." He rose too, and we were standing toe to toe in his narrow room, among his bad decisions and my lamp. "I'm not going to make us even. I'm going to make us *impossible to separate*. You want imbalance? Fine. After tomorrow you'll owe me a rescue and I'll owe you fifteen years and we'll call it a wash and start a new page, together, the way we should have from the beginning. But I'm not letting them keep you. Not the court, not the cough, not the arithmetic. Nobody keeps you, Sera. Not while I can walk."

"You can barely walk," I said. "Your veil lessons nearly killed you twice. Dagny says you have the natural grace of a dropped hymnal."

"Dagny exaggerates."

"She said you screamed."

"I *yelled*. There's a distinction. It's legal."

And despite everything — despite the packed bag and the thinning and the four thousand lamps I suddenly wanted to count again — I laughed. That was the trouble with him. He could always find the one crack in a wall of grief wide enough to fit a joke through, and then the whole wall came down laughing.

Then I stopped laughing, because I remembered what I'd actually come here to say, the thing beneath the forbidding, the true thing, and my mouth decided — against policy, against three years of careful silence — to say it.

"I'd do it again," I told him. "You asked. I'm answering. If I stood in that temple office tomorrow with fifteen years in my hand and you drowning in front of me, I'd sign again. Same terms. No hesitation. And I need you to hear that before you go, because if you're going down into that mountain carrying the idea that you're settling a debt — you're not. There's no debt. There never was. There's just me, choosing you, over and over, and the only thing you can do about it is choose me back or walk away. Those are the options. Choose."

### Orsen

The room went very quiet. Somewhere below, Ash Street murmured its night noises — a cart, a dog, a warden's whistle two streets over — and none of it mattered, because Sera Vane had just handed me the truth like a lamp and told me to carry it or drop it.

Choose me back or walk away.

Every clever thing I owned deserted me at once. I stood there in my funeral boots with my packed bag and my stolen ledger and understood, with total clarity, that I had built this entire expedition on a foundation of cowardice dressed as duty. I hadn't been going to settle a debt. I'd been going because a debt was safer than a choice. Debts end. Choices don't. A debt repaid lets you walk away clean, and some cowardly corner of me had planned, all along, to walk away clean — to buy my freedom from her with a rescue and never once say aloud what she'd just said aloud, bleeding, in a room that smelled of lamp oil and match-strike vials.

"I'm not walking away," I said.

"No?"

"No." I picked up her lamp and held it out to her. "Take this. It goes with me tomorrow, but tonight it goes home with you, because I'm not having you alone in that room coughing in the dark while I lie awake three streets over rehearsing bravery." My voice was doing something unprofessional. I let it. "And I'm coming back. Not to make us even. To make us impossible to separate. Your words. I'm stealing them. Add it to my tab."

She took the lamp. Our fingers overlapped on the handle and stayed there a moment longer than handling required.

"If you die down there," she said, "I will find whatever passes for a bureaucracy in that court and file so much paperwork against you that you'll be doing penance for centuries."

"That's the most romantic threat anyone's ever made me."

"I practice."

She left with the lamp, and I watched from the window — unshuttered now, a coward's architecture be damned — until her light bobbed away down Ash Street, steady as anything, lighting the lamps behind her one by one like she was stitching the dark closed.

Then I turned back to my bag, and that was when I saw it.

Under the table, where it hadn't been an hour ago. A folded square of cheap paper, sealed with a smear of tallow instead of wax — the mark of someone sealing in a hurry, or sealing something they didn't want traced. I broke it open.

One line, in a handwriting I almost recognized and couldn't place:

*They know the date too. The tip about your charms didn't come from Pell. Check who signed the warden's warrant — and don't trust the one who taught you the roads.*

Ellery. Someone was telling me not to trust Ellery, on the eve of the descent, in handwriting that felt like a hand I'd held before.

Outside, far off toward the mountain, a bell rang once — though the Temple of Nine Bells never rings a single toll, and everyone in Vharen knows what a single toll means.

Someone below had opened a door.
