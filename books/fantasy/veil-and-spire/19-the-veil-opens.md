## 19. The Veil Opens

There is a particular quality to the hour before you do something unforgivably stupid. The air goes thin. Sounds arrive from very far away, as though the world has already begun withdrawing its investment in you. I had felt it once before, the night I signed my first debt contract at seventeen, and I felt it now, standing in the undercroft of the Temple of Nine Bells with a forged name sewn into my collar and a dead woman's lamp in my hand.

Not a dead woman. A dying one. I was going to have to stop letting my mind wander ahead like that.

"You're fidgeting," Ellery said.

"I'm adjusting my cuffs."

"Your cuffs don't have buttons."

"Then I'm adjusting their absence." I looked down at the lamp — Sera's lamp, the one she'd carried up and down Ash Street for six years, its brass handle worn bright where her thumb had polished it ten thousand times. It wasn't lit. Below, I'd been told, it wouldn't need to be. "Tell me again why I'm bringing this."

"Because names open doors," Ellery said, "but objects open *memories*. The court deals in what things remember. That lamp has been held by hands that loved someone. Down there, that's rarer than gold." He paused, and his voice did the thing it did when he was trying not to sound afraid. "Also, because she asked you to take it. And below, a request freely given carries weight. More weight than any of us would like."

Sera didn't know I was here tonight. That sentence sat in my chest like a swallowed stone. She thought I'd come to my senses. She thought I'd looked at the prophecy, looked at the odds, and chosen the sensible cowardice of a man who owed money rather than miracles. In fairness, that was usually my signature move. Tonight I was breaking pattern, and breaking patterns, Dagny had warned me, is how people die.

Dagny. She was somewhere above me now, positioned at the surface gate with Bren on lookout, waiting for a signal that would either come or not come, and if it didn't come she had orders — *my* orders, which still felt absurd — to seal the veil and let me rot below rather than follow. She'd agreed to those orders with the flat expression of a woman who intended to ignore every single one of them, and I had decided, in the interest of morale, to pretend I believed her.

The undercroft smelled of dust and old candle smoke and, beneath both, something mineral and cold, like the inside of a sealed tomb. Which, I suppose, is exactly what it was. Nine Bells had been built on top of something older than bells, older than temples, older than the city's long and hypocritical war against magic. The founders hadn't outlawed the old roads. They'd just put a building over them and hoped nobody checked the cellar.

I had checked the cellar. Repeatedly, these past nine days. Mostly while vomiting.

"The ledger," Ellery said, and I patted the flat shape bound against my ribs beneath my coat. Stolen from his own archive, technically — he'd insisted I steal it properly, with witnesses and a broken lock, so that when the tribunal came asking, his guilt would look like incompetence rather than conspiracy. There was a certain grim artistry to a man arranging his own condemnation. "You understand what it is?"

"A record of the court's own debts," I said. "Every bargain they've struck above, every soul they hold as collateral, entered in their own hand. Dagny says no institution survives being read aloud."

"Dagny," Ellery said, "has a talent for aphorisms and a deficit of self-preservation. But yes." He turned to the far wall, where nine alcoves stood in a semicircle, eight of them bricked shut, and I felt my stomach perform a slow, anticipatory roll. The ninth alcove was open. Behind it, instead of stone, there was nothing. Not darkness — darkness is something. This was an absence with edges, a doorway-shaped hole in the world, and standing near it made my teeth ache the way a struck bell hums after the sound should have stopped.

Nine days of training had not prepared me for the sight of it. Nine days of walking shallow veils between here and there, of learning to hold my name behind my teeth like a coin bitten between them, of nearly dying twice — none of it had prepared me for the real thing, which did not shimmer or ripple or behave in any way a respectable portal ought to. It simply *wasn't*, patiently, in the shape of an archway.

"How does it feel," I asked, "opening a door you've kept locked for thirty years?"

Ellery's hands stilled on the old iron ring. For a moment he looked every year of his age and several he'd been saving for later.

"It feels," he said, "like confessing. Only slower, and with more consequences." He pulled. The iron ring turned with a sound like a knuckle cracking in a cathedral, and the absence in the ninth alcove deepened — I can't describe it better than that; it went from *not-there* to *thoroughly not-there* — and a draft came through it that smelled of wet stone and old iron and, faintly, unbearably, of candle wax. Of lamplight. Of home, somehow, though no home I'd ever lived in.

"The road will test you before the court does," Ellery said. "It takes payment as you go. Small things, at first. You'll be tempted to pay with big ones, because the small ones seem worthless. Don't. The small ones are all you'll have left at the end, and you will need every scrap of yourself you can carry." He pressed something into my hand: a plain iron token, stamped with a bell. "For the first toll. After that you're on your own credit, such as it is."

"My credit is terrible."

"I know. I've read your file." Almost a smile. Then it died, and he gripped my shoulder with both hands, and for one unguarded moment Master Ellery, traitor-archivist, keeper of thirty years of shameful bargains, looked at me the way a man looks at a fire he started in a house full of children. "Orsen. When you stand in front of the King — and you will, he'll want to see the thief himself — he will offer you something. He always offers. It will be exactly what you most want, wrapped in terms you can live with. Remember that everything below is a bargain, and every bargain has a second page."

"What's on the second page?"

"The part where it isn't your bargain anymore."

I nodded as though I understood. I understood nothing. Understanding is for afterward; beforehand there is only the doing.

I took out the paper one last time — the forged letter of introduction, written in a hand I'd spent four days perfecting, bearing a name I'd built from the bones of three dead clerks in the archive registries. *Corven Hale, factor of debts, agent to the estate of Vessin Marn.* The court respected paperwork. That was the whole obscene joke of the place: souls auctioned, ledgers kept, receipts issued. Hell with excellent bookkeeping. If I was going to walk into it, I would walk in dressed as one of its own.

"Corven Hale," I said aloud, testing it, and felt the small wrongness of it settle over me like a coat cut for another man. Names have power below. I could only pray this one had enough.

"And your true name?"

"Held behind my teeth." I touched my tongue to the back of them, where I'd learned to keep it, that private syllable that was mine and no one else's. "Bitten down hard."

"Good." Ellery stepped back. The draft from the not-door stirred his grey hair. Somewhere overhead, muffled by nine floors of temple, a bell began to toll the midnight hour, and I thought: *somewhere up there, Sera is asleep, breathing a little less easily every month, and doesn't know that tonight her debt comes due or never.*

I thought about her laugh, which was more cough than music lately but still managed both. I thought about stolen bread on Ash Street, torn in half without discussion because sharing it had stopped being a decision years ago. I thought about fifteen years. Three gone already. Twelve remaining, ticking down in her blood like sand through a cracked hourglass, and every grain of it poured out for a friend who hadn't even known he was being bought.

The anger came, as it always came, clean and clarifying. Good. Anger travels well underground.

"If I don't come back," I said, "burn the ledger copy you kept. Don't be noble about it. Noble gets people hanged."

"And if you do come back?"

"Then I'll expect my deposit back on the iron token."

He laughed — short, startled, genuine — and I decided that was a fine last sound to carry with me, and stepped toward the ninth alcove, and the absence reached out and took the measure of me.

Crossing a veil, for the record, feels like being read. Not seen — *read*, slowly, by something with all the time in the world and no interest in skipping to the good parts. It went through my pockets and found the lamp and lingered there, puzzled, pleased. It went through my coat and found the ledger and made a sound I heard with my spine rather than my ears, a low note of appetite. It went through my mouth looking for my name and found only Corven Hale, Corven Hale, Corven Hale, repeated like a prayer, like a lie told often enough to develop calluses.

And then it let go, and I was standing on a road.

The road ran down. Of course it did. Everything below runs down. It was paved with stones that might once have been headstones, or might once have been thresholds, worn smooth by feet whose owners had long since been spent elsewhere. On either side, the dark was not empty. Things drifted in it the way dust drifts in a shaft of light — a child's shoe, turning slowly; a ribbon of some song, audible only when I wasn't listening for it; a smell of rain on a street I almost recognized. Forgotten things, shed on the way down by everyone who'd ever made this walk, and the road wore them like moss.

Behind me, impossibly, the archway still hung in the air, a pale rectangle showing the undercroft, showing Ellery's face, small and still. He raised one hand. I raised mine. Then the rectangle narrowed, and folded, and was gone, and the sound it made closing was the sound of a book shutting.

Point of no return. People say that phrase like it's a cliff edge, sudden and dramatic. It isn't. It's quiet. It's the moment you notice the door is gone and realize it was gone some time ago, and all your panic accomplishes is using up breath you may want later.

I breathed anyway. Once, twice. Lit nothing — the lamp stayed dark at my belt, patient, trusting. Ahead, the road descended into a gloom that glowed faintly from no source I could name, and far off — miles, or inches; distance below is a rumor — I saw lights. Many lights, arranged in tiers, descending into the mountain's roots like a chandelier drowned in a well.

The court. The auction hall. Where a piece of Sera sat in a glass case, flickering, waiting for a buyer who had never come because no one had ever bid, because who bids on fifteen years of a lamplighter? Who would want them?

*I would,* I thought, and the road seemed to lean closer to listen, and I remembered too late what Ellery had said about objects opening memories, and realized the road had just opened mine.

Fine. Let it look. Let it see a scribe with bad credit and worse intentions, carrying a lamp and a grudge and a name that wasn't his. Let it report me to whatever passes for customs down here.

Let it tell the King the thief is coming.

I settled the ledger against my ribs, bit my borrowed name down tight, and started walking.

Somewhere far below, I would have sworn on my mother's grave — the grave I'd paid for in installments — that something ancient and patient smiled, and turned a page.
