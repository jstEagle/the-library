## 20. Roads Beneath the World

The first thing I learned about the roads beneath the world is that they smell like a library that has been rained on.

I don't know what I expected. Fire and brimstone, maybe, or the cold mineral breath of the mountain itself. Instead: old paper, wet stone, and something sweeter underneath it all, like the ghost of a candle burned in a room no one has entered for a hundred years. Ellery had told me — in his infuriating way of telling me everything except the things I actually needed to know — that the undervaults were built from what the world forgot. Apparently the world forgets a great deal, and apparently most of it smells like mildew.

"Keep your hand on the thread," Dagny had said at the veil's mouth, pressing a length of gray silk into my palm. It was thin as spider's work and strong as anchor chain, and it hummed against my skin like a fly trapped under glass. "Don't let go for anything. Not for voices. Not for faces. *Especially* not for faces."

"What faces?"

"The ones that know yours," she said, and pushed me through.

* * *

The veil closed behind me with a sound like a book snapping shut, and I stood alone on a road that should not have existed, lit by a sky that wasn't one.

I say "sky" because there was light above me and distance above the light, but nothing up there was doing the job honestly. It was the color of the inside of a closed eyelid, that deep bruised red you see when the sun hits your face through the skin, and it moved. Slowly. The way water moves when something very large passes beneath it.

The road itself was paved in slate, each flagstone etched with writing so worn I could only catch fragments as I walked. *...her name was...* said one stone. *...the summer we...* said another. *...please, if anyone finds...* said a third, and I stepped over that one quickly and did not look back.

The thread pulled gently leftward at a fork where three roads met, and I followed, and that was when I came to the first river.

It was not water. I want to be clear about that, because my eyes insisted otherwise for a good ten seconds. It moved like water, caught light like water, lapped at its banks like water — but the banks were made of stacked letters, thousands upon thousands of them, loose type from some infinite printing house, and the river flowing between them was made of *things*. Small things. A child's wooden horse turned over and over in the current. A wedding ring, then another, then a whole glittering shoal of them. A single brown boot. Letters tied in ribbon. A tooth. A key to a door that no longer existed anywhere above.

Forgotten things, drifting downriver toward wherever forgotten things go.

I crouched at the bank — carefully; the ledger strapped under my coat seemed to disapprove of the motion — and watched a paper boat sail past with a name written on its sail in a child's careful hand. The name was already fading as I watched, the ink lifting off the paper like steam.

"Don't touch the river," said a voice behind me.

I did not scream. I want that recorded somewhere. I made a sound, yes, a brief and dignified noise, and I came around with Sera's lamp raised before me like a weapon, which it was not, being unlit and made of tin and sentiment. But I did not scream.

The toll-keeper was sitting on a stool I would have sworn had not been there a moment ago. He looked like every ferryman in every story ever told badly: gray cloak, gray face, eyes the color of coins left too long in water. But he was knitting. That was the detail that broke the archetype. His long gray fingers worked two bone needles with the brisk competence of a grandmother, and what he was knitting appeared to be a scarf made of names, each one appearing briefly in the wool before being swallowed by the next row.

"Toll?" he said, without looking up.

"I don't have any coin."

"Coin." He said the word the way you'd say a curse in polite company. "No. Not coin." He pointed one needle at the bridge ahead — and there *was* a bridge now, arched over the river of forgotten things, made of the same lettered slate as the road. "The road remembers how you got here. The bridge wants to know what you'll give up to keep going. That's the toll. One memory. Small ones only, this far down. The big ones are for deeper bridges." He resumed knitting. "Choose quick. The river's patient, but I'm not."

"How do you take a memory out of someone's head?"

"Same way you take a splinter out of a foot," he said. "Carefully, and it bleeds a little, and afterward you keep feeling the place where it was."

I looked at the bridge. I looked back the way I'd come, at the seam in the air where the veil had been, which was now just air, smug and sealed. Somewhere below me, past more bridges than I cared to count, a lamp-flame flickered in a glass case, and a girl who had sold fifteen years of herself for my sake was coughing her life out on Ash Street.

"All right," I said. "How do I choose? What makes a memory small?"

"You'll know," he said. "Everyone knows. They just wish they didn't."

He was right, damn him. I sat down on the bank — the letters shifted uncomfortably under me, rearranging themselves so that I was briefly sitting on the word *no* — and I went through myself like a man pawning his possessions, holding each memory up to the light and asking, *can I live without this?*

My mother's voice. No. Absolutely not. Next.

The taste of the bread Sera stole for us our first winter, still warm through her coat. No. Next.

The way rain smelled on the temple steps the day I finished my apprenticeship. I hesitated over that one. It was a good memory, but it was a *small* one — a moment of weather, an afternoon's satisfaction, nothing load-bearing. If a man is a house, this was not a wall. It was a window, and windows can be bricked over.

"That one," I said, and hated how easily the words came.

The toll-keeper set down his knitting. He took my chin in fingers dry as moth wings and looked into my eyes, and I felt him *sort*, the way a clerk sorts papers, quick and impersonal and thorough. Then he found it. I felt him find it. There was a tug behind my eyes, a papery rustle, and the afternoon came loose.

I remembered that there had been a day. I remembered that it had rained, because I could see the fact of it written somewhere in me, the way you can read that a page once held ink from the shadow it left. But the rain itself — the smell of it on hot stone, the sound of it on the temple roof while Master Ellery signed my indenture papers, the way the light went green through the courtyard trees — gone. Lifted clean out. And in its place, a small soreness, exactly like the place where a splinter was.

"There," he said, and held up — I swear this — a small glass bead, in which a green light swam. Rain-light. My rain. He dropped it in his pocket, next to what I now realized were hundreds of others, each one glowing faintly with somebody's stolen afternoon. "Bridge is paid. Cross. Don't look down, and don't drink anything the river offers. It offers."

I crossed. The bridge held. On the far side I stopped and checked myself the way you check your pockets after a crowd, counting what remained. Mother's voice: present. Sera's laugh: present. The exact weight of a well-made pen in the hand: present. The rain: a hole shaped like rain.

It should have been worse, losing it. It was bad. But it was also — and this is the part I'm ashamed of — manageable. The memory had been real, and it was gone, and the world had not ended, and I was still walking. Which meant grief, I discovered somewhere over that first bridge, is a currency after all. It can be spent. And a man who learns his grief is spendable learns something about himself that he cannot unlearn.

* * *

The second bridge was longer, and the toll-keeper there was a woman who kept bees.

That's not a metaphor. Her hives lined the bridge rails, dozens of them, and the bees flying in and out of them were not bees. They were small bright motes, and if you watched closely — I did not watch closely for long — each one carried a scrap of something: a few notes of a song, the warmth of a specific hand, the color of a dress worn once to a wedding forty years ago. Honeybees of the forgotten, making honey out of other people's lost time.

"Small memory," she said, before I'd even reached the middle of the bridge. She didn't look up from her hive-work. "One per crossing. You've done this before."

"Once."

"It shows. First-timers cry. Second-timers bargain." She finally glanced at me, and her eyes were kind, which was somehow worse. "Third-timers stop choosing carefully. Don't be a third-timer. It's how they get people like you to arrive at the court with nothing left worth stealing."

"I'm not—" I started, and stopped, because lying to a beekeeper of memories seemed unwise. "I'm going to steal something back."

"Mm," she said, in the tone of a woman who had heard that before, many times, across many centuries, and could report on the survival rate.

I chose faster this time, which frightened me. The summer I was nine, when my father took me to the harbor and let me name every ship. Gone. A bead of harbor-light into her apron pocket. The bridge let me pass, and I noticed — I couldn't help noticing — that the crossing cost less than the first. Not in pain. In resistance. Some part of me, some clerkish part that tallies debts, had begun entering these losses in a ledger and marking them *paid*, and paid things stop weighing on you.

That's the trap. I understood it by the third bridge, and understanding it didn't save me. The third keeper was a child — or wore a child, or was wearing the idea of a child, which down here might be the same thing — sitting cross-legged in the middle of the span, playing jacks with little glass beads that glowed.

"One," said the child.

I gave it the memory of my grandmother's kitchen, the smell of cardamom, and barely paused. The fourth bridge wanted the name of my first teacher. I gave it. The fifth wanted the color of the blanket I was swaddled in as an infant — a thing I hadn't known I knew until it was lifted out of me, and I felt the strangest vertigo, mourning a color I could no longer name or picture, aware only that a drawer in me had been emptied and that the drawer was still there, open, waiting to be filled with something else.

By the sixth bridge I had learned to do it walking. By the seventh, the keepers had stopped seeming strange. That was the moment I made myself sit down on the far bank, take out the stolen ledger, and write, in the margin, in my own hand, so that some future version of me would have to read it:

*They are not taking your memories. You are giving them. Note the difference. Note it every time.*

Because that was the design of it. I saw it plainly now, the way you see the design of a debt contract only after you've signed. The roads beneath the world weren't built to stop travelers. They were built to *lighten* them. Every bridge took a little more of what made me Orsen Ravensworth — the hoarder of small warm moments, the man who kept Sera's lamp trimmed though she never asked, who remembered the birthdays of clients he disliked — and by the time a traveler reached the Gate of Names, he arrived pre-hollowed. Easier to bargain with. Easier to rename. A soul arrives at that court carrying exactly as much of itself as the roads have allowed.

Ellery had mentioned none of this. In his defense, he'd nearly killed me teaching the first veil lesson, so perhaps the curriculum was simply crowded.

I counted what was left. It was still, thank whatever gods keep books down here, a great deal. Mother's voice. Sera — all of Sera, every piece, guarded like the last coin in a collapsing vault. Ten years of mornings. The whole architecture of my grudges, which I was surprised and slightly embarrassed to find intact and load-bearing. I had paid in weather and kitchens and harbors, in the small change of a life, and I told myself that was strategy.

I told myself that twice. The second time, I heard how it sounded.

* * *

The eighth bridge was different, and I knew it was different before I reached it, because the river beside it had changed too.

The rivers of forgotten things had been running alongside me since the start, sometimes near, sometimes far, always murmuring that soft papery rush. But here the water — the current, the flow, whatever it was — ran slow and deep and dark, and the things in it were not small anymore. A cradle went by, rocking on a swell. An entire door, standing upright, drifting. A man's coat, buttoned, empty, floating with the dignity of something that had once mattered enormously.

And the bridge — the bridge was not slate. It was wood, old wood, scarred by fire, and it groaned when I stepped on it, and halfway across I understood why: the bridge was remembering. Bridges down here were made of surrendered things, and this one had been paid for in large coin. Whole years. Whole loves. I could feel them under my boots, straining.

The keeper stood at the center. For the first time, a keeper faced me rather than bent to its work, and I stopped, because the keeper was wearing my father's face.

Not my father. I knew that within a heartbeat — my father had died owing money to six different lenders, and this thing's eyes held no debts at all, only depth, only patience. But it wore him the way the child had worn childhood, comfortably, from the inside.

"Orsen Ravensworth," it said, in a voice like my father's voice played back through water. "Scribe. Debtor. Thief-to-be." It smiled with his smile. "You've paid well. Seven bridges. Seven small sorrows. You're lighter than you were."

"I'm managing."

"You are. That's the trouble." It gestured at the dark river. "You're carrying the heavy things close to the chest, scribe. Clever. But the court below weighs everything, and the gate weighs heaviest of all. So consider: pay now, at friendlier rates, or pay later, at theirs." It leaned closer, and my father's face did something my father's face had never done in life: it looked sorry for me. "There's one payment I'm authorized to ask for. Just one. The lamp."

My hand went to Sera's lamp before I could stop it. Tin, dented, bought secondhand the year we met, trimmed every week for three years by hands that were slowly selling themselves away. The lamp she'd need to relight when — if — her flame came home.

"No."

"It's only tin—"

"It's only tin," I agreed, "and the rain was only rain, and the harbor was only ships. I've learned the arithmetic down here, thank you. Everything is only itself until it's gone, and then it's the whole world." I stepped around the thing wearing my father. "The lamp comes with me."

It didn't stop me. That was the worst part. It simply turned, unhurried, and watched me walk the rest of the span, and as I passed the point where the wood groaned loudest, it said — conversationally, almost kindly:

"She sold fifteen years for you. What will you have sold by the time you reach her?"

I didn't answer. I counted my remaining memories instead, all the way to the far bank, like a miser counting coin in a dark room, and when I looked back the bridge was gone, and the river, and the keeper, and there was only road again — narrower now, descending in long switchbacks, and ahead, impossibly far down and impossibly close, a light that was not light: the red eyelid-sky opening into a vast glow, gold and cold, like lamplight seen through the wrong end of a spyglass.

The auction hall. Already burning down there, already bidding, already selling pieces of people to other pieces of people.

And between me and it, one last obstacle: a gate, visible even from here, carved into the living rock, flanked by pillars of stacked names. The Gate of Names, where I would have to surrender what I was called and hope a forgery could hold.

I touched the gray silk thread at my wrist — still humming, still anchored somewhere far above to a woman with reasons of her own. I touched the ledger under my coat. I touched the lamp.

Seven bridges' worth of holes in me, and one line of prophecy circling tonight's date like a noose.

I put my head down and walked toward the gate, doing the only arithmetic left to me: what I had spent, what I had left, and whether the difference was enough to buy back fifteen years.

From somewhere below, faint as wind through wires, the souls began to whisper. And among them — I would have sworn it, then and always — one voice said my name.
