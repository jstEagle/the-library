## 21. The Gate of Names

The gate was not a door.

I want to be clear about that, because everything I'd imagined on the road down — portcullises, iron bands, a bored guard demanding papers — turned out to be the fantasy of a man who had never seen anything older than a temple archive. What stood at the end of the last veil-road was an absence. A place where the mountain simply declined to continue. The rock of Mount Vhalen's roots curved overhead like the ribs of something sleeping, and where a gate should have been there was a seam of darkness so complete it made the tunnels behind me look like noon.

It breathed.

Not wind. Breath. The darkness swelled and shrank with a rhythm too slow for any living thing, and each exhalation carried a smell I couldn't name — old coins, maybe, or candle smoke that had been burning since before anyone thought to write down what fire was.

Dagny's voice came back to me across three days of training and one near-death lesson in veils: *The court cannot be beaten, only out-bargained. And no bargain begins until you're through the gate. So get through the gate.*

Simple advice. The kind of simple that costs blood.

I stood at the threshold with Sera's lamp wrapped in oilcloth against my chest, the stolen ledger bound flat beneath my coat, and a name in my mouth that did not belong to me.

*Corven Ashe.* That was the name Ellery had forged for me, stitched together from a debtor who died childless forty years ago and a surname common enough to be wallpaper. Dagny had drilled me in it until I dreamed in it. Corven Ashe, minor scribe, debt-bound to the Temple of Nine Bells, come below to settle what he owed the way half the desperate city settled things — by selling pieces of himself to the mountain.

It was a good lie. It was even, in its way, a true one. Every part of Corven Ashe's story was my story with the names sanded off.

The problem, as Dagny had explained while sharpening a knife she would not be carrying through the gate, was that names below were not labels. They were ledgers. Down here, your name wasn't what people called you; it was the sum total of everything you were, written out in full, and the gate could read faster than any archivist alive.

*If you flinch,* she said, *if you hesitate, if some buried piece of you answers when they call Orsen Ravensworth — the gate will know. And the gate does not forgive. It amends.*

I didn't ask what *amends* meant. Some words you can hear the weight of without a definition.

The darkness breathed again, longer this time, and I felt it pass over me like a hand going through my pockets. Not searching. *Reading.* Somewhere behind my ribs, a sensation like a quill scratching parchment — except the parchment was me.

Then a voice. Not from the dark. From everywhere the dark was.

**"Name."**

One word. It didn't echo so much as *settle*, the way dust settles after a bell has been rung. It was neither male nor female nor particularly interested in me, which somehow made it worse. A customs official at the edge of forever, asking for my papers.

I wet my lips. My mouth had gone dry enough to strike sparks.

"Corven Ashe," I said.

The word hung in the air between us, and I watched it hang — actually watched, because below the mountain, spoken names have weight the way stones have weight above, and this one trembled there like a coin balanced on its edge.

The silence stretched.

Somewhere in my chest, my heart was doing arithmetic it didn't like. Because here is the thing about a forged name: it isn't false, exactly. Ellery hadn't invented Corven Ashe from nothing. He'd taken a real dead man's records, a real dead man's debts, and grafted them onto me. The name was a lie wearing a true man's clothes, and I could only pray the gate cared more about the clothes than the body inside them.

The darkness leaned closer. I felt it lean, the way you feel someone reading over your shoulder.

**"Corven Ashe,"** the voice repeated, tasting it. **"Scribe. Debtor. Sold to the Temple of Nine Bells at age eleven. Debt outstanding: four hundred marks, six years' interest, compounded."**

"Yes," I said, and my voice only shook a little, which I counted as a professional triumph.

**"Mother: Maren Ashe, laundress, deceased. Father: unknown. One sister—"**

"There's no sister," I said.

Too fast. Gods, too fast. The pause that followed lasted exactly long enough for me to remember that Corven Ashe *did* have a sister, that Ellery had mentioned her in passing two days ago — *a sister, sold to the court herself, that's why the debts mounted* — and that I had been so busy memorizing ledger figures and veil-roads that the detail had slid off me like rain off oiled cloth.

The darkness made a sound. Not a word. Something lower, older — the sound a book makes when a page turns.

**"The record shows a sister,"** it said. **"Liesse Ashe. Sold thirteen years past. You do not remember her?"**

And here is where I should have frozen. Here is where a smarter man might have realized he was caught and started planning his retreat along the nine days of road he'd bled to learn. But terror does strange arithmetic, and somewhere in the wreckage of my fear, a different instinct surfaced — the same instinct that let me sell luck-charms to widows with a straight face, the same instinct that had kept me one step ahead of the wardens for six years.

Grief. I gave it grief.

"I don't like to speak of her," I said quietly. "That's why I'm here."

Silence. The breath of the dark went still.

"That's why I came below," I went on, and found, to my horror, that the words had stopped being performance somewhere around *speak of her*. "A man sells what he has. I have years left. I'd spend them to buy back what this family lost. If the court will take the trade."

Every word of it was a lie built on a truth, and the truth was Sera — Sera coughing blood into her sleeve on Ash Street and calling it pipe smoke, Sera trading fifteen years of her soul to erase *my* debt while I slept twelve feet away, ignorant and grateful and fed. I hadn't needed to act the grieving debtor. I only had to stop acting everything else.

The darkness considered me.

I have stood before tribunal wardens with contraband in my coat. I have lied to Master Ellery's face about missing folios. I once told a widow her charm would protect her son at sea, knowing it was painted tin, and slept fine afterward, mostly. None of it — none of it — compared to standing at the mouth of that dark and waiting to learn whether I existed.

Then the breath resumed. Slower. Almost satisfied.

**"Corven Ashe,"** the voice said, and this time the name settled instead of trembling, sinking into the stone like a coin into soft earth. **"Entered. The debt stands. The court will weigh what you carry."**

The seam of darkness opened.

I don't mean it swung or parted. I mean it *admitted* me, the way a sentence admits a word, and suddenly I was walking forward into a blackness that had texture and temperature and a faint, far-off sound that took me several steps to identify.

Bells. Bells rung backwards — the sound arriving *after* its echo, wrongness folded into every note. And under the bells, another sound, thinner, woven through them like thread through cloth:

Voices. Hundreds of them. Thousands. Whispering.

Souls. I was hearing souls the way you hear wind through wires, and every hair on my body stood up and informed me, unanimously, that we did not belong here.

I kept walking. One foot. Then the other. That was the whole of my strategy now, and honestly it had been the whole strategy since the temple cellar: keep walking until the walking becomes a story you can survive.

Light bloomed ahead — not torchlight, not lamplight, but a glow like the memory of daylight, amber and dim, rising from below. The tunnel widened. The ceiling lifted into vaulted dark. And then the road ended, and I stepped out onto a gallery of black stone, and I saw the court beneath the mountain for the first time.

I had prepared myself for horror. Dagny had described it. Ellery had described it. I had read the seized accounts in the archive, the ones written by men who'd gone below sane and come back otherwise.

None of it helped.

The hall was vast beyond reason — a cavern the size of the temple district, ribbed with columns of living rock that had been carved, over centuries, into shapes that were almost architecture and almost anatomy. Amber light welled up from a floor of polished obsidian, and upon that floor, arranged in tiers like an amphitheater, sat the court.

They were beautiful. That was the worst of it. I had expected monsters, and instead I saw figures that looked like what marble statues dream of becoming — tall, grave, robed in colors that had no names above ground, their faces smooth and courteous and utterly empty of anything you could call kindness. They did not fidget. They did not whisper. They sat in perfect stillness, hundreds of them, watching the center of the floor the way the devout watch an altar.

And in the center of the floor, on a dais of glass and bone-white stone, stood a case.

No — cases. Dozens of them. Rows of slender vitrines, each lit from within, each holding a small flame that moved the way flames move when they are *thinking*. Some burned steady gold. Some guttered, thin and blue, like candles running low. Some were barely embers at all, huddled in the bottom of their glass like animals hoping not to be seen.

Souls. For sale. Arranged by quality, the way a fishmonger arranges the morning catch.

An auctioneer stood among them — I use the word loosely; it was shaped like a person the way the gate's voice was shaped like speech — and it was speaking, and though I was a hundred yards away and elevated, I heard every word as clearly as if it knelt beside me.

*"—a steady flame, gentlemen of the court, thirty-one years held, lightly used, the seller reports no weeping—"* a ripple of something that was not quite laughter passed through the tiers — *"shall we open at two hundred years of service?"*

Bids came. Not shouted. *Offered.* Each member of the court who wished to bid simply extended a hand, and from each extended hand rose a small light — a sliver of their own substance, I understood with a lurch, years of their own existence, offered as currency. The auctioneer weighed them, compared them, and the lights sank back into their owners as the bidding climbed.

They paid in themselves. Everyone below paid in themselves. It was elegant. It was obscene. It was, I realized with a cold settling in my stomach, exactly the kind of system a temple full of accountants would build if you removed every last scrap of conscience and kept the double-entry bookkeeping.

My hands were shaking. I made them stop, because a man named Corven Ashe, a debtor come to sell his years, would not stand on a public gallery trembling like a thief.

Which raised the question of where, precisely, a man named Corven Ashe was supposed to *be*.

The answer arrived before I finished asking it. A figure detached itself from the shadows at the gallery's edge — a servant, or something serving the shape of one, gray-robed and faceless in the way that things which have given up their faces are faceless. It inclined its head with terrible courtesy.

"The gate registers your arrival, Corven Ashe," it said. Its voice was dry paper. "Debtors awaiting valuation are received in the Antechamber of Weighing. You will follow. You will touch nothing. You will bid on nothing until your own worth is established."

Valuation. Of course. I hadn't just walked into the market. I had walked in as *stock*.

I followed the gray thing down a stair cut into the living rock, each step worn smooth by centuries of feet that had come down here the way I had — afraid, indebted, and certain the mountain was the lesser evil. Behind us, the auctioneer's voice rose and fell, and a soul that had burned gold changed owners for the price of ninety years, and nobody in that vast and beautiful hall so much as blinked.

In the antechamber, other debtors waited on benches of black stone. Living ones, like me — hollow-eyed men and women from the city above, clutching tokens and tally-sticks, each of them wearing the particular stillness of people trying not to think about what they were about to lose. I sat among them. I put my hands in my lap like theirs. I even managed, when a woman beside me asked if it was my first time, to say "yes" in a voice cracked with just the right amount of fear, and she nodded with grim fellowship and told me the taking didn't hurt, that you mostly just felt *lighter*, and thanked me when I said I was sorry.

I was sorry. That was the hell of it. Every soul in this room was Sera, or could be. Every one of them had a lamp on some street above, or a child, or a debt that started with someone else's hunger.

Through a high slit of window cut into the cavern wall, I could see the auction floor, and I swept it with my eyes under the pretense of nervous glancing — once, twice, a third time, cataloguing cases, counting guards, mapping the distance from the dais to the nearest gallery stair.

And then I found it.

Third row from the auctioneer's platform. A case slightly smaller than the others, set apart, its glass older and cloudier — as if it had been handled often, or resented. Inside it burned a flame unlike any I'd seen in that hall. The others flickered or crouched or blazed. This one *leaned*. Toward the east. Toward, I realized with a pain that went through me like a blade finding an old scar, toward home. Toward Ash Street, where a lamp would be going unlit tonight because its lamplighter was dying of a wound three years old that no one could see.

Fifteen years of Sera, burning thin in a jar, leaning toward a city that didn't know what she'd given.

Something in my chest went very quiet and very cold, and I understood, sitting on that bench among the damned and the desperate, that the fear had finally finished its work. It had burned all the way down to the bottom of me, and what was left there wasn't fear at all.

It was arithmetic. Cold, clean, and final: one thief, one ledger of the court's own sins strapped against my ribs, one inquisitor's seal in my boot, and one flame in a glass case that I would carry out of this mountain if it cost the mountain its foundations.

The gray servant reappeared at my elbow.

"Corven Ashe," it said. "The court will see you now."

Of course it would. And I smiled — a small, debtor's smile, apologetic and harmless, the smile of a man with nothing left to steal —

— and rose to meet whatever wore the mountain's crown, praying to every outlawed god that the name on my tongue would hold just a little longer.
