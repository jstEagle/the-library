## 22. The Auction of Souls

The court beneath the mountain did not look like a place where souls were sold. It looked like a bank. That was the first horror of it, and I want to be honest about that, because everything afterward was worse and I would rather start with something almost funny.

No dripping stone. No screaming. No rivers of fire — those had been on the road here, the toll-bridges, the rivers of forgotten things, but the destination itself was clean. Polished black floors that swallowed my footsteps. Columns carved from a single seam of mountain glass, so tall their tops vanished into a darkness that wasn't dark so much as *unlit*, which is a different thing entirely, the way a debt isn't a wound but becomes one. Chandeliers hung from nowhere, burning with a light that cast no shadows, which meant everyone in that hall moved through it like a drawing of themselves, flat and blameless.

I had come in under a forged name — "Corven Ash," a debtor's name stitched together from two gravestones Ellery had taught me to read — and the gate had taken it, barely, the way a moneylender takes a coin he suspects is clipped. Something behind the gatekeeper's eyes had weighed it for three seconds too long. Three seconds. I counted them against my own heartbeat and decided to live.

Now I stood at the edge of the auction hall with a stolen ledger pressed flat against my ribs beneath my coat, Sera's lamp hanging cold and unlit at my hip, and I watched a woman's soul get sold to a man wearing gloves.

* * *

The auctioneer was not a person. I want to be clear about that too, because my eyes kept insisting otherwise and my eyes are liars when they're frightened. It had the shape of a person, the courtesy of a person, a voice like a well-read uncle announcing dinner. But it never blinked, and its shadow — the one thing in that shadowless hall that had one — fell the wrong direction, toward the light instead of away.

"Lot forty-one," it said, gesturing with a hand that ended in fingers slightly too many. "A cooper's soul, thirty-one years held. Sound condition, minor wear at the edges, some weeping. Shall we begin at four hundred years?"

Four hundred years. Not coins. The currency below was time itself — years of life, decades of memory, whole lifetimes bid by things that had lifetimes to spare. A thing in the third row, robed and faceless in the way that furniture is faceless, raised one finger. Another across the aisle countered. They went back and forth with the mild competitiveness of men bidding on a horse, while above them, suspended in a globe of glass no bigger than a fist, the cooper's soul turned slowly like smoke trapped in amber.

It was blue-grey. It flickered when the bids rose, like a candle feeling a draft.

"Sold," said the auctioneer, "to the Honored Gentleman of the Ninth Stair, for six hundred and ten years. May your purchase bring you warmth."

And the globe was carried away on a little velvet cushion by a servant who also wasn't a person, and the crowd murmured its approval, and somewhere far above — I could feel it rather than hear it, a pressure behind my sternum — a cooper in Vharen who didn't know his soul had been collateral this whole time went on hammering hoops onto barrels, a little colder in the chest every winter and blaming the weather.

I had read about this. Ellery had told me. Dagny Holt had told me, in her flat inquisitor's voice: *the court cannot be beaten, only out-bargained.* Knowing a thing and standing in the room where it happens are different animals. Knowing is a map. Standing there is the territory, and the territory has teeth.

I made myself watch lot forty-two. And forty-three. Because Dagny had also told me this: *if you flinch, they'll smell it. Grief reads as weakness down here, and weakness reads as inventory.*

So I watched, and I kept my face doing what scribes' faces do best, which is nothing.

* * *

There were perhaps two hundred souls in the hall's great cases — walls of them, floor to ceiling, arranged like a library of small lights. That image should have comforted me, being a scribe, but it did the opposite. Libraries are organized by care. This was organized by value. Blue flames in one case, steady and bright, labeled with brass plates: *ARTISAN. HEALTHY. MINIMAL WEAR.* Dimmer ones in another, their plates stamped with discounts: *AGED. FRAIL. SOLD AS LOT.*

And in a smaller case near the auctioneer's dais, set apart the way jewelers set apart the good stuff, burned a flame the color of lamplight.

I knew it before I saw the plate. I knew it the way you know footsteps on your own stairs, the way you know a knock at your door at an hour when nobody kind comes calling. Fifteen years I'd spent in rooms lit by that exact shade of gold — Ash Street at dusk, Sera climbing her ladder with the lighting-pole, telling me my scowl would crack my face if I didn't feed it better jokes.

The brass plate read: **LOT SEVEN. LAMPLIGHTER'S SOUL, PORTION THEREOF (15 YRS). CONDITION: THINNING. NOTE: RESISTANT.**

*Resistant.* I stood there reading that word over and over like it was going to rearrange itself into something kinder. Resistant. Of course she was. She'd been carrying fifteen years of herself in a jar under a mountain for three years, coughing her way up and down Ash Street, and the flame had *fought*. It guttered — gods, how it guttered, thin as paper, flickering with each breath of the hall's dead air — but it leaned toward the glass whenever anyone passed, the way she leaned toward people, the way she'd leaned toward me on the step outside my rooms with half a loaf of stolen bread and that grin that cost her more than the bread ever had.

"Admiring Lot Seven?"

I hadn't heard him approach. Nobody approached; that was the trick of the place. Things simply arrived beside you already mid-conversation, as if you'd invited them.

He was old in a way that had nothing to do with years — old the way bedrock is old, old the way the phrase *before the city* is old. Silver-haired, immaculate, dressed in grey so fine it seemed embarrassed to be fabric. His smile was the smile of a man who has never once needed anything from anyone and finds the needing charming in others.

"Corven Ash," I said, giving him the forged name like a man showing a forged pass to a border guard, sweating, smiling, dying inside in an orderly fashion.

"Of course you are." He studied the case, not me. "A fine piece. One of my favorites, if I'm honest, and honesty is such a rare luxury below that I indulge in it when I can. Resistant souls keep better. They burn slower. There's a lesson in that, don't you think? About what suffering is *for*."

"I wouldn't know," I said. "I'm a buyer. Small lots only."

"Mm." He turned those eyes on me then, and they were patient the way a trap is patient. "Small lots. The humble ones always say that. The ambitious ones say it louder." He gestured, and a servant-thing brought a tray of glasses filled with something amber and steaming, though the hall was warm. He took one, offered me one. I took it because refusing felt more dangerous than drinking whatever it was. It tasted like nothing at all, which frightened me more than poison would have.

"You have the look of a man cataloguing," he said pleasantly. "I see it often. Buyers who count exits. Buyers who memorize faces." He sipped. "It's wise. Most purchases below are final, but the house respects diligence."

"The house," I repeated.

"This house." He spread one hand at the columns, the chandeliers, the walls of small captive suns. "Every debt above must settle somewhere, friend Corven. We are merely the cellar." He smiled wider. "And cellars, as any good scribe knows, keep excellent records."

My hand wanted to go to the ledger under my coat. I kept it wrapped around the useless glass instead, and thanked every god I'd stopped believing in for the scribe's face I wore.

* * *

He left eventually — or stopped being beside me, which may be the same verb down here — and I drifted toward Lot Seven the way driftwood drifts, casual as drowning.

The case was locked, naturally. Glass an inch thick, framed in iron worked with script I couldn't read but recognized the *grammar* of — the same grammar as the veil-roads, the same grammar as the toll-bridges. Law, older than law. Around the lock, faint as breath on a window, the air shimmered.

Up close, the flame was worse. From across the hall it had looked like a lamp. From here I could see it struggling — flaring and failing, flaring and failing, a heartbeat with arrhythmia, a singer running out of song. And threaded through its gold were strands of grey, like hair gone white, like ash mixed into a fire. Thinning. The plate hadn't lied.

Three years. She'd had three years left of fifteen, and I'd spent them complaining about my debts, hiding charms from wardens, letting her buy the bread because my pride was cheaper than my gratitude.

I put my palm flat against the glass.

The flame surged.

Not much. A lean, a brightening, the way a plant leans at a window. But it *turned*, all right, turned toward my hand through an inch of enchanted glass and fifteen years of sold time, and for one moment the grey threads burned gold again and the whole case glowed like Ash Street at dusk, like every evening I'd ever walked home to the sound of her whistling off-key three lamps ahead of me.

Then it guttered, exhausted, and I understood that even that small turn had cost it something it could not afford.

Something in my chest closed like a fist around a hot coal, and I let it close, and I was grateful for it, because grief is loud and fury is quiet, and quiet is what this room rewarded.

"Cold fury sharpens into focus" — Dagny had said something like that once, or maybe I'd read it in one of her confiscated manuals, inquisitor wisdom, the kind of sentence written by someone who had done terrible arithmetic and survived it. Whatever the source, I finally understood it. Anger wants to shout. Fury wants a *list*.

So I made a list, standing there with my palm still warm from the glass:

One. The case lock answered to the house's law, not force. Force was for amateurs and corpses.

Two. The house respected *diligence*. The King — and I was now nearly certain the courteous horror in grey had been exactly who Ellery's warnings said he was — had practically invited me to study the records. Every invitation is a trap, but every trap is also an invitation, if you're desperate enough to accept on your own terms.

Three. In my coat, against my ribs, I carried the court's own debt-ledger, lifted from the archive vaults during my descent, pages listing what the court itself owed — to whom, for what, since before Vharen had walls. Debts, the auctioneer had said. Everything below was debts. Which meant everything below could be *called*.

Four. Somewhere in this hall, a buyer's seal could open a case. Dagny's forged inquisitor's seal sat in my pocket next to a stub of chalk and three feet of veil-thread, and I had nine hours before the veil above reopened, minus whatever the roads took as toll.

Five. Sera Vane had sold fifteen years to erase my debt, and she had never once mentioned it, and she was up there right now coughing blood into a rag and telling me it was just the damp.

I looked at the flame until my eyes burned, and I did not blink, because blinking felt like losing.

"Hold on," I whispered, too quietly for anything in that hall to hear, I hoped. "I'm slow, but I'm thorough. You know that about me. You've complained about it for years."

The flame gave the smallest flicker. Maybe the draft. Maybe not.

Behind me, across the hall, the auctioneer's voice rose smooth as oil: *"Lot fifty-two — a scribe's soul, young, unblemished, taken in trade for—"*

And then the chandeliers dimmed, all of them at once, and every not-person in the hall turned its head — mine included, helplessly, like puppets on the same string — toward the far doors, where a servant-thing had appeared bearing a velvet cushion, and upon the cushion lay a folded card of black paper sealed with grey wax.

An invitation. I knew it the way I'd known the flame. And when the servant-thing's blank face swept the crowd and stopped — stopped — on me, on *Corven Ash*, small-lot buyer, diligent cataloguer of exits, I understood that the house had finished its own list, and my name was on it.

The card bore a single line in handwriting older than the city:

**THE KING WILL SEE THE THIEF NOW.**

* * *

I did not run. Running confirms things. I set down the empty glass on a passing tray with the care of a man at a gallery opening, I touched my coat once — ledger, seal, thread, lamp — and I walked toward the doors through a crowd of things that parted for me with the terrible courtesy of an executioner's row.

Behind me, in its case, Lot Seven flared once, hard, gold swallowing grey for a full second — resistant to the last, fighting even now, fighting *for* me, the fool, the stubborn, generous fool — and I carried that flare with me through the doors like a coal cupped in both hands, down the corridor toward the King, toward the offer I already knew was coming, toward the betrayal the prophecy had written into my hand before I ever found the page.

The doors closed behind me without a sound.

Somewhere above, nine hours ticked down to eight.
