## 24. Betrayal, As Written

The King's steward — for that is what I decided to call the thing with too many joints and a voice like a ledger being closed — led me back into the auction hall through a door I hadn't seen before, which is a trick I'd have admired under any other circumstances.

"His Radiance Beneath grants you an hour," it said. "The case stands in the east alcove. The price stands beside it."

"The price," I repeated. "You mean the exchange."

"I mean what is owed. Words are yours to choose. Debts are ours."

I wanted to tell it that was very quotable and would look lovely embroidered on a cushion, but my mouth had gone dry as old vellum, so I just nodded and walked through.

The hall had changed while I was away with the King. Or I had changed. The auction was still going — a soul being bid upon in the far ring, its light guttering each time a bidder raised a hand, like a candle flinching from breath — but now every light in the vaulted dark seemed angled toward me. The glass cases along the walls hummed faintly. Somewhere above, muffled by a hundred fathoms of mountain, I imagined I could hear Vharen's bells, and I hated myself a little for finding the thought comforting.

Sera's case stood in the east alcove, exactly where the steward said. A lamp-flame behind glass, small and stubborn, burning the way she burned: low, steady, refusing to go out on principle.

And beside it, on a pedestal of black stone, sat a brass scale the size of a dinner plate. One pan held nothing. The other pan held nothing. Between them hung a card, and on the card, in handwriting I recognized because I had copied it out illegally nine days ago in a temple archive with shaking hands:

*The thief's hand will be guided by betrayal.*

"You could have put that more subtly," I told the scale.

The scale did not answer. Scales never do. That's the whole problem with them.

* * *

Let me be precise about the arithmetic, because precision was all I had left, and because lying to yourself works best when you dress it up as bookkeeping.

To open the case, I needed to give the court a soul of equal weight. Not equal value — equal weight. The court didn't care about goodness or guilt; it cared about mass, the way a moneylender cares about coin and not about how you wept when you earned it. Dagny's seal could crack the lock. The stolen ledger could void the claim written inside it. But neither of those paid the toll at the door, and the toll was a soul, freely offered, from the thief's own hand.

Mine wouldn't do. I'd asked. The King had smiled his courteous, geological smile and explained that the prophecy required the thief to leave with the soul, and a thief who traded himself away couldn't carry anything anywhere, and besides — this was the part I liked least — "the books have plans for you yet, scribe."

So. Another soul. Freely given. From my hand.

And there was exactly one soul within reach, sitting topside in the cold with his boots on a crate of smuggled brandy, keeping watch on a door he thought led to a wine cellar, whistling through his teeth because Bren whistled when he was bored and bored was his natural state.

Bren. Who had sworn an unlawful oath in a cellar that smelled of tallow and onions. Who had carried me half a mile on his back when the first veil lesson nearly killed me, laughing the whole way, saying, "You weigh less than a bale of silk, scribe, and complain more." Who trusted me.

Who was, I reminded myself, a smuggler. A man who moved contraband past wardens for coin. A man whose soul, by any honest weighing, was not lighter than mine.

That was the first lie. I want that on the record, even if the record is only this: that was the first lie, and I knew it while I was telling it, and I told it anyway, because the alternative was a scale and a flame and a friend dying three years early, and I am not built for arithmetic that comes out that way.

* * *

I climbed back up through the veil-road with the King's hour ticking somewhere in my chest like a second heart.

The roads beneath the world don't obey distance. You walk a hundred steps and arrive where you started, or you walk ten and come out a district away, older by a memory or two. I had already paid the toll-bridges everything small and precious I could spare: my mother's voice, a summer rain on Ash Street, the smell of the ink Ellery mixed himself, which I hadn't even known I loved until it was gone. Walking them again felt like walking through a house after a fire. The shape of everything was right. The contents were wrong.

The exit veil opened onto the cellar behind Corvane's Wine Merchants, and there was Bren, exactly as advertised: feet up, hat down over his eyes, whistling something jaunty and off-key.

He heard me before he saw me — of course he did, the man could hear a warden's conscience from across a street — and pushed the hat up with one finger.

"Well?" he said. "You've got the face. Which face is it? Good news face or 'we're all going to hang' face?"

"It's the 'give me a minute' face."

"We don't got a minute. Dagny said the window closes at the third bell. She said it twice, which means she meant it four times."

I looked at him. I want to be honest about this too, since honesty is apparently the currency I'm trading in tonight: I looked at him the way you look at a purse you're deciding whether to cut.

He must have seen something, because he sat up properly and the whistling stopped.

"Orsen."

"Bren."

"What's below you. What's the price."

And here is where I should tell you I lied outright. Here is where the chapter earns its name, and I could tell you I did it smoothly, with a scribe's silver tongue, and maybe some other version of me did. But what actually happened was worse and simpler: I opened my mouth to lie, and what came out was the truth, most of it, delivered in the flat voice of a man reading someone else's inventory.

"There's a scale," I said. "The case won't open without a soul on the other pan. Equal weight. Freely given."

Bren was quiet for a moment. Down in the dark of the cellar, the veil behind me breathed — a slow, tidal sound, like the mountain sleeping.

"And you came up here," he said slowly, "to ask me."

"No," I said. "Yes. I don't know. I came up here because you were the nearest soul and I hate myself for noticing that, and I thought if I looked at your face long enough I'd find a reason not to—"

"To not what, Orsen?"

"—to need one."

He stood up. He wasn't a big man, Bren; he was quick and narrow, all wire and grin, and standing he still only came up to my chin. But right then he seemed to take up the whole cellar.

"You're asking me to die," he said. "Down there. Forever. So your girl can live."

"She's not—" I started, and stopped, because what was the point. "She sold fifteen years for me. Her soul's been thinning ever since. It's her cough, Bren. It's been her cough the whole time, and I spent three years letting her pay my debts and calling it friendship."

"That's not what I asked."

"I know what you asked."

"Do you?" He stepped closer. "Because I think you're asking me something else. I think you're asking me whether you're the kind of man who does this. And I'll save us both the trouble, scribe: you're not. That's why you came up and told me the truth instead of slipping something in my cup. That's why your hands are shaking. A man who could spend me wouldn't be standing there looking like the ledger's already written and he hates the handwriting."

"My hands aren't shaking."

They were shaking.

* * *

Here is the thing about moral freefall that nobody warns you: it doesn't feel like falling. It feels like standing perfectly still while the floor quietly relocates.

Every argument arrived pre-packaged, reasonable, weighted. Bren's soul against Sera's — except the court didn't weigh souls by worth, so really it was Bren's life against Sera's life, one for one, and wasn't one-for-one the fairest trade in any city in the world? Bren had sworn the oath knowing the risks — except he'd sworn to steal a soul, not to be one. Sera would do it for me — except that was precisely the problem, wasn't it, that was the whole rotten engine of my life, people paying my debts while I called it luck. And underneath all of it, coiled and patient, the oldest argument of all: *the prophecy says betrayal. Maybe this is simply what the words require. Maybe fate isn't asking your permission.*

Maybe fate isn't asking your permission. That one had teeth. That one could carry a man a long way down.

I caught myself composing the justification — actually composing it, in my head, in clean archival script — *the thief acted under compulsion of prophecy; the loss, though grievous, served the greater—* and I made myself stop, because I have catalogued enough seized books to know what a forgery looks like, even when the forger is me.

"Third bell's coming," Bren said.

"I know."

"So decide."

"I can't."

"You can. You just don't like either decision." He picked up his hat, turned it over in his hands. "Tell you what. Let me see her."

"What?"

"The flame. Your lamplighter's soul. Show me what I'd be buying." His grin came back, crooked and terrible. "I'm a smuggler, Orsen. I don't move goods I haven't inspected."

* * *

We went down together. I want to say I argued with him on the road, that I fought him every step, and some of that is true — I told him no eleven times, I counted — but my feet kept walking, and he kept following, and the veil-roads took us both the way water takes two leaves dropped in the same stream.

The auction hall received us without comment. The bidding in the far ring had ended; a new case stood empty where the last soul had been, and the emptiness of it made a noise I will hear for the rest of my life, whatever length that turns out to be.

At the east alcove, I lifted the lamp-flame's case — the steward had granted me that much, hands only, no opening — and tilted it toward the light so Bren could see.

Sera's soul flickered. That's all. A small gold flame in a glass box, bending slightly, the way a real flame bends when someone walks past it. When Bren leaned close, it leaned toward him. Souls do that. They lean toward warmth. Nobody had tended hers in three years and still it leaned toward a stranger because he was warm and near, and I thought: *that's her. That's exactly her. Leaning toward people. All the way down to the last of her.*

Bren looked at it a long time.

"Huh," he said finally. "She's smaller than I figured."

"They burn down," I said. "When they're sold. Piece by piece."

"Yeah." He straightened up. "My mum's flame would fill a cathedral. She'd want you to know that. Loud woman. Terrible cook. Sold nothing to nobody her whole life and proud of it." He set his hat on the pedestal next to the scale, deliberately, like a man marking his place in a book. "Right. Weigh me."

"No."

"Orsen."

"*No.*"

"Then listen." He grabbed my collar, and his grin was gone, and underneath it his face was younger than I'd ever seen it, younger than the cellar oaths and the brandy crates and the easy whistle. "You're going to stand there and refuse until the window shuts, and then we both lose, and she dies coughing on Ash Street, and you spend the rest of your life being the man who let his pride pick the ending. I've met that man. He drinks. Now — I've got a proposition, and you're going to hate it, so breathe first."

I breathed.

"I'm not giving you my soul," Bren said. "I'm lending you my place in line. There's a difference, and it matters, and I'll explain it later if there is a later. But first you're going to swear me something, on whatever you've got left that's holy, which by the look of your eyes is approximately one thing."

"Name it."

"When this is done — when she's breathing easy and the lamps on Ash Street are lit — you find my mum. Corner house on Tanner's Row, blue door, argues with the fishmonger every morning. You tell her where I went and you don't make it pretty. She hates pretty."

"Bren—"

"And you tell her the truth about how. All of it. Even the parts where you were going to do it without asking." His grip tightened. "*Especially* those parts. Swear it."

"I swear," I said, and the word came out of me like blood from a cut, and somewhere in the vaulted dark above us, I would have sworn I heard a page turning.

* * *

Later — much later, in the flight and the fire and the years after — I would try to remember whether the scale tipped before or after he stepped onto the pan. Whether he chose it fully, or whether the mountain chose for him and dressed it up as generosity, the way the court dressed everything up. Whether a freely given soul, given under the pressure of a friend's despair, is free at all.

I never found out. That question went into the undervaults with everything else.

What I remember is this: the brass pans settled level with a sound like a struck bell rung softly, once. The card between them blackened and curled, and the prophecy's line — *guided by betrayal* — burned away letter by letter, and the last thing to go was the word *betrayal*, and I stared at the space where it had been and understood, with a lurch, that the book might not consider what Bren had done a betrayal at all.

Which meant the book was waiting for a different one.

The case cracked along a seam no eye could find, Dagny's seal flaring white in my palm, the stolen ledger humming against my ribs like a trapped bird. The glass fell away in pieces that never touched the floor.

I reached in and took the flame into my bare hands, and it did not burn me, and it leaned into my fingers the way she used to lean against my shoulder on the lamplighting rounds, and I heard — or dreamed I heard — a voice like smoke say, *took you long enough.*

Then every bell in the mountain began to ring backwards, and Bren's hat was still sitting on the pedestal, and the road home was already closing.

"Run," said the darkness, in the King's courteous voice, from everywhere at once. "The court does so enjoy a chase."

I ran.
