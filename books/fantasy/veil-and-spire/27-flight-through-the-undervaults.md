## 27. Flight Through the Undervaults

I have run from wardens before. I have run from creditors, from a butcher named Hobb who caught me copying his ledger, from my own reflection on the morning after I sold my mother's ring. I considered myself, at twenty-six years of age, something of an expert in the art of running.

The Undervaults taught me otherwise.

The first thing you must understand about the vaults beneath Mount Vhalen is that they are not rooms. They are *holdings*. Every archway is a clause, every corridor a contract, and when a soul-flame leaves its case without payment, the whole architecture takes it personally. The ceiling did not simply begin to fall. It began to *foreclose*.

"Run," Dagny had said at the gate, which was, in fairness, the entire extent of her tactical advice, and then she had turned back toward the bells-rung-backwards and the thing that came with them, and I had run.

The flame sat in my cupped hands like a bird made of evening. Sera's soul — fifteen years of her, sold for three years of my debts, flickering in a glass case in a hall where souls were bid upon like heirlooms — and now here, against my sternum, warm as a cheek. It guttered once as I took the first corner, and I learned the second thing about the Undervaults: the flame did not like to be hurried.

*Noted,* I thought, sprinting. *We will discuss pacing later.*

Behind me, the auction hall's alarms were not sound so much as verdict. A tone that lived in the teeth rather than the ears, that made my fillings ache and my thoughts scatter like pigeons. And beneath it, footsteps — not the collectors themselves, not yet, but the vaults rearranging themselves around them, stone grinding against stone as corridors I would need closed themselves like fists.

I ran through a gallery of doors. That is what the Undervaults are, mostly: doors. Doors to debts unpaid, doors to promises broken, doors to every small treachery a person commits and files away. Each one had a nameplate. I did not stop to read them. One of them, I am nearly certain, bore my own name, and I have decided never to think about that again.

The floor tilted. Not dramatically — the mountain is old and patient and does nothing dramatically — but enough that my next step landed six inches lower than expected, and my knee sang a brief aria, and the flame lurched in my hands.

"No," I told it. "No, no, stay lit. Stay lit, Sera, I am begging you, and I do not beg well."

It steadied. Or I imagined it steadied. In the Undervaults the distinction felt academic.

* * *

The toll-bridge appeared where the map in my head said a corridor should be — because of course it did. The old roads beneath the world are threaded with them, bridges spun from rope and regret, and each one keeps a tollbooth manned by something that may once have been a clerk. This one had a face like wet paper and a voice like a ledger closing.

"TOLL," it said.

"I'm being chased by the entire concept of repossession," I said. "Can we waive—"

"TOLL."

Dagny had warned me about this. *The court cannot be beaten, only out-bargained,* she'd said, and *never pay with anything you can't afford to lose.* On the way down I had paid with my mother's voice and one summer rain, and I had grieved them the way you grieve a tooth — dully, constantly, touching the gap with your tongue.

But the flame was guttering again, and behind me the corridor was folding itself shut with the unhurried certainty of a book closing, and the bridge was the only way across the chasm where a river of forgotten things ran black and slow.

"What do you take?" I asked.

The paper face regarded me. Then it regarded the flame. Its expression — if wet paper can be said to express — sharpened with interest.

"Not that," I said. "Never that. Anything else."

"MEMORIES," it agreed. "SMALL ONES. THE BRIDGE IS SHORT."

Short. Right. I stood there with a dying girl's soul in my hands and tried to inventory myself quickly, the way you pat your pockets for coins. What did I have left? My mother's voice was gone. The rain was gone. I still had — I searched — the smell of the temple archive, dust and vellum and Ellery's terrible tea. The weight of Sera's shoulder against mine on Ash Street, three winters of stolen bread. The specific boredom of tallying columns. My own name, which I would surrender at no price, thank you, once had been quite enough.

"The taste of burnt bread," I said. "From her oven. The one on Ash Street. She always burned the bottom loaf and gave it to me anyway."

The clerk extended a hand like a folded receipt.

I gave it. The memory went out of me the way heat leaves a room when the door opens — no pain, just absence, arriving all at once. I knew, intellectually, that Sera had once handed me bread. I could no longer taste it. Somewhere in me a small door closed, and I felt it close, and I hated that I felt it, because grief for a thing you cannot remember is just grief wearing a blindfold.

I crossed the bridge. Behind me, the corridor finished closing with a sound like a sigh of relief.

* * *

The vaults got worse.

I want to be precise about this, because "worse" does inadequate work. The second gallery was a hall of scales — actual brass scales, tall as trees, each one weighing something invisible, and as I passed between them the weights shifted to look at me. The third was flooded ankle-deep with ink-black water that whispered names. Not my name. Other people's. Thousands of them, murmuring, the ambient sound of the court's inventory, and I waded through the middle of someone else's entire existence while the flame shivered against my chest.

It was going out. I could feel it the way you feel a fever coming on — not yet, but soon, and certainly. Fifteen years of a person, kept in glass for three years in a hall where hope goes to be appraised, and now jostled through collapsing corridors by a scribe whose entire plan beyond this point had been, verbatim, *run toward Dagny.*

"Sera," I said, and the name did something to the flame — a flare, a lean, like a plant toward light. "That's right. That's your name. Hold onto it. Names have power down here, remember? Yours especially. You sold years, not the name itself. You're still *Sera*. Be insufferable about it. Insufferable people don't die."

The flame flared again, and I choose to believe it was laughing at me.

Then the ceiling came down.

Not all of it. A section — thirty feet of vaulted stone that had held for a thousand years deciding, in the space of a breath, that it had held long enough. I heard it before I saw it, a groan like the mountain clearing its throat, and I did the arithmetic of a coward: the flame or my arms. There wasn't time for both.

I chose wrong. I chose the flame.

I threw myself over it, curled around it the way you'd curl around a candle in wind, and the ceiling came down on my shoulders and back like the hand of a god who had read my file. Stone. Weight. White noise. I heard my own bones make a sound I will not describe because I have decided, as a policy, not to describe it.

And the flame — pressed between my chest and the floor, shielded by everything I had — stayed lit.

When the dust settled enough to breathe, I discovered two things. First: I was alive, pinned under a slab the size of a dinner table, which the mountain had apparently left as a *warning* rather than a sentence, because the court does not kill thieves. Killing ends the debt. The court prefers its debtors ambulatory and suffering.

Second: my hands were burning.

Not metaphorically. The flame, trapped against my skin during the collapse, had done what fire does. The palms of both hands were seared — not deeply, the healers would later say, though *later* seemed a country I might never visit — and the pain arrived all at once, honest and enormous, and I did the only reasonable thing.

I laughed.

I lay under a collapsed ceiling in the Undervaults of Mount Vhalen, with a stolen soul flickering against my scorched chest and my hands cooking slowly in their own skin, and I laughed until it turned into something else, and then I did that too, briefly, privately, and then I stopped, because the collectors had found the rubble.

They came around the corner like a decision already made. Three of them. I had seen collectors only in Dagny's descriptions, and her descriptions had been restrained to the point of cruelty, because the truth is worse than any description: they wear the shapes of clerks, grey-robed, mild-faced, carrying ledgers, and their mildness is the horror. They looked at me pinned under stone the way a bailiff looks at furniture.

"ASSET RECOVERED," said the nearest one, opening its ledger. "THIEF DETAINED. DEBT RESUMES."

"Actually," I said, from under the rock, "I have a counteroffer."

It looked at me. Patience, ancient and bureaucratic.

"You can't take the flame," I said, "because it isn't in the case anymore, and your own law says possession pending adjudication—" I was making this up. Every word. I am a scribe; fabrication is my trade. "—and furthermore, the prophecy names me, and prophecies are binding contracts, and breach of contract in the matter of a prophesied theft carries penalties I'm sure your legal department has *opinions* about."

This was also nonsense. But bureaucracy is bureaucracy, above or below, and the collector paused. Its quill hovered. Somewhere in its ledger, a footnote was being drafted.

And in that pause — that beautiful, bureaucratic pause — the stone on my back shifted.

Not lifted. Shifted. An inch, maybe two, and cold air poured into the gap, and through the gap came a voice I had last heard offering tactical advice consisting of one word.

"Orsen." Dagny Holt, exiled inquisitor, destroyer of my illusions about heroism, sounded — and I want this on the record — *annoyed*. "You're under a rock."

"Astute."

"Can you move?"

"I can complain extensively."

"Good. Roll left. Now."

I rolled left. The slab ground another inch — she was prying it with something, a smuggler's bar, her inquisitor's seal glowing on her fist like a small furious moon — and I dragged myself out through the gap with the flame clutched against me and my ruined hands screaming a duet. The collectors turned. Dagny stepped past me into their path, seal raised, and said four words in a language that tasted of iron and old courts:

"*Inquisitur. Stet. Adjudicabo.*"

I am exiled, her posture said. I am disgraced. I am, by every law you keep, *still one of you*, and I am invoking audit rights older than your King, and you will stand there filling out forms while this thief and I walk away, because that is what procedure demands, and procedure is your god.

They stood there. Filling out forms. I have never loved bureaucracy more, and I have loved it unhealthily my whole life.

* * *

She half-carried me through three more galleries. I will spare you the details, partly for brevity and partly because my memory of them is mostly pain and the smell of my own burning, which is not a scent I recommend adding to your personal collection. At some point she wrapped my hands in strips torn from her own coat, and at some point I realized she was limping, and at some point neither of us mentioned either fact, because that is the arrangement Dagny and I had: we noticed each other's damage and agreed, silently, never to make it a topic.

The side veil was exactly where she'd said it would be — a seam in the air behind a waterfall of ink-black water, thin as a knife cut, glowing faintly the color of the hour before dawn.

"It goes up to the tannery district," she said. "Smugglers' road. Bren should be at the far door."

"And you?"

"I'm not coming through." She said it the way she said everything, flat and final, a door closing politely. "Someone holds the veil open from this side or it snaps shut on you and takes the flame with it. That's how veils work. You know that."

"That's how veils *die*," I said. "There's a difference, and you're standing on the wrong side of it."

"Orsen." She almost smiled. Exiled inquisitors should not almost smile; it does things to a man's resolve. "I didn't come down here for your friend. I told you that. I came for a flame of my own, and I know which shelf it's on, and I've spent thirty years learning these halls, and I am *very good* at paperwork." She pressed something into my bandaged hand — her inquisitor's seal, warm as a coal. "You'll need this more than I will, up there. Wardens don't argue with it. Neither do magistrates. Use it once, then bury it."

"Dagny—"

"The veil's narrowing. Go."

I went. Of course I went. But I looked back — I will always be glad I looked back — and saw her standing in the falling dark with her seal-less fist and her sister's name held somewhere behind her ribs, facing back down the corridor toward the auction hall, toward the shelves, toward thirty years of waiting finally given a door.

"Burn it all if you have to," I said.

"Oh," said Dagny Holt, in a voice I had never heard from her and have never forgotten, "I intend to."

The veil took me.

* * *

I came out through a wall of hanging hides in the tannery district, into air that smelled of lye and smoke and, God help me, *morning* — the grey beginning of it, the city's curfew hours, bells silent, streets empty. The flame in my hands had gone very low, a blue-hearted ember, and I knelt on the filthy cobbles and cupped it and breathed at it like an idiot, like a man giving mouth-to-mouth to a candle.

"Hold on," I told it. "Hold on. We're nearly — Sera. Sera, listen. It's Orsen. We're out. You're out. Just — stay."

The flame leaned toward my voice. Stayed.

Bren was not at the far door.

I told myself he was late. I told myself the curfew had delayed him. I told myself several other generous lies while I waited in the hides' shadow with my cooked hands and my borrowed courage running out like sand, and the flame dimmed another degree, and the eastern sky stayed stubbornly dark, and somewhere beneath my feet — I could feel it through the cobbles, faint as a heartbeat through a floor — the mountain began, very quietly, to shake.

Bren was not at the far door.

And the door, when I finally reached it, stood open to the empty street — and on the threshold, placed neatly where a lookout would kneel, was a single coin.

A tipster's coin. Temple-minted. The kind Pell had taken for my name.

I stood there holding a dying soul and a dead man's warning, and understood that whatever had happened at that door, it had happened without me, and the exit route I had bled for was sealed — and the mountain, beneath my feet, shook again.
