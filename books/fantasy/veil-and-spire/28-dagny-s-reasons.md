## 28. Dagny's Reasons

The bells of the court rang backwards, and I did what I had spent thirty years training myself to do: I stopped feeling and started counting.

Four vaults between the auction hall and the Gate of Names. One thief with a soul-flame cupped in his burned hands like a man carrying a candle through a hurricane. Two smugglers already scattered — Bren gone from his post at the door, which I'd noticed and filed away under *later*, because *later* was a luxury for people whose sisters were not still burning in glass cases below the world.

"Left," I said, as Orsen came pelting around the corner with all the grace of a man being chased by his own conscience. "Not right. Right goes down."

"Everything down here goes down," he gasped.

"Not everything. Left goes *sideways*. Sideways is what we want." I caught his arm and hauled him along the corridor, past shelves of confiscated names — actual names, written on lead tags, humming faintly like wasps in a jar — and shoved him through a gap in the vault wall that hadn't existed an hour ago. The court was collapsing its own architecture to slow us. Sloppy. Or desperate. With institutions, those are usually the same thing.

Behind us, the backwards bells tolled again, and the sound went through my teeth like a file.

"They're waking the collectors," I said.

"What's a collector?"

"You'll know them when you meet them. Pray you don't." I checked the corridor both ways. Empty, in the way that deep places are empty — not vacant but *holding their breath*. "Move."

* * *

I should explain something about myself, since we're apparently doing confessions now, and since Orsen kept glancing at me during the descent with the particular expression of a man who has decided his life depends on a stranger and would very much like to be told why that stranger is helping him.

My name is Dagny Holt. For nineteen years I was an inquisitor of Vharen. I enforced the magic ban. I kicked in doors on Ash Street when Ash Street still had doors worth kicking. I confiscated charms from widows and ledgers from scribes and once, God forgive me, I arrested a lamplighter for selling luck-knots, and she cried, and I wrote it up anyway, because the law was the law and I was very good at the law and very bad at everything else.

I was good at it for nineteen years. Then I found a ledger.

That's how these things always happen, isn't it? Not with thunder. With paperwork. I was auditing temple seizure records — routine, tedious, the kind of work they give inquisitors who have started asking questions — and I found thirty years of transfers. Debtor souls, quietly purchased by the Temple of Nine Bells and quietly sold down to the court beneath Mount Vhalen. Settling the city's debts. Keeping the peace. Keeping the *prices* down, if you like, though nobody up top ever saw a discount.

And in the oldest transfer book, dated forty-one years ago, in a clerk's careful hand: *Holt, Maren. Fifteen years of soul, sold to settle family debt.*

Maren. My little sister. Who had gone to the Temple at seventeen to pay off our father's ruin, who had come back thin and gray and coughing, who had died at twenty-three while I was out confiscating luck-charms from people exactly like her. They told me it was consumption. I believed them, because believing was easier than asking why a healthy girl wasted away the moment her family's debts cleared.

She hadn't been sick. She'd been *thinner*. A soul stretched over too few years, like butter over too much bread, until there wasn't enough left to hold a person together.

So no — I didn't come down here to destroy the court. Destruction is easy; I've destroyed things professionally. I came to find one specific flame. Forty-one years old. The size of a candle-end, probably. Kept in some case or jar or drawer, filed under a name only I remember saying at her funeral.

Everyone assumes inquisitors burn things. Nobody asks what we do with the ash.

* * *

Orsen stumbled again — the flame in his hands guttered, and he made a sound I recognized, low and involuntary, the sound of a man watching something he loves go out — and I grabbed him by the collar before instinct could make him stop moving.

"Don't stop. Whatever it does, don't stop."

"It's dying—"

"It's *frightened*. There's a difference, and the difference is that frightened things can be carried." I pulled him onward. His palms were blistered where he'd shielded the flame from a spray of collapsing stone, and he hadn't complained once, which annoyed me, because complaining is how you know a man is still sane. "How much farther?"

"The side veil. Ellery marked it — a seam behind the third pillar of the Undervaults."

"Ellery." He said the name like something bitter. Fair enough. The archivist had opened the way down and then gotten himself arrested above so the wardens wouldn't look for us. Cowardice and courage look identical from underneath; I've been mistaken for both.

We ran. The Undervaults are what the mountain does with things it doesn't want to think about — rivers of forgotten objects flowing in channels cut through black rock, umbrellas and letters and children's shoes drifting past in the dark water, all of it whispering. I'd walked this road twice before, on scouting descents, and both times I'd looked for her face in the flotsam. You do that. You tell yourself you're being thorough.

Ahead, the corridor narrowed toward the pillar hall, and I heard it — the sound I'd been dreading. Not footsteps. Collectors don't walk so much as *arrange themselves closer*. It sounds like a room full of people turning pages.

"Dagny." Orsen had stopped, staring back the way we'd come, where the darkness had begun to move with purpose. "They're between us and the auction hall. And the veil is—"

"Ahead. I know." I pushed him into the pillar hall. Third pillar, left face, a seam in the air like a scratch on glass. It was there. Barely. Veils close fast when the court is angry; this one was already knitting itself shut at the edges, threads of ordinary night pulling across the wound.

Through it, I could see Ash Street. Rain. Lamps. The world, stupid and precious and lit.

"Go," I said.

He stared at me. Scribes are quick in most ways but slow in the ones that matter. "You're coming."

"I'm not."

"Dagny—"

"Maren's still down there." I said it flatly, because flatness was all I had left, and I found I wanted to spend it carefully. "Forty-one years, Orsen. Her flame is in one of those cases, filed and forgotten, and the court is about to seal itself for a decade while it hunts us. If I leave now, I leave her in the dark forever. I've done that once already. I won't do it twice."

"The whole place is coming down—"

"Yes." I smiled. I felt my face do it, anyway; whether it reached my eyes is between me and whatever judges inquisitors. "Isn't it wonderful? All those cases breaking. All those flames loose in the dark. Forty years I planned to break in and steal her out like a burglar. Now the building's doing it for me. I just have to stand in the wreckage and catch her."

"That's not a plan, that's a prayer."

"I was an inquisitor for nineteen years. Plans were never my department." I took his shoulders. Under the grime and the burns and the terror, he was still just a scribe who'd learned to run fast for love of a friend, and I thought: *the city produces these after all. Every few generations. Just enough to keep going.* "Listen. When you surface — the wardens will have the temple. Ellery will be taken. Say nothing, show nothing, and get that flame to Sera before dawn, because a soul out of its case starts forgetting which body it belongs to. Can you do that?"

"Yes. But—"

"No *buts*. Buts are how men die in doorways." I turned him toward the seam and gave him a shove, gentle, the way you'd send a child into cold water. "Go. Tell your friend—" I stopped. Started again. "Tell her someone paid her debt a long time ago too. She'll understand. People like us always do."

He hesitated half a heartbeat more — long enough for me to see he meant to argue, long enough for me to be glad the bells chose that moment to toll again, closer, filling the pillar hall with that awful inverted peal — and then he stepped through the veil, and the rain-sound of Ash Street swallowed him, and the seam drew shut like a mouth deciding to keep a secret.

Gone. Good. That was the mission accomplished, technically speaking, and I have always respected technicalities. They're the load-bearing walls of every institution I ever served.

I turned to face the page-turning dark, and I rolled up my sleeves, and I went to find my sister.

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about grief given a mission: it stops hurting. That's the trap. You think the pain is the bond, so when it quiets, you panic — but it hasn't left. It's just changed trades. Grief with nothing to do sits in your chest and rots. Grief with a task becomes something else entirely. Purpose, if you're lucky. Fuel, if you're honest. I'd carried mine for forty-one years, and somewhere in the last decade it had stopped being sorrow and started being *direction*, the way a river stops being rain and starts being a current.

I walked back into the collapsing vaults of the Buried King's court with my hands empty and my heart full of arithmetic.

The auction hall was half-ruined. The great cases had cracked when the alarms began — the court's own defenses turning on its inventory, or perhaps the inventory simply refusing, after all this time, to stay shelved. Flames everywhere. Loose in the dark. Little lights drifting through the rubble like fireflies over a flooded field, each one a person, each one humming a fragment of a song only they remembered.

I knelt in the wreckage and held out my hands.

"Maren," I said. My voice cracked on the second syllable, which surprised me. Forty-one years of practice, and it still cracked. "Maren Holt. It's Dagny. I'm late."

The flames drifted. Dozens of them, curious, circling. None of them hers.

I said her name again. I said it the way our father used to, calling her in from the river at dusk — two syllables, the first rising, the second falling, a small melody worn smooth by repetition. I said it until my voice gave out, and then I said it silently, and the flames drifted closer, drawn by something, gathering around me like moths who had forgotten what they were looking for but not that they were looking.

And then — at the edge of the light, small and guttering and older than any flame had a right to be, barely bigger than a birthday candle —

A flicker. Turning. Listening.

I didn't reach for it. Reaching is how you frighten them. I stayed kneeling, hands open, and I let her come to me, and when the little flame finally settled into my cupped palms — feather-light, warm as a fever, trembling like a bird that has been caged so long it has forgotten windows exist — I bowed my head over it, and I said the only thing left to say.

"I found you. I'm sorry it took so long. Let's go home."

Somewhere above us, far away, a veil collapsed with a sound like the world clearing its throat, and I knew — without knowing how — that Orsen had made it out, and that the exit was gone, and that whatever happened next would happen down here, in the dark, between me and the mountain.

I stood up, cradling my sister against my chest, and started walking toward the deepest part of the ruins, where the King's voice still echoed, patient and ancient and suddenly, for the first time in centuries, *afraid*.

Because I had read the prophecy too, back when Ellery showed me the copy. Every line except the last.

And I had noticed what none of them had: the last page was blank.

Whatever was about to be written there, I intended to be standing in it — with a flame in my hands and forty-one years of fuel to burn.
