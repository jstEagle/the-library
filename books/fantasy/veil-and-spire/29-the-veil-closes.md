## 29. The Veil Closes

I have read accounts of drowning. Sailors' memoirs, mostly, seized and catalogued by the Temple because they mentioned charms against storms. Every one of them said the same thing: the last thing you feel is not terror. It's a kind of peace, the water closing over you like a door.

The veil closing behind me felt exactly like that, except I was the one being spat out instead of swallowed, and there was nothing peaceful about it at all.

One moment I was running — vaults collapsing behind me in slow, grinding sequence, like a god clearing his throat through a mile of stone — and Dagny was at the gate, her hand raised, holding it open with something that was either will or grief or both. She shouted something. I want to believe it was *go*. I have decided it was *go*.

Then light, then cold, then cobblestones, hard under my shoulder blades, and the taste of ash and lamp oil in my mouth, and the night sky of Vharen above me — actual sky, actual stars, smug and indifferent as tax collectors.

And silence.

Not the good kind. Not the tired-but-safe kind. The wrong kind. The kind where your own breathing sounds like an accusation.

"Dagny," I said.

Nothing answered. Behind me, where the veil had been — the space between two leaning walls in the ruined chapel off Tanner's Row, the spot Ellery had marked with chalk that no longer looked like chalk — there was only wall. Old wall. Mortar gone soft with a century of rain. If there had ever been a door there, the stones had agreed among themselves to forget it.

"Dagny!" Louder this time. My voice cracked on the second syllable, which was embarrassing, and I did not care, because embarrassment is a luxury for people whose friends are not on the other side of a closed door.

I beat on the wall with the flat of my hand. Once. Twice. On the third blow I stopped, because my palm came away wet — not blood, worse, the burn from shielding Sera's flame had split the skin across all four fingers and the heel of my hand, and the pain had been waiting politely for me to notice. Now that I had noticed, it introduced itself properly.

I sat back on the cobbles and took inventory, because inventory is what I do. It's the whole of my trade, really. Debts and inventories.

Item: one soul-flame, wrapped in oilcloth and held against my chest, warm as a sleeping bird. Sera's. Fifteen years of her, bought back with a forged name, an inquisitor's seal, and the court's own ledger turned against it. Intact. Flickering. Hers.

Item: two hands, burned, blistered, shaking. The right one would need binding. The left might scar.

Item: one stolen ledger, still tucked inside my coat, heavy as sin and worth more than every debt in Vharen combined.

Item: zero companions. Zero exits. Zero idea whether Dagny Holt had walked out of that gate or been buried under a mountain wearing her sister's hope like a coat she never got to put on.

"Go," I told the wall. "She said go."

The wall declined to confirm or deny. Walls are very good at plausible deniability. So is the Temple. I'd know; I used to work for them.

* * *

Getting home was its own small comedy.

Vharen was under curfew. I learned this the way one learns most things in this city — by nearly dying. I had made it perhaps four streets from Tanner's Row, hugging shadows, cradling my burned hands and my impossible cargo, when a warden lantern swept the intersection ahead of me and a voice said, "Curfew's been called. Anyone abroad after bell answers to the tribunal."

I pressed myself into a doorway that smelled of old fish and older regret, and while I waited for the patrol to pass, I listened to the city talk to itself. Cities do that, if you know how to hear. And Vharen was saying things I had never heard it say.

*They sealed the temple.* Whispered from a window above me, woman to man. *Wardens everywhere, chains on the archive doors.*

*Old Ellery,* said another voice, further off, half-laughing, half-afraid. *The archivist himself. They took him out in irons. Said he tore a hole in the world under the Nine Bells.*

*Hole in the—* A scoff. Then, quieter: *Did you see the mountain?*

I hadn't. I made myself look, past the rooftops, east, toward Mount Vhalen, black against the stars.

It was smoking. Not fire-smoke. Something finer, grayer, rising from cracks I could see even from here, even in the dark — as if the mountain were exhaling after holding its breath for a hundred years. As if somewhere far beneath it, a court built on ledgers and law and the patience of centuries had just discovered that its books didn't balance anymore.

Good, I thought. Let it choke.

Then, immediately, less bravely: please let Dagny be out from under it.

The patrol passed. I moved. Street by street, doorway by doorway, I carried fifteen years of a lamplighter's soul through a city that didn't know yet what I'd done, or what it had cost, or what was going to come walking up the old roads looking for it. I passed a lamp on Cordwainer Lane — unlit, because Sera wasn't there to light it, because she was dying in a room above the chandler's shop while I carried the missing piece of her through the dark like the world's most guilty courier — and I hated that lamp, briefly and completely, for burning so easily when everything else was so hard.

* * *

Sera's window was dark. Of course it was. It was past midnight, and she was ill, and ill people sleep, or lie awake pretending to.

I stood in the alley below her window for a long time before I knocked. This is a thing about me I am not proud of: I rehearse. Give me a difficult conversation and I will draft it three times in my head, strike the weak lines, polish the strong ones, and then deliver none of it, because the person across from me says something I didn't plan for and I'm a scribe, not an actor.

What do you say? *Hello, I've been to hell, here are your years, sorry about the burns, also everyone we know may be arrested by morning?* There's no elegant draft of that. Believe me. I tried.

In the end I climbed the outside stair the way I had a hundred times, on the nights when bread was shared and debts were teased about and neither of us knew — *neither of us knew*, and the not-knowing sat in my chest now like a coal — that she had paid for all of it with pieces of herself.

My knock woke her instantly. Lamplighters sleep lightly; a life spent listening for gutters and gas-leaks teaches you that.

"Orsen?" Her voice through the door, rough with sleep and something else, something thinner than it had been three weeks ago. "It's past bell. If the wardens are after you, I know nothing, I've seen nothing, and you were never born."

"They're not after me." My voice came out strange. Smaller than I intended. "Can I come in?"

A pause. Then the bolt, and the door, and Sera in her nightdress with a shawl dragged over her shoulders, squinting at me by the light of one candle, and I watched her face do three things in quick succession: relief, alarm, and the particular fury of a person who has just realized she is about to be worried.

"What happened to your hands?"

"I'll explain."

"You'll sit down first." She steered me to her one chair with the brisk authority of someone who has spent years telling drunks and dreamers to get home safe, and went for her kit — clean cloth, the waxy salve she used on cracked lamp-glass, which smelled of pine and was, I suspected, technically theft from the city works, and which I had never once reported. Priorities.

She unwrapped the oilcloth from my hands without asking what was inside it. I let her. Some things you don't hand over in an alley. Some things you wait until there's a candle lit and a chair to sit in and someone who will understand what they're holding, or at least hate you properly for it.

She worked in silence for a while. Salve, cloth, wrap. Her cough surfaced once, twice, and she strangled it both times, and I pretended not to notice, and we were both lying, and it was almost like old times.

"You look terrible," she said finally.

"I've been underground."

"That explains the dirt. Not the face." She tied off the bandage on my right hand, gently, her fingers cool against the burn. "Orsen. Look at me."

I looked at her. Candlelight, gray eyes, the hollows under her cheekbones that I had been pretending not to see since autumn. Fifteen years of her soul, warm against my knee in a bundle of oilcloth, and her sitting right there, alive, furious, mine — friend-wise, I mean, though the distinction felt suddenly academic.

"I got it back," I said.

Her hands went still.

"The court," I said. "Under the mountain. Three years ago you sold them fifteen years of yourself to clear my debt. I know. I've known for nine days, and I should have told you, and I didn't, because—" I gestured with my bandaged hand, which hurt, which felt correct. "Because I'm a coward with excellent penmanship. But I went down. Tonight. And I stole it back. All of it. It's yours."

Silence. The candle flame leaned in the draft from the window and straightened itself, dutiful, doing its job, being a small light in a large dark, the way she'd taught a thousand lamps to do.

"You went under the mountain," she said at last, very evenly, in the voice she used on men who whistled at her from doorways. "Alone."

"Not entirely alone. There was an exiled inquisitor, two smugglers, and a traitorous archivist. It's a long story and several of us are probably dead."

"And your hands?"

"I held a soul-flame while running through a collapsing vault. It runs hot."

Something moved through her face then — too fast to name, too big to hold. She looked down at the oilcloth bundle at my knee, and her breath caught, and I watched her understand, the way you watch frost form: slowly, then all at once.

"That's—"

"Yes."

"That's *mine*?"

"Fifteen years," I said. "Plus interest. The court charges interest. I didn't pay it. I'm given to understand they'll be cross."

She laughed. It broke in the middle and became something else, and then she was crying, quietly, furiously, the way she did everything — as if emotion were a chore to be gotten through efficiently — and I did the only reasonable thing, which was to put my bandaged arms around my oldest friend and hold on while fifteen years settled back into their owner.

For a while, neither of us spoke. Below us, Ash Street slept under curfew, lamps cold, wardens' boots echoing somewhere far off. Above us, Mount Vhalen smoked against the stars. Somewhere between those two facts, Ellery sat in a cell, and Dagny Holt was either free or stone, and a book in a sealed archive was doing something I wouldn't learn about until tomorrow.

"You shouldn't have," Sera said into my shoulder. "You absolute idiot. You shouldn't have."

"I know."

"I'd have done it again, you know. I don't regret it. I'd sell twenty years."

"I know that too." I pulled back and looked at her. "That's why I didn't ask."

She wiped her eyes with the heel of her hand, glaring at me through the wreckage. "What happens now? They'll come for it. Whatever's down there — they don't strike debts, Orsen. Everyone knows that. That's the whole *point* of them."

"I know."

"And Ellery's arrested. The whole city's talking about it. If they question him—"

"He won't give you up." I said it with more certainty than I felt. "He's spent thirty years making bargains to keep this city safe. He'll spend whatever he has left doing it. Even from a cell. *Especially* from a cell — it's the first position he's ever held where honesty costs him nothing."

"And you?" She took my wrapped hands in hers, careful of the burns, and her grip was stronger than it had any right to be. "What happens to you?"

I considered lying. It would have been easy. It's always easy; that's the trouble with lies, they're cheap as charcoal.

"Now," I said, "now the interesting part starts. Because the prophecy—the book, the one that started all this—it named tonight. Named me. Said the thief returns with a soul and a betrayal." I flexed my bandaged fingers. "I returned with a soul. No betrayal. Bren walked away rather than be spent, Dagny stayed at the gate, and I cheated the line entirely. Which means the prophecy is wrong, Sera. For the first time in a hundred years, the words and the world disagree."

She frowned. "Books don't just *disagree*."

"This one might. This one's been circling dates and naming names for a century, and I intend to find out what it does when it loses." I stood, and my knees informed me of their opinions regarding stairs, mountains, and running. "Tomorrow. When the curfew lifts. There's a book in the archive with my name in it, and I want to know what it's writing now."

"Orsen—"

"It's late," I said, "and you've just reacquired fifteen years, and I have a ledger to hide and hands to ruin further. Sleep. Light your lamp in the morning. All of them. Make the whole street angry with brightness."

At the door she caught my sleeve. "Orsen. Thank you doesn't cover it. Nothing covers it. But—" She stopped, jaw working, a woman who had spent three years paying silently for a man who never knew, and who now owed a different debt entirely and hated owing. "Come back tomorrow. That's not a request. Come back, and we'll figure out what fools do next."

"Fools," I said, "figure out everything. It's the wise ones who assume the story's already written."

I went down the outside stair into the curfewed dark, carrying a stolen ledger and a pair of ruined hands and the strangest feeling of my life: victory with a hole in the middle of it, shaped exactly like a woman with an inquisitor's seal and a sister's flame, standing at a gate that no longer existed.

Behind me, in her window, a candle flickered — and steadied, and burned a little brighter, as if something fifteen years deep had just remembered how.

And far off, so faint I could have imagined it, the mountain exhaled again.

Tomorrow, the book. Tomorrow, whatever it had written in its anger. But tonight — tonight I walked home through a sleeping city with a debt paid, a friend saved, and the growing, vertiginous suspicion that the hardest line of the prophecy was still ahead of me, and it was blank.
