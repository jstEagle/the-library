## 32. The Court's Answer

Let me tell you something about theft that no thief has ever understood.

Thieves believe that stealing is an event. A moment of risk, a flash of triumph, a door closed behind them and the prize clutched to the chest. They think the story ends when they get away. They write ballads about it. They name their children after it.

But in my court — in the court beneath Mount Vhalen, where every debt is written and every debt is collected — theft is not an event. Theft is a *balance*. And balances, unlike events, do not end. They wait. They accrue interest. They sit in the dark with the patience of stone and the appetite of water finding its way through a mountain's roots.

The boy had taken a flame from my auction hall. He had broken a case older than his city, rung my alarms like a blasphemy, and fled up through the undervaults trailing smoke and burned skin and the particular stink of mortal panic.

And I had smiled.

I want that understood before anything else, because the wardens above will tell you otherwise. They will say the King raged. They will say the mountain shook. The mountain did shake — mountains are dramatic creatures, all granite and grievance — but I did not rage. Rage is for beings who fear loss. I have been losing things for nine hundred years and gaining them back for nine hundred years, and somewhere in the middle of all that arithmetic I stopped being capable of surprise.

A soul was stolen from me. Very well. Souls come home. That is what souls are *for*.

* * *

I convened the court at the third bell of the deep morning — we keep bells down here too, though ours ring in frequencies the living feel only as unease, a prickle at the nape, a dream half-remembered. The auction hall filled the way it always fills: not with people arriving but with people *remembering they were already here*. My clerks took their benches. My assessors unrolled their ledgers. The ushers of the old roads stood along the walls in their grey livery, faces like closed doors.

"Item," said First Clerk Vess, who has served me since before her city had a name and who speaks with the cadence of a woman reading a shopping list at her own funeral. "One flame, human origin, fifteen years' principal plus accrued interest, removed from Case One Hundred Eleven by unauthorized party. Status: outstanding."

"Outstanding," the hall repeated, because repetition is law down here. Everything must be said twice or it isn't true.

"Circumstances," said Vess. "Theft by deception. False name presented at the Gate of Names. Forged seal of the Inquisition employed. Ledger of court debts taken." She paused, and I watched her pause, because Vess does not pause; she *allocates* silence, the way a miser allocates coin. "Also taken: one lamp. Brass. Ordinary."

"The lamp is not evidence," said Assessor Crane, whose jurisdiction is objects and who resents anything sentimental entering his ledger. "The lamp is irrelevant."

"The lamp was carried into this court by the thief," Vess said. "Therefore the lamp entered the record. Therefore the lamp is relevant. Everything that enters the record is relevant forever."

Ah, Vess. Nine centuries and she still delights me. Somewhere above, her descendants were probably arguing about inheritance over a dead man's table, and here she was, arguing about a lamplighter's brass lamp with the gravity of a war council. Bureaucracy is the only immortality I have ever envied.

"Findings?" I asked.

Vess consulted a page that had not existed a moment before and would not exist a moment after. "Theft confirmed. Debt re-established. Remedy available under Statute Nine: retrieval by collectors."

"Remedy approved," I said, and the hall said it after me, twice, and the case was closed in less time than it takes a baker above to decide his bread is done.

You see how it works? No shouting. No thunder. The court beneath the mountain does not forgive theft, but neither does it punish it, not exactly. Punishment implies anger, and anger implies that something unexpected happened. Nothing unexpected ever happens here. There is only the ledger, and the ledger's remedy, and the remedy walking up the old roads on its own patient feet.

* * *

Now. About the collectors.

Above, in Vharen, they tell stories about the collectors, and like most stories told by the living about the dead's business, they get the important parts wrong. They imagine hooded figures, scythes, wailing. Theater. The collectors wear whatever the occasion requires — a clerk's coat, a grandmother's shawl, a child's face if the debt demands tenderness — but their true shape is simpler than any costume. Their true shape is *arithmetic*. Each collector is a sum looking for its remainder.

They do not take blood. They do not take breath. What they take is finer than either: memory, obligation, warmth, the small unrecorded wealth of a life. If you sheltered the thief, you hold something of his, and what you hold of his can be levied against what he holds of mine. It is elegant. It is fair, in the way that glaciers are fair. And it requires no force whatsoever, because a man who wakes with fewer memories does not report a crime. He reports a Tuesday.

Statute Nine authorized six collectors. I authorized twelve. When Vess raised her eyebrow at me — she has one eyebrow, technically, the other having been traded away in 1319 for reasons she declines to discuss — I said, "Redundancy."

"You are being sentimental," she said.

"I am being *thorough*," I said, which is what sentimental people say when they have had nine centuries to practice the substitution.

But I will confess something here, in the privacy of my own skull, which is the only confessional I trust: I was curious. The boy had done something the prophecy did not predict. Do you understand how rare that is? I built my court on the bones of fulfilled prophecy — every bargain sealed, every line come true, the whole architecture of my power resting on the certainty that what is written is what happens. And this scribe, this ink-stained little debtor, had walked out of my hall carrying a flame and carrying *no betrayal*, and the final line of the century-old book had snapped like dry thread.

When a prophecy fails, everything downstream of it becomes negotiable. Including, potentially, me.

So yes. Twelve collectors. Redundancy. Sentiment. Call it what you like.

* * *

The old roads run from my undervaults up through the mountain's roots and surface in Vharen wherever the city has forgotten to look — a cellar that remembers being deeper, a stair that goes down farther than the building above justifies, a well nobody has drawn from since the plague years. The collectors went up them singly, spaced a night apart, so that the city would not feel the tremor of a march but only, night after night, the soft closing of one more door.

I felt each of them pass my threshold. You develop a sense for it, after a while. Like a landlord feeling rent leave the building.

The first surfaced beneath the Temple of Nine Bells, naturally. Debts pool around temples the way rats pool around granaries. It put on the face of a junior archivist and began its work among the stacks, and by dawn three clerks who had never met Orsen Ravensworth could no longer remember why they had once covered for him.

The second came up under Ash Street itself, into the lamplighter's district, and I confess I gave that one particular instructions. Not cruelty — the court does not deal in cruelty; cruelty is inefficient, it wastes the material. Precision. I wanted the street to remember the boy fondly and then to remember that fondness was expensive. Grief is a fine solvent, but shame is better. Shame makes people hand you things.

The third, fourth, fifth, sixth went up through the merchant quarter, the warden barracks, the widow's row along the canal. The remaining six I held in reserve, sleeping in the stairwells between worlds, because a good administrator always keeps reserves, and because I had begun to suspect — the way one suspects weather, without evidence, in the bones — that the boy was not finished.

He had taken a flame he could not keep and exposed a trade he could not survive exposing, and now the Temple's tribunal was eating its own tail in public, and the exiled inquisitor was gone — swallowed by my gate, her sister's flame still locked in Vault Seven where it had sat for thirty years, patient as everything else down here — and the archivist Ellery sat in a warden cell reciting his confession like a rosary. From my vantage, the board looked settled.

But the book in his archive was rewriting itself. I could feel it scratching away up there, faint as a mouse in a wall. Prophecies do that when they are denied. They get *opinionated*.

* * *

On the fourth night of the collection, Vess brought me a report I had not requested, which meant she considered it important, which meant I considered it alarming.

"The flame," she said. "Case One Hundred Eleven. The lamplighter's fifteen years."

"What of it?"

"Its account has been marked paid."

I set down my pen. I have a pen. Yes, kings use pens; swords are for people with shorter ledgers. "Paid by whom?"

"That is the irregularity." Vess laid the page on my desk, and I read it, and I read it again, and the second reading did not improve my temper, which is to say it did not disturb my temper, which is the same thing in a being of my vintage.

The entry read: *Received in full satisfaction of all claims against the flame of Sera of Ash Street: the memories of Orsen Ravensworth pertaining to Sera of Ash Street. Tendered freely. Witnessed.*

Freely. Tendered *freely*. Do you see? The boy had not waited for my collectors to strip him. He had opened his own veins, in the only currency I truly trade in, and paid the debt himself — ahead of schedule, at par, no interest negotiated. Somewhere above, while my collectors were climbing the stairs of his neighbors, he had knelt down in whatever poor light he had left and let them take her out of him. Every lamplit evening. Every stolen loaf. Three years of friendship, rendered up like taxes.

"He bought her back twice," Vess said. "Once with theft. Once with payment."

"And the second purchase is the binding one," I said. "Free tender extinguishes claim. The flame is legally hers. Ours is—" I searched for the word, and hated it. "—remedied."

Vess studied me with her single eyebrow. "You sound disappointed."

"I sound *accurate*," I said. "There is a difference, and the difference is the entire reason courts exist." But I turned the page over, and there on the back, in handwriting I did not recognize — not the boy's careful scribe-hand, not Vess's clerk-hand, not mine — was a single line:

*He will weep at lamplight, and not know why, and it will be enough.*

The book. The damned book had written itself into my ledger. It was leaving notes now. It was *corresponding*.

"Vess," I said. "Recall nothing. Accelerate everything. If the debt on the flame is dead, then the collectors' warrant shifts to the next outstanding balance — the false name at my gate, the forged seal, the stolen ledger, the broken case. The boy still owes me a great deal. And the city above still owes me more."

"How much more?"

"All of it," I said. "Every debt in Vharen comes due through my court eventually. It is time the city remembered that. Wake the reserves. All six."

She bowed and went, and I sat alone in my hall with the bells ringing their unease through the dark, and I looked at the strange handwriting on the back of the page for a long time.

Nine hundred years, and I had never once seen the future refuse to cooperate. It was, I admit, almost interesting.

Almost.

Then I stamped the warrant, and far above, in the sleeping city of Vharen, six more doors quietly learned how to open.
