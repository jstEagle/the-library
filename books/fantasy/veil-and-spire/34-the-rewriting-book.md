## 34. The Rewriting Book

The book had been sulking for three days.

I don't know how else to describe it. A century-old prophecy that rewrites itself in furious, cramped script when reality fails to cooperate with it — you learn its moods the way you learn the moods of a cat or a creditor. And for three days, since my tribunal performance had turned Master Pell from a respected scribe into a cautionary tale, *The Veiled Thief* had sat in my lap like a thing offended.

Its last line still read: *the thief returns with a soul and a betrayal.*

No betrayal had occurred. I'd checked. Repeatedly. I had committed no betrayal of any kind — a personal first, if I'm honest, and one I intended to put on my headstone. The smugglers were all alive. Sera was alive and insufferably healthy about it. Dagny was missing beneath a mountain, which was not a betrayal but a grief, and grief is a different animal entirely.

So the prophecy sat there, wrong, and every morning when I opened it, the ink around that final line had darkened another shade, as though the book were bruising.

"You're staring at it again," Sera said from the doorway of my rooms above the chandler's shop. She had taken to letting herself in with the key she'd never returned, on the theory that a man who'd once stolen her soul out of hell couldn't be trusted to eat regularly.

"I'm working."

"You're glaring at a book."

"It's a very bad book."

"It's the book that ruined your life," she said, setting down bread and cheese she absolutely could not afford and pretending otherwise. "You should throw it in the river."

"The river would probably throw it back." I turned a page with a pencil stub, careful not to touch the vellum with bare skin. Dagny's parting advice — shouted through a closing veil, so take it with the appropriate grain of salt — had been: *never let it learn your hands.* Whatever that meant. I'd decided it meant gloves.

Sera leaned over my shoulder. Her hair smelled of lamp oil and the cheap soap from the temple baths, where she'd been bathing like a woman making up for lost years, because she was. Fifteen years sold and returned; you'd bathe too.

"Has it changed?"

"Not since Tuesday. It's sulking."

"Books don't sulk."

"This one does. Look at the margins. It's been writing little notes to itself." I tilted the page toward the window light. In the margin beside the failed final line, in handwriting that was almost but not quite the same as the body text, someone — something — had added: *inconsistent. recalculate.* And below that, smaller, angrier: *the hand was guided. the hand erred. how did the hand err?*

"It's trying to work out what went wrong," I said. "Like an accountant who can't make the ledger balance."

"And what's the discrepancy?"

I tapped the page. "Me."

* * *

Here is what I had learned in the weeks since the tribunal, in the hours between dodging collectors' whispers and pretending to resume a normal life: prophecies are not visions. They are contracts. And like all contracts drafted by institutions — temples, courts, banks, the mountain — they are written by people who assume they will be the ones holding the pen at the end.

Ellery had told me once, in the archive, in his dry archivist's voice: "A prophecy isn't a prediction, boy. It's a schedule. The court doesn't see the future. It writes the future down early, and then makes sure everyone reads it."

Which meant the book wasn't magic in the way charms are magic, wild and half-feral. It was magic the way ledgers are magic — the terrible, binding kind, where words become debts and debts become chains. The court beneath Mount Vhalen ran on fulfilled prophecy the way a mill runs on water. Every line that came true was a gear turning. The King's power — whatever remained of it now, after the vaults burned — had been built on a hundred years of this book being *right*.

And I had made it wrong.

Not by heroism. Not even, really, by choice. Bren had walked away from the door before I could spend him. Dagny had stayed behind at the gate before I could bargain with her. The court had handed me betrayal after betrayal like coins, expecting me to spend them, and fate itself had apparently gotten bored of waiting and spent them for me — wrongly, mercifully, in directions no one had scheduled.

So the book was stuck. A contract with a clause that would not close.

I am a scribe. It took me embarrassingly long to think like one about it.

* * *

On the fourth night, Sera fell asleep in my chair — she claimed it was because my chair was better than hers, which was true, since mine had all four legs — and I sat on the floor with the book open across my knees and finally, properly, read it.

Not the prophecy. The *book*.

There's a difference. A prophecy is the text. A book is an object, with a binding and a history and, if you're lucky, a colophon. I'd been reading the words for weeks, hunting meanings, chasing the way the ink crawled. I hadn't once looked at the thing as a manufactured article.

It was bound in grey leather gone soft as cloth at the corners. The spine had been resewn at least twice — I could see the old stitch holes under the new thread, and the new thread was temple linen, fifty years old at most, while the pages were older than the city charter. Someone had repaired it. Someone had kept it alive.

And here was the thing that stopped my breath: the signatures weren't uniform.

A book's signatures — the folded gatherings of pages sewn together — follow the text. If a scribe planned his quires properly, each gathering holds a neat multiple of leaves, and the text breaks at sensible places. This book's gatherings broke at *nonsense*. Mid-sentence, mid-verse, mid-line. As if the text had been written first, endlessly, and the gatherings assembled afterward to fit whatever length the words demanded.

Or — and my stomach dropped through the floorboards of my ordinary skepticism — as if the text kept growing, and the binders kept catching up.

I counted. Twelve gatherings. Eleven of them full, dense, the prophecy proper: the thief, the descent, the court, the king, the betrayal, the return. All of it up to and including the line that refused to come true.

The twelfth gathering held four leaves.

Three were blank.

One was blank too — but not the way the others were blank. The other pages were blank the way unwritten paper is blank: receptive, waiting, indifferent. This page was blank the way a held breath is blank. When I passed my gloved finger an inch above its surface, the vellum warmed. When I took the glove off — against every piece of advice I'd ever been given, including my own — the warmth became a pulse, faint and steady, like something sleeping just under the skin of the world.

The last page of the prophecy was not merely unfilled.

It was unfinished. There's a distinction that matters more than any I learned in nine years of copying deeds and debt instruments. Unfilled means nothing was ever meant to go there. Unfinished means the writing stopped — deliberately, provisionally — with the intention of resuming.

I sat very still on the cold floorboards while Sera snored softly in my chair, and I thought about contracts again.

Every contract has a signature block. Every deed, every debt instrument, every bargain the Temple ever fed me to copy had that empty space at the bottom: *signed and sealed in the presence of.* The whole document exists to funnel you toward that space. Everything above the line is argument. Everything below it is fact.

The court wrote a hundred years of prophecy. They wrote the thief, the theft, the betrayal, the return. They wrote me into existence as a function — a hand, guided, per Ellery's planting of the book where I'd find it. They assumed the ending the way creditors assume payment: not because it's guaranteed, but because it always has been.

But they left the signature block blank.

Because the ending can't be written by the institution. That's the flaw in their beautiful machine. A prophecy of free will — a prophecy whose entire engine is a man *choosing* betrayal — cannot dictate the choice. It can only arrange the table, stack the deck, salt the bread, and wait. The final line requires a hand acting freely. Their whole system runs on the fiction that when the moment comes, the hand will do what it was designed to do.

They never imagined the hand would pick up a pen instead.

* * *

"Sera." My voice came out strange. She woke the way she always did — instantly, completely, a lamplighter's reflexes — and had a lit taper in her hand before her eyes finished opening, which is either admirable or alarming depending on how much you trust her.

"What. What happened. Is it the book."

"The book has a last page."

"It has lots of last pages. Books generally end."

"No. I mean—" I held it up. "The prophecy ends at the betrayal line. Everything after that, everything the court built the mountain on, everything the King drew his power from — it's all been *assumed*. Extrapolated. But the actual text stops. There's a page left. One page. Blank. Waiting."

She looked at me. Then at the book. Then at me again, with the particular expression she reserved for moments when I was being dense, which historically numbered in the thousands.

"So finish it," she said.

"Just like that."

"Why not just like that? You're a scribe. It's a book. You've been complaining for weeks that it keeps rewriting itself and getting it wrong." She set the taper down and crouched in front of me, and her voice gentled into the tone she used for explaining lamps to children. "Orsen. Who taught you that endings belong to whoever started the story?"

"The Temple. The court. Every debt instrument I've ever copied. The whole architecture of Vharen runs on the principle that whoever holds the ledger decides what you owe."

"And how's that been going for everybody?"

I laughed — a short, cracked sound, surprised out of me. "Badly. Lately. For everyone involved."

"Then write your own line." She said it simply, the way she said most true things, and then ruined it by adding: "Although if you write 'and they all lived happily,' I'm going to point out that Dagny is still underground and the collectors are still eating people's memories, so maybe aim slightly higher than happily."

* * *

But it wasn't that simple. Of course it wasn't. Nothing in Vharen is ever that simple; simplicity is a luxury we import at ruinous cost.

Because there was the question of ink.

I tried ordinary ink that first night. Iron gall, good stock, the same I used for everything. The page drank it in and gave back nothing — worse than nothing, the words faded even as I formed them, letters dissolving like sugar in rain until the vellum sat there pristine and smug. I tried charcoal. Charcoal wouldn't mark it at all; the line simply didn't happen, my stylus skating across a surface that declined to exist for the purpose.

I tried blood on a whim at dawn, having cut myself on the book's brass corner-catches — the book, I suspected, had done that on purpose, and I've decided to be flattered rather than disturbed.

One drop on my fingertip, touched to the page.

The vellum *drank*. Not absorbed — drank, eagerly, the way the toll-bridges under the world had drunk my mother's voice and a summer rain. And where the drop landed, a single letter rose faintly to the surface, unbidden, unformed, a ghost of an *O* that might have been the start of anything.

My name. Or the start of it. The page asking, politely, hungrily: *who speaks?*

I closed the book and did not sleep for the rest of that day.

Blood-ink. Of course. The old roads ran on memory; the veils ran on names; and prophecy — real prophecy, the binding kind — ran on the only currency that can't be counterfeited, borrowed, or sold by anyone else. The court had built its machine on other people's choices. The only thing that could overwrite it was a choice paid for at full price, in the writer's own substance.

Dagny would have known. Dagny, who'd spent thirty years studying the court's law from the outside, hunting it with an inquisitor's patience. Dagny, who was somewhere beneath the mountain holding a flame thirty years waited-for, if she was anywhere at all.

I thought about her sister. I thought about the vaults burning gold-white when the freed souls rose. I thought about the King's face in that private audience — courteous, patient, ancient — and the condition he'd named, the price he'd taken, the hole in my chest shaped like a friend that I only knew was a hole because everyone else kept looking at me like I'd lost something enormous.

He'd taken my memories of Sera. That was his condition, freely accepted. And the court's claim on me had fractured mid-sentence when I wrote the truth over their lie — I'd felt it go, felt the whole edifice shudder — because their power was built on prophecy fulfilled, and I had made fulfillment impossible.

But the book remained. The machine remained, wounded, sulking, recalculating. And somewhere in the deep roads, whatever was left of the court was doing exactly what the margin notes said: *recalculate. recalculate.* Looking for a new ending to impose, a new thief, a new betrayal, a new schedule. Books like this don't die when you break one line. They get revised.

Unless someone got to the revision first.

* * *

Sera found me at dusk, sitting where she'd left me, the book closed under my hands like a lid on a well.

"You've got your thinking face on," she said. "The one where you look constipated by philosophy."

"I know how to end it."

Her teasing stopped. She sat down across from me, cross-legged on the floor, lamp-lighter's eyes steady in the low light. "Tell me."

"The last page takes blood-ink. Only blood-ink. Whoever writes on it writes the ending — actually writes it, makes it true, the way the court made their lines true. It's the signature block, Sera. A hundred years of prophecy funneling toward one blank page, and the court assumed they'd hold the pen through some puppet thief fulfilling their design." I turned the book over, showed her the resewn spine, the mismatched gatherings. "But the page doesn't care whose blood it is. It just asks *who speaks*. The court never accounted for the possibility that the thief might answer."

"And what would you write?"

"That's the question, isn't it." I looked at my hands — the burn-scarred ones, the ones that had shielded a soul-flame through collapsing vaults, the ones that had signed a surrender I couldn't remember signing. "Whatever goes on that page becomes the rule. Not just for me. Prophecies are schedules, remember? If I write an ending, it becomes the shape the future tries to fit. Write it small and I save myself. Write it large and—"

"—and you could end the trade," she said quietly. "For good. No more souls sold to settle the debts of the living. No more fifteen-year girls coughing their lives away on Ash Street while the Temple tallies interest."

We sat with that. Somewhere below us the chandler was banking his fires, and the smell of tallow drifted up through the floorboards, ordinary and warm, the smell of a city that didn't know how close it had come to being a feeding trough forever.

"There's a cost," I said. "There's always a cost. Blood-ink means the words are paid for. I don't know yet what a whole ending costs — a line cost me everything I remembered of you. An ending might—"

"Then we find out before you write it," Sera said, and there it was, the thing I'd been afraid to hope for: *we*. She reached over and put her hand flat on the book's cover, and the leather warmed under her palm, and the book — the sulking, furious, century-old machine of fate — went quiet as a held breath. "You don't get to martyr yourself alone anymore. That's the new rule. Consider it written."

I smiled despite everything. "You can't just say things and make them true."

"Watch me," said the woman who'd sold fifteen years of her soul to erase my debts and lied to wardens' faces without blinking. "I've got practice."

I opened the book to the last page. The blankness pulsed, patient, hungry, waiting for a hand. In the margin, the angry little script had added one fresh note sometime in the past hour, in ink darker than any I'd seen it use:

*the page will accept one author. choose before the court chooses for you.*

Below the mountains, I had no doubt, something ancient had picked up its own pen.

I sharpened my knife.
