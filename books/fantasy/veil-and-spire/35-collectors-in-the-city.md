## 35. Collectors in the City

### Orsen

The first collector came for the widow Marta, and I learned about it from a bread queue.

That is how catastrophe arrives in Vharen — not with trumpets, but with gossip, passed along a line of people waiting for day-old loaves like a coin too hot to hold. *Did you hear about the widow on Coppersmith Row? Stood up at her loom yesterday and couldn't remember her son's face. Had to ask him his own name. He's eight.*

I dropped my bread. Someone behind me stepped on it. I let them.

Marta had bought one luck-charm from me. One. A knuckle of river-stone wrapped in copper wire, worth perhaps four coppers and a blessing. She'd hidden it in her flour jar because her boy was sickly and she was frightened, and being frightened in Vharen is a crime you pay for in installments.

But that wasn't why the collectors came for her. The wardens' tribunal had gone through Pell's papers after his arrest — my doing, my ledger, my righteous little bonfire of justice — and among the wreckage they found names. Not the Temple's names. Mine. And beneath mine, in a clerk's tidy hand, everyone who had ever sheltered me, bought from me, or stood between me and a locked door.

The court beneath the mountain does not forgive theft. I knew that. What I hadn't understood — what no one had thought to tell me, though in fairness I hadn't asked — was that the court also keeps accounts receivable. Every kindness done to the thief was, in its ledgers, an accessory to the theft. And the court collects in the only currency it has ever valued.

Memories.

### Sera

I have lit lamps on Ash Street for eleven years, and I know every flicker on this road the way a mother knows breathing. So when old Tammer's lamp guttered at dusk and stayed down — when I climbed his ladder myself and found the wick trimmed perfect and the oil full and the flame simply *unwilling* — I knew it wasn't the lamp.

It was Tammer. He'd hidden Orsen in his cellar for two nights back in autumn, when the wardens were sniffing around the charm trade. Fed him soup. Complained about his snoring. Now Tammer sat on his step looking at the sunset like a man reading a letter written in a language he used to speak.

"What did it take?" I asked him.

He frowned at me, patient, the way you're patient with a stranger. "Took what, miss?"

"From your head."

He thought about it. That was the worst part — watching him think, reaching for something that should have been right there. "I had a wife," he said slowly. "I think I had a wife. There's a shape where she ought to be. Like a coat hook with nothing on it." He touched his temple, gently, as if the absence might bruise. "Her name started with a soft sound. M. Or N. It'll come back."

It would not come back. I knew that, and he didn't, and there are mercies in ignorance that I envied him even as my chest cracked open over them.

By the end of the week there were nine of them on Ash Street alone. Nine neighbors who had fed him, lied for him, bought his contraband charms against their better judgment because he always knocked twice and always paid his debts. Nine holes in the world shaped like small kindnesses.

And the city did what cities do. It did arithmetic.

I heard it at the well, in the tone people used before they caught themselves. *All this for one scribe.* *My cousin lost twenty years off his wedding day because he once sold the man a candle-snuffer.* *Why should we all pay for his soul-thieving?* Nobody said *turn him in* yet. Nobody had to. You could hear it coming the way you hear weather — a pressure change, a hush, dogs going quiet.

I went to find Orsen.

* * *

### Orsen

Sera found me in the archive, because Sera always finds me, and because I had stopped bothering to hide. There seemed little point. Hiding is a skill that requires other people to be indifferent to you, and I had spent three weeks making myself the most interesting man in Vharen.

"They took Tammer's wife," she said, without greeting. "Out of his head. And the Ferrows' daughter can't remember learning to walk. And Widow Marta's boy —" Her voice snagged on something and she pushed past it. "Do you understand what's happening out there?"

"I understand it's my fault."

"Don't do that."

"Do what?"

"That thing where you agree with the worst version so nobody can hurt you with it." She sat down across from me, in Master Ellery's old chair, which still smelled of dust and cowardice. "I came to tell you we need a plan. You're sitting here writing a eulogy."

"A plan." I laughed, and it came out wrong, flat as a dropped plate. "Sera, they're collecting debts I ran up by saving you. Every person who was kind to me is a line item. The whole city is a ledger with my name at the top, and the interest compounds nightly. What plan covers that?"

"The one where you stop it."

"With what? I broke their case once with a stolen ledger and an inquisitor's seal and a dead woman's courage, and look at the result. The court doesn't send armies. It sends clerks. Patient ones." I put my head in my hands. "I keep thinking about Marta's boy. Eight years old, and his mother looked at him like a lodger."

Sera was quiet a moment. When I looked up she was watching me with an expression I couldn't read — grief and fury and something underneath both, something that kept surfacing around me lately like a fish rising toward light, and diving again before I could name it.

"You'd give yourself up," she said. It wasn't a question.

"If it stopped the collections. Yes."

"And if it doesn't? If they take you and keep collecting anyway, because the debt isn't yours alone anymore — it's the court's pride?" She leaned forward. "Orsen. They don't want payment. They want the story finished. The thief taken, the prophecy closed, the lesson taught. Walking into that hall with your hands up isn't surrender. It's delivery."

She was right, which was unbearable, so I said nothing, which she correctly interpreted as agreement.

"There's another way," she said. "Ellery."

"Ellery is in a warden cell awaiting tribunal."

"Ellery made a bargain thirty years ago that kept this city fed to the mountain in dribs and drabs instead of gulps. He knows the roads. He knows the King's terms. He knows things he hasn't told anyone because telling them would mean admitting what he is." She stood. "Get him out."

"He'll hang for opening the veil."

"He'll hang either way. At least this way he hangs useful." She paused at the door, and for a heartbeat she looked back at me with that unreadable expression again, and said, quietly, almost to herself: "You'd better come back from this. Whatever this turns out to be. Promise me nothing. Just — come back."

Then she was gone, and I sat in the dark archive surrounded by books that remembered everything, wondering why her voice sounded like something I'd already lost.

### Bren

I want it noted, for whatever record survives this mess, that I am not a good man and never claimed otherwise. I'm a smuggler. My virtues are punctuality and a strong back. But I walked away from that door under the mountain, and I've told myself it was self-preservation, and mostly I believe it.

So when the collector came for me, I nearly laughed.

Nearly.

It found me at the Tallow Lamp, mid-morning, in the form of a grey person in a grey coat who sat down across from me without asking and folded its hands like a notary. Its face was fine. Perfectly fine. That was the problem — faces should move more than that.

"Bren of the Salt Quay," it said. "You served as lookout for the thief's descent. You abandoned your post. The court notes the abandonment with interest."

"I abandoned nothing. I left. There's a difference."

"The court disagrees." It produced a slate — actual slate, chalked with figures, as if my soul were a warehouse inventory. "Your memories of the door itself are forfeit. The location, the seal-work, the hour. All of it."

"And if I say no?"

It tilted its head, considering, the way a man considers whether to swat a fly now or open a window. "Then the court takes the memories of your daughter instead."

I hadn't spoken of my daughter in six years. She lived in Corvess with her grandmother and believed I was a shipping clerk. No one in that cellar knew her name. No one except —

No. Not Pell. Pell was in irons. Which meant the court had simply *read* me, down there, the way you'd read a label. Everything I was, filed and cross-referenced while I stood guard above a hole in the world feeling sorry for myself.

"Take the door," I said. "Take all of it. Take the whole night."

It did. I watched it happen from inside my own skull — the grey person touching two fingers to my brow, and the night under the mountain folding up like a map, and the door, and the seal, and Orsen's face in the lamplight saying *watch the exit, Bren, watch the exit* — all of it rolling up smooth and gone, leaving a hole with clean edges. I remembered agreeing to the job. I remembered the oath in the cellar, the candle, Dagny Holt's voice like a blade being drawn slowly. I remembered walking home afterward, whistling.

A night with the middle cut out. A coat hook with nothing on it.

When the grey person left, I sat with my ale going warm and did the sums. If they'd come for me — a lookout, a minor accessory, a man whose chief contribution was *leaving* — then the lists were long indeed. Everyone who'd sworn that unlawful oath. Everyone who'd trained him. Everyone who'd loved him.

And one name kept surfacing in the empty space where the door used to be, attached to a memory I still had, because the court apparently considered it worthless:

*Sera. He's doing all this for Sera.*

They'd leave her till last. Of course they would. She was the prize the thief had stolen; you don't damage the prize.

Unless someone got to her first.

I finished the ale. Then I did the second-worst thing I've done that year, after abandoning a door: I went to find the lamplighter, to warn her, because warning her meant standing near her, and standing near her meant I might finally learn what it was I'd helped steal back, and why a man with no stake in it keeps dreaming about a lamp burning in a glass case.

### Sera

Bren found me at the top of my ladder on Ash Street, which is the only place I'm ever truly alone, forty feet up with a pole in my hand and the city spread out below like spilled jewelry.

"They'll come for you last," he said, shouting slightly, because Bren has never mastered the concept of a quiet word at any altitude. "You're the flame. They won't risk singeing you until the rest of the account's settled."

"Then I have time."

"You have *days*. And Orsen's sitting in an archive doing nothing, and the city's one bad night from dragging him to the wardens themselves to make the collections stop, and —" He ran out of breath, which happens to Bren roughly every fourth sentence. "Look. I don't remember most of it. They took it. But I remember enough to know he went under that mountain for you, and I remember enough to know the court doesn't lose. And I remember —" he hesitated, and for once his voice dropped, "— I remember a woman named Dagny staying behind at a gate so the rest of us could run. I don't remember her face. Just the staying."

I trimmed the last lamp and climbed down.

"So what do we do?" Bren asked.

What we did, in the end, was what nobody planned: we gathered. Not an army — a residue. Bren and me. The two smugglers who hadn't turned. Old Tammer, who couldn't remember his wife but remembered, stubbornly, that he'd once hidden a young man in his cellar and would do it again, because some decisions live deeper than memory. Even the widow Marta, who stood in Orsen's doorway with her eight-year-old son holding her skirt and said, "They took my mother's lullabies. All of them. I hum to the boy and there's nothing there." Her chin was up. "So I've decided I'm angry instead of afraid. Anger remembers better."

We were seven people in a cellar with one lamp, none of us knowing quite why we'd come, all of us knowing exactly why we'd come.

And it was there — in that cellar, with that lamp burning — that I felt it for the first time. A pulling. Low in the chest, behind the sternum, like a thread tied to something far below the floor, drawing tight. The flame in the lamp bent sideways. Toward the mountain.

Toward *me*, I realized. Or toward what had been taken from me, and was now owed back with interest, and was calling its own home.

"Sera," Bren said. "Your nose is bleeding."

I touched my lip and looked at my fingers, red as lamplight.

Down the street, at the corner where Ash meets Bellows Lane, a figure in a grey coat had stopped. It was not looking at any house. It was looking up at the lamps — at each one in turn, patiently, the way a man counts sheep he intends to buy — and then, at last, at me.

It smiled. Faces shouldn't move like that.

It raised one hand — not a threat, a *greeting*, courteous as a summons — and turned down Bellows Lane toward the temple district, where a certain disgraced archivist sat in a warden cell, full of thirty years of bargains, terms, and the King's private conditions.

Terms I suddenly needed to hear before anyone else did.

"Get Orsen," I told Bren, already running. "Tell him the court just sent for its translator — and I don't think it's planning to translate."
