## 36. The Last Bargain

The thread of the old veil hung in Ellery's cellar like a spider's web spun from smoke, and I confess I spent a long moment simply looking at it, the way a man looks at a rope over a long drop and thinks, *that's it? That's what held my whole life?*

It was thinner than the last time. Thinner than the night of the theft, when Ellery had opened the great veil beneath the temple with a word that made the candles gutter and my teeth ache. The collectors had burned through most of the old roads on their way up, and what remained was this — a single filament, trembling, anchored between a wine rack that hadn't held wine in thirty years and whatever waited at the other end of everything.

"You don't have to do this," Sera said.

She'd said it eleven times since midnight. I'd counted. Counting was what I did instead of answering, because every answer I tried came out either a lie or a eulogy, and I wasn't ready for either.

"I know," I said, for the twelfth time, which by my arithmetic meant we were even.

"It won't work."

"It might."

"It's *stupid*, Orsen."

"That," I said, "is the first honest appraisal anyone's given me all week." I checked the lamp — her lamp, the one I'd carried down and carried back, its glass still smudged where my burned hands had gripped it. My hands had healed crooked. The physicker in Lowmarket had set the bones badly and charged me for the privilege, because in Vharen even your own screaming costs extra. "But stupid has been working for me lately. Ask the prophecy."

The prophecy. The book sat on Ellery's desk upstairs, sulking. It had spent three days rewriting itself in furious little strokes, striking out its final line — *the thief returns with a soul and a betrayal* — and writing it again, as though repetition could make a lie true. There had been no betrayal. Bren had walked away rather than be spent, Dagny had stayed behind at the gate for a flame that wasn't mine to carry, and I had come back with Sera's soul and nothing else, and somehow that had broken something in the words themselves. Fate, it turns out, is a contract, and contracts can be breached. Nobody had told the court this. Or perhaps they had, and the King simply refused to believe it, the way old men refuse to believe their sons.

And so the collectors had come.

* * *

You've seen what they did. Everyone in Vharen has seen it now; it's the only thing anyone talks about, in the markets, in the temples, in the low taverns where talk is cheap and beer is cheaper. They came up through cellars and wells and one memorable instance through the floor of a bakery, and they took memories. Not souls — memories, small ones, the way a tax collector takes copper when he can't get silver. From Marta the widow who'd bought my luck-charm, they took the memory of her husband's face. From the smugglers' cellar-keeper, they took his mother's lullaby. From a boy who'd once run an errand for me — an *errand* — they took the name of his dog.

They were precise about it. That was the horror of it. No rage, no hunger, just the flat administrative courtesy of an institution collecting on an invoice. The city owed them a thief, and until the thief was delivered, they would take payment in installments, and the installments were everything Vharen had ever loved that couldn't fight back.

Pell's tribunal had felt like justice. This felt like arithmetic.

"They'll reach me eventually," I told Sera. "Then you. Then everyone whose name ever appeared next to mine in a ledger. And then they'll start on people who merely *thought* about me, and Vharen has a lot of thoughts, most of them unflattering."

"So you'll walk into the mountain and hand yourself over. And then what? You think they'll tear up the city's debts out of gratitude? Out of *fairness*?" She laughed, and it came out wrong, cracked down the middle. "Orsen, they're not fair. They're a *court*."

"Courts honor their judgments," I said. "That's the one thing I learned down there. Everything else down there is appetite. But a judgment — a judgment they keep. It's the only currency they respect more than souls." I turned the lamp in my hands. "I'm not asking for fairness. I'm offering them a trade they can't refuse without breaking their own law. And they would rather die than break their own law. It's the only thing they *can* die of."

* * *

Ellery met me at the veil-thread with a face like wet paper. Thirty years of guilt will do that to a man; it leaches the color out gradually, from the edges in, until there's nothing left but the middle, and the middle is always afraid.

"I've prepared the words," he said. "For the opening. And—" He hesitated, then pressed a folded paper into my hand. "A statement. Confessing everything. The bargain, the supply of debtor souls, Pell, all of it. If you don't come back—"

"I'll come back."

"If you don't come back," he repeated, with the gentle stubbornness of a man who had finally learned to finish his sentences, "then someone should know what I did. Not the tribunal version. The true one." His hands shook. "I told myself for thirty years that I saved the city. I've decided to stop telling myself things."

I took the confession. It seemed rude to refuse a man his penance; besides, paper weighs almost nothing, and I've always traveled light.

Sera wouldn't come down to the cellar. She stood at the top of the stairs with her arms crossed and her jaw set, and she said, "If you do this, I swear on every lamp in Ash Street I will find a road down after you. Don't think I won't. I sold fifteen years once; I'll sell the rest."

"I believe you," I said. "That's why I'm leaving you instructions to *not* do that. Page four. In my handwriting, which you've always said is illegible, so consider it a security measure."

"Orsen."

"Sera."

"You come back," she said, "or you write me the longest apology in the history of the written word, and I *will* grade it."

I laughed. It hurt somewhere under the ribs, in a place I didn't examine too closely. "I'll aim for passing marks."

* * *

The thread took me the way water takes a dropped coin — no ceremony, no pain, just a sudden change of medium. One moment I was standing in a cellar smelling of dust and old dishonesty; the next, I was walking the last of the old roads, and they were dying around me as I went.

I had seen the undervaults alive, the first time: rivers of forgotten things flowing past in the dark, toll-bridges humming with the memories they'd eaten. Now the rivers ran thin. The collectors' passage upstream had disturbed everything, and the roads themselves seemed to be closing behind me like a book being shut. Bridges I remembered were gone. A stretch of shore where I'd once paid two memories for crossing — my mother's voice, a summer rain — was simply absent, a hole in the world shaped like a place.

Good, I thought. Fewer ways back means fewer ways for them to follow. I was already lying to myself with the calm fluency of a professional. It's a scribe's gift. We spend our lives making false ledgers balance, and eventually the skill turns inward.

At the Gate of Names, the doorkeeper was new. Or rather, the doorkeeper was the same, but wearing a different face, the way the court wore faces — borrowed, ill-fitting, patient. It looked at me and did not ask for a name.

"We know yours," it said.

"Then let's skip the pleasantries."

"The gate does not open for the known. Only for the named." A pause, and something like amusement moved across the borrowed face, slow as oil. "Give us a name, thief."

I gave them the forged one again — Corvus, the name Dagny had built for me out of dead men's paperwork, the name that had held once, barely, held by a thread thinner than the veil itself. The gate considered it. Somewhere inside the mountain, I imagined, a clerk consulted a ledger, found the entry, found the asterisk beside it, found the note reading *provisional*, and decided, tonight, to let it stand. Bureaucracy is the same everywhere. It runs on momentum.

The gate opened.

* * *

The auction hall had changed. Where before there had been tiers of seats filled with the court's gray congregation, now there were mostly empty benches, and the great glass cases along the walls stood dark, their flames withdrawn or hidden — the court was hiding its inventory, the way a merchant hides his stock when the tax man comes. Only the King's dais was lit, and the King sat upon it, alone, waiting, in the shape of a tall man with a courteous face and eyes like the far end of a well.

"Thief," he said. "You've cost me a great deal."

"I've been told I have that effect on people." I walked down the center aisle, boots loud on stone that wanted to be quiet. "Though in fairness, Your Majesty, you started it. You bought a lamplighter's soul at a discount and called it commerce."

"She sold. Freely. That is the oldest law there is."

"And here's my offer, freely made." I stopped at the foot of the dais and put the stolen ledger on the step between us — the court's own debts, its own accounts, the record of every bargain it had ever struck, including the ones it had quietly failed to honor. I'd kept it. Of course I'd kept it; I'm a scribe, we hoard paper the way dragons hoard gold, and for exactly the same reason. "The city above owes you a thief. Me. Delivered voluntarily, no collection required, no further raids, no more memories taken from widows and children and bakers. In exchange, you close the accounts. All of them. Every soul currently held against the debts of the living goes free, and the old roads are sealed behind me, and Vharen forgets you exist. Which, if we're honest, it mostly wants to anyway."

The King was silent for a long moment. Around us, the hall creaked — the sound of a mountain thinking.

"You are offering," he said at last, "to give me the one thing I have wanted since the prophecy wrote your name. Freely. Which is worth more than taken, as any collector knows." His well-eyes didn't blink. Wells don't blink. "And in exchange you want me to forgive debts that total more than this city has earned in a century."

"I want you to stop collecting them. There's a difference. Forgiveness is for priests. I'm negotiating with a creditor."

Something happened at the corner of the King's mouth. On a human face it would have been the beginning of a smile. On his, it was more like the beginning of weather.

"You have learned bargaining below the world," he said. "I could almost be proud. But you mistake the nature of my acceptance, scribe. I accept your offer. The city's accounts close tonight. The collectors withdraw. You will sit upon a bench in my hall and be mine, freely given, forever — the thief who stole a soul and paid for it with himself. It is, I grant you, a better ending than the prophecy wrote. Poets will be annoyed."

"Poets survive," I said. "It's practically their job."

"But." He raised one finger, and the temperature of the hall dropped the way it does when a large door opens somewhere far away. "A bargain requires terms from both parties. You have set yours. I set mine. That is the law beneath the law, older than me, older than the gates, older than the words themselves. And my condition is this—"

He leaned forward on his throne, and for the first time I saw what was underneath the courteous shape: something vast and patient and terribly tired, an appetite that had been eating for so long it had forgotten what hunger was for.

"—the thief comes to me *empty*. Whatever he carries of the stolen flame — its warmth, its memory, its light in him — stays above. You will not bring her down here with you, thief. Not her face, not her voice, not the years she covered your debts, not the bread you shared on Ash Street, not one hour of her. The court does not buy a man and his ghosts. You will surrender your memories of Sera the lamplighter, all of them, before you pass my threshold. You will arrive knowing only that you owe me, and that you chose to pay. Nothing else crosses."

The hall was very quiet. Somewhere far off, a river of forgotten things whispered through wires.

I had known, walking down, that there would be a condition. Kings don't accept clean trades; it's against their religion. And I had guessed — guessed in the cellar, guessed while Sera glared at me from the top of the stairs — that the condition would be exactly this, because it was the only coin I owned that the court truly valued. Souls are common down here. Memories of the living, warm ones, freely surrendered? Those are rare. Those are *vintage*.

What I hadn't expected was how easy it would feel to say yes.

Not because I didn't care. Because I did — because every memory of her was a room I'd lived in, and I knew what it was to lose rooms; I'd already paid my mother's voice and a summer rain on the toll-bridges, and I was still standing, still me, still able to make a bad joke at the foot of a throne. Memories are not the self. I'd staked my life on that theory twice already, and the theory had held.

But theories are cold comfort at 3 a.m., and I heard myself ask, "Will it hurt?"

"No," said the King, with terrible gentleness. "That is the cruelty of it. Forgetting never hurts. It's the remembering afterward that hurts — the reaching for a room that isn't there. You will not remember reaching. You will simply be a man who sometimes stands very still near lamps, and does not know why."

"Well," I said, and my voice held, which I counted as the finest piece of forgery I'd ever produced, "at least you're honest about the fine print. That puts you ahead of the Temple of Nine Bells."

"Do we have a bargain, thief?"

I looked at the ledger on the step — the court's own sins, itemized in its own hand. I thought of Marta's husband's face coming home to her. Of a boy remembering his dog. Of a city that had spent a century pretending the mountain wasn't there, and might, at last, be allowed to keep pretending.

Of Sera, grading my apology from the top of the stairs.

"Freely given," I said. "Freely taken. Sealed in blood and ink, as is traditional."

The King extended his hand, and it was the hand of the courteous man, and I took it, and the mountain closed around the handshake like a fist around a fly.

"Then come," he said, rising, and the empty cases along the walls lit one by one, a corridor of small fires bowing to their master. "We will write the terms. And then, thief, we will see what you are worth when you are nothing but debt."

I followed him toward the light, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who expect to return, and I am, whatever else I am, a realist.

Behind us, far above, through a thread of dying veil, a lamp on Ash Street was burning steady and bright, trimmed and waiting, for reasons its keeper could not yet name.

The terms were brought on paper black as beetle-shell, and the King handed me the pen himself.

"Your blood or mine?" he asked pleasantly. "The ink matters less than the hand. Write, thief. Sign yourself away."

I dipped the pen.
