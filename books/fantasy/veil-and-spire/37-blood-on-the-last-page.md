## 37. Blood on the Last Page

They bound me to a chair made of ledger-leaves.

I want that on the record, because it sounds like a joke and it was not a joke. The Buried King's court had no iron — iron is an above-world vanity — so they built my restraints out of the one material they had in abundance: debt. Pages of it, pressed and lacquered and folded into a chair shape, and when I pulled against them the pages rustled like a thousand small voices saying *mine, mine, mine*.

"You know," I said, "in Vharen this would be considered cruel and unusual."

"In Vharen," said the King, "you would be considered dead already."

He sat across from me in the auction hall, which was empty now except for us and the glass cases lining the walls, each one holding its little guttering flame. Sera's case was gone. Her soul-flame was tucked against my chest inside her lamp, wrapped in my coat, beating like a second heart I'd only just learned I had.

The King was not what the stories said. The stories said mountain, said crown of stone, said a face like a landslide. What sat before me was a tall figure in robes the color of wet slate, with a face that kept almost resolving into someone I knew — a magistrate, a temple elder, my father, no, not quite, never quite — because he wore borrowed shapes the way other men wore rings.

"Here is where you ask for terms," he said pleasantly.

"Here is where I tell you I've already written mine."

"Ah." He folded his hands. "The page."

Of course he knew. He had always known. That was the whole architecture of him — a king built out of prophecy the way a cathedral is built out of stone, every fulfilled line another course of masonry. *The thief descends.* Fulfilled. *The thief steals back a sold soul.* Fulfilled, an hour ago, with bells ringing backwards and vault doors slamming shut behind me like the court clearing its throat. Every word I'd obeyed had made him stronger. Fate, it turns out, is just compound interest paid by suckers.

"The book is blank at the end," I said. "Has been since the beginning. A hundred years of archivists assuming the ending was fixed, and none of them thought to check whether the last page simply hadn't been written yet."

"I checked," the King said. "The day the book was bound. And do you know what I found?" His smile was patient as erosion. "Nothing. Which meant the ending belonged to whoever arrived there first. So I have spent a century making sure that whoever arrived would be mine. A debtor. Desperate. Predictable. A man who would trade anything — anyone — to walk out whole." He leaned forward. "You were never going to write a defiant ending, Orsen Ravensworth. You were going to write a transactional one. They always do. Give me the flame freely, and I will release your city's debts, spare your friends, open the veil, and let you keep your memories. All of them. Even her."

And there it was. The offer I'd climbed down through rivers of forgotten things to make, handed back to me with the price inverted. My own bargain, worn like a stolen coat.

"That's generous," I said.

"It's cheap," he said. "Your city owes me eleven thousand souls' worth of paper. You are offering me one flame and a story with my name in it. I have eaten better bargains from kinder men." He rose, and his shadow rose with him, and his shadow had too many joints. "But I accept. Because here is the condition *I* choose, scribe: you will write the ending in your own blood, and the ending will say what we agree it says. *The thief returns with all he came for, and the court is owed forever.* Your hand. My words. Prophecy fulfilled to the last syllable, and every syllable a brick in my floor."

A knife appeared in his hand — or rather, a knife stopped pretending not to be in his hand.

"And if I refuse?"

"Then I take the flame, take your memories anyway, take the city, and write the ending myself in yours. Blood is blood. Ink is ink. The book has never cared whose pain makes the letters legible."

I looked at the knife. I looked at the pages binding my wrists, rustling their little chorus of *mine*. And I thought about something Dagny Holt told me three weeks ago, over a cellar table, with the flat certainty of a woman who had buried her whole life in one sentence: *"The court cannot be beaten, Orsen. It can only be out-bargained. So make sure the bargain has a hole in it, and make sure you're the only one who knows where."*

There was a hole. There had been a hole since chapter nine, more or less, though nobody had thought to number it.

The King wanted my hand. He had specified my hand. He had specified blood. He had specified the words *the thief returns* — because those words were load-bearing, because his entire kingdom stood on the grammar of return, on the assumption that whatever the thief carried out of the dark would come back up into the light where debts could be collected on it.

He had not specified what else the sentence might contain.

"Agreed," I said.

His smile widened by exactly one tooth. The knife crossed the space between us without appearing to travel, and then my palm was open and bleeding, and the book was in my lap — the old book, the century-old book, the one Ellery had planted and Pell had sold and I had stolen twice now, once with my eyes and once with my feet. It lay open to the last page. Blank. Patient. Hungrier than any page has a right to be.

My blood hit the paper and did not bead. It soaked. It ran. It moved toward the nib of the knife the way water moves toward a drain, and the knife, I realized, was not a knife at all but a pen that had been waiting a very long time for a hand foolish enough to hold it.

"Slowly," the King murmured. "Savor it. This is the last line of a hundred years."

I put the point to the page.

*The thief returns—*

The hall shivered. Somewhere far above, through forty fathoms of mountain, I felt the temple bells notice.

*—with nothing—*

The King's smile stopped.

I want to describe that moment properly, because I have thought about it every day since, and every day I get it slightly wrong, and today I want to get it right. It was not anger. Anger is a living thing's weather. What crossed the Buried King's face was the look of a building discovering a crack in its foundation — the long, slow arithmetic of a structure realizing that one of its stones has stopped agreeing to be a stone.

"*Nothing,*" he repeated. Not a question. An audit.

"You said blood," I told him. "You said my hand. You said *the thief returns*. You never said what he returns *with*." I pressed harder; the pen drank. "You built your court on prophecy, Your Majesty. On the lovely certainty that fate is a contract and contracts favor the party who wrote them first. But a blank page doesn't favor anyone. A blank page is the only honest thing in this entire mountain. And I—" my voice cracked, and I let it, because some cracks are load-bearing too, "—I am a *scribe*, you arrogant pile of slate. Words are the one thing I have ever owned outright."

*—and it is enough.*

The sentence closed. The period landed like a door.

And the court began to come apart.

Not dramatically — that came later, that came with fire. First it came apart the way a lie comes apart: quietly, at the seams, in the places where everyone had agreed not to look. The chair of ledgers beneath me sighed and slumped into loose paper. The glass cases along the walls fogged from the inside, and the flames within them brightened — not with fear, with *recognition*, the way faces brighten when a long trial ends. The King's borrowed face tried to hold itself together and failed at the mouth first, then the eyes, features sliding like wax above a candle that had finally been allowed to gutter.

"No," he said. "The prophecy is *fulfilled*. I have a century of fulfillment. I am the most fulfilled thing under this mountain—"

"You're the most *obeyed* thing under this mountain," I said, standing up out of a puddle of loose debt. "Turns out there's a difference."

He lunged — or the shadow lunged, or the idea of him lunged, all the accumulated weight of ten thousand collected souls swinging at me like a wrecking ball wearing a crown — and I did the single bravest and stupidest thing of my life, which was to stand still and hold up the book.

The book took the blow.

Pages tore. A hundred years of careful, terrible text shredded past me like migrating birds, and every torn line was a promise the court could no longer collect on. *The thief descends* — gone. *Guided by betrayal* — gone, and good riddance, that one had nearly cost me Bren. Line after line after line, unmaking itself as it unmade him, until the King was less a figure than a draft, less a draft than a draught, cold air moving through a room that was learning how to be a ruin.

That was when Dagny Holt walked out of the smoke at the far end of the auction hall carrying a flame the size of a fist.

She had been down here, I understood much later, for longer than either of us admitted. She had come down the night of the great descent and not come back up, and while I was running the undervaults with Sera's lamp against my heart, she had been doing what exiled inquisitors do best: finding the archive, reading everything, and locating one specific entry. One specific flame. One sister, sold thirty years ago by a husband with gambling debts, filed under a name Dagny had not spoken aloud since she was nineteen.

She held the flame cupped in both hands like a bird she was afraid to love.

"Dagny!" I shouted. "The gate's collapsing — we have to go *now*—"

She looked at me. She looked at the dissolving King. She looked at the walls of the vault, at the cases, at the thousands of small fires flickering in their thousands of small prisons, and I watched her arrive at a decision the way you watch a lamp run out of oil: gradually, then all at once.

"They can't all fit through the veils," she said. "There aren't enough roads. Some of them would burn forever in the dark waiting for a door." She turned the flame over in her hands. "Hers has been down here thirty years, Orsen. Thirty years of *this*." Her voice didn't break. Inquisitors' voices don't break; they erode. "So I'm going to give this court a funeral pyre it will still be paying off when the mountain forgets its own name. And you are going to take my sister's light up with the rest, and you are going to tell no one what I did, because I was an inquisitor and inquisitors don't get remembered kindly even when they're right."

"Dagny—"

"*Run,* scribe."

She opened her hands.

The flame she had carried — her own held flame, the one she'd been keeping lit since the cellar oath, the one I'd assumed was just a torch — went up like a struck match in a room full of gas. Because that's what the undervaults were. That's what they had always been: sealed chambers packed to the rafters with dry, compressed, centuries-old longing, and every soul in every case turned toward the heat at once with a sound I will hear for the rest of my life, a sound like ten thousand people inhaling before saying a name they'd been forbidden to say.

Fire took the auction hall. Fire took the ledgers — and oh, the ledgers burned *beautifully*, every page of every debt curling into ash with a little sigh of relief, eleven thousand souls' worth of paper going up like the world's most justified bonfire. Fire took the toll-bridges and the whispering rivers and the Gate of Names, and the names, freed from their gate, scattered upward through the cracking rock like startled birds.

The King made one last attempt at dignity. He gathered himself — what was left of himself — into a shape approximating regal, and he looked at me across the burning hall with eyes like the last two coals in a dead hearth.

"This isn't victory," he said. "This is a *revision*. And revisions—" the firelight bent around him, "—revisions can be appealed."

"Then appeal it," I said. "I'll bring my own ink."

He laughed. It was a horrible, wonderful sound, genuinely amused, the laugh of a creature who had just lost everything and found it funny, and then the fire reached him and he came apart into sparks that rose instead of fell, and I choose to believe that somewhere in that upward drift there was something that had once, very long ago, before the debts and the crown and the hunger, actually been a person.

I ran.

Through halls that were shedding their geometry, past cases bursting open, flames streaming past me in the dark like a migration, like a river deciding to flow the wrong way, like dawn happening underground. Sera's lamp beat against my chest. Dagny's sister-light streamed beside it for three corridors before peeling away, upward, through a fissure I would never find again, going home.

The old road was collapsing behind me in sections, the way a sentence collapses when you remove its verbs. I ran the route Ellery had taught me — left at the river of forgotten umbrellas, straight past the bridge that had eaten my mother's voice, don't look at the toll-keeper, *don't* look — and the whole time the mountain groaned overhead like an old man standing up from a chair he'd sat in too long.

The veil was ahead. Thin, tearing, lit from above by lamplight — actual lamplight, Ash Street lamplight, somebody's ordinary Tuesday evening — and beyond it, impossibly, blessedly, the smell of rain.

I put my bleeding hand against the tear.

And the last thing I felt from below, as the veil closed behind me like a wound healing, was not fire and not fury and not the King's appealing ghost.

It was gratitude. Ten thousand small warmths passing me in the dark, each one brushing my sleeve on the way up, the way strangers touch the arm of the man who opened the door.

* * *

I came out through the temple undercroft, which was in chaos, which is a story for the wardens and the tribunals and Ellery's very interesting next few years. I came out holding a lamp with a flame in it that had no business being as steady as it was. I came out with scorched hands, a ruined coat, a book full of holes, and a sentence in my own blood that had unmade a kingdom:

*The thief returns with nothing, and it is enough.*

Except.

Except as I climbed the undercroft stairs, the book — half-destroyed, smoking gently, clutched under my arm like a rescued cat — grew warm against my ribs. And when I finally stopped, in the doorway, in the rain-smell, and looked down at it, I saw that the torn pages were knitting themselves back together. Slowly. Reluctantly. Like a court reassembling itself in exile.

And on the final page, beneath my drying blood, in handwriting that was mine and was not mine, one new line had appeared:

*The court keeps what is freely given.*

I read it twice. I felt the shape of it settle over me like a coat I hadn't agreed to wear.

Freely given. I had given nothing freely. I had fought, I had tricked, I had written my way out —

Hadn't I?

Somewhere below, faint as a heartbeat through forty fathoms of stone, something ancient and patient and *not finished* began, very quietly, to count.
