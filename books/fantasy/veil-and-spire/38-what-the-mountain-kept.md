## 38. What the Mountain Kept

*Orsen*

The last thing I wrote was the truest thing I have ever written, and it was seven words long.

*The thief returns with nothing, and it is enough.*

I wrote them in my own blood on a page that had been blank for a hundred years, bound to a table in the auction hall of a court that had been eating this city's debts since before my grandfather learned to write his own name. And the moment the ink — the blood, rather; let's be honest about what we're discussing — dried, the mountain began to die.

Not dramatically, at first. That's the thing nobody tells you about ancient evil: it doesn't scream when it goes. It *itemizes*. The Buried King looked down at me where I lay strapped to the auction table, still bleeding from the wrist, and his face did something I will remember for exactly as long as they let me keep memories, which is to say: not long at all. It wasn't rage. It was an accountant watching a ledger burn.

"That line," he said, "was never yours to write."

"Neither was Sera," I said. My voice came out steadier than I had any right to expect. "Neither were any of them. You've been collecting on debts that were never yours to hold, and you did it for so long that everyone forgot the difference between a debt and a theft." I lifted my head as much as the bindings allowed. "I just wrote the difference down."

Around us, the vaults were coming apart in slow, luminous sections. The glass cases that held the souls — hundreds of them, thousands, each one a little flame in its own prison of blown glass and old law — were cracking one by one, and each crack released a sound like a bell being un-rung, like a word being taken back, like a breath held for decades finally let go. The flames didn't scatter. They rose. All of them, together, drifting up through the fissures in the vaulted ceiling as though the mountain had opened a second sky beneath the first.

The King watched them go with those patient, ancient eyes, and then he turned back to me, and he smiled, and I want it recorded somewhere — anywhere — that even at the end, defeated, unmade, his courtesy never cracked. That was the most frightening thing about him. Not the power. The *manners*.

"You understand," he said, "that a bargain freely offered cannot be refused, and mine was accepted."

"I know what I owe."

"The condition was mine to choose." He raised one hand, and the bindings fell away from me all at once, which should have felt like freedom and instead felt like being handed the bill. "You took a soul tonight, thief. It seems only balanced that you leave one behind. Yours has too many rooms in it anyway. One occupant takes up far more space than she knows."

I knew. God help me, I knew before he finished saying it. There are only so many people who live in a man's head, and only one of them had spent three years coughing her way through winter lamplight because of me.

"No," I said. And then, because the mountain deserved honesty if nothing else: "Yes. Fine. Do it."

"It will not hurt," said the King, almost gently. "That is the cruelty of it. You will simply wake lighter. You will look at a lamp and feel something pull behind your ribs, and you will spend the rest of your life wondering why, and you will never once know her name." He leaned close, and his breath smelled of cold stone and old paper. "But the words stand. *It is enough.* Your own blood, scribe. I intend to hold you to every letter."

The last thing I saw before the memory went was the flame rising out of the case where Sera's soul had burned for three years — rising past me, pausing, I would swear on any book you like, *pausing*, the way a person pauses in a doorway — and then the King touched my forehead, and the room went white, and Orsen Ravensworth stopped knowing the best thing that had ever happened to him.

*Dagny*

Thirty-one years ago I stood in a courtroom in Vharen and swore the inquisitor's oath: that magic was a wound in the world, that its practitioners were wounds walking, that I would cut them out wherever I found them. I believed it the way you believe things at twenty-two, which is completely, and briefly, and usually wrongly.

Then they sold my sister's soul to pay my father's gambling debts, and I discovered that the law I'd sworn to uphold had been feeding the very court beneath the mountain since before either of us was born, and belief curdles fast when you find out who's holding the ladle.

So no, I didn't come down here to destroy anything. Dagny Holt doesn't do grand gestures anymore; grand gestures are how you end up exiled. I came for one flame. I told the boy it was about burning the whole rotten court down because a lie needs a shape other people can carry, and he carried it beautifully, and I'm not sorry. But when the vaults started coming apart around me — when the ceiling split open like an egg and the light came pouring through — I ran, yes, but I ran *toward* something. Toward Vault Nine. Row Twelve. Case marked HOLT, M., entered into record thirty-one years ago, debtor: Holt, R., amount: everything.

The case was already cracked when I got there. Of course it was. The boy's seven words hadn't just broken the King's claim on himself; they'd broken the claim on *everyone*. Every contract in that hall was written in the same ink, and the ink had decided, all at once, that it was blood after all, and blood wants out.

Maren's flame sat in the wreckage of her glass like a candle someone had sheltered with both hands. Small. Steady. She'd been nineteen when they took her. I'd spent thirty-one years imagining what a soul looks like after three decades in a jar, and I'd imagined wrong every time, because what I saw was just — her. Brighter than her lamp ever was. Brighter than she'd been at the end, coughing in that rented room while I stood in my inquisitor's whites and called it justice.

"Mare," I said. My voice did something embarrassing. Inquisitors aren't supposed to have voices that do things. "It's me. It's Dagny. I'm late."

The flame tilted toward me. If you've never had a soul recognize you, I can't explain it, and if you have, you don't need me to. I gathered her out of the glass the way the boy had shown me — palms open, no grasping, you don't *take* a soul, you offer your hands and hope — and she settled against my chest like a bird that had been flying home for thirty-one years and had finally, finally landed.

The mountain was folding in around me by then. Walls going soft, corridors forgetting their own lengths, the old roads unraveling like hemmed thread. I walked out of there carrying my sister against my heart, stepping over the ruins of a court that had eaten a city for a century, and I did not look back, because looking back is for people who left something behind.

For once in my miserable, oath-breaking, exile-shaped life, I hadn't.

*Sera*

I woke on the floor of Ellery's archive with a lamp burning beside my head and a taste in my mouth like rain.

Here is what dying feels like, for the record, in case anyone asks: it doesn't. That's the horror of it. Fifteen years gone out of you, and what remains just gets quieter, thinner, colder — a house with the furniture carried out room by room until you're sleeping on the floor and calling it simplicity. I'd been dying for three years and calling it a cough. I'd made my peace with it. I'd even, in my worst moments, been a little proud of the trade. Fifteen years of me against a lifetime of him — ask anyone on Ash Street which was worth more.

And then I woke up, and the quiet was *gone*, and the cold was gone, and I could hear — this is going to sound mad — I could hear the building breathing. Not the draft. The *building*. Because the mountain under Vharen had just come apart, and every soul it had swallowed was coming up through every veil at once, and for about four minutes the whole city glowed.

I got to the window in time to see it. Ash Street, the temple spires, the river, the rooftops — light rising everywhere, out of cellars and wells and cracks in the flagstones, threads of gold and green and one particular blue-white that I knew the way you know your own handwriting. They went up like reversed snowfall. People came out of their doors in their nightclothes and stood in the street with their mouths open, and old Mother Cass on the corner dropped to her knees, and a warden — an actual warden, helmet and all — took off his helmet because he couldn't see properly through the tears, though he'd deny it to his grave.

Nobody spoke. What is there to say, when the sky over a city that outlawed wonder fills up with it anyway?

I pressed my hand flat against the glass, and I thought: *he did it. The fool. The beautiful, impossible, self-immolating fool.* And then I thought: *what did it cost him,* because nothing costs nothing below that mountain, and Orsen Ravensworth had never once in his life gotten away with paying less than full price.

I found him an hour later, sitting on the archive steps with his hands bandaged — burned, Ellery said, shielding the flame with his bare palms through half a mile of collapsing vault — and he looked up at me with eyes that were dry and clear and utterly, catastrophically empty.

"Hello," he said politely. "Are you hurt? There was some kind of — event. Everyone keeps crying and hugging each other and I can't work out why."

And I understood, standing there with fifteen years of my own life humming in my chest like a struck bell, exactly what the mountain had kept.

*Orsen*

They tell me I wept at the window when the lights went up. I have no memory of it. They tell me a great many things these days, and I have no memory of any of them, and I have learned to nod along the way you nod along to a story about a stranger.

Apparently I stole a soul from the court beneath the mountain. Apparently I broke a prophecy with my own blood. Apparently there was a woman — a lamplighter, they say, on Ash Street, they say — who sold fifteen years of herself to erase my debts, and I went down into the dark and bought her back, and the price was every memory I had of her.

It sounds like something I'd do. That's the strange part. Everyone says it sounds exactly like something I'd do, and I believe them the way you believe a witness to a crime you slept through.

What I have instead of her is this: a scar across my left palm shaped like nothing in particular. A spare lamp, trimmed and filled, that I keep by my door and refill without deciding to. A habit of looking up whenever I pass a lit window. And a feeling — not a memory, feelings apparently survive where facts don't, which strikes me as a design flaw — a feeling like a hand resting between my shoulder blades whenever the lamps come on at dusk. Warm. Familiar. Gone the moment I turn around.

There's a woman who lights the lamps on Ash Street. I've seen her at it. She has a cough she's getting over, they say, and a laugh that carries, and yesterday she caught me staring and smiled at me like I was a puzzle she'd already solved, and I smiled back like a man agreeing to terms he can't read.

I don't know her name.

I know — and I would swear this on any book in the archive, including the one that started all of this, the one that sits closed and quiet now on Ellery's shelf like a cat that got what it wanted — I know that I used to know her name. I can feel the hole where it was. It's the strangest sensation of my life: grief with no object, love with no address, a debt paid to someone whose face I couldn't pick from a crowd.

Ellery watches me sometimes with an expression I've decided not to interpret. The book watches me too, though books can't watch, obviously, obviously they can't, and yet twice now I've walked past it and felt the way you feel passing a door you're not supposed to open.

Tonight the lamps came on along Ash Street, all down the row, one after another, and I stood in my doorway with my bandaged hands and my trimmed spare lamp and I wept like a child at a funeral I couldn't name.

I don't know why.

I suspect — and this is the only suspicion I have left, the one thing the mountain missed — I suspect that's going to be enough.
