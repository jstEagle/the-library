## 2. Smoke Over Bellharbor

The bells of the Tidefather's temple had not rung for anything but the hour in three hundred years, and so when they began to toll in earnest — wild, overlapping, one bell chasing another like gulls after a fish-cart — Sera Ilyen was on her knees scrubbing salt-stain from the sanctuary floor and did not at first believe them.

She sat back on her heels. The rag dripped in her hand. Above her, the great bronze throats of the bell tower poured their voices down through the light-wells, and the sound came out strange, cracked at the edges, as if the bells themselves had forgotten how to be rung and were remembering all at once.

"Sister Ilyen!" A novice — Tam, small and quick, always out of breath — came skidding across the wet flagstones in sandals meant for dry ones. "Sister, you have to come. The harbor. There's a child."

"A child." She rose, knees aching. "Tam, if this is another of your—"

"Drowned," he said. "Three days drowned, Sister. They pulled him from the eel-beds by the fish market and he's *breathing*."

* * *

Bellharbor in autumn smelled of brine and woodsmoke and the sweet rot of overripe pears sold off barrows along the Quay of Small Fortunes. Sera ran through it with her habit gathered in one fist, Tam's hand locked in hers, past the rope-sellers and the oyster-wives, past the customs house where a clerk stood in his doorway with his ledger forgotten under his arm, staring toward the water.

The crowd had gathered at the eel-bed shallows where the harbor's edge went soft and reedy, where the poor who could not afford quay-space gutted their catch on flat stones. They stood in a ring perhaps forty deep, and they were silent.

That was what frightened her first, though she would not understand why until later. Bellharbor crowds were never silent. They shouted prices and insults and prayers with equal volume, and here were four hundred people making no sound at all, standing shoulder to shoulder in the gray morning light, looking down at something on the stones.

Sera pressed through. An oyster-wife recognized her habit and pulled others aside; hands helped her more than hindered, and she heard the word pass back through the crowd like a current under ice: *priestess, priestess, let the priestess through.*

Then she saw the child.

He lay on a gutting stone, and he was blue. Not the blue of cold — the deep, terrible blue of water that has been inside a body too long, the color of lips gone past purple into something the sea keeps for itself. He was perhaps six years old. His clothes were good wool, merchant-class, soaked through. His mother knelt beside him with her forehead pressed to his small cold chest, and she was not weeping. She was rocking, a low keening sound coming out of her like wind through a broken shutter, and around her on the stones someone had arranged — or scattered, Sera could not tell — a ring of white shells.

"He went in Tuesday," said a voice near her ear, an old fisherman, his voice hollowed out. "Off the ferry steps. We dragged the beds two days. Two days, priestess. My own son found him this morning, caught in the eel-weir, and—" He stopped. His mouth worked. "And when they laid him out, he coughed."

Sera knelt.

Up close the boy was worse than blue; he was wrong in ways her eyes kept trying to refuse. Water beaded on his skin still, though the morning was dry and he had been out of the water, by every account, for half an hour. His chest moved — rose, fell, rose — slow and even as a sleeper's, and each breath carried a faint sound with it, a sound she would spend years trying to describe afterward and never quite manage: a sound like the sea heard through a wall. Like tide against the far side of something.

She put two fingers beneath his jaw.

His pulse beat slow and vast, deeper than any pulse she had ever felt, as though she were not touching a vein but pressing her hand against the hull of a ship and feeling the ocean move beneath it.

"Who spoke over him?" she asked. Her voice came out steadier than she felt. "When he coughed — who was praying?"

"No one, priestess." The fisherman again. He crossed himself with the old sign, thumb to brow to breast, the gesture of men who had learned it from grandmothers and believed it the way one believes a roof holds. "That's the thing. No one dared. His mother'd gone quiet as the rest of us. It was just — he coughed. On his own. Like the sea changed its mind."

Around them the crowd had begun, very slowly, to make noise again. Not words yet. A murmur, rising and falling, the sound of four hundred people each privately deciding whether to hope. Hope is dangerous in crowds. Sera had been taught that in her novitiate, in the grey years when the Tidefather's altars stood unlit and the seminary masters taught history instead of liturgy: *the gods are silent, children, and silence is a mercy, because a god who answers once may answer twice, and then who among us will dare ask for anything small?*

She looked down at the drowned child breathing sea-sounds, and she thought: *the altars have been cold my whole life. My whole life, and my mother's, and hers.*

"Bring me oil," she said. "And the censer. And send someone to the temple for the Rite of Returning — the old book, the one in the sealed case. Go!"

They went. And because there was nothing else her hands knew how to do, Sera Ilyen took the child's cold blue hand in both of hers, bowed her head, and prayed aloud — the first public prayer of intercession offered in Bellharbor in three centuries, and she did not even know she was doing it until the words were already falling out of her, the old words, the ones printed only in the sealed books, the ones she had copied out secretly at nineteen because some stubborn ember in her refused to believe the gods were done:

*Tidefather, keeper of the long road home, if this soul walks your dark country still, walk him back to us. If he stands upon your shore, turn him gently. What was given to the water, let the water give back.*

The crowd had gone silent again.

And the sea answered.

Not with thunder. That was the horror of it, afterward — everyone expected thunder, expected the sky to split, expected the grand theatrical violence of the old stories. Instead the water in the eel-beds simply went still. All of it, every pool and channel across the flats, smooth as poured glass, while the open harbor beyond continued its ordinary chop. A hush fell over the shallows like a held breath.

Then the boy exhaled, and the breath smelled of deep water and old stone, and he said — clearly, in the piping voice of a six-year-old, in a tone of mild complaint as though remarking on the weather:

"*It let me go.*"

His eyes opened. They were brown. Ordinary brown, a child's brown, and Sera would cling to that detail later like a drowning woman clinging to driftwood: his eyes were brown and human and afraid, and whatever had spoken through the water had spoken *about* him, not *through* him.

"It let me go," he said again, and began to cry, ordinary child's crying, and his mother made a sound that Sera would hear in her dreams for the rest of her life, a sound like a door torn off its hinges, and gathered him up.

The crowd broke.

There was no other way to describe it. Four hundred people shattered all at once into noise — screaming, laughing, sobbing, falling to their knees on the wet stones, clawing at their neighbors, holding up their children to see. Someone began to sing the old hymn to the Tidefather, the one no one had sung in living memory, and got the words wrong, and a hundred voices corrected them, and the hymn swelled up ragged and magnificent over the eel-beds while the water in the pools trembled and the drowned child wept in his mother's arms.

Sera knelt on the gutting stone amid the white shells and felt awe go through her like a spear.

This is what the seminary masters had never told her, she thought dizzily. They taught the silence as doctrine, as safety, as the natural order. They never told her what it would do to a person, to hear the answer come. It was not comfort. It was not joy, exactly. It was *recognition* — the feeling of a bone setting back into joint after a lifetime of walking crooked. Three hundred years the world had been dislocated, and now, in a stinking eel-bed in the free city of Bellharbor, something had clicked home.

*We were right,* she thought, and the thought burned. *All of us who kept the empty altars swept. We were right, and it was real, and it is real—*

Then she saw the smoke.

* * *

It rose above the rooftops to the east, beyond the fish market: a thin black column, straight in the windless air. Torch-smoke. Sera knew torch-smoke; every priestess in the free cities knew it; it was the smell of the century just past, of the purges in Veyran's territories, of shrines burned and priests hung from their own temple beams.

The hymn faltered. Heads turned eastward. And through the parting crowd, moving with the unhurried confidence of men who owned whatever ground they stood on, came twelve soldiers in the black-and-gold of the Usurper.

Veyran's writ did not run in Bellharbor — not officially. The free cities had held their independence for two hundred years precisely by keeping the Usurper's soldiers outside their walls. But Veyran's coin ran everywhere, and so did his fear, and the garrison commander of Bellharbor's mercenary watch had grown very friendly, these last months, with certain envoys from the south. Everyone had noticed. No one had said.

Now twelve soldiers walked through a kneeling crowd as if it were a field of grass, and at their head rode a man on a grey horse whom Sera did not know, and would remember forever: lean, precise, with the pale unweathered face of a man who worked indoors, and eyes that moved over the crowd the way a factor's eyes move over cargo.

"Inquisitor," someone whispered, and the word went through the crowd like a blade through silk.

The soldiers reached the gutting stone. The mother with her living son shrank back against it, and a soldier — young, nervous, human underneath the black — actually hesitated before reaching out and taking the boy's ankle, pulling him from her grip with a gentleness that somehow made it worse.

"The child comes with us," the inquisitor said. His voice was mild. Mildness, Sera would learn, was worse than shouting. "By order of the Lord Protector's commission on false miracles and sedition. There has been no miracle. There cannot have been a miracle. Whatever occurred here was fraud, hysteria, or sorcery, and in all three cases the law is the same." His pale eyes found Sera, still kneeling in her grey habit among the shells. "You. You are the priestess?"

"I am Sera Ilyen, of the Tidefather's temple."

"You prayed over the body."

"I prayed. Yes."

"And the body breathed."

"The Tidefather breathed," Sera said, and heard her own voice ring out strange and clear over the crowd, and understood in that moment that she had already chosen, that the choice had been made somewhere below thought, in the marrow. "I only asked."

Something flickered in the inquisitor's face — hunger, she thought, or its cousin — and he dismounted, unhurried, and drew from his coat a folded paper. "Sister Sera Ilyen. Of course. Your name is already known to the commission." He unfolded the paper with the care of a man handling something valuable. "You will come with us quietly, and the crowd will disperse, and this will be remembered as a small sad confusion. Or—" he glanced up, at the crowd, at the hundreds of faces, and smiled without warmth, "—it will be remembered differently."

The crowd did not disperse.

Later, Sera would try to reconstruct how it happened — which stone started it, whose rage caught first — and she never could. She remembered the oyster-wife suddenly standing beside her with a gutting knife. She remembered the fisherman whose son had found the child, roaring the Tidefather's name. She remembered a cobblestone leaving someone's hand and striking a soldier's helm with a sound like a bell cracking. She remembered the inquisitor saying, softly, almost sadly, "Ah," as though a hypothesis had been confirmed.

Then the soldiers drew steel, and the morning broke apart.

What followed was not a battle; the crowd had no weapons worth the name and no formation, only numbers and fury and the unbearable momentum of people who have just seen the impossible and been told it is illegal. The soldiers fought well, professionally, backs together, blades flat more often than edge — they had orders about this, clearly, orders not to slaughter a crowd over a drowned boy, and that restraint was the only mercy in the next hundred heartbeats. But a knife found a soldier's thigh. A boat-hook caught another across the shoulders and bore him down into the eel-pool. The young one holding the child lost his grip in the press, and the mother vanished into the crowd with her son, and whether anyone would ever find them again, Sera did not know.

Someone seized her arm — Tam, white-faced, blood on his cheek that was not his own. "Sister. *Sister.* The back way. Come *now*."

She came. It shames me, she thought, even as she ran — it will shame me all my life, that I ran while they bled for me. But the inquisitor's pale eyes had marked her, and a marked priestess helps no one, and there was a thing — she could not yet have said what — a thing she had to carry out of this morning alive.

She saw it as Tam hauled her through the fish market's reeking lanes, past stalls overturned and a shrine-niche to some minor harbor spirit smashed by a fleeing elbow: set into the wall of the old pilot-house, half-hidden behind hanging nets, a wayside altar older than the city itself. The Tidefather's shrine, the first one, the one the founding pilots had raised when Bellharbor was three huts and a rope-ferry. Every child in the city knew it. Every child had left a shell or a copper on it.

It was shattered.

Not weathered, not worn — *shattered*, burst outward from within, its ancient stone split into a dozen jagged pieces as though something inside it had woken and pushed. And in the center of the wreckage, one fragment larger than the rest lay humming.

She heard it before she touched it — a note below hearing, felt in the teeth, in the sternum, in the scar tissue of an old burn on her palm. The fragment was the size of a loaf, wedge-shaped, and where its broken face caught the light it did not reflect the light so much as hold it, the way deep water holds a lantern. Carved characters spiraled across its surface in a script she knew only from the sealed books: covenant-script, the writing the old gods had taught mortals in the years of the pact, the writing that was supposed to exist nowhere anymore except in forbidden pages.

*Sera,* said a voice, and it was not Tam's, and it was not in her ears.

She picked up the stone.

It was warm. Warm as a living thing, warm as a fevered child, and it hummed against her palms like a struck bell that would not finish ringing, and through the humming she felt — distantly, the way one feels a storm over the horizon by the pressure in the air — something vast turning its attention toward her from very far away. Attention like a lighthouse beam sweeping the dark. Attention like an eye opening.

"*Found,*" said the voice, or the stone, or the sea, or the thing behind the sea, and the word was glad, and the gladness was the most frightening part.

"Sister!" Tam, at the mouth of the alley, frantic. "They're searching the streets — the watch is with them — Sister, *please*—"

Sera wrapped the fragment in the sash of her habit, bound it against her chest, and ran.

Behind her, smoke climbed straight into the gray sky over Bellharbor, and the bells of the Tidefather's temple rang on and on, wild and cracked and alive, calling a city to a dawn it had stopped believing in three hundred years ago — and somewhere north, though she could not have said how she knew, something else had also woken, and was also listening, and had begun, very quietly, to move.
