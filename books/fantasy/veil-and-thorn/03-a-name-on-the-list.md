## 3. A Name on the List

The rain had followed him down out of the Thornfells for two days, thin and gray as spun wool, and by the time Ada Joubert crested the last hill above Bellharbor it had soaked through his coat to the skin and turned the road to a brown ribbon of porridge. He didn't mind. Rain was honest weather. It asked nothing of you except that you endure it, and it left no marks worth mapping.

From the hill he could see the city spread along its crescent bay like a hand of cards dealt carelessly — the harbor with its forest of masts, the salt-stained walls, the tiled roofs going green with moss, and above them all the slender tower of the Tidehall where the council met and argued and accomplished nothing at a very high standard. Smoke rose from ten thousand chimneys and bent eastward in the wind. Somewhere in that sprawl was his workshop, his narrow bed, his kettle with the chipped spout, and the debt that had brought him home heavier than any cargo.

He touched the coin purse at his belt without meaning to. Forty crowns of the sixty owed. The caravan master had paid well for safe passage through the smuggler's pass — better than well, because word of Ada's maps had traveled, and men who moved goods through the Thornfells paid for certainty the way drowning men paid for air. Twenty more and the debt would be settled. He did not let himself think about who held the debt, or why. That was a door he kept locked, and he had grown skilled at walking past it.

But the shrine stone at the pass's heart kept surfacing anyway, unbidden, like a body rising in still water.

He had pressed his palm against it to steady himself while the caravan picked its way over scree, an old habit from years of surveying — you learned the age of a stone through your hand, its grain, its settling, whether it would hold your weight. And the stone had *hummed*. Not the hum of wind through a hollow, not the tremor of a cart passing. It had hummed the way a struck bell hums, deep and deliberate, and it had gone on humming after the cart was a mile away, and it had stopped only when Ada took his hand away.

And then there was the woman. The pilgrim wife whose boy had fallen into the flooded culvert, blue-lipped and still, while her husband screamed at the sky. She had knelt at the shrine stone — the same stone, Ada realized later, though he hadn't marked it then — and she had prayed with the flat, ugly desperation of someone who has no prayers left, and the boy had coughed up half the culvert and breathed.

Ada had told himself it was luck. He was a cartographer; he dealt in what could be measured, walked, drawn. But when the woman had seized his sleeve afterward, weeping her thanks as if he'd done it — *you touched the stone, you touched it first* — something cold had slid down his spine and pooled there, and it hadn't drained since.

*The mountain noticed me.* That was how it had felt. Like being watched by something that had been asleep for three hundred years and had opened one eye.

He shook it off and went down into Bellharbor, because whatever waited in the mountains, first he had debts to pay and ink to buy and a map of the Saltmarsh trade routes to finish before the autumn caravans. Ordinary things. Measurable things.

The city swallowed him in its usual noise and stink — fish offal and tar and woodsmoke, gulls screaming their endless litigation overhead, a fishwife cursing a porter in three dialects. He passed the moneylender's office on Cordwainer Row and paid forty crowns across the counter, watching the clerk's face for the flicker of contempt he'd braced for, but the man only counted the coins twice and stamped the ledger. *Twenty remaining,* the stamp said, in effect. *Twenty more, mapmaker, and your name comes out of my book.*

His name. In books. He thought of the ledger, and then — absurdly, unwelcome — of lists, and of names written on them, and he didn't know why until much later, when knowing was no use at all.

* * *

He smelled the wrongness before he saw it.

His workshop stood on Tallow Lane, third door past the cooper's yard, above a dyer's shop that perfumed the whole stairwell with mordant and urine. You could smell his street from the corner of Cordwainer Row on a good day. Today he smelled it from two streets away: not dye, not tallow, but lamp oil and wet ash and the particular sourness of too many strangers breathing in a small room.

Ada stopped in the mouth of the alley opposite and did what he always did when something was wrong: he mapped it.

Two men at his door, wearing the livery of the city watch but standing like soldiers, weight forward, hands free. A covered wagon in the cooper's yard with its tailgate down. Through his own windows — the ones he'd paid a glazier good silver to make, so he could work by northern light — lamplight moved where lamplight should not be burning at midday. And paper. Papers everywhere, carried out in armfuls by a third man who laid them on the wagon's bed with the care of a priest arranging relics.

They were taking his maps. Every chart, every survey, every tracing of every smuggler's road he had spent eleven years walking, measuring, bleeding for.

The cold thing in his spine turned over.

He should have run. Some part of him, some older and wiser animal part, said *run now, run north, lose yourself in the Thornfells where even the king's writ goes to die.* But eleven years of work was stacked on that wagon, and beneath the floorboard under his drafting table — wrapped in oilcloth, weighted with river stones — were the maps he had never shown anyone, the ones that weren't of anywhere, and the animal part of him lost the argument.

He went in through the dyer's shop, up the back stair, quiet as a man who had spent a decade learning to move over scree without dislodging it.

The workshop door hung open.

Inside, his life was being taken apart by four men with white gloves. His drawers were out on the floor, their contents sorted into piles: instruments here, papers there, personal effects — his mother's ring, he saw with a jolt, sitting in the *personal effects* pile like a piece of evidence — in a third. They had unscrewed the legs of his drafting table looking for hidden compartments. One of them stood at his map chest, lifting out charts one by one, holding each to the light as if truth might be visible through vellum.

"Master Joubert." The speaker sat in Ada's own chair behind Ada's own table, and this offended Ada more than anything else, in some small stubborn way he couldn't have explained. The man was perhaps fifty, gray at the temples, with the soft hands and patient eyes of a career bureaucrat, and he wore no livery at all — just good black wool and a chain of office that bore no device Ada recognized. Only a blank oval of polished steel where a sigil should have been. "You're expected. Sit down."

"I'm expected," Ada repeated. His voice came out steadier than he felt. "By whom?"

"By us." The man gestured at the room with a small, apologetic motion, as if the ransacking were weather he regretted. "My name is Inquisitor Halle. I serve the Crown Office of Inquiry. You've been away, I think. Caravans. Mountains." He smiled faintly. "It must be restful work."

"It pays," Ada said. He stayed by the door. There was a second door, behind the map chest, that gave onto the roof of the dyer's shed; he put it in his mind and left it there, the way he left landmarks. "You're stealing my work."

"We're *sequestering* it. There's a difference, and the difference is that you'll be compensated if the inquiry concludes in your favor." Halle folded his soft hands. "Though I'll be honest with you, Master Joubert, because I find honesty saves time. The inquiry will not conclude in your favor."

One of the gloved men came to Halle's elbow and set down a document — heavy paper, folded once, sealed with black wax bearing the same blank oval. Halle broke the seal and smoothed it flat on the table, and turned it around, and pushed it toward Ada.

"Read it," Halle said. "I want you to read it. Most men don't. They look at the seal and stop listening. Read it."

Ada read it.

It was a denunciation. He knew the form — every licensed guide and charter-master in the free cities knew the form, because the usurper Veyran's edicts required them all to swear loyalty oaths and inform on one another, and most signed and none informed, and the forms piled up in offices like snow that never melted. This one was not a form. This one was specific. It named him. *Ada Joubert, cartographer, of Tallow Lane, Bellharbor.* It named his trade, his routes, his clients. It named — and here the cold thing in his spine went from cold to burning — *the bloodline of Joubert, of the Thornfell shrines, being of the old stock, keeper-stock.*

At the top of the page, above his name, was a heading: *Register of Suspected Bloodlines, First Priority.* His family name was the first entry. Below it, other names he half-knew — mountain families, shrine-keepers' families, names from gravestones he'd sketched in forgotten valleys. But his was first. His was the head of the list.

And at the bottom, the signature. Not a clerk's abbreviation. A full signature, in a hand Ada knew better than his own — the strong downstrokes, the little hook on the final letter that Corvane had tried for thirty years to train out of his own writing and never managed.

*Master Aldous Corvane, Royal Academy of Cartography, Charter-Master Emeritus.*

The room went very quiet. Or perhaps it didn't, and it was only Ada who went quiet, everything in him narrowing to a single point like light through a lens.

"He signed it," Ada said.

Halle inclined his head, almost gently. "Master Corvane has been most cooperative. He provided the genealogical evidence. Your mother's line, the shrine records, the — " he consulted the document without touching it — "baptismal carving at the high pass. Did you know there's a carving of your family name in the oldest shrine in the Thornfells? Master Corvane knew. He's known for a long time, apparently. Since you were a boy."

Ada's hand had drifted, without his permission, to the compass at his belt — his father's compass, brass-cased, the one thing he carried everywhere. He wasn't aware he'd done it until Halle's eyes flicked to it and sharpened.

"That's an interesting instrument," Halle said. "May I?"

"No."

"No?" Halle sighed, and the gentleness went out of him all at once, like a tide withdrawing, and what was underneath was not cruel exactly but vast and indifferent, the way a rockslide is indifferent. "Master Joubert. Let me explain your situation, because I think you believe you have one. Three hundred years ago the gods went silent, and the world improved immeasurably — no miracles, no mad prophets, no villages burned by things out of stories. Then, this season, the silence ends. Prayers are answered. Drowned children breathe. Shrines sing. And His Majesty — " the title came off Halle's tongue with the smoothness of long practice and the hollowness of a drum — "has commanded the Office of Inquiry to determine why. We have determined that it has to do with blood. Old blood. Keeper-blood. And we have a list, and your name is at the top of it, and the man who taught you everything you know is the one who wrote it there."

He leaned forward.

"So the question is not whether you come with us. The question is whether you come walking, or whether these gentlemen — " a nod at the gloved men, two of whom had drifted, casually, toward the door behind Ada — "carry what's left. The compass, please."

Ada looked at the compass. He looked at the map chest, and the second door behind it. He looked at the pile marked *personal effects*, where his mother's ring lay glinting under lamplight, and he understood, in a single cold flash, that they had already searched everything that mattered and found nothing — because the impossible maps, the ones that weren't of anywhere, were under the floorboards, and either Corvane had not remembered them or —

Or Corvane had remembered, and had chosen not to say.

He didn't know which possibility hurt more. He suspected he would spend a long time finding out.

"The compass stays," Ada said, and drew it, and threw it.

Not at Halle. At the lamp.

The globe of glass burst against the wall in a bloom of flame and burning oil, and the room went orange and shouting, and Ada was already moving — over the table, shoulder into the nearest gloved man, sending him into the map chest hard enough to crack the lid, through the smoke, wrenching open the loose floorboard with fingers that tore a nail and did not care. Oilcloth bundle. Under his coat. Behind him Halle was shouting not *stop him* but *the documents, save the documents*, which told Ada something about what the Crown valued, and then hands were on his coat and he shrugged out of the coat itself, leaving it in their grip like a shed skin, and went through the low door onto the dyer's shed roof with firelight roaring at his back.

The drop to the alley was eight feet. He took it rolling, came up running, and ran.

* * *

He ran the way water runs, downhill and by instinct, through the tannery lanes where the stink drove casual pursuit away, across the washing-lines court where he ducked under forty strangers' linens and emerged the far side merely a tall man in a shirt, hood up, pace unhurried. Twice he heard boots behind him and twice he took turns no pursuer could guess, because he had mapped Bellharbor's underside years ago — the cut-throughs, the collapsed cellars, the drain that a slim man could walk stooped from the tannery to the harbor steps — and the city's secret geometry unfolded ahead of him like a chart he'd drawn in the dark.

By full dark he was crouched in the hull-shadow of a beached fishing smack beyond the seawall, breathing salt air, listening to the bells of the Tidehall ring the evening watch, and shaking. Not from fear, or not only. From the other thing.

*Corvane signed it.*

He made himself hold the thought, the way you probe a wound to learn its depth. Thirty years. The old man had found him — a half-starved boy of nine, alone, no family anyone would claim — and had fed him and housed him and taught him letters and numbers and the grammar of the world: how a contour line holds a mountain's shape the way a hand holds water, how a map is a promise that the world can be known. *The world yields to the patient, boy. Everything true can be measured, and everything measured can be trusted.* Corvane's voice, Corvane's creed, Corvane's hand on his shoulder at his licensing, saying *proud of you* in the gruff way that meant it.

And Corvane had known — had known all along, Halle said, since Ada was a boy — what Ada was. What the name Joubert meant, carved in a shrine above the high pass. Had kept it like a knife in a drawer, and this season, when the shrines began to sing and the Crown came hunting keeper-blood, had taken the knife out and written Ada's name with it.

Why? For favor. For safety. For the Academy's charter, renewed. For thirty pieces of anything. It didn't matter. What mattered was the signature, the little hooked letter, the hand he'd have known blindfolded.

Ada pressed his palms against his eyes until he saw stars, and hated — this was the worst of it, this was the part he would carry — that underneath the hatred there was still love, stubborn and stupid as a dog that returns to the boot that kicked it. You did not stop loving the man who raised you because he sold you. You just carried both, and both were heavy, and no one had ever made a map showing where to set them down.

Resolve came the way dawn comes in the mountains: not suddenly, but inevitably, gray becoming gold whether you willed it or not.

They wanted him for his blood. Very well. His blood was the one thing they couldn't take from him, and if the old powers were waking — if the shrines sang under his hand and drowned children breathed — then the blood meant something, and whatever it meant, it was *his* to understand before it was theirs to use. He would go north. He would go into the Thornfells, into the passes only he could read, and he would find out why the mountain had noticed him, and he would do it ahead of Halle and his blank ovals and his list.

There was one problem, and it was the fire.

In the workshop, when the lamp burst, he had seen the flames catch the spilled oil and leap toward the map chest — toward the charts, the surveys, eleven years of work. But that wasn't what made his stomach clench even now. What made it clench was the bundle under his coat, and what he'd seen in the instant of snatching it: the topmost sheet, the one he'd drawn three winters ago in a fever-dream week he had never explained to himself, the map of a realm that shouldn't exist. Roads that matched no roads. Mountains that matched no mountains. A coastline he had traced with a hand that shook, and place-names in a script he didn't recognize and yet had written fluently, stroke for stroke, as if remembering rather than inventing.

He had buried it under the floorboards because it frightened him. Because every attempt to burn it had failed — the vellum wouldn't catch, wouldn't even scorch — and because sometimes, late at night, the roads on it seemed to have shifted slightly from where he'd drawn them.

He unrolled it now by the light of the moon off the harbor water, and the moon went behind a cloud as if the sky preferred not to watch, and in the darkness the lines of the impossible map seemed faintly, faintly warm under his fingertips.

Somewhere across the city, bells began to ring out of pattern — alarm bells, many of them, probably for a fire on Tallow Lane. Ada rolled the map, tied it, slung his field kit over one shoulder, and looked north, where the Thornfells stood black against the last of the twilight, higher than the smoke, older than the names.

"All right," he said aloud, to the map, to the mountains, to the mentor who had betrayed him and the blood that had doomed him. "Show me the road."

And far above, at the tree line, where no traveler was and no torch burned, a shrine stone on a high pass began, very softly, to hum.
