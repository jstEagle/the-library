## 5. What the Gods Want

The wayshrine had a hearth that had not been lit in a hundred years, and Sera Ilyen lit it anyway, because her hands needed something holy to do.

She had found flint and a nest of dry moss beneath the altar's lip, and when the flame caught — small, blue-hearted, stubborn — she felt something in her chest unknot by a single turn. Fire was fire. Even here, even now. Even after what she had seen in Bellharbor.

Behind her, the cartographer sat with his back against the shrine wall and his ruined compass open on his knee, watching the needle tremble toward the stone fragment in her satchel like a hound straining at a scent. He hadn't spoken since they'd made their wary bargain on the road: his knowledge of the ways for whatever protection her fragment offered. She suspected he regretted it already. He had the look of a man who had mapped a coastline all his life and just watched the sea move.

Outside, the Thornfells went on being the Thornfells — wind worrying the pines, snow hissing against the shuttered window, the long grey light of late afternoon draining off the peaks. A ordinary evening, if you didn't listen too closely. If you didn't hear the way the mountain hummed under its skin.

"You should eat," she said, and pushed half her bread across the hearthstone to him.

"I'm not hungry."

"You will be. Fear spends the body faster than labor." She said it the way she'd said it to penitents for eleven years, and then heard herself, and almost laughed. *Penitents.* As though she were still anyone's priestess. As though the order had not scattered like startled birds the moment the soldiers came for her.

Ada Joubert took the bread. He ate mechanically, eyes still on the compass. "It's been doing this since we met," he said. "Pointing at your stone. Before that, it pointed at the shrine stones on my routes. I thought the needle was magnetized wrong. I thought—" He stopped. Started again. "I've been mapping these mountains for nine years. I know every smuggler's cut from Bellharbor to the Salt Gate. And three days ago the road I've walked two hundred times put me somewhere I have never been, and the somewhere knew me."

Sera fed another stick to the fire. "Tell me what you felt. At the pass. When the stone answered the stranger's prayer."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer. Then: "Like being recognized. Like walking into a room full of people who all turn to look at once." His jaw tightened. "I don't like being looked at."

"No one does. Not truly. Not by *that*."

"And what is *that*, priestess? You've been circling it since the road. You crossed half the Thornfells with me and you still haven't said what's in your bag or why Veyran's soldiers would burn a chapel full of people to get at one woman holding a rock."

The question hung there, fair and sharp as a drawn blade. Sera looked into the fire and let herself remember — because she had been refusing to remember, wrapping the memory in liturgy, in procedure, in the comfortable grammar of *the miracle* — and remembering hurt the way truth always hurts: cleanly, deeply, without mercy.

"The drowned child," she said. "You want to know about the drowned child."

"I want to know what's happening to the world."

*So do I,* she did not say. Instead she wiped her hands on her robe — the grey robe of the Order of the Veil, ash-stained now, torn at the hem where a soldier had grabbed her — and began the way she had been trained to begin everything: at the beginning.

* * *

"Before the Silence," she said, "there were nine gods. Not nine statues, not nine names carved over nine doors — nine *presences*. The oldest records call them the Nine Voices, because that is how they were known first. Not seen. Heard. In the wind off the saltmarshes, in the creak of glaciers, in the sound a river makes going under ice. My order holds that the world itself was their speaking."

Ada had gone still. It was the stillness of a man listening to a map being read aloud, she thought — following the terrain of her words, checking them against something.

"They were not distant," she went on. "That's the first thing the modern mind refuses to believe. We imagine gods as kings behind walls, petitioned through intermediaries. But the Nine were *near*. They walked the boundary between their realm and ours the way a farmer walks his fence line. And the boundary — the veil — was not a wall. It was a covenant. An agreement, written into the structure of things: they would lend us their power, their presence, their *attention*, and in return we would keep the veil whole. Keep the two realms from bleeding into each other. They could not hold it closed from their side alone. It needed mortal hands. Mortal blood. Mortal *keeping*."

"Keeping how?"

"There were keepers. Bloodlines sworn to the maintenance of the boundary — the old texts call them the Threshold Kindred. They tended the anchor-stones, the covenant-stones, set at the thin places and the strong places both. There were four great stones, and numberless lesser ones, and every shrine you've ever passed on a smuggler's road was built on one. Your roads, cartographer—" she nodded at him "—your precious unmapped passes. They are not roads. They are *veins*. The covenant ran through them like blood through a body. That's why they're straighter than any road has a right to be. That's why they survive avalanches that should bury them. They were made to carry something."

She watched him absorb that — watched the cartographer in him war with the fugitive, the man who wanted to understand losing to the man who wanted to be anywhere else. Understanding won. It usually did, with his kind.

"And mortals broke it," he said. Not a question.

"Mortals broke it." The fire popped; a log collapsed into embers. "Greed, the texts say, but greed is too simple a word. It was more like... hunger. We had been lent power — healing, weather-sense, the blessing of fields, the turning aside of storms — and power is a thing that teaches appetite. The Kindred began to ask for more. Then to demand. Then to take. There is a record — one fragment, salvaged from the burning of the first library — describing the last century before the Silence. The keepers were cutting pieces out of the veil itself. Using shards of the covenant-stones as... batteries. Draining the boundary to work wonders the Nine had never sanctioned. Raising the dead. Forcing love. Peering into realms that were never meant to be peered into."

"And the gods?"

"Went silent." She said it flatly, because there was no other way to say it. "Not angry. Not vengeful. Silent. One dawn, three hundred years ago, every prayer in the world went unanswered, and stayed unanswered, and within a generation half the world had decided there had never been any gods at all. The other half built my order — the Order of the Veil — to keep the memory. To tend the empty shrines. To wait."

"To wait for what?"

"For this." She reached into her satchel and drew out the fragment.

Even now, even knowing what it was, her hands shook. It was no larger than a fist, a jagged piece of pale stone shot through with veins of silver that moved — slowly, slowly, like frost forming on glass, like something breathing under ice. The firelight bent around it wrongly. The compass on Ada's knee chattered against its case.

"This broke loose from the great stone under the Chapel of the Drowned Tide in Bellharbor," she said. "Three days ago. When the child came up out of the harbor water."

* * *

She told him about the child then. She made herself tell it whole, without the softening liturgy, because he deserved the truth and because saying it plainly might help her understand it, and she did not understand it, and the not-understanding was a stone of its own sitting on her chest.

A fisherman's daughter, four years old, slipped from the quay at dusk. Forty minutes in the black water. Pulled out cold and still, lips grey, and Sera had been called because someone remembered the old chapel nearby still had a priestess, poor mad Sera Ilyen who prayed to nothing at an empty altar.

"I did what we do," she said. "What we *did*. The offices for the dead. Words of committal. And somewhere in the middle of the words I touched her forehead, and I prayed — not the office, not the liturgy, a *prayer*, the kind we're taught is vanity, the kind that presumes an audience — and the stone under the chapel floor *answered*."

"How?"

"It got warm. It sang — no. It *hummed*, low, like a struck bell that never stops. And the child breathed." Ada had stopped eating. "Not revived. Not coughed up the water and wept, the way the stories say. She *breathed*, in, out, steady, while still cold, while still grey, like the sea had agreed to give her back and was slow about honoring the terms. And then warmth came into her the way tide comes into a channel, gradual, total, and she opened her eyes and asked for her mother, and the crowd—" 

Her voice caught. She let it.

"The crowd went mad. Joy-mad. They sang. They tore their clothes. They pressed forward to touch the stone and weep and beg. And I stood there with the child in my arms feeling the most terrible thing I have ever felt in a life spent around terrible feelings."

"What?"

"That it was *wrong*. Not false — wrong. Like a voice doing an impression of a voice you love. The cadence was right. The warmth was real. The child lived; I'll swear that on anything. But underneath the miracle there was a note I couldn't name, and the note was hunger. Do you understand? It felt like being given bread by something that was watching how I chewed."

Ada said, quietly, "And then the soldiers came."

"And then the soldiers came. Because joy makes noise, and noise reaches the ears of men who profit from silence. Veyran's inquisitors burned the chapel by morning. I went back for the stone — the great stone cracked when the child breathed, cracked like ice on a thawing pond — and this is what I carried out." She turned the fragment in the firelight. The silver veins pulsed. "Eleven years I served an empty altar and called it faith. Eleven years of *waiting*. And now the waiting is over and everything in me says run."

* * *

The fire had settled to coals. Outside, full dark, and the wind had changed — come around from the north, carrying a smell Sera couldn't place at first, and then could: wet stone. Stone after rain. Petrichor, the old texts called the smell of the veil's near side. She had smelled it only twice before. Once at her ordination. Once at the chapel, in the moment before the child breathed.

It should not be smelling of petrichor in the high Thornfells in deep winter.

"Here is what frightens me," she said, because Ada was watching her with the patience of a man used to waiting out weather. "Not that the gods have returned. That would be terror enough, but terror of a clean kind — awe, judgment, the old arithmetic of sin and grace. What frightens me is the *manner* of it. Listen to what the returning looks like. A drowned child breathes — but so, they say, did a mule in the Saltmarsh villages last month, and the beast has not slept since, stands in its field all night facing east. A woman in Greyford prayed for rain and got rain — three days of it, a flood, seven drowned, and the woman has not spoken since. Prayers are being answered, cartographer. But they are being answered the way a drunkard answers a door. Too fast. Too much. Without asking who knocks or what they need."

"You think it isn't the Nine."

"I think the Nine kept the covenant for ten thousand years and broke their silence only when the covenant broke past mending. I think a god's attention is the most expensive thing in creation, and what I felt in that chapel was *cheap*. Eager. Grateful to be asked." Her hands closed around the fragment, and its warmth soaked into her palms, and she hated that the warmth was comforting, hated that some starving part of her leaned into it like a child to a hearth. "The texts say the veil was wounded, not destroyed. Wounds heal wrong if the flesh around them is corrupt. Something may have grown into the wound. Something wearing the old voices the way a crow wears the feathers of a hawk it killed."

Ada was silent. Then, in a voice carefully stripped of expression: "Someone is feeding it."

The words dropped into the room like a stone into a well. Sera stared at him. "Say that again."

"Your hungry god. Your thing behind the veil. Miracles cost something — you said yourself, attention, power, *expense*. Nothing answers prayers for free, not even gods. Especially not gods. So either it's spending down some hoard, which ends, or someone is feeding it. Keeping it fat. Keeping it answering." He turned the compass over in his fingers. "I'm a mapmaker, priestess. I deal in causes and routes. Every road has two ends."

She wanted to laugh. She wanted to weep. A smuggler's guide, a debt-ridden cartographer with sawdust on his sleeves, had taken her half-formed dread and given it a shape in six sentences, and the shape was worse than the dread. Because if someone was feeding the thing behind the veil — feeding it deliberately, patiently, with intent — then the miracles were not a return but a *lure*. Bait scattered on waters. And the fish being drawn in were everyone who had ever knelt at an empty altar and meant it.

Every prayer answered was a hook set.

"Veyran," she whispered. "The inquisitors burned the chapel but they came for *me*, for the fragment. They knew what to look for. Someone told them what the stones mean. Someone at court understands the covenant is reforgable — and understands that whoever holds the stones when it reforges..." She stopped. The next thought arrived with a physical sensation, cold sliding down her spine like meltwater. "Whoever feeds it controls it. Whoever controls it doesn't need armies. Armies are for men who can't buy gods."

The coals ticked. Somewhere far below them, faint as a pulse through a wrist, the mountain hummed.

Ada rose, went to the shuttered window, and eased it open a hand's width. Cold poured in, and with it the smell — stronger now, unmistakable, the sweet mineral breath of the near side of the veil — and with it, distantly, from the direction of the pass they'd fled that morning, a sound neither of them could name and neither of them forgot: a low, vast exhalation, like something on the far side of the world turning over in its sleep.

Or waking.

Sera wrapped the fragment in wool and bound it with cord, three knots, the old way, the way the texts prescribed for carrying dangerous holiness. Her hands were steady. That surprised her. Perhaps dread, fully arrived, was steadier than doubt.

"Tomorrow we go north," she said. "There's a second stone. The drowned chapel's records named it — the Marshstone, under the saltmarshes, in a chapel the sea took two hundred years ago. If someone is gathering the stones—"

"Then we find it first." Ada closed the shutter. In the dark his face was all planes and shadow, a face she was beginning to read the way she read scripture, line by line, finding more beneath than the surface promised. "I know the marsh roads. Nobody else alive does."

"No," she agreed softly. "But something else is learning them."

She banked the fire. She lay down on the cold flagstones with her satchel under her head, and sleep came for her at last, shallow and salt-tasting, full of a bell that would not stop ringing and a voice beneath the water saying, gently, patiently, in the cadence of every god she had ever loved:

*Ask me. Ask me. Ask.*
