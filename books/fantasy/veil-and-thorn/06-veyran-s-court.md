## 6. Veyran's Court

The palace at Vhalcrest had been built by kings who believed in permanence, and so it was fitting that a usurper lived there now, wearing its stones like stolen clothes.

Corvane felt the irony of it as he climbed the long stair to the audience chamber, each step worn hollow by centuries of feet that had once belonged to men who ruled by right and not by appetite. He had been summoned an hour past midnight, which meant either urgency or theater. With Veyran it was always both.

The guards at the doors did not search him again. They had searched him twice already, at the outer gate and the inner, their hands thorough and impersonal as butchers' hands, and now they simply looked through him, the way one looks through furniture. That was the thing about power that no book taught: it did not need to touch you after a certain point. It only needed to know where you stood.

The doors opened on a room lit by a single brazier.

Veyran sat with his back to the fire, so that his face was all shadow and his outline burned — a man reduced to silhouette, which Corvane suspected was deliberate. The usurper had learned early that faces invited judgment and silhouettes invited fear. Behind him, the great windows showed the capital spread out below like embers raked across black cloth, ten thousand hearth-fires and torch-lit streets, all of it his, none of it enough. Corvane could feel the hunger coming off him even before he spoke, a heat like standing too near a forge.

"Master Corvane." Veyran's voice was soft. It was always soft. Men who shouted were men who had not yet learned what volume could do. "You come quickly at night. I find that interesting."

"I come quickly whenever I'm summoned, Majesty. Night is when scholars work best anyway. Fewer interruptions."

"Do you joke to me?" No anger in it. Curiosity, almost gentle, the way a man might be curious about a moth's patterns against a lamp. "Sit."

There was one chair. Of course there was one chair; Veyran never offered equality, only proximity. Corvane took it, arranging his robes with the care of long habit, and folded his hands over his knee, and waited. Waiting was a discipline he had practiced for forty years — waiting through other men's tempers, other men's suspicions, other men's questions — and he had grown very good at making patience look like calm rather than calculation.

Veyran let the silence stretch. Then he rose, crossed to a table against the wall, and lifted from it a single sheet of paper.

"You wrote me a list," he said. "Names of bloodlines. Descendants of the covenant-keepers. You told me it was superstition, mostly. Dying families with dying claims. You told me the old gods were dead or dreaming and their pacts were dust." He turned the paper over, studying the blank side as though the truth might be written there. "And yet three days ago, in Bellharbor, a drowned child breathed again in front of two hundred witnesses. And last week a shrine stone in the Thornfell passes sang under a traveler's hand. My reports are very thorough, Master Corvane. Are yours?"

"The gods waking is not the same as the gods returning," Corvane said carefully. "A body can twitch after death. The physicians call it—"

"Do not lecture me on corpses." Still soft. Still almost kind. "I have made enough of them to know how they move."

He came back toward the fire, and for a moment his face came into the light, and Corvane saw what the reports did not say: that the usurper had not slept well in weeks. There were shadows under his eyes like bruises, and a fine tremor in his left hand that he stilled by pressing it flat against his thigh. Something had gotten into Veyran's dreams. Corvane had seen that look before, in older men, in desperate men, in the eyes of pilgrims who had prayed too hard at shrines that answered too readily. Hunger that had begun to feed on its host.

"You knew," Veyran said. "You have always known. Not superstition — *mechanism*. The covenant. How it was made. What it costs. Who can carry it." He dropped the list onto the table between them. "So we will stop pretending, you and I, and you will tell me everything. Beginning with why your own name is not on this list."

The question landed exactly where Veyran had aimed it, and Corvane admired the aim even as he felt the blade go in. He had kept his name off the list for thirty years. He had kept a great many things off lists.

"My line ended generations ago," he said. "The blood thinned. Whatever the covenant-keepers carried, my family spent it or lost it or married it away. I am a historian of the pact, Majesty, not a vessel of it."

"Mm." Veyran studied him. "And yet you knew which names to write down. You knew the Joubert line mattered most of all. You knew it so well that you put it at the top, above families with more land, more history, more sons." He leaned forward, and the firelight found one eye, dark and patient. "You did not learn that from books."

*He knows.* Corvane kept his breathing even, kept his hands loose, kept the scholar's mild expression fixed in place like a mask nailed to a skull. But beneath it, the old machinery began to turn — the weighing, the bargaining, the cold arithmetic that had governed every choice of his life and which he had always, always called prudence.

"There is a boy," he said at last. "A student of mine, once. Ada Joubert. I had him educated. Watched. His mother fled the mountains before he could walk, and I let her run, because a hidden thing stays hidden better than a guarded one. I signed the denunciation because hiding him had stopped being possible — your inquisitors were already pulling threads I could not cut. Better I held the thread than someone else."

"And where is the thread now?"

"Gone to ground. Fleeing north, if my information holds. He has taken something from his workshop — papers, maps. He does not yet understand what he carries."

"What does he carry?"

Corvane rose, slowly, and went to the window. Below, the capital breathed its smoky breath. Somewhere out there, past the saltmarshes and the free cities and the whole width of the world, a young man with ink-stained fingers was running through mountains that had begun to remember him. Corvane had taught him to read contour lines. Had taught him that a map was a promise the land makes to itself. Had taught him, without ever saying it, that love was a debt you paid forward to people who would never know they owed it.

He had also sold him. He needed to be honest with himself about that, at least in the privacy of his own skull. He had weighed a boy's life against his own and found the arithmetic satisfactory, and then he had dressed the arithmetic in the language of necessity, which is what clever men do instead of weeping.

"He carries the covenant's last thread," Corvane said. "The bloodline bred to renew the pact. And I will tell you the mechanism, Majesty, since you've asked so nicely, and since you will torture it out of me otherwise and waste both our evenings." He turned back. "The covenant was forged at four anchor points — four stones, set where the veil between realms wears thin. Three remain in the world. The fourth passed beyond the veil when the pact broke. Whoever gathers the stones and stands at the thin place can reforge what was broken. The pact requires a life poured into the veil. That is the cost. That is always the cost."

"And the one who pours it?" Veyran's voice had changed. Softer still. Hungrier. "What becomes of him?"

"That depends on how it is done. Done as the ancients did it, the giver dies and the veil holds for another age. But the texts describe... another reading. A vessel-reading. If the reforging is performed with the stones bound to the giver rather than given up—" Corvane spread his hands, the gesture of a man offering a gift he had wrapped himself. "Then the power does not pass into the veil. It pools in the giver. All of it. The covenant's strength, the gods' lent authority, the whole architecture of the pact — held in one living body. A man who cannot be unmade while the veil stands. A man the old gods must obey, because he holds their leash."

The silence that followed had weight, like deep water.

Veyran had gone very still. Corvane watched the idea take root behind those bruised eyes, watched it flower, watched the usurper's whole posture change — the tremor in his hand forgotten, the exhaustion burned away, replaced by something bright and terrible and utterly certain. This was what Corvane had come to give him. Not information. *Direction.* Because a hungry man pointed at a feast forgets to ask who cooked it.

"You believe this reading is true," Veyran said.

"I believe it is *possible*. Which in my experience is worth more than true."

"And the boy? The thread? Can any blood perform this vessel-reforging, or only his?"

"Only his. The stones answer the covenant-blood. Anyone else who touches them holds pretty rocks." Corvane smiled thinly. "Which brings us to the matter of price."

"Price." Veyran laughed — a short, genuine sound, surprised out of him. "You dare name a price to me."

"I dare because I'm the only map you have. Kill me and you'll spend years digging through burnt archives for what I can tell you in an evening. And my price is simple." He let the pause breathe. "The boy comes to you alive. Unharmed. Your inquisitors have a talent for breaking things they're sent to fetch, and broken vessels hold nothing. Deliver him intact — deliver him *trusting*, if you can manage it — and when the reforging is done, when you stand at the thin place and take what's owed to you, I want three things. My archives restored. My name cleared. And passage out of your empire, anywhere I choose, with whatever I can carry."

"You want to leave," Veyran said. "After."

"After is a country I'll consider when I reach it. Perhaps I'll stay. Perhaps I'll want to see what a god-emperor looks like up close." Corvane shrugged. "I'm a scholar. Curiosity is my disease."

Veyran walked to the table. He picked up the list — Corvane's list, thirty years of careful scholarship compressed into a column of names — and held it over the brazier. For a moment Corvane thought he would burn it, some theatrical gesture of contempt, and he braced himself for the loss of it.

Instead Veyran set it down, unburned, and smoothed it flat with one palm.

"Your terms are accepted," he said. "The boy alive. Trusting, if possible — I'm told trust makes the blood easier to work with. In exchange, your freedom and your clean name." He looked up, and now both eyes caught the firelight, and Corvane saw that there was nothing behind them anymore except the idea that had just been planted there, growing, branching, putting out roots through every chamber of the man's mind. "You will send word of his route. You will tell my hunters where the roads bend and where the wayshrines lie. And Master Corvane—" the softness returned, the terrible gentleness, "—when he is brought to me, you will be there. To confirm the blood. I find I trust your eye."

"Of course, Majesty."

"One more question." Veyran tilted his head. "This vessel-reading. When the power pools in the giver — what happens to everyone else who touched the stones along the way? The priestess in Bellharbor, perhaps. Any hands that helped carry them."

Corvane considered lying. He was, genuinely, briefly, tempted. But lies to Veyran were seeds planted in soil that tested everything, and the truth here served him better anyway.

"Burned away," he said. "The covenant takes back what it lends. Anyone who handled the stones in service of the reforging gives up whatever claim they might have had — health, years, luck. The old texts call it *the tithe of hands*. It's why the covenant-keepers worked alone. Why they died alone."

"Good," said Veyran, and the word fell into the room like a stone into a well, and Corvane heard the depth of it, the long fall before any bottom. "Then there will be no one to share it with. No priests. No councils. No free cities full of people who think miracles belong to them." He turned back to the window, to the ember-scatter of his capital, and Corvane watched his shoulders rise and fall once, slow, like a man savoring a smell. "Go. Send your first message within the day. And Corvane — pray for me tonight. I'm told the gods are listening again. I intend to give them something worth listening *to*."

The doors closed behind him. The stairs descended. The night air of Vhalcrest hit his face like a slap, coal-smoke and river damp, and Corvane stood a moment in the courtyard breathing it, an old man with clean hands and a filthy ledger, telling himself the story he had told himself for forty years:

*It was necessary. Someone had to steer this. Better me than a fanatic. The boy is resourceful; the boy may yet slip them; and if he doesn't, then his death buys order, and order buys lives, and lives outweigh a life.*

The story fit well. It had fitted for decades. It was only lately, only since the denunciation, only since he had written *Ada Joubert* at the top of a list with a steady hand, that the story had begun to chafe — to sit wrong on his shoulders like a coat tailored for a thinner man.

He pushed the feeling down where he kept everything else, and went to write his letter.

* * *

The message went north by rider before dawn: three lines, ciphered, naming the smuggler roads out of the Thornfells and the wayshrine at the marsh crossing where a fugitive cartographer might reasonably shelter. Corvane sealed it with his own ring and handed it to a courier whose face he did not look at, because looking at faces had become difficult lately.

Then he climbed to his study, alone, and sat among his books in the dark without lighting a lamp.

On the desk lay a letter he had drafted eleven times and never sent — addressed to a workshop in Bellharbor's lower city, beginning *My boy,* and continuing nowhere. Eleven drafts. Eleven failures. He had told himself there was no safe way to warn him, which was true, and also that warning him would accomplish nothing, which was a lie, and the difference between those two sentences was the whole of his soul, laid out on a desk in the dark.

He took out fresh paper. He dipped the pen. He wrote:

*Ada. Listen to me. Do not go to the marshes. Do not trust the roads to keep you. Veyran knows what you are, and worse — he knows what you're for. Run west, run to sea, run anywhere the stones aren't, and do not—*

The pen stopped.

Because he heard it, faintly, from beyond the study window — from the direction of the palace, from the captured shrine two streets over that Veyran's soldiers had desecrated and claimed — a sound like a voice under water. Like something vast turning over in its sleep and murmuring a word it had been saving.

And the word, though no tongue Corvane knew possessed letters for it, resolved in his mind with perfect, sickening clarity:

*Soon.*

The pen slipped from his fingers and left a long ink-wound across the page, through *do not*, through *run*, through everything he had tried to say. Corvane sat looking at the ruined letter, at the black line cutting his warning in half, and understood suddenly, with a scholar's precision and a father's horror, that the thing he had bargained with was not only Veyran. That appetite had been listening at the door of this conversation, taking notes. That somewhere in the dark between realms, something had just been promised a meal — and that he had drawn up the menu.

He burned the letter. He watched the eleven drafts' successor curl and blacken in the dish, *my boy, my boy, my boy* flaring gold and gone.

Then he rang for a courier, and gave him the first message after all, and did not pray, because he had finally understood that prayer was a door, and doors, tonight, opened inward.
