## 8. Hollow Prayers

The saltmarsh at dusk was a country of drowned bells.

Sera Ilyen had learned its moods in three days of walking: the way the tide came in without wind, sliding over the flats like a hand smoothing a bedsheet; the way the light went amber and then bruise-purple and then nothing; the way sound carried — a cough half a mile off, a whispered prayer from the next fire. The pilgrims' fires burned low and greenish in the wet air, and around each one sat people who had come seeking miracles, and people who had already found them.

It was the second kind she watched.

They were easy to pick out now that she knew what to look for. They ate, but slowly, as if remembering how. They spoke when spoken to, in voices pitched exactly right and yet somehow wrong, like instruments tuned by someone who had only ever heard music described. And their eyes — their eyes had a stillness to them, a patience no living person owned. They looked at you the way a well looks back.

The man they called Tammas had been healed on the first night out of Bellharbor. His fever had broken before a hundred witnesses; his lungs, ruined by the foundry smoke he'd breathed for twenty years, had opened like windows. He had wept and kissed the mud and sworn himself to the gods who'd done it. Now he sat apart from every fire, facing east, whispering.

Sera had been a priestess of the Vigil for nineteen years. She knew what prayer sounded like in a human mouth — the bargaining, the gratitude, the fear dressed up as praise. What came out of Tammas was none of those things. It had the shape of prayer the way a glove has the shape of a hand: something had been inside it, and had left, and the shape remained.

She crossed the trampled sedge toward him, her sandals sinking with each step, the smell of sulfur and rot-thick mud rising around her ankles. Behind her, Ada Joubert watched from the fire's edge. He'd been watching all day, sketching in his book — not the marsh, not the pilgrims, but the pattern of them, the geometry of answered prayers. She had seen his map that afternoon: a scatter of marks across the flats, each one a miracle, and when he'd drawn a line through them her stomach had dropped, because the line ran straight and true toward the heart of the marsh, toward whatever waited there.

*Toward the second stone,* he'd said. *Or toward the thing that wants us to think it's the second stone.*

Tammas did not turn when she stopped beside him. The eastern sky was going the color of an old scar.

"Brother," she said gently. "You should eat. The soup is thin, but it's hot."

He smiled. That was the worst part — the smile arrived a half-second too late, like a messenger who had gotten lost between the feeling and the face. "I have eaten," he said. "I am full."

"Of what?"

"Of *listening*." He said it with reverence. "Sister, do you know what it is like, to be heard? Truly heard? All my life I prayed into a silence so vast I thought it was the whole of heaven. And then—" his hand rose, trembling, and pressed against his own chest, "—and then the silence opened, and something looked *out*."

Sera knelt in the mud beside him, because you knelt to the sick and the blessed both, and she no longer knew which he was. "What does it say to you?"

"It doesn't say." Tammas turned his head at last, and in the failing light his eyes caught the green of the distant fires and held it. "It *remembers* at me. It remembers at me until I remember too. It says: *we were*." His voice dropped to a hush. "It says: *we are still*. It says: *the door was shut, but doors are only wood, sister, and wood burns*."

A cold moved through her that had nothing to do with the marsh wind.

"In whose name?" she asked. "When you pray — whose name do you call?"

"The old names." He recited them, and they were the true names, the ones from the liturgies, the ones Sera had spoken over baptisms and funerals and the small daily offices of her life: *Vael who keeps the threshold. Moruath of the deep water. The Ninefold Choir. The Shepherd of Roads.* Each name landed in her like a stone dropped down a well, and each one fell wrong.

Because she had felt Vael's presence once — truly felt it, years ago, in the shrine at Bellharbor during the night vigil, a presence like a hand laid flat against the world. It had been *warm*. Whatever spoke through Tammas was warm the way a fever is warm.

"You love them," she said. "The ones who healed you."

"I love being healed," Tammas said, with terrible simplicity. "The rest is — arrangement."

* * *

She walked out onto the flats after full dark, because she could not pray among the fires, and she could not sleep, and the truth was pressing against the inside of her skull like a tooth gone bad.

The marsh at night was a black mirror. The sky lay upon the ground so perfectly that walking felt like wading through stars, and the only true lights were the pilgrim fires behind her and, far ahead, a pale glow rising from the deep marsh where the drowned chapel stood — or where the thing wearing the chapel stood. She could not tell anymore which was which. That was the sickness of it. Three days ago she had believed in a clean architecture: gods above, veil between, mortals below, and the covenant like a keystone holding the arch. Her whole life had been built under that arch. Her ordination vows. Her years sweeping the shrine steps. The night she'd pulled a drowned child back from death with light pouring out of a broken stone and heard, for the first time in three centuries, a god answer *yes*.

Now she stood on a field of reflected stars and understood that she might have been looking at a painted ceiling all along.

"*Vael,*" she said aloud, to the dark, to the marsh, to whatever listened. Her voice sounded very small. "If you are there. If any of you are there. Answer me."

Nothing. The wind moved the sedge. Somewhere a marsh bird made its sad wooden cry.

"I have kept your vigils since I was nine years old," she said, and her voice cracked and she let it. "I gave you my name, my hands, my nights. I taught children your stories. I buried the dead in your peace. And now something answers prayers in this place, and heals fevers, and opens drowned lungs, and everything it touches comes away *hollow*, and I need to know — I need to know if it is you. If this is what you've become. If the silence broke you." She pressed her fist against her mouth. "Or if it was never you at all, and I have spent my life speaking to an empty room, and something else has moved into the house."

The marsh held its breath.

And then — not a voice. Not anything so kind. A pressure, like the change before a storm, like the moment a wave draws back from the shore and shows you the bones of the seabed. The reflected stars shivered. The mud beneath her feet hummed, a low note she felt in her teeth, in the roots of her teeth, in the marrow behind them.

*We were,* said the marsh, in no voice at all. *We are still.*

Sera's knees nearly folded. It was the phrase from Tammas, but unfiltered now, raw, vast, and underneath the words she felt the speaker — an attention the size of weather, turning toward her the way a great eye turns.

"*Who are you?*" she whispered.

*We are the ones you named.* The warmth again, the fever-warmth, wrapping her like a blanket soaked in something sweet. *We are Vael. We are the Choir. We are the Shepherd. We hear you, daughter. We have always heard you. Come to the chapel. Bring the bearer. Bring the stones. We will show you such mercy—*

"No." The word tore out of her before thought. "No. Vael keeps the threshold. Vael would never cross it. Vael *is* the crossing, the keeping, the — you're using his name like a coat—"

The attention flared. For one instant the sweetness burned away and she saw what stood behind the borrowed names, and it was not a god. It was not even a shape. It was a hunger given grammar, a want so old and total that it had worn through the veil the way water wears through stone, and it had found the scattered, caged, diminished remnants of the true gods on the other side and it had *learned* them, the way a mimic learns a songbird — note perfect, soul empty.

*Daughter,* it said, gentle as rot. *All names are coats. All doors are wood.*

Then the pressure withdrew, sudden as a hand lifted, and the marsh was only a marsh, black and smelling of sulfur, and Sera Ilyen was on her knees in the mud with tears freezing on her cheeks and the entire architecture of her life lying in ruins around her.

* * *

Ada found her there. Of course he did; he seemed to find everything, moving through the world like a man reading a text no one else could see. He said nothing at first. He crouched at the edge of the star-field of reflected light, just outside the circle of disturbed mud where she knelt, and waited, the way you wait by a sickbed.

"It spoke to me," she said finally. Her voice was wrecked, scraped hollow. "Out there. It used the names. It used *all* the names, Ada, perfectly, and it isn't them. It's — imagine a cage of songbirds, and something outside the cage learning every note, and then imagine the birds stop singing, one by one, and the thing keeps singing anyway, louder, because it wants the door opened. That's what's answering the prayers. That's what healed Tammas." She laughed, and the laugh came out broken. "Three centuries of silence, and the first voice to answer is a *mimic*."

Ada lowered himself into the mud beside her, unbothered by the wet, and studied the far glow of the chapel. "The real gods," he said quietly. "Where are they?"

"That's the question, isn't it." She wiped her face with a muddy hand and didn't care. "The covenant bound them to the other side of the veil — that was the bargain, that was the *whole point*: they lend power through the stones, we keep the boundary sealed so nothing crosses either way. When the covenant broke—" She stopped. Started again, slower, because saying it plainly made it real. "When the covenant broke, the door didn't just close. From what it showed me — from what it *let slip* — the breaking trapped them. Scattered them. Caged them in the ruins of their own law. They've been silent for three hundred years not because they abandoned us, but because they *couldn't reach us*. And the thing on the other side has been pressed against the veil ever since, licking through the cracks, learning our prayers, waiting for someone to feed it enough to push."

"And someone is feeding it." Ada's jaw tightened. "Veyran."

"Maybe. Maybe Veyran thinks he's commanding it. Things like that don't get commanded." She shook her head. "But it doesn't matter tonight. Tonight I learned that every prayer I've ever prayed went into a sealed room. That the child I saved — the light I felt — that was real, some fragment of the true stones, the true power, leaking through. But the *voice* everyone's hearing now, the miracles in the marsh—" her breath hitched, "—it's a lure. It heals you and hollows you and walks you toward the chapel, and I have led a hundred pilgrims within a mile of it."

"You'll lead them away tomorrow."

"With what authority?" The bitterness surprised her. "My faith is the ground I stand on, Ada, and the ground is *gone*. Nineteen years. My vows, my order, my god — and the first time I ask a direct question, something answers wearing my god's face and offers me mercy in a voice like a fever. How do I lead anyone anywhere after that? How do I kneel again? How do I know that anything I ever felt in the shrine wasn't—" She couldn't finish.

Ada was quiet for a long moment. Then he took out his compass — the strange brass instrument he guarded like a relic, the one that had screamed in resonance against Sera's stone fragment the day they met — and set it on his open palm between them. The needle swung, steadied, and pointed not north but east, toward the chapel-glow, quivering like a living thing.

"Because the false things imitate," he said. "That's all they can do. Which means somewhere, faint and far and locked behind a broken door, the true note still exists — or the mimic would have nothing to copy." He looked at her, and his eyes in the starlight were tired and certain at once. "Your faith isn't the ground, Sera. It never was. It's the map. Maps aren't the territory — they're just the best honest drawing of it we can make. Yours got torn. So we redraw it. That's all faith ever was."

She stared at him. "You talk like a priest for a smuggler."

"I talk like a cartographer. Same disease." Almost, he smiled. Then the smile faded, and he turned the compass in his fingers, and his voice dropped. "The needle's getting worse. Stronger. Whatever's in that chapel knows we're coming — it told you to bring 'the bearer.' It knows about the stones. It knows about *me*." He closed his fist around the brass. "Which means tomorrow we walk into the deepest part of the marsh, into its open mouth, and take the second stone out of its throat."

"Yes," Sera said.

And she found, standing up out of the ruined mud of her belief, that her legs held. That beneath the grief and the fracture and the nineteen fallen years, one thing remained untouched, simple and load-bearing as a foundation stone: *whatever is true, I will look at it.* Even if it wore her god's face. Even if it emptied her. The Vigil had never meant keeping watch for comfortable things.

Behind them, at the far edge of the pilgrim camp, Tammas rose from his fire and began to walk east, unhurried, barefoot, toward the pale glow — and as he went, Sera saw that he was not alone. One by one, silent as a tide, the hollow-eyed ones were rising from their fires and falling into line behind him, a procession forming itself in the dark, moving toward the chapel ahead of any command.

Toward the door.

"They're going early," Ada breathed. "Why are they going *now*—"

The marsh-glow pulsed once, slow and satisfied, like a throat swallowing.

And in the silence that followed, every bell in the drowned chapel — bells sunk three fathoms deep, bells that had not rung since the chapel went under — began, together, to toll.
