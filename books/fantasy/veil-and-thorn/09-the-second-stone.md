## 9. The Second Stone

The marsh did not want them there.

Ada had mapped enough wetlands to know the difference between ground that merely resisted a traveler and ground that refused him, and the saltmarsh south of Veyran's occupied coast was the second kind. Every step sank past the ankle into peat that exhaled a cold breath of brine and rot. The reeds grew in patterns that made no sense — not clumped as water would gather them, but arranged in long, deliberate lines, like script written by a hand too large to see. When the wind moved through them they did not rustle so much as whisper, a dry sibilance that Ada had spent three days telling himself was only wind.

Sera walked ahead of him with her hood back despite the drizzle, her stone fragment hanging at her throat where it had hung since Bellharbor. It had changed since the wayshrine. In the city it had been dull as river glass; now it caught light that wasn't there, throwing faint violet glimmers across her collarbone even under cloud cover. She said it didn't hurt anymore. She said it *sang*. Ada had stopped believing everything Sera Ilyen said about pain somewhere around the third night, when he'd woken to find her sitting upright in the dark, gripping the fragment, tears running silently down her face while she smiled.

"The chapel is close," she said now, without turning. "I can feel it pulling."

"Chapels don't pull."

"This one does." She pointed with her chin toward a stand of dead alders leaning out of the water like drowned men frozen mid-scream. "The pilgrims' line bends there. You drew it yourself."

He had. Three days of mapping answered prayers — the healed man with hollow eyes, the woman whose arthritis vanished along with her memory of her daughter's name, the child who spoke backward — and when he'd laid the points on his chart and connected them, the line hadn't scattered as miracle should. It had bent, curved, converged, all of it funneling toward a low rise half-drowned at the marsh's heart where old maps showed nothing at all except, on one crumbling survey from before the Silence, a notation in faded sepia: *chapel, submerged*.

The tide was going out. That was their window. At low water, the chapel's roof broke the surface of a shallow lagoon — black ribs of ancient slate furred with weed, and between them a gap where a roof-tile had fallen away centuries ago, opening onto darkness below.

They waded the last hundred yards through knee-deep water that steamed faintly where it touched Sera's fragment. Ada kept his compass in his hand the whole way. Since the wayshrine it had been useless for north — the needle spun lazily, drunk — but useful for something else: it strained toward divine things the way iron strains toward a lodestone, and right now it pulled downward and ahead with an insistence that made his wrist ache.

At the roof-gap, Sera went first. She was smaller than she looked, folding herself through the broken tile with a grace that seemed borrowed from somewhere older than her body, and then she was gone, down into the dark, and Ada counted ten heartbeats before her voice floated up, echoing strangely: "There are stairs. Come down. Bring your light."

He descended into the drowned chapel feet-first, lamp held high, and the world below the waterline opened around him like the inside of a bell.

The flood had taken only the lowest course of stones. Above the high-water mark the interior stood dry, preserved by salt and silence — a single vaulted nave no longer than a fishing boat, its walls crowded with carvings worn nearly smooth. Salt crystals hung from the vaulting like stalactites of glass. And at the far end, where an altar should have been, something sat in a niche of carved hands — dozens of stone hands, cupped together, holding up—

The compass in Ada's fist slammed flat against its housing so hard it cracked the glass.

"There," Sera breathed.

It was smaller than he'd expected. Smaller than a fist, a rough ovoid of grey stone shot through with veins of violet light, pulsing slowly, steadily, like the heart of some sleeping animal. It sat in the cradle of those carved hands the way a child sits in its mother's arms, and the air around it was warm, and it smelled — impossibly, in this tomb of brine — of woodsmoke and bread.

"That's the smell of my mother's kitchen," Ada said, and hated how his voice shook.

"The stones remember what people prayed for," Sera whispered. She had gone very still, the way she went still before miracles. "Home. Safety. Someone keeping the fire lit." She turned to him, and in the lamplight her eyes were wet. "This is what they were. Not weapons. Not crowns. *Hearths.*"

Ada approached the altar slowly. The nearer he came, the more the violet veins brightened, and the warmer the air grew, and the stronger the smell became until his eyes stung with it, until he could almost hear a kettle singing, almost hear a woman humming —

His mother's tune. The one she'd hummed while sharpening her cartography pens, the one he'd never heard anywhere else, the one he'd assumed he'd invented in memory because grief invents things.

He stopped three steps from the altar. His hand, of its own accord, had risen halfway toward the stone.

"Wait," Sera said sharply. "The texts say the bearer must—"

"I'm not the bearer," Ada said. "I'm a mapmaker who's having a very bad month."

But his hand kept rising, and the stone's light rose to meet it, veins flaring, and beneath his feet he felt the chapel hum — felt it in his teeth, in the wet bones of the drowned nave, in the water outside which had begun, very softly, to glow.

And that was when the roof tiles above the entrance shattered inward, and men dropped through the gap with drawn steel, and the lamplight found the grey-and-crimson livery of Veyran's inquisition.

* * *

There were six of them. They came down the stairs in pairs, boots splashing in the flooded transept, crossbows levelled, and behind them came a thin man in scholar's robes with an inquisitor's chain of office and a voice like dry paper.

"Ada Joubert." He pronounced the name carefully, tasting it. "Master Corvane sends his regards. He said you'd come here. He said you'd always find the old places — it's why he taught you, after all."

The words went into Ada like a knife between ribs. Corvane. Of course. Who else knew the old surveys, knew the notation about the submerged chapel, knew exactly which roads a fleeing man would trust?

"Step away from the altar," the inquisitor continued. "The usurper's grace extends even to bloodline traitors, if the bloodline cooperates. You have until I finish this sentence to decide whether your companion dies first or—"

The stone answered before Ada did.

It happened without his consent, without his thought — the way a flinch happens, the way a heartbeat happens. The soldiers nearest the altar took one step forward, and the violet veins in the covenant-stone blazed from ember to furnace-white, and every carved hand on the altar closed.

Ada heard the stone hands grind shut like portcullises. He heard the soldiers shout. He saw the light pour off the stone in a wave — not fire, not quite, but something hotter than fire and colder than ice, a radiance that passed through armor as if armor were mist — and the three men closest to the altar simply *stopped*. Not thrown. Not burned. Stopped, the way a clock stops, the way a heart stops. They folded down into the flooded floor with a gentleness that was worse than violence, their faces slack, their weapons ringing against stone.

The remaining three fired. Bolts tore the air where Ada had been standing — he wasn't standing there anymore; he was somehow against the wall, though he had no memory of moving — and the fourth soldier screamed and dropped his weapon, clutching his arm where the light had grazed him, the flesh beneath unmarked but the arm hanging dead as driftwood.

"*Stop it!*" Sera seized Ada's shoulder and spun him around, and her face was white as salt. "*Stop it, Ada, you're doing it!*"

"I'm not—" 

"You ARE!" Her voice cracked across the nave like a whip. "Look at your hands!"

He looked. Light ran beneath his skin, following the lines of his veins the way ink follows a pen, violet threading up from his fingertips toward his elbows, and where it passed, the old scar on his knuckle — the one from Corvane's workshop, the chisel slip when he was twelve — had vanished. Smoothed away. Erased.

The last two soldiers fled. He heard them thrashing up through the flooded transept, screaming for the boats, and the thin-voiced inquisitor went with them, robes streaming, and then the chapel was silent except for the drip of water and the sound of Ada Joubert's own ragged breathing.

The light under his skin dimmed. It did not go out. Even as it faded, he could feel it settled into him now, permanent, patient, a coal banked in the marrow of his arms.

Three men lay dead in the shallows. Their faces were peaceful. That was the worst part — they looked like sleeping pilgrims, like men who had finally gotten what they prayed for.

"I killed them," Ada said. His voice sounded far away, someone else's voice. "I didn't — I never touched them. I didn't even *decide*—"

"No." Sera crouched beside the nearest body and gently closed the man's eyes. When she looked up, her expression was terrible and tender at once. "The stone decided. Through you. This is what the covenant was, Ada. This is what your blood was *for*." She rose, wringing out her sleeves, and her voice steadied into something like ritual cadence. "The gods lent power to mortal keepers so the veil could be mended and maintained. Power like this. Power that answers before thought, because thought is too slow when realms are tearing." She gestured at the bodies. "And this is why it was never given lightly. Why it cost. Why it demanded—"

She stopped herself. Ada watched her stop herself, watched the sentence die behind her teeth, and understood that she knew something about what the covenant demanded that she had not yet told him.

"Sera."

"Take the stone," she said instead. "Please. Before I lose my nerve to let you."

He crossed to the altar. Up close, the covenant-stone was warm as a loaf fresh from the oven, and the humming in it matched — he pressed his palm against it and felt it match — his own pulse. The carved hands opened, finger by finger, releasing it to him, and the moment his skin closed around the stone the violet light sank inward, dimming to embers, going quiet, going *home*, and the smell of woodsmoke and bread faded to nothing.

The chapel felt colder immediately. Emptier. A tomb again.

"It knows you," Sera said quietly. "Whatever you are, whatever your mother ran from — the stone has been waiting for you specifically. For your blood." She picked her way toward the stairs, then paused, looking back at him across the bodies of the dead. "We need to talk about what the covenant asks of its keepers, Ada. Truly talk. Not on the road. Not with soldiers at our backs. But soon."

"How soon?"

"Soon enough that I wish we had longer." She climbed into the grey daylight, and Ada followed, the second stone heavy in his satchel against his spine, heavier than any map he'd ever carried.

* * *

They rowed away from the drowned chapel as the tide turned, the black ribs of its roof sinking behind them inch by inch until only the weathervane of the old steeple broke the surface — an iron bird, corroded wingless, pointing eternally at nothing.

Ada sat in the stern with the stone in his lap and his charts spread across his knees, and he did not look at either of them. He looked at his hands.

The light was gone from his veins, but the memory of it wasn't. He kept flexing his fingers, waiting for the numbness to fade, and it didn't, because it wasn't numbness. It was *room*. As if something vast had moved into a house he'd thought was small, and was rearranging the furniture, and was very quiet, and was not leaving.

"Your scar," Sera said from the bow, not turning around. "The one on your hand. It's gone, isn't it."

"Yes."

"My order had a word for that. From the old texts. *Anointed* — no. Older than that." She trailed off, and the marsh whispered around them, and the dead alders leaned past like witnesses refusing testimony.

Ada unfolded the impossible map — the one from his workshop, the chart of the realm that shouldn't exist — and laid it over his charts of the marsh. He'd done this every night since fleeing Bellharbor, and every night the overlays had shown him something new, and every night he'd told himself it meant nothing.

Tonight the lines matched perfectly. The route of answered prayers through the saltmarsh, the bend toward the drowned chapel, the position of the second stone — all of it reproduced, stroke for stroke, a section of the other realm's geography printed in exact miniature upon the saltmarshes of his own. As if the two worlds weren't neighbors. As if one was drawn *on top of* the other, and in places the ink was bleeding through.

And at the center of the matching lines, where the chapel stood in both worlds at once, the impossible map bore a notation in a hand Ada recognized with a sick lurch of his stomach.

His mother's hand. Her careful cartographer's lettering, the same letters she'd used to label his childhood drawings of mountains:

*Here the keeper's road begins. Here the price is named.*

Below it, smaller, hurried, as if written by someone running:

*If you are reading this, I failed. Do not go to the Thornfells. Whatever answers you, do not—*

The rest was lost, the vellum torn away long ago, ragged edge trailing off into blankness like a scream cut short.

Ada read it four times. Then he folded the map along its old creases, very carefully, the way he folded all his maps, and put it away, and did not tell Sera what it said.

Because the Thornfells were where they were already going. Because the third stone waited there, in territory Veyran held, inside the mountains that had hummed beneath his hand at a smuggler's pass on the day the world began to notice him. Because his mother had run from those mountains, and maps — his whole life, every map he'd ever drawn — had been leading him back.

In the bow, Sera's fragment pulsed once, twice, in rhythm with the stone against his spine. Two hearts learning each other's beat.

Ahead, the marsh gave way to hard ground, and beyond the hard ground, blue and jagged and patient, the Thornfells stood against the horizon like a wall built by something that intended to be climbed.

Ada's hands, on the oars, were steady. He made himself watch them be steady.

Inside his marrow, something vast turned over in its sleep, and dreamed, and the dream smelled of woodsmoke and bread.
