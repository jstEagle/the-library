## 10. Corvane's Mercy

The rain had followed Corvane for three days, as if the sky itself had taken Veyran's coin and now trailed him like a hired informer. It came down in gray sheets over the high moorland north of the saltmarshes, filling the peat hollows, drowning the heather, turning every track into a brown vein of water. Corvane rode through it with his hood drawn low and his hands numb on the reins, and he told himself — for the hundredth time, for the thousandth — that what he did was mercy.

He had left the inquisitors' column at dusk, alone, with a story about scouting the pilgrim road ahead. Captain Dreyse had not believed him; Dreyse believed nothing that was not written down and countersigned. But Dreyse also knew whose signature sat at the top of the denunciation list, and so he had let the old man ride into the wet dark without argument.

Corvane found the cold camp an hour past midnight.

It was well hidden — he would have expected no less of the boy he'd trained — tucked into the lee of a granite outcrop where a wind-bent rowan grew sideways out of the stone like a hand reaching for something it could never hold. A small fire, banked to embers. Two bedrolls. One occupied by a young woman with a priestess's shorn hair and a stone fragment wrapped in oilcloth beside her head like a child's toy she could not sleep without. The other by a man who sat up before Corvane's horse took its third step into the camp, a knife already in his fist, his eyes catching the ember-light.

Ada Joubert had grown a beard since Bellharbor. It made him look older. It made him look, painfully, like his father.

"Master," Ada said. The word came out flat, stripped of everything it used to carry. He did not lower the knife.

"Put that away before you hurt yourself," Corvane said, and dismounted, slowly, showing both hands empty, palms upturned in the rain. "If I'd wanted you taken, boy, I'd have brought forty men instead of my own bad knees."

"You signed the list." Ada's voice cracked on it, just slightly, and Corvane felt the crack go through him too, somewhere under the ribs where he kept the things he did not look at. "Your seal. Your hand. I know your *a* — you taught me letters with it. I've read that denunciation a hundred times in my head."

"I know."

"And you came here *alone*?"

"I came here alone."

Sera Ilyen had risen too, silent, her back against the rock, one hand resting on the wrapped fragment. She watched Corvane the way a deer watches a snare it has already stepped near once. He inclined his head to her — respect cost nothing, and he had learned long ago that courtesy was armor you could wear into any room.

"You should know," she said quietly, "that I have prayed for guidance about what to do with you, Master Corvane. And lately, when I pray, things answer. So be careful what you let me learn tonight."

The words settled over the camp like frost. Corvane looked at her a moment longer than politeness required. *Lately, when I pray, things answer.* Three centuries of silence, broken in a single season, and this girl spoke of it the way one speaks of weather turning.

"I'll be careful," he said. "May I sit? My legs are seventy years of bad masonry, and this conversation will take longer than my dignity can stand."

Ada said nothing. Sera glanced at him, and after a moment Ada nodded once — a small, grudging motion, the ghost of the boy who used to nod the same way when Corvane told him to check his ink twice. Corvane lowered himself onto a flat stone at the fire's edge, close enough to feel its dying warmth, far enough to make plain he claimed nothing. Rain drummed on the rowan leaves. Somewhere down the slope, water moved through the peat with a sound like slow swallowing.

"They're coming," Corvane said. "You know that much. What you don't know is how, or why, and those are the two things worth your life right now, so listen."

"We're listening," Sera said.

"Veyran doesn't want you dead, Ada. Understand that first, because everything else follows from it. If he wanted you dead, you'd have been dead in your workshop. The inquisitors who ransacked it were ordered to leave the body intact if they found one. They were *disappointed*, some of them. There are men in that column who've been promised land and titles for bringing you in breathing, and men who've been promised worse for bringing you in any other way."

"Why breathing?" Ada asked. His knife-hand had dropped to his knee, but his knuckles were white around the grip. "What does a usurper want with a cartographer?"

Corvane looked into the embers. This was the part he had rehearsed on the road, riding alone through the rain, rehearsed until the words wore smooth as river stones. And yet now, with the boy's face turned toward him across the fire — the face of the child who had once fallen asleep over his maps, ink-smudged, trusting — the smooth words caught in his throat like fishbones.

"Because of what you are," he said finally. "Not what you do. What you *are*. You've felt it. Don't insult us both by denying it. The shrine at Thornfell Pass hummed under your hand. The roads bend toward you. The stones wake when your blood touches them." He raised his eyes. "Veyran has scholars who read the old covenant-texts. Real ones, not the burned ones, not the ones the Church of the Veil was allowed to keep. They told him the pact between gods and mortals was never a treaty between equals. It was a vessel-arrangement. Someone had to *carry* it. Someone bred for it, bound to it, threaded through generations until the blood itself became a hinge."

Sera drew a sharp breath. "That's not doctrine. That's—"

"That's the truth the doctrine was built to bury," Corvane said. "Yes. I know. I helped bury some of it myself, in my younger years, when I still believed burning books protected people." He smiled, and it was a terrible thing, a smile with nothing behind it but arithmetic. "Veyran believes that whoever reforges the covenant becomes its vessel. Its living anchor. Power beyond anything the old kings dreamed — the gods' own strength, flowing through one mortal throat. He wants that throat to be his."

"And me?" Ada asked. His voice was very quiet. "You said fuel."

And there it was. The word Corvane had ridden three days through rain to say aloud, because saying it was the only penance available to him, and because — he admitted this only in the deepest hour of the night, riding, soaked, unable to sleep — because saying it might buy him what he needed from the boy, and he hated himself for that too, hated the ledger-keeper in his own chest who never stopped weighing.

"The scholars disagree," Corvane said. "Some say vessel. Some say the reforging demands a life poured into the veil — that the covenant renews itself the way a field renews itself, with blood and ash and burial. That the bloodline exists to be *spent*. Veyran has heard both readings." He paused. "He's decided he doesn't need to choose. He means to bring you to the first shrine alive, force the reforging, and see what happens. If you become a vessel, he takes you apart to find where the power pools. If you become fuel—" Corvane spread his hands, and the gesture was helpless in a way he despised. "Then the ritual tells him what he needs to know about the mechanics, and he finds another of your line."

"There is no other of my line," Ada said.

"No," Corvane agreed softly. "There isn't. His scholars haven't worked that out yet. It buys you time. Not much."

The rain thickened. Sera crouched and fed a twist of dry heather to the embers; a small flame stood up, throwing their three shadows huge against the rock. In the enlarged light Corvane saw how thin Ada had grown, how the shadows sat under his eyes like bruises, how the impossible map — the chart of the realm that shouldn't exist — showed at the open mouth of his satchel, its ink lines faintly luminous even now, even in this wet dark, as though the paper remembered being something else.

"Why?" Ada asked.

Corvane had prepared for every question except that one. Or rather, he had prepared answers for it — *because I had no choice, because they had your name and mine, because Veyran would have found you anyway and this way I could steer the hunt* — a whole armory of answers, each one true the way a bent nail is true, holding something up while pointing the wrong direction.

"Because I'm old," he said instead, "and old men make bargains with whatever power is closest. Veyran was closest. I told myself I could protect you better from inside his court than outside it. I told myself a great many things." He looked at his own hands, spotted and trembling slightly, and was surprised to find the trembling was not entirely performance. "I taught you to draw coastlines, Ada. Do you remember? You were nine. You kept drawing the sea as if it were solid, all one color, and I told you: *the sea is not a thing, it is a pressure, it is ten thousand forces agreeing for a moment on a shape.*" He laughed, short and bitter. "I have spent my whole life being the sea. Agreeing, for a moment, on a shape."

"That's not an answer," Ada said. "That's a confession with the crime left out."

"No," Corvane said. "It isn't an answer. You deserve one, and I don't have one that survives daylight. I sold your name because I was afraid, and because they offered me my archives, my library, my life's work — spared, preserved, *respected* — and I discovered at seventy years old that I loved my life's work more than I loved—" He stopped. The rain filled the silence.

"Than you loved me," Ada finished.

"Yes." The word cost more than the denunciation had. The denunciation had been ink and wax and a steady hand; this was a knife turned in his own oldest wound. "And I have regretted it every hour since, which you will correctly note changes nothing, and which I note anyway, because it is the only honest thing I have left to give you."

Sera shifted. "The warning," she said, practical as a splint. "The warning is real?"

"The warning is real," Corvane said, grateful for the pivot, despising himself for the gratitude. "Dreyse's column is two days behind you, moving fast because they have remounts and you have marsh-mud. There's a second force — riders, thirty strong, no banners — swinging west through the high passes to cut you off from the Thornfells. Veyran knows the third stone is in the mountains. He's known since the drowned chapel. One of the soldiers you killed there carried a dispatch case, and the case was recovered." He met Ada's flinch without flinching himself; he owed the boy that steadiness at least. "You cannot go back the way you came. You cannot go straight north. The only clean road left is the old pilgrim way through the Grey Stones, and it's watched, but it's watched by men, and men can be misdirected. I can misdirect them. For two days, perhaps three."

"Why would we trust a word of this?" Sera asked. Not hostile — worse than hostile, *careful*, the carefulness of someone measuring rope before trusting it with weight.

"You shouldn't," Corvane said simply. "Trust nothing I say. Check everything. When you reach the Grey Stones, look for the cairn with the split capstone — I'll mark the safe approach with a white rag on the eastern side. If there's no rag, assume I lied, or failed, and go around by the water meadows even though it costs you a day." He reached slowly into his coat, watching Ada's knife rise again, and drew out a folded packet of oilskin. "Routes. Dreyse's order of march. The watch-signals at the Grey Stones. Everything I could carry without being missed." He set it on the stone between them and withdrew his hand. "Use it or burn it. Both would be wiser than believing me."

Ada looked at the packet for a long time. Then, without touching it: "You said *ostensibly* nothing, but I'll ask plainly. What do you get, Corvane? Men like you don't cross columns in the rain for remorse. Remorse stays warm by a fire. What do you *get*?"

And there was the question Corvane had ridden three days to avoid, and it had caught him anyway, because the boy had always been too sharp for his own good — too sharp for anyone's good — and Corvane loved him for it, God help them both, loved him the way you love a blade you know will one day cut you.

"What I get," Corvane said, "is to be in the room. That's all. Whatever happens at the first shrine — the reforging, the vessel, the fuel, whatever the old horror actually requires — I will be there, because Veyran trusts me to manage the scholars and the relics, and because I have made myself useful in exactly the way that guarantees proximity. And when the moment comes, I intend to be standing between you and it. Whether you forgive me or not. Whether it kills me or not." He heard his own voice go rough and was too tired to sand it smooth. "It may be the only act of loyalty I have left in me, Ada. I'm spending it in advance, on credit, like everything else in my miserable account-book of a life. But I am *spending* it."

The fire popped. Sera's hand had gone to the stone fragment again, unconsciously, the way a believer's hand goes to a talisman. Ada stared at his old master across the small flame, and Corvane watched him do the arithmetic — watched him weigh the denunciation against the packet, the betrayal against the warning, seventy years of raising against one signature on one list — and knew that no sum the boy reached would ever be clean. That was the cruelest thing Corvane had done to him, in the end. Not the selling. The making sure that the selling could never be simple.

"Stand up," Ada said at last.

Corvane stood, joints cracking, rain running off his hood.

"Take off your coat."

He did, folding it over his arm, standing in his soaked shirt in the cold.

"If I find a rag missing at the Grey Stones," Ada said, "if one traveler dies because of a route in that packet, if I learn you've led Veyran to us by so much as a *mile* — I won't come for you, Corvane. I won't need to. The stones answer my blood now. Do you understand? I don't have to forgive you or condemn you. The mountain will do it for me." His voice didn't shake. That was the worst of it. The boy he'd taught to double-check his ink now spoke of killing with a cartographer's calm precision, and Corvane had given him to that fate with his own signature. "Do you understand?"

"I understand," Corvane said. "I'd expect nothing less. I taught you to be precise."

For one moment — one unguarded, unforgivable moment — something passed between them across the fire that was neither forgiveness nor judgment, just the old current, the current of a workshop smelling of ink and woodsmoke, of a small boy asking *why do the borders wiggle*, of a lonely old man answering *because rivers are stubborn and so are the people who follow them*. It lasted the length of a heartbeat. Then Ada looked away, and it was gone, and Corvane understood that it would not come again, that he had spent it along with everything else.

"Go," Ada said quietly. "Before dawn. Before someone notices you're missing."

Corvane dressed, mounted, gathered the reins. At the edge of the firelight he paused.

"The third stone," he said, not turning around. "In the shrine where you were born. When you find it — and you will find it — read the walls before you touch anything. Read them *all*. Your mother fled that place for reasons carved into its stones, and I have spent twenty-five years hoping you'd never have to read them." He nudged the horse downslope. "I hid the truth of your blood because I thought ignorance was a kind of shelter. It was the second-worst mistake of my life. The first was signing my name."

He rode into the rain without looking back, because looking back was a luxury for men with clean accounts, and his had been red for a very long time.

Behind him the camp light shrank to an ember, then a memory. Ahead, down the black slopes, the inquisitors' fires burned in their hundreds, patient as stars, and among them Captain Dreyse was already awake, already noting in his ledger that Master Corvane had returned alone from his scouting ride, already writing down the exact hour.

Corvane gave Dreyse his report at first light: the fugitives sighted, heading east, toward the coast road — a lie so clean it deserved framing, and Dreyse swallowed it whole and ordered the column turned. Thirty riders went east into the empty hills. The road to the Grey Stones lay open behind them, unwatched, for exactly as long as Corvane's lies held.

Two days, perhaps three.

He walked back to his tent through the mud and the drizzle, past soldiers sharpening knives and priests of the new order whispering to shrines that whispered back, and he permitted himself, for the length of one breath, to believe he had bought the boy his passage.

Then he opened his tent flap and found the black-robed figure sitting inside, waiting, its face hidden, its voice like dry paper turning.

"Master Corvane," it said. "The Lord Usurper wonders why your scouts ride east, when the stone fragment sings *north*."
