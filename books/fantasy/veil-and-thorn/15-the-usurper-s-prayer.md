## 15. The Usurper's Prayer

The shrine had been a granary once, before Veyran's engineers stripped its roof and its purpose. Now it stood at the center of the war camp like a pulled tooth — a round stone chamber with a hole where the sky came in, and in the center of the floor, the god-stone they had dragged from the temple at Karst with chains and forty oxen and no reverence at all.

It was smaller than Veyran had expected. Waist-high, weathered, its carvings worn to suggestions: figures with raised hands, figures kneeling, a line of small marks that might have been words or might have been rain running down old stone for a thousand years. The priests of Karst had wept when it was taken. One of them had thrown himself from the temple roof rather than watch it loaded onto the wagon. Veyran had thought, at the time, that it was a waste of a useful man.

Now, alone in the chamber at dusk, with the camp's ten thousand fires banked low outside and the smell of woodsmoke and horse and wet wool pressing through the empty doorway, he understood the priest better.

The stone was humming.

Not loudly. Not even certainly — it might have been wind over the broken roofline, or the settling of the walls, or blood in his own ears after a day of orders and maps and the particular exhaustion that came from holding a kingdom together with fear. But Veyran had learned, these past months, to trust the things other men called imagination. Imagination had won him a crown. Imagination, properly armed, would win him the coast.

He knelt. The stone floor was cold through his breeches, and he was briefly, absurdly aware of how he must look — the Lord Protector of the Reaches, the man who had strangled the old dynasty in its sleep, kneeling on a granary floor like any peasant wife asking for rain on her turnips.

*Let them laugh,* he thought. *Let the whole world laugh, if it works.*

He had prayed before, of course. As a boy, to a mother who beat him; as a young officer, before battles he'd been sent to lose; as a usurper, once, in the first terrible week, when the treasury was empty and three provinces were in open revolt and he'd caught himself whispering *please* into his cupped hands like a beggar. He had never been answered. He had decided, somewhere in those years, that this was because there was nothing to answer — that prayer was a lever with nothing behind it, and men who pulled it were pulling air.

Then the gods had started answering everyone else.

He had read the reports himself, initially with contempt. A drowned child breathing in Bellharbor. A blind woman seeing in Doss. Crippled soldiers walking out of field hospitals with their scars gone pale as milk. His own quartermasters swearing that grain stores lasted longer than arithmetic allowed. The world was coming unstitched, and the stitches were going back in wrong, and every report ended with the same useless phrase: *the people believe.*

Belief. Veyran had built an empire on the understanding that belief was a resource, like iron or salt — mined, refined, spent. And now here was a vein of it opening under his feet, wild and unclaimed, and every priest and peasant and free-city councilor scrambling to drink from it first.

There was one report he kept separate from the others. Locked. He took it out now, from the lacquered case he carried against his ribs, and read it again by the light of a single lamp: the account of a captured pilgrim from the saltmarshes, a man whose prayers had been answered so thoroughly that he no longer spoke any language his interrogators knew, whose eyes had gone the color of shallow water, who smiled — the report said — *as though listening to something wonderful just behind the wall of the world.*

And the man had said one thing they could transcribe. Over and over, in that tongue no one living should know:

*He is generous. He is generous. He is so hungry to be loved.*

Veyran folded the paper away. Outside, the wind moved through the broken roof, and the stone hummed, and he made his choice the way he made all his choices: quickly, completely, and without looking back.

"Old ones," he said. His voice sounded strange in the round room — too loud, then too small. "Gods of the covenant. Whatever you are. Whoever listens."

Nothing. Wind. The ordinary dark.

He pressed his palm flat against the stone. It was cold, colder than the night warranted, cold as a coin left in snow.

"I have an army," he said. "I have the Reach, the river country, the passes. I will have the coast by spring. I am not asking you for miracles. I am offering you a kingdom that *works*. No more shrines rotting in hillsides. No more gods remembered only by shepherds. Give me victory, and I will build you altars from the mountains to the sea. Every city. Every road. I will make your names the only names spoken."

Still nothing — and then, as he drew breath to rise, to file this away with all his other gambles, the cold under his hand moved.

It flowed. That was the only word for it. The chill in the stone gathered beneath his palm like water finding a channel, and climbed his wrist, and Veyran did not pull away. He was a man who had held a red-hot sword hilt to prove his nerve to mutinous captains. He could hold this.

*You speak,* said a voice, *as though kingdoms were yours to give.*

It did not come from the stone. It did not come from anywhere. It arrived inside his skull fully formed, the way a thought arrives, except that it was not his thought — it was vast and patient and amused, and it tasted of deep water and old honey and something underneath both that made the animal part of his brain want to run until his heart burst.

Veyran stayed on his knees. Sweat slid down his spine.

"Kingdoms are mine to give," he said. "That is what I've spent my life establishing."

*A thief,* the voice said, fondly. *A fine, ambitious thief. We remember thieves. Thieves built half our temples, in the old days. Men who took what they wanted and called it destiny.* A pause, luxurious, savoring. *You want the coast. You want the free cities. You want Bellharbor's harbor and the council's submission and your name on the lips of every child born this generation. Shall I tell you what I want?*

"Speak."

*To be needed again.* The voice softened, and the softness was worse than the vastness — it was intimate, the voice of a lover, of a mother, of the thing in the dark that children invent and adults pretend to outgrow. *Three hundred years, Veyran. Three hundred years of silence, of being remembered wrong, of prayers falling on nothing. Do you know what that does to a god?*

"No," Veyran said honestly. "But I know what it does to a man."

*Yes,* the voice breathed, delighted. *You do. That is why I like you. That is why I will give you everything.*

The cold spread up his arm, across his chest, and it was not painful — that was the horror of it, later, when he still had the capacity for horror. It was *pleasant*. It was warmth wearing cold's clothing, a fullness, a certainty pouring into him like wine into an empty vessel, and he heard himself sigh, heard his own voice say, "More," like a drunkard at a tap.

*Your army marches in four days,* the voice said. *You have worried about supply lines. About the marsh roads. About Bellharbor's walls and the council's stubbornness and the season turning. Worry about none of it. Your soldiers will not hunger. Your soldiers will not tire. Your soldiers will not break, Veyran — not their ranks, not their hearts, not their bodies. What is a wall to men who cannot die?*

"And the cost?" Veyran asked. He was a careful man. Even drowning in honey, some cold clerk in the back of his mind was writing terms in a ledger. "There is always a cost."

The voice laughed, and the laughter rolled around the stone chamber, around the inside of his skull, warm and enormous.

*Such a suspicious creature. So well-taught by a world that always collected its debts early. Here is my price, usurper: remember me. Speak my name to your army. Let them pray to me before battle, and let me hear it. Feed me their voices, their fears, their hopes — a god starves without worship, and I have been starving so long. Give me your ten thousand throats, and I will give you the coast.*

"That's all?"

*That is all. For now.* The amusement sharpened, delicately, like a knife being turned in a wound the victim hasn't felt yet. *We can renegotiate after your victory. Victors always get better terms.*

Veyran knelt in the ruined granary with a dead god's hand inside his chest, and considered the bargain, and found — this was the thing he would examine for the rest of his short life, if he'd had the wit to examine anything — that he felt no fear at all. Only appetite answering appetite. Only the magnificent clarity of a man who has finally found a partner worthy of his ambition.

"Done," he said.

The cold withdrew. The stone went silent. The wind moved through the broken roof, ordinary again, and Veyran rose on steady legs, flexed his right hand — warm now, warmer than the left, warmer than it had ever been — and walked out into the night to inspect his army.

* * *

He began with the officers' tent, unannounced, as was his habit. General Hask was awake, bent over supply tables by lamplight, and looked up with the weary dread of a man expecting another impossible requisition.

"My lord. The marsh roads—"

"Forget the roads." Veyran set his hands flat on the map, on the blue smear of the saltmarshes, on the inked square of Bellharbor beyond. "We march in four days. Direct route. No baggage train beyond five days' provisions."

Hask stared. "My lord, the marsh crossing alone is nine days in bad weather. If the wagons bog down—"

"They won't." Veyran heard his own voice and marveled at it. There was no performance in it anymore. For twenty years he had performed certainty, had manufactured it like coinage, and his officers had obeyed it the way men obey any currency — because enough people agreed to pretend it was real. This was different. This was *minted*. "The men will march faster than you think possible. They will eat less than you think survivable. They will fight at the end of it as though it were the first morning of a festival. Mark my numbers, General. When I exceed them, you'll owe me an apology."

Hask was a solid man, a careful man, and Veyran watched him search his lord's face for the madness he clearly suspected and fail to find it — because there was no madness there. That was the trap of it. Madness would have been safer. What sat behind Veyran's eyes was calm as deep water.

"The men are already talking," Hask said carefully. "About the shrines. About what happened at Karst, when the stone was taken. Some of them say the gods are angry. Some of them—" he hesitated, "—some of them say the Lord Protector has found favor."

"Both true," Veyran said, "if you understand favor correctly." He straightened. "Tomorrow at dawn, assemble the host. All of it. I will speak to them myself."

* * *

Dawn came up gray and slow over the plain, mist lying in the hollows like spilled milk, and ten thousand men stood in their companies in the half-light, breath steaming, a forest of spears and pikes stirring restlessly against the sky.

Veyran mounted the review platform and looked out over them, and for the first time in his life understood what priests saw when they looked at congregations. Not a weapon. A congregation. Ten thousand throats.

"Soldiers of the Reach," he said, and his voice carried — unnaturally far, he would realize later; the morning was windless and yet the men in the rearmost ranks swore they heard every word as though he stood at their shoulders. "You have marched far, and you will march farther. You have heard stories — of drowned children breathing, of the blind seeing, of the old gods waking after three hundred years. I am here to tell you the stories are true."

A murmur ran the ranks like wind through wheat.

"And I am here to tell you which side of the waking you stand on." He lifted his right hand — the warm one — and the mist seemed to lean toward him. "The free cities have hoarded their walls and their gold and their councils while the world changed under their feet. They think the old powers belong to them, to their priests and their pilgrims. They are wrong. The power belongs to whoever is strong enough to hold it. It has always belonged to such men. I have spoken with it. I have *bargained* with it. And it has promised me — promised *us* — the coast."

He paused, and let the silence stretch, and when he spoke again his voice dropped into something almost gentle, almost holy.

"So I give you a new prayer. Not the mumbled nonsense of frightened peasants. A soldier's prayer, short enough for a march, strong enough for a battle. Say it with me. Learn it. Say it before you sleep and when you wake and when the arrows come. Say: *Lord of the Open Door, feed us and we will feed you. Break what stands and keep what serves. We are yours; be ours.*"

Silence. Then, raggedly at first, from the front ranks, the words came back at him — *Lord of the Open Door* — and spread, company by company, ten thousand voices stumbling over a prayer two minutes old as though it were ancient, as though they'd always known it, and Veyran stood on the platform with his warm hand raised and felt them pour into him like a tide into a breach: their fear, their hope, their hunger, ten thousand small lives offering themselves up without knowing what they signed.

Behind his ribs, something drank, and sighed with pleasure, and grew.

* * *

They marched on the fourth morning, and the reports that came back up the column in the first days were the reports of a miracle, and no one used that word aloud, but everyone thought it.

The men did not tire. That was the first thing. A forced march that should have broken them — thirty miles through hill country in autumn rain — and on the second evening the soldiers came into camp not staggering but *bright*, faces flushed, complaining only that the pace was insultingly slow. The sick tent stood empty. A man who had taken a fever in the river country marched out of it on the third day, color high, eyes clear. Too clear. Hask noticed that. Everyone was beginning to notice, in the way people notice a wrong note sustained so long it becomes a drone beneath everything.

Their eyes were changing.

Not all at once. Not all of them. But Hask, riding the column on the fifth day, looked down rank after rank of upturned faces and counted — forty, sixty, a hundred — men whose eyes had gone the color of shallow water. Pale. Still. Reflecting nothing. They obeyed orders instantly and perfectly. They did not grumble, did not gamble, did not sing the filthy marching songs that armies are made of. They ate what was given and asked for nothing and stood their watches without fidgeting, and when the prayer was called at dawn — *Lord of the Open Door, feed us and we will feed you* — the pale-eyed men said it loudest, and sweetest, and smiled as they did, all together, all the same smile.

Hask reported it to Veyran on the sixth night, choosing his words like a man walking on river ice.

"The men are... remarkably fit, my lord. Remarkably obedient. But I've lost forty deserters this month and gained four hundred volunteers, and the volunteers—" He stopped. Started again. "Some of the volunteers don't ask about pay, my lord. They don't ask about anything. They just say they heard the call. And their eyes—"

"I know about their eyes," Veyran said.

He stood at the flap of his command tent, looking out over the camp, and Hask followed his gaze and felt his stomach drop, because below, in the firelight, thousands of soldiers had gathered — unprompted, off-duty, unsanctioned — in rings around small stones and cairns and even upturned helmets, and they were praying. Not the soldier's prayer. Older words, in no tongue Hask knew, spilling out of them in a low collective murmur like bees in a wall, swaying gently, faces lit from within by a joy that had nothing human in its architecture.

"They're happy," Hask whispered. "God help us, my lord, they're *happy*."

"They're fed," Veyran said. "So am I. Look at me, General."

Hask looked. And the thing looking back at him wore his lord's face and his lord's calm, and behind the calm, deep down, something moved with an appetite so vast and so patient that Hask's body understood it before his mind did, the way a lamb understands the shadow of the hawk.

"You have doubts," Veyran said kindly. "That's natural. Doubt is just hunger pointed the wrong way. Go and pray, General. You'll feel better."

Hask went. That was the worst of it, he thought later, in the last hours when he still had thoughts that were entirely his own. He went. He knelt at a ring of murmuring men, and he opened his mouth to shape the words ironically, safely, just to move among them unnoticed —

— and the words came out in the old tongue, fluent, loving, and his eyes filled with tears of gratitude, and somewhere very far away, behind a wall of honeyed light, the last private room in his mind put up its shutters and went dark.

* * *

On the ninth day the vanguard crested the last ridge above the marshlands, and the whole host halted on the heights at Veyran's order, ten thousand men standing in the wind to look at the distant glitter of the sea and, on its edge, the smoke-hazed smudge that was Bellharbor.

Veyran rode forward alone to the ridge's edge. In his breast pocket, against his heart, the lacquered case held his papers and his seals and the folded report of the hollow-eyed pilgrim, and he found he no longer needed to read it. He knew the man's smile now from the inside. He wore it himself, some mornings, before he was quite awake.

*Bellharbor,* the voice said, warm as bathwater, coiled contentedly along his spine. *Walls, harbors, councils, pride. And somewhere in that tangle of streets, the last thread of the old covenant is running — a cartographer, a thief of roads, carrying stones that belong to me.*

"Joubert," Veyran said aloud. The name meant little to him — one entry on a long list Corvane had given him, one bloodline among many — but the voice had begun to speak of the cartographer often lately, with an attention that bordered on craving, and Veyran had learned to notice what his partner craved.

*Bring me the stones,* the voice murmured. *Bring me the bloodline. And when the veil comes down, usurper, I will not make you a governor of provinces. I will make you a prince of the opened world. You will never kneel again — not to councils, not to crowns, not to death itself.*

Below, the army waited in perfect silence, ten thousand men, and as the sun broke through the cloud and struck the sea, they turned their faces toward the light in unison — every pale eye in ten thousand heads, all at once, like a field of flowers following the sun — and began, softly, joyfully, to sing.

Veyran raised his hand, warm as a brand, and gave the signal to march.

Far to the north, past the marshes, past the passes, in a free city that did not yet know what was coming for it, a bell began to ring.
