## 17. Battle of the Broken Gate

The dawn came up gray and salt-stained, and Sera Ilyen stood on the wall of the Broken Gate and watched the horizon learn to breathe.

Not with wind. With men.

Veyran's army had made camp three nights past in the low hills east of the city, and each morning its cookfires had multiplied like mold across bread, until the hills themselves seemed to smoke without burning. Now, as the light strengthened, she saw them form — rank upon rank upon rank, a dark tide gathering itself out of the earth, banners hanging dead in the windless air. The free cities had not seen an army of this size since her grandmother's grandmother's day, and those had fought each other over grain rights. This one had come to unmake everything.

"Archers to the third tier," she said, and her voice carried along the wall the way it had learned to carry through three years of preaching in the Saltmarket, when the crowds were restless and the sermons ran long. "Oil crews stand ready at the gatehouse. No one looses until my word."

Beside her, Captain Ondrey Voss shifted his weight from foot to foot, a habit that would have gotten him flogged in any proper garrison but which she had long ago stopped correcting, because it was the only prayer he knew. He was a broad man gone soft at the middle, with a face like a dropped plate, and he had commanded the Bellharbor watch for twenty years without ever once commanding anything larger than a riot.

"Three thousand," he said. "Maybe more. We have six hundred on the walls and another four hundred in reserve, half of them fishermen with spears."

"Then we had best be very good at this," Sera said.

He looked at her. "You've never held a wall."

"No." She smoothed her priestess's gray robe where the morning damp clung to it. Beneath it, against her sternum, hung the fragment of the first covenant-stone on a leather cord, and it was warm, and it had been warm for two days now, warm the way a fever is warm. "But I have watched people die believing something, Captain, and I have watched them die believing nothing, and I will tell you freely: the first kind are harder to kill."

Voss grunted, which from him was assent, or perhaps indigestion. It was often difficult to tell.

Below and behind her, Bellharbor woke to its last ordinary morning. Gulls screamed over the fish-stands. Somewhere a woman was singing to a child, some nonsense song about a crab who wanted to be a bishop. The council had voted at midnight — barely, bitterly, after the merchants' faction had finally broken when word came that Veyran's outriders had burned the granaries at Millbrook, which meant there would be no bargaining because there would be nothing left to bargain *with* — and now the whole city was conscripted into its own survival. Bakers carrying stones to the wall. Sailors hauling oil. Children too young to fight being marched inland with their mothers, weeping, looking back.

Sera had stood before the council and promised them the gods had returned. She had said it with the certainty of a woman who had watched a drowned child draw breath in her arms, and the council had believed her, or wanted to, which amounted to the same thing on a vote.

She had not told them what else she had learned in the saltmarshes. That the voice answering prayers might not be a god at all. That the true gods might be caged, scattered, silent — and that whatever wore their voices now was hungry in a way that made hunger seem a gentle word.

Ada Joubert knew. Ada had crossed the veil itself, or so he'd told them, holding three stones that hummed against each other like struck bells. And Ada had left before midnight, slipping out the harbor postern with a small company of volunteers, bound for the first shrine behind enemy lines, bound for the keystone, bound for whatever terrible arithmetic the covenant demanded of him.

She had watched him go and found, to her surprise, that she was afraid for him in a way that had nothing to do with strategy.

"He'll make it," Voss said, following her gaze to the eastern hills. "The mapmaker. If anyone can thread those lines, it's him."

"You didn't believe in him a week ago."

"I don't believe in him now. I believe in his maps." Voss spat over the parapet, an old soldier's benediction. "Gods, look at them move."

And they were moving. The tide of Veyran's host had begun to flow down out of the hills toward the landward gate, and the sound reached the wall a heartbeat later — not the roar she had expected, not the war-cries and drumbeats that armies were supposed to make, but something far worse.

Silence. Ten thousand men advancing in silence, boots striking earth in perfect unison, no voice raised, no horn blown, as if the entire host were one body and the body had no need of speech.

Sera's hands found the stone fragment at her throat. It pulsed against her fingers, once, twice, like a second heart.

"Oh," she whispered. "Oh, that's wrong. That is deeply, terribly wrong."

* * *

They came on through the morning like weather.

The first arrows flew at the fourth bell after dawn, when the vanguard came within range of the third tier, and Sera gave the word, and the sky above the Broken Gate filled with a black whispering rain. She had imagined this moment — she admitted it to herself now, standing in the stink of oil and fear — she had imagined the enemy line breaking, wavering, the way lines broke in every account of every siege she had ever read in the temple archives.

The line did not break.

Arrows took men in the throat, in the eye, in the chest, and the men did not fall. They staggered — some of them staggered — and then kept walking. A soldier with three shafts standing out of him like a pincushion turned his head slowly, considered the arrows with what looked like mild curiosity, and broke them off one by one without breaking stride.

"They're drugged," Voss said hoarsely. "Some root, some—"

"They're not drugged." Sera's mouth had gone dry. "Look at their eyes."

Through her spyglass she found faces in the front rank, and the faces were wrong in a way that was hard to name and impossible to forget. Not blank — blankness she could have borne, blankness was just terror frozen. These faces were *full*. Full and glazed, eyes wide and shining with a rapture that had no business on a battlefield, mouths slightly open as though each man were listening to the most beautiful music ever composed, played just beneath the threshold of hearing.

Every tenth man or so still looked human. Afraid. Marching because the ranks pressed them forward. Those were the ones whose eyes she could meet, even at this distance, even through glass. The others did not see her at all. They saw something else. Something wonderful.

"The false god," she breathed. "It's poured itself into them."

The boiling oil went down at the fifth bell, sheets of it cascading off the murder-holes onto the scaling parties, and the screaming that rose should have broken the assault. Instead the screaming stopped almost immediately — and the burned men climbed on. Flesh sloughing from their forearms, hair scorched to the skull, they planted ladders with hands that left black prints on the wood, and they climbed, and they did not stop climbing, and Sera heard a young archer beside her begin to pray and then stop mid-word, because what was the use of praying when the thing you'd pray to was wearing the enemy like a coat?

"Hold the wall!" Voss roared, running the parapet, shoving men back to their places. "Hold! They can die, everything can die, put your steel in them and *push!*"

The first ladders hit the wall at the sixth bell.

What followed, Sera would spend the rest of her life failing to describe. The chronicles called it the Battle of the Broken Gate, eight hours that decided the fate of the free cities, and the chronicles got the sequence right and the soul of it entirely wrong, because the soul of it was this: it was like fighting drowned men. Men who could not be drowned further. The defenders fought with courage that surprised even themselves — fishermen and bakers and grey-bearded watchmen holding the wall hour after hour — and every act of courage was met by enemies who felt no pain, feared no death, and died only when they were taken apart so thoroughly that whatever rode them lost its grip.

A sailor named Brannock held the eastern stair alone for eleven minutes with a boathook. When they pulled his body off it afterward, he had taken nine wounds that should each have killed him, and his face was peaceful, and Sera closed his eyes and could not make herself say the blessing, because the blessing named the gods, and she no longer knew who was listening.

By the eighth bell the wall still held, but barely, and the courtyard behind the gate was a charnel house, and the reserves were spent, and Veyran's host — which had lost, by Voss's count, better than a thousand men — simply reformed its ranks below and began again, fresh companies flowing forward from the hills as if the hills were manufacturing them.

"They're going to come again," Voss said. He was leaning against the parapet, breathing hard, a cut across his scalp matting his hair red. "And again. And again. Until we drop. Priestess, I've held riots, dock strikes, a pirate landing once at Grayrock. This isn't war. This is erosion. We're a cliff and they're the sea."

Sera looked out at the enemy host, at the banners hanging dead, at the shining-eyed thousands waiting their turn to climb and burn and die without dying, and she felt the thing she had been holding at bay all morning finally arrive and settle into her chest like cold water filling a hull.

*Terror.*

Not of death. She had made her peace with death years ago; a priestess in a port city attends enough plague beds and wreck sites that death becomes a colleague, tiresome but familiar. What filled her now was worse. It was the terror of meaninglessness — the growing certainty that nothing she did mattered, that faith itself was a coin minted by something that ate faith, that every prayer rising from this wall, hers included, was food.

The stone fragment at her throat pulsed hard, once, painfully, and she gasped.

Then it changed.

The warmth drained out of it — no, not drained. *Turned.* Like a compass needle swinging. The warmth moved from her chest northward, or rather the sense of warmth did, a direction without distance, pulling at the cord around her neck like a fish on a line. North and east. Toward the hills. Toward—

Toward the first shrine. Toward Ada.

Something was happening there. Something vast. She couldn't have said how she knew; she knew the way she had known the drowned child would live, the way she had known in the saltmarsh chapel that the voice answering prayers was a lie wearing a beloved face. The stone was a fragment of the covenant, and the covenant was a thread between realms, and somewhere along that thread, at this moment, a cartographer's son was doing something that made the whole web shiver.

"Sera?" Voss was watching her. "What is it? You've gone white as sailcloth."

She gripped the fragment and the pulling sensation steadied, became bearable, became almost — guidance. "I don't know," she said. "But the enemy felt it too. Look."

Below the wall, the assault had faltered. Not stopped — the ladders still swung, the dead-still ranks still pressed forward — but the perfect unison had cracked. Here and there among the shining-eyed soldiers, men had stopped mid-stride and turned their heads north, all together, all at the same angle, like a field of flowers finding a sun that wasn't in the sky. Their rapturous expressions flickered. Underneath the rapture, visible for just a moment, was something else.

Hunger. Vast, thwarted, furious hunger.

"It's distracted," Sera said, and hope arrived in her like a blade being drawn — bright, dangerous, impossible to hold safely. "Whatever holds these men, it's looking away. Looking at *him.*"

"Then now," Voss said, straightening, blood dripping past his eyebrow, "would be the time to hit them with everything we have left."

* * *

They had very little left, and they hit the enemy with all of it.

Sera herself led the sortie — Voss had objected, and she had reminded him gently that she was the ranking authority of the Ilyen rite in Bellharbor and that he could court-martial her if they both survived — two hundred men out the sally gate and into the flank of the scaling parties, priests and sailors and one elderly fruit-seller with a billhook who fought, by every account, like a legend out of the founding wars. And because the false god's attention was wrenched northward, its grip on its host slackened, and for one hour the defenders discovered that the enemy could feel pain after all, could break, could run.

They drove the assault back from the wall. They fired the siege towers. The fruit-seller took a spear through the shoulder and laughed about it. For one hour, on the plain before the Broken Gate, the free cities remembered what winning felt like.

And then the attention turned back.

Sera felt it happen — felt it in the stone against her sternum, a lurch like a ship changing heading, and then a *scream*, though not with ears. The fragment flared hot enough to brand her skin, and she cried out and clawed the cord over her head, and the stone lay in her palm blazing white, and through its light she saw — or sensed, or was shown — a single image that lasted less than a heartbeat:

A shrine in the mountains. Black stone under open sky. A figure standing at its heart with his arms spread wide, lit from within, lit from everywhere, becoming a doorway —

— and around the doorway, coiling in from every direction, the hunger.

"*No,*" Sera said aloud, on the bloody plain, with the battle roaring back to life around her and the enemy's rapture returning tenfold, and she did not know if she was praying or cursing or simply naming the thing she refused to accept.

The counterattack collapsed. The survivors fought their way back inside the gate, and the wall held through the afternoon by increments and miracles of stubbornness, and when full dark came the assault finally, grudgingly, withdrew to the hills — leaving the field heaped with dead who, the burial crews reported in shaken voices, did not rot. Did not stiffen. Lay in the moonlight looking as though they might sit up at any moment and ask what all the fuss had been.

Sera stood on the wall at midnight, bandaged hand curled around the cooling stone, and looked north to where the mountains were a blacker darkness against the stars.

Somewhere up there, the door was opening. Or closing. Or both.

"Whatever you're doing, mapmaker," she whispered, "do it fast. Because we cannot hold another day."

Behind her, in the hills, the silence of ten thousand waiting men went on and on, patient as the sea, and high above the highest peak a light was rising that no star had any business making — gold-white, trembling, beautiful, and Sera Ilyen, who had given her life to the gods, fell to her knees on the broken gate and prayed to it with everything she had left, not knowing whether she was praying *for* the light or *against* the dark that coiled beneath it, knowing only that by tomorrow's dusk, one of them would be gone forever.
