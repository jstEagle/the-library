## 18. Beyond the Veil

The first shrine did not look like a place where worlds touched. It looked like a wound that had learned to be patient.

Ada came to it in the hour before dawn, when the mountains held their breath and the siege fires of Veyran's army burned far below like a scatter of orange seeds across the valley floor. Behind him, Bellharbor's landward gate was already thundering — he could feel it more than hear it, a deep concussion rolling up through the stone under his boots, each impact arriving a heartbeat after the flash of light at the city's rim. Sera was down there. The council's last soldiers were down there. Every life that had trusted him with the road was down there, and here he stood at the top of the world, at a ring of leaning monoliths older than any kingdom, about to do the one thing no living soul had done in three hundred years.

The thin place.

He had mapped it once, years ago, as a boy's curiosity and nothing more: a hollow in the Thornfells where his compass spun, where birds would not nest, where snow fell upward on certain nights if you watched long enough. He had marked it on no chart. Some part of him, even then, had understood that some doors should be drawn only in memory.

Now he unslung his field kit with hands that would not quite stop trembling, and laid out the three covenant-stones in the snow between the monoliths.

They were not beautiful things. The first, from the smuggler's pass, was a fist of grey granite veined with light like frozen lightning. The second, raised from the drowned chapel beneath the saltmarshes, still wept brine though it had been dry for weeks; it left dark stains wherever it rested. The third he had carried out of his mother's shrine wrapped in her letters, and it was warm — always warm, warm as a sleeping animal, and when he slept near it he dreamed in a language he almost remembered.

As he set them in a triangle, they began to sing.

Not aloud. The sound happened somewhere behind his sternum, three notes braiding into a chord that made his teeth ache and the snow around them shiver into patterns too regular to be wind-blown. The stones knew each other. The stones had been waiting for each other, perhaps since before the breaking, and now that they were together again they pulled at the air like tide against a moored boat.

And the air answered.

It began as a shimmer above the triangle, the way heat rises off summer stone — except this was winter, and the shimmer bent *inward*, folding the mountain view behind it like a page being turned. Ada saw the peaks beyond the monoliths warp, slide, and vanish. Saw stars appear where there should have been dawn-grey sky. Stars in configurations no astronomer of the free cities had ever charted, because those skies belonged elsewhere.

His mother's letters had told him what to expect. Reading about it and standing before it were different countries entirely.

*The veil is not a wall,* she had written, in a hand that grew shakier toward the end. *It is a skin. And skin, my love, remembers every touch.*

Ada knelt at the edge of the fold. He pressed his palm flat against the stones — all three at once, as the carvings in his birth-shrine had shown, keeper's hand upon the pact — and said the only prayer he had left, which was no prayer at all, just a name:

"Mother."

The veil opened.

* * *

Falling without falling. That was the crossing — a sensation like stepping off a cliff into a wind that carried him sideways through color. There was no tunnel, no ladder of light, none of the pilgrims' pretty imaginings. There was only a moment of unbearable pressure, as if the whole sky had leaned its weight on his chest, and then —

Grass.

Grey grass, silvered like old coins, stretching to a horizon that curved the wrong way, bending up instead of down, so that the world seemed to cup him rather than spill him. Above, the impossible stars wheeled slowly, and among them hung shapes that might have been moons or might have been eyes. The air tasted of rain that hadn't fallen yet, of iron and honey and something older underneath, something like the smell of a book closed for centuries.

Ada got to his knees in the grey grass and was quietly, thoroughly sick. Then he wiped his mouth, checked that the stones had crossed with him — they lay in the grass beside him, dim now, their song muted to a pulse — and made himself look at where he was.

He was a cartographer. Looking was what he did, even here. Especially here.

So he looked, and what he saw broke his heart in a way he had not prepared for.

The gods were small.

He had expected — he didn't know what. Cathedrals of cloud. Figures of storm and fire striding a heaven-sized plain. Instead, scattered across the silvered grass like cairns on a moor, were shapes barely larger than the shrines that had once housed them. A woman formed of river-water, curled tight, her flow reduced to a trickle circling her own knees. A bull of black oak, head bowed, moss growing along his back — actual moss, patient and indifferent, growing on a god. A figure of woven reeds and lantern-light who flickered like a candle in a draft, guttering, refusing to go out.

Cages. Each god sat within a cage of their own making — rings of carved stone, fragments of the old covenant-stones, the very pacts they had once blessed turned inward to bind them. Not prisons built by enemies. Prisons built by the terms of a contract that had collapsed, collapsing its signatories with it. His mother's letters had guessed at it. Seeing it was another matter. The gods had lent their power across the veil for a thousand generations, and when the covenant broke, the power had nowhere to return to — so it had turned around and become the bars.

The river-woman lifted her head as he approached. Her eyes were two drops of water, and in them he saw the Thornfells, every pass he had ever mapped, seen from above, from within, from the memory of the mountain itself.

"You're the Joubert boy," she said. Her voice was the sound of a stream over stones, thin and tired. "The last thread. We felt you coming for days. Like warmth through a cracked window."

"I've come to reforge it," Ada said. His voice sounded absurdly small. "The covenant. I have three of the stones. I know what it asks."

The river-woman laughed, and it was the saddest sound he had ever heard — water laughing in a drought. "Do you? Do you know what it asks?" She gestured with a hand of flowing water at the cages, at the diminished shapes, at the vast grey country of a heaven gone to seed. "We wrote the terms ourselves, little mapmaker, in an age when we were large enough to be cruel to ourselves. A life poured into the veil. Always a life. Always one bloodline bred and pruned and spent like a candle to keep the lamp lit." Her water-eyes fixed on him. "Your mother hated us for it. She was right to."

Somewhere behind him, soft as a lover settling onto a bed, something else spoke.

"She was right about many things. She was wrong about me."

* * *

Ada turned.

There was no body to it, not at first — only a presence at the edge of the grey grass, where the horizon curved up and the light went strange. Then it gathered itself, the way fog gathers, the way a crowd gathers around an accident, and it wore a shape because it knew shapes comforted meat. It wore many. For a heartbeat it was his mother, young, laughing, mud on her cheeks from a garden long burned. The next it was Master Corvane, whole and kind, holding out a hand. The next it was Sera, and Ada flinched as if struck.

"Forgive the theater," it said, in a voice that was all of theirs and none of theirs, smooth as poured wax. "I forget sometimes how much you people live inside your faces."

"What are you?" Ada kept the stones at his back. He could feel them pulsing faster, alarmed, or warning him, or both.

"Honest," it said, and the word landed strangely, heavily, like a coin dropped in a quiet room. "That is more than can be said for anyone else you've met on this errand. The river-lady will tell you she's a god. Ask her what she's been doing for three centuries while her faithful burned and starved and prayed into silence. Ask her why the prayers went unanswered." It drifted closer, and where it passed, the grey grass turned briefly gold, then wilted. "I answered. I was here, behind the skin of the world, listening, when they were caged and deaf and gone. Every prayer that fell through the cracks — I caught it. I fed on it. And I learned to speak in their voices, because hunger is a wonderful teacher."

"The false voice." Ada's mouth was dry. "Veyran's god."

"Veyran's patron. Your enemy's benefactor. The answer to every desperate prayer in the saltmarshes." It spread hands that were everyone's hands. "You say false like it's a flaw. Little cartographer, I have done more good in three years than the true gods did in three hundred. I healed the drowned child in Bellharbor. I mended the pilgrim's rotting lung. I gave the lame man his legs. That they came away hollow afterward is a matter of... portioning. Nothing given is free. They knew the price. Everyone always knows the price, and everyone always pays it gladly until the bill arrives."

"And Veyran?"

"Is a man who understands appetite." The voice warmed, going intimate, going gentle, and Ada hated how much of his own longing it found. "Which brings me to you. Oh, Ada. May I call you Ada? Your mother called you that, in the end, when the fever took her. She called it into the dark, and I heard, and I kept it. I keep everything. Would you like to hear her last words? I have them. I have all of them, every word she ever spoke, filed and preserved like pressed flowers—"

"Stop."

"—or we could skip ahead. I'm flexible. I'm generous. That's the difference between me and the caged things behind you." The presence circled him now, slow, and the landscape turned with it like a wheel. "Here is what I offer, and I offer it plainly, because I want you to understand exactly what you're refusing, if you refuse it.

"The reforging requires a life. You know this. The stones are in your pack and the keystone is here, past the veil, and when you pour yourself into the veil, the covenant closes and the hungering thing behind it — me — starves. Sealed out. Forever. That is the road your mother died trying to spare you, and you walked it anyway, brave little fool.

"But." The word dropped like a stone into a well. "There is another way. The covenant doesn't require your death, Ada. It requires *power*. Power sufficient to remake the veil. And power comes from anywhere. From anyone. From *me*."

The grey grass bloomed. All at once, in a wave rippling outward from where it stood, flowers burst up — real ones, bellharbor roses and thornfell edelweiss, impossibly vivid, impossibly alive. The sky brightened. The caged gods stirred in their rings of stone, turning their diminished faces toward the light with expressions of terrible, starving hope.

"Take my hand," it said, and offered one — his mother's hand, young and strong, the hand that had taught him to hold a stylus. "Reforge the covenant as its vessel, not its fuel. Take the keystone and my strength with it, and step back through that door a god. Not a symbol. Not a saint. A *god*, Ada, walking out of the mountains with the armies of the dead-eyed kneeling. Veyran will break like weather against you. The free cities will build you temples before the snow melts.

"And because I am generous — because unlike the river-lady and her moss-backed brother, I pay my debts in advance — I will give you gifts. Freely. First."

The world folded.

Suddenly Ada stood in a garden that had burned twenty-five years ago. Sunlight. Bees. And his mother, alive, kneeling by a bed of herbs, looking up at him with soil-darkened fingers and a smile that undid him completely. "There you are," she said. "You're late. The rosemary won't plant itself."

He made a sound he had never made before, not at her deathbed, not in all the years since. He took one step toward her before his mind caught his heart by the collar and hauled it back.

"She isn't real," he whispered.

"She *is* real," the voice said softly, from everywhere. "I can make her real. Flesh, breath, memory, the scar on her left thumb from the carving knife. I have her pattern entire. Second gift: Corvane. The man who sold your name. I will reach into him and take the treachery out like a splinter. He will love you cleanly for the rest of his days, with no shadow in it, no bargain, no betrayal waiting in his bones. Is that not mercy? Is that not better justice than anything you'd wring from him yourself?

"Third gift: the covenant, remade *right*. No more bloodlines bred for slaughter. No more daughters fleeing shrines in the night. No more sons carrying sentences in their veins. You will write the new terms yourself, with my power behind the pen. End the sacrifice forever — isn't that what you want? Isn't that what you *told* the council? You can have it. All of it. You need only do one thing."

The garden faded. His mother faded mid-smile, and Ada's knees nearly went with her. He stood again in the grey grass, among the blooming flowers, beneath the watching stars, and the presence leaned close, wearing now no face at all, which was somehow more honest than all its borrowed ones.

"Open the door wider," it whispered. "Let me through with you. That's all. One door, opened wide, held open by a god who owes you everything. Feed me a little, and I will feed your world for a thousand years. The old gods made you a candle. I am offering to make you the sun."

Silence, then, vast as the curved horizon.

In their cages, the true gods watched him. The river-woman's water-eyes held no judgment — only exhaustion, and something that might have been pity, and something that might have been the oldest kind of prayer, the kind a prisoner makes without words.

Ada thought of the hollow pilgrim in the saltmarshes, whispering in no living tongue, healed and emptied. He thought of Veyran's soldiers with their glazing eyes, fighting without pain, without death, without *themselves*. He thought of the price, and the bill, and the portioning.

Then he thought of his mother's last letter, the ink blurred where something — tears, or marshwater, or time — had fallen on it. *A gift that asks for a door is not a gift,* she had written. *It is an invitation.*

And he thought — God help him, he would think of it all his life — of the rosemary. Of how badly, how *humanly*, he wanted to kneel in that burned garden and plant it with her, and how the wanting itself was the trap, because a hunger that knows your name will always set the table with your childhood.

"No," Ada said.

The flowers stopped blooming.

"No," he said again, louder, and reached into himself for the thing he had spent his whole life denying — the inherited fire, the covenant-thread, the bloodline's burden — and found it waiting, furious and bright, like a banked coal that had been waiting twenty-eight years for exactly this breath. "You wear my mother's face and you don't even know why it's a lie. She never planted rosemary. She hated it. She said it took over everything it touched." His voice shook, and he let it shake. "Just like you."

The presence went very still.

"So here are *my* terms," Ada said, and knelt, and laid his hands on the three stones, and felt the keystone humming somewhere ahead in the silvered grass, close now, closer than his own heartbeat. "No vessels. No bargains. No doors held open. Just the veil, and a life — but *my* life, spent *my* way, on *my* terms. You wanted a meal. I hope you choke on it."

Behind him, faint and fierce, the river-god began to laugh — and this time it was not sad at all.

The false voice screamed, and the sky split, and Ada crawled forward through the tearing light toward the keystone, toward the end of the covenant and the beginning of whatever came after —

— and in the world below, at the broken gate of Bellharbor, the first of Veyran's deathless soldiers suddenly stopped mid-stride, and looked up, and *waited*.
