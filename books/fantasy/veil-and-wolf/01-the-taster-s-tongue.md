## 1. The Taster's Tongue

There are two kinds of men who wake before dawn in Hollowmere: the devout and the doomed. I am neither. I wake early because the kitchen does, and because a taster who arrives late to his own poisoning has already failed at the only job he will ever hold.

The bell above the castle kitchens had not yet finished its first ring when I came down the servants' stair, boots in hand so as not to wake the scullery boys sleeping in the alcoves. Old habit. A taster learns to move quietly for the same reason a mouse does — not because he expects to be stepped on, but because he has thought about it enough to prefer not finding out.

Cook Halvard was already at her station, a mountain of a woman with forearms like bread loaves and a face that suggested she had personally been insulted by every ingredient in the pantry. She nodded at me without warmth. We had an understanding, Halvard and I: she cooked, I tasted, and neither of us pretended we liked each other. Twenty years of shared mornings had worn our civility down to its load-bearing bones.

"Barley broth," she said, jerking her chin at a copper pot. "For the King's table. And before you ask — no, I don't know who sent the recipe."

That was the interesting part. Recipes arrived at the royal kitchen the way weather arrived over the pass: from somewhere, unbidden, carrying whatever they carried. Most were harmless. Some were ambitious. One, eleven years ago, had contained enough powdered foxglove to stop a horse's heart, and I had drunk it anyway, because that was the morning's work, and then I had sat very still for an hour while my pulse did something creative, and then I had reported *foxglove, moderate dose* to the steward, and someone in the west wing had been hanged by lunchtime.

I did not think about the hanging part. That was another old habit.

* * *

Let me explain what I am, since you'll be stuck with me for some length of time.

Dominic Voss. Thirty-one years old. Royal food-taster to the crown of Hollowmere, which sounds grander than it is. Hollowmere itself is a valley folded into the mountains like a letter into an envelope — green, narrow, self-satisfied. At each of the seven passes stands a warding stone older than any kingdom, older than any language anyone still speaks, and between them stretches the Veil: a ward laid down so long ago that no one remembers whether it was raised to keep something out or to keep us in. The priests say both. The priests say a great many things with their eyes closed.

What matters is this: inside the Veil, magic does not reach. No hex crosses the stones. No curse rides the wind. And no poison born of sorcery — the kind that blooms in a vial only under moonlight, the kind that kills a man through his shadow — can exist here. Within Hollowmere, if you are poisoned, you are poisoned by a person, with a substance, and a person can be found, and a substance can be named.

Which is where I come in. Because even ordinary poison kills kings, and kings are fond of continuing not to be dead.

And here is the part I have never said aloud, not to the stewards, not to the priests, not to Maren: I cannot die of poison. Any poison. I have swallowed things that should have opened me like a letter and read me aloud to the room, and I have stood up afterward and asked for bread. The physicians call it a constitution. The priests called it a blessing, once, before deciding it was too strange to bless and settling on calling it nothing at all. My sister calls it a miracle, and kisses my forehead, and does not meet my eyes while she does it.

I call it my tongue. It is the only remarkable thing about me, and I have made it a profession, and I have made that profession a life, and if you press me on whether those are the same thing I will change the subject with the smoothness of a man who has practiced.

* * *

The broth was still warm when I carried it to the tasting alcove off the great hall — a stone niche with a stool, a candle, and a view of nothing, because the architects of palaces understand that the man who might die for his king should not have to do it in front of guests.

I sat. I lifted the spoon. I performed the ritual that twenty years had ground into muscle and marrow: smell first, eyes closed, three slow breaths. Then a sip held on the tongue, counted to ten. Then swallow, wait, wait again.

Hemlock.

Not much. A careful amount — the dose of a man who wanted the King sick for a week rather than dead, or perhaps a man testing how good our defenses were, which was worse, because testers come back. It sat on my tongue like a cold coin, that faint bitter-green signature I could identify now the way other men know rain coming. My body registered it the way a house registers a draft: a small tightening, a whisper of numbness at the edges of my mouth, and then nothing. My blood met the poison, considered it, and declined the invitation.

I set down the spoon. I wrote *hemlock, low dose, likely water-parsnip root, added after cooking* on the slate, because a taster who can't say *where* in the dish is only half a weapon. Then I sat for a moment longer than necessary, hands flat on my knees, watching the candle flame stand perfectly straight in air that never moved.

Here is what nobody tells you about surviving what should kill you, over and over, for twenty years: it stops feeling like survival. It starts feeling like paperwork.

The first time I tasted death and lived, I was eleven, and I cried, and Maren held me all night and swore on every saint she could name that no one would ever hurt me again. The tenth time, I was relieved. The hundredth time — I stopped counting after the hundredth, the way you stop counting stairs — the hundredth time I noticed my own heartbeat staying level and felt something I didn't have a word for until years later, when I read it in a smuggled novel: *ennui*. The boredom of a man whose worst day is indistinguishable from his best.

I am very good at my job. I want you to understand that, because everything else about me is going to look like failure soon enough. I am the finest palate in the valley. I can taste the difference between hemlock gathered in spring and summer, between arsenic ground in iron and ground in bone. I have saved the King's life four times that I know of, and probably more that I don't, because a taster's successes are invisible by design. You only ever hear about us when we fail, and the failures are always posthumous.

But some mornings, sitting in that alcove with death cooling on my tongue like tea gone stale, I would catch myself thinking: *and then what?*

And then I report. And then the culprit swings or flees. And then tomorrow there is another pot, and another sip, and another evening where I walk home past the warding stone at the town gate and put my palm against it, the way you'd touch a wall to check for fever, and feel nothing, because the Veil doesn't answer to the people it protects. And then sleep. And then dawn. And then the bell.

A man can live inside a routine the way a snail lives inside its shell, and call the shell a home right up until something taps on it.

* * *

The morning proceeded as mornings do, which is to say it ground forward with the enthusiasm of a millstone. I cleared the broth. I cleared the honeyed rolls (clean), the smoked trout (clean, though the brine was heavy — Halvard's moods announce themselves in salt), and the spiced wine intended for the King's private cup, which carried nutmeg, clove, and a ghost of something floral that took me three sips to place: oleander petals, steeped long enough to leave perfume but not enough to leave harm. A message, not a murder. Someone in the court was reminding the King that they *could*.

I reported it. The steward went pale in his particular way, which is to say he went pale everywhere except around the mouth, where he went pink. Courtiers are weather systems; I have learned to read them the way sailors read clouds.

By midmorning I was free until the evening meal, and I did what I always did with my freedom, which was spend it walking.

Hollowmere Town huddles below the castle like a dog beneath its master's chair: loyal, nervous, and convinced that safety is a location. Cobbled streets, slate roofs, the smell of bread and tanneries and woodsmoke braided together into something I have never once thought of as home but have also never once thought of leaving. Why would I? Everything I am works here. My tongue, my trade, my strange invulnerable blood — all of it belongs to the valley the way a key belongs to its lock. Outside the Veil, I had been told since childhood, magic saturates the world like water saturates a sponge, and a body like mine, a body built without the smallest defense against it, would drink that water and drown in an afternoon.

So I stayed. Obviously. You stay where your one gift functions and your one flaw is fatal. This is not courage or cowardice; it is arithmetic.

I walked down to the market square, bought an apple I didn't want because the seller looked like she needed the sale more than I needed the apple, and ate it on the steps of the temple while the noon bells rang. The temple of the quiet gods — that's what we called them, the gods beyond the Veil, the ones who slept or died or simply stopped answering sometime before my grandmother's grandmother. The temple was beautiful and empty in the way of things maintained out of politeness. Sera Vane presided over it in those days, though I didn't know her name yet. A young priestess with ash-grey eyes who lit candles for gods who never came, and smiled at worshippers with a smile I would later learn was armor.

I remember noticing her, that day, only in the way you notice furniture. Forgive me. I was not yet a man who paid attention to anything that couldn't kill him.

* * *

Evening brought the second sitting, and with it the day's real business: a feast for the eastern lords, six courses, forty dishes, and me moving down the line like a needle through cloth, sampling each stitch. Goose in plum sauce. A custard that fought back — bitter almond, cleverly hidden under orange blossom, and I flagged it and watched a serving girl get dragged out weeping innocence, and told myself the magistrates would sort it out, and knew they wouldn't, and ate the next course.

This is the part of my profession I have never managed to make peace with, and I have stopped trying. Every poison I find has an author. Every author has a reason, and the reasons are usually hunger — for land, for favor, for a dead husband's title. I do not judge them. I have eaten their work and survived it, and they have not survived my finding it, and somewhere in that arithmetic a man could lose his soul if he wasn't careful to keep it locked away during working hours.

I kept mine locked away. That was the arrangement. Numbness in, numbness out. A taster who feels too much starts hesitating, and a hesitating taster is a dead king.

The feast ended. The lords got drunk. The King laughed at something and slapped the table, alive for one more night thanks to my tongue, and never once looked at me — kings don't, we're furniture with a function — and I collected my pay from the steward and walked home through streets gone blue with dusk.

My house sits at the edge of town, last before the fields, close enough to the western warding stone that on clear nights I can see it from my window: a grey finger of rock taller than three men, carved with spirals no scholar has ever translated, humming — not audibly, but the way a struck bell hums after the sound has left it. I have touched that stone ten thousand times. Ten thousand times it has been cold, silent, indifferent, perfect.

Maren was waiting on my step.

You need to understand my sister before you understand anything else that happens, so let me give her to you properly. Maren Voss is four years older than I am, built like our mother — small, quick, all elbows and opinions — with laugh lines she earned honestly and a tiredness around the mouth she tried to hide and never once succeeded. She kept a dyer's shop near the river and a garden behind it and me, in roughly that order of devotion. She was the one who fed me through the fever when I was a child. She was the one who sat up with me after my first tasting, my tenth, my fiftieth. If my life had a foundation, it was her, and I had built on it for thirty-one years without once checking what the foundation was standing on.

She was wringing her hands. Maren did not wring her hands. In nineteen years of knowing her as an adult, I had seen her calm through a fire, a flood, and the death of our mother, and her hands had stayed exactly where hands belong.

"Mare," I said. "You look like you've seen the ward flicker."

It was a joke. It was the kind of joke you make in Hollowmere, where the ward flickering is about as likely as the mountains getting up and walking away, and everyone laughs at it the way you laugh at death — comfortably, from a distance, certain it isn't listening.

She didn't laugh.

She looked past me, at the darkening sky, at the distant stone standing sentinel against the stars, and when she finally spoke her voice came out small in a way I had never heard it, small as a child's, small as it must have been nineteen years ago on whatever night she was about to tell me about.

"Dominic," she said. "Come inside. There's something I have to tell you before winter."

And because I was a man who tasted everything twice, I noticed — even then, even before I understood — that she was shivering, and that the evening was not cold, and that for the first time in my carefully numb, carefully ordered, carefully survivable life, I was afraid of something that had never once appeared in a dish.
