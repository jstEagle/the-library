## 2. What the Ward Keeps Out

The pilgrim arrived on a Tuesday, which everyone agreed was suspicious. Miracles, in the experience of the court of Hollowmere, preferred to arrive on feast days, when there were crowds to witness them and wine already flowing. A Tuesday miracle smelled of desperation, and desperation, like arsenic, had a particular tang I had been trained since boyhood to detect.

I saw her first from the kitchens, where I took my morning broth standing up, as I did everything. She came down the pass road at a limping walk, one hand pressed to her side, her cloak stiff with frost and something darker that I chose not to speculate about. The gate guards stopped her, as they stop everyone, and asked their three questions: name, business, and whether she carried iron.

She answered all three. I couldn't hear the answers from the kitchen window, but I watched the younger guard's face change — that specific loosening, the way a man's jaw drops a quarter inch when he hears something he doesn't believe and wants desperately to. Then he looked over his shoulder toward the keep, toward me, because word travels fast in a valley the size of a soup bowl, and the taster is the closest thing we have to an authority on what can kill you.

By the time I'd crossed the courtyard, a small crowd had gathered with the appetite Hollowmere always has for novelty, which is to say: ravenously, and with immediate suspicion.

"—and it *answered*," the woman was saying. Her voice had the cracked, overused quality of someone who has been repeating herself for days. "Three nights I knelt at the shrine past the pass, the old one, the fallen one, and on the third night the air went silver and a voice — not a voice, more like — it said my daughter would live. And she *will*. She's fevered half to death and this morning the fever broke. This morning! Before I even got home to tell her!"

"Ma'am," said the older guard, whose name was Brannic and who had the patience of a fence post, "the gods don't answer prayers. That's rather the point of the ward."

"The ward keeps magic *out*," she said. "I was *out*."

There it was. The sentence dropped into the crowd like a stone into still water, and I watched the ripples move through faces. Because she was right, of course. The ward kept magic out. Everything beyond the pass was out. Everyone knew the ward worked; no one had ever thought very hard about what that meant for the rest of the world.

I should explain, perhaps, what the ward is — or rather, what it isn't, because nobody in Hollowmere knows what it is, only what it does. It was raised before living memory, before written memory, before the memory of memory. It sits along the valley's rim like a ring of held breath. Nothing magical crosses it. No curse, no blessing, no hex, no healing, no whisper from anything with power. Inside the valley, the world runs on cause and effect alone: bread rises because of yeast, fevers break because of willow bark or luck, and people die because of ordinary, honest reasons — knives, age, bad water, poison.

And poison. Yes. Let's be honest about why the ward matters most to me.

I am Dominic Voss, royal food-taster, and I am the only man in Hollowmere who can swallow hemlock and live to describe its finish. Not immunity exactly — the physicians argue about what to call it. My body simply declines to cooperate with toxins. They pass through me like gossip through a town that has better things to do. It made me valuable at thirty seconds old, apparently, though I don't remember the negotiation. What I remember is twenty years of mornings like this one: sipping suspect broth, cataloguing bitterness, and going home to my sister Maren's cooking, which is the only food I eat for pleasure, because everything else I taste professionally, the way a hangman knows rope.

The magistrates arrived within the hour, because a claim of answered prayer is, in Hollowmere, functionally a report of a crime.

* * *

They held the hearing in the small council chamber, which has terrible acoustics and worse light, and they put the pilgrim — her name was Odile Tanner, widow, of Millbrook, two days' walk downvalley — on a stool like a defendant, which in Hollowmere she effectively was.

Magistrate Corven presided. Corven is a narrow man with narrow views and a voice like a drawer of cutlery being opened slowly. He had brought his clerk, his seal, and his lunch, in that order of importance.

"Describe the voice," Corven said.

"I've told four people—"

"You'll tell me. Describe the voice."

Odile's hands were folded so tightly in her lap that her knuckles had gone the color of the frost on her cloak. "It wasn't a voice. It was more like... remembering something you never learned. Like the knowledge was just *there*, all at once. Silver. Cold. It said, *the debt is paid, the child will live.*"

"*Debt*," Corven repeated, and wrote it down with the relish of a man who has found a hook to hang a prosecution on. "Interesting word. Debts imply creditors. Who taught you that phrase?"

"No one taught me—"

"Someone did. Words come from somewhere. Prayers come from somewhere. And answers—" he tapped the table once, "—answers come from outside the ward, which means either you're lying, mad, or a smuggler of enchantment, and I'll thank you to tell me which."

I stood by the wall, as I always stand by walls, tasting the room instead of the food. And here is what I tasted: fear. Not Corven's — Corven's fear was buried under procedure, where he kept it, like most men keep it. The room's fear. Because every soul in that chamber had been raised on the same catechism: *the ward holds, the ward has always held, the ward is why we are safe.* And a woman sitting on a stool saying *it answered me* was a crack in the floor of the world, and everyone present could feel the draft coming up through it.

Except, I noticed, they weren't afraid of the crack itself. They were afraid of admitting it existed.

"Your daughter's fever broke," said the second magistrate, a softer woman named Hesk. "Physicians see that happen. Fevers break."

"Not after six weeks they don't. Not when the physicians had given her until spring." Odile turned, suddenly, and found me against the wall. Her eyes were red-rimmed and absolutely steady. "You. You're the taster. You know poisons. Tell them — is there a medicine that does what happened to my girl? Is there anything in your books?"

Every eye in the room swung to me. I considered lying, briefly, the way one considers a comfortable chair. But I have spent my life being the man who tells the truth about what's in the cup, and the habit holds under pressure.

"No," I said. "Nothing in our pharmacopoeia pulls a fever like that. If her symptoms are as described, then either she recovered against all natural course..." I let the pause sit. "...or something unnatural recovered her."

The chamber went very quiet.

"Or," said Corven, "she's lying, and her daughter is dead, and grief has invented a god." He rose, gathering his papers. "The court finds insufficient evidence of enchantment. The woman is dismissed. If any further claims of answered prayer arise, they are to be reported and treated as sedition against the peace of the valley. The ward holds. The ward has always held. Court is adjourned."

He said it like a man closing a door against weather. But I watched him as he left, and I saw him glance, just once, up at the high window that faced the pass. Just once. Long enough.

That glance stayed with me all afternoon, the way a bone stays lodged.

* * *

Here is a thing about me that few people know, and fewer understand: I test things. Professionally, yes — but privately too. Compulsively. When Maren cooks, I taste the salt before she adds it and after, and I can tell her which well it came from. When the merchants bring spice up the pass road, I can tell you which caravan, which season, whether the saffron was stretched with marigold. My tongue is a ledger, and it never fully closes.

So that evening, after supper, after Maren had gone to bed with the cough she'd been nursing for a month — a small thing, a nothing, but I listened to it through the wall the way you listen to a clock that might be slowing — I told her I was taking a walk. She didn't question it. Twenty years of my odd hours have worn grooves of acceptance into her.

The ward's edge nearest our house is a forty-minute climb above the terraces, where the valley wall goes steep and the pines give way to bare granite. There's a shepherd's cairn there, and a tradition: children are brought up on their tenth birthday to touch the boundary stone and feel nothing, and are told, *this is what safety feels like.* I had done it myself, at ten. I remembered the nothing of it. The clean, warm, absolute nothing.

I climbed in the dark without a lantern, because I know the path the way I know the back of my own hand, and because some part of me — the part that tastes hemlock without flinching — wanted to do this alone and unannounced.

The boundary stone stands at the top, a waist-high slab of grey granite carved with the old sigils that no one can read anymore. Beyond it, maybe ten feet, the air changes. You can't see the change. You can't hear it. But every person in Hollowmere can *feel* it, the way you feel the threshold of a house — the sense of inside and outside, of held and holding. On my tenth birthday I had stood at that line and felt the ward press back against my palm like the surface of deep water.

I reached the stone and stopped.

The night was clear and cold, stars hard as nailheads. The pass wound away below me, black and silver. Somewhere out there, past the line, was a world where a widow's prayer had been answered — or a widow had lied, or grieved herself into invention. One of those three things was true, and two of them were safe, and I found I could not make myself believe in the safe ones anymore. Corven could. Corven had a lifetime of practice. But I am paid to notice what others miss, and what I had noticed in that chamber was the shape of a lie being maintained by exhausted people: the too-quick dismissal, the charge of sedition, the glance at the window.

I put my hand out toward the line.

Ten feet from the stone, the feeling of the ward should rise against your skin like the pressure change before a storm. It's how the guards check the boundary each solstice — palms out, walking the rim, confirming the pressure. Uniform. Eternal. Boring, even, in its reliability.

My hand met the line and felt—

Flicker.

I want to describe it precisely, because precision is the only prayer I know. Imagine a lamp behind frosted glass — steady, warm, constant your whole life — and imagine that tonight, for less than a heartbeat, the light *stuttered*. Not dimmed. Stuttered. Like a swallowed syllable. Like a pulse skipping. For one instant the pressure wasn't there, and in that instant I felt something on the other side of the line that I can only call *attention*. Vast, cold, and interested. Turning toward me the way a head turns.

Then it was gone. The pressure returned, steady as ever, warm as ever, and I was standing on a mountainside with my arm out and my heart slamming against my ribs like a prisoner against a door.

I did what any sensible man would do. I tested it again. Fourteen times, actually, over the next hour, palms flat, breath steaming, counting heartbeats between touches. Twelve flickers. Two touches where the ward felt entirely normal. The flickers weren't regular — they came in clusters, like rain starting, like something on the other side pressing experimentally, learning the membrane, finding the thin places.

At some point I realized I was shivering, and it wasn't the cold.

Because here is the arithmetic that kept running under the thought, the way a sour note runs under a song: if the ward flickered, then the ward could fail. If the ward could fail, then everything it kept out could get in. And everything it kept out included, presumably, the things that had made the ward necessary in the first place — the things our ancestors had been willing to wall off the entire sky to avoid.

But there was a second arithmetic, quieter and worse, and it was this: the ward had never been tested in my lifetime. Nobody alive had ever seen it hold against anything, because nothing had ever tried. We had built our entire lives — my profession, my strange body, Maren's kitchen, Corven's court, the whole drowsy valley — on the assumption that a wall none of us had ever inspected was load-bearing.

I had spent my career trusting other people's assurances about what was safe to swallow. I knew exactly how much a reassurance weighs.

I walked home in the dark, faster than dignity allowed.

* * *

Maren was awake when I came in, sitting by the banked fire with a blanket around her shoulders, and the sight of her hit me strangely — harder than usual. Perhaps it was the flicker still echoing in my palm. Perhaps it was the memory of Odile Tanner saying *my daughter will live* with that terrible steadiness, and the thought, arriving unbidden and unwelcome, that I would burn down every law in the valley for the same sentence about my sister.

"You're pale," she said. "Even for you."

"Went for a walk."

"To the boundary stone." She said it flatly, not a question. Maren has always known my habits better than I announce them. "Dominic. Why?"

I considered telling her. I opened my mouth to tell her — and what came out instead was a question I hadn't planned, one of those questions that has clearly been waiting years in some back room of the mind and chooses its own moment to walk out.

"Maren. When I was a baby. When I was sick — really sick, the sickness that killed other children that winter. What actually happened? Not the story. What *happened*?"

The fire ticked. Somewhere outside, a dog barked twice and thought better of it.

Maren looked at me for a long moment, and I watched something cross her face — something I had never seen there before, and I have spent my life reading faces across tables where the wrong read means death. It wasn't guilt, exactly. It was the expression of a person watching a door they'd braced shut for nineteen years suddenly swing open on its own.

"You're shivering," she said finally. "Sit down. I'll make tea."

"Maren."

"I said I'll make tea." Her hands were shaking as she reached for the kettle, and she wouldn't look at me, and outside my window, far up the mountain, the ward I had flickered fourteen times stood between us and the dark like a lamp behind frosted glass —

and I no longer believed, even a little, that it was burning steadily.
