## 3. A Debt Spoken Aloud

My sister had never once, in thirty-one years of living under the same crooked roof, asked me for anything. She fed me when I was broke, stitched my court livery when the palace seamstresses were too proud to mend a taster's coat, and buried our mother with money she pretended she'd been saving for a loom. So when I came home on the third evening after the pilgrim and found Maren sitting in the dark without a lamp lit — in autumn, when the light dies by mid-afternoon and any sensible person would have candles going — I knew before she said a word that something had gone badly wrong.

"Maren," I said. "Where's your fire?"

"Gone out." Her voice was thin as onion skin.

I knelt at the hearth and coaxed it back from the embers, because that's what you do, because small tasks are how men like me keep their hands from shaking. The room smelled of cold ash and something else underneath it — fever-sweat and dried herbs, willow bark and bitterroot, the pharmacy of someone who'd been fighting an illness alone for days rather than trouble anyone.

"How long have you been sick?"

"A few days." A lie. The pile of herb-stalks by the basin said closer to two weeks. "It's nothing. A chill."

I put my palm against her forehead and she flinched from the touch like I'd struck her, which frightened me more than the heat coming off her skin. My sister had set broken bones without weeping. She'd once pulled a fishhook through her own thumb with pliers and a mouthful of curses. And now she was flinching from her brother's hand.

"You're burning," I said. "You need a physician."

"I need you to sit down, Dom." She looked at me then, really looked, and there was something in her face I'd never seen before and hope never to see again: a person measuring how much time they have left to say a thing, and finding it too little. "Please. Before I lose my nerve again."

"Again?"

"Nineteen years of nerve," she said, "and I've lost it every single day."

* * *

I sat. Of course I sat. You spend your life waiting on the whims of princes and you learn to sit when told, hands folded, expression pleasant, stomach ready.

She wouldn't take the chair across from me. She paced instead, wrapped in our mother's old shawl even though the fire was climbing now and the room was warming, and she talked to the floor more than to me. It took her three tries to start.

"You remember when you were seven," she said. "The winter sickness."

I remembered it the way you remember a fire — not the details but the fact of it, the shape of the burn. The sweating sickness had come up the valley road with a wool merchant and taken forty children in six weeks. I'd been one of its favorites. Fourteen days of fever, they told me later; I recalled only fragments, our mother's face swimming above mine like a moon underwater, the taste of everything turning to copper, and then — nothing. Then waking, and being fine. Better than fine. Being *fixed*, in some way no one could name, because after that winter I never caught so much as a head-cold, and the poisons that should have killed a food-taster inside his first year at court simply... didn't.

"The physicians called it a miracle," I said. "The priests called it providence. Mother called it luck and made me thank every saint in the calendar anyway."

"Mother didn't know." Maren stopped pacing. Her hands were working at each other, twisting, and I noticed for the first time how thin her wrists had gotten. "Nobody knew. That was the whole point of nobody knowing."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside a cart went by, wheels grinding on frozen mud, and I remember thinking, absurdly, that the world was continuing to make its ordinary noises while my sister stood in front of me looking like a woman about to confess to a murder.

"Dom, what do you know about the court beneath the mountain?"

And there it was — the phrase from the pilgrim woman, the one Magistrate Corvel had sneered at in the council chamber while I stood at the back with my tasting spoon and my professional silence. Cold walked up my spine on little feet.

"Fairy stories," I said. "Old wives' tales. A court of the dead under Hollowmere Peak that buys souls from desperate folk. Grandmother used to threaten us with it. *Behave, or the mountain-folk will come collect.*"

"It's not a story." Her voice cracked down the middle. "Dom, it's not a story, and I need you to let me finish before you say anything, because if you interrupt me I'll stop, and if I stop now I will never start again, and then we'll both die wondering."

I let her finish. It was the hardest thing I did that night, and I once drank a broth laced with enough hemlock to stop a horse's heart.

* * *

She'd been eleven. Our mother was dying — not the slow kind of dying, either, but the quick kind, the lung-rot that took her breath away in fistfuls — and there was no medicine, no coin, no kin to beg from, because our father had already gone into the ground and the valley had already decided the Thackerays were finished. And then I got sick. Both of them at once, mother and son, and Maren — eleven years old, barefoot, alone — went out into the worst blizzard of that decade and climbed toward Hollowmere Peak because a peddler's tale had said there were doors in the mountain for those who truly needed them, and doors meant someone behind them, and someone behind them meant someone who might bargain.

"They answered," she said. "God help me, Dom, they *answered*. There was a door in the rock where no door should be, and it opened before I knocked twice. And inside there was..." She pressed the heels of her hands against her eyes. "I can't describe it. Not won't. *Can't.* Every time I try, the memory goes smooth and gray, like a stone worn down by water. But I remember the voice. It wasn't unkind. That's the worst part. It was patient and reasonable and it explained everything so clearly, the way you'd explain terms to a merchant, and I understood all of it, and I was eleven, and I said yes."

"Said yes to what?" My own voice sounded like it belonged to someone else.

"To a trade." She lowered her hands. Her eyes were wet and absolutely steady, and I realized she'd rehearsed this confession so many times in the dark that the words had been polished smooth as river stones. "They would cure you. Completely. Not just the fever — they'd remake something deep in you, so that no poison, no magic, no ill-working of any kind could ever touch you again. Immunity. Total and permanent." A breath. "In exchange, they would hold my soul in escrow. Nineteen years of grace. And at the end of the nineteen years, they collect."

The fire had gone quiet. Even it, apparently, wanted to hear.

"That's absurd," I heard myself say. "Maren, that's — souls aren't *collateral*, that's not how anything works—"

"It's exactly how things work," she said, "outside the ward. Inside the valley we tell ourselves the world is tame. We built a wall around our little garden and called the garden the world. But the wall is just a wall, Dom, and on the other side of it are powers older than the valley, older than the crown, and they keep ledgers. They kept mine. I've felt it since the day you woke up healthy. Like a hook behind my breastbone, thin as thread. Some mornings I could almost hear paper rustling."

"When?" I asked, though I already knew. I'd known since the council chamber, since the pilgrim, since the flicker at the ward's edge that I'd felt under my palm like a skipped heartbeat. "When do they come?"

"Winter solstice." She said it the way you'd read a sentence at a funeral. "Nineteen years from the night I signed. Six weeks from tonight."

Six weeks. I did the arithmetic the way I did everything — automatically, precisely, uselessly. Sixty-two days until the longest night. Sixty-two days of broth and bread and bowing to magistrates, sixty-two mornings of sipping poison and surviving it, and every single one of them purchased with a piece of my sister that I had never once thought to question.

Because here is the thing I could not get past, the thing that sat in my chest like a swallowed stone: I had built my entire life on that immunity. The career, the wages, the strange prestige of the man who cannot be poisoned — I'd worn it like a talent, like something I'd earned. And it wasn't mine. It had never been mine. It was bought goods, secondhand salvation, paid for by an eleven-year-old girl walking barefoot into a blizzard because the adults in her life had failed her so completely that bargaining with the dark seemed reasonable.

"You were a child," I said finally. "They took a contract from a *child*."

"They took a contract from someone desperate," Maren corrected gently, and that gentleness was unbearable. "That's their whole business, I think. Desperation is the door. The door opened for me because I meant it. I would have given anything. They simply told me the price." She smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was fond. "And look what it bought. Look at you. Thirty-one years old and strong as an ox. It was worth it, Dom. That's what I need you to understand. If I died tomorrow with my soul in their vault, it would still have been worth it."

"Don't say that."

"Somebody has to say it. You certainly won't."

* * *

We argued after that, in the tired way families argue — circling the same ground, wounding each other with accuracy. I told her we'd find a way to break the contract. She laughed at me, actually laughed, a dry cracked sound. I told her I'd go to the magistrates, petition the crown, invoke whatever ancient law protected valley-born souls from foreign claims. She shook her head slowly, and the pity in it stung worse than mockery.

"The ward keeps magic out, Dom. It doesn't keep contracts out. Paper crosses borders every day. Whatever they hold on me, it's valid here — maybe it's *more* valid here, maybe the ward was part of the deal somehow, I don't know, I've never dared find out. All I know is that for nineteen years I've felt that hook, and last month—" her breath hitched, the first true break in her composure, "—last month it started to *pull*. Little tugs. Like something reeling in a line. And then that pilgrim woman came through saying the gods are waking, and I knew. I knew the season changed. Debts come due when the debtor thinks he can collect."

I crossed the room and took her hands, and this time she didn't flinch. Her fingers were ice despite the fever.

"Then we fight it," I said. "Together. I don't care what it costs."

"You don't know what you're saying." Her voice dropped to a whisper. "You've lived your whole life inside the veil, Dominic. You don't know what's out there. You don't know what they are. I caught one glimpse through that door and nineteen years later I still wake up hearing that patient voice explaining terms to me. If you go poking at this—"

"If I *don't*," I said, "then in six weeks something comes to collect my sister, and I go on sipping soup for princes until I die of old age, full and safe and sick to my stomach every day for the rest of my life. I've tasted every poison in the world, Maren. I know exactly what regret tastes like. I'm not doing it."

She stared at me for a long moment. Outside, the wind had picked up, worrying at the shutters, and beneath it — or I imagined beneath it — there was a sound I couldn't name. Not quite knocking. Not quite whispering. Something patient, far off, taking notes.

"You always were the stubborn one," she said at last, and lay back against the pillows, exhausted by the confession as if it had been a physical labor, which perhaps it had. "Promise me one thing. Before you run off to the magistrates with your petitions and your good intentions. Promise you'll think about what breaking this contract might cost, and whether you're willing to pay it. Because they don't do refunds, Dom. And they don't do charity. Everything has a price, and they always, always tell you what it is first. That's the courtesy of the damned."

"I promise," I said, and I meant it the way you mean things at deathbeds — completely, and knowing you'll break it.

She slept after that, truly slept, the fever finally breaking somewhere in the small hours while I sat beside her bed watching the fire eat itself down. And I sat there doing the thing I do best, the thing I was apparently bred — *bred*, some traitor corner of my mind whispered, *chosen, cultivated* — to do.

I turned the problem over on my tongue like a suspect dish.

Immunity. Nineteen years. A child's signature. A debt due at solstice. And underneath it all, faint as arsenic in wine, a question I couldn't stop tasting:

Why would the court beneath the mountain want my sister's soul at all — unless my soul, the immune, untouchable, unspoiled thing they'd built with her bargain, was the vintage they'd been aging all along?

The fire died. I didn't relight it.

Outside, far up the black shoulder of Hollowmere Peak, something that was not wind began, very softly, to count.
