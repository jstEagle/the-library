## 4. The Last Safe Supper

The Magistrate's Office of Hollowmere occupied the ground floor of the old customs house, which was fitting, because that was exactly how the crown saw its citizens: as goods moving through a checkpoint. I had walked past it every working day for eleven years without once going in. Tasters don't need magistrates. We are the law's last instrument — the palate before the noose — and there is a certain comfort in being an instrument. Instruments don't have problems of their own.

Maren's confession had ruined that comfort in the space of one feverish night, and so on a grey Tuesday smelling of wet slate and yesterday's fish, I pushed open the customs house door and asked to file a petition regarding a soul-contract.

The clerk behind the desk looked up at me with the expression of a man who has heard everything twice and believed none of it once.

"Name?"

"Dominic Voss."

"Occupation?"

"Royal food-taster."

Something flickered behind his eyes — recognition, maybe, or the particular boredom of a bureaucrat meeting a minor celebrity. "Voss. You're the one who drank the hemlock at Lord Aldery's investiture. They say you didn't even blink."

"I blinked," I said. "Afterward. When no one was watching." I slid my petition across the desk. It had taken me until three in the morning to write, mostly because I kept stopping to press my palms against my eyes and ask the ceiling how my life had come to this. "I'm seeking information on a contract held by the court beneath the mountain. A soul-debt contracted nineteen years ago by Maren Voss of Tanner's Row. I'd like to know the terms, the balance outstanding, and whether it can be redeemed for consideration paid by a third party."

The clerk read it. His eyebrows performed a slow migration upward, like two caterpillars who had finally found something worth climbing for.

"You're serious."

"Deadly."

He laughed. Not cruelly — that was almost worse. He laughed the way you laugh at a child who has announced she'll marry the moon: fond, dismissive, already turning back to his ledger. "Master Voss. There's no procedure for this. The contracts below aren't crown instruments. The crown doesn't recognize them, doesn't enforce them, and — this is the important part — cannot buy them back. The magistrates' charter is explicit. What passes beneath the pass is beneath the crown."

"My sister is a citizen of Hollowmere."

"Her body is. Her—" he tapped the petition, delicately, as if the word might stain him, "—the rest of her is foreign-held property. If the collectors come for her at solstice, we're obligated to... well." He shrugged. "Not obstruct them."

"Not obstruct them," I repeated.

"It's in the Accords. Page forty. You're welcome to read it." He said it kindly, which somehow made it obscene. The kindness of a man explaining that the fire consuming your house is, regrettably, scheduled.

I did read page forty. I read pages one through sixty-three, actually, hunched in the archive annex while dust settled into my collar like a second skin. The Accords of Hollowmere were older than the current dynasty, older than the ward itself if the marginalia could be trusted, and they were written in the language of all such documents: smooth, reasonable, and soaked through with the assumption that whatever they protected deserved protecting and whatever they abandoned had been weighed and found wanting.

*The Court Below shall hold sole jurisdiction over all souls freely pledged within its demesne,* said Article Twelve. *The Crown of Hollowmere, in exchange for continued exclusion of wild magic from the valley, waives all claim of protection over such pledges.*

Freely pledged. I thought of Maren at nineteen, sitting up all night beside my fevered crib while our mother wept in the doorway, and I wondered what the word *freely* meant to the man who drafted that sentence. I wondered what it would have meant to him with his own child dying in the room.

At noon I climbed the stairs to the magistrate's actual office — Magistrate Corren Hale, a broad, ruddy man whose portrait hung in the palace corridor where I passed it daily, and whose face I now wore a groove in from staring at it. He received me standing, which I took as a bad sign, and offered me wine, which I took as a worse one. A taster learns to read hospitality the way sailors read weather.

"Magistrate Hale. Thank you for seeing me."

"For the man who saved the king from the wolfsbane at midwinter?" He smiled broadly. "Any time. Though I suspect this isn't a social call."

I laid out the whole of it. Maren. The debt. The solstice. I kept my voice level, the way I keep my voice level when I'm swallowing something bitter and want the table to believe it's water. I even used the word *consideration*, having learned it from page twelve. I watched his face the entire time, and here is what I saw: not disbelief. That was the thing that hollowed me out afterward, lying awake. Not disbelief. *Recognition.* He had heard this before. Perhaps many times. Perhaps every year, when the solstice came round and the desperate came climbing the customs house stairs with their petitions and their hope, and he sent them away with wine and sympathy and page forty.

"Master Voss," he said, when I finished. "Sit down."

"I'd rather stand."

"Suit yourself." He poured himself wine, didn't offer again. "What you're describing is real. I won't insult you by pretending otherwise. Souls go under the mountain; debts come due; collectors cross when the ward thins. It's been happening since before my grandfather's grandfather. And every few years someone like you comes through that door and asks us to fix it, and I tell them the truth, which is that we can't. The ward keeps magic *out*, Voss. It doesn't reach *down*. Whatever happens beneath the pass is outside our writ, outside our power, and — forgive me — outside our interest."

"Outside your interest. She's a tanner's daughter. She mends saddles. She taught me to whistle."

"And I'm sorry." He said it and meant it, and both of those things were true and neither of them mattered. "But think about what you're asking. If the crown moved against the court below — if we contested a contract, or harbored a debtor, or so much as *queried* a ledger — the Accords lapse. The ward falls. Every bargain, every pledge, every scrap of old business comes due at once, across the whole valley. Do you understand? One girl's soul against ten thousand people's safety. Any magistrate who made that trade wouldn't be a magistrate long. He'd be a memory, and a cursed one."

"One girl," I said. "You've counted her already. That was fast."

His smile died. "That was arithmetic."

"Arithmetic." I tasted the word the way I taste everything, hunting for the poison in it, and found it immediately: it was in the calm. In the certainty that some lives were line items. I had served this law for eleven years. I had knelt at coronations and sworn at funerals. I had put my tongue between the king and death more times than I could count, and the whole time, the law I was tasting for had a page — page forty — that said my sister was nobody's problem.

"Magistrate," I said, "if I put poison in the king's cup tomorrow, what would happen to me?"

He blinked. "You'd be executed. Drawn, probably. It's treason."

"Even though I only poured it. Even though I didn't brew it. Even though someone else made the bargain and I just — carried the cup."

"That's different."

"How?"

"Because the king matters."

There it was. No varnish, no page number. I nodded slowly, thanked him for the wine I hadn't drunk, and let myself out. My hands didn't shake until I reached the street, and then they shook so badly I had to hold onto a lamppost like a drunkard while the valley went on around me — carts, gulls, a child chasing a hoop, the ordinary machinery of a place that believed itself safe.

Here is what I learned that day, and I learned it completely, the way you learn a flavor you'll never forget: the law was not blind. The law had eyes, and it had looked at Maren Voss, and it had seen collateral.

* * *

I cooked supper that night. It's what I do instead of praying — hands busy, knife steady, the small liturgy of salt and heat. Maren sat at the table with a blanket over her shoulders even though the hearth was going, watching me with the guilty vigilance of someone who has confessed a crime and is waiting for sentencing.

"You went to the magistrates," she said. Not a question. News travels fast on Tanner's Row, faster than shame.

"I went to the magistrates."

"They laughed at you."

"The clerk did. The magistrate was too dignified. He just explained, very patiently, that my sister is a rounding error." I set down the bowl — barley and lamb, rosemary, nothing exotic, nothing anyone would ever poison — and sat across from her. "Maren. I want you to tell me everything. All of it, this time. Not the fever version. The whole contract."

She turned her spoon over. Turned it back. "There's not much more. I told you — the crossroads, the rain, the voice like a ledger opening. Nineteen years ago. I signed."

"What did you sign *with*? Ink? Blood?"

"Ink. Black ink, but it smelled wrong. Sweet, like overripe plums." Her hand drifted to her sternum, an unconscious gesture I'd seen a hundred times and never understood until now. "And there was a clause. I remember the collector reading it back to me, slow, the way you'd read a recipe. Something about... surety. About the debt being secured." She looked up. "Dominic, I didn't understand half of it. I was nineteen and my brother was dying and the thing in the dark said it could make him immune to poison forever, and I would have signed away the sun if it asked."

The candle guttered. Outside, the wind had changed — I noticed it the way a taster notices everything, professionally, helplessly — carrying a smell off the mountain that didn't belong to wind. Cold iron. Old paper. Plums gone soft.

"Maren," I said quietly. "When you gave your soul to buy my immunity. Did they ever say the immunity was *for* anything?"

"What do you mean?"

"I mean nothing. Eat your supper."

She ate. I watched her eat, cataloguing her the way I catalogue every dish — color, texture, the tremor in her hand, the gray at her temples that hadn't been there a month ago — and I hated myself for it, because cataloguing is what you do to things that might be taken from you, and I had spent my whole life learning to survive loss by tasting it in advance.

It was near midnight when the dog started.

Tanner's Row kept dogs the way other streets kept gossip, and ours — a brindled bitch named Sorrel who belonged to the cooper three doors down — began to scream. Not bark. Scream, high and continuous, the sound of an animal confronting something its blood remembered and its mind refused. Then every dog on the row joined in, and then, horribly, they all stopped at once, mid-note, like a choir cut by a conductor's fist.

Maren and I were at the window before either of us decided to move.

The fog had come up from the river, thick and wrong, clinging to the cobbles in shapes that didn't drift so much as *arrange* themselves. And in the middle of the street, where the lamplight should have reached and didn't, stood a figure. Tall. Too still. It wore a coat the color of ledgers and it stood with the patience of furniture, and though I couldn't see its face I felt — physically felt, the way you feel a draft — the sensation of being *read*. Of being opened to a certain page.

It raised one hand. Long fingers, ink-stained to the knuckle. And it drew, in the air above our door, a single looping mark that glowed faintly plum-sweet and then sank into the wood like water into cloth.

Then the fog folded around it, and it was gone, and the dogs began again all together as if released.

Maren's hand found mine in the dark. Hers was ice.

"It marked the house," she whispered. "Dominic, it marked the house. That means — that means the debt's been called forward. That means it isn't waiting for solstice anymore."

I stood in my doorway, in the valley that had kept me safe my entire life, behind a ward that had begun to flicker, under a law that had filed my sister under *not our problem*, and I understood with total clarity that no one was coming to help us. Not the crown. Not the ward. Not the godless quiet of the sky.

Whatever happened next, we would have to do ourselves.

I went inside and packed a bag.
