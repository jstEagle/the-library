## 5. Crossing the Line

I had spent nineteen years learning every way a body can be poisoned, and it turned out I'd never once considered the possibility that a person could simply *be* poison — that the whole world beyond Hollowmere's ward might sit in my stomach like bad shellfish and stay there.

But I'm getting ahead of myself. Let me describe the dawn properly, because it deserves to be remembered accurately, even by someone as unreliable as I was that morning.

* * *

The ward's edge was a half-mile east of the tannery road, marked by nothing. That was the joke of it, if you thought about it too long: the most important thing in the valley had no wall, no gate, no stone with a carved warning. Just a line in the grass where the air changed texture, the way water changes when a current runs beneath it. Children were taught to feel for it the way they were taught to swim — hold your breath, reach out slow, don't go past where your skin prickles.

I had reached past that line exactly twice in my life, both times as a dare, both times before my tenth birthday, when Maren still had the reflexes of a feral cat and could yank me back by my collar before anything worse than a nosebleed arrived.

Now she was feverish in our mother's old bed, sweating out words she couldn't take back: *nineteen years ago, solstice, sold, sold, sold.* And now the mark on our door — a black sigil like an inkblot shaped like a hand — that hadn't been there yesterday and would not scrub off, no matter how hard I tried, no matter that I knew every solvent known to cooks and apothecaries alike.

So. Dawn. The tannery road. A satchel containing: two days of bread, a flask of well-water, my good knife, salt (habit; you'll understand), and the stolen page torn from a magistrate's ledger that proved what Maren said was true, written in ink that moved when you weren't looking at it directly, which is the sort of thing you'd think a man would have noticed about his own government sometime in thirty-four years.

I stood at the line and felt the ward against my palm like the skin of warm water.

Here is what nobody tells you about leaving home forever: it looks exactly like leaving home temporarily. You pack the same things. You hesitate at the same door. You tell yourself the same comfortable lie — *I'll be back by dark, I'll be back by week's end, I'll be back* — because the truth is too heavy to lift on an empty stomach.

"I'll be back," I told the grass, and stepped through.

* * *

The first three seconds were fine.

I want that on the record. Three full seconds of nothing, just morning air and wet grass and the distant complaint of the tannery dogs, and I remember thinking, with the specific stupidity of a man who has made survival his profession and therefore believes he understands survival: *that's it? Nineteen years of dread, and it's nothing?*

Then the magic hit.

I say "hit" because there's no better word for it. It didn't seep or drift or wash over me. It *struck*, the way a wave strikes a man standing in shallow water who has made the error of turning his back on the sea. Except this wave came from everywhere — from the ground, from the sky, from the light itself — and it did not break and recede. It kept coming.

Imagine tasting everything at once. Not food — everything. Iron in the soil, sap climbing in the trees, the mineral patience of stones, the sour electric tang of a storm building forty miles off, the copper-and-wool smell of a living body (mine) from the inside. Imagine all of it arriving on your tongue simultaneously, at full strength, with no way to spit.

I got maybe ten steps before my legs informed me they were resigning.

I vomited into the ditch. Then again, further along, though I don't remember walking there. My eyes stopped agreeing with each other; the world split into two pale copies of itself that refused to align. Sounds went strange and enormous — birdsong like breaking crockery, my own heartbeat like someone hammering on a door at the bottom of a well.

This, I understood distantly, through the roaring, was what the ward had actually been doing all those years. Not keeping danger out. Keeping *me* numb. Every taste I'd ever survived — hemlock, arsenic, the nightshade broth in chapter one of my miserable career — I'd survived because the ward had been holding the world's noise down to a murmur, and now the murmur was a shout and my body, bred quiet, had no defenses worth the name.

Livestock, raised in a quiet pen, let out into weather for the first time.

I crawled. I'm not proud of it, but pride is a luxury for men whose organs are functioning. I crawled off the road into the bracken because some animal part of my brain still remembered the first rule of being poisoned in public: *get out of sight.* Whatever was happening to me, I did not want to be found lying in a ditch looking like a man the world had tried to eat.

The world, as it turned out, had other plans for who would find me.

* * *

I heard her boots before I saw her — unhurried, deliberate, the walk of someone who has decided that running attracts attention. Through my doubled vision she resolved slowly into a woman perhaps fifty, gray-streaked hair cut short and practical, wearing the travel-stained remains of vestments I recognized from funerals: the waking gods' order, sun-and-key embroidered at the collar, though the embroidery had been picked at, worried at, half-unraveled by fingers that had clearly done it many times over many nights.

She crouched beside me. She did not touch me. Points for that.

"Valley-born," she said. Not a question. "You're reacting to ambient magic like it's plague air."

"Is it," I managed, "supposed to feel like swallowing a forge?"

"No." She sounded genuinely interested, which I resented. "Most people feel nothing at all. You're built wrong for the outside. Or rather—" her gaze flicked back toward the invisible line behind us, and something in her face hardened, "—built *deliberately* wrong. Who bred you?"

"Bred. Charming. I'm a food taster."

"A taster." She exhaled through her nose, and it might have been a laugh if laughter hadn't apparently gone extinct in her mouth. "Of course you are. Of course they'd use one of you for that."

"Use one of us for—"

"Later." Her head snapped up, toward the road, and every line of her body changed. "Down. Now. Don't breathe."

I pressed my face into the wet bracken, which tasted of rot and iron and, faintly, of something cold and clean and utterly inhuman — my first taste of true magic up close, and even drowning in sensory agony, some professional corner of my mind noted it with detached horror: *cold, clean, no earthly origin, do not ingest.*

The collector came up the road like a rumor made solid.

I saw it in fragments between fronds: tall, too tall, wrapped in a gray that wasn't cloth so much as the memory of cloth. Its face was smooth where a face should be, and where eyes should be there were two small ledger-lines of light, moving, as though it were reading something. Reading *me.*

It stopped. The light-lines narrowed.

"It can smell valley blood," the priestess breathed, barely a sound at all. "Don't move. Don't pray. Praying is louder than screaming."

The collector drifted off the road. Toward the bracken. Toward us. Each step made no sound and yet I felt them in my teeth, like a tuning fork struck against my skull.

And here is the part I will be honest about, because a man who lies in his own memoirs is wasting everyone's time: I was terrified. Not brave-terrified, not storybook-terrified. I was sick, blind, shaking, twenty feet from the only place in the world I had ever been safe, and every instinct I owned was screaming at me to crawl back through the ward and bar the door and let the solstice come and let Maren's soul be filed away in whatever cathedral of ledgers held it, because *at least I would live.*

That instinct lasted approximately four seconds. Then I thought of Maren's face when she confessed — the way she watched me like a woman watching a lit fuse she'd laid herself, waiting for the explosion, ready to accept it — and the terror did something strange. It didn't leave. It *turned*. Fear pointing inward became fear pointing outward, and outward-pointing fear has another name.

The collector was six feet away when the priestess stood up.

"Sera Vane," she announced, loud and clear, in a voice that had clearly once filled a cathedral and had lost none of its architecture. "Disgraced priestess of the Nine Who Woke. Formerly of the Order of the Opened Door. I invoke the Rite of Witness. You know it. Your masters wrote it. A mortal under witness may not be taken without answer given."

The collector paused. The light-lines flickered — reading, searching.

"You have no standing," it said. Its voice was dry paper and closing doors. "Your god disowned you."

"My gods woke up last month," Sera Vane said. "Which means their rites woke up with them. Check."

A silence. The light-lines flickered faster. Somewhere far above us, I would learn later, something vast and irritated consulted something older and more irritated, and the arithmetic came back unfavorable.

"This mortal is contracted," the collector said at last.

"He isn't. His sister is. And contracts name their parties." She smiled, and it was not a kind smile; it was the smile of a woman who had spent thirty years praying into silence and had recently discovered that silence could be beaten with paperwork. "You've been harvesting illiterate peasants for centuries. Try it on someone who reads."

The collector regarded her for a long moment. Then it regarded me — I felt those ledger-lines pass over me like a clerk's finger down a column — and it inclined its smooth head a precise, insulting fraction.

"Witnessed," it said. "For now. The debt comes due regardless, taster. Solstice does not negotiate." And it poured itself back up the road and was gone, the way a stain leaves when you finally stop looking at it.

* * *

I lay in the bracken a while longer, mostly because standing seemed ambitious.

"Well," Sera Vane said. She sat down across from me, cross-legged, and produced a flask. "Drink. Slowly. It's just well-water — I checked it for hexes, which is more courtesy than you'll get from anyone else on this road."

I drank. It was the best water of my life, which says less about the water than about the morning.

"You invoked a rite," I said. "You bought us time with *liturgy.*"

"Liturgy is the only weapon the mountain court honors. They wrote half of it." She looked east, toward the mountains, and her jaw did something complicated. "They wrote the other half too. That's rather the problem."

"You said 'bred.' Back there. You asked who bred me like I was a—"

"A cow? A vine? Yes." She met my eyes, and hers were the particular gray of a sky that has given up pretending it won't rain. "You're going to want to argue with me about that. Most people do, right up until they don't. But not here, and not while you're turning colors." She stood, briskly, and offered me a hand. "Can you walk?"

I took her hand. It was the first kindness anyone had offered me since Maren's confession, and it steadied me more than the water had.

"I walked a mile vomiting and half-blind," I said. "I can walk."

"That's either courage or stupidity."

"In my experience they're the same ingredient, differently measured."

Something happened to her face then — not quite a smile, but the ghost of where a smile used to live, like the picked-at embroidery at her collar. "Come on, taster. There's a shrine two miles north. Abandoned. We can shelter there while I decide how much of the truth you can survive."

"I survive poisons for a living," I said, and hauled myself upright, and found — to my genuine surprise — that my legs held, and that the world's terrible chorus had faded to something a man could work under, the way a cook works under the roar of a kitchen. "Truth is just another toxin. Give me the dose and I'll tell you if it kills me."

We walked north, away from the ward, away from the only life I had ever known, toward a mountain where my sister's soul sat filed like a vintage in a cellar of the damned.

Behind us, faint as a memory, I felt the ward flicker again — weaker this time, thinner, like a candle guttering — and somewhere in the valley it was protecting, I heard, very distantly, a dog begin to bark.

Sera Vane heard it too. She glanced back once.

"They're coming through already," she said quietly. "Your pen door doesn't latch anymore. Walk faster."

I walked faster.
