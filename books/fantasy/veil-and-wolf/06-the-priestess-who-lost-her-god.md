## 6. The Priestess Who Lost Her God

I want it on the record that I did not save Dominic Voss out of kindness.

Kindness is a luxury for people whose gods still return their letters. What I felt, crouched over that retching valley-boy in the ditch while a collector's shadow slid across the wheat like oil across water, was something colder and more useful: recognition. Here was another creature the divine machinery had chewed up and spat back out, blinking and furious about it. You don't leave your own kind lying in a ditch.

Also, he was between me and the road, and I am, at heart, a practical woman.

"Can you walk?" I asked him, after the collector had passed — drawn off by a farmer's prayer two fields over, because the gods had woken hungry and were answering everything now, everything, at rates no honest priest would quote aloud.

He lifted his head. His face was the color of old candle wax, his eyes streaming, and there was vomit on the collar of what had probably once been very fine court livery. "Walk," he repeated, as though I'd suggested he fly.

"Good. You can talk, too. That's more than most people manage after their first dose of the world." I hauled him upright by the arm. He weighed less than he looked, which told me things about how he'd been living. "My name is Sera Vane. You are going to tell me why a warded man from Hollowmere is dying in a barley field, and you are going to do it before sundown, because sundown is when the collectors get ambitious."

"I'm not—" He stopped. Swallowed. Started again with the careful diction of a man who had spent his life choosing words the way other men choose knives. "I'm looking for the mountain."

Of course he was. Everyone was, lately. The desperate came up my road in ones and twos now, asking after the court beneath the mountain the way pilgrims used to ask after shrines. The difference was that pilgrims used to bring offerings, and these brought debts.

* * *

Let me tell you how you lose a god, since apparently we're being honest with each other, you and I, reader of whatever record this is.

You don't lose a god the way you lose a key or a lover. There is no dramatic moment, no slammed door. A god leaves the way warmth leaves a room: so gradually that you spend years adjusting your coat before you admit the fire went out.

I was consecrated at sixteen to Veyra-of-the-Open-Hand, goddess of mercy and small mercies both, which even then struck me as a goddess hedging her bets. For thirty years I served her temple in the town of Callowmere. I lit the lamps. I heard confessions. I buried the congregation's dead and blessed its babies and prayed, every morning and every night, the way you're supposed to: *Veyra, open hand, open door, look down on us.*

And every morning and every night, for thirty years: silence.

Not hostile silence. Not the silence of a god who has turned her face away in anger — I've read about those, they leave marks, they leave *signs*, a blighted harvest here, a prophet struck mute there. This was the silence of an empty house. Of a lamp burning in a window for someone who moved out decades ago and left no forwarding address.

The senior clergy knew. That's the part that curdles in me still. Old Mother Cassia, who taught me doctrine, got drunk on festival wine one year and told me the truth with her teeth stained purple: "The prayers go somewhere, girl. They just don't come back. Hasn't been a true miracle in this province in two hundred years. We keep the lamps lit because the alternative is telling them."

Telling them. Telling the miller whose daughter was dying that the goddess of mercy had, as far as anyone could prove, never existed or had long ago ceased to care, and that his daughter's survival would depend entirely on the skill of a physician he couldn't afford. Telling the widow that her husband wasn't waiting for her anywhere. Telling a whole valley of frightened, hopeful people that they were alone in the dark, and then watching what they became without the belief that someone was watching over them.

So we kept the lamps lit. We performed the rites with full ceremony and empty conviction. We were, if I'm honest — and I promised honesty — frauds. Devout frauds, which is the most pathetic kind. Men who counterfeit coin at least get to spend it.

Then, last month, the gods woke up.

I was leading evening prayer when it happened. Forty congregants, the usual litany, my voice doing the work my faith no longer bothered attending. And mid-sentence the words turned to ash in my mouth — literally, I tasted smoke — and every lamp in the temple guttered blue, and I heard, for the first time in thirty years, an answer.

It was not *yes*.

It was a voice like a glacier calving, like a continent shifting in its sleep, and it said — and I have spent every night since turning the words over, looking for some kinder translation and finding none — it said: **WHO DARES SPEAK IN OUR ABSENCE.**

Our absence. As if the silence had been our fault. As if thirty years of praying into a well were a crime committed by the people at the bottom of it.

They woke angry, the old gods. All of them, everywhere, all at once, like a household of sleepers roused by a debt collector pounding on the door. And the first thing they did — the very first thing — was disown us. Their priests, their temples, their faithful. *Failed servants,* the voice called us. *Stewards who let the storehouse empty.* Every miracle since has gone to laypeople, to farmers and miners and frightened girls at crossroads, anyone desperate enough to pray directly and foolish enough to accept the terms. The professional intermediaries — me, mine, the whole greasy apparatus of organized faith — we are cut off. Disinherited. The gods have answered thirty years of my prayers with a single sentence, and the sentence was *you're fired.*

Mother Cassia didn't survive the waking. When the voice came through her, three days after mine, it used her up like a candle burned from both ends. I buried her myself. I did not pray over the grave. It seemed rude, under the circumstances.

* * *

Dominic told me his story in fragments, the way people tell stories that have broken them — out of order, with gaps, watching my face the whole time to see which parts I'd flinch at.

His sister. Nineteen years ago. A soul sold to buy his immunity, and a solstice deadline, and magistrates who laughed at him. I'll give him this: he didn't weep. He sat in my camp with his hands wrapped around a cup of tea he was too nauseated to drink and laid out his ruin like a merchant laying out samples, precise and dry-eyed, and only his hands shook.

"The ward," he said. "It flickered. I tested it myself, at the boundary stone. It's failing, isn't it? That's why I could feel the magic out here. Why it nearly killed me."

"It's failing," I agreed. "Everything is failing. That's rather the theme of the age."

"And the collectors. The thing in the field—"

"Soul-collector. Low-rank. The mountain court sends them out now that the ward's down enough to slip through. They mark houses where contracts are coming due." I fed a stick to the fire. "Your sister's house will have been marked already, if her debt is real. Probably yours too, by association. Debt sticks to blood like grease to cloth."

He looked at the fire for a long time. Then he said, in the same level voice: "Will you take me to the mountain?"

"No," I said.

"I can pay—"

"With what? Court scrip? Hollowmere doesn't mint anything the outside world honors, and you gave your purse to the first ferryman who saw you coming, don't think I didn't notice the way you checked your empty pockets." I leaned forward. "Listen to me, taster. The mountain isn't a place you visit. It's a ledger with a roof on it. Everything that goes up that stair gets written down, and everything written down gets collected eventually. Your sister sold herself to save you; that was her choice, made in desperation, and it was a bad bargain, but it was *hers*. Walk away. Go home. Grieve her properly instead of joining her."

"You said the gods woke up," he said. "That they're answering prayers now. Answering *badly*." His eyes came up to mine, and there was something in them I recognized from my own mirror — the particular stubbornness of a person who has decided that despair is a luxury they cannot currently afford. "If they're awake, then someone is home. And if someone is home, then someone can be *argued with*. I spent nineteen years tasting poison for a king who thinks I'm furniture. I know how to sit at a table with powerful things and not die. Let me use it."

I should have refused. Any sensible woman would have refused. But I had spent a month watching the gods hand out miracles like a drunkard handing out his last coins, and I had a hypothesis forming, and hypotheses require experiments, and experiments require a fool willing to walk into the mountain.

Here is my hypothesis, and I give it to you freely: the gods did not wake up. They were *woken*. Something changed in the deep places — something stirred the sleeping creditors, and now they're calling in every debt ever spoken aloud, harvesting the desperation of mortals like a second crop. And the mountain court, the soul-brokers, the ledger-keepers beneath the stone — I think they're either the reason the gods woke, or the first customers of the new harvest, or both. And I think that somewhere in that cathedral of contracts is a page with my name on it too, because I served their rivals' temples for thirty years and the gods do not strike me as entities who forgive a career.

I wanted to see the machine. That's the ugly truth. Thirty years of defrauded faith, and I wanted to stand in front of the engine that ate my life's work and *understand it*.

"All right," I said.

Dominic blinked. "All right?"

"I'll take you as far as the trade-town at Grayhollow. Past that, the road stops being a road and starts being a suggestion, and you'll need someone who knows which suggestions kill you. In exchange—" I held up a finger before he could offer money he didn't have. "In exchange, you carry my pack on the climbs, you eat what I cook, and when we meet anything with more power than sense, you keep your mouth shut and let me do the talking. Agreed?"

"Agreed," he said, and then, because he was apparently constitutionally incapable of leaving a bargain clean: "Why? Why help me?"

Because you are the first person in a month who looked at the waking gods and saw a negotiation instead of a miracle. Because I buried my mentor in unconsecrated ground and I need to know what she died of. Because everyone I ever served believed in something that wasn't listening, and I would burn down heaven itself before I let that be true without checking.

What I said was: "Misery loves company, and I've been lonely."

* * *

We walked north for three days, and I learned him the way you learn any traveling companion: through his habits, which are more honest than his words.

He counted things. Under his breath, constantly — steps, birds, the beats between lightning and thunder. I asked him about it once, and he said, "When you taste for a living, you learn that panic changes flavor. Counting keeps the panic down." A man who had made a discipline of his own terror. I knew the type. I'd been one for thirty years behind an altar.

He couldn't touch magic — not wards, not blessings, not the little hedge-charms farmers nail over their doors. It slid off him and then it *sickened* him, the way it had in the barley field. "Bred immune," he said, and spat the word *bred* like a seed husk. He didn't yet understand what that word meant, what it implied about the valley and the ward and the gentle hands that had raised him inside it. I suspected. I'd seen the shape of it before, in older stories, uglier ones. I didn't tell him. Some knowledge needs to arrive on its own schedule or it arrives as a wound instead of a weapon.

And at night, when he thought I was asleep, he whispered lists. Poison names. Hemlock, foxglove, belladonna, the salts and the alkaloids, a litany of everything he'd survived. It took me two nights to realize what it was: a rosary. The man had invented himself a rosary out of survived poisons, and I lay there in the dark beside him feeling something crack in my chest that I'd thought had scarred over years ago.

Because that's what faith looks like when it's honest. Not the lamps and the litanies and the incense-smoke theater I performed for three decades. This. A man counting his survivals in the dark because the dark is real and counting is what keeps you in your body. I had preached surrender to the divine for thirty years, and here was a man practicing the only theology that had ever actually worked: *endure, remember, endure.*

On the third night, rain drove us under a fallen oak, and he asked me the question I'd been waiting for.

"Do you hate them?" he said. "Your gods. After what they did."

I considered lying. I was, after all, professionally trained in it.

"I hated them for about a week," I said. "Then I realized hatred requires believing they owe you something. And they don't. That's the joke, Dominic. The whole cosmic joke. We built thirty centuries of civilization on the assumption that someone upstairs was minding the store, and the store was empty the entire time, and now the owners are back and they're *furious about the mess we made.*" I pulled my cloak tighter. "No. I don't hate them. I intend to *bill* them."

He laughed — a short, rusty sound, like a hinge that hadn't turned in years, and I suspect that was true of the laugh itself.

"Bitter hope," he said. "Is that what we have?"

"It's the only kind that survives contact with the divine. Sweet hope dies the first time heaven ignores you. Bitter hope has already been ignored. It knows the terrain." I poked the fire flat. "Sleep, taster. Tomorrow we reach Grayhollow, and after Grayhollow nothing on this road is human anymore."

He slept. I watched the rain silver in the firelight and thought about Mother Cassia's purple-stained teeth and the empty house and the voice like a calving glacier, and I made myself a promise I had no right to make:

Somewhere under that mountain, in whatever cathedral of ledgers holds the accounts of gods and men, there is a line item with my name on it. And when I find it, I am going to read every word.

* * *

Grayhollow announced itself a day early, the way rot announces itself by smell.

We crested the ridge at noon and Dominic stopped dead, and I watched him do the arithmetic on the town below — the crooked towers, the smoke, the market sprawled out beyond the walls like spilled ink — and then watched him arrive at the wrong total.

"That's a city," he said. "You said a town."

"It was a town. Then the gods woke up and the desperate started walking, and desperation is the most profitable crop there is." I started down the slope. "Stay close, keep your hood up, and whatever you hear being sold in there — favors, fortunes, fragments of divinity, bottled luck — do not buy. Do not barter. Do not *ask the price* of anything, because in Grayhollow, asking the price is how they hook you."

Below us, the market of small gods was opening for the day, and the bells of its hundred stalls were ringing, and every one of those bells sounded, to my ear, exactly like a lamp going out.

Dominic followed me down.
