## 7. Roadside Miracles

The first village we came to was called Bracken Hollow, and it smelled like a funeral that had been going on for years.

That's not fair. It smelled like wet earth and woodsmoke and pig, the way most villages smell. But underneath it there was something else — a sweetness, cloying and wrong, like fruit rotting in a bowl nobody wants to empty. I'd spent my life around food. I knew what things were supposed to smell like when they were good, and what they were supposed to smell like when they were quietly killing you. This village sat somewhere between.

"It's rained here," Sera said, before we were even through the gate. She said it the way other people say *there's been a murder*.

"I can see that," I said. The fields were black and glistening, the ditches running full. "Is that remarkable?"

"Three weeks ago this valley was dust. I walked through it on my way south. The wells were down to mud." She pulled her hood lower. "Now look at it."

I looked. The barley stood tall and heavy-headed. Children ran between the rows shrieking at each other, which is the universal sound of children who have eaten recently enough to have energy to waste. By every measure I knew, Bracken Hollow had been saved.

So why did everyone over the age of twelve move like they were carrying something fragile?

We stopped at the well because Sera insisted on water from sources she could see, and because my feet had developed opinions about the road that I was choosing to respect. An old man sat on the well's edge with a bucket he wasn't drawing, watching us come with the flat, patient stare of livestock.

"You're travelers," he said. Not a question. Nobody traveled anymore if they could help it; the roads had gone strange since the pass opened.

"We are," Sera said. "We're bound for the mountain."

Something moved behind his eyes. Pity, maybe. Or envy. With these people it was hard to tell the difference.

"The rain came on the ninth night," he told us, unprompted, the way people tell you things they've been telling everyone, trying to make the story smaller by repetition. "Old Marta prayed for it. Prayed proper, out loud, at the standing stone up past the mill. And it came." He held up a hand before either of us could speak. "Before you ask — yes, it cost her. Everything costs now."

"What did it cost?" I asked.

He looked at me for a long moment, and I watched him decide I was worth the truth, or perhaps just too tired to lie.

"She doesn't remember her husband's face. Nor her sons'. Nor her own wedding day, nor the flood year, nor the name of the god she prayed to." He smiled, and it was the worst smile I've ever seen, because it was genuine. "But the barley's in. Her boys eat. She says it was worth it. She says so every day, because every day she has to learn again what she gave."

Sera made a sound low in her throat. I pretended not to hear it.

"And the rest of you?" I asked. "The whole village praying?"

"Oh, we all paid something." He tapped his temple. "Tam the smith lost the names of colors. He still works iron — knows the heat by feel better than any man alive — but he calls everything *that one* and *the other*. The midwife forgot her mother's voice. The priest—" he laughed, short and bitter, "—well. The priest forgot why he'd ever believed in anything, which some of us thought an improvement."

*The gods' return is a harvest.* Sera had said something like that our first night on the road, and I'd nodded along the way you nod at a priestess about theology — politely, without comprehension. Standing in Bracken Hollow, smelling that sweet rot under the living smells, I began to understand.

They weren't answering prayers. They were *buying*.

* * *

We stayed the night in the village hall because Sera argued that sleeping outdoors near a place where miracles had been transacted was asking for attention, and I argued back that sleeping indoors among people who had sold their memories to unknown powers was arguably worse, and then we both realized neither option was good and split the difference by taking turns watching the door.

It was my watch when the woman came.

She was young — younger than me, though it was hard to say how much younger, because her face had that smoothed-over quality I'd started noticing in these parts, like a page written on and then scraped clean. She sat down across from me without asking and put a clay cup in front of me.

"Barley tea," she said. "It's all we have that tastes of anything."

"That's kind." I drank it. It tasted faintly of nothing, which I was learning was the local flavor.

"You're the taster," she said. "Word came ahead of you. A royal taster, walking east with a fallen priestess. People talk."

"People talk," I agreed. "What do they say I taste like?"

She didn't smile. "I want to know if it's true. That you can't be touched by magic. That poison doesn't take you."

"It's true enough. Why?"

Her hands wrapped around her own cup, knuckles whitening. "My daughter has the wasting fever. The fever that took half the valley two winters back. There's a stone up past the mill — everyone knows someone who got something there. Rain. Healing. A good harvest. I've been up three times and I can't—" Her breath caught. "I can't make myself pray. I stand there and I open my mouth and nothing comes out, because I know, don't I? Everyone knows now. You get what you ask for and you lose what you didn't think to count."

"What would healing cost her?" I asked gently. "Do you know?"

"The miller's boy was healed of a broken back in spring. Cost him his name — not the word, he remembers he's called Tam, but the *weight* of it. Says it's like wearing someone else's coat forever. Old Hesk's wife was cured of the lung-rot and lost every dream she'd ever had. Wakes in the morning with nothing behind her eyes but the day ahead." The cup trembled. "And I keep thinking: what will they take from *me*? What if they take my love for her? What if I get my daughter back healthy and I look at her and feel — nothing? What if the price of saving her is never knowing why I wanted to?"

I set down my cup. My hands were steady, because twenty years at a poisoned table teaches your hands to be steady no matter what your stomach is doing.

"Then don't pray," I said.

"She'll die."

"Maybe. I'm not going to pretend otherwise — I've watched people die of things I couldn't fix, and I won't insult you with comfort." I leaned forward. "But listen to me. I grew up in a valley behind a ward that keeps magic out. My whole life, I thought that wall was mercy. Now I'm starting to suspect it was a fence, and I was the animal inside it. And the thing I've learned on this road, in three weeks, is that everything on the other side of that wall wants something from you, and none of them lead with the price."

"They answered the rain," she whispered. "Bracken Hollow eats because of it."

"Bracken Hollow eats and can't remember why eating matters." I heard my own voice go hard and stopped myself. Softened it. "I'm sorry. I'm not your judge. But you asked me if the stories were true, and here's the truest one I have: I once knew a woman who made a bargain to save someone she loved, and she paid for nineteen years in a way no ledger ever wrote down. Fear. Guilt. Waiting. The gods took their cut up front, but the interest was hers alone."

She stared at me. "Did she regret it?"

"No," I said, and found it was true. "That's the cruelest part. She'd do it again. So will you, probably. So would I." I stood, because sitting close to that much grief felt like leaning over a well. "But you asked. So: don't pray tonight. Sit with your daughter instead. Tell her stories until she sleeps. If the gods want her soul's worth, let them at least compete with a mother who's still whole."

She left before dawn. I don't know what she chose. I've thought about it more than I'd like to admit.

* * *

The second village had no name anymore.

That's not poetry. We asked three different people what the place was called and got three different answers, and then a fourth person explained, with the cheerful vagueness of the deeply damaged, that the village had pooled its prayers last autumn — everyone together, one great collective plea for the blight to lift off their orchards — and the gods had accepted, and the blight had lifted, and the price had been the village's name. All of them. At once. Nobody could recall what they'd called themselves, or what anyone else was called either, so they'd taken to calling each other by trades and traits. The baker. The limper. The one who whistles.

"A whole village," Sera said that night, staring into the fire like it owed her money. "A thousand souls, and the bill was one shared word."

"One word seems cheap," I said.

"It's not the word." She looked up at me, and firelight did unkind things to the bitterness in her face. "Dominic. Names are how the old gods filed things. Under the old rites, a name was a handle — you could be summoned by it, bound by it, claimed by it. They didn't take the village's name as payment. They took it as *receipt*. As claim. Those people aren't free farmers who happen to be forgetful. They're marked inventory."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, the one who whistles was whistling, tuneless and content.

"And the memories?" I asked. "The faces, the dreams, the colors? Receipts too?"

"Worse. Memories are *substance*, in the old accounting. Thirty years I studied the liturgies and I thought most of it was metaphor — the gods feeding on devotion, the soul as grain, all of it poetry for illiterate congregations." She poked the fire hard enough to scatter sparks. "Then last month they woke up, and I felt them, and I understood that the poetry was *bookkeeping*. They don't feed on worship, Dominic. Worship is just the aroma. What they eat is what worship is made of. Memory. Identity. The accumulated weight of a life. Every prayer answered on this road is a meal, and the desperate are the only ones who sit down at the table."

I thought of the old man at the well, telling his story over and over, trying to make it smaller. Trying to hold the shape of what he'd lost by describing its edges. I thought of Marta, learning fresh each morning that she had been robbed.

"There's a word for what's happening out here," I said slowly. "In my trade. When a merchant sells grain in a famine year and names whatever price he likes, and the buyer pays because the alternative is dying."

"Usury," Sera said.

"I was going to say *harvesting*." I turned my cup in my hands. "You harvest what you planted. You don't plant wheat and then apologize to it. These gods went to sleep — or were made to sleep, or whatever happened thirty years ago when your prayers started hitting silence — and while they slept, the world filled up with desperate people. Droughts and plagues and wars and crowns that tax the starving. The field ripened. Now they're awake, and they're moving down the rows."

Sera was quiet for a long time. When she spoke, her voice had gone very flat, which I'd learned was how she sounded when feeling something too large to show.

"I prayed for thirty years," she said. "Thirty years of dawn services and funerals and plague vigils, and I heard nothing, and I blamed myself. I tore my faith apart looking for the flaw in *me*. And the whole time, they weren't listening because they were *asleep*, or sealed, or — or being kept somewhere. And now they wake, and the first thing they do isn't to heal the world out of love. It's to open a market." She laughed, one dry note. "I lost my god twice. Once to silence, and once to finding out what he actually sounds like."

I should have said something comforting. I've been told I'm bad at comforting; my professional instinct is to identify the poison, not to pretend it isn't there. So instead I said:

"Then we'd better find out what's at the top of the mountain before the whole world gets eaten row by row. Because Maren's contract is up there, and if the court beneath the mountain is doing what I think it's doing — collecting souls, filing them, aging them like wine in a cellar — then my sister isn't a debtor. She's *stock*. And solstice is the market day."

Sera looked at me across the fire, and for the first time since I'd met her — vomiting my guts out at the ward's edge while she drove off a creature made of shadow and courtesy — there was something in her face besides pity and exhaustion.

Respect, maybe. Or recognition. Two people who had both spent their lives serving institutions that turned out to be mouths.

"Tomorrow we cross into the hill country," she said. "Past the last of the praying villages. After that, the land goes quiet — the collectors patrol it, and even the desperate have learned not to pray there." She hesitated. "There's something else. I've been feeling watched since Bracken Hollow. Not bandits. Something patient. Something that walks like it has all the time in the world, because it does."

I had felt it too, if I was honest. A sensation at the nape of my neck like a spoon hovering over a cup, deciding whether to drink.

"Well," I said, and lay back with my boots on, as one does when sleep has become a rumor. "If it's coming to make us an offer, I hope it leads with the price this time. I'm tired of reading the fine print after I've signed."

I was asleep within minutes, which proves either courage or stupidity, and I woke at grey dawn to Sera's hand on my shoulder and her voice, low and tight:

"Dominic. Someone's been waiting on the road since before first light. She says she's been looking for you specifically." A pause. "She says her name is Ilvane. She says she's a collector. And she says—" Sera's jaw worked, "—she says she has a proposal regarding your sister's debt. One that wouldn't cost Maren anything at all."

I sat up. The morning was cold and clear, and at the edge of the trees stood a figure in grey, perfectly still, smiling the way a ledger smiles.

Every instinct I owned — the taster's tongue, the survivor's gut, the brother's heart — all of them said the same thing at once:

*Here comes the easy bargain. Here comes the trap.*
