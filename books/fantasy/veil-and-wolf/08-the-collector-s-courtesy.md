## 8. The Collector's Courtesy

The trouble with a road is that it goes somewhere. I'd spent thirty years in a valley where the farthest you could walk was the far side of the market square, and I had somehow failed to appreciate that this was a feature, not a limitation.

Sera Vane walked ahead of me with her hood up and her god's absence hanging off her like a wet coat. Three days out from the pass, we had developed a rhythm: she scouted, I cooked, and neither of us mentioned the thing that had happened at the river crossing, where a collector had come within a knife's length of taking my name and Sera had burned half her remaining faith to stop it.

We didn't mention it because mentioning it would have required deciding what it meant — that she'd spent something precious on a stranger, or that she was investing in whatever she thought I could become. Both possibilities frightened me in different currencies.

"Town ahead," she said, without turning. "Ashford Hollow. Don't drink anything."

"I'm a food taster."

"You're a *former* food taster walking through country where the rain answers prayers. Drink nothing you haven't watched boil for an hour."

I liked that about her. Most people, upon learning what I did for a living, either recoiled or got clever — *oh, so you'd know if I poisoned your soup* — as though surviving hemlock were a party trick instead of a scar worn on the inside. Sera treated my palate like a tool she might need to borrow later, which was, I suppose, exactly what it was.

Ashford Hollow smelled wrong before we saw it. Not rot — I know rot the way other men know their mothers' voices. This was sweeter. Honey and hot iron and something underneath like the air after lightning, except the sky was a flat gray quilt with no storm in it anywhere.

"Shrine town," Sera said grimly. "There'll be a shrine at the crossroads. There's always a shrine at the crossroads."

"And?"

"And shrines are beacons now. The old gods wake hungry, and every candle lit to them is a finger pointing up saying *here, here, something desperate lives here.*"

"Then we go around."

"We go through." She finally looked back at me, and her eyes had the particular flatness of a woman doing arithmetic she hated. "Around adds two days, and collectors don't tire, and you do. Through, quickly, heads down."

I went through. It's worth recording that I went through, because everything afterward will look like foolishness otherwise, and I'd prefer history — if there is any history left after all this — to record that I was merely unlucky rather than stupid.

* * *

The shrine sat where she said it would, at the meeting of three roads, a squat stone cairn furred with moss and hung with ribbons gone gray from weather. Someone had left offerings: a heel of bread going green, a clay cup of milk with a skin on it, a child's shoe. The milk bothered me most. Milk spoils fast; someone had set it out recently, which meant someone had come here recently, which meant someone in Ashford Hollow was still praying.

"Don't touch it," Sera said.

"I wasn't going to."

"You were looking at it the way you look at food."

"That's just how I look at things," I said. "It's not a character flaw, it's a profession."

But she was right, and I knew it even as I said it. My whole life had been built on the principle that anything offered to you might be trying to kill you, and that the only defense was to taste it first, small, controlled, on your own terms. The shrine was an offering. The whole world outside the ward was an offering, laid out on a table the size of the horizon, and I was the only taster left who hadn't eaten yet.

That was when the light changed.

Not dimmed — *changed*, the way a room changes when someone opens a door behind you. The gray quilt of sky developed a seam, and along the seam came a figure walking, unhurried, the way a creditor walks when he knows he holds the note.

She was tall and narrow as a winter tree, dressed in traveling grays that matched the sky too well to be coincidence, and her face was pleasant in the way of bank clerks and undertakers — a face designed to put you at ease while it counted your assets. She stopped ten feet away, folded her hands, and inclined her head with genuine courtesy.

"Dominic Voss," she said. My name in her mouth sounded like a signature on a document. "And the priestess Vane. What an efficient morning. I do hate chasing people across provinces; it's undignified for everyone."

Sera moved. I'll give her that — no hesitation, no negotiation, just salt from her pouch and a word in a language that made my teeth ache, flung between us like a thrown blanket. The collector regarded the shimmering line of it the way one regards a child's drawing held up for approval.

"Charming," she said. "You've improved since the last time we met, Vane. You used to be on the *other* side of these conversations."

"Sera," I said quietly. "Who is this?"

"This," Sera said, and her voice had gone thin, "is Ilvane. Collector, third rank, court beneath the mountain. Dominic, whatever she offers — and she will offer — the answer is no. Say no and keep saying it. They can't take what isn't freely given. That's the oldest law they have, older than their court, maybe older than them."

"Your priestess flatters our legal tradition," Ilvane said pleasantly. "It's true, though. I'm not permitted to take. Only to trade." Her eyes found mine, and they were the color of tea gone cold. "And I have such a trade to offer you, Master Voss. May I sit? Courtesy costs nothing, and I find courtesy puts mortals at ease."

"You're not sitting," Sera said.

Ilvane remained standing, hands folded, patient as stone. "Very well. Standing, then. Master Voss. Your sister Maren sold her soul to my court nineteen years ago. The contract comes due at solstice. You are walking toward my mountain to steal her page from the ledger — yes, we know; the ledger knows its own pages — and I am here to tell you that you needn't walk another step."

The world went very quiet. Not silent — there were birds somewhere, and wind in the dead grass — but quiet the way a room goes quiet when a doctor enters it.

"Speak plainly," I said. "I taste things for a living. I have no patience for flavoring."

Something flickered across her face — appreciation, perhaps, or the memory of appreciation. "Plainly, then. Your sister's debt is real, and debts to my court are paid in full or not at all; there is no partial redemption, no mercy discount, no installment plan. But contracts may be *substituted*. One soul of equal or greater value retires the obligation entirely." She paused, letting the silence do its work. "Yours, Master Voss, is worth considerably more than hers."

"Why?" I asked, and hated how steady my voice came out. "Because I'm immune?"

"Because you are *unspoiled*." She said the word the way sommeliers say *vintage*. "Nineteen years inside a ward, untouched by magic, unmarked by bargains, unbent by prayer. Do you know how rare that is, out here? Every soul I collect arrives already nibbled. Promised to this god, pledged to that saint, stained with little mercies and little sins. Yours has been kept in a cellar in the dark." She smiled, and it was almost warm. "You are, forgive the term, a masterpiece of terroir."

Behind me, Sera made a sound low in her throat, but I barely heard her. Because here is the thing I have never told anyone, the thing I barely told myself: some part of me had always known I was being saved *for* something. Nobody survives hemlock nineteen times by luck. Nobody gets a valley-sized shield around their cradle for free. Gratitude had sat in my chest for twenty years like a swallowed coin, and now a polite monster was telling me what it had been minted for.

And worse — worse — the arithmetic was simple. Beautiful, even. One soul for one soul, and Maren walked free. No climb up the mountain. No stolen pages. No Sera burning pieces of herself to hold doors open. Just me, stepping forward, saying *take mine instead*, and the whole ugly machinery of heaven grinding to a satisfied halt.

I thought of Maren at nine years old, holding my fevered hand while our mother wept in the doorway. I thought of her fever now, her terror, her confession delivered in a voice scraped down to the wick. Nineteen years she'd carried it alone because she loved me enough to damn herself quietly.

All I had to do was love her back in the correct denomination.

"Yes," I heard myself say.

"*Dominic.*" Sera's hand closed on my arm.

"No," I said, and it took me a moment to understand I wasn't answering Ilvane. "No — wait. That's the trap, isn't it? That's the whole shape of it." I looked at Ilvane, and my mouth had gone dry, but the taster in me had woken up, the part of me that knows poison doesn't announce itself, that the deadliest dish is the one you *want* to eat. "You didn't corner us by accident. You let us see the shrine. You wanted me doing this arithmetic in front of you, because a bargain made by a man in love feels like his own idea."

Ilvane's expression didn't change, which was answer enough.

"And there's a clause," I went on, because once the tongue starts working it won't stop. "There's always a clause. If my soul retires Maren's debt — fine. But my soul is collateral on more than her contract, isn't it? Whatever the ward was for. Whatever my bloodline was *for*. If I hand myself over freely, the court doesn't just get a payment. It gets what it bred me for, gift-wrapped, with consent stamped on top so no one upstairs can ever call it theft."

For the first time, Ilvane's courtesy acquired texture. Something moved behind the cold-tea eyes — not anger. Respect, maybe, or its close cousin, disappointment.

"You would have made an excellent clerk," she said.

"That's the second-worst thing anyone's said to me this year."

"The offer stands until solstice," she said. "It will stand at the mountain gate, too, when you arrive ragged and pageless and out of allies. Consider that the court is patient, Master Voss. We were patient with your grandmother, and her mother before her. We can be patient with you." She unfolded her hands — the first gesture she'd made, and somehow the most threatening. "A courtesy, before I go. Your priestess should check her supplies. And the boy who tends this shrine watches the road at dusk. Roads are watched from both directions, in these days. Good fortune."

She stepped sideways into the seam of the sky and was gone, and the gray closed over her like water over a dropped stone.

* * *

Sera grabbed my shoulder and spun me around hard enough to hurt. "Say it back to me. What did you almost do?"

"I almost—"

"Say it."

"I almost traded my soul for Maren's." It sounded obscene aloud. "It would have worked, Sera. It would have *worked.*"

"It would have ended the world, is what it would have done, but yes, it would have worked, and that's why they offer it." She released me and pressed both palms against her eyes. "They don't hunt souls like wolves, Dominic. They farm them. A collector's favorite harvest is the one that walks into the barn on its own. Ilvane didn't corner us. She *cultivated* us. Three days of easy travel, one convenient shrine, one perfectly timed appearance. She wanted you negotiating with yourself, because once you've negotiated with yourself, you'll finish the deal eventually. Tonight. Next week. At the gate."

"So what do we do?"

"We make sure she doesn't get to watch you ripen." Sera lowered her hands, and there was something new in her face — not hope, exactly. Direction. "She said the shrine-tender watches the road at dusk. Which means Ilvane has eyes here, which means she'll follow us, which means—" She stopped. Looked at the cairn. Looked at the ribbons, the bread, the cup of milk with its skin of cream.

"Sera."

"She gave us a courtesy," Sera said slowly. "Collectors only give courtesies that cost the recipient something. So we return the favor." She was already kneeling at the cairn, pulling her satchel open. "Help me gather the offerings."

"Gather—"

"Not keep. *Gather.* We're going to burn it. The shrine, the offerings, all of it."

"That's a shrine to the old gods. Your gods. The ones you've spent your whole life serving and just spent your whole savings defending me from a collector over."

"Yes." Her jaw was tight. "And a burning shrine makes light, and smoke, and noise, and every desperate soul within five miles will come running to see which god is angry — and every collector tracking us will have to decide whether a crowd of witnesses is worth the paperwork. It buys us a night's head start. Maybe two." She struck her flint, and her voice cracked on the next words. "It's a decoy, Dominic. It's not desecration. It's — gods forgive me — it's spending them the way they spend us."

I knelt beside her. I stacked the bread and the cup and the child's shoe on the moss-grown stones with the care I'd give any final plate, because that's what it was — a last service, a funeral supper for a faith — and when Sera's hands shook too badly to strike the flint a third time, I took it from her and did it myself.

Tinder caught. Moss caught. Ribbon caught. Fire climbed the cairn like something waking up, and the smoke went up straight and black into the gray quilt of sky, and I swear — I swear on my sister's name — the flames bent once, briefly, toward the north. Toward the mountain. Like a head turning to look.

Or like a warning.

"Move," Sera said hoarsely. "Now. While they're all looking at the fire."

We ran. Behind us Ashford Hollow began to empty into the fields, torches blooming, voices rising — grief, wonder, terror, the whole choir of the desperate — and somewhere in that noise, I knew, Ilvane stood watching us go, patient as a ledger, already writing down the date.

Two days to the foothills. Solstice in eleven.

And the smoke followed us for a mile before the wind finally let it go.
