## 9. Salt and Iron

The waystation smelled of old smoke and older lies, which I suppose is what you'd expect from a place built by smugglers and maintained by no one.

It sat in the crook of a dead river's bend — a low stone building with a roof that had opinions about gravity and a door that opened only if you lifted it on its hinges while pushing, a trick I'd learned thirty years ago from a man who is now either dead or a bishop, and in Hollowmere those categories overlap more than they should. The gods had been silent then too. At least the smugglers were honest about their silence. They charged for it up front.

"We're sleeping here," I told Dominic, who was looking at the building the way a man looks at a dog he suspects will bite him.

"It looks like it's already lost an argument with the weather."

"It has. It won on points." I shouldered the door until it gave its grudging groan. "In. Before dark. The collectors travel faster at night."

He came in without arguing, which was new. Two weeks on the road had taught him that arguing with me was like arguing with rain — technically possible, ultimately dampening. He set down his pack, looked around at the soot-black hearth, the trestle table scarred by a hundred card games played for stakes worth killing over, the straw pallets along the wall, and said, "I've eaten in worse places."

"You've eaten in worse places *professionally*."

"That's fair."

I got a fire going while he swept the worst of the straw into a corner, and for a little while we were just two people doing the small domestic work of not dying, which is most of what survival actually consists of. Nobody tells you that. The ballads make it all sword-flashes and midnight rides. In truth it's sweeping straw and arguing about whose turn it is to fetch water, and if you're lucky, someone to argue with.

I was starting to feel lucky. It alarmed me considerably.

* * *

The trouble started — as trouble does — with salt.

I'd bought some in the last village, three coppers' worth from a woman who tried to charge me five because I "had the look of a woman in need," which was true, but I resented the accuracy. Now I poured it out onto the floorboards in a line across the doorway, and Dominic watched me with the particular patience of a man humoring a madwoman because she knows where the food is.

"Superstition?" he asked.

"Ward," I said. "Crude one. Salt remembers being part of something whole. Sea-water carries prayer better than almost anything, and when it dries into salt it keeps a little of that memory. Lay it in a line and things that move by magic have to stop and decide whether they want to cross it. Most of them don't bother."

"Most of them."

"I never claimed it was a cathedral ward. This is smuggler theology. It works the way smugglers work: cheaply, badly, and often enough to stay in business."

He crouched beside the line, studying it. And here is the thing about Dominic Voss that took me weeks to understand properly: he doesn't study things the way scholars do, hunting for meaning. He studies them the way a taster does, hunting for poison. Every fact goes in his mouth first. If it kills him, he'll know.

"So it's not the salt itself," he said. "It's the belief behind it. The sea-prayers soaked into it."

"Now you're getting it."

"And iron?"

I pulled the horseshoe from my pack — I'd pried it off a dead nag two villages back, which felt appropriately symbolic — and hung it on a nail above the door. "Iron is older than the gods' current mood. It doesn't remember anything. That's its virtue. Magic is memory and appetite; iron is neither. Hang it over a threshold and anything crossing has to arrive as exactly what it is. No glamour, no borrowed shape. A collector can still walk through your iron-hung door, but it has to do it wearing its own face."

"Which is presumably terrible."

"Which is presumably terrible, yes. But terrible and *visible*. There's a difference between being murdered and being murdered by something you can see coming."

He was quiet a moment. Then: "And the third thing? You said salt, iron, and—" he gestured vaguely at his own head, "—whatever this is."

"Disbelief." I sat back on my heels. "The hardest one. The one nobody sells at market."

"Go on."

"Magic runs on consent. Not the big obvious kind — nobody signs anything, well, almost nobody — but the small daily kind. When you flinch from a shadow, you've agreed shadows can hurt you. When you believe a collector's face is beautiful, you've given it a handhold. The old wards work because generations believed in them. So the counter-ward is refusing. Deliberately. Persistently. You look at a thing of power and you think, in the deepest voice you own: *that isn't real, or if it is real, it isn't in charge.*"

"That sounds exhausting."

"It is. It's the most exhausting discipline there is. Priests break themselves on it. Faith is easy compared to doubt done properly — faith just asks you to kneel. Doubt asks you to stand."

Dominic turned his hands over, looked at his palms. Taster's hands. Soft in the wrong way, scarred in the right ones. "I've spent my whole life swallowing things and surviving them," he said slowly. "Hemlock. Belladonna. Things the court wouldn't even name out loud. Everyone always assumed it was the ward protecting me, and maybe it was. But every time, before the ward could act, I had to decide to swallow. To trust that I'd live. Maybe—" he laughed, short and surprised, "—maybe I've been practicing disbelief my entire life. Just pointed the wrong direction."

I stared at him.

Because he was right, and worse than right — he was *naturally gifted*, which is the sort of thing you say about children who paint well and about people who are about to be very dangerous. Thirty years I'd served gods who never answered, and here was a food-taster holding the hardest doctrine of my broken faith in his soft palms like a coin he didn't know he'd been carrying.

"Don't look so pleased," I said, because I am a coward about tenderness. "You'll still die stupidly if you get careless. Disbelief stops magic from gripping you. It does nothing whatsoever against a knife, a cliff, or bad clams."

"I'm immune to bad clams."

"Of course you are. Why would anything be simple about you."

* * *

We drilled that night, because I couldn't sleep and he couldn't either, and misery loves company and company loves drills.

I made him stand at the salt line and hold disbelief in his mind while I threw pebbles at him.

"This is absurd," he said, dodging.

"Pain is the fastest teacher. Now — a collector touches you and pours suggestion into your ear. It says *you're tired, put down the knife, lie down, sleep.* What do you do?"

"Sleep, apparently." Another pebble bounced off his shoulder. "Ow. All right. I refuse. I say it isn't in charge."

"You *feel* it isn't in charge. Words are just the handle. The blade is underneath. Again — it says your sister is already lost, that you're walking toward a mountain that eats people, that the smart move is to go home."

His jaw tightened. The pebble I threw next hit him square in the chest because he'd stopped moving entirely, gone still as a held breath.

"The smart move," he said quietly, "is whatever gets Maren back. Try again."

"There it is." I lowered my arm. "That's the blade. Anger's a fine anchor, so long as you steer it instead of it steering you. Salt keeps them out. Iron shows them true. Doubt keeps their voices out of your skull. Three walls, and you're the fourth."

He rubbed his chest where the pebble had struck. "You learned all this from a god who never spoke to you."

"I learned it from thirty years of needing it to be true." I fed another stick to the fire. "Turns out the gods' silence was the best sermon anyone ever gave me. Taught me to build my own shelter."

He was quiet for a long moment, watching the flames. Then, in the careful voice of a man stepping onto ice: "What happened to them? Your congregation. You've never said."

Ah. There it was. I'd known the question was coming since the pass, the way you know rain is coming by the smell of the air.

"They scattered," I said. "When the gods woke last month and disowned us. Some went home to families who'd written them off. One drowned himself in the millpond — I buried him. A few are still preaching to empty chapels, waiting for an apology that isn't coming." I shrugged, and the shrug cost more than I let it show. "Thirty years of service. Dawn prayers, harvest rites, funerals, weddings, the whole architecture of a life. And then the gods open their eyes after a thousand years and the first thing they say to their servants is *who are you.*"

"I'm sorry."

"Don't be. Sorry implies it wasn't deserved. We built our whole lives on a silence and called it faith. They woke up and found us squatting in their houses, telling stories about them to each other. Honestly, I'd have burned us out too." I smiled, and it was one of my real smiles, the ugly kind. "At least now I know the silence wasn't holiness. It was just absence. There's a freedom in that."

"Is there?"

"There's a *cold* in it," I corrected myself. "Cold and free. Like swimming in winter. You come out changed or you don't come out."

He nodded slowly. And then he did the thing that undid me a little, though I hid it well, because I'd had practice hiding things for thirty years too.

"Thank you," he said. "For teaching me. For the pebbles. For—" he waved a hand at the salt line, the horseshoe, the fire, the whole ramshackle apparatus of her care, "—all of it. Nobody's ever taught me how to survive outside the valley. Inside it, survival was just my job description."

"Well," I said. "Nobody's ever needed me to teach them anything in thirty years. The gods didn't answer, but apparently I did, so we're both having an unusual season."

He laughed — really laughed, the sound startled out of him, unguarded — and I thought: *oh no.* Because that laugh did something to the cold in me. Thawed a seam of it. And thawed things refreeze harder, or else they flood.

I chose to think about the fire instead.

* * *

Near dawn, half-asleep on my pallet, I heard him moving at the door.

Not leaving — I'd have woken fully for that, some animal part of me keeping watch even through exhaustion. No: he was standing at the threshold, and he was doing something with his hands. I cracked one eye.

He was laying a second salt line. Outside mine. A backup, a redundancy, laid crooked and uneven because he'd never done it before, using the last of our salt because he hadn't thought to ask whether we could spare it (we couldn't; I'd buy more; the point stood). And as he worked he was murmuring under his breath — not a prayer, I realized. A list. Poison names. Hemlock, belladonna, aconite, strychnine, the litany he'd used on the mountain stair to anchor Sera — no. To anchor *me*. He was reciting every poison he'd ever survived, the inventory of his own endurance, and laying salt to the rhythm of it.

Making a ward out of his own history. Smuggler theology upgraded with taster's discipline.

I lay in the dark and watched him finish, dust off his hands, and stand there a moment looking at the crooked double line like a man looking at a letter he's not sure he should send.

Then he went back to his pallet, and I closed my eye, and neither of us mentioned it in the morning, because that is also a kindness — the kind that leaves a person their dignity.

But I lay awake a while longer, feeling the thaw spread, and thinking about the Market of Small Gods we'd reach tomorrow, and what it might cost us both.

* * *

Morning came gray and smelling of snow that hadn't decided yet. We packed in the efficient silence of people who've learned each other's rhythms — he doused the fire, I checked the lines, we stepped over the salt together.

At the door he paused. "The market town. The one with the lesser spirits selling favors."

"What about it?"

"You said they trade in scraps. Pieces of divine favor." His voice was level, but I'd spent two weeks learning his levels, and this one had a current under it. "How specific can the knowledge they sell get? Names. True names. The shape of a gate. Could a spirit sell something like that?"

Everything in me said *don't answer honestly.* Everything in me had learned, over thirty years of failed ministry, that hope is the most expensive thing a desperate person can carry.

But he'd laid a second salt line in the dark without asking permission, and I owed him honesty the way I'd once owed the gods worship — completely, and with far better returns.

"Yes," I said. "They can sell exactly that. And Dominic — whatever you're planning to pay them with, it will be more than coin. Those spirits deal in pieces of people. Memories. Talents. Years. You'll walk in whole and walk out lighter, and you won't always notice what's missing until you reach for it."

He looked at the road ahead, toward the smoke of the trade-town rising over the hills like a smudge on the world.

"I've spent my whole life tasting things," he said, "and spitting out what would kill me. Maybe it's time I swallowed something on purpose."

And because I had no doctrine left to offer him, no god, no chapel, only salt and iron and stubborn disbelief — I picked up my pack and walked with him toward the market, praying to nothing in particular that whatever he swallowed, I'd be there to taste it second.
