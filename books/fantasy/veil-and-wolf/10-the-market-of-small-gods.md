## 10. The Market of Small Gods

The town was called Gullet, which told you everything you needed to know about it before you'd walked a single street.

"It's a trade-town," Sera said, in the tone of a woman warning me about weather she had no intention of waiting out. "No crown, no temple, no law worth the name. Everything is for sale and nothing is guaranteed. If anyone offers you something that sounds too good—"

"I taste things for a living," I said. "I have never once accepted something that sounded too good."

"You drank hemlock every morning for twenty years."

"Because it was my job," I said. "There's a difference between courage and employment."

She almost smiled. It was the closest thing to warmth I'd gotten from her since the waystation, where we'd slept back-to-back like a pair of knives in a drawer, and I'd woken at dawn to find she'd salted the perimeter around us both without being asked. Small kindnesses. We were learning each other's shapes.

Gullet sat in a fold of hills like a coin dropped in a beggar's palm — a sprawl of timber and canvas and lantern-light that didn't so much glow as fester. Even from the ridge above it, I could hear the town: haggling, music, laughter with too many teeth in it. And underneath all of it, faint as a pulse through a wall, something else. A hum. The same wrongness I'd felt at the ward's edge, but multiplied, braided, alive.

"That's them," Sera said quietly. "The small gods."

"Small gods," I repeated.

"Lesser spirits. Scraps of divinity that broke off when the old ones woke — or maybe they were always there, and the waking just gave them customers." She pulled her hood up. "They sell favor. Blessings. Luck. Little miracles, priced accordingly. Desperate people come here because desperate people will pay anything, and the small gods know it."

"And what do they take as payment?"

Sera looked at me the way a physician looks at a wound he's about to open. "Whatever you have that's yours. Coin means nothing to them. They want *pieces*."

* * *

We went down into Gullet at dusk, because Sera said the spirits kept better hours than honest merchants and the market only truly opened when the sun lost its nerve.

The main street was a canyon of stalls and awnings, lit by lamps that burned colors fire has no business burning. And the sellers — I want to be precise here, because precision was the last professional habit I had left. They looked mostly human. That was the trap. A woman selling bottled sleep whose shadow moved a half-second behind her. A fat man with a tray of golden apples whose eyes never blinked, not once, not in the entire time I watched him. A child-sized thing in a fine coat, standing on a crate so it could see over its own counter, grinning with a mouth that went too far back on either side of its face.

And everywhere, buyers. Farmers trading what, I couldn't see. Mothers holding out their hands for charms. A young man laughing as he signed something with a stylus that wrote in red, walking away lighter somehow, though his purse hadn't changed.

"They're not evil," Sera murmured. "That's the worst part. Most of them are honest, by their lights. Fair rates, clearly posted. It's the *customers* who don't read."

A stall near the mouth of the market caught my eye because it was the plainest one there: a table, a lantern, an old woman with a scale. Not a merchant's scale — an apothecary's balance, brass pans worn silver at the centers. On one pan sat a pebble. On the other, nothing.

She looked up as we approached, and her eyes were the gray of old ice, and I knew her for one of them before she said a word. Something in the stillness. Mortals fidget. Gods, even small ones, wait.

"Taster," she said.

My spine went cold. "You know me?"

"I know the shape of you." She tapped the empty pan. "Everyone who walks past this table has a weight. Yours is strange. Heavy in some places, hollow in others. Sit."

I glanced at Sera. She gave me a look that said *your funeral*, which was, I was learning, her primary dialect of affection.

"What are you selling?" I asked.

"Answers. One question, weighed against payment." She turned the scale slightly so I could see it properly. "The pebble is what most people can afford. A memory of a summer afternoon. The color of a childhood wall. Small change. You'd be surprised how much of a person that buys."

"And if someone wanted more than a pebble's worth?"

Her smile was thin and genuine and terrible. "Then we weigh what they bring."

* * *

Here is what I knew, going in:

From the Market itself — from three days of listening in taverns and watching transactions with the patient eye of a man who once spent six hours determining whether a suspected arsenic was actually powdered glass — I had assembled the following. The mountain court did not have a gate in any sense I understood. People who went up the mountain by the pilgrim road came back changed or didn't come back. There was another way in. Nobody knew it. Everybody knew somebody who claimed to.

And the court had a true name. Names mattered now — the waking had made names into handles, and handles opened doors. Sera said it herself: *you cannot bargain with a thing you cannot address.* She'd spent thirty years addressing a god who wouldn't answer. She wasn't going to let me walk up to a mountain full of soul-collectors without knowing what to call them.

But true names weren't sold for pebbles. True names were the whole till.

So I sat down across from the old woman with the apothecary's scale, and I put my hands flat on the table like a man laying down cards, and I said:

"I want two answers. The mountain court's true name. And the shape of its gate — how it opens, and for whom."

The market noise seemed to lean away from us. Even the colored lanterns guttered, attentive.

"Two questions," she said. "That's not a pebble's weight. That's not ten pebbles." Her gray eyes traveled over me again, that inventory-taking stare. "What do you have?"

"Coin," I said, and she shook her head before I finished the word.

"Memories?"

"A few I'd miss. But I'll need most of what I am where I'm going."

"Years of life? A love? An enemy? Fear is popular — fear keeps well."

"No," I said. "I have something better. I have a tongue."

She went very still. Stillness, in her, meant interest; I was beginning to read the grammar of these creatures.

"I am Dominic Voss," I said, "royal food-taster of Hollowmere, and for nineteen years I have tasted every poison the world's malice could brew and survived them all. My palate can identify a poison from a single drop — not just the substance, but the source, the vintage, the intent of the hand that made it. Wolfsbane from the northern slopes tastes different than wolfsbane grown in a cellar. Hemlock cut at dawn versus dusk. I can tell you what a man ate, what killed him, and whether he deserved it." I leaned forward. "There is no tongue like mine east of the mountains. Possibly nowhere at all. And I will give you a piece of it."

"*A piece,*" she repeated.

"I keep enough to do my work. Enough to keep myself alive on the road. But the rest — the depth of it, the part that took nineteen years to build — that goes on your scale."

The old woman looked at me for a long moment. Then she laughed, low and delighted, and the sound made the lantern flames bow.

"Oh, you clever, stupid boy. Do you know what a palate like that is worth down here? Half the spirits in this market survive on flavor — offerings, sacrifices, the taste of prayer. A mortal tongue that can parse the *composition of a soul* from a spoonful of broth?" She shook her head. "They'll fight over it. I'll be rich in ways coin can't measure."

"Then we have a price," I said. "Name your terms."

* * *

Sera argued with me afterward, in the alley behind the stall, while the old woman prepared whatever preparations such creatures prepare.

"You're giving away the only thing that makes you *you*," she hissed. "Your gift. Your identity. Nineteen years—"

"Nineteen years of tasting other people's deaths so I wouldn't have to face my own," I said. "It's a skill, Sera. A good one. But I've been mistaking it for a self." I held up my hand before she could speak. "Maren gave everything — literally everything, her whole soul — to buy me five more years of life when I was seven. I've been spending that purchase my entire existence without ever once looking at the bill. I'm not doing that anymore. If a piece of my tongue buys the name that gets me up that mountain, then the piece is cheap."

She stared at me. "When did you become this person?"

"About four hundred miles ago," I said. "It happened somewhere between vomiting past the ward and watching you burn your god's shrine. You were there for most of it."

That earned me the almost-smile again. Faint. Real.

"The terms," she said. "Make sure of the terms. These things live in the wording. Ask exactly what leaves you and exactly what stays."

"I intend to," I said. "I've read contracts before. I grew up inside one I couldn't see."

* * *

The weighing happened at midnight, in the back room of the stall, which was larger inside than outside in the way that places like this are, and smelled of ozone and old honey.

The old woman set her scale on a table of black wood. In one pan she placed a sliver of crystal that caught the lantern light and bent it wrong. "Your payment goes here," she said, "when it's ready. The answers go in the other pan, already weighed. Two questions, two weights. The scale decides if the trade is fair. The scale is older than me, and I am older than the idea of unfairness, so do not insult it by trying to cheat."

"How does it weigh a piece of my tongue?" I asked.

"Speak into the crystal," she said. "Tell it what you're giving. Truthfully. It will take what you offer and no more — but understand, taster, it takes *exactly* what you offer. Word for word. So word it carefully."

I thought of Maren, nineteen years old and terrified, signing away her soul with words she probably didn't understand, drafted by clerks who understood them perfectly. I thought of Sera's warning. I thought about wording.

I spoke slowly.

"I give the deep discernment of my palate — the ability to distinguish the origin, history, and intent behind any poison or substance I taste. I keep the base senses: sweet, salt, sour, bitter, umami, and the power to detect simple presence of harm. What I surrender is the *reading*. The story in the flavor. All of it, permanently, to the holder of this scale and whomsoever she grants it."

The crystal flared white. The pan sank — gently, inevitably, like a leaf settling onto water — and the scale balanced.

Something left me.

I don't know how else to describe it. No pain. Just a quieting, the way a room goes quiet when a clock that's ticked your whole life finally stops. I picked up the cup of wine the old woman offered — a test, I realized, a courtesy and a cruelty both — and sipped.

Wine. Grape, alcohol, a little oak, a little sour at the finish. That was all. Where before there would have been a vineyard, a season, a vintner's patience or haste, a hundred small truths arranged like sentences — now there was just *wine*, flat and mute as a stone.

I set the cup down. My hand was steady. I was almost proud of that.

"Well?" Sera asked softly.

"It's gone," I said. "The deep part." And then, because I needed to say it aloud to believe it: "Nineteen years of craft, traded for two answers. My mother would say I finally found a worse bargain than my sister's."

"The scale says otherwise," the old woman said. She turned it. "Fair weight. More than fair — you undersold yourself, taster, and I won't pretend otherwise, because the scale heard it too. Ask."

I asked.

* * *

The first answer came as a taste.

That was her joke, I think, or her mercy, or both — she poured a drop of something onto my tongue, something cold and mineral and vast, and where the deep discernment used to live, the knowledge simply *was*, planted whole, the way a dream plants a city you've never visited.

The mountain court's true name.

I cannot write it here. I have tried, in the weeks since, and the ink dries to nothing, and the spoken syllables curdle into coughing. But I carry it. It sits behind my teeth like a seed. When I speak it — when I *address* the court beneath the mountain with its own name, the name it bore before it was a court, before it was hungry — its gates must hear, and its ledgers must open, and every collector in its service must pause, one heartbeat, mid-stride.

One heartbeat. Sera says one heartbeat is enough, if you know what you're doing with it. Sera says a lot of things in that voice that means she's guessing.

The second answer was worse, because it was visual, and because it confirmed what I'd begun to suspect.

The gate of the mountain court is not a door. It is a *mouth* — I mean that with precision, since precision is nearly all I have left. The mountain itself is the body, and the gate opens the way throats open: from the inside, at the swallowing, and only for what is being swallowed. Nothing goes in that isn't *owed*. The gate reads debt the way my tongue used to read poison — instantly, totally, judging composition and provenance and intent. It opens for contracts. It opens for collectors carrying harvest. It opens for souls called by name.

And it opens, the knowledge whispered, for those who arrive *already owed* — who carry a debt in their own flesh, written into their bloodline, bred there generation upon generation like a mark on livestock.

I stood very still in the black-wood room, holding that last phrase up to the light.

*Bred.*

"Why," I said slowly, "would a gate care whether someone arrives owed? Why would bloodline matter to a door?"

The old woman's gray eyes met mine, and for a moment — just a moment — something in them flickered, some ancient discomfort, the expression of a creature that has sold ten thousand answers and disliked perhaps three of them.

"That," she said, "is a third question."

"And its price?"

"More than a tongue," she said quietly. "Go ask the mountain. Go ask it in person. And taster—" she pressed something into my hand, a small iron token shaped like a closed eye, "—you paid fair and the scale knows it. If any spirit in Gullet troubles you, show them this. Consider it a merchant's respect."

Outside, dawn was graying the edges of Gullet, and the market was folding itself away like a moth's wings. Sera waited by the alley mouth, arms crossed, reading my face the way I used to read broth.

"You got it," she said. "Both answers."

"I got both answers." I turned the iron token over in my fingers. "And a third question I can't afford, that I think might be the only question that matters."

She frowned. "Which is?"

I looked north, toward the mountain I could not yet see, and felt the seed of its true name shift behind my teeth, and heard again the gate-knowledge whispering its last line like a hook set in my cheek:

*Those who arrive already owed.*

"Why," I said, "does the mountain's gate recognize my family's blood?"

* * *

We were two miles out of Gullet, on the high road with the morning fog burning off, when the collector stepped out of the treeline ahead of us.

Not Ilvane. Someone smaller, quicker, wearing courtesy like an ill-fitting coat. It raised one pale hand — not threatening, merely *present* — and said, in a voice like paper turning over:

"Dominic Voss. The court extends its compliments. You've been asking excellent questions." Its head tilted. "Perhaps you'd like to hear some answers from the other side?"
