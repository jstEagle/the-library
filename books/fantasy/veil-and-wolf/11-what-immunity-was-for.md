## 11. What Immunity Was For

The collector's name was Ossric, and he weighed less than a sack of turnips.

That was the first thing I noticed after Sera knocked him out of the air with an iron poker and I sat on his chest until he stopped thrashing — that whatever a soul-collector was made of, it wasn't much. Bone and parchment and a kind of resentful gravity. His skin had the gray translucence of a man who hadn't eaten in a century and didn't miss it. His eyes were ledger-black.

"You can't hold me," he said, from underneath my knee. "I am an instrument of the court beneath the mountain."

"And I'm a royal food-taster," I said. "Between us, I think we know which profession has better job security."

Sera stood at the mouth of the ruined mill we'd dragged him into, turning the iron poker over in her hands like she was deciding whether to keep it or marry it. Rain came down outside in long gray sheets. Somewhere behind us, the road to the mountain wound up through country that got less forgiving with every mile.

"We need to know what you're doing here," she said. "How many collectors crossed the ward. Where they're staging."

Ossric laughed. It was not a pleasant sound. It sounded like a drawer of knives being opened one at a time.

"The ward," he said. "You still say it like it means something."

"It kept magic out of Hollowmere for three hundred years," I said.

"Did it." He turned his black eyes on me, and there was something in them I hadn't expected to find in a creature like this: pity. Actual pity, thin as broth, but real. "Taster. What do you know about your own valley?"

I told him what everyone knows. The ward was raised by the first kings after the Sundering, when magic ran loose through the world like a spilled fever. Hollowmere sat inside it safe while the rest of the world learned to live with gods and ghosts and things that wore your face. We grew wheat without blessing and children without omens. We were poor and plain and protected, and if any of us ever felt the lack of wonder, we were polite enough not to mention it.

Ossric listened to all of it with the expression of a physician hearing a patient describe a tumor as a freckle.

"Ask me who built the ward," he said.

"The first kings."

"Ask me *who paid for* the ward."

I didn't ask. He told me anyway.

"The court beneath the mountain. Three hundred years ago, before your kings had names worth carving, the court lent the founders of Hollowmere the working — the stones, the bindings, the blood-price. And in exchange, the ward was written with a clause."

"Wards don't have clauses," Sera said sharply. "Wards are wards. I was trained in—"

"You were trained by men who read the front of the contract," Ossric said, "not the back." He shifted under me, and I pressed down harder, though I'm not sure why; he weighed nothing and fought like a filing cabinet. "Your valley is not a sanctuary, taster. It is a *larder*. Sealed against every other power in the world so that nothing else could reach what grows inside it."

The rain filled the silence for a moment. Water dripped through the mill's broken roof, ticking against stone like a slow clock.

"That's a lie," I said. My voice came out very level, which is what happens when I'm most frightened. Twenty years at the royal table taught me that. Terror sounds like calm if you practice enough.

"Is it," Ossric said. "Then tell me about your family. Your mother's mother. The Voss line."

"They were farmers."

"For how many generations?"

"I don't—" I stopped.

Because I did know, actually. Maren used to joke about it when we were children, tracing our family back through the parish rolls the way other kids traced knights and heroes. Farmers, farmers, farmers, going back and back, and then — a gap. A blank page where the records should have been, right around the time the ward went up. The priest at the time had blamed damp. Damp gets blamed for a lot in Hollowmere.

"There are no Vosses before the ward," I said slowly. "There's no record."

"There are no *anyones* before the ward," Ossric said. "Not as you'd recognize them. The court seeded the valley. Chose bloodlines. Bred them, generation over generation, for a single trait." His black eyes never left mine. "Immunity. Souls that magic cannot touch. Do you understand what that means, taster? Every soul in the world carries the stain of its dealings — every prayer answered, every charm worn, every bargain whispered at a crossroads leaves a mark on the soul like smoke on a ceiling. Except yours. Except your sister's. Except every man, woman, and child behind that pretty little wall."

"Why?" I asked. My knee had gone numb. I couldn't feel my own leg, which seemed appropriate. "Why would anyone want that?"

"Because unspoiled souls are *rare*," Ossric said, and now the pity in his voice curdled into something hungrier. "A soul that has never touched magic is clean. Unmixed. It keeps. Like wine sealed in glass versus wine left open by the fire. The court deals in souls, taster. We have for longer than your gods have been asleep. And the finest vintage in the world has been ripening behind your wall for three hundred years."

* * *

I want to tell you that I reacted with dignity. That I stood up, dusted off my knees, and delivered some cutting remark worthy of the ballads.

What I actually did was get sick in the corner of the mill.

Not because of the poison this time. Just because of the words. I crouched there with my hands on the wet stone and my stomach emptying itself of nothing, and Ossric watched me with something almost like patience, the way you watch livestock react to the sight of the abattoir.

"You bred us," I said, when I could speak again. "Like cattle."

"Like *vintage*," Ossric corrected. "Cattle is unkind. Cattle doesn't get a whole valley. Cattle doesn't get peace, and safety, and three hundred years of never having to be afraid." He said it like a gift he was reminding me I'd received. "Every soul in Hollowmere has lived a charmed life. No famine ever crossed the wall. No plague. No war. Do you know what that does to a soul? Keeps it sweet. Keeps it *whole*. You've never had to sell anything to survive — well." A dry click that might have been a laugh. "Almost none of you."

Maren. The word landed in my chest like a dropped knife.

"My sister sold her soul," I said. "To save me. If the whole valley is already yours, why would the court bother buying hers?"

"Ah." For the first time, Ossric looked genuinely interested. "You really don't know, do you? What your immunity *is*?"

"A curse," I said bitterly. "A leash. I've gathered that much."

"It's collateral." He said it simply, the way you'd explain rain to a child. "Your bloodline isn't just immune, taster. It's *reserved*. Marked stock. The immunity isn't a wall around your soul — it's a claim tag. Your soul, your mother's, her mother's, all of it, already spoken for. Which means when your sister went to the court nineteen years ago and asked for a miracle for her dying brother—"

"She wasn't selling her soul," I said. The realization came cold and complete, like hemlock hitting the tongue. "She was selling something she didn't own. She was selling *collateral*."

"Now you're tasting it," Ossric said.

Nineteen years. Nineteen years I had carried my sister's sacrifice like a stone in my chest, gratitude and guilt fused together into something I thought I understood. She had given everything so I could live. And the truth was worse — so much worse — because she hadn't given anything at all. She had signed away property that already belonged to someone else, and the court had accepted the paper because the paper was useful, because a debt on a Voss was a hook set deep in marked stock, because someday — solstice, or any day the ward thinned enough — they could come collect the interest.

And the principal. The principal was always me.

* * *

Sera pulled me upright by the collar. I don't remember getting to my feet on my own. Her grip was iron and her face was the face of a woman who had spent thirty years being lied to by her own gods and had developed a certain professional sympathy for others in the same condition.

"Breathe," she said.

"He's lying," I said.

"Probably some of it," she agreed. "That's how contracts work. But look at him, Dominic. Look at his hands."

I looked. Ossric's fingers were long and gray and stained to the second knuckle with ink — old ink, layered ink, decades of signatures soaked into the skin. And wrapped around his right wrist, sunk into the flesh like a brand, was a ring of small stamped seals. I'd taken it for jewelry. Up close, each seal was a tiny contract, folded impossibly small, and each one bore a name.

"How many?" Sera asked him.

"Does it matter?"

"Humor me."

He smiled with too many teeth. "Four hundred and eleven thousand, two hundred and six," he said. "Current count. I was mortal once, you know. A clerk, in a kingdom that no longer exists. I signed myself over for thirty years of guaranteed harvests during a famine. The court found my penmanship agreeable." He flexed the branded wrist. "I've been collecting ever since. It's not so bad. You stop wanting things. Wanting is the first thing they take."

"What happens at the solstice?" I asked. My voice sounded like someone else's. Someone older. "The harvest. What exactly comes through the ward?"

Ossric studied me for a long moment. Whatever he saw in my face seemed to satisfy some private accounting of his own.

"The ward is failing because the contract that sustains it is coming due," he said. "Three hundred years, taster. The founding bargain was always a lease, not a gift. And when the lease ends, the court collects everything inside the walls. Every unspoiled soul in Hollowmere. Filed, catalogued, cellared." He tilted his head. "Unless, of course, the debt is renegotiated. The court is reasonable. The court loves a negotiation. It's the only entertainment we get."

"And you're telling me this because?"

"Because I've watched four hundred thousand souls go into the ledgers," Ossric said, "and every single one of them walked in believing they'd made a fair trade. It gets tedious." His black eyes flicked toward Sera, then back to me. "You, though. You might actually refuse. That would be novel."

* * *

We tied him with salt-soaked rope in the mill's cellar, which wouldn't hold him forever — Sera said hours, maybe less — and then we stood in the rain under the broken roof and I tried to reassemble myself out of the pieces.

Everything I was had been designed. My tongue, my survival, my careful numb little life measured out in sips and swallows — none of it was mine. I was a cask. A sealed, protected, lovingly maintained cask, and the whole valley was the cellar it sat in, and every festival and harvest and quiet evening by the fire was just the slow work of keeping the wine from spoiling before the lord of the house came to drink.

"Maren knew," I said. "Some of it. She must have. Nineteen years ago, when she went to the mountain — she would have seen the ledgers. She would have seen what the court *is*."

"Maybe," Sera said. "Or maybe she saw what they showed her. They're good at that. I served a god for thirty years and never once saw behind the altar." She wrung rainwater out of her braid with a violence that suggested her thoughts were elsewhere. "Dominic. There's something you should hear from me before you hear it from a collector with better timing."

I looked at her. Rain ran down her face and she didn't blink it away.

"When I told you I was disgraced," she said, "that the waking gods disowned their servants — that was true. But it wasn't the whole truth. Before I was a priestess of the waking gods, I was younger. Hungrier. And there was another court, in those days, that needed a human voice to write its contracts in language mortals could sign." Her jaw tightened. "I helped draft the standard terms. The collateral clauses. The template." She met my eyes and did not look away, which took a courage I recognized, because I'd seen it once before — in my sister, the night she finally told me the truth. "If your sister's contract followed the standard form, then somewhere in its wording, in a clause she couldn't have understood, is a sentence I wrote."

The rain ticked on the stones. In the cellar below us, faintly, Ossric began to laugh.

"Sera," I said, and my voice was very calm, the way it always is when I am most afraid, "start talking."

Below us, the laughter stopped. And in its place came a sound I knew from the royal table, from the moment before the poison announces itself — a small, soft, unmistakable sound of something beginning to break free.
