## 12. Sera's Confession

There is a particular kind of silence that comes before a confession. I know it intimately. I spent thirty years listening for the voice of gods in that silence and hearing nothing but my own pulse, so believe me when I say I have developed an ear for it. It sounds like a held breath. It sounds like a room deciding whether to forgive you.

The smugglers' waystation had gone quiet around us — salt lines drawn, iron poker within reach, Dominic asleep by the banked fire with his hands curled like a man who expected to be robbed even in dreams. And I sat awake with the truth in my chest like a swallowed coal, and I thought: *tonight, or never.*

Because here is the arithmetic of secrets. Every mile we walked toward the mountain made mine heavier. By the time we reached the gate, it would be too heavy to carry and too heavy to drop. Better to set it down now, while he could still walk away from me without dying of exposure.

I fed the fire. He woke the way tasters wake — all at once, no grogginess, hand already moving toward the knife.

"It's me," I said. "If I were a collector, would I bother stoking your fire?"

"You might," he said. "Ilvane was very courteous."

*Very courteous.* Gods, the boy had learned to speak collector in under two weeks. That was my doing too, though he didn't know it yet. Everything was my doing, if you followed the thread back far enough. That was rather the point of the evening.

"Sit up," I said. "I need to tell you something, and you're going to want to be sitting."

He sat up. He didn't ask what. I've noticed that about him — he rationed his questions the way a starving man rations bread, and it made him very hard to rattle. But I saw his eyes go to the door, measuring the distance, calculating whether whatever came next would require running.

"I wasn't always a priestess," I began, and then stopped, because that was a lie of arrangement rather than substance, and I'd promised myself no more of those. "No. Let me do this properly. I *was* a priestess. I am one, technically; the gods just stopped taking my calls. But before that — before the waking gods, before the congregation at Ashford, before any of it — I worked for the mountain court."

The fire popped. Somewhere out in the dark, wind moved through the pines with a sound like pages turning.

"What does 'worked' mean?" Dominic asked quietly.

"It means I was their mouthpiece. Their voice on the mortal side of the veil." I wrapped my arms around my knees, an old habit for making oneself small. "Every court needs someone who can hold a pen in daylight. The collectors can cross over, but they can't draft contracts — not binding ones. Mortal law has to touch mortal paper. So they kept human clerks. Scribes who wrote out the terms in language a desperate farmer could read and still fail to understand. I was one of them. For eleven years."

"Eleven years," he repeated. Not a question. Just placing the weight of it.

"I was nineteen when they recruited me. My mother was dying, and a collector found me crying in a shrine — the old shrine, the pre-waking one, back when nobody prayed there anymore because everyone assumed the gods were dead or deaf. She offered me a position. Good wages. My mother healed. All I had to do was write." I laughed, and it came out wrong, the way laughter does when it's been stored too long. "Do you know what I told myself? That I was helping people. That the contracts I drafted were fairer than the ones the other clerks wrote. I used plain words. I put the prices in bold. I once rewrote an entire soul-lease so the interest clause couldn't be hidden in a footnote. I was *proud* of myself, Dominic. I was proud of being the honest parasite."

He said nothing. The firelight moved on his face and gave nothing away, which I suppose is the occupational hazard of loving — no, of *knowing* — a man trained to keep his expression pleasant while swallowing poison.

"The template," I said, because there was no way around it, and I'd promised myself I wouldn't take the coward's road through the middle of this. "Every clerk worked from templates. Standard contracts, standardized clauses. There was one we used for medical desperation — a parent, a sibling, a child dying, and the court offers immunity, protection, cure. In exchange for collateral. A soul held against future harvest." I looked at him. I made myself look at him. "It was called the Voss Provision. Because the first family it was ever used on was yours."

* * *

I want to tell you he shouted. I want to tell you he threw something, or wept, or did anything at all that would have given me something to push against. Anger is a gift, in its way. Anger means the wound is fresh enough to bleed, and bleeding wounds can be cleaned.

Dominic Voss went very still instead. Stillness is what twenty years of tasting hemlock teaches you — the body learns that panic spreads poison faster than the poison itself. He sat by the fire with his sister's death sentence folded into his shirt pocket, stolen off a clerk's desk in a cathedral of ledgers, and he looked at me the way he must have looked at ten thousand suspect dishes: cataloguing, weighing, deciding whether this thing in front of him would kill him.

"How much did you know," he said finally. Not loudly. That was worse than shouting.

"About Maren specifically? Nothing. I swear it on what's left of my faith. I left the court's service twelve years ago — I'll get to why — and her contract was signed seven years after that. I never saw her name. I never saw your name." I heard my own voice climbing and dragged it down. "But the *template*, Dominic. The shape of it. The clause structure. The way it wraps the collateral around the whole bloodline so the debt outlives the signer. I built that. I refined it over six revisions. If some clerk under the mountain pulled a standard medical-desperation contract off the shelf to put in front of a terrified girl named Maren Voss, she pulled down a document with my fingerprints on every page."

"And Sera Vane," he said, "disgraced priestess, woman of the waking gods, guide who asked for nothing—" he stopped. Started again. "You asked for nothing. That's what I couldn't figure out. Everyone in this world wants something. Ilvane wanted my soul. The market spirits wanted my palate. You wanted nothing, and I thought — I actually thought — maybe that meant you were clean."

"I wanted absolution," I said. "That's not nothing. That's the most expensive thing there is, and I had no right to expect you to pay for it."

He stood up. Walked to the window slit in the waystation wall, where the night pressed in black and cold, and stood there with his back to me, and I counted his breaths the way you count waves between you and drowning.

Twelve breaths. Then: "Why did you leave? Twelve years ago. What happened?"

And that, strangely, was easier to answer, because it was the part of the story where I was also the victim, and even in the middle of confessing, some shameful corner of me reached for that comfort. I made myself not reach.

"A farmer," I said. "Aldous Wren. He leased his daughter's soul to pay for a cure for his wife — same template, my drafting. And then the wife died anyway, because the court's cures are real but they're not miracles, and Aldous came back to the shrine where I collected payments and he asked me one question. He asked, 'When my daughter turns sixteen, will she feel it when they take her?' And I opened my mouth to give him the standard reassurance — the process is painless, the transition is gentle, all the language we used to make harvesting sound like falling asleep — and I realized I didn't know. I had written four hundred contracts and I had never once followed a single soul down to see what collection actually looked like. I had sold people into the dark and called the darkness *gentle* because it was in the style guide."

"So you went and looked."

"So I went and looked." My hands had gone cold. They always went cold, remembering. "I bribed a collector — they respect bribery, it's practically liturgy — and I watched a collection. A boy of seventeen, harvested against his grandfather's gambling debts. And it wasn't gentle, Dominic. It wasn't anything. He didn't scream. That was the worst part. There was nothing in him left to scream *with*. They took the whole of him, not just the soul — the wanting, the remembering, the part that chooses porridge over eggs in the morning. What walked out of that room was a body with excellent posture." I swallowed. "I burned my contracts that night. All four hundred. I stole the master templates so I could burn those too, and the court sent collectors after me, and I ran until I found a congregation of the waking gods who took me in, and I spent thirty years praying to them and hearing nothing, and I told myself the silence was penance. That it was fair. That a god who ignored me was exactly what I deserved."

"But last month they woke up," Dominic said.

"Last month everything woke up." I looked at my hands. "And do you know what my first thought was, when I felt them stirring? Not joy. Not vindication. My first thought was: *the contracts are active again.* A dormant contract is a stone. An active one is a clock. Every soul the mountain court holds — every Maren Voss in every valley — started ticking the moment the old gods opened their eyes. And I knew, I *knew*, that somewhere out there was a person who'd come asking me for help, and that helping them would be the closest thing to forgiveness I'd ever get, and that I had no right to ask for it. So I decided I'd help you and never tell you. Take the secret to the mountain. Let it die there with me, probably, since our odds weren't—"

"Are you still lying to me?" he asked. Flat. Precise. A taster identifying the ingredient.

I closed my mouth. Opened it. "One thing," I admitted. "One more thing, and then the ledger's clean, I swear it. When I stole the templates, twelve years ago — I didn't destroy them all. I kept one. The Voss Provision. The original. I told myself it was evidence, that someday I'd use it to break the contracts it created. But I think — I think part of me kept it because it was the truest thing I ever wrote, and I'm enough of a coward that I wanted proof I'd been good at something, even if the something was monstrous." I reached into my coat and drew out the oilskin packet I'd carried across three hundred miles, through ward-flickers and collector country and the Market of Small Gods, and set it on the floor between us. "There. That's everything. That's all of me. The rest is yours to do with what you like."

* * *

Here is what I expected: the packet thrown in the fire. The word *liar* carved out of him at last. Him walking out into a night full of collectors without me, because righteous anger is lighter luggage than I am.

Here is what happened instead: he looked at the packet for a long time. Then he looked at me, and his face did something complicated — grief moving under composure the way fish move under ice.

"Nineteen years ago," he said slowly, "my sister sold her soul to save my life. Seven years ago, you burned four hundred contracts trying to undo the machine that let her do it. And I've spent this entire journey thinking of you as the person who knows the way." He laughed — short, unsteady, nothing like humor. "You want to hear my confession? I'm a food-taster. Do you understand what that means? For twenty years, people have poisoned things and handed them to me, and my whole job has been to swallow first and find out. And I got *good* at it. I got so good at it that when Maren told me what she'd done, my first feeling — my actual first feeling, before the guilt, before the horror — was relief. Because someone else had paid my bill. Someone else had swallowed the poison, and I hadn't even known there was a dish." His voice cracked, just slightly, on the last word. "So don't stand there telling me you're the monster in this story, Sera. I've met the monster. The monster is anyone who survives by letting someone else drink first. I've been the monster professionally since I was nine years old."

"That's not the same—"

"It's exactly the same. That's the whole point. It's *exactly* the same, and that's why I'm not walking out that door." He crouched, picked up the oilskin packet, and weighed it in his hand without opening it. "You kept the original template. Fine. We're going to need it. Because a page tells us what Maren owes, but a template tells us how the contract was *built* — and anything built can be unbuilt, if you know where the joints are." He glanced up at me, and there was something in his eyes I hadn't earned and couldn't refuse. "You wrote it. You're the only person alive who knows where the joints are."

"You should hate me," I said. My voice came out smaller than I intended. "It would be healthier. Cleaner. Forgiveness given this fast isn't forgiveness, Dominic, it's just — efficiency. You need me, so you're choosing not to despise me. That's not grace. That's logistics."

"Probably," he agreed, and sat back down by the fire, and held the packet out to me. "But here's the thing about tasting. You learn pretty quickly that the antidote doesn't care whether you took the poison on purpose or by accident, and it doesn't care if you're sorry. It just works, or it doesn't. So." He shrugged, a small, tired motion. "Take the packet. Write me the antidote. And when this is over — when Maren's free, if she gets free — then you can spend the rest of your life being properly sorry, and I'll spend the rest of mine deciding whether to accept it. Deal?"

It was not absolution. I want to be clear about that, because I've had thirty years of practice distinguishing the real thing from counterfeits, and what passed between us by that dying fire was not the warm clean grace of a god wiping a slate. It was rougher than that. It was two damaged people agreeing to postpone judgment until after the war, which is either the beginning of forgiveness or the end of honesty, and neither of us knew which.

I took the packet. My hands shook. His didn't, and I understood suddenly that his steadiness cost him more than my shaking cost me.

"Deal," I said.

We spread the template open on the floor between us, and I showed him the clause architecture — the collateral wrap, the succession-of-debt language, the little hinge in section nine that most clerks never noticed, the one that let a contract be voided if the collateral was exchanged freely and in full knowledge. *Freely offered,* the old text said. *In full knowledge of its worth.*

Dominic read that line twice. I watched him read it twice. And I saw the exact moment he understood what it meant — what *freely offered* could be made to cover, what a man with an unspoiled, bred, court-coveted soul might offer at an altar stone — and I watched him decide not to say it out loud.

"Dominic," I said carefully. "What are you thinking?"

"Nothing," said the best liar I ever met, which is to say, the most honest one. "I'm thinking it's late, and we climb tomorrow, and the mountain's been waiting nineteen years. It can wait a few more hours."

He rolled himself into his blanket with the template's page tucked inside his shirt, next to Maren's stolen ledger leaf, and within minutes his breathing went slow and even — a taster's trick, sleeping on demand, resting wherever rest could be found.

I stayed up. I fed the fire and I watched him sleep and I turned section nine over in my mind like a stone with something coiled underneath it, because I had written that clause, and I knew what it was for. It wasn't a loophole. Loopholes are accidents. Section nine was a door, deliberately installed, and I had never known who built the house that needed it.

Now, watching Dominic breathe, watching the packet rise and fall with his heartbeat, I began to be afraid that I did.

Far above us, past the tree line, past the storm-veil, the mountain glimmered faintly against the stars — and for the first time since I'd fled its service, I felt it notice me back.
