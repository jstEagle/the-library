## 13. The Ascent

The stair was not on any map, which was the first sensible thing about it. Everything else about it was an insult to the concept of stairs.

Sera found it at dawn on the mountain's north face, behind a curtain of frozen waterfall that wasn't frozen so much as *paused* — water hanging in the air like a bead curtain someone had abandoned mid-argument. She pushed through it and came out soaked but grinning, which I had learned in three weeks of travel was her face for "I have found something that will be terrible."

"Hidden stair," she said. "Carved before the valley existed. The old texts call it the Throat."

"Charming name."

"It goes up through the mountain's windpipe. The court built it so pilgrims could come to bargain." Her grin thinned. "And so the bargains could follow them home."

I looked up. The stair rose into cloud, cut into black stone, narrow enough that two people would have to take turns being brave. Somewhere above us, the weather was doing something weather should not do.

"And the whispers?"

Her grin left entirely. "You've been talking to Ilvane's people again."

"I've been talking to a smuggler who charged me two teeth for a warning," I said. "He said the mountain talks to climbers. Offers them things."

The smuggler had said more than that. He'd said his brother went up the Throat eleven years ago chasing a cure for a dying daughter, and came back down three days later with no memory of having gone, smiling, empty, and still climbing in place whenever he stood near a slope. But Sera already looked like a woman being asked to walk into her own funeral, and I'd learned that Sera absorbed bad news better in small doses, like poison.

"Everyone hears what they want most," she said quietly. "That's how the Throat works. It doesn't attack you. It *invites* you. You just have to keep saying no."

"For how long?"

"Until we're through it."

* * *

The first hour was merely miserable, which I noted with something like gratitude. My legs burned. The steps were cut for legs longer than mine, or perhaps for legs that belonged to things that had never needed to worry about knees. The paused waterfall dripped on us from behind, each drop striking my neck like a small cold accusation. Sera climbed ahead of me with her priestess's staff wrapped in salt-soaked linen — her idea, from the waystation, one of the crude wards she'd taught me. Salt against magic. Iron against spirits. Disbelief against everything else.

At the second hour, the whispers started.

They didn't come from outside. That was the first horror of them. They came from the general direction of my own skull, in a voice like my voice after it had been left out in the rain — familiar, softened, wrong.

*Dominic,* it said, warm as broth. *Dominic Voss. Taster of kings. Do you know what waits below? Maren, waking every morning with a hole where her future used to be. Turn around. Go down. We will give you a kitchen. A small one, yours alone. No crown to serve, no poisons to swallow, no sister to fail. Bread rising. A window over the sink.*

I stopped on the step and gripped the wall.

Here is the thing nobody tells you about being a food-taster: you spend your whole life training your mouth to notice what's wrong with something lovely. Honey with a bitter undernote. Wine with the ghost of arsenic. You learn that the most dangerous dish is always the one that tastes exactly like what you wanted.

So I did what I do.

I tasted the offer.

Bread rising. A window. Warmth underneath, yes — but beneath the warmth, a flavor I knew intimately, the flat mineral nothing of hemlock, the taste of a thing that kills you politely while your body thanks it. The whisper was offering me comfort the way a collector offers a contract: everything true except the part where you get to keep anything.

"No," I said aloud, because Sera had told me the whispers fed on silence, and climbed on.

Behind me, faintly, the voice sighed like a lover refused, and began again.

* * *

By the fourth hour the storm found us, if you could call it a storm. There was no rain. There were voices instead of wind — hundreds of them, thousands, pouring down the Throat like weather, and each one wore a different want.

I heard a child laughing somewhere above us. I heard a woman singing a lullaby in a language dead longer than Hollowmere. I heard, once, horribly, my mother's voice, though my mother had been eleven years in the ground: *Dominic, love, come inside, supper's ready.* I put my hand against the rock and kept walking and did not answer, because answering felt like swallowing.

Sera was worse off. I could see it in the set of her shoulders, the way her staff-hand had gone white-knuckled. She had thirty years of unanswered prayers stacked up inside her like dry timber, and this mountain was throwing sparks.

"Dominic." Her voice came thin through the voices. "Do you hear him?"

"Hear who?"

"My god." She laughed, and it was the worst sound I'd heard all day, and I had heard my mother's corpse offering me supper. "Vael. He never answered me. Thirty years, not once. And now — now he's *here*, in the wind, and he's saying my name, and he's sorry, Dominic, he says he was sleeping, he says it wasn't my fault the congregation starved—"

"Sera."

"He says if I come down he'll forgive me. He says if I turn back, all of it — the silence, the shame, the ones I buried — none of it was ever my—"

"*Sera.*"

She had stopped climbing. Worse — she had turned. She was facing down the Throat, toward the waterfall, toward the valley, her face lit with a joy so naked it hurt to look at, the joy of a woman being handed back the thing grief had taken. Her foot moved backward onto the next step down.

I grabbed her wrist.

She was strong — stronger than me, road-hardened where I was table-softened — and for a moment she simply pulled, and I dug my heels into the cut stone and held on, and the whispers swelled around us like a tide coming in, and I understood with perfect clarity that if she reached the bottom of this stair she would climb it again tomorrow, and the next day, forever, a woman eternally almost-home, until the mountain wore her smooth as a river stone.

I couldn't out-shout the voices. I couldn't out-pray them; I'd never prayed in my life, unless counting courses counts. I had exactly one skill, and it was this: when a thing offered itself as salvation, I could find the poison in it.

So I did the only thing I could think of. I anchored her the way I anchor myself.

"Aconite," I said, loud, into the storm. "Monkshood. Root and seed, worst in the root. I took it at Lord Aldery's harvest feast, year I was nineteen. Numbness first, then the heart forgets its rhythm. I survived it because the cook steeped the flowers instead of the roots, and flowers are gentler, and because I spat half of it into my napkin out of habit."

Sera stared at me. The pull on her wrist slackened by a finger's width.

"Hemlock," I went on. "Water dropwort, actually, worse cousin. The magistrate's banquet, four years ago. Kills in hours. I survived because the taster before me had trained my mouth to catch the parsley-smell of it before I swallowed, and because I made myself vomit in front of two hundred nobles, which is a story I will tell you someday when you need cheering up."

"What—" she started, and the whispers surged, her god's voice crying her name, and I talked louder.

"Arsenic. Slow, that one. Three months in the household of the Duchess of Cael Row, and we never found who. Gray skin, garlic breath, feet going numb like the world was leaving you from the bottom up. I survived it because I noticed the taste in the well water and everyone thought I was mad, and I let them think it, because mad tasters live longer than right ones."

"Dom—"

"Cyanide, from bitter almond, at the wedding of the border-lords. Belladonna, twice, once deliberate, once because a cook confused it with spinach, which remains the single stupidest poisoning in Hollowmere's history. Ergot, from rye bread, whole village, forty sick, me among them, because the taster eats first and the taster eats *everything*—"

"You don't have to—"

"—foxglove, lead, mercury in a pie, mercury in a *pie*, Sera, some people should not be allowed near ovens—"

She was laughing now, or something adjacent to laughing, tears freezing on her cheeks, and her feet had stopped moving downward, and I kept going, because the list was long, God help me, the list was twenty years long, and every entry was a night I had decided to keep living by noticing what was trying to stop me.

"Nux vomica. Cantharides. Amanita, death cap, the ambassador's dinner, they tell that story at the guild still. Antimony. Colchicum. Something at the spring equinox no herbalist has ever identified, which I have privately named 'the soup incident' and refuse to discuss further."

The storm screamed. Her god screamed her name. I leaned close, forehead nearly touching hers, salt-crusted and shaking, and finished:

"That's what I have. Not faith. Not courage. A list of every poison I ever swallowed and outlived. It's not much. But it's *true*, and it's mine, and nothing on this mountain can take it, because I paid for every line of it in full." I tightened my grip on her wrist. "Turn around, Sera. Climb up. Say no with me."

For a long moment she hung there between voices — his and mine, the sweet offer and the stubborn inventory — and then her eyes focused on me, really focused, and she exhaled a breath she might have been holding for thirty years.

"Bitter almond," she said hoarsely. "That's cyanide?"

"Among other things. Nature is stingy with flavors."

She turned. She climbed. And when the whispers came for her again — and they came, all night, wearing her god's voice like a stolen coat — she answered them the way I'd taught her without meaning to, muttering poison names like a rosary. Monkshood. Dropwort. The soup incident. Each one a small true thing, a stone in the shoe of every lie the mountain told her.

We climbed until the voices thinned. We climbed past the point where my legs stopped reporting to me and became rumor. And somewhere in the black hour before dawn, with the storm finally falling silent behind us and real stars showing through a break in the cloud, Sera stopped on a wide landing, sat down hard, and looked at me with an expression I couldn't read.

"Why poisons?" she asked. "Of all the things you could have recited. Why that?"

Because it was all I had. Because survival was the only prayer I knew. Because every item on that list was a moment I'd chosen, deliberately, stupidly, stubbornly, to go on — and maybe that was the whole of what love was, stripped of poetry: refusing, together, to stop.

"Because they're mine," I said. "Every one. Nobody gave them to me. Nobody sold them to me. I earned them the hard way, one swallowed cup at a time." I sat down beside her, and my legs filed a formal complaint. "Your god gave you nothing for thirty years. This mountain is offering you everything for free. I figured you should hear what things cost when you pay for them yourself."

She was quiet a while. Then she leaned her head on my shoulder, briefly, the way you'd rest a hand on a wall to check it would hold.

"The soup incident," she said. "You're telling me eventually."

"When you need cheering up."

"We're about to walk into a court of soul-devourers, Dominic."

"Then soon."

* * *

We slept in shifts on the landing, salt circles drawn around us, and woke to a light that was wrong.

Not dawn-light. It came from below — a deep red-gold glow rising up the Throat from the mountain's roots, pulsing slow as a heartbeat, slow as a ledger being opened. The whispers were gone. All of them. The silence they left was worse, the way an empty chair is worse than a filled one.

Sera stood at the edge of the landing, looking down at the glow, and her face had gone the color of old paper.

"That's not the court waking," she said. "That's the court *noticing*. Something's happened down there, Dominic. Something's made them look up."

I thought of the page hidden against my ribs, Maren's contract, stolen from a cathedral of ledgers. I thought of Ilvane, letting me walk. I thought of collectors massing at a failing ward around a valley full of bred and unspoiled souls.

"Then we'd better get there," I said, "before they come looking for what's missing."

Above us, the last stretch of stair climbed out of the rock into open air — and at the top of it, framed against the bruised sky, stood a gate of black iron taller than the chapel in Hollowmere, and standing before the gate, patient as winter, was a figure in gray.

It raised one hand. Not in threat.

In welcome.
