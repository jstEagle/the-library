## 14. Beneath the Mountain

The first thing I noticed about the underworld was the smell of paper.

I had expected sulfur, or rot, or the cold mineral breath of deep stone. Instead the air smelled like an old library that had been left out in the rain and dried badly — vellum and dust and something faintly sweet underneath, like honey left too long in a warm room. Later I would learn what that sweetness was. At the time, I made the mistake of finding it pleasant.

Sera stopped at the threshold behind me and did not come further.

"Envoy," she said quietly, adjusting the collar of my borrowed coat with hands that were not quite steady. "You are Envoy Voss of the Royal Household of Hollowmere, carrying the crown's tribute to the court beneath the mountain. You have never met me. You do not know my face. If they ask how you found the stair, you followed a rumor and a bribe."

"And if they ask why the crown sends a food-taster?"

"They won't ask." She smoothed the coat's shoulder one last time, a gesture that was half fussing and half blessing. "They'll assume the crown sent someone expendable. That's what courts are for."

"That's what tasters are for," I said. "Courts just borrow us."

She almost smiled. It didn't take. "Dominic. The ledgers in there — everything is written down. Every word you say can become a clause. Don't promise anything. Don't accept anything. Don't drink anything."

"I'm rather good at not drinking things," I said. "It's the one talent I've never had occasion to regret."

She held my eyes for a moment longer than comfort allowed, and then she stepped back into the dark of the stairwell, and I turned to face the doors.

They were taller than the gates of Hollowmere's keep. Black wood, or something pretending to be wood, carved all over with script so fine it looked from a distance like grain. As I approached, the script moved — slowly, patiently, the way frost creeps across a window — rearranging itself into words I could almost read before sliding away again. A contract, I realized. The doors themselves were a contract, and every soul that walked through them agreed to terms it hadn't read.

I thought about that for exactly as long as it took to remember Maren's fever-bright eyes, nineteen years of guilt stacked on her like stones, and then I pushed the doors open and walked through without reading a word.

* * *

The cathedral of ledgers opened around me like a throat.

It was vast beyond sense — a nave of black stone going up and up into a dark that no torch reached, and where a cathedral would hold pews, this held shelves. Shelves by the thousand, rising in tiers toward a ceiling that might have been sky, each shelf crowded with books and boxes and jars and scrolls, some bound in leather, some in things I chose not to identify. And moving among the shelves, silent as moths, were figures in grey — clerks, dozens of them, their faces smooth and featureless as eggs, carrying armfuls of souls to their appointed places.

Souls filed like vintages. Sera had warned me, but warning is a thin coat against seeing.

A jar near the entrance caught my eye: small, stoppered with wax, holding a curl of light the color of a candle seen through fog. The label read, in neat clerk's hand: *Tamsin of the Ford. Age 9. Cause of bargain: mother's fever. Terms: 40 years' service, remainder forfeit.* Below that, smaller: *Notes: sweet, uncomplicated. Pairs well with grief.*

Pairs well with grief. They catalogued children the way I catalogued wines.

Something in my chest went very cold and very still, which was, I would later understand, the most useful thing that could have happened to me. Fear burns. Rage burns. But cold — cold keeps. Cold lets a man stand in front of a king with hemlock on his tongue and report, evenly, that the broth is poisoned and he will need a new position by week's end.

I put Tamsin of the Ford out of my mind — not forgotten, filed, the way they filed things, in a place where I could find her later — and walked down the central aisle with the gait of a man who has every right to be there.

Halfway to the far end, where a dais rose beneath a canopy of hanging chains, a clerk detached itself from the shelves and glided to intercept me. Up close its smooth face was worse than a blank; it was *expectant*, the way an empty chair is expectant.

"Business," it said. Not a question. Not quite a greeting either. A word placed on the table like a coin.

"The crown of Hollowmere sends tribute to the court beneath the mountain," I said. My voice came out level. Twenty years of announcing poison at royal tables will teach a man to keep his voice level while his stomach climbs the walls. "I am Envoy Voss. I've come to discuss terms."

The clerk regarded me for a long moment. I had the distinct sensation of being tasted — a palate pressed briefly against my mind, checking for flaws, for rot, for anything that would make me unsuitable to serve.

"Terms," it repeated. "The valley's accounts are current."

"The valley's accounts," I said, "are about to be renegotiated. Or did your masters believe the ward's failure went unnoticed above?"

That was a gamble. I knew nothing about what the mountain court believed. But Ilvane had told me once, in that courteous voice of hers, that collectors hate surprises, and clerks, I reasoned, must be worse — a creature whose whole existence is the tidy recording of facts cannot abide a fact it hasn't recorded. The smooth face did something that was almost a flinch.

"Wait here," it said, and glided away up the aisle faster than it had come.

I waited. I looked at the shelves. I counted my breathing the way I counted sips when testing arsenic — slow, even, timed. Around me the clerks moved and murmured and shelved the light of ten thousand lives, and none of them looked at me, and all of them knew I was there. It occurred to me, standing in that silence, that I had spent my whole life being watched by people deciding whether I would live, and now I stood in the one place where that decision was made for everyone, everywhere, and the watching felt exactly the same. That was the moment I understood the mountain court and the crown of Hollowmere were the same animal wearing different coats. It did not help my composure. It did, strangely, steady it. You cannot be intimidated by a thing you have already served under another name.

At the end of the aisle, the dais. Upon the dais, three thrones, and upon the thrones —

I'll tell you what I saw, though I still don't fully believe it. Three shapes like robes worn by nobody, tall as doorways, crowned with antlers of black glass. Where faces should have been, pages turned. Slowly. Endlessly. Reading.

The middle throne spoke, and its voice was the sound of a ledger closing.

"Envoy Voss."

Not a question. Of course not. They had written my name somewhere already; I was certain of it. Somewhere on those shelves there was a page with *Voss* at the top, and I found I desperately wanted to see it and desperately feared doing so, and both desires I folded away into the cold place where I kept things until they were useful.

"Your Grace," I said, and bowed the precise depth I'd been taught for royalty one rank above my own — respectful, not groveling. Groveling invites contempt. Contempt invites scrutiny. "The crown of Hollowmere acknowledges the lapse of the ward and offers tribute in settlement, before the solstice, before any claim may be pressed against the valley's people."

"Offer," said the throne on the left, in a voice like wet paper.

I unslung the satchel Sera and I had prepared and set it on the floor between us, opening it so the contents caught whatever light there was: silver, old coins, a reliquary, three sealed writs bearing the chancellor's forged seal — Sera's forgery, and better work than the chancellor's own, which was its own quiet indictment of the church. "Silver of the valley mines. Coin struck under the old kings. Relics of the founding faith. And writs of perpetual passage — the crown grants the court's collectors free crossing of the ward's ruins, in perpetuity, documented and witnessed."

Silence. The pages on their faces turned. Somewhere high above, a chain swayed with no wind to move it.

"You offer," said the right-hand throne, softly, "what is already ours."

My blood went cold in a way that had nothing to do with fear and everything to do with arithmetic. Free crossing of the ward's ruins. I had offered them a deed to ground they already walked. Which meant the ward was not merely failing. Which meant they had already decided the harvest was theirs, and my tribute was, to them, a formality — a receipt for goods already claimed.

Good, said the cold place. Formalities have rules. Rules have edges. Edges can be worked.

"Then consider it acknowledgment rather than payment," I said. "The crown prefers its debts settled cleanly. But I confess, Your Graces, I did not climb your stair only to deliver silver." I let a pause stretch, the way you let a suspect dish sit on the tongue before swallowing. "There is a matter of accounting. An error. In your favor, I believe — but errors fester, even favorable ones, and the crown thought you'd want it corrected before the solstice closes your books."

The middle throne leaned forward. Robes do not lean; these leaned. The pages turned faster.

"An error," it said.

"A soul entered into your ledger nineteen years past," I said. "Sold under a template contract — the standard instrument, the one used throughout the northern valleys. But the seller was a child of seventeen, and the consideration given was immunity from poison for her brother." I kept my eyes on the middle throne and my voice bored and dry, a magistrate's voice, a man discussing grain tariffs. "Immunity, Your Graces, that your own breeding program provided. The crown's magistrates reviewed the instrument and found the consideration fraudulent. One cannot sell a soul in exchange for a gift the seller's own patron manufactured. The contract is voidable. We assumed the court would prefer to void it quietly, before the solstice, rather than have the flaw discovered in open audit."

I had no idea whether any of that would survive contact with their ledgers. Sera had helped me build the argument from fragments — her knowledge of contract-law as the mountain court practiced it, my memory of Maren's confession, and one crucial truth Ilvane had let slip in a moment of what I now suspected was deliberate carelessness: that the court prized clean books above nearly everything. Souls, yes. Power, certainly. But a ledger with a flaw in it offended them the way a fly offends a banquet.

The pages on the three faces stopped turning.

For the first time since entering, the cathedral was truly silent. Even the clerks had gone still among their shelves, smooth faces tilted toward the dais like flowers toward a sun that wasn't there.

"Show me," said the middle throne, "the name."

And here it was. The moment the whole performance balanced on. Because I did not know where Maren's page was filed, and asking to *see* the ledger of Voss was precisely the request a fraud would make, and precisely the request an envoy with a genuine grievance would also make, and the difference between those two men was nothing but nerve.

I did not give them nerve. I gave them boredom.

"Voss," I said, as if reciting from the writ in my pocket. "Maren Voss, entered winter of the year the old king died. The crown's copy is fragmentary — the original instrument was executed below, as I understand your custom. I'll need to inspect your record to certify the error for the audit." I produced the forged writ, held it out, and waited with the patience of a man who has waited out kings.

A clerk materialized — I never saw it approach — took the writ in fingers like rolled parchment, and carried it up the dais steps. The middle throne read it. The pages turned once, twice.

Then the throne raised one sleeve, and somewhere in the dark of the shelves, high and to the left, a book sang.

There is no other word for it. A single clear note, like a wet finger on a crystal rim, and a slim volume slid itself from its shelf, drifted down through the dark, and landed in the clerk's arms. The clerk brought it to the foot of the dais and set it on a reading stand that I swear had not existed a moment before.

"Inspect," said the middle throne.

I climbed the steps to the stand. My legs worked. I was mildly proud of them.

The book was titled, in blind-stamped letters, simply: *VOSS.*

I opened it with a taster's hands — steady, delicate, reverent, the hands I used for opening a duke's wine — and turned past the early pages. Entries in a clerk's hand, generations of them. *Voss, Aldric. Cause of bargain: debt. Terms: harvested, year 312.* *Voss, Ede. Cause of bargain: rain for the crops. Terms: fulfilled, remainder sold onward.* My family, vintage after vintage, poured out into this dark for two hundred years. I read the names and felt nothing, because feeling could wait, and turned the pages forward, forward, to the newest ink, still faintly wet.

*Maren Voss.*

And beneath her name, in the same neat hand, the terms — and the terms were wrong. Wrong in a way that stopped my breath. Not forty years. Not service. The line read:

*Collateral against the Voss immunity. Due upon collection of the principal. Principal: Dominic Voss.*

Due upon collection of the principal. Not the solstice. Her soul was not the payment. It was the *down payment* — a lien held against mine, and mine, bred and unspoiled and walking freely into their cathedral, was the vintage they had always meant to pour.

They had let me come. They had opened the doors for me. The whole court had watched me walk down the aisle offering silver for a debt that was merely the cork on a much older bottle.

I looked up. The three thrones had not moved. But every page on every face had stopped turning, and all of them, I understood at last, were open to the same entry.

"Envoy Voss," said the middle throne, gently, the way one speaks to a wine that has finally been decanted. "We're so pleased you've come to discuss terms."

Behind me, soft as a moth landing, the great doors closed.
