## 15. The Ledger of Voss

The clerk who led me deeper into the mountain had a face like an unpaid bill.

I don't mean that as an insult. I mean it precisely. His features were arranged the way a magistrate arranges documents — everything filed, nothing felt — and when he smiled at me, which he did twice on the descent, it was the smile of a man confirming receipt. Somewhere behind his eyes, I suspected, there had once been a person. Somewhere further back than that, a name.

"Envoy Voss," he said, for the third time, tasting the syllables like I tasted broth. "The Archivist will see you now."

"The Archivist," I repeated, because in my experience repeating a title back to its owner made them feel important, and feeling important made them talk.

"Keeper of the Ledger of Debts Unpaid." He said it the way Sera said her old god's name — with reverence worn down to habit. "You stand in the only library in creation where every volume is alive."

I had seen the cathedral of ledgers from the gallery above, and even at a distance it had rearranged something in my chest. Now, walking among the shelves, I understood why pilgrims used to weep at relics. The books *breathed*. Each spine rose and fell in slow rhythm, and the leather — no, not leather, I realized, and made myself stop realizing it — was warm under my gloved fingers. Ink moved beneath the covers like veins under skin. When we passed, some of the volumes turned toward me. All of them, actually. A soft rustle ran down each aisle as we walked, ten thousand souls shifting in their sleep to look at the living man in their midst.

"They know what you are," the clerk said, without judgment. "Unharvested. It's rare enough to be noticeable. Like a vintage that was never bottled."

"I'm flattered."

"You shouldn't be. Bottling is a kindness. Out here—" he gestured at the vast dark above us, where the ceiling should have been and instead was a slow churn of shadow, "—vintages spoil. We preserve. That is the mercy of the court."

I kept my face doing what it did at the royal table: pleasant, attentive, faintly stupid. Twenty years of practice. A taster's first skill isn't detecting poison. It's detecting nothing — letting your face become a wall so smooth that a nervous cook can't read whether you've found the arsenic in the frangipane. I had stood before kings with hemlock blooming on my tongue and thanked the chef. I could stand before a library of the damned and ask about filing systems.

"Her Majesty's tribute," I said, patting the satchel at my hip, where the forged writs Sera had inked sat folded over a brick of lead wrapped in silk. Weight matters. Bureaucrats everywhere trust weight. "The crown wishes to confirm the status of certain contracts before transfer of sovereignty. Standard audit."

"Of course." He stopped before a shelf that looked identical to every other shelf, except that the books here were older, their breathing slower, deeper, like sleepers long past dreaming. "Name?"

"Voss."

Something happened to his face then. Not much. A flicker, the way candlelight flickers when a door opens somewhere in a house. "Voss," he said again, and this time he wasn't confirming receipt. "Maren Voss. Contract 4,417. Collateral instrument." He turned to the shelf and drew out a slim volume with hands that were gentle the way an undertaker is gentle. "And you are... related?"

"Brother."

"Ah." He held the book out to me. "Then you may read. Family has reading rights. It's in the charter." And there — I would swear this on any altar, including the ones that had recently started answering — there was something almost like pity in his voice. "Read all of it, Envoy. Most families don't."

* * *

The book was warm. Of course it was warm. I carried it to a reading desk carved from black stone, sat down beneath a lamp that burned without oil or flame or, I suspected, permission, and opened my sister's soul to its first page.

The script was beautiful. That was the first obscenity. Whoever had drafted Maren's contract had done it in a hand so elegant it belonged on a wedding invitation, each letter a small act of devotion, and I understood suddenly that this was how they lured people. Not with claws. With calligraphy. You come to the devil's door desperate and ashamed, and the devil's paperwork is more lovely than anything your parish priest ever showed you, and some starving animal part of you thinks: *anything written this beautifully must be true.*

*Contract of Exchange,* it began, *entered into beneath the witness of stone, by Maren, daughter of Aldric Voss, being of sound mind and desperate circumstance—*

Sound mind. Desperate circumstance. She'd been nineteen. I'd been six and drowning in my own blood, some childhood fever the valley's physicians called "the wasting" and treated with leeches and prayers to gods who weren't listening because the ward kept them out along with everything else. I knew the story the way you know family stories, worn smooth by retelling: a stranger at the door during the worst night, an offer made in a voice like cool water, and Maren saying yes before our parents came back from the shrine.

*The undersigned covenants to convey her soul entire, upon maturity of term, to the Court Beneath, in consideration of services rendered: namely, the preservation of the life of Dominic Voss, minor child.*

There it was. Nineteen years old, in ink that breathed. My life, priced. Her signature at the bottom, small and slanted, pressing hard enough to tear the page slightly — she'd been crying, or shaking, or both.

I kept reading, because the clerk had told me to read all of it, and because a taster knows that the poison is never in the first sip.

*Term of maturity: the winter solstice following the nineteenth year.*

That matched what Maren had told me. What she hadn't told me — what she likely didn't know herself, because who reads to the end of a contract when their brother is dying — was the next clause.

*Be it further covenanted that the soul herein conveyed stands as collateral against the continued efficacy of the Consideration. Should the Beneficiary's immunity lapse, fail, or be otherwise extinguished prior to maturity, the collateral shall be forfeit immediately and without further process.*

I read it three times. The words didn't change. Words from the mountain court, I was learning, never changed; that was their whole nature. They were the one thing in creation that meant exactly what they said.

My immunity. Her soul wasn't payment for saving me. It was a *deposit* — held against the possibility that whatever they'd done to me might wear off. If I had died of the wasting anyway, if the bargain had failed, they'd have taken her years ago. And if I lived—

If I lived, the immunity held, and the collateral matured at solstice, and they collected.

Either way, they won. Either way, Maren's soul was theirs from the moment she signed. The solstice wasn't a deadline. It was a *harvest date*. And the ward, the great blessed ward that kept magic out of Hollowmere, that had kept me safe my whole life — the ward had also kept me from ever learning this, from ever meeting a priestess or a hedge-witch or a smuggler who might have told me what my sister's feverish confession actually meant. The pen had kept the livestock calm.

I put the book down very carefully, because part of me wanted to throw it into the lamp, and you do not throw living things into fires, even when they deserve it. Especially when they deserve it. That, I was beginning to understand, was the whole theology of this place.

"Envoy Voss?" The clerk had returned without making a sound. Of course he had. "The Archivist will see you now."

* * *

The Archivist sat at the center of the cathedral, at a desk the size of a hay wagon, and the Archivist was a book.

Not a person holding a book. Not a person *reading* a book. The desk itself was bound in that warm not-leather, and from its pages rose a figure made of ink — a tall suggestion of a body, drawn in flowing script that never quite settled into features, letters swimming across where a face should be, reforming, dissolving. When it spoke, the words appeared briefly on its chest like a line of verse being typeset and then wiped clean.

*Envoy of Hollowmere.* The voice came from everywhere, from the shelves, from the floor, from inside my own skull where it resonated like a finger run around the rim of a glass. *We have audited your credentials.*

"Have you." I set the satchel on the desk and did not open it. Let them wonder about the weight.

*The crown of the valley sends tribute after two hundred years of silence, in the season the ward fails, through an envoy whose blood smells of our oldest vintage.* The ink-face rippled, and I got the impression — absurd, unbearable — of amusement. *We are not fools, little taster. We invented fine print. We can taste it too.*

Well. There went fourteen chapters of careful lying.

I took off the envoy's seal-ring Sera had forged and set it on the desk beside the satchel, and I heard myself say, in the flat clear voice I used for announcing poison at the royal table: "Then let's skip the course where I pretend, and the course where you pretend to believe me, and get to the one dish you actually brought me here to taste."

The cathedral went quiet. Ten thousand books held their breath.

*Speak.*

"My name is Dominic Voss. Contract 4,417 is my sister. I've read the collateral clause. I know the solstice is theater — that you hold her regardless, that you've held her since she was nineteen years old, that the ward you built around my valley was never a shield, it was a *pen*, and my family has been fattening in it for four generations waiting for someone with my blood to walk up your stairs." My voice stayed level. Some distant part of me admired it, the way you admire a tightrope from underneath. "I'm not here as an envoy. I'm here as an account holder. What does it cost to close contract 4,417?"

The ink flowed. For a long moment the Archivist simply regarded me, letters chasing each other across the space where eyes would be, and I had the sense of an enormous calculation running — not moral, nothing here was moral, but *actuarial*. Weighing me. Pricing me.

*You have been speaking with Ilvane,* it said at last.

Ilvane. The collector who had cornered us on the road, who had offered to erase Maren's debt if I surrendered my own soul instead, and whom I'd refused while Sera burned a shrine for cover. I'd thought that offer was a trap. Apparently it had been a *referral*.

*She flagged your file. An unspoiled Voss, walking freely, asking questions.* The ink-face smoothed into something almost like a smile. *You should know that your bloodline was not bred for immunity alone, Dominic Voss. Immunity was merely the pen. What we bred — what took nineteen generations — is a palate. A soul that has tasted every bitterness the mortal world offers and remained unclaimed by any of them. Do you understand what that is worth?*

"No," I said honestly. "But I'm about to."

*Everything.* The word appeared across the Archivist's chest in letters a foot high, and the whole cathedral seemed to lean in. *A soul untouched by pact, prayer, or god. Every other soul in this room was discounted the moment it was made. Bought with desperation, seasoned with faith, watered down by hope. Yours is full-strength. Neat. The last of its line and the finest of its vintage.* The ink gathered itself taller. *So. Here is our counteroffer, since you asked the price.*

It slid a fresh page across the desk. Blank. Waiting.

*A clerkship. Your sister's contract dissolved — paid in full, closed, her soul released to whatever quiet end mortals imagine awaits them. In exchange: you remain. Not harvested — preserved. You will keep your body, your senses, your famous palate. You will sit at a desk in this cathedral and taste contracts for us, as you tasted food for kings. You will detect the flaw in every bargain before it is signed. You will want for nothing. You will feel nothing you do not choose to feel. The work is not unpleasant. The work, in fact, is peace.*

I looked at the blank page. I looked at Maren's book, still open on the reading desk behind me, her small slanted signature trembling in the lamplight.

Peace. That was the poison in this dish, and it was subtle, and it was aimed exactly right. Twenty years I had built my life out of controlled numbness — the wall-face, the flat voice, survival mistaken for living. They had read my whole history the way I read a broth. They knew that some tired, starved corner of me had dreamed, on bad nights, of exactly this: a room where nothing could hurt me, work that mattered, silence without dread. They weren't offering me damnation. They were offering me retirement.

And Maren would go free. Truly free. That was the cruelty of it — the offer was *good*. That's how you know it's from the mountain court. The devil doesn't cheat you with a bad bargain. He cheats you with a fair one.

"You said I'd feel nothing I don't choose to feel," I said.

*Correct.*

"And what happens to what I don't choose? Does it stay in the vault with the rest of your inventory?"

The ink stilled. For the first time, the Archivist did not answer immediately, and I understood that I had found the seam in the garment — the place where the fine print showed. Everything I declined to feel, filed away. Every grief, every love, every stubborn useless tenderness, harvested by inches and stored on a shelf to breathe. A clerkship wasn't servitude. It was *self-harvesting*, administered politely, one voluntary numbness at a time. They wouldn't take my soul. They'd let me hand it over in installments and thank them for the shelving.

I picked up Maren's book. I held it against my chest, and it beat against my ribs like a second heart, which I suppose it was.

"Here's my counteroffer," I said. "You release her. Now. Tonight. And in exchange, I'll give you the one thing you actually want — not my servitude, not my numbness. My *soul*, whole, unspoiled, full-strength, the last of nineteen generations of your breeding program. Freely offered, no contract, no fine print, no clerkship. But on my terms, at my altar, in my valley, under the open sky — because I have read enough of your calligraphy to know that anything signed in this room belongs to this room."

The silence that followed had texture. It had *weight*. Somewhere far above us, the mountain groaned, and I had the sudden, lunatic certainty that the whole court had gone still to listen.

*You would trade yourself for her,* the Archivist said. *Knowing what we are. Knowing what collection means.*

"I'm a food taster," I said. "I've spent my whole life putting known poisons in my mouth on the chance it saves someone else. This is just the last course."

The ink began to flow again, faster now, letters streaming down the Archivist's frame like rain down glass, and the lamp above the desk guttered though it burned on nothing, and every book in the cathedral exhaled at once —

and behind me, at the far end of the aisle, a slim volume on a high shelf snapped itself shut with a sound like a bone breaking, and began, quietly, to scream.

The Archivist's attention snapped toward it. So did mine. I knew that scream. I'd heard it muffled through Maren's fever, through nineteen years of guilt, through every silent supper we'd ever shared.

Sera. Something had happened to Sera.

*I will consider your terms,* the Archivist said, and its voice had changed — colder, faster, the voice of an institution discovering a fire in its archives. *Remain here, Envoy. Do not touch the shelves.*

Which was, of course, the moment I picked up my sister's soul, tucked it inside my coat against my heart, and ran.
