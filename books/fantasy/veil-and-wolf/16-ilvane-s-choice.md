## 16. Ilvane's Choice

There is a particular sound a ledger makes when a page is torn from it.

Most people never hear it. Most people never want to. It is not paper tearing — paper is nothing, paper is the least of what a soul-contract is made of — it is more like a bone remembering it was once part of something that could walk. A small, wet, ancient protest. The sound of an argument the mountain has been winning for ten thousand years.

I heard it from three corridors away.

I was doing what I always do at the turn of the third bell, which is standing very still in the Gallery of Unpaid Interest and listening to the debts breathe. That's the trick of collection nobody tells you when they harvest you: you don't go hunting souls. You go hunting *sound*. A soul in good standing is silent. A soul coming due hums. And a soul being stolen — a soul being *ripped* out of the mountain's filing system by a mortal boy with flour still under his fingernails — makes a noise like the world's smallest grief.

I set down my ledger-clip. I straightened my collar. I did not run, because collectors do not run; running implies urgency, and urgency implies that outcomes are uncertain, and the mountain's entire architecture — architectural, theological, and bureaucratic — rests on the proposition that outcomes are never uncertain.

Then I ran.

* * *

Let me tell you what I was, before I was this.

My name was not always Ilvane. That is the first thing the mountain takes — not your memories, not your face, those it keeps, the way a vintner keeps the label on a bottle it intends to open later. It takes your name first because a name is a handle, and handles are how you get picked up. My name had been something short and warm. I've spent four hundred years trying to remember it and all I ever get is the shape of it, like a footprint in snow after the snow has half melted.

I was a midwife. In a village whose name I remember perfectly, which is the mountain's little joke — it takes what you'd trade and keeps what you wouldn't. There was a winter, the kind of winter that comes maybe twice in a century, when the fever went through us like a scythe through wet wheat. I held forty-one children into the world that year and I watched thirty of them leave it, and on the coldest night, kneeling in the straw with a dying girl in my arms, I said the words. You know the words. Everyone knows the words, even people who have never prayed in their lives. They're stitched into the lining of every desperate throat:

*Anything. Take anything. Just let her live.*

The mountain answered. Of course it answered. It had been waiting four hundred years for someone in that valley to say the words, the way a fisherman waits by a hole in the ice. The girl lived. She grew up, married, had children of her own, named her first daughter after me — she thought I'd died of the fever, which in every way that mattered, I had. And I came below, and they put me in a uniform the color of wet slate, and they explained the terms, and I signed, because the alternative was the Vats, and even then, even new, even screaming, I understood that the Vats were not a threat. They were a *destination*. Everything here flows toward them eventually. The mountain is just very patient about the plumbing.

Four hundred years I have collected. I am good at it. I am — and I say this with the flat professional pride of a hangman complimenting his own rope — excellent at it. I know every clause, every contingency, every loophole shaped like a mercy and every mercy shaped like a trap. I have talked farmers out of their grandmothers' graves and poets out of their unborn books. I have stood at ten thousand deathbeds holding out a contract and a pen, smiling the smile they teach you in orientation, which is the smile of a door that opens only inward.

And in four hundred years, do you know how many people have refused the bargain cleanly?

I'll save you the arithmetic. Before last month: none.

* * *

You need to understand what "cleanly" means, because the mountain has spent millennia making sure the word rots.

People refuse bargains all the time. They refuse stupidly, with screaming. They refuse conditionally — *anything but the memories, take the memories but not the name.* They refuse and then un-refuse two bells later when the pain spikes. They refuse on their own behalf and fold instantly when you mention a daughter. Every refusal has a seam in it somewhere, a place where the needle can go back in, and finding the seam is the whole art. The mountain does not need everyone to sign. It needs only that no one refuses *cleanly*, because a clean refusal — a refusal with no seam, offered by someone who understood the full price and paid attention while counting it — sets a precedent. And precedent, in a place built entirely of contracts, is a load-bearing wall.

That taster. Voss. The brother of the midwife-girl Maren, whose contract sat in our files like a plum waiting for solstice.

In the shrine town, weeks ago, I offered him the easy trade. His protected soul — bred unspoiled, a vintage the mountain had been cellaring for generations — in exchange for his sister's freedom. It was a generous offer. It was, frankly, the best offer anyone in that valley had received in living memory, and I delivered it with all the courtesy my office requires, and he looked at me with those taster's eyes — eyes that have learned to look at a beautiful dish and see only the poison in it — and he said no.

Not *no, but.* Not *no, unless.* Not *no, and here is my counteroffer, let us negotiate like reasonable people while my sister's soul accrues interest.*
