## 17. The Flight Down

I have died before. Twice, if you count the time I drowned in the Mirren as a girl and was pulled out by a fisherman who charged my father a week's wages and never once stopped chewing his bread. And once more, thirty years into my priesthood, when I prayed at the high altar of Vael and heard nothing back — not silence exactly, but the particular silence of an empty house, the kind where the furniture is covered in sheets.

So when I say that running through the roots of the mountain felt like dying, understand that I am speaking as an authority on the subject.

Dominic ran ahead of me with the stolen page crushed against his chest like a man carrying a wounded bird, and behind us the mountain began — slowly, with the terrible patience of a thing that has waited nine hundred years — to wake.

* * *

It started as light. Not torchlight; the ledgers' cathedral had been lit by nothing we could name, a pale glow that came from the vellum itself, from all those souls filed and shelved and catalogued. But as we fled down the stair behind the altar dais, the glow behind us turned gold, then amber, then the deep angry red of a forge being fed.

"They know," Dominic said. He didn't pant. I noticed that about him even then, even with collectors shrieking somewhere above us like kettles left too long on the fire. He conserved himself the way he did everything — precisely, economically, a man who had spent his whole life measuring out exactly what a moment required. "The page. The moment I lifted it, they knew."

"Then stop apologizing with your face and run faster."

"I wasn't—"

"You were. You have a whole liturgy of guilt expressions. I catalogued them somewhere around day four." I grabbed his sleeve and hauled him left at the fork, because I had spent three decades reading maps of holy places and the mountain's roots were nothing so much as a cathedral turned inside out — buttresses of black stone, naves of tunnel, and everywhere, everywhere, the sense of being *inside* something that could feel you.

The whispers found us at the second landing.

You'll want me to describe them, and I will, but poorly, because they don't travel well into language. They were the same voices that had plagued our ascent — the ones that offered each traveler their heart's desire — but stripped now of their courtesy. On the way up they had been merchants. On the way down they were creditors.

*Sera Vane,* said the stone, in a voice I knew. Of course I knew it. It was the voice I had prayed into for thirty years, the voice that had answered me exactly once, last month, with a single word: *No.*

*Come back. Kneel again. We remember what you were before you failed.*

"I didn't fail," I said through my teeth, because arguing with hallucinations is a bad habit of mine and I see no reason to reform at my age.

*You built the contracts. You wrote the beautiful words that made theft sound like devotion. Come home, little mouthpiece. The court forgives its instruments.*

"That's the other one," I told Dominic. "That's not Vael. Vael never forgave anything in its existence. That's the mountain wearing his voice like a stolen coat."

"How can you tell?"

"Because it's being nice to me."

He almost smiled. Even then. Even running for both our lives with a soul-contract under his shirt and the underworld waking around us like a beast rolling over in its sleep. I want that recorded: the man nearly smiled.

* * *

The gate was where Ilvane's directions said it would be — which should have comforted me and instead made me furious, because it meant the collector had planned this escape route for us, had perhaps planned everything, centuries of patience folded into a single act of mercy she hadn't yet committed. A door of grey stone set into the mountain's root, uncarved, unadorned, the only honest piece of architecture in the entire underworld. No ornament. No invitation. Just a way out, for whoever was desperate enough to look.

It was sealed.

Not locked — sealed, the way a wound seals, with a skin of something that shimmered faintly when Dominic held the stolen page near it. Court-work. The gate would open for the page or for a key of equal weight, and we had no key, and the red light was coming down the stair behind us now in slow tides, and in those tides I could hear footsteps. Not hurrying. Collectors never hurry. Why would they? Time is the one thing they own outright.

"Sera." Dominic's voice had gone very quiet, the way it did when he was tasting something and didn't yet know if he'd survive it. "Tell me you have a plan."

"I have half a plan."

"I've learned to dread your fractions."

"The gate opens for the page," I said. "Or for something the court wants more than the page. Something with enough weight to bargain the lock open." I looked at my own hands. Thirty years of prayer had left calluses on them shaped like nothing useful. "Or someone the court remembers owing."

"No," Dominic said immediately, which told me he'd followed me there faster than I'd hoped. "Whatever you're thinking, no."

"It's sweet that you think 'no' works on me. I took vows, Dominic. Vows are just 'no' with better posture."

* * *

Here is what I have not told him, what I am telling you now because this chapter belongs to me and I am tired of carrying things quietly:

When the gods woke — angry, ravenous, answering prayers at exchange rates that would make a loan-shark blush — I went looking for mine. Vael. My god. The one who had given me thirty years of silence and one word of refusal. I climbed to the high place above the ruined chapel where I'd been ordained and I shouted myself hoarse, and Vael came.

Not descended. Came. The way a debt collector comes.

And I understood, standing in that presence, why the gods had slept so long, and why they'd woken furious, and why every miracle in the valley country came with a price tag now. They had woken to find their names on contracts. Their power mortgaged. Somewhere in the deep accounting between heaven and the mountain, the old gods had signed things — or been made to seem to sign things — and the court beneath the mountain had been collecting interest ever since.

Vael looked at me, its last priestess, its failed servant, its human mouthpiece during the years I helped draft the templates — Maren's template among them, though I didn't know it then — and it said the second word it had ever said to me.

It said: *Owed.*

Not that I owed. That I was *owed*. That somewhere in the ledger of heaven there was a line with my name on it, an ancient grievance, a balance in my favor that no one had ever honored because no one had ever asked.

I had laughed. I remember that. I stood in the presence of a waking god and laughed until I cried, and said, "Then pay me," and Vael said nothing, because gods are cowards about paying their debts, and I walked away believing that was the end of it.

It was not the end of it. Debts like that don't expire. They wait.

* * *

The red tide reached the landing below us. I could see them now — three collectors, tall and wrong, their faces like pages someone had written on and regretted. Ilvane was not among them. I noted that too. Ilvane, I was beginning to suspect, was elsewhere doing something the rest of her kind would very much disapprove of.

"Sera." Dominic caught my arm. His grip was stronger than it looked; years of lifting trays, I suppose, of holding steady while poison did its work. "Whatever you're about to do — and I can smell a sacrifice forming, it has a distinct odor, like burnt sugar and self-pity — find another one."

"Burnt sugar?"

"You'd be surprised what I can smell."

"Of course you can. You're a sommelier of doom." I turned to face the gate. The seal shimmered. Behind it, impossibly far and impossibly close, I could feel morning air. Real air. Air that had never been inside a ledger. "Here's the arithmetic, taster. The gate opens for the page or for a greater weight. The page gets us out. But the moment we're through, the court sends everything it has after us, and we arrive at Hollowmere with an army of collectors on our heels and a solstice deadline, and Maren dies anyway, and so do you, and probably me, though honestly I've been dead twice already and the third time hardly frightens me."

"And your alternative?"

"My alternative is I spend what I'm owed."

He stared at me. In the red light his face looked carved, all planes and shadow, a face made for coins. "Spend it how?"

"Vael owes me. An old grievance. A balance in my favor, thirty years of unanswered prayer compounded at whatever rate heaven uses." I laughed again, briefly, because the alternative was weeping and I refuse to weep in front of a man who catalogs my guilt expressions. "Debts can be transferred, Dominic. Spent. I can burn the whole balance in one act — call it due, here, now — and buy us a door."

"And what does that cost you?"

"Everything I have left of him." My voice did something I didn't authorize. It cracked. "Thirty years I served a god who never spoke. Last month he finally did, and what he said was that I was owed. Do you understand what that means? It means the silence was never indifference. It means he was *ashamed*. It means somewhere in all that emptiness there was a god who watched me fail and grieve and keep praying anyway, and kept a tally of what that cost me, and couldn't bring himself to say so." I pressed my palm flat against the cold seal. "It's the only proof I ever had that any of it was real. And I'm going to set it on fire to hold a door open for a food-taster and a piece of paper."

"Sera—"

"Don't. Don't do the guilt face. Save it. You'll need it later; you're saving up for something enormous, I can tell."

* * *

I want to be honest about what happened next, because priests lie mostly by omission and I am trying, in these last pages of my old life, to stop.

I called the debt due, and it hurt more than drowning.

Vael came — not in body, gods don't have bodies anymore, if they ever did — but as a pressure, a presence filling the tunnel like water fills a lung. And I felt every year of service leave me. Not painfully, worse than painfully: *gently.* The memory of my ordination, the oil on my forehead. The thousand small prayers I'd whispered over sickbeds and cradles and graves. The single word *no*, and the strange comfort I'd taken from even that. All of it drawn out of me like thread from a spool, and woven — I could feel it happening — into the seal on the gate.

The seal drank it. Light moved under its skin like dawn under frost.

*Paid in full,* said Vael, and the voice was different this time, or I was different, and either way I heard what I'd never heard in thirty years of service: something that might have been love, or might have been grief, and in gods I've learned the two are indistinguishable.

*Was it real?* I asked. The last question of my priesthood. *Any of it?*

*All of it,* said my god, spending my faith like coin to hold the door. *That was always the problem.*

Then the gate opened, and Vael was gone, and I was empty in a way that had no bottom to it.

* * *

Dominic pulled me through. I remember that much — his hand on my wrist, iron-firm, hauling me across a threshold while the collectors reached the landing behind us and the mountain's voice rose from a whisper to a roar, the whole court waking at once, every ledger in that cathedral snapping open like ten thousand wings.

We came out onto a hillside under a sky gone the color of a bruise, solstice-dark at midday, and the mountain behind us shuddered from root to crown. Snow slid from its shoulders. Somewhere deep below, something that had been pretending to be architecture for nine hundred years stood up.

"Is that—" Dominic began.

"Yes."

"Is it coming *out*?"

"Not yet." I sat down hard in the snow, because my legs had resigned without notice. "But it's awake. And it knows where the ward is thin."

He crouched beside me, the stolen page still clutched to his chest, snow gathering in his hair. For a long moment neither of us spoke. Below us the valley of Hollowmere lay in its winter grey, small and safe-looking and utterly unprepared, and above us the mountain breathed.

"You gave up your god," he said finally. Not a question. He had watched the whole thing and understood it faster than most theologians would have.

"I gave up the *proof* of my god. Which may be the same thing." I looked at my hands. They felt like anyone's hands. "Thirty years I wanted an answer. I got one. It cost me the ability to ever hear another." I laughed, and it came out wrong, wet at the edges. "There's a lesson in there about prayer. I'm too tired to find it."

"Sera." He said my name carefully, the way he tasted suspect broth — testing for poison before swallowing. "Thank you isn't—"

"No. It isn't. So don't." I wiped my face with a sleeve. "Just get your sister back. That's the only tithe I'll accept."

But even as I said it, I felt it — faint, absurd, impossible — a warmth in the emptied place where Vael had been. Not a voice. Not a presence. Just warmth, like a hearth in a house whose owner has stepped out and left the fire burning.

As if something, somewhere, had decided I wasn't finished being owed after all.

Down in the valley, at the edge of Hollowmere, the ward flickered — visible now to eyes like mine, eyes newly emptied of one god and therefore able to see the workings of others — and I counted the shapes gathering beyond it in the failing light.

"Dominic," I said. "How many days to solstice?"

"One."

"Then we have a problem," I said, "and it's already at the border."
