## 18. Solstice Eve

The valley of Hollowmere smelled different from the outside.

I'd never known that. Nineteen years inside a ward and you stop noticing what air is supposed to smell like — you assume the whole world tastes faintly of nothing, like water that's been boiled too long. But standing now on the ridge above my home, with Sera breathing hard beside me and Maren's stolen page pressed flat against my chest where I'd sewn it into my coat, I could taste the valley from a mile off: ward-smoke and old stone and the particular flavor of a lie told so long everyone had stopped calling it one.

It tasted like safety. It tasted like a pen.

"Stop making that face," Sera said.

"What face?"

"The one where you're about to say something bitter and clever instead of feeling something."

"I'm a food taster. Bitter and clever is the whole profession."

She didn't laugh. She hadn't laughed properly since the mountain's roots, since she'd burned whatever was left of her connection to her god to hold that gate open for us — and she'd come out the other side of that fire changed in ways I couldn't name yet. Her eyes did something at the edges when she looked at open sky now, like she was checking whether anyone up there was looking back. Sometimes they were. I tried not to think about whose side of the glass I was on.

Below us, Hollowmere lay under early dusk, lamplight blooming street by street, smoke rising straight from chimneys because there was no wind inside the ward — there never was, I realized, not once in my life. The valley was a bowl with a lid on it, and we had all lived our whole lives congratulating ourselves on how cozy the bowl was.

And around the lid, like flies on honey: collectors.

I counted eleven before I stopped counting. They stood at intervals along the ward's rim, patient as fence posts, their shapes wrong in the way that made your eye slide off them if you weren't careful. Ilvane had taught me — unwillingly, in a moment of mercy I still hadn't fully processed — that they weren't guards. Guards keep things out. These were keeping something *in*. Or rather, keeping the vintage sealed until decanting.

"They're waiting for solstice," Sera said. "Tomorrow night. The ward won't survive it. Whatever your sister's contract did to shore it up—" she tapped the leather satchel where the page rode against my ribs, "—it comes due, and the whole valley opens like an oyster."

"One soul," I said. "The contract is for one soul. Hers. Why would the ward fall for one soul?"

Sera looked at me with the particular patience she reserved for moments when I was being slow on purpose, which she seemed to believe was most moments.

"You read the page, Dominic. You carried it out of a cathedral of ledgers past three collectors and a clerk who eats memories. Tell me what it says."

I closed my eyes. I could recite it. I'd been reciting it in my sleep for six days, waking with the clauses stuck to the roof of my mouth like tannin.

*Collateral instrument. One (1) soul, Maren Voss, held against the maintenance of the Hollowmere Ward and the preservation of the Voss bloodline's immunity. Upon default, collection shall proceed against the bloodline entire.*

Against the bloodline entire.

"It was never just her," I said. My voice came out flat, which is what it does instead of shaking. "Her soul wasn't payment. It was a *stopper*. As long as the court holds her, the ward holds, and the herd stays penned. And when the debt comes due—"

"—they collect her, and the pen opens, and every unspoiled soul in Hollowmere goes onto the ledger." Sera's breath fogged in the cold. "Nineteen years ago someone in your family traded one child's life for a town's worth of souls, and nobody down there has any idea."

My parents were dead. That was the mercy of it, or the cruelty — I couldn't decide which. My mother had signed. I knew her hand; I'd seen it on recipes and letters and the inside cover of my childhood books. *E. Voss.* Somewhere in a cathedral beneath a mountain, her signature sat in a ledger next to a clause she probably hadn't understood, buying her son another nineteen years at the price of everything after.

"She thought she was saving me," I said.

"She was. That's the worst part. It worked." Sera pulled her cloak tighter. "Every prayer answered, every bargain kept — the court always delivers. That's how they get you. Hell doesn't cheat you. Hell gives you exactly what you asked for and lets you live with the arithmetic."

Down in the valley, someone was lighting the solstice lanterns early. Little orange points along the market square, the ones we hung every year to "keep the dark honest," a phrase my grandmother used that meant absolutely nothing and had always, I now understood, been a prayer to something with excellent hearing.

"We have to go down," I said. "Warn them. Get them out."

"Out where?" Sera asked gently. "Dominic. Look at them."

I looked. Lamplit windows. Chimney smoke. A dog barking somewhere near the tannery, the same dog that had barked at me every morning of my working life. People moving behind glass, setting tables, doing the small domestic machinery of an ordinary evening, the last ordinary evening some of them would ever have.

They wouldn't leave. I knew it the way I knew hemlock on the tongue — instantly, completely, without needing to test it. These were people who believed the ward was a wall God himself had built. Tell them it was a fence around a pasture and watch them call *you* the poison.

"No more running," I said. It came out before I decided to say it, which is usually how the true things arrive.

Sera turned to look at me fully then, and in the last grey light her face was older than it had been a month ago. Running had aged us both. We'd run from the mountain for six days — down the hidden stair, through the whispering passes, across the salt flats where the lesser gods sold favors out of carts — and we'd made it, actually made it, back to the edge of the only safe place either of us had ever known, and it wasn't safe, it had never been safe, it was a larder.

"The page," she said slowly. "A stolen page isn't a broken contract. You know that."

"I know that."

"A ledger can be copied. The original still stands. Come solstice, they collect her, the ward falls, and stealing the page buys us maybe an hour of confusion in the filing system."

"I know that, Sera."

"And the altar stone—"

"*I know.*"

The words came out harder than I meant them, and they hung between us in the cold. The altar stone. Of course. There had always been an altar stone — the old grey slab on the hill above the shrine quarter, worn smooth by centuries of knees, where the founders of Hollowmere had supposedly sworn the covenant that raised the ward. Every child in the valley got set on it once, for luck. I had a memory of sitting on it, age four or five, kicking my heels against the granite while my mother laughed, and the memory had a taste attached to it, the way all my strongest memories do: cold stone, wool blanket, and underneath it all, faintly, like a note in a wine you can't quite place —

Blood. Old blood, soaked so deep into the stone it had become part of its flavor.

The covenant hadn't been sworn with words. Covenants never are. The ward ran on a payment, and payments have a place of exchange, and tomorrow at solstice the collectors wouldn't bother coming down into the valley at all. They'd stand at the altar stone and wait for what was owed to be brought to them, the way you don't chase a debt — you let the debtor come to the bank.

"If I put the page back," I said, working it through aloud because saying things aloud was the only way I'd ever been able to think, "if I return it and offer myself — my soul, the bred one, the vintage they've wanted all along — would they take the trade? One for one? Would that close it?"

Sera was quiet long enough that I turned to look at her.

"You're asking me if hell honors its contracts," she said finally. "Yes. It does. That's the horror and the hope of it. If you offer freely, at the stone, on the eve — they'll take it. They'll take it so fast the wind will change direction." Her jaw tightened. "And I am asking you not to, because I have watched this court operate for thirty years from the inside, and I am telling you that a freely offered soul is not a trade to them. It's a *treat*. They will take yours, and Maren's, and then they will look at the rest of the valley the way a man looks at a cellar he's already broken into."

"Then what's the alternative? We've got no army. No god—" I caught myself, glanced at her. "No god who's speaking clearly."

"No," Sera agreed. "But the old gods owe the mountain court a grievance older than the valley. I told you that under the mountain. I felt it in the roots — the court is afraid of something, Dominic. Courts that are afraid build thick walls and keep meticulous ledgers." She looked out over the lamplit valley, and her voice dropped into something I'd only heard from her once before, in the waystation, half-asleep: reverence with the rust scraped off it. "A freely offered mortal soul is leverage even the old gods can't ignore. If it's offered — truly offered, not tricked, not coerced — it becomes a claim. And claims can be heard."

"Heard by whom?"

"By whoever's listening." She smiled, and it wasn't a comforting smile. "Pray someone is."

* * *

We went down through the shrine quarter because the wards there were thinnest — Sera's word, though I could feel it too now, the way my tongue felt poison. A month ago I'd crossed the ward line and vomited until I saw colors that don't exist. Now I walked through the failing edge of it and felt only a pressure, like wading upstream, like the valley itself was trying to turn me around and send me back to my table and my little cups and my professional numbness.

The streets were emptying toward the solstice fires. Good. Fewer witnesses to the man in the travel-stained coat walking like he owned the grief of the whole town.

Maren's house still stood on Tallow Lane, and her light was lit, and I stood outside it for a full minute like a coward before I knocked.

She opened the door and looked at me, and I watched her do the arithmetic of my face — the weight I'd lost, the scar on my jaw from the salt flats, the eyes she'd known for twenty-nine years suddenly wearing a month of mountains.

"You went," she said. Not *you're alive*, not *you came back*. She'd known I'd go. She'd spent nineteen years knowing I'd go, the way you know a debt exists even when the collector isn't at the door.

"I went. I came back. Maren, we need to talk about the stone."

Her face did something complicated and then settled on fury, which was easier for both of us than the thing underneath it.

"You stole a page out of hell and you want to talk about the *stone*—"

"Maren." I stepped inside and closed the door. Her house smelled of rosemary and fever-sweat; she'd been sick again, the sickness that had started the week the gods woke, the court tightening its grip on collateral. "Sit down. Both of us. Because I'm going to say this once, calmly, and then neither of us gets to pretend anymore."

She sat. I told her. All of it — the ledger, the clause, *the bloodline entire*, the collectors massed on the ridge like a jury that had already voted. I told her about the altar stone and what I intended to do there tomorrow night, and I watched my sister — who had sold herself at seventeen to buy a dying child another lifetime — listen to her brother explain that he intended to sell himself right back.

When I finished, she was quiet for a long time. Then:

"No."

"Maren—"

"No. Absolutely not. Do you know what I *did*, Dominic? I made a bad trade to save one person. One. And it has eaten nineteen years of my life and it is going to eat the whole valley when it comes due, and you want to walk up that hill and make the same trade *again*, bigger, and expect a different answer?" She was on her feet now, shaking. "That's not courage. That's just our family's one idea, performed twice."

"It's not the same trade."

"Because you're adding *drama*?"

"Because I'm not offering alone." I took the page from my coat and laid it on her table, and her eyes went to it like a bird to a snake. "Sera says a freely offered soul is a claim. A claim gets heard. I don't know by what. But I know this court, now — I've seen its ledgers, I've smelled its cellars — and I know it fears one thing above all others: an account it can't settle cleanly. So tomorrow night I'm going to stand on that stone and offer them the cleanest account they've ever been handed, and I'm going to make them take it in front of whatever old gods are listening. And if those gods want me afterward—" I shrugged, and it surprised me how light the shrug felt, "—then they can queue up with everyone else."

Maren stared at me. Then she laughed — a wet, broken sound with no humor in it, the laugh of someone watching a second cart drive toward the same cliff.

"You taste poison for a living," she said. "You have spent your whole life putting terrible things in your mouth and staying calm so other people could eat. You think that prepared you for this?"

"It's the only thing that prepared me for this."

* * *

Solstice eve fell proper an hour later, and the valley lit its fires, and I walked out to see the ward fail.

Not fail entirely — not yet. But as the true dark came down, I stood on the altar hill, alone, Sera keeping watch below among the shrine ruins, and I put my hand flat on the grey stone and *listened* the way I listen to a suspect broth.

The ward hummed under my palm like a struck bell fading. Thinner than it had been that morning. Thinner than it had been an hour ago. Every hour of solstice eve, it rang a little quieter, and out along the rim, the collectors had begun to move — not closer, not yet, but *inward*, adjusting their positions the way wolves adjust when they can hear the heartbeat of the thing in the pen.

Eleven of them at dusk. Fourteen now.

And beneath the stone, beneath the hum, I tasted what the founders had soaked into it all those centuries ago, and it was exactly what I'd remembered from childhood, concentrated: old blood, old fear, and a signature. My family's signature. E. Voss, and before her, generations of Voss hands on this rock, renewing the payment, feeding the pen, each of them told they were building a wall.

My mother had knelt here. Had pressed her palms to this granite and paid for my life with her daughter's, and gone home and made dinner, and never told me, and died carrying it.

I knelt where she had knelt. The stone was cold through my trousers. Below, the first solstice fire caught with a whump of pitch, and the valley cheered, small and human and doomed, and fourteen collectors stood at the rim of the world with their hats in their hands like undertakers at a wedding.

"Grim clarity," I said aloud, to the stone, to my mother, to whoever was listening. "No more running. Tomorrow night, one way or another, this account settles."

The stone said nothing. Stones are honest that way.

But as I stood and turned back toward the fires — toward Maren's light on Tallow Lane, toward Sera's silhouette among the ruins, toward the last supper I would ever cook in the valley that bred me — the air above the altar moved.

Not wind. The ward kept out wind; there was never wind inside.

Something breathed across the top of the hill, and every collector on the rim turned its head at once, in unison, like iron filings answering a magnet, and looked — not at me, not at the valley, but at the empty dark above the altar stone.

Something had heard the account.

And it was coming to audit.
