## 19. The Altar Stone

There is a particular skill to walking toward your own death, and I am pleased to report that after twenty years of professional hazard-tasting, I have it.

It is mostly in the shoulders. You keep them loose. You breathe through the nose, shallow and even, the way you do when the broth in front of you smells of bitter almond and everyone at the table is watching your face for the twitch that means *poison* and the other twitch that means *fear*. A taster who flinches dies twice — once in the body and once in reputation — and only one of those is survivable. So: loose shoulders, level chin, and a face arranged into the pleasant blankness of a man who has already decided how he feels about things.

I had been practicing the walk since sundown.

Behind me, Hollowmere burned its last candles against the longest night. The ward was down to embers — I could feel it now, the way you feel a fever breaking, the valley's long-held breath finally going ragged. Beyond the ridge, on the slopes where no honest shepherd grazed his flock, the collectors were gathering. I'd seen them from Maren's window: shapes like tall men made of ledger-paper, standing very still among the pines, patient as interest accruing. Dozens of them. Maybe hundreds. They did not advance because they did not need to. The ward would fail by midnight, and then they would simply walk downhill and begin.

Unless someone paid.

"Dom." Maren caught my sleeve at the door. Her fever had broken two days ago, but her hands still shook, and she gripped my wrist with the strength of a woman holding onto a boat in rough water. "Tell me again what you're doing."

"I'm going out."

"Don't." She said it flatly, without pleading, which was worse. My sister had never pleaded in her life; she'd bargained, once, nineteen years ago, and look what that had bought. "Don't you dare do the noble thing, Dominic Voss. I've seen what the noble thing costs in this family."

"You mean you've *paid* what the noble thing costs," I said. "Different line item entirely."

Her grip tightened. In the candlelight she looked like our mother, which was unfair of her, since our mother had died believing both her children were safe. "There has to be another way. Sera said—"

"Sera says a lot of things. Most of them involve salt." I covered her hand with mine. Hers was cold; mine, I noticed distantly, was not. That seemed significant somehow, though I couldn't have told you why. "Maren. Listen to me. Nineteen years ago you sat across from something with too many teeth and you traded yourself for me, and you did it so quietly that I got to grow up thinking I was just lucky. You gave me every morning I've ever had. Every meal. Every cup of poison I didn't die of." I smiled, because if I didn't smile I was going to do something undignified, and a man walking to his death should at least be dignified about it. "You don't get to be the only one in this family who knows how to make a bad bargain well."

"That's not—"

"It's hereditary," I said. "Apparently."

She hit me. Open-palmed, on the chest, once, hard enough to sting through my coat. Then she pulled me in and held on the way she used to when we were small and the winter storms came off the pass, and I let her, and I counted her heartbeats against my ribs the way I used to count sips between courses, marking time, marking safety, knowing neither would last.

"Come back," she whispered.

"Maren." I said it gently. "You know better than anyone in this valley that I don't make promises I can't taste first."

Then I went out into the snow.

* * *

The altar stone stood in a hollow above the old shrine road, a grey slab older than the valley's name, older probably than the valley's ward, which — as a captured collector had cheerfully explained to me while Sera held him in a circle of iron filings — was less a wall than a fence around a very particular pasture. The stone was furred with frost and lichen and, tonight, with candle stubs left by three generations of farmers who had prayed here before the gods went quiet and the prayers started costing extra.

Sera was waiting for me there. Of course she was. I had told her not to come, and she had looked at me with the expression of a woman who had spent thirty years being ignored by heaven and had developed strong opinions about being ignored by lesser beings.

"You're terrible at sneaking away," she said. "You announced it to your sister. You kissed your sister goodbye. You took the good boots."

"They're the only boots."

"You took the *good* boots, Dominic. Everyone in Hollowmere knows what that means. We're not subtle people. We're a valley full of farmers who name their cows."

I looked past her at the stone. The collectors had not come down yet, but I could feel them the way you feel weather in an old wound — a pressure behind the eyes, a wrongness in the air, the sense of a vast attention turning slowly toward this one frozen hollow. "You shouldn't be here, Sera."

"No," she agreed. "Probably not. But I've spent my whole life being where I shouldn't be — it's the only place I've ever heard anything worth hearing." She dug in her satchel and pressed something into my hand: a twist of paper, heavy for its size. Salt. "For the road down, if there is one. And this—" a second object, small and cold, an iron nail bent double, "—for whatever comes before the road."

"You said iron doesn't work against them once they've accepted payment."

"I said a lot of things." Her jaw tightened. "Most of them were wrong. Some of them were lies I told myself. But this one thing I know, taster, and I know it in my bones where all the theology failed: a freely given thing cannot be taken. Whatever happens up on that mountain tonight, whatever they do to you — they will tell you it's a transaction, a contract, a fair exchange. It isn't. It's theft wearing a very nice coat. Remember that. Remember it when it gets loud."

"When what gets loud?"

She didn't answer. She just looked at me, and for a moment the bitterness fell away from her face and I saw what was underneath, which was the same thing that had been underneath since the waystation: a woman who had lost her god and found, in the wreckage, something smaller and harder and more durable. Faith's ugly little sister. Loyalty.

"If the old gods wake," she said quietly, "if any part of what I felt last month on the pass was real and not just my heart finally giving out — then they'll come. Tonight. For you or for the court, I don't know which. And if they come, Dominic, whatever they offer you —"

"I'll read the contract first," I said. "I always do."

That earned me half a laugh, bitten off at the edges. She stepped back. Snow began to fall, fat and slow, catching in her grey hair until she looked briefly, absurdly, like a saint in a village fresco — the kind I'd grown up ignoring.

"Loose shoulders," she said.

I hadn't told her about the shoulders. But then, she'd watched enough men walk into things.

* * *

Midnight came the way debt does: not suddenly, but all at once.

The ward broke with a sound I felt rather than heard — a deep, subterranean *pop*, like a cork leaving a bottle, like a held breath released after nineteen years. The pressure behind my eyes vanished. The valley exhaled. And in the same instant the collectors moved, all together, without a signal I could see, pouring down the slopes like ink finding the grooves of a page.

They stopped at the tree line.

Because I was standing on the altar stone, and I had lit every candle stub, and I had done the thing that Sera had taught me and that Ilvane, in her strange, tired mercy, had confirmed: I had spoken aloud, in the old formula, the words that opened negotiation with the court beneath the mountain.

*"I bring an asset to market."*

The silence afterward lasted exactly as long as it takes a predator to decide whether the thing in front of it is prey or merchandise.

Then the snow stopped falling in a perfect circle around the hollow, hanging suspended mid-air like a held note, and the cold changed quality — from winter's cold to something older, drier, the cold of deep cellars and sealed vaults. The collectors at the tree line knelt, all of them, in unison, and the space between the trees filled with a presence that did not arrive so much as *become relevant*.

It spoke without sound. The words assembled themselves directly behind my eyes, in a voice like a clerk reading terms: courteous, unhurried, faintly amused.

**THE TASTER.** A pause, savored. **WE HAD HOPED YOU WOULD COME TO US EVENTUALLY. WE DID NOT DARE HOPE YOU WOULD COME ALONE, UNARMED, AND STANDING ON THE OLD STONE. YOU CONTINUE TO EXCEED YOUR BREEDING.**

"Flattery," I said. "From a debt-collection agency. I'll admit that's new."

**IT IS NOT FLATTERY. IT IS APPRAISAL.** Something vast shifted in the dark between the trees, the way a man shifts in his chair when the item under discussion pleases him. **YOU KNOW WHAT YOU ARE. THE PRIESTESS TOLD YOU. THE COLLECTOR TOLD YOU. NINETEEN GENERATIONS OF SELECTED BLOOD, RAISED BEHIND A WARD, FED ON NOTHING BUT ORDINARY FOOD AND ORDINARY AIR, TOUCHED BY NO MAGIC, MARKED BY NO GOD. DO YOU UNDERSTAND HOW RARE THAT IS? EVERY SOUL IN THIS VALLEY HAS PRAYED AT LEAST ONCE. PRAYER IS A PIN-PRICK. PRAYER LETS THE FLAVOR OUT. BUT YOU —** the voice warmed, if a ledger can be said to warm, **— YOU HAVE NEVER PRAYED. NOT ONCE. NOT EVEN AS A DYING CHILD. YOUR SISTER PRAYED FOR YOU. SHE PAID FOR YOU. AND YOU SIMPLY... DRANK WHAT WAS POURED, AND LIVED, AND NEVER ONCE LOOKED UP.**

"That's not true," I said. "I prayed once. When I was seven. I prayed that the goose at the autumn feast wouldn't be overcooked."

A pause. The amusement sharpened into something almost delighted.

**A PRAYER ABOUT GOOSE.**

"It was a very important goose."

**YES,** the court said, and the word rolled through the hollow like a stone settling into a socket. **YES, YOU WILL DO PERFECTLY.**

The candles guttered. The suspended snow trembled. And I understood, with the clarity of a man who has just tasted something and identified it in the first half-second, that everything else tonight — the solstice deadline, the stolen page, the flight down the mountain, all of it — had been, from the court's perspective, merely seasoning. The dish was always going to be me. All that remained was the presentation.

So I presented.

"Maren Voss," I said, and my voice held, which surprised me. "Page eleven thousand and forty-one of your ledger. Collateral against a contract of immunity, dated nineteen years ago this season. I'm told her debt comes due regardless of the solstice. I'm told there's no version of events in which she keeps what's hers." I reached into my coat and drew out the stolen page — Ilvane's mercy, folded twice, warm from my body — and laid it on the altar stone, weighting its corners with four candle stubs. "I'm offering a trade. One soul, freely offered, unprayed, unborrowed, untouched by god or magic, nineteen generations in the making. The finest vintage your cellar has ever been offered." I looked up, into the dark where the voice lived. "In exchange, you burn her page. Not transfer. Not reassign. Burn. She walks out of this hollow owing nothing to anyone, above or below, forever. And your collectors leave Hollowmere tonight."

**THE VALLEY'S WARD IS ALREADY BROKEN.**

"The ward was a fence," I said. "I'm selling you the herd's prize bull. You don't need the fence anymore. And you'll find, if you take this bargain, that the rest of the herd tastes different now — prayed over, fought over, *noticed*. Spoiled, by your standards. This—" I put my hand flat on my own chest, over the heart, the way a merchant presents a cut of meat, "—is the last unspoiled soul your operation will ever produce. Take it tonight or lose it forever."

Silence. Long enough that I heard my own heartbeat, steady, professional, counting out the seconds like sips between courses.

Then, softly, with the satisfaction of a connoisseur confirming a label:

**DONE.**

* * *

Here is what nobody tells you about having your soul collected: it starts in the hands.

Not pain. Cold first — a clean, cellar-deep cold rising up through the fingers, and underneath the cold, a sensation I can only describe as *inventory*. Something was counting me. Not my body; my body stayed exactly where it was, kneeling on the frost-furred stone with the stolen page burning itself to ash beside me in a slow, deliberate flame that smelled of ink and old promises. No — the counting was happening somewhere underneath my skin, in the place where a person keeps the sum of themselves. Every memory lifted, weighed, and set back down with a small tick of approval. My mother's kitchen. The hemlock, aged nine. The goose, overcooked anyway. Maren's heartbeat against my ribs an hour ago. Each one examined, catalogued, filed.

And terror — yes. Let's be honest about that. Terror like I had never tasted, and I have tasted everything, terror with a finish that went on and on, a black current running under everything. My body wanted to run. My body suggested, quite forcefully, that we reconsider the entire arrangement, that we bolt for the tree line, that we claw at the frozen ground, that we do anything, *anything* —

But underneath the terror, quieter and stranger, was the peace.

Because I knew this feeling. God help me, I knew it intimately. It was the feeling of the moment after you swallow. The sip is taken; the tongue has spoken; the verdict is rendered and cannot be recalled. There is a horrible freedom in it. For twenty years I had lived in the half-second between tasting and knowing, and I had built a whole self out of that suspense — careful, watchful, numb, a man who survived by never fully committing to anything, even his own life. Now the swallow was done. The verdict was in. Whatever came next, I had chosen it, freely, with open eyes and loose shoulders, and no power in heaven or beneath the mountain could make it not-mine.

Maren would wake tomorrow owing nothing. That was the whole of it. That was the trade, and it was good, and I would have made it hungrier.

The cold reached my elbows. The inventorying slowed, grew luxurious, thorough — the court was savoring, and I could feel its satisfaction the way a chef feels a diner's pleasure through the empty plate. Somewhere far above, the suspended snow began to fall again, one flake at a time, unhurried.

**REMARKABLE,** the voice murmured, closer now, everywhere now. **NINETEEN GENERATIONS, AND NOT ONE FLAW. NOT ONE PRAYER. NOT ONE CRACK FOR ANYTHING ELSE TO GET IN.** A pause, and then, with the tone of a clerk noting an anomaly in an otherwise perfect column: **AND YET.**

And yet.

The cold stopped.

Not faded — *stopped*, the way a clock stops, mid-tick, mid-sip, mid-heartbeat. The inventory froze with my memories still in its hands. And in the sudden absolute silence of the hollow, I heard — or felt, or tasted; by then the senses had begun trading places — a second presence arrive.

It did not come from the trees. It came from *above*, from beyond the snow, from the direction that the mountain court had spent nineteen generations teaching this valley not to look. It came the way dawn comes: gradually, then completely, and with an authority that required no announcement.

The court beneath the mountain went very, very still.

And on the altar stone, kneeling in the falling snow with my soul halfway into a ledger, Dominic Voss — food-taster, poison-survivor, the most prayed-over man in Hollowmere precisely because he had never once prayed — felt something ancient turn its attention upon the transaction in progress and say, in a voice like the first day of spring cracking a river open:

***We will discuss the price.***

The candles all went out at once.

And in the dark, the cold around my hands began — carefully, the way one handles another creditor's property — to let go.
