## 20. What the Old Gods Owe

I have spent thirty years learning how silence sounds from a god. I could tell you its texture — the particular grain of an unanswered prayer, the way it sits in the chest like a stone swallowed whole. For three decades I knelt on cold flagstones and offered up my voice and my hope and my congregation's griefs, and what came back was nothing, and I learned to call that nothing *faith*.

So believe me when I say I know exactly what it means when a god finally answers.

It means you should have asked more carefully.

* * *

The altar stone stood at the valley's edge like a rotten tooth, and around it the night had gone wrong in every direction at once. The ward — the great invisible pen that had held Hollowmere safe and captive for two hundred years — was tearing itself apart above us, and through the rents came the collectors: dozens of them, then scores, pouring down the mountainside like ink spilled down a page. Ilvane stood apart from them at the stone's edge, hands folded, watching Dominic with something I had no better word for than *pity*, though pity from a soul-collector is a coin of dubious weight.

And Dominic — Dominic was kneeling on the altar stone with his wrists open and his chin lifted, offering himself the way he'd once offered a spoonful of suspect broth to his own tongue. Calm. Professional. Terrified out of his mind and doing it anyway.

I had known him for half a season. I had watched him vomit his way across a border he'd never crossed, barter his palate to spirits in a lawless market, climb a mountain that whispered his heart's desires at him until his ears bled. I had confessed my worst sin to him somewhere in the mountain's roots — that I had helped write the very contracts that bound his sister — and he had chosen, imperfectly, angrily, to forgive me.

So when the mountain court began to take him, I did what any reasonable woman would do.

I prayed.

Not the way I used to pray. Not the kneeling, patient, letter-to-an-empty-house prayer of my priesthood. I prayed the way you scream into a well when your child has fallen in — wordless, furious, bargaining with anything that might be listening.

*You woke up last month,* I said into the dark behind the world. *You woke up angry, and you've been collecting debts ever since, and I don't care. I don't care if you're harvest or plague or judgment. He is offering freely what they have been trying to steal for two hundred years, and if there is one scrap of justice left in whatever you are, you will not let this stand.*

The wind stopped.

Not gentled. *Stopped.* The whole night held its breath, and every collector on the hillside froze mid-stride, and Ilvane's folded hands went very still, and I felt — for the first time in thirty years, felt, not hoped, not believed, *felt* — attention settle over the valley like a hand on the back of my neck.

Three voices spoke at once, and none of them used the air.

**"SHE PRAYS,"** said the first, and it sounded like frost forming.

**"THE FAILED ONE,"** said the second, and it sounded like my name being read off a list I didn't want to be on.

**"SHE PRAYS ANYWAY,"** said the third, and that one almost sounded amused, which frightened me more than the other two together.

I got to my feet because my legs decided kneeling was no longer dignified. "Yes," I said aloud, because apparently I was negotiating with gods now, and my voice only shook a little. "I pray anyway. That's rather the point of me."

* * *

Here is what I understood in that moment, standing on a frozen hillside while the old gods considered us like merchants considering a stall: they were not good. I want that on the record, written somewhere, carved into something durable. Everyone in the villages we'd passed through had thought the waking gods meant blessing — rain for memories, healing for names, miracles at terrible exchange rates — and everyone had been wrong, and so had I, in a different way. I had assumed their anger was righteousness. It wasn't. It was grievance. Old, cold, bookkeeping grievance, the kind that accrues interest.

They owed the mountain court something. I would learn the shape of it later — a theft at the beginning of the world, a ledger-line older than writing, a debt the court beneath the mountain had been dodging since before mortals learned to count. The court had built its cathedral of ledgers on the assumption that no one above ground would ever have leverage.

And now here was a mortal man, bred in a pen for two centuries, offering his unspoiled soul *freely* — not sold, not tricked, not contracted, but given, with open eyes and open hands — and a freely offered soul, in the old arithmetic, the arithmetic that predated the court's entire fraudulent enterprise, was worth more than any stolen thing.

Leverage. We were leverage. Dominic had walked up an altar stone and made himself the fulcrum of an argument between powers older than language, and he didn't even know it yet, because he was currently being unmade.

**"THE COURT CLAIMS HIM BY CONTRACT,"** said the frost voice.

**"THE COURT HOLDS NOTHING FREELY GIVEN,"** said the amused one. **"IT HAS NEVER ONCE HELD SUCH A THING. IT WOULD NOT KNOW THE WEIGHT."**

**"VOID IT,"** said the third voice, and the other two were silent, and I understood that the third voice was the one that mattered, the way in any argument there is always one voice that matters.

* * *

What happened next happened everywhere at once, which is difficult to describe and worse to experience.

The collectors came apart. Not died — I don't know if they can die — but unraveled, each one folding inward like a letter being returned to sender, and where they had been there was suddenly hillside, ordinary and dark and wet with frost. Ilvane alone remained standing, and she looked down at her own hands with an expression I will carry to my grave: not relief, exactly. Something adjacent to relief. The look of a woman who has carried a debt so long she's forgotten what her shoulders felt like before it.

Above the altar stone, the air opened.

I don't mean a door. I mean the sky developed a depth it shouldn't have had, layers behind layers behind layers, and in those layers were shapes that my eyes kept trying to file as *trees* or *mountains* or *faces* and failing, because they were all of those things and none, and they were looking down at the valley of Hollowmere with the unhurried attention of something that had waited a very long time for this appointment.

Dominic screamed.

I ran to him. I don't remember deciding to; my body simply went, salt lines forgotten, disbelief abandoned, every crude ward I'd taught him on the road rendered suddenly quaint. He was still kneeling on the stone, but the taking had stopped halfway — the court's claim broken off mid-pull like a cork drawn partway from a bottle — and what filled the gap was not the court. It was the other thing. The three-voiced thing. Pouring into him along the channel his own sacrifice had opened.

"Is it killing him?" I shouted at the sky, at the layers, at whoever was listening. "You're supposed to be voiding a contract, not filling him like a wineskin!"

**"THE VESSEL OFFERED,"** said the frost voice, from everywhere, including, horribly, from just beside my ear.

"He offered his *soul*. To the *court*. As payment. That was the transaction. You don't get to amend the terms after—"

**"PRIESTESS."** The amused voice again, close now, and I felt it the way you feel a change in pressure before a storm. **"YOU OF ALL MORTALS KNOW THAT EVERY CONTRACT HAS FINE PRINT. YOU HELPED WRITE ENOUGH OF THEM."**

The words went through me like a blade finding an old scar. Yes. Yes, I had. Thirty years ago, young and certain and starving for a god who never answered, I had served as mouthpiece to the mountain court, transcribing contract templates in my own careful hand, and one of those templates — I knew it, I had *known* it since the day Dominic told me about Maren — had been the one used on his sister. Clause by clause. My handwriting, in essence, on the shape of his family's ruin.

"You're using my sins as precedent?" My voice cracked on it. "That's — that's obscene."

**"IT IS LAW,"** said all three voices together, and the hillside shuddered. **"A SOUL FREELY OFFERED IN PAYMENT CREATES A BINDING UPON ALL PARTIES. THE COURT IS BOUND TO RELEASE WHAT IT HELD AGAINST THE SISTER. AND THE OFFEROR—"**

The pause stretched. Below me, Dominic had stopped screaming. He lay on the altar stone breathing in long, slow draughts, and when he opened his eyes they were wrong — not black, not glowing, nothing so theatrical, but *deep*, the way a well is deep, the way the space between stars is deep. He looked at me and I saw my oldest friend and total stranger wearing the same face.

**"—THE OFFEROR IS BOUND TO US,"** the gods finished. **"AS VESSEL. AS VOICE. AS ANSWERABLE SERVANT. THIS IS THE OLD TERMS. THIS IS WHAT WE ARE OWED."**

"No," I said.

**"YOU OBJECT, PRIESTESS?"**

"I object to *everything about this*!" I was shouting at the sky on a frozen hillside at midnight, and I found I did not care. "He traded himself to save his sister and you're — you're *foreclosing* on him! You're doing exactly what the court does! You dress it in older language, you cite deeper law, but it's the same bargain, the same harvest, the same — " My voice broke entirely. "I spent thirty years serving gods who said nothing. Do you know why I stayed? Because silence couldn't hurt anyone. And now you're awake, and the first thing you do with your waking is *this*."

Silence. Real silence, the kind I knew intimately, and for one black moment I thought I had finally, truly, permanently driven off divinity with my own two hands — a fitting capstone to my career.

Then the amused voice, quieter:

**"THAT IS WHY WE CHOSE HIM."**

I stared.

**"A MAN WHO TASTES POISON FOR A LIVING. WHO SURVIVES WHAT WOULD KILL OTHERS. WHO WALKED INTO A CATHEDRAL OF LEDGERS AND STOLE A PAGE."** The layers behind the sky shifted, settled. **"WE DO NOT WANT AN OBEDIENT SERVANT, PRIESTESS. WE HAVE HAD TEN THOUSAND YEARS OF OBEDIENT SERVANTS. THEY BUILT THE COURT. THEY BUILT THE PEN. WE WANT ONE WHO WILL LOOK AT US AS YOU JUST DID — AND TELL US NO."**

"That's not — you can't appoint someone to defy you, that's not how defiance works—"

**"NO,"** agreed the frost voice, and I swear it sounded satisfied. **"BUT HE WILL MANAGE."**

* * *

The layers closed. The sky became merely sky, enormous and cold and scattered with indifferent stars, and the hillside was only a hillside, and the silence that followed was mortal silence, ordinary silence, the kind I had once mistaken for the absence of God.

Dominic sat up.

He did it slowly, the way a man sits up after a fever breaks, testing each movement against some new interior landscape. His eyes were his own again — mostly. There was a depth in them now that hadn't been there before, a stillness at the back, like water over a well. He looked at his hands. He turned them over. Then, being Dominic, being the most infuriatingly practical man I have ever known, he said:

"I can taste the air."

"What?"

"The air." He breathed in, deliberate, and his expression did something complicated. "It's full of — everything. Ward-residue. Frost. Old blood soaked into that stone. And underneath it, something sweet. Prayer. There's prayer all through this valley, Sera, there has been for years, and nobody was listening." He looked up at me, and his mouth twisted wryly, and he was himself, thank every god waking or sleeping, he was still himself. "Apparently now somebody is. Apparently the somebody is me."

"Dominic—"

"They gave me the terms while I was — wherever I was." He climbed off the altar stone, unsteady, and I took his arm without asking and he let me, which was its own small miracle. "Every prayer spoken in my hearing, I'm obliged to carry. Every debt the high courts and the low courts ignore, I'm expected to — settle. Answerable servant. Their voice in the world." He exhaled. "They dressed it up as an honor. It's a leash, Sera. A longer one than the ward ever was, but a leash."

"I know." My throat was tight. "I tried to stop them."

"I heard." Something flickered across his face — gratitude, unarmored, the rarest thing he owned. "Nobody's told the old gods no in a long time, I think. They liked it. Which is its own kind of disturbing."

"Maren," I said, because I couldn't hold it anymore, because the whole night had been for this. "The contract — they voided it, they said—"

He turned toward the valley.

Hollowmere lay below us in the dark, and I watched him read it the way he'd once read a bowl of broth — tilt, scent, the smallest sip. His face went through fear and then through wonder and landed somewhere I had no name for.

"The ward's gone," he said quietly. "All of it. Two hundred years of pen-wall, dissolved. And Maren—" His breath caught. "She's awake. She's *awake*, Sera, her soul came home twenty minutes ago and she's sitting up in her bed asking what happened, and she's free, she's actually free—"

He laughed. It was not entirely a laugh; there was grief in it, and exhaustion, and the specific hysteria of a man who has paid nearly everything and lived. He pressed the heel of his hand against his eyes.

"I'm half-divine," he said to his palms. "I'm a *vessel*. I have obligations to entities older than language. And my sister is making breakfast questions at the neighbors at midnight." He lowered his hand and looked at me, and the depth behind his eyes caught the starlight. "Come on. Someone has to explain to her why her brother tastes like a thunderstorm."

We started down toward the valley, toward the homecoming neither of us had dared plan for, and behind us the altar stone stood silent under the stars — but not empty. I felt it the way I had once felt silence from God: a presence, patient, attentive, waiting to see what its new servant would do next.

And for the first time in thirty years, I found I was curious too.

Then the first light crested the eastern ridge, and every bell in Hollowmere began to ring at once — and not in celebration.
