## 21. Aftertaste

The first thing I tasted when I woke on the altar stone was snow.

Not the idea of snow — I'd known snow my whole life, the way a man knows his own hands. This was different. This was every flake at once, each one carrying its own small signature: mineral dust from the pass, woodsmoke from Hollowmere's chimneys three miles downvalley, the faint iron tang of the ward's last breath dissolving into the air like sugar into warm milk. I lay on my back in the dark with Maren's hand in mine and catalogued the sky the way I used to catalogue a suspect broth.

Hemlock. Somewhere out there, past the valley's edge, hemlock was growing, and I could taste it from here.

I laughed. It came out cracked and strange, and Maren stirred beside me.

"Dominic?" Her voice was hoarse, scraped thin by nineteen years of being somewhere else. "Why is your hand glowing?"

I looked. My right hand — the one that had pressed the stolen ledger page against the altar stone, the one the old gods had held while they unmade the mountain court's claim and remade me as something between a man and an argument — was suffused with a pale gold light, like a lamp seen through skin. It didn't hurt. That was somehow worse.

"Long story," I said. "It starts with you selling your soul, which we are absolutely going to discuss, and ends with me becoming a minor theological incident."

"You always did bury the important part of a story under the jokes." She sat up slowly, wincing, and looked around at the altar stone, the scattered salt, the scorch marks where Sera had burned her last thread of faith to hold the gate open against everything the mountain court had thrown at us. "Where's the priestess?"

"Alive. Barely. She's asleep by the fire Ilvane built before it left."

"Before *it* left?"

"Collector," I said. "Formerly human. Waited four hundred years for someone to refuse a bargain cleanly, then decided we were worth a fire and a head start." I paused. "Also she called me 'little vintage,' which I've decided not to think about until spring."

Maren stared at me. Nineteen years of debt had been lifted off her soul maybe six hours ago, and already she was doing the thing she used to do when we were children — the look that said *you're lying by omission, brother, and I will wait you out*. She'd waited me out for nineteen years without knowing it. She could certainly manage till dawn.

"The solstice," she said instead. "Did we—"

"We made it. With about forty minutes to spare, which if you ask Sera is the only way anything worth doing gets done."

"And the contract?"

"Voided. Paid, actually. The court accepted my soul in exchange for yours, and then the old gods showed up like creditors at a funeral and voided *that* too, because apparently a freely offered mortal soul is leverage in a grievance older than the mountain." I flexed the glowing hand. "They didn't give it back, though. They kept it. Or kept *me*, rather. I'm told I'm a vessel now. Answerable servant. Their words."

"Vessel of what?"

"That's the part nobody's explained yet. I have a feeling it's going to be explained *at* me rather than *to* me."

Maren was quiet for a long moment. Then she did something I hadn't seen her do since we were children: she leaned over and put her forehead against my shoulder, and cried, silently, the way people cry who learned young that loud grief attracts attention.

I held her and tasted her tears — salt, yes, but underneath it something else, something bright and unclaimed, the flavor of a soul back in its own body after nineteen years in a filing cabinet beneath a mountain. If I lived to be a thousand I didn't think I'd taste anything better.

If I lived to be a thousand. There was a thought I wasn't ready to examine. Vessels might not get to die on schedule. Nobody had mentioned that either way, and I suspected the omission was deliberate.

* * *

We walked down into Hollowmere at first light, and the valley broke my heart in a brand-new way.

For twenty-nine years the ward had been the shape of my whole world. I'd tested its edge exactly once, back when the pilgrim woman came through claiming the gods answered prayers beyond the pass, and felt it flicker under my fingers like a candle flame deciding whether to stay lit. Everything inside it had been safe, small, certain. Poison couldn't touch me. Magic couldn't reach me. The crown fed me lies with the soup and I swallowed them professionally, one spoonful at a time, and called it a career.

Now the ward was gone — shattered when the old gods claimed me, unraveled like a dropped stitch running the length of a sweater — and Hollowmere lay open to the sky like a wound.

You wouldn't see it, walking in. The houses were the same. The mill wheel still turned. Smoke still rose from the bakery where I'd bought honey rolls every morning of my adult life. But I could *taste* the openness, the way you taste a room after a window's been opened in winter. The valley's air had always had a particular flatness to it, a stillness, the flavor of water kept too long in a covered jug. Now it moved. Things came in on the wind from beyond the pass — pollen, prayer, the distant green smell of wild magic doing whatever wild magic does when nobody's penning it up — and settled over the rooftops like a new kind of weather.

And the people. The people were standing in their doorways looking up.

"They can feel it," Maren said. "Can't they. Even without — whatever you are now. They can feel the ceiling's gone."

Most of them looked frightened. Some looked furious — I watched old Aldous the cooper shake his fist at the empty sky and shout something about the crown's guarantee, the crown's protection, as if safety were a product he'd paid for and someone had shorted him. A few — children, mostly, and one or two of the very old — looked up with expressions I recognized from the pilgrim woman's face all those months ago. Hunger. Hope. The look of people who have just been told the world is bigger than the room they were born in, and don't yet know whether that's a promise or a threat.

Sera walked beside me with her hood up and her hands in her sleeves, moving like a woman made of bruise. Burning her last connection to her god should have killed her; the fact that it hadn't was, she informed me, "either a miracle or an insult, and I haven't decided which." She surveyed the valley with thirty years of a priestess's eye and pronounced judgment quietly.

"They'll split. Within a season. Some will build shrines. Some will build walls. And some will do both and pretend that's coherent." She glanced at me. "The crown will send magistrates within the month. They'll want to know how the ward fell and who's responsible."

"I'm responsible."

"Yes. And you're also the only person in this valley the old gods answer to, which makes you considerably more interesting than guilty." Her mouth did the thing that was almost a smile. "Congratulations. You've been promoted from livestock to diplomat."

"I never asked for—"

"No," she said. "That's rather the point of you."

* * *

The magistrates arrived in eleven days, which even by the crown's standards was fast, and they arrived with writs, seals, and the particular smell of men who have been told a problem exists and intend to locate it nowhere near themselves.

I met them in the great hall, because I was still technically the royal food-taster and because Maren had threatened to come in my place otherwise, and I judged that a woman with nineteen years of stolen time to make up for would be less diplomatic than me, which was saying something.

The senior magistrate was a soft-spoken man named Corvel with ink-stained fingers and eyes like a ledger's margins. He laid out the situation with the gentle clarity of a physician describing a tumor: the ward was a crown asset. Its destruction was a matter of state. The souls collected beneath the mountain were collected under contracts the crown had historically chosen not to examine, and the crown would prefer to continue choosing not to examine them, and would I care to assist in restoring the previous arrangement?

"You want me to rebuild the pen," I said.

"I want stability," Corvel said. "The valley has been safe for three hundred years. Your family in particular has benefited from that safety."

"My family was *collateral* in that safety." I kept my voice level. Twenty years of tasting poison had given me excellent control of my face, and I spent all of it now. "My sister's soul was the deposit on my immunity. The ward wasn't a wall, magistrate. It was a fence around a herd. You bred us — did you know that? Did the crown know? Bred my bloodline immune so the mountain court could harvest us later, unspoiled. I found it in the ledgers. Page after page. Voss, Voss, Voss. We weren't protected. We were *cellared*."

Corvel's expression didn't change, which told me he'd known, or half-known, or had suspected and arranged never to confirm. "These are serious accusations."

"They're written accusations. In the court's own hand. Would you like to read them? I brought copies. I also sent copies to three neighboring kingdoms, two archbishoprics, and a trade-town newspaper that pays by the scandal." I smiled at him, and I knew exactly what the smile tasted like from the inside: copper and cold. "Stability, magistrate. Let's talk about stability. The old gods are awake. They are answering prayers across four valleys at exchange rates that would embarrass a loan shark. The mountain court is wounded, angry, and no longer holds a single contract in this region — because I burned the template, and Sera Vane, who wrote half those templates, is currently teaching farmers to make wards out of salt and stubbornness. The world outside your ledgers changed while you were pretending it hadn't. You can't put the ward back. What you *can* do is negotiate — with them, through me — before they decide mortals aren't worth negotiating with."

Silence. Somewhere in the hall a shutter banged in wind that, three months ago, would never have reached us.

"And what do you want, Master Voss?" Corvel asked finally. "In exchange for your... intermediacy."

I had thought about this for eleven days, so the answer came easy.

"Every contract the crown holds on a mortal soul in this valley, publicly burned. An accounting of every payment Hollowmere ever made to the court beneath the mountain, posted in the square. And bread." He blinked. "Bread?"

"The crown poisoned my profession into existence, magistrate. Somebody in this valley sold their daughter's soul because medicine cost more than magic, and somebody else let a child die rather than admit the ward was a leash. I want grain prices fixed. I want a healer in every village who doesn't charge by the prayer. I want the desperation that feeds these courts starved out at the root." I leaned forward. "You wanted stability? Desperate people sell things. Fed people don't. Do the arithmetic."

Corvel did the arithmetic. I watched him do it behind his eyes, and I watched him arrive at the same place I had: that it was cheaper to feed a valley than to fight gods.

He agreed to take terms to the crown. Whether the crown would accept was above both our pay grades, but he shook my hand on leaving, and his hand smelled of ink and fear in equal measure, and I thought: *good.* Fear of the right things, in the right proportions, is the beginning of wisdom. I'd survived on it my whole life.

* * *

Winter loosened its grip slowly that year, the way a fist opens finger by finger.

Maren rebuilt faster than the valley did. She took over the widow Anselm's empty house, planted a garden absurdly early, argued with the baker about starter cultures, and generally behaved like a woman making up for lost decades at a pace that frightened me sometimes. At night she'd stop mid-sentence and go quiet, touching her own chest, checking that she was still there. I pretended not to notice. She pretended not to notice me noticing. Between us we maintained a fiction of recovery that was, in its way, its own kind of warding.

Sera stayed. She claimed it was because the roads were bad and her knees were worse, but she started holding services on the altar stone — small ones, honest ones, no promises of miracles, just the old words spoken aloud to gods who, for the first time in her thirty silent years, occasionally answered. People came. First out of curiosity, then out of need, then — this was the part that made her cry when she thought I couldn't taste it, and I could always taste it — out of faith.

And me. I learned what I was now, mostly by accident.

I learned that prayer had a taste — different flavors for different needs, fear sharp as vinegar, gratitude round and warm as brown butter — and that when the old gods answered one nearby, I felt it in my teeth like a struck bell. I learned that poison no longer touched me at all, not because I was immune but because I was *upstream* of it now; venom passed through me the way a rumor passes through a man who already knows the truth. I learned that the gods spoke to me rarely, briefly, and always at inconvenient hours, usually in the voice of whatever I'd last eaten, which meant that in early spring the divine will of the cosmos addressed me in the tone of a pickled radish, and I chose not to share this detail with anyone, especially not Sera, who would have made it a doctrine.

I learned, hardest of all, to miss being safe.

Not often. Not enough to regret — I'd do it all again, every step, every bargain, every mile of that terrible stair. But some mornings I'd wake in my old rooms above the kitchen and lie there tasting the whole open world pouring through the windowless walls — every herb, every metal, every emotion drifting up from the village like steam off soup — and I'd grieve a little for the man I'd been, who had known so little and suffered so much less. Safety had been a small room. But it had been *quiet* in there.

On one such morning, near thaw, Maren found me on the doorstep with a cup of tea I'd been tasting for ten minutes without drinking.

"You're doing the thing," she said.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you taste the air like it owes you money." She sat down beside me, close, shoulder to shoulder, the way we'd sat as children. Below us the village was waking: smoke, hammering, Sera's congregation straggling up toward the stone with their breath steaming. "Is it terrible? Being what you are?"

I considered the question honestly, the way I'd once considered a suspect broth — turning it, testing its edges, waiting for the bitterness to show itself.

"It's full," I said finally. "Everything's full. Every breath is a hundred flavors and half of them are grief and half of them are prayer and some of them are both at once, and I can't ever put the cup down again." I looked at my hand, which glowed faintly even in daylight now, gold under the skin like a coal banked for the night. "But you know what the strangest part is? Underneath all of it — the poison, the prayer, the gods using me as a mouthpiece — underneath everything, it tastes like *morning*. Like the first morning after a fever breaks. The whole world is convalescing, and I can taste it getting better."

Maren leaned her head against my shoulder. "And the parts that don't get better?"

"Those I'll handle," I said. "I'm told I'm answerable now. Might as well answer."

She laughed — really laughed, the first true one since she'd woken — and the sound tasted like honey and iron and home.

Which was when the radish-voiced god spoke up at the back of my skull, brief and cold as a knife wrapped in cloth:

*Vessel. There is a debt unpaid in the eastern passes. The mountain court's collectors are massing where the ward once stood — not ours. Another court. Older. And they are asking for you by name.*

I set down my tea, and stood, and went to find Sera.

Some tastes, I was learning, you don't get to spit out.
