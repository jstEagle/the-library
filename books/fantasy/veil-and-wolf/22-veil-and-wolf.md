## 22. Veil and Wolf

The trouble with being half-divine, I've decided, is the paperwork.

Not mine. The gods' paperwork. When you carry a piece of the old ones in your chest like a coal that never quite goes out, every prayer within a day's walk arrives at your door whether you want it or not. Most of them are small. *Let the well refill. Let my mother's cough break. Let him write back.* The gods used to answer these with rain or memory or a child's name, whatever the exchange rate demanded. They answer them through me now, and I answer them the way a food-taster answers everything: carefully, in small sips, waiting to see what kills me.

So far, nothing has. That's either growth or a very long setup for a punchline, and after twenty-two years in Hollowmere's kitchens I've learned not to trust either.

* * *

The borderlands don't appear on any map worth trusting. They're the seam where the valley's old ward used to sit — a scar of country running from the salt flats to the black pines, neither inside nor outside, claimed by no crown and no court. Before the solstice, nobody lived here because nothing could: the ward's edge made crops sulk and compasses lie. Now the ward is gone, shattered into weather, and people are moving in the way water moves into a dry bed — slowly at first, then all at once, then wondering how they ever managed without it.

I walk the seam with Sera Vane, who carries an iron bell she refuses to ring and a ledger of her own, though hers is written in confession rather than ink. We settle debts. That's the trade we've fallen into, she and I, and like most trades it found us before we chose it.

Here is what the borderlands are full of: bargains. Nineteen years of desperate prayers answered at terrible rates left a residue across three valleys — rain bought with memories, healing bought with names, luck bought with firstborn laughter. The mountain court kept its ledgers even after Ilvane walked out of it carrying our stolen page under her arm like a woman leaving a bad marriage. Contracts outlive courts. That was the first lesson of my new life, and I learned it the way I learn everything, by tasting something I shouldn't have.

A widow outside Ferrow had traded her husband's name for a frost-free orchard. She couldn't remember him anymore — not his face, not his voice, only that there had been a shape beside her for thirty years and now the bed was too wide. The contract was technically valid. Technically. Sera found the clause; I supplied the leverage; the old gods, consulted through the coal in my chest, agreed that a name given by a grieving woman to a court that no longer existed was a debt owed to a ghost, and ghosts can't collect.

The name came back to her on a Tuesday. She said it out loud in her kitchen and sat down hard on the floor, and when we left she was making two cups of tea out of habit and crying about it, which Sera told me later was the happiest thing she'd seen all year.

That's the job. That's most of it, anyway.

* * *

Maren writes every week. Her letters arrive smelling of woodsmoke and rosemary, because my sister has never once sent a letter without tucking something practical into the fold — dried herbs, a button, once an entire spoon, which raised questions I decided not to ask.

Hollowmere is rebuilding itself around its own absence. Without the ward, the valley breathes differently. Maren says the mornings taste of frost and far-off rain instead of nothing, and that the children born this spring cry louder, as if they've finally been given permission. The crown's magistrates — the same ones who laughed me out of their offices when I came asking about my sister's soul — have been notably quiet. Cowardice, Maren reports, is doing more for reform than courage ever did. Half the court fled to the coast. The other half discovered humility around the time the old gods started answering prayers directly, without intermediaries, and discovered how many intermediaries had been skimming.

My sister runs the taster's guild now. Our old profession, rebuilt from the bones. But the charter's changed: no more tasting alone, no more silence about what's in the cup. Every suspect dish gets named aloud in front of witnesses. Poison thrives on secrecy; she learned that watching me swallow hemlock for a decade and call it employment.

She signs her letters *Maren Voss, Guildmistress*, and every time I see it I grin like an idiot. She sold her soul to save a dying boy. The boy grew up, broke heaven's accounting department over his knee, and got her back. And somehow she's the one who turned out formidable.

* * *

Sera and I make camp wherever dusk catches us, which lately means a shrine.

She builds them small — a flat stone, a candle, a bowl of water if water's to be had. She doesn't pray to be heard. She prays the way she says she should have prayed for thirty years: as conversation, not transaction. Sometimes the gods listen. Sometimes they don't, and she shrugs, and that shrug is the healthiest theology I've ever witnessed.

They always listen to me. That's the part I'm still getting used to.

When I speak — even idly, even to complain about my boots — something in the dark leans closer. I feel it the way I used to feel poison in a broth: a texture, a pressure, an attention. The old gods woke angry last winter, and anger doesn't evaporate; it looks for hands. Mine were available. A mortal vessel the courts above and below must answer to — that was the price of my sister's freedom, and some nights, holding that coal in my chest, I think I got the better end of the bargain, and other nights I'm less sure.

What I've learned is that divinity, like cooking, is mostly restraint. The gods want to give. Giving is how they proved they existed again, after thirty years of silence nearly unmade them. Left alone, they'd answer every prayer in the borderlands by tomorrow noon, and by tomorrow night the exchange rates would start climbing again — a harvest dressed as generosity. So I stand between. I take each prayer in small sips. I decide what a wish costs, and mostly I decide it costs nothing, because someone has to teach the old ones that grace isn't a ledger.

They're learning. Slowly. Last month a farmer prayed for rain and got rain, free, no memories deducted, and the gods were so pleased with themselves about it that I felt them preening for two days straight. Immense, ancient, terrible beings, waiting for praise like hounds waiting for a scrap off the table.

I know how that feels. I waited nineteen years for Maren to tell me the truth, and when she finally did, I'd have settled for a scrap.

* * *

Ilvane finds us twice a season, always at the edge of camp, always announced by the smell of cold stone and old paper.

She's not a collector anymore. When she let us go beneath the mountain — page stolen, gate burning, the whole machinery waking behind us — she burned her own bridge with the same fire. Centuries of service, ended in one act of mercy she'd apparently been rehearsing since before my grandmother's grandmother was born. The court would have unmade her for it. Instead the old gods offered her a position, which tells you everything about how the new order differs from the old: mercy is now a hiring qualification.

She refused. Ilvane doesn't work for anyone anymore, she says. She audits.

"You're accumulating enemies," she told us last month, sitting on a log with the perfect posture of someone who used to terrify kings. "The coastal magistrates. Two lesser spirits you embarrassed at the Ferrow crossing. Whatever's left of the mountain court's clerks — and clerks are worse than collectors, collectors only take souls, clerks take *notes*."

"We're accumulating debts settled," Sera said. "It balances."

"Nothing balances." But Ilvane almost smiled when she said it, which from her is roughly equivalent to a sonnet. She left us a list of names — contracts still outstanding, souls still filed in dead ledgers — and took payment in tea. Actual tea. She drank it slowly, looking at the fire, and I understood that this was the first meal anyone had ever served her without expecting something back, and that she'd waited centuries for it too.

Everyone in this story was waiting for something. That's what the veil was for, I think. Not keeping magic out. Keeping everyone hungry.

* * *

Today we're walking the salt flats toward a village called Brindle, where a boy traded his shadow to a lesser spirit for his father's recovery from fever. The father recovered. The boy walks crooked now, lopsided, half-lit, and the village crossed themselves when he passed until he stopped passing at all — stopped going out, stopped eating much, started standing in doorways at noon trying to find himself on the ground.

Lesser spirits aren't covered by the old gods' jurisdiction. They're vermin, essentially — divine rats feeding on scraps the great courts dropped. But the coal in my chest burns brighter around them, and I've learned that a vessel of the old gods doesn't need jurisdiction. He needs presence, a witness, and a very good memory for ingredients.

We reach Brindle at dusk. The boy's mother meets us at the well, wringing her apron, and takes us to a house where the lamps burn low because low light hides what's missing.

The boy is twelve. His name is Tam. He sits against the wall like furniture and watches us with the flat patience of someone who has already been told no by everyone qualified to say yes.

"I'll need the spirit's true name," I tell Sera quietly.

"Market of Small Gods gave you three," she says. "You spent your palate on them. Don't tell me you've forgotten."

I haven't forgotten. I paid for those names with pieces of my taster's gift — the one talent I was bred for, the tongue that could name hemlock in a single sip. It comes back unevenly now. Some flavors arrive loud and clear; others are gone entirely, like rooms closed off in a house I still live in. Honey tastes of nothing. Grief, oddly, I can taste from across a room.

But the names stayed. Names are cheap to keep and expensive to buy, and I'd do it again.

I set the bowl of water on the floor, light the candle, and speak the spirit's name — *Vessik-of-the-Under-Threshold* — the way you'd announce a guest you intend to eject. The room's shadows lean. Something thin and gray unfolds from the corner where the boy's shadow should be, all angles and appetite, wearing a smile it borrowed from somewhere kinder.

"The debt stands," it says. "Freely traded. Freely traded."

"He's twelve," I say.

"Age is not a clause."

"No," I agree. "It isn't. Which is why I brought a clause." And I open the coal in my chest, just a crack, and let the old gods look through my eyes at the thing that's been eating children's shadows along the seam.

You can't argue with what looks back. Vessik tries — there's a long moment of pressure, of cold, of a voice reciting contract law in a dialect older than law — and then the pressure breaks like a wave on rock, and the spirit folds down smaller and smaller until it's the size of a moth, and then it isn't anything at all except a smudge on the air and a bad smell.

The shadow comes back to the boy the way names come back: all at once, and with crying. He presses himself flat against the floor just to feel it there, whole and dark and his, and his mother holds him, and Sera lights a second candle because one never seems like enough for moments like this.

Afterward, outside, the sky over the flats is doing something extravagant with the sunset, gold going to violet going to a blue so deep it feels private. Sera stands beside me with her iron bell silent at her belt.

"You flinched," she says. "When you opened it. Just slightly."

"It still scares me." I roll my shoulders. "Every time. Like swallowing something and not knowing yet if it's poison."

"And?"

"And then it isn't. Every time. You'd think I'd learn."

"You did learn," Sera says. "You learned the difference between danger and fear. Took me thirty years and a god disowning me to manage it. You did it in a year. Insufferable."

I laugh, and the sound goes out over the flats, and somewhere in the deepening dark something immense and ancient hears it and — I swear this is true — hums back, pleased, like a hound getting its scrap.

* * *

We camp that night on a rise above Brindle, and I dream the way I always dream now: in flavors.

In the dream, the valley is a broth. I can taste every ingredient — the ward that was a leash, the immunity that was breeding, the poverty and the loyalty and the long slow simmer of a town that mistook safety for goodness. It's not a clean taste. It was never a clean taste. But it's *mine*, all of it, the bitter and the burnt and the unexpectedly sweet, and I hold the spoon — I have a spoon in the dream, Maren would approve — and I drink it down without flinching.

I wake before dawn with the coal warm in my chest and Sera already up, feeding a small fire, and for a while neither of us talks. The borderlands stretch out below us, stitched with new roads and new roofs and the smoke of people daring to live where nothing could. Somewhere east, Hollowmere is waking up. Somewhere west, the sea. Somewhere below, a broken court is relearning what it owes. Somewhere above, old gods are learning, prayer by prayer, sip by sip, that giving can be free.

"Brindle next week?" Sera asks. "There's a miller who traded his grandfather's war stories for a good harvest. He wants them back before his daughter forgets the man existed."

"Brindle next week," I agree.

I get up and shake the dew off my coat and put it on, and it fits. That's the whole miracle, if you want the truth of it. After twenty-two years of tasting other people's poisons and calling it a life — after the pen and the leash and the ledger and the altar stone — I am walking a lawless seam of the world with a disgraced priestess and an iron bell, settling debts that heaven and hell both abandoned, and my coat fits, and my sister is alive, and when I pray, something answers.

Carefully. Always carefully.

But it answers.

* * *

*—end of Veil and Wolf—*
