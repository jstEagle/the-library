## 1. The Weight of Small Bones

The dead man would not stop talking about the soup.

"It was barley," he said, for what Emrys Thackeray judged to be the eleventh time since dawn. "Barley soup at my own funeral wake. Forty years I told Marta I hated barley. Forty years. She stood over that pot weeping and she put in barley."

Emrys turned the rib bone in her vise and kept carving. The awl moved in small, patient strokes, following the grain the way water follows stone. Outside her workshop window, Thornfell's morning fog was doing its usual work of erasing the town from the mountainside, which suited her fine. Invisible town, invisible woman.

"You could have mentioned it to her while you were alive," she said quietly. She'd learned long ago not to move her lips much when she talked to the dead. In a smuggler town, talking to yourself earned you pity; talking to someone else who wasn't there earned you worse.

"I did mention it." Aldric Voss's voice came from somewhere near her left shoulder, aggrieved and thin as smoke. He had been a cooper by trade, barrel-maker, and even his ghost sounded like a man used to being listened to about hoops and staves. "I mentioned it every harvest festival when she made it. I mentioned it on our wedding anniversary. She said, *Aldric, you're too particular.* And now look. Now look what she's done."

"She's grieving," Emrys said. "Grieving people make barley soup."

"That's no excuse."

"It's the only excuse there is."

The bone was a good one — a lamb's rib, curved like a crescent moon, already taking shape under her blade. Mourning charms were her bread and butter here in the high passes, where the winters were long and the graves were dug more often than anyone liked to say aloud. A widow would come to her with a bone from her husband's table stock or, if she had coin, something finer, and Emrys would carve it into a charm: a small bird, usually, or a leaf, or a knot with no beginning and no end. Something the widow could hold in her pocket and squeeze when the nights got too quiet.

What the widows didn't know — what none of them knew — was that the charm worked because Emrys listened first. Every bone remembered its owner. Not the big things, mostly. Not the great loves and the great sins. Bones remembered the small stuff: the way a man hummed off-key when he planed wood, the exact weight of a child asleep against a shoulder, whether a person took their tea bitter or sweet. Emrys carved those small things into the charm, and when the widow held it, some echo of them seeped up through her palm, warm as a hand held in the dark.

It was honest work, more or less. It was quiet work. It was the only kind she was any good at.

"The eulogy," Aldric said, changing grievances the way other men changed hats. "The priest got my name wrong."

"He did not."

"He called me *Aldous*."

"He coughed," Emrys said. "Midway through. It was a cough."

"It was a name. My own brother didn't correct him. Bren just sat there picking at his cufflinks like a man waiting for a coach." A pause, and when Aldric spoke again, the bluster had gone out of him, leaving something smaller underneath. "Forty years in this town. Barrels in every cellar from here to the lowlands. And they can't keep my name straight for the length of one prayer."

Emrys set down her awl. She rubbed her eyes with the heel of her hand, feeling the grit of bone dust there, the ache behind them that never quite left anymore.

"They'll remember," she said. "Give it time. Grief is slow, but it's thorough."

"Is that so?" Aldric's voice drifted closer, and she felt the particular cold that meant a ghost was trying very hard to be present. "And how would you know, carver? You don't go to funerals. You take their bones and you sit in this little room with your little knife, and then you hand the widow a bird and shut your door. Nobody mourns *you*. Who remembers the carver?"

"Nobody," Emrys said, and picked the awl back up. "That's rather the point."

She heard him sigh — or felt it, the ghostly equivalent, a stirring in the air like breath on a winter pane. He didn't mean cruelty by it. The dead rarely did; they simply lost the knack for tact along with everything else. And he wasn't wrong, either, which was the irritating part. Emrys Thackeray had been in Thornfell eleven years, and in three towns before that, and in all that time she had accumulated exactly what she'd started with: a workshop, a cat of dubious loyalty named Turnip, and a reputation as a competent woman who kept to herself and asked no questions.

Competent women who asked no questions lived long lives in the Thornfell passes. That was arithmetic even a child could do.

* * *

The widow came at midday, as arranged, wrapped in a shawl the color of wet slate. Her name was Marta Voss, though of course Emrys pretended she needed reminding, and she carried a cloth bundle the way people carry things they are afraid will bite.

"You'll want the rib," Emrys said, before the woman could speak. "From the left side. He favored his left — leaned on it when he laughed."

Marta stared at her. "How did you—"

"Lucky guess." Emrys held out her hand for the bundle. "It's a common thing, favoring a side. People lean toward what amuses them."

Inside the cloth lay six bones, cleaned and wrapped in wool, each one tagged in a careful hand. Emrys unwrapped them slowly, letting her fingers rest on each in turn, and let the memories rise up through her skin like heat from a hearthstone.

The femur: a boy running through a barley field — *there it is again, the barley, stubborn to the last* — knees skinned, laughing at nothing. The shoulder blade: a young man carrying a bride over a threshold, both of them drunk on cider and terror. The skull fragment, which she would not use, because skulls were too loud and she had rules about loud: a middle-aged man arguing cheerfully with a tax collector, winning, somehow, always winning.

And the ribs. The ribs were full of the small hours. A man sitting up at night beside a cradle, counting breaths that weren't his own. A man holding his wife's hand in a sickroom, saying nothing, because there was nothing to say and everything. A man humming, off-key, always off-key, some tuneless thing he'd made up himself and never shared with a living soul.

Emrys closed her eyes for half a heartbeat. Then she opened them and was the carver again.

"A bird," she said. "I'll make you a bird. Wrens are traditional, but he strikes me as more of a thrush. Louder than he needs to be. Sings after everyone's gone inside."

Marta's face crumpled and reassembled itself, the way faces do when they've been practicing composure all week. "He sang," she whispered. "All hours. Drove me mad. I used to tell him—" Her voice failed.

"You used to tell him you'd pay good money for a moment's peace," Emrys finished gently. "And he'd say the peace was included free, you only had to listen for it."

The widow pressed her knuckles to her mouth. For a moment the workshop was very still, just the two of them and the bones and the fog pressing gray against the windows, and Emrys felt the old familiar ache open under her sternum — the one that reminded her that she could give a woman back her husband's laughter in carved lamb's rib, but she could not give her back the man, and neither, in the end, could God or the Choir or any power that ever claimed dominion over the quick and the dead.

"He complained about the soup," Marta said suddenly, wetly, almost laughing. "At the wake. I know it sounds mad, but I swear I felt him complaining. Standing right behind me, complaining about the barley."

"Not mad," Emrys said. "He's been complaining about it to me all morning. Eleven times, by my count."

Marta went white, then pink, then settled on a kind of trembling wonder. "You hear him."

Emrys weighed her answer. This was the hinge, the moment she'd navigated a hundred times before, and her rule was iron: deny, deflect, dim. A bone-carver with a gift for comfort was a treasure. A bone-carver who *heard the dead* was a spectacle, and spectacles drew crowds, and crowds drew attention, and attention in the border country drew the wrong sort of eyes — tax men, relic brokers, and once, long ago, in another town she did not let herself think about, grey robes asking questions in voices soft as falling ash.

But Marta Voss was standing in her workshop with her whole face undone, and Emrys was tired, bone-tired, tired in the marrow the way only a woman who listens to the dead for a living can be tired.

"He talks a great deal," she said, "for a man who spent forty years married to a woman who wouldn't make soup right."

Marta laughed — a cracked, startled, real laugh — and then cried, and Emrys put the kettle on, because grief ran smoother with tea, and because it gave her hands something to do besides shake.

* * *

By late afternoon the wren was nearly done. Emrys worked with the door open to the cold, because Aldric liked to watch, and because Turnip had developed opinions about closed doors. The cat lay draped across the windowsill like a discarded scarf, supervising.

"You've got the breast feathers wrong," Aldric said. "I was rounder than that."

"You were a barrel-maker, not a wren."

"Rounder than *that*, regardless."

Emrys adjusted the curve by a hair's breadth, mostly to humor him. That was the trick of the charms nobody guessed: half the craft was listening, but the other half was arguing. The dead were vain about their own bones. Carve a man too slender and he'd sulk about it for weeks; get his hands right and he'd follow the charm anywhere, settle into it like a man into a favorite chair, and stay warm for the widow as long as the carving held true.

"There," she said at last, blowing away the last of the dust. The wren sat in her palm, no bigger than a thumb, wings folded, head cocked as if it had just heard something worth hearing. Small bones, light as a lie. She'd carried heavier things across fewer miles.

"It looks like me," Aldric said, satisfied.

"It looks like a fat bird."

"A fat bird that sings." A pause. When he spoke again, his voice had that scraped-thin quality ghosts got when they were circling the thing they actually wanted to say. "Carver. When she holds it. Will she hear me?"

Emrys turned the wren in the light. "She'll feel something. Warmth. A hum, maybe. The sense of a man humming off-key in the next room." She hesitated, then broke her own rule by a finger's width. "And sometimes, on bad nights, when she squeezes it hard enough — she'll hear exactly what you'd want her to hear. Whatever that turns out to be."

"And what would I want her to hear?"

"You know what."

He was quiet a long moment. Ghosts were quiet most often right before honesty, in her experience; shame weighed nothing, but it still bent them.

"Tell her the soup was fine," Aldric said finally. "Tell her the soup was the best thing I ever ate, and I only complained because complaining was how I talked, and talking was how I loved, and she knew that, she always knew that, and if she knew it then why does it hurt so much that she might have forgotten—"

"Because forgetting is the second death," Emrys said quietly. "And everyone's afraid of it. Even the ones who've already died."

The fog outside had thickened toward evening, swallowing the rooftops one by one. Somewhere down the lane a cart creaked past, and a dog barked twice and thought better of it. Ordinary sounds. Safe sounds. Emrys wrapped the wren in wool, tied it with red thread, and set it in a box for tomorrow's delivery, and felt the day settle into its usual shape: work done, doors barred, self contained.

Her rule, recited nightly like a prayer: *Hear them, help them, never hold them. Never get involved. Never be known.*

Eleven years, the rule had held. Through three towns and a hundred charms, through every close call and curious stare, she had stayed a shadow with an awl — useful, forgettable, safe.

Which was why, when the knocking started at her door near midnight — heavy, desperate, wet-sounding knocks, from a man who was already dying on her step — every instinct she owned told her not to answer.

She answered anyway.

Later, she would blame the soup.
