## 2. Smuggler's Arithmetic

The trouble with guiding contraband through the Thornfell mountains, Emrys had long ago decided, was not the cold, or the cliffs, or the men who paid her. It was the arithmetic.

Everything up here was a sum. A mule carried forty pounds before it sulked. A man carried sixty before he complained, and a smuggler complained at fifty. The Widow's Stair lost you an hour in fog but saved you three at the checkpoint below. Ice on the north face of Cwm Ddu meant the high road was closed, which meant the low road was watched, which meant you took the Goat's Lament and prayed to whatever god looked after people stupid enough to trust their lives to a woman who talked to bones.

She did the sums in her head as she walked, because that was safer than talking, and because the numbers never argued with her the way the dead did.

Behind her came six men and fourteen mules, laden with salted eel, unlicensed brandy, and—because the world was what it was—a crate of prayer-books printed without the Church's blessing, which was somehow the most criminal of the lot. The men were Brannic's crew, which meant they were loud when sober and louder when they weren't, and Emrys had already fined two of them in silence by simply looking at them until they remembered how to be quiet.

"Thackeray." Brannic came puffing up beside her, a barrel of a man wrapped in a coat that had once belonged to someone larger. "How much farther to the crossing?"

"Four hours if we're quiet. Six if you keep asking."

He grinned. He was one of perhaps four living people who knew her name, and she permitted it because he'd earned it pulling her out of a crevasse eleven years ago, and because debts between the two of them were settled in favors rather than words. "And the checkpoint at Grey Ford?"

"Gone."

His grin faltered. "Gone? They've pulled the post?"

"They've moved it." She nodded ahead, where the trail bent around a shoulder of rock and dropped toward the river valley. From here you could see the old ford: a stone bridge, a tollhouse, a stove-smoke thread rising from its chimney. Except there was no smoke this morning, and the tollhouse door stood open, and the bridge was empty of soldiers. "They want us to think it's abandoned. They want us to come down singing and walk straight into the new post, wherever they've put it. Which will be somewhere I haven't found yet, and that is the part I don't like."

"You always find it."

"I always have," she said, and left the rest unsaid, because the rest was: *the mountains are telling me less than they used to.*

It wasn't quite true, what she'd said. She didn't find checkpoints by scouting them. She found them the way she found everything—by listening. Every pass in Thornfell had its dead. Men who'd fallen off the Widow's Stair, miners buried in the slate quarries, a customs officer from sixty years back who still walked his patrol along the Grey Ford road out of habit so deep it had outlasted his flesh. The dead noticed things. Soldiers tramping through their stretches of mountain offended them, or amused them, or bored them, and they mentioned it, the way the living gossiped, only forever.

The customs officer—Corporal Vess, though he'd been dead so long he answered mostly to *hey, you*—had been her eyes on the Grey Ford for years. *Three of them at the bridge,* he'd told her last week, in his fussy, ledger-keeping way. *Two at the window, one asleep. They never salute me anymore. No manners, the young ones.*

This morning, passing through the ford's stretch of air, she'd felt nothing. No Vess. No bored soldiers' ghosts complaining about bad rations. Just a silence with a shape to it, like a room swept clean.

Someone—or something—had cleared the dead away from Grey Ford.

That was the part she hadn't told Brannic. That was the part she turned over in her mind like a stone with something underneath it, because in forty years of walking these passes, no one had ever managed to sweep a ghost out of anywhere. Ghosts clung. Ghosts were stubborn as lichen. You could no more clear a dead man from his haunt than you could scrub the moss off the north face of Cwm Ddu.

Unless you knew how. Unless you'd been made for it.

She filed the thought away in the drawer where she kept all the thoughts she refused to think, and did some more arithmetic instead.

* * *

They came down off the Goat's Lament in the grey hour before dawn, mules stepping careful as dancers on the frost-slick scree, and Emrys called the halt with a raised fist at the mouth of a ravine that wasn't on any map worth owning.

"Rest here," she murmured. "Water the beasts. Nobody smokes. Nobody sings. Nobody tells Jorun he can't sing, because then he'll do it out of spite."

Jorun, at the rear, looked deeply wounded, having been thinking about exactly that.

Emrys climbed the ravine wall alone, boots finding holds she'd known since she was nine years old, and lay flat against a granite shelf to look down at the valley road.

There it was. The new post.

Not at the ford—upstream, half a mile, where the road narrowed between the river and a wall of rock. Two canvas tents, a brazier glowing coals-red in the dark, six soldiers, and a sergeant walking the line with the restless energy of a man who'd been told to expect something tonight and intended to earn his pay finding it. Behind the tents, picketed horses. Behind the horses, a wagon with a canvas top, and under the canvas top—

Emrys narrowed her eyes. Even at this distance, even in the dark, she could see the shape of it. Not a supply wagon. Supply wagons sagged in the middle. This one sat square and heavy on its axles, and there were iron fittings catching the brazier-light.

A cage wagon. Or something built to serve as one.

Her stomach performed a slow, cold turn that had nothing to do with the altitude.

Six soldiers and a cage wagon, posted at a crossing nobody important used, on a night when the moon was dark and every smuggler in Thornfell would be moving cargo. That wasn't tax collection. That was fishing—with a net fine enough to catch one particular fish.

Or it was nothing. She made herself say that too, silently, in the reasonable voice she used on frightened clients and restless dead. Armies moved posts all the time. Kings fiddled with tariffs. There was a war brewing somewhere or ending somewhere, there always was, and border towns got squeezed like grapes in a fist, and none of it had anything to do with a bone-carver who kept her head down and her name to herself.

Below her, the sergeant stopped walking. Turned his head. Looked directly up at the granite shelf where she lay.

Emrys went absolutely still, the way she'd learned to go still as a child—the stillness of small animals and forgotten people, the trick of being furniture, of being scenery, of being nothing anyone's eye would snag on. The wind moved her hood. The frost crept into her knees. Below, the sergeant stared at the blank rock face for the space of ten heartbeats, twenty, and then shook his head and resumed his pacing, muttering something to a soldier that made the soldier laugh.

She let out her breath in a thin white ribbon and discovered her hands were shaking.

*Forty years,* she thought. *Forty years of doing this right.* Forty years of carving charms in back rooms and guiding cargo on nights when the law was elsewhere, of being useful and unremarkable, of paying her taxes in a town that barely taxed and keeping her gift quiet as a held breath. She had rules the way other people had religion. Never carve for the same family twice in a season. Never take coin from anyone who asks questions. Never, ever be interesting.

And now a sergeant was staring at empty rocks in the dark, and a cage wagon was sitting where a cage wagon had no business sitting, and the dead had been swept from Grey Ford like dust from a floor.

The world was closing its hand, and she could feel each finger coming down.

* * *

"We go through the water," she told Brannic, crouched with him behind the ravine's lip while the mules dozed. "Upstream of the post. It's knee-deep, maybe thigh, and it's cold enough to stop your heart if you let it, so nobody lets it. We cross above them, cut through the alder brake on the far side, and we're past before their tea's cold."

Brannic squinted down at the glinting thread of the river. "Why not just wait for the watch to change? They'll be groggy at dawn."

"Because at dawn there are more of them, and because I don't know why they're here, and when I don't know why soldiers are somewhere, I get my clients across and ask questions later." She paused. "Also the water takes our scent off the mules. They have dogs?"

"No dogs."

"There'll be dogs next time," she said, with a certainty that surprised her, and Brannic gave her a look—the look of a man deciding whether to ask a question and wisely choosing brandy instead.

The crossing was misery, which was to say it went perfectly. The river took her breath at the thighs and gave back nothing but ache; the mules complained in the silent, eloquent way of mules; Jorun slipped once and was hauled upright by two crewmen with hands clamped over his mouth before the splash finished echoing. On the far bank they wrung themselves out in the alder brake, shivering, alive, unseen, and Emrys allowed herself one moment of the fierce, private pride that was the only luxury her life afforded.

*Unmatched,* the smugglers called her, when they talked about her at all, which was rarely and in lowered voices. *The Thackeray doesn't lose cargo. The Thackeray doesn't lose people.* In thirty years of runs, through storms and checkpoints and one memorable avalanche, she had never lost a client, never lost a load, never been seen by anyone whose seeing mattered. Her craft was invisibility, and she was very, very good at it, and if the price of mastery was that almost no one alive knew her name—well. Names were just another kind of cargo. Best kept light.

They made the far ridge as the sky went the color of dirty pewter, and that was where the morning went wrong.

* * *

It began with the birds.

Thornfell's choughs were bold, ragged things that followed travelers hoping for crumbs, and a column of mules usually drew them like gossip draws a widow. But the sky above the ridge was empty—not one bird—and Emrys stopped so abruptly that Brannic walked into her.

"What?"

"Listen."

They listened. Wind in the scree. Mule-breath steaming. And beneath it, faint and getting fainter, a sound Emrys had heard only twice in her life and had spent both times running: singing. Many voices, unhurried, in harmony, singing something that wasn't quite a hymn and wasn't quite a lullaby and slid off the ear like water off wax, so that even now, listening with everything she had, she couldn't have hummed a bar of it to save her soul.

The mules felt it first, or rather feared it first. Fourteen animals began to pull in fourteen directions, and the men had to lock arms with their halters, and Brannic swore with the fluency of a lifelong professional.

"What in the nine hells is that?"

"Get them off the skyline," Emrys breathed. "Into the hollow. Now. Move."

They crowded down into a fold of the hillside among the boulders, and Emrys pressed herself against the stone and listened harder, reaching out with the sense she usually kept leashed—the sense that heard the dead in bone and stone and old wood.

The mountains here were full of voices, the usual crowd: a shepherd dead two centuries, grumbling eternally about fences; a child's laughter caught in a cairn like a fly in amber; the great slow murmur of the rock itself, which wasn't a voice at all, really, more a feeling of being listened to.

And over all of it, like frost creeping across a window, the song was closing them down. Voice by voice. The shepherd's grumble thinned to a thread and snapped. The cairn-child's laughter dimmed as if a door had shut on it. Not destroyed—she would have felt that, would have felt it like a wound—but *muffled*, packed away, silenced the way you silence a room by filling it with wool.

Something was walking through her mountains, and the dead were going quiet around it, and the song was doing it, and the song was getting closer.

"How many?" Brannic whispered. He'd gone pale under his weather-leather. Whatever the song was, it scraped something raw in the living too.

"Three," she said, because she could feel them now, three knots of presence moving up the valley road they'd just fled, unhurried as weather. "Maybe four. Robes. They're in robes, I think—I don't know how I know that, but I know it." She swallowed. "They're following the mule trail. Our trail. Exactly our trail."

"Coincidence."

"On a night with a cage wagon at Grey Ford and no birds on the ridge? Brannic." She looked at him, and found that her fear had done something strange: it had crystallized into calm, the cold clean calm of a woman doing sums. "I need you to take the crew and the cargo down the Shepherd's Cut and not stop until Tarnholt. Can you find it in the dark?"

"In the dark, drunk, and backwards."

"Good. Don't use my name again. Not to them, not to anyone. If anyone asks who guided you, you hired a dead man named Hollis, and Hollis died last spring, and isn't that a shame."

"And you?"

Emrys looked back the way they'd come, toward the valley road, toward the singing. The smart move—the move she'd have taught any apprentice, if she'd ever been fool enough to take one—was to run the other direction and keep running and never guide another run through Thornfell as long as she lived.

But the dead were quieting all along that road, and one of them was Corporal Vess, who had helped her for fifteen years and asked nothing in return except that someone occasionally listen to his complaints about modern saluting, and she found she could not simply leave him to be packed in wool by strangers in robes.

It wasn't bravery. She wanted that understood, if only by herself. It was accounting. Vess was owed.

"I'm going to see what they are," she said. "From a distance. A large distance."

"That's not a plan, Thackeray, that's a eulogy."

"It's arithmetic," she said. "Go."

He went, with the crew and the mules and the eel and the brandy and the illegal prayer-books, down into the Shepherd's Cut, and Emrys watched the last mule-tail vanish into the dark, and then she stood alone on the ridge with the wind pulling at her coat, and did the bravest and stupidest thing in forty years of careful living.

She stayed.

* * *

Dawn found her flattened in a hollow above the valley road, watching three figures walk up the mule trail.

They were wrong in a way that took her a long moment to parse, because individually they were unremarkable: three people in travel-grey robes, hoods back, faces mild and middle-aged and forgettable, the sort of faces you'd swear you'd never seen even while looking at them. One carried a staff with a silver band. One carried nothing at all. The third—

The third was younger. Dark-haired, sharp-featured, walking slightly apart from the others the way a man walks when he's counting his steps and doesn't like the total. His eyes moved over the trail, over the mule droppings, over the crushed frost-grass where fourteen mules had passed, and Emrys saw him crouch, and touch the ground, and go very still.

Then he lifted his head and looked directly at her hollow.

Not searching. *Looking.* As if the ground had told him something, or as if forty years of practiced invisibility had finally, quietly failed, and he had simply known where to find the thing everyone else missed.

Emrys pressed herself into the stone and did not breathe.

The young man held her gaze across three hundred yards of dawn-lit air for five heartbeats, ten, his face giving away nothing at all—and then one of the robed figures spoke to him, and he rose, and turned away, and the three of them continued up the trail, singing their wool-and-water song, leaving the mountains quieter behind them than they'd found them.

Emrys lay in the frost until her limbs ached, doing the only sum that mattered:

*They didn't see me. He saw me.*

And somewhere below, in the silence where Corporal Vess used to complain about salutes, the mountains kept their new, terrible quiet.
