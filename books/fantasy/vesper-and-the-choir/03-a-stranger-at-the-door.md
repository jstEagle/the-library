## 3. A Stranger at the Door

The storm came down the mountain like something with a grudge.

Emrys heard it coming an hour before it arrived—the way you hear a debt collector's boots on the stairs. The dead in her workshop went quiet first, all of them at once, which was never a good sign. The dead chattered the way sparrows did, constant and thoughtless, and when they stopped, it meant either a blessing or a warning, and in Emrys's experience the ratio ran about one to forty.

She set down her knife and looked at the half-finished charm on her bench: a knucklebone from a mule, carved into the shape of a sleeping bird for a shepherd who'd lost his dog to a crevasse. The dog's ghost had been giving her notes all morning. *More ears,* it kept saying. *You've given him a bird. Birds are useless. Make it more ears.*

"I'll make you a whole flock of ears if you let me work," she muttered, and the ghost made a sound like a huffing laugh and settled.

Outside, the light had gone the color of old pewter. Wind worried at the shutters she'd reinforced twice this year, and rain began—not falling so much as arriving sideways, flung against the roof tiles like handfuls of gravel. Thornfell storms were famous in three valleys and infamous in a fourth. They ate chimneys. They carried off chickens. Once, according to a very reliable dead man, one had picked up the entire tannery and set it down again six feet to the left, which everyone agreed was showing off.

Emrys went around the workshop doing the evening ritual: banking the fire, latching the shutters, covering her tools with oilcloth because damp was death to good steel. She talked as she worked, because the dead liked it and because silence, in a room full of ghosts, felt rude.

"None of you wander tonight," she told them. "Storms pull souls about like loose laundry. If you feel yourself getting dragged toward the pass, dig in. Think of your names. Names hold."

Old Brannic, who'd been dead eleven years and still complained about his funeral potatoes, gave her a thumbs up from his corner by the stove, where he sat every evening pretending he wasn't waiting for someone to come visit him. Nobody ever did. It was the one thing that made Emrys's chest ache about him, and she'd learned not to look directly at that ache, the way you learn not to press a bruise.

She was reaching for the last shutter when the knocking started.

Not knocking, exactly. More of a *thud*. A soft, wet, desperate thud, like a fish thrown against a door by a bored cat. Then another. Then a long scrape, as though whatever was making the noise had slid partway down and was arguing with gravity about it.

Emrys stood very still.

Her rule—her only rule, the one she'd kept for fifteen years through smugglers and tax collectors and one memorably persistent marriage proposal—was simple: *never get involved.* She carved bones for the grieving and guided contraband through passes no map admitted existed, and she did both invisibly, and she asked nothing of anyone and offered less. Involvement was a door, and doors opened from both sides.

The thudding came again, weaker now.

"Please," said a voice through the wood. Young. Male. Frayed at the edges like rope about to part. "Please, I can see the light—I saw the light—"

Every sensible bone in Emrys's body told her to blow out the lamp and wait him out. Storms passed. Strangers left. Or didn't leave, but that was a problem for morning, and morning problems could be lied to.

But the dead had gone quiet again, all of them, every ghost in the workshop turned toward the door like flowers toward a terrible sun, and Old Brannic rose from his corner and said, in the voice he used maybe once a year:

"Girl. Open the door."

"You don't get a vote," Emrys whispered. "You're dead."

"And I know dying when I hear it." His face, which usually wore the expression of a man perpetually disappointed in soup, was grave. "That boy's got something on his heels. Something quiet. Open the door before it arrives, or open it after and find out what it leaves behind."

Emrys swore—a word she'd learned from a mule driver and refined over years into something almost musical—and threw the bolt.

The stranger fell into her workshop the way storms fall: all at once, and taking things with him. He was young, mid-twenties perhaps, dressed as a courier in leathers gone dark with rain and something else, something that hadn't come from any cloud. Blood sheeted down the left side of his face from a wound above his ear, and there was more blood on his shoulder, and his hands—she noticed this with the strange clarity that sometimes visits in emergencies—his hands were ruined, torn across the palms and fingers as though he'd climbed something that had objected.

He got one knee under himself, then the other, and looked up at her, and his eyes were the worst part. Not wild. *Focused.* The focus of a man who has decided exactly one thing matters and is spending his last coins of life to buy it.

"You carve bones," he said. Not a question. "They said—down in Lowmark—they said there's a woman who hears them. Who listens."

"They say a lot of things in Lowmark," Emrys said. "Mostly about other people's wives."

He laughed, and the laugh turned into a cough, and the cough brought up blood, and Emrys found herself moving without having decided to, catching his arm, steering him to the stool by the fire. Her body, apparently, had voted while her mind was still filibustering.

*Never get involved,* said the sensible part of her, distantly, like a neighbor shouting advice from across a river.

"Shut up," she murmured—to herself, not him—and pressed her hands to his shoulder wound. Too deep. She knew wounds the way she knew bones, and this one had the deep, patient geometry of something meant to kill slowly, so the runner would die tired and alone and unable to tell anyone anything.

"What did this to you?" she asked, tearing strips from a linen rag.

He shook his head, and the shake cost him, and he gripped her wrist with those ruined hands hard enough to hurt. "Listen to me. There isn't time. There's a thing behind me and it doesn't make noise. You understand? No footsteps. No breath. You won't hear it coming. You'll just—" He swallowed. "You'll notice the silence getting bigger."

Emrys's skin crawled. She knew silences. She lived in a house full of voices, and she had spent her whole life learning their textures, and she suddenly understood that the storm outside had a hole in it. A patch of nothing, moving up the track toward her door. Where the rain should have drummed, it didn't. Where the wind should have howled, it held its breath.

"It's close," she said.

"Minutes. Maybe less." He fumbled at his belt, at a pouch beneath his coat, and his fingers wouldn't obey—he'd torn them too badly, climbing, running, holding on—and Emrys took the pouch from him and opened it herself.

Inside was a bundle wrapped in oilskin, tied with waxed cord, small enough to fit in two cupped hands. It was warm. That was the thing she noticed first and would remember longest: after everything, after the blood and the storm and the silent thing on his trail, the bundle was warm as a sleeping bird.

"Take it," he said. "South. Follow the spine of the mountains until the roads stop being roads. There's a woman in the valley villages who knows the maker. Maren. Find—" Another cough, folding him nearly double. "Find the gate. Vesper Gate. It's the last—"

"The last what?"

"The last door out." His eyes were going glassy, but the focus held, ferocious, a candle flame refusing the wind. "Vellichor isn't gone. Everyone thinks it's gone. It's not gone, it's *hidden*, and the ones who hid it will kill anyone who remembers, and they're coming, they're already—" He grabbed her sleeve. "Say it back. Say you'll take it south."

"I don't even know your name," Emrys said, which was not agreement, and they both knew it, and he smiled anyway, bloody and crooked, like she'd said something kind.

"Cael," he said. "My name is Cael. Now you can haunt me properly, when I'm dead. I hear you do that."

"I don't *haunt* anyone. I listen. It's different."

"It's not," Cael said gently, "and we both know it."

Outside, the hole in the storm's noise reached the edge of her yard. Emrys felt it the way you feel someone standing behind you in a dark room—not hearing, not seeing, just knowing, the animal knowledge older than language. The rain fell everywhere except in a man-shaped absence ten feet from her door, and in that absence there was a patience so vast and so cold that her breath fogged despite the fire.

Old Brannic had backed into his corner. All the ghosts had. Fifteen years of the dead drifting through her workshop complaining about funerals and unfinished business, and every single one of them was now pressed against the far wall like children hiding from a headmaster.

"That thing," Emrys whispered, "is it—"

"Don't look at it," Cael said. "It comes for the package. If it finds the package gone, it goes. But it has to be sure, and it is very, very thorough." He pressed the oilskin bundle into her hands and closed her fingers over it with his ruined ones. Warmth bled through the wrapping. "Hide it somewhere it would never think to look. Somewhere ordinary. Somewhere *yours*."

Somewhere hers. Emrys's mind, trained by years of smuggling contraband past checkpoints, did its work without consulting her fear. Bone. Hide it in bone. She crossed to the bench in four steps, took up the femur she'd been hollowing for a walking-cane grip—ordinary, ugly, unmistakably her own work—and fed the oilskin bundle into the marrow channel, sealing it with wax and shavings, and set it in the basket with the other finished charms where it looked exactly like what it appeared to be: another piece of a bone-carver's stock-in-trade.

Behind her, Cael made a sound. Not a word. A sigh with edges on it.

When she turned, he was sliding off the stool, and she caught him, and lowered him, and knelt with his head in her lap on her own floor, which was already more involvement than she'd permitted herself in a decade.

"Vesper," he whispered.

His eyes were on hers. His mouth kept moving after the sound stopped, shaping words she couldn't read, and then his hand, resting on the floorboards, twitched once, twice, and went still, and the focus went out of his face all at once, like a lamp snuffed, and what remained was just a young man, soaked and torn and twenty-something forever, on the floor of a stranger's workshop at the top of the world.

And then the pressure lifted. The hole in the storm's noise hung at her threshold a moment longer—she felt it considering, the way winter considers a window—then withdrew, unhurried, down the track, and the rain closed over its silence like water over a stone, and the storm was just a storm again.

Emrys sat on her floor with a dead courier's head in her lap and did not move for a long time.

* * *

The dead came back to themselves slowly, the way birds return to a garden after a cat leaves. Brannic approached first, circling the body at a respectful distance.

"Choir," he said finally.

"What?"

"The ones who hunt him. That's what my grandfather called them, and his grandfather before. Grey robes. They eat memories—take them out of people like splinters. Whole towns wake up missing pieces and call it weather." He looked at the body, and his usual disappointment-in-soup expression had gone somewhere else entirely. "Nobody's seen one in forty years. We all thought the stories died out."

"Well, the stories are wrong. One of them just stood in my yard being quiet at me." Emrys eased Cael's head onto the folded cloak she kept for exactly this purpose—though the purpose had always been theoretical until tonight—and stood, and her knees cracked, and she discovered her hands were shaking. She watched them shake with detached interest, the way you watch someone else's hands. "I need to bury him."

"In the morning."

"He deserves—"

"In the *morning*, girl. Tonight you bar that door, sit by that fire, and decide what you're going to do, because that boy died buying you time and you owe him better than panic." Brannic settled onto his corner stool, solid as he'd been in life, and fixed her with a look. "And Emrys? Whatever you decide—you burn that map or you follow it—decide it as the woman who opened the door. Not the woman who wanted to keep it shut. Those are two different women, and only one of them gets to sleep well."

Emrys looked at him. Eleven years dead, and still the only person who talked to her like she was worth talking to straight.

"When did you get wise?" she asked.

"Death's mostly waiting around," Brannic said. "Gives you time to think."

She banked the fire high. She laid Cael out properly, closed his eyes, folded his hands—ruined palms upturned, as if he'd finally put down whatever he'd been carrying. From her stock she chose a small vertebra, smooth and white, and began to carve, because her hands needed work the way other people's hearts needed prayer.

She carved him a mourning charm while the storm shouted itself hoarse outside. A simple one. A traveler's knot-work, roads crossing roads, so that wherever he went next, he wouldn't lose his way.

And all night, as she worked, she felt the bundle hidden inside the hollowed femur in her basket, warm as a sleeping bird, warm as a secret, warm as the beginning of absolutely everything she had spent fifteen years making sure would never happen to her.

Near dawn, the storm broke apart, and in the sudden enormous quiet, from the corner where the new ghost was learning to stand, Cael spoke his first words as a dead man:

"So. About that map."
