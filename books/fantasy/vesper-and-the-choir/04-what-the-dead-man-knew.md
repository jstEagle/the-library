## 4. What the Dead Man Knew

The storm had spent itself by morning, leaving the Thornfells rinsed and glittering, as if the mountains were showing off for no one in particular. Emrys Thackeray stood at her workbench with a cup of barley tea going cold at her elbow, staring at the oilskin bundle on the table as though it might bite.

It hadn't moved. She'd checked. Twice.

"You could at least have the decency to be gone," she told the room.

"I'm afraid I can't." The voice came from behind her, dry and apologetic, the voice of a man who had spent his life apologizing to people for things that weren't his fault. "Believe me, I've tried."

Emrys did not turn around. Turning around would make it real, and she was still holding out hope that the whole night—the blood on her doorstep, the stranger dying against her door with his fingers pressed to hers, the word *Vesper* breathed out like a prayer—had been an unusually vivid dream brought on by bad tea.

"The dead cross over," she said. "That's how it works. I carve them their little doors, they walk through, everyone's very relieved. I've done it four hundred times. You are not supposed to be *here*."

"And yet." She heard him shift—she could feel him now without looking, a faint coolness in the air, like standing near a cellar door. "I've been walking since dawn. There's a road out of this valley that I remember taking, except when I get to the bend by the black pine, there's nothing there. Just more mountain. It's the strangest thing. It's as if the road only exists where someone remembers it."

Emrys set down her tea with exaggerated care. "That's because you're dead," she said. "Dead men don't get roads. They get what the living give them, which is usually a hole in the ground and a good cry, and then off you go."

"Most of us, perhaps." He sounded genuinely interested, which was somehow worse than if he'd been frightened. "You've spoken with many of us, then? In your carving?"

Emrys finally turned.

He looked exactly as he had last night, which was to say: soaked through, middle-aged, unremarkable in the way of men who carried things for a living—a courier's frame, lean and hard-used, with a face built for forgetting. The wound in his side was still there, dark and wrong, but he didn't favor it. The dead rarely did. Pain was one of the first things they left behind, along with hunger, cold, and any sense whatsoever of when they weren't wanted.

"My name is Corvin Hale," he said. "Late of the courier service, though which courier service is rather the question now. And you are Emrys Thackeray, bone-carver, who talks to her work and lives alone on purpose."

"How do you know my name?"

"You said it. Last night. When you were trying to stop the bleeding." He smiled faintly. "You talk quite a lot when you're frightened. It's charming, actually. Most people go quiet."

Emrys crossed her arms. "I don't want to know your name. Knowing names is how this starts. Names lead to errands, errands lead to trouble, and trouble—" she gestured at him, at the map, at the entire catastrophic situation— "*finds me*, apparently, even when I have spent forty-one years being spectacularly un-findable."

"That's not true," Corvin said mildly. "You found me. On your doorstep. Technically."

"I did *not*—"

"There's also the matter of the map." He nodded toward the oilskin bundle. "Which is mine. Or was. And which I cannot deliver, because I am, as established, dead, and the person I was meant to deliver it to is—well. That's rather the heart of it."

Emrys looked at the map. The map sat there, being an object, minding its own business, radiating the particular innocence of things about to ruin someone's life.

"No," she said.

"You haven't heard—"

"*No.* Whatever it is, whoever it was for, whatever dying wish you're about to lay on me with those sad, patient, dead-man eyes—I don't do errands. I carve charms. I tell grieving widows that their husbands thought the funeral flowers were excessive. I keep my head down and my hands busy and my name out of other people's mouths. That has kept me alive through smuggler wars, two plagues, and one extremely memorable tax revolt. It is a *system*, Corvin Hale, and your map is not part of it."

Corvin considered this with the unhurried air of a man who had all the time in the world, which, Emrys reflected bitterly, he literally did.

"May I tell you a story?" he asked.

"No."

"It's about a country that doesn't exist."

Emrys opened her mouth. Closed it. The fire popped. Outside, meltwater dripped from the eaves in a slow, patient rhythm, like the mountain counting something down.

"...One story," she said. "And then you cross over, or I find a way to make you, and I have been doing this long enough to know there's always a way."

* * *

Corvin Hale told his story sitting in the chair by the window, though he never quite touched it, hovering a half-inch above the wood the way the dead did when they forgot they no longer weighed anything. Emrys stayed at her bench, ostensibly tidying her tools, actually listening so hard it hurt.

"I was born in Vellichor," he began. "In a town called Ambergate, on the river Sere. I know how that sounds. I know you're already reaching for the reasons it can't be true, because I reached for them too, for years. But I remember it. I remember the terraced orchards above the river, and the bells—Vellichor had bell-towers instead of fortresses, did you know that? Every town rang its hours, and the bells were tuned to each other, so that from a hillside at dusk you could hear three towns at once, and it sounded like—" he paused, searching— "like the mountains themselves were humming."

"Bells rust," Emrys said flatly. "Towns fall. People forget. That's not erasure, that's just history."

"This wasn't history." His voice changed, and Emrys's hands went still on the whetstone. Forty years of listening to the dead had taught her every register of a ghost's voice—the griefs, the rages, the small contentments—and she knew what she was hearing now. Not sorrow. *Wrongness*. A man describing a wound in the world. "There are no ruins, Emrys. No records. No songs with the words worn off. I have walked the length of these mountains asking after my home, and people look at me kindly and say, *never heard of it, friend,* and then—here's the part that took me twenty years to notice—they forget they said it. Ask them again an hour later and they'll frown, because they don't remember the conversation. The country wasn't destroyed. It was *unmade*. Taken out of the world the way you'd take a stitch out of cloth, and the cloth closed up behind it, and the Choir keeps the seam."

"The Choir," Emrys repeated. The word came out of her mouth before she could stop it, and Corvin's ghostly face went sharp.

"So you've heard of them."

"I've heard *whispers*. Grey robes. They come through the passes sometimes, asking questions, and afterward nobody remembers being asked. My mother told me to be elsewhere whenever they passed through, and my mother was a woman who was elsewhere for a living." She hesitated. "They were following you. Last night. Whatever it was in the storm—it didn't make sound. Sound follows men. That thing followed you the way frost follows a window."

"They know I have the map," Corvin said simply. "Or they knew. Now they know *you* have it, which is worse, and I am sorry for that in ways I will spend whatever eternity I've got making up for." He leaned forward—or seemed to; the light bent strangely around him. "But listen. Listen, because I died for this and I would like it to have bought something. Vellichor bargained itself away. Forty years ago, the whole country—every border, every bell, every birth record—was folded out of the world, and the price of the folding was memory. Nobody who remembered it could keep the memory. Everyone who might have learned of it was kept from learning. And the ones who enforce it, the singers in grey—they aren't soldiers. They're *custodians*. They walk the world eating the country back out of people's heads, one mind at a time, forever."

"Why?" Emrys asked, despite herself. Despite the system, despite the rules, despite everything. "What plague, what war, what horror is worth that?"

"I don't know." For the first time, Corvin looked tired—not dead-tired, but the old human tiredness of a man who had asked a question too many times and gotten silence. "No one knows. That's the cruelty of it. You can't even mourn properly, because mourning requires remembering what was lost. My mother's face—I have it, Emrys, I have it still, because I died outside the reach of their song. But everyone I ever loved who's still alive? They don't know they lost me twice. Once to death, once to the erasure. Somewhere there may be a woman who was my wife, and she has a shape in her life where I used to be, and she doesn't grieve it because she doesn't know it's a shape at all."

The fire had burned low. Emrys realized she was gripping the edge of the bench hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and made herself let go.

"And the map," she said. Her voice came out rougher than she intended. "Where does the map come in?"

"From a man named Aldous Maren—one of the cartographers of Vellichor, one of the few who saw the erasure coming and hid his work inside other work. Coastlines inside coastlines. Roads drawn as rivers. My father carried his papers out the night it happened; I've carried them since my father died. The map shows the way to the Vesper Gate." Corvin's eyes—dead eyes, patient eyes—fixed on hers. "The last door. The place where the bargain was struck, and the only place it can be unstruck. If the gate still stands, then Vellichor isn't gone, Emrys. It's *held*. Held behind a door, in a country that refuses to admit it ever existed—and held things can be released."

Emrys stood up. She walked to the hearth. She picked up the poker, and then she put it down again, because the thing she actually wanted to burn was sitting on her table wrapped in oilskin, and burning it would mean touching it, and touching it felt suddenly, horribly like agreeing to something.

"Do you know what happens to people who carry things for the dead?" she said, not turning around. "I do. I've carved four hundred of them. They die badly, Corvin. They die *loudly*. The widow whose husband's charm I carved—her neighbors whispered about her for a year. And that was a charm about funeral flowers. You're asking me to carry a door out of a country that powerful people unmade on purpose, while grey-robed memory-eaters hunt it across the mountains. That's not an errand. That's a funeral with extra steps, and I'd be the corpse."

"Yes," Corvin agreed placidly. "All of that is true."

"*That's your argument?*"

"My argument is that you haven't burned the map yet." He rose from his half-sitting, half-hovering position and drifted closer, and Emrys hated—hated with her whole solitary, well-defended heart—that she didn't step back. "I watched you all night, Emrys Thackeray. You wrapped the oilskin in dry cloth. You put it high, away from the damp wall. You checked on it twice before dawn. You are a woman who has spent her whole life telling herself she doesn't care about anything, and you have just spent eight hours caring for a dead man's parcel."

"I was keeping it *evidence*," Emrys snapped. "In case anyone comes asking, I need to know where it is so I can deny having it accurately."

"Of course," said Corvin gravely. "Very sensible."

She turned on him then, and the words came out of her in a flood, hot and fast, the words she'd been building her whole life out of: "You don't understand what it costs. Being invisible. It costs *everything*, every day, and I paid it, I paid it gladly, because the alternative is this—caring about a country I've never seen, about *your* mother's face, about bells I've never heard, and then losing it. Everything I touch that matters gets taken. So I stopped touching. That's not cowardice, that's *arithmetic*, and I am very, very good at arithmetic."

The room went quiet. Meltwater counted on. Corvin Hale looked at her with an expression she recognized from four hundred deathbeds—not pity, never pity, but the terrible gentleness of the dying, who have no use left for lies.

"I know," he said softly. "I know exactly what it costs. I'm paying it right now. I can't cross over, Emrys. Do you understand? I've tried. There's a road out of this valley and it isn't there, because somewhere in the world, my errand isn't finished, and the dead don't get to rest until what they carried is delivered. I will stand in this workshop, watching you drink your tea and sharpen your knives and live your careful life, for as long as it takes. Years, maybe. I'm rather hoping you're too kind for that, but I confess I don't actually know if you are. Neither, I think, do you."

Emrys stared at him.

"You're blackmailing me," she said. "You're a *ghost* and you're *blackmailing me*."

"I prefer 'presenting the full arithmetic,'" said Corvin. "You respect arithmetic."

For a moment—just a moment—something in her chest cracked along a line she'd thought was scar tissue. She saw it, absurdly: terraced orchards, dusk, three towns' bells humming together in the mountains. She saw a woman's face she'd never known, waiting in a country that didn't exist for a husband who couldn't come home. She saw the map, high on its shelf, dry and safe, cared for through the night by hands that claimed not to care.

Then she saw grey robes in the market, and her neighbor's wife smiling at nothing, and her own name dissolving like sugar in rain.

"No," Emrys said. Quietly, this time. Final as a closing door. "Find another courier."

* * *

She lasted until dusk.

Not because of Corvin—he kept his word, mostly, sitting silent by the window with the patience of the truly dead, though she could feel him watching her the way sun watches a sundial. Not even because of the map, though her eyes kept snagging on the shelf, and once she caught herself halfway across the room with her hand out before she remembered what she'd decided.

It was the widow's charm that broke her.

She'd left it half-finished on the bench—the mourning piece for old Marta, with her husband Tomas's complaints still murmuring inside it about the flower arrangements. All day it had been quiet, the way the dead went quiet when something bigger was in the room. But at dusk, as the light went blue and the meltwater kept its count, the charm began to murmur again, and Emrys picked it up automatically, the way she'd done ten thousand times, and Tomas's voice said:

"—and tell her the left-hand wreath was tacky, I *said* so at the time—but Emrys, girl, Emrys, there's a man in your house who isn't breathing, and he's been there all day, and girl, *girl*, why does he keep looking at you like you're the last lit window in a dark country—"

Emrys set the charm down very gently, as if it might go off.

"He's dead," she told it. "He's a dead man with a dead man's errand, and I told him no."

"Mm," said Tomas, in the tone of a man who had been married thirty years and recognized a lie of long standing when he heard one. "And how's that working out?"

She looked at the window. Corvin sat in the fading light, translucent as breath on glass, watching the mountains with an expression she now understood completely: homesickness for a place that had never existed, which was either the saddest thing in the world or the most stubborn, and Emrys Thackeray had built her whole life on knowing the difference between surrender and endurance, and this—

This was not surrender.

"Oh, hells," she said, to the charm, to the ghost, to the mountains, to whatever power arranged for couriers to bleed out on the doorsteps of women who knew better.

She took the map down from the shelf. The oilskin was cool and heavier than it should have been, the way important things always were. Inside, when she unwrapped it at last, the leather was soft with age, covered in fine lines that were not quite coastlines and not quite roads, and in the corner, in a hand that trembled only slightly, someone had written a single word in a language she almost—*almost*—recognized:

*Vesper.*

Behind her, Corvin Hale let out a breath he no longer needed, and the sound of it was like a bell beginning to ring.

"Now," he said, "we should talk about the Choir."
