## 5. The Choir Comes Singing

The dead man in the mourning charm had opinions about the eulogy.

*Too short,* he said, as Emrys turned the little bone sparrow over in her hands, checking the grain of the wing against the light. *Four minutes. Four! I lived sixty-one years and Widow Prue got four minutes and a poem about shepherds. I never kept sheep in my life.*

"You didn't keep sheep," Emrys agreed under her breath. She was alone in her workshop, which was fortunate, because talking to charms was the sort of habit that got a bone-carver talked about, and being talked about was the one thing Emrys Thackeray had spent thirty-one years avoiding with the dedication other people gave to religion or drink.

*And the flowers,* the dead man went on. *Chrysanthemums. Funeral flowers for a man who once wrote three pages to the town council complaining about chrysanthemums blocking the millrace.*

"I'll mention it to her."

*You will not. You'll carve my complaints into that bird and let it sit on her mantel, and when she holds it at night she'll feel me grumbling, and she'll smile, because that's what she married me for.* A pause, the particular quality of silence a ghost made when it was embarrassed by its own tenderness. *She'll think the smiling is grief. Let her.*

Emrys set down her knife. That was the trouble with the dead — they were honest in ways the living couldn't afford to be, and they had a way of saying things that stuck in a person like splinters of ice. She picked up the sparrow again. Alder-bleached goat femur, sanded to the softness of a river stone. Inside its hollow chest, folded smaller than a moth, were six years of a dead man's grievances and one line of his love, and Widow Prue would never know which was which, and neither, really, would he.

That was the craft of it. That was the whole craft.

*Something's wrong outside,* the dead man said suddenly.

Emrys looked up. "There's always something wrong outside. It's Thornfell."

*No. Listen.*

She listened. Rain on the slate roof, the usual percussion. The creak of Brannic's cart two doors down, the usual complaint. The mountain wind coming off the col, the usual moan. And underneath it all — nothing. That was what was wrong. The dogs weren't barking. In eleven years of working this stall at the edge of the market square, Emrys could count on one hand the number of times the dogs of Lower Hallow hadn't announced every cart, every peddler, every cloud that looked at them funny.

*They're afraid,* said the dead man, who knew fear when he heard its absence. *Girl. They're afraid of something walking quiet.*

Emrys's hands went still on the carving.

Her rule — the rule she'd made at nineteen, bleeding and furious, after the incident with the magistrate's son whose ghost had followed her home from the graveside — was simple: hear everything, say nothing, touch no one's trouble. The dead came to her whether she liked it or not; what she did with their voices was the only part of the gift she controlled. She carved their small angers and small loves into charms and lockets and left the large things — the murders, the buried gold, the dying confessions — exactly where they lay. Invisible. Uninvolved. Alive.

She was reaching for the shutter to close the stall early when she saw them come around the fountain.

There were five of them, walking in a loose line up the market road, and they were grey. Not drab-grey, not the grey of travel-worn cloaks, but a grey that seemed to have been decided upon somewhere far away by someone who understood that grey was the color of forgetting — the grey of ash, of dawn fog, of the moment just before you lose the thread of a dream. Their robes were unadorned. Their hoods were back. Their faces were pleasant, ordinary faces, the kind you'd forget on purpose, and each of them wore a thin chain from which hung a small silver tuning fork, and each fork, though there was no wind, was trembling. Singing. A note so high and thin that Emrys only heard it because of who she was — a note like a held breath, like the last syllable of a word spoken forty years ago.

The dogs had gone silent. Every dog in Lower Hallow, pressed flat to the ground beneath porches and carts, ears back, making themselves small.

*What are they?* whispered the dead man in the sparrow, and his voice had gone thin as paper. *Why can't I hear them thinking? Everyone thinks. Even the living think, it's just rubbish most of it. These ones are — girl, these ones are quiet inside. Like rooms after a fire.*

"Stay still," Emrys murmured. "Don't sing out."

*I can't help it if—*

"*Still.*"

The five figures moved through the market without hurry. That was the first terrible thing about them, Emrys thought later, turning it over in the dark: they did not hurry. Soldiers hurried. Tax collectors hurried. Whatever these were, they had never once in their existence been refused, and so they had lost the habit of urgency the way a caged bird loses the habit of flight.

The first one stopped at Grelda's cheese stall. Emrys saw him lean in — a man perhaps fifty, kind lines around his eyes, the face of a favorite uncle — and saw his mouth move. Saw Grelda laugh. Actually laugh, hand to her breast, charmed. He spoke with her for perhaps half a minute. Then he touched two fingers to his own temple in a small courteous salute and moved on, and Grelda stood there for a moment with her smile fading like frost in sun, looking at her cheeses as though she'd forgotten why she was standing behind them. As though she'd forgotten the morning entirely. As though she were waiting for someone to tell her what day it was.

*He asked her something,* said the dead man. *I couldn't hear it. Girl, I couldn't hear the question. It went past me like — like water through a net.*

"What did she answer?"

*Nothing. That's the thing. She answered before he finished asking. Like her mouth knew the shape of it and her mind never caught up.*

The second figure stopped at Brannic's cart. The third paused by the well, where old Tam Ossler was drawing water, and spoke to him briefly, gently, head tilted like a man asking directions. Tam nodded along, agreeable, agreeable — and then stood holding his bucket rope with both hands and an expression of profound, drifting confusion, as if he'd surfaced from deep water and couldn't remember which way the shore was.

They were asking questions. Emrys understood that much. Questions that unwove themselves from the minds that heard them, leaving only the answering, like a debt collected while the debtor slept. And they were moving through her market — her shabby, rain-slicked, gossip-ridden market where everyone knew everyone's business and nobody knew anything worth knowing — methodically, politely, the way a tide moves through a rock pool.

Looking for something. Or someone.

The oilskin packet sat in the drawer beneath her workbench, wrapped in a woman's shawl, wrapped again in oilcloth, wedged between the blanks of bone she hadn't yet carved. It had been there for two days. Two days of telling herself to burn it. Two days of taking it out at midnight, holding it over the candle, and putting it back, because the dead courier stood in the corner of her workshop every time she tried, watching her with those patient drowned eyes, and because — she admitted it now, in the privacy of her own skull, where admitting things cost nothing — because a country that had been erased from every record and every memory was a country that had been *murdered*, and Emrys Thackeray had spent her whole life listening to murdered things, and they had never once asked her to avenge them. This one hadn't even asked. It had simply pressed a map into her hands and died, and left her holding the question.

She should burn it. She was going to burn it. Tonight, probably. Almost certainly.

First she had to survive the afternoon.

The grey figures were working their way up the row. Cheese, then fruit, then ironmongery, then the fortune-teller's empty table — Marta had seen them coming, apparently, and possessed better survival instincts than anyone gave her credit for — and then, in perhaps four minutes, the stall of Emrys Thackeray, bone-carver, who sold charms against sorrow and had never in her life sold anything worth a grey robe's attention.

Think, she told herself. Her heart was going like a drum in a procession, and her hands — her good, steady, clever hands — wanted to shake, and she made them be still through sheer tyranny. Think the way you carve. Find the shape inside the block.

The map couldn't stay in the drawer. If they asked her questions — questions that slid past the mind and made the mouth answer — then anything she knew, they'd know, and worse, anything she *had* might be drawn out of her like a splinter. She couldn't hide it on her body. She couldn't hide it in the workshop; they might search, or might not need to. Where do you hide a thing from people who eat memory?

You hide it in plain sight. You hide it the way she hid everything else.

Emrys took up the largest blank on her bench — a cow femur, thick as her wrist, meant to become a bedpost finial for the innkeeper's new wife — and with the calm of thirty-one years of practice she began to carve. Not the finial. Something else. Her knife moved in the patterns her hands knew better than her thoughts: the hollowing cuts, the inner chamber, the secret seam that ran along the bone's length and closed so perfectly that even she had to find it by feel. A reliquary. The oldest trick of her trade. Grief-charms held words; some pieces, the ones she never sold, held more.

She worked the seam open, drew the oilskin packet from the drawer, and folded it — folded it small, smaller than sense allowed, pressing it flat and rolling it tight until it was a rod no thicker than her little finger — and slid it into the marrow-channel of the bone. Then she closed the seam, sanded it flush, and began, with the same knife and the same unhurried strokes, to carve the surface: climbing roses, because the innkeeper's wife liked roses, and a border of leaves, and nothing else. An ordinary piece of decorative carpentry. Boring. Boring as bread.

*They're coming,* said the dead man. His voice had gone very small. *Oh, girl. Oh, I don't like this. I'm going to be quiet as the grave, and I mean that as a professional courtesy.*

"Thank you," Emrys breathed.

The first of them stopped at her stall.

Up close, the grey robe smelled of nothing at all, which was somehow worse than any smell — no sweat, no wool, no rain, no person. The man beneath the hood had a pleasant face and eyes the color of weather, and he regarded her wares with the mild interest of a traveler killing time. His tuning fork trembled at his throat, singing its one impossible note, and Emrys felt the note pass through her like a fish through water, tasting everything, catching nothing.

"Good morning," he said. His voice was warm. It was the warmest voice she had ever heard, and it was warm the way a hearth is warm in a house where everyone has died. "Lovely work. Do you carve to commission?"

"Sometimes." Keep it small. Small as a bead. "Charms, mostly. Keepsakes."

"For grief?" He picked up a finished sparrow — not the widow's, thank every saint — and turned it in fingers that were careful and clean. "People do grieve here, I imagine. Mountain towns. Hard lives."

"People grieve everywhere."

"They do." He smiled at her, and the smile reached his eyes, and that was the second terrible thing: it was a genuine smile. He was not pretending kindness. Somewhere far back, whatever kindness was originally made of was still in him, worn smooth as a river stone by decades of use. "May I ask you something?"

Here it comes, she thought. Here is the question that slides past the mind and makes the mouth open.

"You may ask," she said, and gripped the counter, and waited to betray herself.

"Do you take payment in coin, or only in trade?"

The question went through her like cold water and found nothing to catch. Of course. Of course — they were being thorough, they were being *polite*, they were asking everyone in the market the same harmless questions, and the harmless questions were the trap, because afterward no one remembered being asked at all, and so no one ever wondered what they'd answered. She almost laughed. It came out as a cough.

"Coin," she said. "I'm sentimental, not stupid."

His smile widened a fraction, as if she'd said something genuinely pleasing. "A rare distinction." He set the sparrow down and aligned it, precisely, with the others on her display board — aligned it so its breast faced outward, facing *him*, and Emrys felt the hair rise on her arms. "My colleagues and I are travelers. We're looking for someone. A courier, we believe, who passed through these mountains some days ago. Would you have seen him?"

"No," said Emrys's mouth, smoothly, easily, before her mind had even finished deciding — and she felt it happen, felt the answer come from somewhere below thought, obedient and instant, and understood with a lurch that it didn't matter whether she lied or told the truth. The question itself was the theft. Whatever she said, she would say it *for them*.

"Mm," said the grey man, and there was nothing in the sound, no suspicion, no satisfaction. He was a man checking items off a list that had no end. "Thank you. Your work is lovely. Someone loved you enough to teach you that."

It was such an odd thing to say that she blinked. "My grandmother," she heard herself say. "Mostly."

"Mostly," he repeated softly, and for one moment — one long, cold, endless moment — his weather-colored eyes rested on her face with an attention that had nothing mild in it at all, an attention like a hand turning over every stone in a streambed, and Emrys stood in the rain-smell of her own market square and understood that she was seeing, for the first time in her life, what it looked like when something hunted by *listening*. "Grandmothers," he said, "are where we keep our oldest songs."

Then he inclined his head, touched two fingers to his temple, and moved on down the row, and the note of the tuning fork slid out of her bones, and Emrys discovered she had been holding the carving knife hard enough to whiten her knuckles, and that she had, without any memory of doing it, positioned herself directly in front of the rose-carved femur like a hen over a single egg.

The remaining four passed her stall without stopping. One of them — younger, quicker, with a restless way of walking, as though patience were a garment that fit him badly — glanced at her as he went. Just a glance. Grey eyes, a face too sharp to be forgettable, which must have been a professional embarrassment to him. His gaze snagged on hers for half a heartbeat, and Emrys braced for the sliding, obedient feeling, the mouth-opening feeling.

It didn't come. He looked at her the way a person looks at another person — startled, curious, *caught* — and then his expression closed like a door, and he walked on, and Emrys stood there with her heart hammering and the insane certainty that she had just been *seen*, actually seen, by one of them, and that he had chosen not to report it.

The five grey figures reached the end of the market row, conferred briefly — heads together, voices too low to catch, though the dead man swore he felt the shapes of words go past him like birds — and then walked on up the road toward the tavern district, unhurried, patient, polite, and the dogs of Lower Hallow did not begin barking again until they had turned the corner and were quite out of sight.

* * *

They stayed three days.

That was the worst of it. Emrys had expected them to sweep through like weather and be gone, but they settled into the Wayfarer's Rest like pilgrims, paid their bills in exact coin, complimented the stew, and were seen each morning walking the town's streets with their hands folded, asking their gentle, unanswerable questions. By the second day, the town had stopped noticing them — that was the horror of it, the true horror, the thing Emrys lay awake cataloguing at three in the morning: not that people forgot the questions, but that people forgot the *men*. Grelda remembered a pleasant traveler, maybe, or maybe remembered nothing, and either way she smiled at the grey robes in passing on day two as though greeting an old friend she couldn't name. The town's memory of them wore away like a path worn by feet, and they walked it daily, re-treading it, patient as erosion.

On the third evening, Emrys sat in her workshop with the door barred and the candle low, and took inventory of her soul.

The map slept in its bone. She checked the seam twice a day now, by feel, the way a miser checks a floorboard. The dead courier stood in his corner — he'd returned the night the Choir arrived, drawn back across whatever distance ghosts measured by, and he watched the door with an expression that needed no translation. *They're following my trail,* he'd said. *I led them a crooked road, but roads end, girl. Roads always end at the door you're standing in.*

"I know," Emrys said. She was packing. Not frantically — frantically was how people got caught — but with the grim, methodical calm of a woman dismantling her own life into a rucksack. Carving knives, wrapped in oiled leather. Whetstones. The good sanding papers. A change of clothes and a second pair of boots, because the passes ate boots. The little brass scale she used to weigh charms, because a tool was a tool. Everything else — the furniture, the stock, the sign with her name on it — could burn or rot or be sold by whoever came next.

Because she was leaving. She had finally, formally, in the privacy of her own skull, admitted it. The Choir had come to Lower Hallow asking after a courier, and couriers did not carry maps to erased countries for their health, and sooner or later — tomorrow, next week, next season — the polite grey tide would wash back up this street and ask its sliding questions again, and this time the questions would have her name in them. Her invisible life was over. It had ended the moment a bleeding stranger put his blood on her doorstep, and everything since had been the long echo.

*You could give them the map,* the dead courier said quietly. It wasn't a suggestion. He said it the way a man says a hard thing to prove he isn't hiding behind her. *Walk into the Wayfarer's Rest, hand it over, tell them where you found me. They'd let you be. That's the bargain they offer everyone, even if no one knows they're offering it. Forget, and be forgotten, and live.*

"I know that too."

*And instead you're packing your grandmother's whetstones to walk into mountains that killed me.*

Emrys tied off the last bundle and sat back on her stool, and for a moment she let herself look at the workshop — the bench worn concave by her elbows, the rows of blanks, the little window where the rain came down the glass like slow glass tears. Thirty-one years she'd kept this room. It was the only place in the world shaped exactly like her.

"The dead man in the widow's charm told me something yesterday," she said. "Before the Choir came. He said his wife would hold the sparrow at night and feel him grumbling, and she'd smile, and she'd think the smiling was grief. And he said, let her." She turned the rose-carved femur over in her hands, feeling the map inside it like a second heartbeat. "That's what they do, your grey singers. They make everyone think the smiling is grief. They take the whole country and leave the whole country smiling at nothing. And everyone agrees to it because agreeing hurts less than remembering." She looked up at the ghost. "I've spent my life keeping other people's dead comfortable. I'm not going to stand in my own doorway and watch a whole nation get buried twice."

The courier was quiet a long moment. When he spoke, his voice had gone rough. *Your grandmother taught you mostly,* he said. *Who taught you the rest?*

"The dead," Emrys said. "They're thorough teachers. They've had practice."

Outside, the rain had stopped. Through the little window she could see the lamplit street, and the wet cobbles shining, and — at the corner, where the light didn't quite reach — a single grey figure standing motionless in the dark, face turned toward her workshop door.

Not one of the five. Younger. Sharper-faced. Standing in the rain-shadow with his hood down, making no attempt at all to hide, and when Emrys's eyes met his through the glass, he did not look away.

He raised one hand — slowly, deliberately, where the other Choir members could not see — and laid one finger against his lips.

*Be quiet,* the gesture said. *Be still. I won't sing you out.*

And then he was gone, folded back into the dark like a page turned, leaving Emrys alone with her packed bags, her hidden map, and the wild, dangerous, wholly unprecedented thought that somewhere inside the enemy's song, one voice had just gone off-key on purpose — for her.
