## 6. Sable

The Choir came to Thornfell on a Tuesday, which Emrys thought was typical of them. Catastrophe, in her experience, preferred Tuesdays and Thursdays—days too ordinary to be believed.

She saw them first from her stall in the market square, where she sold charms and buttons and the occasional honest trinket to people who mostly wanted the buttons. Three figures in grey robes, moving through the crowd at a pace that could only be described as *unhurried*, the way water is unhurried as it rises around your ankles.

They were asking questions. She knew this not because she heard them—the market noise swallowed everything—but because of what happened after they passed. Old Piet the fishmonger, who had once talked her ear off for forty minutes about the migratory habits of trout, stood blinking at his own stall like a man who had walked into a room and forgotten why. A woman by the well pressed her hand flat against her chest, frowning, as though checking that something was still there.

*They ask,* said the dead man in the mourning charm at her wrist—a retired cooper named Aldous who had been complaining about his funeral for three weeks now and had recently expanded into general commentary—*and then you forget they asked. That's the trick of it. Like a debt collected while you sleep.*

*I know,* Emrys murmured into her collar.

*You don't know. You're guessing. There's a difference. Guessing is how I ended up buried in the wrong coat.*

Emrys kept her head down and carved. Her knife moved through a piece of linden wood on pure habit, shaping nothing in particular, letting her hands do the thinking while her mind ran cold arithmetic. Three of them. Grey robes, no sigils—or sigils so plain they read as none. They would work outward from wherever the courier had died, which meant inward toward her workshop, which meant—

The map was hidden inside a carved femur on her shelf at home, wrapped in oilskin, nested in wax, looking for all the world like a very boring piece of religious folk art. It was the best hiding place she'd had time to devise, and it was terrible. If they touched it, if one of them held it in those patient grey hands, they would feel whatever the map felt. And the map felt like Vellichor. The map felt like a wound dressed as a country.

One of the Choir stopped at her stall.

Emrys's knife did not slip. This was, she would later reflect, the single greatest act of craftsmanship of her life: keeping the blade steady on the wood while every muscle in her body tried to bolt.

"Good morning," said the Choir member.

He had pushed back his hood. He was younger than she expected—young enough that the grey robe looked borrowed, a coat handed down from someone larger. Dark hair going silver early at the temples, which struck her as unfair; even his aging was overachieving. His face was unremarkable in the way of faces designed to be forgotten, except for his eyes, which were the color of rain about to decide something.

"Morning," Emrys said. "Charms? Buttons? I've got a lovely anti-wart pendant, very popular with the goat-keeping community."

Something flickered across his face. Not quite a smile. The memory of where a smile would go.

"You carve bones," he said.

"When the mood takes me. Mostly it's wood. Bones are temperamental."

"What do they say?"

And there it was. Not *do they say anything*—not the polite skepticism of a townsperson humoring the odd bone-carver—but *what do they say*, asked with the flat patience of a man reading an inventory list.

"They say," Emrys replied, "that I charge too much."

This time the flicker reached further, almost becoming an actual expression before he mastered it. He picked up a button from her tray—a plain horn button—and turned it over in his fingers with a delicacy that made her stomach clench. As if listening.

"These are goat," he said.

"Very good."

"I grew up around goats." He set the button down with the same care. "A long time ago."

"A long time ago," Emrys agreed pleasantly, "goats were everywhere. Still are, really. Very successful animal. No regrets."

He looked at her then. Properly looked, the way nobody in Thornfell ever bothered to look at her, because looking at people cost something and Emrys had spent thirty years teaching everyone that she wasn't worth the price. His gaze went through her performance of dullness like a knife through wet paper, and what he found underneath seemed to interest him enormously.

"The woman at the well," he said quietly. "Did you speak with her this morning?"

"No."

"The fishmonger?"

"I don't eat fish. It tastes like regret."

"That isn't an answer."

"It's the best kind," Emrys said. "An answer shaped exactly like no answer." She held his eyes, which was either very brave or very stupid, and she genuinely could not tell which, and the not-knowing made her feel more alive than she had in years, which frightened her more than the grey robes did. "Was there anything else? Only I've got a widow expecting a charm by noon, and the dead are very punctual when it comes to other people's grief."

For a moment—one long, suspended moment, in which the market noise seemed to lean away from them like a crowd giving a fight room—he simply studied her. Then he did something impossible.

He remembered her.

She knew it the way she knew weather coming: a pressure change, a wrongness in the air. Because when he turned away, he glanced back—not the glance of a man leaving a stall, but the glance of a man confirming a location. Marking her. Filing her somewhere the rest of his order apparently couldn't file things.

"Bone-carver," he said, as if testing the word. Then, softer, almost to himself: "Run."

And he walked away into the crowd, grey among greys, and old Piet blinked and resumed shouting about trout as though nothing had ever interrupted him, and Emrys sat very still with her heart hammering out a rhythm Aldous identified, unprompted, as *approximately the tempo of a man falling down stairs.*

*Well,* said the dead cooper. *That went well.*

*Shut up, Aldous.*

*He told you to run. In my professional opinion, when a man in a doom-robe tells you to run, you run. That's just arithmetic.*

Emrys looked down at her hands. They were steady. Of course they were steady. Her hands had been steady through worse than this.

But her heart was doing something new and unwelcome, which was remembering the exact color of a stranger's eyes, and she understood—with the particular dread of a woman whose entire survival strategy was being forgettable—that she had just been *seen*. Truly seen. By the one person in the world built to see.

*Oh no,* she said aloud, to no one.

*Oh yes,* said Aldous, with relish.

* * *

Sable had broken a rule.

Not a large one. Not yet. The rules of the Choir were like the rungs of a ladder: each one small, each one load-bearing, and you did not notice you were climbing until you looked down. Speaking to a subject outside the protocol of questioning was a minor infraction. Remembering a subject afterward was not minor at all.

He stood in the upper room of the tollhouse inn the Choir had commandeered, watching the market square through a gap in the shutters, and performed the internal audit that was as natural to him as breathing—which was fitting, since he could not remember ever learning to breathe either. Some things predated memory. Duty was one of them. It lived in him below the floor where the Cantor's song had scrubbed everything else away, load-bearing and dumb as a foundation stone.

Brother Corvane was cataloguing the day's harvest downstairs: small memories, gleanings, the stray recollections of a border town. Who had spoken to whom. Which faces had lingered near the courier's death-road. The Choir did not need much. A thread here, a thread there, and the pattern assembled itself in the Cantor's great loom, miles away, where the counter-song was always being woven.

The bone-carver was a thread. Sable had known it within four seconds of reaching her stall.

Her hands were wrong, first of all. Market women's hands were cracked and blunt. Hers were scarred but precise, knife-callused along the thumb in the pattern of someone who worked fine material for hours, and they hadn't paused—not once, not a tremor—when he'd set his attention on her like a lamp. Everyone trembled. Trembling was the body's honesty. Hers had lied to him fluently, cheerfully, offering him jokes about goats while her pulse hammered hard enough that he could see it in her throat.

And then there was the femur.

He had seen it yesterday, in the window of her workshop before the storm drove them to shelter: a carved bone in the folk style, all whorls and knotwork, utterly unremarkable except that it had been *warm*. He had passed within arm's reach of it, and the relic-sense the Choir trained into its singers—the sense that read memory the way tongues read texture—had brushed against it and found heat. Fresh heat. Grief-shaped heat.

Someone had been hiding something inside it. Something that burned.

*Sable.* Corvane's voice, from the stairwell. *The Cantor's office wants our preliminary findings.*

*Nothing yet,* Sable called back. *A border town. Everyone's memory is mud and turnip prices.*

There was a pause. Corvane appeared in the doorway, tall and dry as a winter stalk, his hood back, his face wearing the mild blankness that all senior singers wore—the face of men who had been emptied so thoroughly that emptiness had become a kind of serenity. Sable knew he wore the same face. He practiced it in mirrors. He was fairly sure he'd gotten good at it, although he had no memories against which to compare his progress.

*You spoke with the carver,* Corvane said.

It was not a question. Sable felt the ladder shift underfoot.

*Protocol requires contact with every vendor in the square.*

*Protocol requires questioning. You joked with her.* Corvane's voice never rose. That was the horror of the senior singers; they had been sanded down past anger, past impatience, into something smooth and terrible. *About goats.*

*Goats are disarming. Subjects volunteer more when disarmed.*

*Is that what she volunteered?*

*Turnip prices,* Sable said.

Corvane regarded him for a long moment. Outside, the market noise drifted up, thin and human and oblivious. Somewhere a child was laughing, and Sable found himself listening to it the way a starving man listens to a smell.

*The Cantor says the courier's errand ended in this valley,* Corvane said at last. *Which means the errand's cargo is still in this valley. When we find it, we will also find whoever received it. People who receive such cargo are not punished, Sable. They are corrected. Keep your curiosity on your side of the ledger.* He turned to go, then paused. *You are a good singer. Do not become an interesting one.*

When he was gone, Sable returned to the shutter gap.

The bone-carver was closing her stall. She moved efficiently, packing her trays, and she did not look up at the tollhouse even once, which was itself remarkable, because everyone looked at the tollhouse. Guilty people looked. Innocent people looked. Only people managing their fear with real discipline didn't look, and discipline like that belonged to smugglers, spies, and women with maps hidden in bones.

*Run,* he had told her. He didn't know why he'd said it. That was the trouble with having no past: your present came out of you sideways sometimes, unbidden, like water finding a crack in a wall. He had looked at her—this sharp, tired, funny woman who lied about fish with total commitment—and some part of him older than his memory had said *run* the way you'd warn someone of a fire, urgently, personally, as though her burning would cost him something.

Below, she shouldered her pack and started up the lane toward the workshops, small and straight-backed against the grey afternoon.

Sable took out his slate, on which the day's findings were to be recorded, and considered it.

Then he wrote: *Vendor interviews complete. Nothing of note. Town consistent with profile. Recommend proceeding to next valley.*

It was the first lie he had ever told the Choir. He examined the feeling of it the way he'd examine a strange coin—turning it over, testing its weight. It did not feel like shame. It did not feel like fear.

It felt, he decided, like the button. Goat horn, worn smooth, warm from her tray. Like something small and ordinary that had somehow survived a fire that took everything else.

Down in the lane, the bone-carver paused at the corner—just once, just briefly—and looked back at the tollhouse after all.

Their eyes met through the shutter gap. He was certain of it. Certain in the way he was certain of duty and breath and the color of rain: below memory, below proof.

She lifted two fingers off her pack strap. Not a wave. Barely a gesture. An acknowledgment of the ledger between them, and a promise to keep cooking the books.

Then she turned the corner and was gone, and Sable stood in the grey light holding his slate full of lies, and understood with perfect clarity that he had just become the most dangerous thing in Thornfell:

A Choir singer with a reason.

* * *

That night, Emrys packed by candlelight and did not sleep.

The map stayed in the femur. The femur stayed on the shelf, looking bored. She sharpened her knives, oiled her boots, and ate the last of the good cheese, because if she was going to die or flee, she refused to do it without cheese, a principle she recommended to everyone.

At midnight, the charm on her wrist began to whisper.

Not Aldous. Under Aldous's endless funeral complaints, beneath even his grudging commentary on her love life (*you blushed at a man in a doom-robe, girl, I've seen turnips with better survival instincts*), something else was coming through the bone. Thin. Far away. A chorus of voices singing a note that had no bottom to it.

Emrys held the charm to her ear, and the dead man inside it—the widow's husband, the one she'd carved the charm for, the one whose only complaint had been the eulogy—began, very softly, to scream.
