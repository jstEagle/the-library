## 7. The Widow's Charm

The trouble with carving a mourning charm for a man who wouldn't stop talking was that he wouldn't stop talking.

"He put the wrong flowers on the casket," Aldous Vetch grumbled from inside the river-stone of bone I'd shaped three days ago. "Wrong *flowers*, Emrys. Forty years married and the woman puts marigolds on my box. Marigolds. I told her a hundred times—snowdrops. Snowdrops or nothing."

"You're dead, Aldous," I said, sanding the edge of the charm with a scrap of chamois. "You don't get an opinion on flowers anymore."

"I have every opinion. Death is just life with worse seating." He rattled around in his stone like a pebble in a boot. "And she's wearing grey. Grey! I hated grey. She knows I hated grey. She wore it out of spite, I swear it. Forty years of marriage and she buries me in my least favorite color and the wrong flowers and that nephew of hers gave a eulogy that went on longer than my actual life—"

"Aldous."

"—and nobody cried properly. Old Tammo sniffed once, the great fraud, and then asked if there'd be food—"

"*Aldous.*" I set down the chamois. My workshop smelled of bone dust and lamp oil and the particular sourness of a man who had been complaining for three days without pause. Outside, the rain had settled into its autumn habit of falling sideways, and the gutters of Cindermarrow ran brown and loud. "Do you want me to finish this charm or not? Because I can leave you as a rough blank, and rough blanks echo. You'll hear yourself thinking. It's dreadful."

That shut him up for nearly four seconds.

"Fine," he said, sulky as a cat in a bath. "Finish it. But make it snowdrops somewhere. She'll know what it means even if she pretends not to."

I almost smiled. That was the trick of it, the thing nobody understood about the dead: they weren't ghosts of tragedy. Mostly they were ghosts of *opinion*. They wanted the flowers right, the debts settled, the insult at their wedding avenged. They clung to the small grievances because the big ones—the dying itself, mostly—were past arguing about.

I carved a single snowdrop into the face of the charm, fine as a fingernail paring, and felt Aldous go quiet in the way of the satisfied dead. The stone warmed under my hands. Somewhere across town, a widow would hang this at her door and feel her husband near, and she would never know he was near because he wanted to complain about marigolds.

That was my whole life, laid out plain: small bones, small voices, small mercies. Never involved. Never noticed. A rule I'd kept for twenty years, and it had kept me alive through two border wars, one plague scare, and the entire career of a smuggler named Dree Halfmark who owed me money in three currencies.

I wrapped the charm in wool, and that was when the screaming started.

* * *

It began as a sound like a kettle left too long—a thin rising note from the shelf where I kept finished work—and then Aldous's voice tore out of the wool wrapping at a pitch I had never heard from any dead man, living or otherwise.

"*They're coming up the hill. Emrys. EMRYS. They're taking them off the street—old Pemmy first, they took old Pemmy right off her own doorstep and now nobody's—listen to me, girl, LISTEN—*"

I dropped the chamois. Dead men didn't scream. In twenty years of hearing the dead, through charms and relics and the odd talkative gravestone, I had heard grief, rage, pettiness, boredom, and once a truly operatic complaint about a will. But never fear like this. Fear was a living thing's privilege. The dead were past it, the way they were past cold and hunger and marigolds.

"Aldous." I picked up the charm and held it close to my mouth like a lover. "What's coming up the hill?"

"They're grey." His voice shook, and stones shouldn't shake. "Grey robes, grey faces, walking soft as snowfall. They came through the low quarter an hour ago and they're *unmaking* people, Emrys. Not killing. Unmaking. Pemmy's door is still open and her bread is still warm and there is no Pemmy anymore. Her sister walked past her house and didn't go in. Didn't even look. There's nothing left of her to look AT—"

My hands had gone cold around the warm stone. Grey robes. I thought of the travelers who'd come through last week asking questions no one remembered being asked afterward. I thought of the oilskin map hidden beneath my floorboards, wrapped in tallow-cloth, humming faintly against my dreams. I thought of a young man in grey who had met my eyes across a market crowd and *remembered me afterward*, which should have been impossible, and whose warning—*run*—had been sitting in my chest like a swallowed coal ever since.

"How many?" I whispered.

"I can't see, I'm a rock, I'm a ROCK—" Aldous caught himself, gathered whatever a dead man gathers instead of breath. "The other charms are going quiet. Do you understand? One by one. The dead in them. Being pulled out like splinters. There was a woman's voice in the locket you made for the ferryman's daughter—gone. The soldier in the belt-buckle—gone. Something is eating them, Emrys, and it's working its way uphill toward your street."

I looked at my shelves. Thirty-odd pieces, each holding a passenger. My boarders. My congregation of complainers and mourners and one extremely persistent ghost of a tax collector who haunted a coin purse and narrated interest rates to anyone who'd listen.

"The tax man's gone," Aldous said quietly. "Just now. Like a candle pinched."

I sat down hard on my stool.

* * *

I did what any sensible person does when the impossible comes calling: I made tea, and I told myself stories.

It's a fever, I told myself, pouring water over the leaves with hands that only trembled a little. You've been breathing bone dust for twenty years. You dreamed of grey robes because you ate bad cheese. The map under your floor is a smuggler's forgery and the young man in grey was flirting, badly, and everything is ordinary.

Then I heard the bell over my shop door, and Marta Crane came in out of the rain, and the stories stopped helping.

Marta was my nearest neighbor—a broad, flour-dusted woman who baked bread that could raise the dead, though mercifully none of mine had ever tried it. She came in most mornings to complain about her husband Bren, who was slow, and sweet, and spent his days mending nets by the window where the whole street could watch him work and love him for it.

"Marta," I said. "Morning."

She blinked at me. Polite. Blank. The blink of a stranger deciding whether she knew me.

"You're the carver," she said slowly. "Aren't you. Up the hill."

"Marta, it's Emrys. We've shared a fence for eleven years. Your goat eats my rosemary weekly."

Something flickered behind her eyes—doubt, the awful polite doubt of someone being told they've forgotten something and having no way to check. "I—yes. Perhaps. I'm sorry, I'm..." She pressed a hand to her temple. "I've lost something. I keep feeling like I've lost something and I can't think what. I woke up this morning and the second chair at our table was pushed in and I couldn't tell you why we own a second chair."

The floor tilted. I gripped the counter.

"Marta. Where's Bren?"

"Bren," she repeated, tasting the name like a word from a foreign language. And then—this was the worst part, this was the part I would carry for the rest of my life—she smiled. Warmly. Kindly. As if I'd asked after the weather. "Lovely name. Is he family of yours?"

Behind her, through the fogged window, I could see her house. The window where Bren sat every day mending nets. The nets were gone from the sill. The chair was pushed in. The whole shape of him had been lifted out of the world so cleanly that the world hadn't even scarred over—it had simply closed, like water behind a stone.

Only the second chair remained. Some small stubborn fact the erasure had missed, the way a tide misses a single shell.

"Marta," I said, very gently, "go home. Bake something. When you're baking, you remember things. Trust me on that."

She thanked me—thanked me, a stranger—and stepped back out into the rain, and I watched her go up the hill to a house with a chair in it that meant nothing, and I stood in my workshop shaking.

From the shelf, Aldous said softly: "Now you believe me."

* * *

I took up the floorboards with a crowbar and my bare hands, tearing a nail halfway down my thumb and not caring. The oilskin bundle lay where I'd buried it, wrapped in tallow-cloth against damp and prying eyes. It seemed heavier than before. Maps don't gain weight. Guilt does.

I carried it to the table and unrolled it under the lamp, and the courier's ghost stirred in the corner where he'd been standing for three days, patient as furniture, waiting for me to be ready.

He'd been a young man when he died against my door—bleeding from a wound that made no noise and left no mark a surgeon could find, whispering *Vesper* like a prayer and pressing the map into my hands with the last of his strength. He hadn't crossed over. He couldn't, he said, until the errand was done. So he stood in my workshop, translucent and tired, and watched me decide.

"It's real," I said. My voice came out flat. "Vellichor. The Vesper Gate. All of it."

"You know it's real," he said. "You've known since the night I died. You just hoped knowing wasn't the same as believing."

"I hoped I could burn it and go back to marigolds."

"And now?"

I looked at the map. Roads drawn in ink that shifted when I wasn't looking directly at it, roads that bent toward a mountain pass that appeared on no survey, no ledger, no memory—roads only the dead remembered, the courier had said, and he'd been right, because when I let my gift brush the parchment I heard them: hundreds of voices, faint as breath on glass, murmuring the names of towns that weren't there. A whole country, whispering from underneath the world.

And upstairs and downhill, a woman was baking bread in a house with an empty chair, trying to remember why her heart hurt.

"They got Pemmy," I said. "Pemmy Osk, who paid me in eggs for twenty years and called me 'girl' when I was forty. They got her, and her sister walked past her open door. They got Bren Crane, who once carried my entire shipment of tools two miles through mud because my cart axle broke and never let me thank him properly. They're eating my town one person at a time and everyone left thinks the holes are normal." I pressed my palms flat against the map. "How long has the Choir been doing this?"

"Forty years," the courier said. "Since Vellichor. But they've never hunted a map before. They've never needed to hunt anything." He drifted closer, and his edges guttered like a candle in wind. "They're not here for the town, Emrys. They're here for you. The Cantor felt the map move. Every step you take toward that gate, they'll take two toward you, and they'll take apart everyone you've ever been kind to, looking for the road you're on."

"So I should run alone. Leave no trail. Forget Cindermarrow."

"Yes."

"And that stops them?"

"No," he admitted. "Nothing stops them. But it might slow them. It might save—" He stopped himself. Couriers are trained to deliver facts, not hope.

But I'd already done the arithmetic, the smuggler's arithmetic I was good at, the cold weighing of cargo and risk. Staying bought nothing. Marta was already hollowed. Pemmy was already gone. The Choir didn't punish involvement—they punished *witnesses*. My rule, my beautiful twenty-year rule of never getting involved, had only ever worked because I'd never mattered. Now I mattered, and invisibility was a currency I'd stopped carrying.

There was only one direction left with anything on the far side of it.

I began pulling tools from the walls. My finest knives. The roll of chamois. The little brass calipers my mother had—no. Not my mother. I reached for the memory of her teaching me to hold a blade and found... smoke. A warmth. A certainty that someone had loved me enough to teach me, and no face attached to it. I stood there with a knife in my hand, grieving a woman whose face I could not picture, and could not even say when I'd lost it, and that—that was the moment the grief hardened into something with edges.

"Emrys?" the courier said.

"They took her face too," I said. "Or the tollhouse did, or something did, somewhere along the line. Everyone's been robbing me my whole life and I only just started counting." I rolled the map tight and bound it with cord. "I'm going to the gate, courier. Not because I believe in countries. Because somebody has to walk into the Choir's song and break it over their knee, and apparently I'm the only person alive rude enough to try."

For the first time since he'd died on my doorstep, the young man smiled.

"What do I call you?" I asked, winding cord. "I can't keep thinking of you as 'the bleeding stranger.' It's undignified for both of us."

"Tam," he said. "Tam Vessic. Courier, third rank, out of—" He faltered. Looked down at his fading hands. "Out of somewhere. Isn't that strange. I can't remember my depot. I can remember my mother's laugh but not my depot."

"That's how it starts," I said grimly. "Hold onto the laugh."

* * *

Night came down on Cindermarrow like a lid. I worked by lamplight, packing with the economy of someone who'd fled places before, though never a whole life. Tools. Dry rations. The good boots. Aldous's charm—I hesitated over it, then tucked it into my coat's inner pocket, against the heart, where a stone could feel like a heartbeat.

"I'm coming with you, then," Aldous said. It wasn't a question.

"You're a mourning charm for a widow across town. You belong on her door."

"She hung me wrong anyway. Hook's crooked. And besides—" the stone warmed against my palm— "you'll want someone to complain at. You strike me as a woman about to do something stupid and heroic, and stupid-heroic types need a critic."

I laughed. It came out cracked, but it came out. "Fine. Boarder's rates. You carry your own weight."

"Emrys." His voice changed, went quiet in the way of the dead when they set aside their grievances, which was rare, and always meant something. "The grey ones are at the bottom of the hill. Three of them. Walking slow. They'll reach your street by morning."

I snuffed the lamp and stood in the dark, listening to the rain, and to the faintest thread of sound beneath it—far off, almost below hearing, a melody with no kindness in it. A choir, rehearsing.

"Then I leave tonight," I said.

I wrote no note. Who would read it? I left coin on Marta's windowsill for the fence my goat-eating neighbor's goat would never eat my rosemary over again, and I slipped out the back of the workshop into the wet dark, with a map full of whispering roads against my ribs, a complaining dead man over my heart, and a ghost named Tam walking beside me, pointing the way up into the mountains along a path that no living map had ever dared to draw.

Behind us, at the bottom of the hill, the singing grew louder.

Ahead, the passes waited—black, and full of the dead, and remembering everything.
