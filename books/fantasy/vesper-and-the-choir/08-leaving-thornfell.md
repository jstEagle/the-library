## 8. Leaving Thornfell

The workshop burned beautifully.

Emrys had expected it to hurt more. She had built the place herself, board by board, over eleven years—had planed the workbench smooth with her own hands, had hung the drying racks for bone shavings in exactly the right draft from the mountain crack above the door. She had thought she would stand there weeping while it went up like a struck match.

Instead she stood across the lane in the pre-dawn dark and felt something closer to relief.

"Well," said the ghost of the dead courier, drifting at her shoulder like a man leaning on a fence he couldn't quite touch. "That's one way to settle your accounts."

"Fire settles everything," Emrys said. "It's very thorough."

She had been careful about it. The fire was set to look like an accident—a lamp knocked against a shelf of linseed oil, a tragedy for the poor bone-carver woman, who everyone agreed worked too many hours alone. By morning the town would cluck its tongue and shake its head, and by afternoon they would have half-forgotten her, because that was what small towns did with grief that wasn't theirs. She'd left the widow's charm finished on the widow's doorstep, wrapped in brown paper with no note. The dead man inside it would keep complaining about his funeral arrangements for years yet. Someone would hear him. Not her.

Her tools were in the satchel against her hip: nine knives of varying temper, three rasps, a burnisher worn to the shape of her thumb, a whetstone her mother had carried before her. Everything else—the cot, the kettle, the forty-odd unfinished commissions, the good chair she'd never once sat in because sitting still made her itch—could go to ash.

"You're not going to take anything else?" the courier asked. "Sentimental objects? A lock of hair? People usually take a lock of hair."

"I'm taking my hands," Emrys said. "Everything else is replaceable."

"That's either very brave or very sad."

"It's arithmetic." She watched a rafter collapse inward, sending up a fistful of sparks that the wind took east, toward the passes. "A life is just a pile of things you've decided you can't live without. I've decided differently. It's cheaper than grieving."

The courier was quiet a moment. Dead men were often quiet when the living said things too true too fast; she'd noticed that over the years. They had all the time in the world to be honest and it embarrassed them anyway.

"The map's safe?" he asked finally.

Emrys patted her chest, where the carved femur hung on a cord beneath her coat. Inside its hollow core, rolled tight as a wasp's nest, the oilskin map rode against her heartbeat. The Choir had come through town asking their soft questions and left without remembering they'd asked them, but she didn't trust that silence to hold. Silence was just a held breath. Sooner or later the breath came out as song.

"Safe," she said. "Let's go find your country."

* * *

The high passes above Thornfell were hers in a way nothing else had ever been. She knew them the way other people knew their own kitchens—which scree slopes sang hollow underfoot before they slid, which snowfields lied about their depth, where the goats grazed in the third week of thaw so you could follow the droppings like signposts. For six years she had run contraband through these mountains: salt, silk, unlicensed prayer-books, once an entire disassembled printing press packed into cheese wheels. The smugglers called her the Magpie because she could find any route through the stone, and because she never talked, and because if you tried to cheat her she would know, somehow, and say nothing, and simply never guide you again, which in the Thornfells was a death sentence delivered politely.

Now she climbed those same routes with a dead man chattering in her ear, and found—to her considerable irritation—that she didn't hate it.

"—and the thing is," the courier was saying as they picked their way up a goat track above the tree line, "nobody remembers Vellichor, but everybody misses it. That's the part I can't get over. You'll be walking through some valley and there'll be a bridge going nowhere, or a road that stops at a cliff face, and people will build shrines to them without knowing why. Little cairns. Offerings. They think they're honoring spirits." He laughed, a dry sound like paper folding. "They're honoring a border crossing."

"Which is why the map follows roads that don't exist," Emrys said. "You told me. Roads only the dead remember."

"And a few of us living ones who were stubborn about it." He sobered. His form flickered, the way it did when he thought too hard about being dead. "I carried messages for the remnant families for twenty years, Thackeray. Names, birthdays, recipes. Little bits of a country kept alive in pockets. The Choir calls it sedition. My grandmother called it Sunday dinner."

Ahead, the pass narrowed between two shoulders of granite, and the wind funneled through with a low moan that sounded almost like singing. Emrys stopped. Her hand went to the knife at her belt out of pure habit.

"That sound," she said. "Is that—"

"Just wind," the courier said. But he said it quickly, and ghosts who lied quickly were worse than men who did.

They went through the narrows single file, Emrys leading, and on the far side the land opened into a hanging valley where the dawn had finally arrived, gilding the snowfields pink and gold. Below them, tiny as toys, the rooftops of Thornfell smoked peacefully. One plume rose thicker than the rest, grey-black, already thinning.

Her workshop. Or what remained of the smoke of it.

Emrys stood looking down at it longer than she meant to. Eleven years. The widow whose charm she'd carved. The neighbor boy she'd taught to whistle through cupped hands. The innkeeper who always gave her the corner table and never asked why she ate facing the door. None of them knew her name beyond *the carver*, and now even that would fade, and she had chosen this, had lit the lamp herself—

"You're allowed to feel bad about it," the courier offered.

"I'm aware of what I'm allowed to do."

He made a noise that in a living man would have been a snort. "You remind me of my grandmother. She fled Vellichor with nothing but a spoon. A spoon! Wouldn't leave it behind. When I asked her why, she said, 'Because they took everything else, and I'll be damned if they teach me how little I need.'" He drifted ahead toward the ridge line. "Anyway. The pass forks ahead. Left goes down to the river road, which is fast and watched. Right goes up along the old causeway, which doesn't exist anymore, which is why we're taking it."

Emrys took one last look at the smoke, then turned her back on it and followed the dead man up.

* * *

Sable knelt in the wet grass behind the shrine at the top of Widow's Stair, and watched the bone-carver climb out of her own life.

He had been awake all night, which was unusual; the Choir slept little, but they slept evenly, like well-tuned instruments resting between performances. Tonight his tuning had been off. He had felt it first as a wrongness in the town's hum—Thornfell's collective memory was a chord, faint and constant, and somewhere in it a note had gone sharp. He had followed the sharpness through the sleeping streets to the burning workshop, and then up the goat tracks, tracking a woman who moved through dangerous ground with the unhurried certainty of someone walking to her own kitchen.

*Subject is mobile,* he should report. *Heading northeast via the high passes. Request intercept at the Tollhouse.* Fourteen words. He had composed them four times in his head, each time watching her figure shrink against the snowfield, each time not saying them.

The trouble was the market. Three days ago, in the market, she had looked at him. Not past him, not through him—at him, with the particular flat assessment of someone counting exits, and something in Sable had turned over like a stone in a streambed. And afterward, impossibly, he had remembered her. The Choir's grace kept every face soft in the memory, every encounter dissolving by morning like frost on glass. But her face stayed. Grey eyes. A smudge of bone dust on her cheekbone. The way she'd looked at him as though he were a knot in the wood she was deciding whether to carve around.

He had told himself it was a flaw in the grace. He had told himself he would report it. He had done neither.

Below and behind him, his partner Corvane was making slow progress up the stair, hampered by dignity and a bad knee. Corvane believed the subject was still in town. Sable had told him so last night, carefully, weaving the lie into the shape of an observation: *her stall was stocked this morning; she wouldn't abandon inventory.* It had been shockingly easy. The lie had come out of his mouth smooth as water, and no binding had flared, no discipline had gripped his tongue, because the Choir's oaths bound memory, not honesty. They had never needed to bind honesty. No singer had ever wanted to lie.

Until, apparently, now.

Sable watched Emrys Thackeray reach the ridge line and pause there, a dark speck against the brightening snow. For a moment—absurdly—he thought she looked back. At the town, probably. At her smoke. Not at him; she couldn't possibly have seen him kneeling in the grass like a penitent. But the pause lasted a heartbeat too long, and he felt seen anyway, thoroughly and specifically, and then she turned and was gone over the crest, and the mountain took her the way the mountain took everything: completely, without comment.

"Thackeray," he said aloud, testing the name against the morning air. It stayed. That was the other impossible thing. He had spoken it last night, quietly, in his room, and the word had not dissolved. Names were the first thing the grace took—you forgot your own within a year of initiation—but this name sat in his mouth like a coin, solid, spendable, *hers.*

Corvane's boots crunched on the path below. Sable rose, brushed the dew from his grey robe, and arranged his face into the patient blankness the Choir wore like vestments.

"Anything?" Corvane puffed, arriving.

"She went to ground," Sable said. "Probably a cellar on Tanner's Row. The locals are fond of her; they'll hide her a day or two out of habit, then give her up."

Corvane nodded slowly, satisfied, and began composing his report in the murmuring cadence singers used for dictation. Sable listened to the false picture assemble itself—cellar, Tanner's Row, a day or two—and felt the strangest sensation, warm and terrible at once, spreading through his hollow chest.

It was, he realized, the feeling of having something to lose.

* * *

By midday Emrys was above the tree line for good, and the world had become white rock, blue shadow, and wind. The courier led her along a shelf of stone that her eyes insisted ended in empty air and her feet insisted otherwise. Where he walked, the ground remembered being a road: the scree lay in suspiciously straight lines, and twice her boot found the edge of a paving stone worn smooth by centuries of cartwheels, jutting from the hillside like a whale's rib.

"Careful here," the courier said, hovering over a gap where the causeway had fallen away entirely. "There's a bridge. Was a bridge. The Kettlewright family maintained it for two hundred years and then everyone forgot the Kettlewrights existed and it fell down out of sheer loneliness."

"So I jump?"

"You jump. I'll catch you."

"You can't catch anything. You're a rumor with opinions."

"Jump with confidence, then. Confidence catches."

Emrys measured the gap—eight feet, maybe nine, with a drop into fog below—and thought about the map against her heart, and the tools on her hip, and the smoke she could no longer see behind her. Then she backed up three steps, ran, and jumped.

For one lurching moment there was nothing under her, and the fog below looked like the inside of forgetting itself. Then her boots hit stone—real stone, solid, ancient—and she staggered forward onto the far side of a bridge that did not exist, laughing despite herself, a short startled bark of laughter that the wind stole immediately.

"Told you," the courier said smugly.

"Shut up."

But she was smiling as she shouldered her satchel and started up the far slope, into the high passes, along roads only the dead remembered, toward a country that no longer existed and a gate that might not open—and behind her, far below and growing smaller with every step, the whole remembered world fell away like a coat she had finally, finally taken off.

She did not see the grey figure on the ridge behind her, standing motionless in the wind, watching her go.

She did not need to. Somewhere in the hollow of a carved bone, against her heart, the map seemed to grow very slightly warmer—as if it, too, knew that somewhere back down that mountain, a man was choosing, for the first time in his emptied life, to lie.
