## 9. Roads Only the Dead Remember

The ghost's name was Aldous Finch, and he had been dead for six days and complaining about it for five.

"You're walking too fast," he said, drifting a half-length behind her left shoulder the way he'd apparently decided was his permanent post. "In my day, couriers were given consideration. Fresh horses. Hot meals. A decent burial."

"In your day," Emrys said, "you died in a stranger's doorway with your blood freezing on her floorboards."

"And whose fault is that? I asked for a doctor."

"You asked for a priest. Then you asked me to carry a map that gets people killed." She adjusted the strap of her pack where it bit into her shoulder. The morning was high and cold, the kind of mountain light that made everything look newly minted. "I'm still deciding whether to thank you or haunt *you*."

"Can ghosts be haunted?" Aldous considered this with genuine scholarly interest. "Because if so, I know several people I'd like to send round."

She almost smiled. That was the trouble with him. Six days of his company and she'd stopped flinching when he spoke from behind her, which was its own kind of intimacy, and intimacy was the thing she had spent thirty-one years avoiding with the discipline of a nun.

The map lay against her ribs, wrapped in oilskin, hidden inside the hollowed femur of — she preferred not to think too hard about whose femur. It had been a fair trade at the time. Now, three days out of Thornfell, following a line of ink that bent where no road should bend, she thought about it constantly.

"Here," Aldous said suddenly, all the humor gone out of him. "Stop here."

They stood on a shelf of grey scree above a river valley. Below them: boulders, pine, a stream running silver over slate. Nothing else. She'd walked this country her whole life. There was no road here. There had never been a road here.

"Finch," she said carefully, "there is no road here."

"There is," he said. "You just can't see it yet. Neither could I, when I was alive. That was rather the point." He drifted forward to the edge of the scree and looked down at the empty valley with an expression she hadn't seen on him before — not complaint, not fear. Longing. "Vellichor didn't hide itself with walls, bone-carver. It hid itself the way you hide a letter you don't want found. You put it somewhere only certain eyes can read."

"I don't have certain eyes. I have ears full of dead men grumbling."

"That," said Aldous, "is exactly what makes them certain."

* * *

He made her sit. He was very insistent about the sitting.

"Close your eyes," he said.

"No."

"Emrys Thackeray. You are sitting on a mountainside holding a map to a country that officially does not exist, guided by a man who is currently more theoretical than you are. At what point exactly did your standards for strange requests collapse?"

She closed her eyes.

"Now listen. Not with those ears. With the other ones. The ones you use when a widow brings you her husband's knucklebones and you pretend you're reading the grain of the bone instead of listening to him complain about the eulogy."

"The eulogy *was* bad," Emrys muttered. "He'd rehearsed better ones himself."

"Listen," Aldous said again, gentler now.

So she did what she had spent her whole life doing carefully, quietly, in back rooms with the shutters drawn — but never like this, never wide open, never letting the whole world in at once. She let down every latch she owned.

The mountain roared.

Not sound, exactly. Sound was only the top layer of it, the way skin is the top layer of a body. Underneath: the stream remembering every stone it had ever rounded. The pines keeping count of storms. The scree beneath her settling by fractions, muttering in its sleep. And under all of that, thin as wire, patient as roots —

A road.

She felt it before she understood it: a long straight intention running through the valley below, the way a scar runs through skin. Something had been there once. Wheels had worn it. Feet had packed it. Ten thousand small departures and arrivals had pressed themselves into the earth so deeply that even forty years of forgetting couldn't quite rub them out.

"Oh," she said.

"Yes," said Aldous.

"It's *still there*."

"Roads are stubborn things. People forget countries. Roads take it personally." His voice had gone soft. "Open your eyes now. Slowly."

She opened them.

For one heartbeat the valley was as it had been — rock and water and pine, indifferent, ordinary. And then her ears caught up with her eyes, the way they sometimes did with the dead, and the valley *shifted*.

It was subtle. That was the horror and the wonder of it. A line of paler stone winding along the far slope where the scree looked wrong somehow, too regular, too purposeful. A gap in the treeline that wasn't a gap until you looked at it sideways. The stream bending around a shape that might have been a bridge footing, if bridges had ever stood in valleys that had none.

And then — for less than a breath — more than that.

Lamplight. Warm yellow squares of it hanging in the air above the far bank, where no house stood. The smell of bread, impossibly specific, rye and caraway, drifting up the slope from nowhere. A bell, one clear note, rung by a hand forty years dead. And voices — not the dead's voices, not the murmuring she knew, but living ones, laughing, arguing about the price of wool, calling a child home for supper.

Then it was gone. The valley was empty. Her eyes stung.

"Aldous," she whispered. "What was that?"

"Vellichor," he said. "Or the ghost of it. The country's been erased, but erasing isn't killing. It's more like..." He searched for it. "More like when you carve a name off a headstone and sand the stone smooth. The letters are gone. But the stone remembers being carved. If you hold it right, in the right light—" He gestured at the valley. "Right light."

Emrys sat down properly, because her legs had opinions about standing.

* * *

They walked the road all afternoon, and the road — she could not stop marveling at this — held.

It shouldn't have. She knew the physics of the world the way a carver knows bone: solid, predictable, indifferent. But the vanished road bore her weight. Where it crossed the stream, stepping-stones rose just above the waterline, mossy and sure, though she'd have sworn an hour ago that the crossing was bare slick rock. Where it climbed, the grade eased, as if the land itself remembered being kinder here.

"Is it doing this," she asked, "or am I?"

"Both. Neither." Aldous floated along beside her, hands clasped behind his back in the posture of a man strolling to market, which he maintained even uphill, out of what she suspected was pure vanity. "The road exists because people remember walking it. You exist on it because you can hear them remembering. Put those together and—" he spread his hands "—you get a road."

"Whose memories?"

"Everyone's. No one's. The dead keep what the living throw away, bone-carver. Every forgotten thing has to go *somewhere*." He glanced at her sidelong. "You of all people should know that. What do you think you've been hearing your whole career?"

She had no answer for that. She walked.

By late afternoon the overlays came faster, as if the road, having decided she could bear it, stopped being gentle. A field of bracken became, in flickers, a field of barley, gold and heavy-headed, with scarecrows that moved between blinks. A stand of birches wore lanterns. Once — only once — she saw a person: a woman in a blue shawl leaning on a fence rail that wasn't there, watching Emrys pass with an expression of mild, unhurried curiosity, as if travelers on the old road were common enough not to excite comment. Then bracken. Then nothing.

"She saw me," Emrys said. Her voice came out strange. "That woman. She *looked* at me."

"Memory looking at memory," Aldous said. "Don't fret. Or do. Everyone else does."

But it wasn't fright that sat in her chest. That was the alarming part. All her life, the dead had come to her as need — grief, grievance, unfinished business, the whole exhausting catalogue of human leftovers. She had built her rules around that need. *Never get involved. Take the coin, carve the charm, close the shutter.* Need was a debt, and debts collected.

This was different. This woman in the blue shawl hadn't needed anything. She'd just been *there*, leaning on her rail in the evening light, part of a country going about its business — and the business had included, apparently, watching strangers walk its roads. Vellichor hadn't reached out to her. It had simply failed to notice it was dead.

Something in Emrys's chest, something old and locked and load-bearing, shifted half an inch.

"Don't," Aldous said quietly.

"Don't what?"

"Don't fall in love with it. I watched them do the erasure, Thackeray. I carried dispatches about it for years before I understood what I was carrying. Whatever you're feeling right now — the warmth, the *rightness* — remember that someone wound that feeling around a trap." He was silent a moment. "Remember why I'm dead."

"I try not to think about it. It makes me angry, and anger makes me clumsy."

"Good," he said. "Stay angry. It suits you."

* * *

They camped where the road widened into what had clearly once been a resting place — a flat shelf above the water, ringed with stones that might have been a wall once, or seats, or graves. Emrys got a fire going small and smokeless, the way smugglers do, and ate hard bread and dried plums while Aldous hovered at the edge of the firelight, unable to warm himself and pretending not to mind.

"Do you miss it?" she asked. "Being alive. Eating. You always used to complain about the inns, but you complain differently about things you've lost."

He was quiet long enough that she thought he wouldn't answer.

"I miss the boring parts," he said finally. "Nobody tells you that. Dying, everyone assumes you'll miss the big things — your wedding, your mother, the war you survived. I miss breakfast. I miss being cold and then getting warm. I miss being tired at the end of a day and knowing the tired meant something." He looked at the fire with its light passing straight through him. "I miss being *finished*. Alive, you finish things. Meals. Letters. Days. Dead, everything's open. My errand's still open. That's why I'm here, I expect. Can't cross over with a delivery undelivered."

"You delivered it. I have the map."

"You have the map." He shook his head. "The errand isn't the map, Thackeray. The errand is what the map's *for*. I don't get to stop until somebody opens that gate. So." He attempted a smile. It nearly worked. "Until then, you're stuck with me."

Emrys looked at him across the fire — this fussy, brave, dead man who had run a message through a country that didn't exist because someone had to — and felt the second dangerous feeling of the day, the one she recognized even less than the first.

"I'll open it," she said. "Whatever it costs. I'll see your errand finished."

"I know," said Aldous Finch. "That's what worries me."

* * *

She dreamed of the road that night, but in the dream it ran through Thornfell, past her workshop, past the widow Marta's door, and every window glowed with that same warm lamplight, and the woman in the blue shawl leaned on Marta's fence and said, in a voice like caraway and rye: *You're late. Supper's been ready for forty years.*

She woke before dawn with frost on her blanket and the certainty, absolute and unreasonable, that she was going home — not to Thornfell, but to some home older than any she'd owned. She lay still and let the feeling pass, the way you let a cramp pass, breathing through it.

When the light came grey through the pines, Aldous was already up — up being a relative condition — standing at the edge of the campsite and staring down the road with his whole insubstantial body rigid.

"What," she said, sitting up.

"Smoke," he said.

She joined him. Far down the valley, where the vanished road bent north toward a saddle she couldn't name, a thread of smoke rose against the pale sky. Ordinary smoke. Woodsmoke. Someone was camping on a road that did not exist, in a country that had never been there.

"Travelers?" she said. "Pilgrims? Other people who can hear it?"

"Maybe," Aldous said. But his voice had gone careful, the way it went careful whenever the Choir came up, and Emrys had learned in six days to hear what he didn't say as clearly as what he did.

There was a second thread of smoke, further back along the road behind them. And a third, off to the west, where no road ran at all.

Three fires. Spaced like a net.

"They're not following us," Emrys said slowly. "They're *ahead* of us. They know the route."

"Then we have a problem," Aldous said, "because the only way forward goes through that valley. And half a mile past those fires, bone-carver, sits the tollhouse." He turned to look at her, and for once there was no humor in him at all. "The dead on this road don't take coin. They take memories. And I have a terrible feeling they're going to want the ones you can least afford to lose."
