## 10. The Tollhouse of Names

The road did something wrong at dawn.

Emrys had been walking it for two days by then, following the courier's ghost along a track that existed only because he remembered it, and she had learned to trust the strange logic of it—the way the pines leaned back to let them pass, the way her boots left prints in moss that no living foot had pressed in forty years. But at dawn the road simply stopped being sure of itself. It forked where there was no fork, then un-forked, then forked again, like a man changing his mind mid-sentence.

"She's nervous," Corwin said.

He drifted ahead of her, translucent as breath on glass, still wearing the mud of the ditch he'd died in. In life he must have been a cheerful sort of man; death hadn't managed to grind the cheer out of him entirely, though it had thinned it, the way rain thins paint.

"Roads aren't nervous," Emrys said.

"This one is. She remembers being paid from." He pointed with a hand that was more suggestion than hand. "There."

Emrys saw it then, down in the fold of the valley: a tollhouse. Or what was left of one. A squat stone arch straddling the vanished road, its roof long gone, its walls furred with lichen the color of old bruises. Beyond it the land fell away into cloud, and beyond *that*, if Corwin's memory could be believed, lay three hundred miles of country that had never happened.

She stopped walking.

"I know that look," Corwin said. "That's the look of a woman doing arithmetic she doesn't like."

"What's the toll?"

"You already know."

She did. The dead had been murmuring about it since the pass—dead things gossiping the way birds do before weather turns. *The tollhouse takes. The tollhouse keeps. Don't go through empty-handed and don't go through full-hearted.* Dead advice was always riddles wrapped in warnings wrapped in more riddles, but this one had come through clear enough.

"It takes memories," she said.

"Aye." Corwin's cheer flickered. "And I'll not pretend otherwise, because you'd hear it from them anyway, and they enjoy telling it."

"They?"

"The keepers. Come on. Better to meet them standing up."

* * *

The tollhouse smelled of cold stone and, strangely, of beeswax, as though someone had polished every surface within the last hour and then left forever. Emrys stepped under the arch and felt the change immediately—a pressure against her skin like wading into deep water, and beneath her feet, faintly, a hum. Not sound exactly. More like the memory of sound, the way a bell keeps ringing in the bone after the ear gives up.

They rose out of the floor.

Not rising, quite. Unfolding. Figures made of the same stuff as mist over a grave, grey-green and patient, and there were nine of them, arranged in a crescent, and each of them wore the shape of a person who had once stood at this arch and asked travelers a question. Their faces were smooth as river stones. Where eyes should have been there were only shallow hollows, and yet Emrys felt seen—felt *inventoried*—down to the fillings in her teeth.

The center figure spoke. Its voice was many voices speaking slightly out of step, a choir rehearsing in different rooms.

*A traveler.*

"That's me," Emrys said. Her voice came out steadier than she deserved credit for.

*The road past this point is not a road. It is a remembering. And rememberings are not given freely.* A pause, courteous as a bowed head. *What will you pay?*

"I don't—" She stopped. Lied badly. Tried again. "I don't have money worth anything here."

*We do not take coin.* The nine figures shifted, almost gently. *Coin is only memory wearing a coat. We take the thing itself.*

Corwin drifted close to her shoulder. "You can turn back," he said quietly. "I want you to know that's true. The map leads through here, but turning back is always true. It's the one door nobody locks."

She looked at him. Forty years dead and still doing his errand, still carrying other people's business like a man carries fire in a wind. If he could spend four decades on a promise, she could spend one memory.

Except.

Except it wasn't *one* memory. That was the trap, wasn't it? You didn't get to choose which memory they took. You chose what you *offered*, and they chose what they *kept*. She thought of the widow's charm back in Thornfell, the dead husband grumbling inside the carved bone about the cheap wine at his own funeral, and how she'd known him—known his whole small, stubborn, ridiculous self—in an afternoon of listening. Memories weren't coins. They were load-bearing walls. Pull one out and you never knew what the house would do.

"What are my options?" she asked.

*Whatever you hold dear enough to hurt,* said the keepers, and there was no cruelty in it. That was somehow worse. It was arithmetic. Pure, patient, impersonal arithmetic. *Grief is the measure of value. What costs nothing, buys nothing.*

Emrys sat down on the cold threshold, because her legs had opinions, and thought.

Her mother had been dead eleven years. Fever, quick and ugly, in a winter Emrys preferred not to revisit. And here was the terrible thing she discovered while sitting in the ruins of a tollhouse at the edge of a country that didn't exist: when she reached for her mother, the first thing that came was not her voice, or her hands, or the way she'd said Emrys's name like it was a prayer she was embarrassed to be caught saying.

It was her face.

Specifically: her mother laughing. That particular laugh, the rare one, the one that cracked through the worry like sun through shutters—and the face that went with it, eyes creased nearly shut, a gap where a tooth had been, the flush across the cheekbones. Emrys had carried that face through every dark year like a lamp. When the carving went badly. When the loneliness got teeth. When she woke at night in the workshop with frost on the windows and no reason to get up, she had that laugh-face, and it was enough. It had always been enough.

If she offered anything else—if she offered the smell of bread, or her mother's habit of humming off-key, or even the memory of dying, which God knew she'd gladly be rid of—the keepers might take the face anyway. They chose. They'd said so. *What costs nothing, buys nothing.* They would weigh what she gave against what she kept, and they would keep the heavier thing, because that was the toll, that was the whole point of the toll: it had to cost.

Or.

Or she offered the face herself. Named it. Put it on the table with her own hands, so that whatever else they might have taken stayed safe behind the wall of her deliberate grief.

"There's a memory," Emrys said. Her mouth had gone dry as chalk dust. "My mother. Laughing. Her face when she laughed."

The nine figures went very still, the way water goes still before it freezes.

*You would give us this freely?*

"No," Emrys said honestly. "I'd give it because you'd take worse. But yes. Freely enough."

Something passed through the crescent of them—not laughter, nothing so warm, but a ripple, a recognition. Perhaps approval. Perhaps only hunger adjusting its collar.

*It is accepted.*

"Wait." Corwin moved between her and the keepers, insubstantial as smoke and twice as useless. "Girl, wait, there are other roads—"

"There aren't, and you know it."

"There are *slower* ones—"

"Corwin." She said his name gently, the way you'd gentle a spooked horse. "I've spent my whole life keeping everything I love locked in a box so small I could carry it. This is the first time the box has ever bought me anything." She stood. Her knees held. She was quietly proud of them. "Stand aside, dead man. Let me pay my toll."

* * *

Afterward, she would try to describe it, and fail, and try again, and fail better.

It did not hurt. That was the horror of it. The keepers did not reach into her skull with hooks or claws; they simply *asked*, and her memory—her treacherous, obedient memory—rose up to meet them like a dog hearing its name. She felt her mother's laugh-face lift out of her the way a splinter lifts when the flesh around it finally softens. One moment it was hers. Then it was leaving. Then—

Then there was a hole.

Not darkness. Darkness would have been kind. A hole, shaped exactly like a woman laughing, and around the hole, all the edges of everything else: her mother's voice, her mother's hands, the fever-winter, the funeral, the off-key humming—all intact, all present, all pointing at the space where the face should have been and finding nothing there. It was like losing a tooth. Her tongue kept going to the gap. Her mind kept going to the gap. *Mother laughed and her face was—*

Nothing.

Emrys made a sound she was not proud of. Somewhere between a word and a wound.

She was on her knees on the threshold—she didn't remember getting there—and Corwin's ghost-hands hovered near her shoulders, unable to touch, doing their best anyway, the way people do.

"The rest of her," she whispered. "I still have the rest of her."

*You do,* said the keepers, and for the first time their chorus-voice sounded almost kind. Almost sorry. *This is why we take what is dearest. So that what remains is loved harder.*

"That's not comfort. That's just arithmetic with a ribbon on it."

*Yes,* they agreed placidly. *Most truth is.*

They unfolded back into the floor, or the floor folded up around them, and the pressure lifted, and the hum under the stones faded to nothing, and Emrys knelt alone in a ruined tollhouse with a hole in her heart shaped like sunlight through shutters.

She cried. She was not ashamed of it—or rather, she was ashamed of it, thoroughly and immediately, and cried anyway, because some things outrank pride. She cried for the face and for the woman who'd worn it and for the girl she'd been who'd hoarded that face like a miser hoards gold and never once, not once, told her mother she was looking. That you're beautiful when you laugh, Mum. That I keep it. That it's the lamp.

Too late. Eleven years too late, and now later than that.

* * *

They walked on.

The road past the tollhouse was different—she felt it in her teeth, in the soles of her feet. Before, it had been a track that remembered being real. Now it was a remembering, just as the keepers had said, and walkable the way a dream is walkable: the pines were pines, but sometimes they were also columns, white marble veined with gold, and the cloud below the path was sometimes cloud and sometimes the glittering roofs of a city that caught the morning light like a spilled jewelry box. Vellichor, bleeding through. The dead country showing its bones.

Emrys walked and grieved and walked.

Corwin kept pace beside her, quieter than she'd ever known him. After a mile or so he cleared a throat he no longer had.

"My wife used to sing," he offered, to the landscape generally. "Badly. Enthusiastically. Like a cat in a sack, my daughter said." A pause. "I can't recall her face either. Not anymore. Been too long." Another pause. "So I'm telling you: it doesn't stay this sharp. The missing. It goes dull. Like everything."

"That's supposed to help?"

"No," Corwin admitted. "But it's true, and true things are the only currency I've got left."

Despite herself—despite the hole, despite the ache that flared every time her mind went probing at the gap like a tongue at a broken tooth—Emrys huffed something that was almost a laugh.

"Your daughter sounds like she had your tact."

"She had my everything. Best thing I ever made, and I wasn't even the craftsman." He drifted a little ahead, gesturing at the half-marble, half-pine world. "Look there. See it? The gatehouse. My grandfather sold apples under that arch, back when it was an arch instead of a rumor."

Emrys looked. For a heartbeat she saw it: a fat man in a stained apron, apples pyramided on a barrow, a city roaring gently behind him. Then the vision folded away and it was only lichen and ruin and morning fog.

"He looks happy," she said.

"He was. Terrible businessman. Gave away half his stock." Corwin's voice had gone soft. "That's the thing they don't tell you about erased countries, girl. They don't erase the happiness. Happiness soaks into stone. It waits. It's the most patient thing there is."

They walked on through the patient happiness of a dead nation, and Emrys found, to her surprise, that the hole in her was still there but no longer the only thing in her. Around its edges, other things crowded in: the weight of her tools against her back, the map dry and safe in its oilskin, the absurd courage of a dead courier who'd spent forty years on an errand. And underneath it all, new and unwelcome and growing:

Resolve. Hardening like candle wax.

Because she had paid the toll, and the toll had teeth, and now she knew—knew in her knees, in her banked and furious heart—that somewhere ahead, someone had decided that a whole country's worth of faces was worth less than a bargain. Someone had weighed every mother's laugh in Vellichor and found it affordable.

She meant to find that someone.

The road bent eastward, toward a smudge of ruins on the far ridge, and Corwin stiffened—or did whatever a ghost does instead—and pointed with his not-quite-hand.

"There," he said. "Do you see the smoke?"

Emrys squinted. There was smoke, thin and domestic, rising from the ruin. Which was impossible, because no one lived in a country that didn't exist.

Unless, she thought, someone had made it their life's work to disagree.

"Someone's home," she said slowly.

"Not just someone." Corwin's grin came back, thin and sharp as a carving knife. "Girl, I think you've found the cartographer's daughter."
