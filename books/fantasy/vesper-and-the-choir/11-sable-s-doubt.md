## 11. Sable's Doubt

The Choir's waystation at Greyfall Pass had once been a shepherd's hut. Sable knew this because the hut remembered being a shepherd's hut, and the Choir's singers could hear buildings the way other men heard weather. The stones still hummed faintly of wool-smoke and someone's grandmother cursing a stubborn ewe. It was one of the few honest sounds left in the mountains.

He sat at the plank table with his report half-written and his quill hovering like a bird that had forgotten which direction south was.

*Subject observed departing Thornfell by the eastern passes,* he wrote.

This was true in the sense that east was a direction and she had departed. It was false in every particular that mattered. Emrys Thackeray had gone north-northwest, into the high country where the roads were only rumors and the snow didn't bother melting until July. He knew this because he had watched her go from the ridge above her burning workshop, and because he had then spent an entire night lying to himself about why he hadn't reported it immediately.

The eastern passes, then. The eastern passes were a week's hard march from anywhere she actually was, full of switchbacks and rockfall and one particularly vindictive goat that Head-Singer Corvane's hunters would deserve.

*Subject travels alone,* he added. *No allies apparent.*

Also true, if you didn't count the dead man walking beside her, which technically Sable did not, because the dead were not on any roster the Choir kept. Though he suspected that was a loophole the Grand Cantor would close personally, and painfully, if he ever learned of it.

Sable set down the quill and rubbed his eyes. Behind him, the hut's stones hummed their little song of sheep. He found himself humming along before he caught himself and stopped, embarrassed even though no one was watching.

Someone was watching. There was always someone watching. That was rather the point of the Choir.

* * *

Head-Singer Corvane arrived at dusk, as punctual as frost.

She was a tall woman with a voice like a cellar door — low, heavy, and opening onto darkness. Her grey robes were immaculate, which was itself a kind of boast; the rest of them looked like what they were, which was people who slept in ditches for doctrine. Corvane looked like doctrine that had learned to iron itself.

"Sable." She said his name the way she said everything: as though confirming it against a list. "Your report."

"The subject took the eastern passes." He handed over the page without letting his hand tremble, because trembling was a language the Choir read fluently. "Alone. Moving fast. I lost her in the rockfall below Crag's Tooth."

"You *lost* her."

"I am reporting it honestly, Head-Singer. Would you prefer I report something more flattering?"

Something flickered behind Corvane's eyes — not anger exactly. Interest. That was worse. In the Choir, interest was how you got chosen for things.

"You've changed," she said. "Since Thornfell. You met the carver's eyes, didn't you. And you remember it."

Sable kept his face still. Stillness was the first thing they taught you, back when they taught you anything at all. *A singer with a face is a singer with a weakness*, the initiates were told. *Sand your face smooth as river stone.* He had been very good at it for eleven years.

"Everyone remembers meeting someone's eyes," he said. "It lasts half a second."

"Not for us." Corvane stepped closer. The hut's stone-song seemed to hush, the way birds go quiet when a hawk crosses the yard. "We are not permitted to keep what we see, Sable. You know the discipline. Faces slide off us like rain off oilcloth. So tell me why, when I ask you what the bone-carver looks like, you have an answer ready."

Because I do, thought Sable. Because I've had it for six days. Dark hair going grey early at the temples. A burn scar on the left thumb. Eyes the brown of river water over stones, and when she laughs — when she laughed, once, at the ghost's complaint about the eulogy — it starts in her eyes first, like a lamp being lit in a far room, and —

"She looked like a smuggler," he said. "They all look like smugglers."

Corvane studied him for a long moment. Then, mercifully, she turned away to the window, where the last light was dying red on the snowfields.

"The Grand Cantor has composed a new verse," she said. "For the carver specifically. When it is sung, she will not merely be forgotten. She will be *unremembered*. No grave, no ghost, no echo. Even the dead will look through her." She glanced back. "You understand what that means?"

"It means we should find her quickly."

"It means," said Corvane softly, "that whatever small treason is growing in you, you should dig it out now, while it is still small. I have seen singers try to keep things, Sable. Do you know what happens to a vessel that tries to hold water it was never sealed to hold?"

"It cracks."

"It *sings*," said Corvane. "Badly. Off-key. And the Cantor hears every note." She gathered her robes. "Rest. We march east at dawn."

When she was gone, Sable sat in the dark with the stone-song humming around him, and realized his hands were shaking after all.

* * *

He had been seven years old when they found him, though "found" was a generous word for what the Choir did.

His memory of before was a single image, worn soft as a coin passed through too many hands: a kitchen. Yellow light. A woman humming — he could never recall the tune, only the fact of humming, the shape of it in the air. Sometimes, in the worst hours, he tried to build a face onto the humming woman, and the Choir's training rose up in him like a reflex and sanded her away again. Smooth as river stone. Nothing to hold.

The orphanage at Vail was run by the Choir the way a spider's web is run by the spider: patiently, and with excellent structural engineering. There were forty children there when Sable arrived and thirty-nine songs in the dormitory at night, because the fortieth child — a boy named Pell, who bit — was kept apart until his initiation.

They told the children they were blessed. They told them the world outside was a wound, full of grief that festered in memory the way rot festered in flesh, and that the Choir had been made to lance the wound. They told them that forgetting was mercy, and that they, the orphans, unloved and unclaimed, were the perfect vessels for it, because they had so little to lose.

Pell, who bit, had stood up on initiation day and said, "I have things. I have a name. My mother gave me a name."

And the Grand Cantor — Sable saw him only once in those years, a figure of terrible gentleness, all soft voice and patient hands — had knelt before Pell and said, "Yes. And we will take such good care of it."

Then they sang, and Pell stopped biting, and stopped speaking much at all, and eventually stopped being Pell in any way that mattered. He became Second-Verse Ashen, and he was very good at his work, and sometimes at night Sable would hear him humming fragments of a lullaby he could not possibly know the source of, and both of them would pretend it wasn't happening.

Sable's own initiation came two years later. He had watched Pell's, so he knew the choreography: the white room, the circle of grey robes, the Cantor's hands on your temples like a father's blessing. He had decided, in the fierce private way of a nine-year-old, that he would keep one thing. Just one. Everyone keeps one thing. Even spiders leave a thread for themselves.

He chose the humming woman's kitchen. Yellow light. Safety.

And when the song began, he held onto it with everything he had, a nine-year-old fist closed around a scrap of yellow light, and the song pried his fingers open one by one, gently, so gently, the way you'd open the hand of someone sleeping, and the kitchen went — not torn away but *melted*, like butter in a hot pan, like snow on a palm — and what the Choir poured into the space it left was duty, clean and bright and weightless, duty like a spine, duty like a purpose, and nine-year-old Sable had felt the emptiness where the kitchen used to be and thought, with a child's terrible adaptability:

*Oh. This is lighter.*

That was the trick of it. That was the thing no one warned you. Forgetting didn't hurt. Grieving hurt. Forgetting just made room, and the Choir filled the room with singing, and you went out into the world hollow as a bell and ringing with purpose, and you called it peace for eleven years.

He had been a good singer. He wanted that recorded somewhere, in whatever ledger tracked such things: Sable had been a *good* singer. He had eaten memories in villages where the bread was shared with him first, out of respect. He had unmade a widow's forty years of marriage because a treaty required it, and he had held her hand afterward while she wept for a husband whose name she could no longer find, and he had felt nothing, nothing, nothing, and had counted that feeling-nothing as mastery.

Until Thornfell. Until a woman with river-water eyes had looked at him across a market stall full of small carved bones and seen him — not the robe, not the office, *him* — and instead of sliding off him like rain, the sight of her had stuck, and stuck, and stuck.

He had gone back to the waystation that night and lain awake listening to the stones hum about sheep, and he had done something no singer was supposed to be able to do.

He had wondered.

Wondering, in the Choir, was a symptom. Wondering was the crack in the vessel. Corvane had smelled it on him in under a minute, and Corvane was not even the sharpest nose in the order. Which meant the Grand Cantor would smell it soon, if he didn't already, and the Cantor's remedies made the initiation song look like a lullaby.

So. Options.

Option one: confess. Report the true route, report his own fracture, throw himself on the order's mercy. He examined this option honestly and found it shaped exactly like the white room, and set it down.

Option two: run. Desert. Become one of the grey-robed ghosts the mountain folk told stories about, living wild above the treeline. He examined this option and found it cold, and lonely, and — this was the part that surprised him — pointless. Running solved nothing except his own skin, and he had recently discovered, to his considerable alarm, that he cared about more than his own skin. He cared about a bone-carver's thumb scar. He cared about whether she ate enough on the road. He cared, absurdly, about a dead courier's opinion of funeral oratory.

Which left option three, the one he had already chosen without admitting he'd chosen it, the way you fall asleep without admitting you're falling asleep: lie. Lie beautifully. Lie with craft. Buy her time with falsehood, day by day, mile by mile, and pray the crack in him stayed quiet long enough to matter.

It was, he reflected, the first plan of his entire life that was built out of something instead of emptiness. It fit poorly. It was terrifying. He loved it the way you love a coat that doesn't belong to you and might be stolen back at any moment.

* * *

At dawn they marched east, into the teeth of the false route, and Sable marched with them, and the vindictive goat of Crag's Tooth lived up to its reputation by butting Second-Verse Ashen into a snowdrift, which Sable privately considered the finest omen he had ever received.

On the third day, camped beneath the rockfall where he had claimed to lose her, Corvane found him at the fire.

"You're humming," she said.

Sable went still. "Am I."

"All evening. Some little tune." She crouched, warming her hands, her cellar-door voice almost gentle. "Do you know what it is?"

"No," he said. And then, because lying to the Choir apparently worked best when salted with truth: "But I think it might be the only thing I have left that's mine."

Corvane looked at him for a long time, firelight moving in her eyes, and Sable understood with cold clarity that she was deciding something — whether to report him, whether to use him, whether to wait. Whatever she decided, the clock on his usefulness had started running.

"East," she said at last, standing. "You're certain she went east."

"Certain," said Sable, and packed the lie away carefully, the way you'd pack a coal to carry fire through the night.

Three hundred miles northwest, he hoped, a bone-carver was following a road only the dead remembered, and did not know that her margin of safety now rested entirely on the shoulders of a hollow man humming a tune he couldn't name — a tune that, if he let himself listen closely, sounded less like a lullaby than like a door, opening.
