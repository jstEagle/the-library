## 12. The Cartographer's Daughter

The valley announced itself the way secrets do: reluctantly, and all at once.

One moment Emrys was picking her way down a scree slope with her collar up against the wind, the ghost of the courier drifting beside her like a man walking through water, and the next the mountains simply stepped aside and there it was—a green thread of a valley stitched with slate roofs and woodsmoke, terraced fields climbing the walls like green ladders, and a river running through the middle of it so bright it looked like someone had dropped a coin.

"Lowbrass," said the ghost, whose name was Aldous Pell and who had been dead for eleven days and had not stopped complaining about it once.

"You've been here?"

"I *died* near here. Practically neighbors." He drifted ahead of her down the switchbacks, which was a habit of his she found both useful and insufferable. Dead men didn't tire, so they never understood why living ones needed to stop and breathe. "There's an inn at the crossroads. The Sow and Thistle. Terrible ale. Wonderful stew. The landlady waters the ale to make the stew taste better by comparison."

"That's not how flavor works."

"That's what I told her. She couldn't hear me. Most people can't." He said it lightly, but Emrys had learned to hear the weight underneath Aldous's jokes—the same weight she carried herself, the loneliness of being the only one in the room who knew something true.

She had been on the vanished road for six days now. Six days of walking through landscapes that flickered at the edges, where a ruined wall would suddenly stand whole in her peripheral vision and crumble again when she looked straight at it. The map rode against her ribs inside her coat, wrapped in oilskin that still smelled faintly of the dead man's blood. And the word he'd whispered—*Vesper*—had become a kind of compass point in her mind, pulling her east and south through passes that existed only because the dead remembered walking them.

Aldous believed the map's maker lived in this valley. He'd been carrying the map *to* someone when the Choir caught him; that much his ghost remembered, though the rest was torn like wet paper. "The cartographer," he kept saying. "Find the cartographer. Or his kin. There was a daughter—I think there was a daughter. I remember a girl arguing about contour lines."

"Arguing about contour lines," Emrys repeated.

"With great passion. It stuck with me. You don't forget a person who argues about contour lines like they insulted her mother."

Emrys adjusted the pack straps on her shoulders and thought about how strange her life had become. Three weeks ago her biggest problem had been a widow in Hollowmere who wanted her husband's mourning charm carved from elk bone instead of rowan-wood, because her husband had been bitten by an elk as a boy and Emrys, honestly, should have known better than to ask why that mattered. Now she was following a dead smuggler into a valley to find a woman who fought with maps, pursued by an order of grey-robed memory-eaters, carrying the last door out of a country that had never existed.

Her rule—*never get involved, carve your bones, keep your head down*—was forty years old and buried under six days of road. She missed it the way you miss a tooth.

* * *

Lowbrass smelled of bread and river-stone. Emrys came in along the cart track with her hood up and her hands in her pockets, doing what she did best: being nobody. A woman with a pack and a tired face, worth a nod and nothing more.

Except.

Except that as she passed the well at the village center, an old man stacking firewood looked up at her and frowned—not at her, but *past* her, at the space beside her left shoulder where Aldous floated—and muttered, "Huh. Thought I saw—" and then shook his head and went back to his stacking.

And except that the inn at the crossroads, the Sow and Thistle, had a window display that stopped Emrys flat on the cobbles.

Maps. Dozens of them, hung in the window like laundry: hand-drawn, inked, some of them clearly decades old and some fresh enough that the ink still shone. Maps of the Thornfell passes. Maps of the river country. Maps of coastlines Emrys had never heard of. And in the center of the window, framed in black walnut, a map of a country that made her heart slam against her ribs like a fist on a door.

It was labeled, in careful copperplate: **VELLICHOR**.

"It's wrong," said a voice behind her. "Before you ask. Everyone asks, and everyone's disappointed, and I'd rather get the disappointment over with early."

Emrys turned. The woman in the doorway of the inn was perhaps thirty, with ink stains up both forearms like bad bruises, hair escaping a braid in every direction, and eyes the particular shade of gray-brown that Emrys associated with river fog and stubbornness. She was holding a mug of tea and looking at Emrys with the frank appraisal of someone who assessed strangers professionally.

"The map," the woman said, jerking her chin at the window. "Vellichor. It's wrong. My father drew it from memory before he died, and memory is a liar, and also half of it is mine, and mine are worse. Come in if you're coming in. The stew's better than the ale, and the ale's terrible, so the arithmetic works out."

"She knows her stew," Aldous said gleefully, floating past Emrys through the doorframe without opening it, because ghosts considered doors a suggestion at best.

Emrys stood on the threshold for three full seconds, weighing every instinct she owned. Then she went in, because the map in the window had said *Vellichor*, and there was no version of her life now in which she walked away from that.

* * *

The common room was empty except for a cat asleep in a chair that was very clearly the cat's chair. The woman poured Emrys a cup of tea without asking, set down bread and butter and a pot of honey, and then sat across from her and folded her arms.

"So," she said. "You're either a scholar, a madwoman, or trouble. In my experience those categories overlap more than people admit. Which is it?"

"I'm a bone-carver," Emrys said. "From the Thornfell towns."

"A long way from home."

"Yes."

The woman waited. She was good at waiting; Emrys could see it in her, the patience of someone who had spent years asking questions and getting silence back. Finally she leaned forward.

"My name is Maren Voss. My father was Aldric Voss, royal cartographer of Vellichor, which is a sentence that gets me fed to exactly no one, because nobody has ever heard of Vellichor, including me." She said it fast and flat, the cadence of a speech given many times. "He survived whatever happened—the forgetting, the erasure, call it what you like—and he got out with nothing but his memory and his hands, and he spent thirty years trying to draw his own country from inside his head. And every year the drawing changed. Not because the country changed. Because *he* was leaking. Whatever took Vellichor away was eating him too, slowly, from the edges inward. First he lost the names of the southern provinces. Then the shape of the coastline. By the end he was drawing the capital as a blank square with a question mark in it, and he wept while he inked the question mark, and I held the pen for him because his hand shook too badly, and that—" her voice caught, just slightly, and she pushed through it, "—that is the last thing I have of him. A question mark."

The cat woke, yawned, and resettled. Somewhere upstairs a floorboard creaked. Emrys realized her tea had gone cold in her hands.

"I'm sorry," she said, and meant it.

"Don't be sorry. Be useful." Maren's eyes were dry and fierce. "Because here's what I know, bone-carver. For thirty years I have been collecting memories of Vellichor. Wrong ones, mostly—that's the joke, isn't it? People come through this valley, travelers, old soldiers, mad pilgrims, and sometimes one of them says something impossible. Says they swam in the Vell as a child. Says their grandmother sang a lullaby in a language nobody speaks. Says they dreamed of a city with seven bridges and woke up crying. Little splinters. Wrong-shaped pieces that don't fit anywhere else in the world. And I write them down. Every one. I have four hundred and eleven of them." She tapped the table. "Four hundred and twelve, if you count my father's question mark, which I do."

"Why are you telling me this?" Emrys asked quietly. "You don't know me."

"No," Maren agreed. "But you looked at that map in the window the way people look at a grave. And you flinched at the name before I said it, which means you already knew it, which means somebody told you, which means—" she leaned in, and her voice dropped, "—somebody *remembered*. So. Who are you, and what do you know?"

Emrys looked at her for a long moment. Then, with the feeling of a woman stepping off a cliff and discovering midair that she'd always meant to fly, she unwrapped the oilskin package and laid the map on the table between them.

Maren Voss went absolutely still.

Then she put both hands flat on the table on either side of it, the way you'd steady yourself against a wave, and read. Her lips moved. Her breath went ragged. When she reached the signature in the lower corner—*A. Voss, Royal Cartographer, by appointment to the Crown of Vellichor*—she made a sound that Emrys had only ever heard from widows receiving their mourning charms: grief and relief tangled together so tightly you couldn't tell which was which.

"This is his," she whispered. "This is his hand. This is his *hatching*, look at the hatching on the mountains, he did it in fives, everyone else does it in fours, he said fours were lazy—" She looked up, and her eyes were wet and blazing. "Where did you get this?"

"A dying man gave it to me," Emrys said. "He was running from the Choir. They killed him for it. His ghost has been guiding me ever since."

Most people, offered a sentence like that, would have backed toward the door. Maren Voss nodded slowly, as if Emrys had confirmed a suspicion rather than confessed to haunting.

"The Choir," she said. "Grey robes. They came through here two years ago asking questions. Nice as you please. Bought rounds of drinks. And afterward nobody in the village could remember a single thing they'd said—including me, and I *never* forget anything, it's my one talent and my entire personality." She drummed her fingers on the table. "I started writing everything down after that. Everything, every day, in a book I keep hidden. If they come back, I'll know what they took."

"They're coming back," Emrys said. "They're hunting this map. They're hunting *me*."

"Good," said Maren.

Emrys blinked. "Good?"

"Good," Maren repeated, with the serene certainty of a woman who had been waiting her whole life for a war to arrive at her inn. "Because I'm coming with you. To the gate. To Vellichor. Wherever this map ends, I end up there too, and you can argue with me all you like, but I will tell you right now that I have argued with my father about contour lines for fifteen years and I have never once lost."

* * *

They argued, of course. Emrys pointed out—with what she felt was admirable restraint—that Maren was an innkeeper, not a soldier; that the road ahead ran through country that ate memories for tolls; that anyone who traveled with Emrys became a target. Maren countered each point by refilling Emrys's tea and dismantling it with the cheerful ruthlessness of a siege engine.

"An innkeeper. Yes. Who has spent thirty years interviewing every traveler, peddler, and lunatic between here and the coast, which makes me the best-informed person in four hundred miles. Who keeps daily written records, which means even if the Choir eats my head, the paper remembers. And who," she added, standing to fetch a battered leather satchel from behind the bar, "has this."

She opened it on the table. Inside, packed with obsessive care, were notebooks—dozens of them, spines cracked, pages soft with handling. And wrapped in waxed cloth, a brass compass whose needle spun lazily, aimlessly, pointing at nothing in the ordinary world.

"My father's," Maren said. "It doesn't point north. It never has. He said it pointed *home*. After the erasure it just spun. But—" she lifted it, and the needle trembled, wavered, and swung, settling with a small decisive click toward the northeast. Toward the mountains. Toward, Emrys realized with a chill that was not entirely unpleasant, the exact bearing of the vanished road.

Toward the gate.

"It's been doing that for six days," Maren said quietly. "Six days. Before that it spun for thirty years. So whatever you're carrying, bone-carver, it's waking things up. Things that have been asleep a long time." She closed the satchel and slung it over her shoulder with the finality of a woman locking her front door. "I'm coming. Also, I make excellent travel bread, and you look like you've been eating pine bark and regret."

"She's not wrong about the bread," Aldous said from the corner, where he'd been listening with his chin in his hands like a man at a particularly good play. "I had her father's bread once. Well. I smelled it. Dead men get the smell but not the chew, it's a cruel arrangement." He paused, and something moved across his translucent face—recognition, arriving late, the way it did with the dead. "*Voss.* Aldric Voss. I knew that name. I carried for him, near the end—letters, rolled maps, sealed things I wasn't supposed to look at. He's the one who gave me the errand. He's the one who told me where to take the map." He looked at Maren with an expression Emrys had rarely seen on a ghost: wonder. "You're his girl. You used to sit under his drafting table and correct his coastlines."

Maren stared at the empty corner where Aldous's voice came from. She couldn't see him—almost no one could—but Emrys watched her grip the edge of the table and know, somehow, anyway.

"He talked about a courier," Maren whispered. "Near the end. Said if anything happened to him, a man named Pell would know where the truth went."

"Pell," Emrys said. "Aldous Pell."

For a moment nobody spoke. The cat, sensing history, went back to sleep.

"Well," said Maren Voss, wiping her eyes briskly with her sleeve and squaring her shoulders like a woman taking up a pack twice her size. "Then it's settled. We leave at first light. I'll write a note for my neighbor to watch the inn, I'll bake for the road tonight, and you—" she pointed a floury finger at Emrys, "—are going to tell me everything, starting with why a bone-carver from the Thornfells is carrying the last map of a dead country, and I warn you now, I will know if you skip parts."

Outside, far off in the high passes, the light was going the color of old pewter, and the wind coming down the valley carried a sound that might have been weather and might have been singing.

Emrys looked at the compass needle trembling toward the gate, at the map lying open between them like a wound finally dressed, at the ink-stained woman already rolling up her sleeves, and felt—for the first time since a bleeding stranger collapsed against her door—something that was almost hope.

Hope, tempered by the knowledge that somewhere behind them, patient and polite and grey, the Choir was already asking its quiet questions.

But that was tomorrow's weather. Tonight there was bread to bake, and for the first time in forty years, Emrys Thackeray would not be baking it alone.
