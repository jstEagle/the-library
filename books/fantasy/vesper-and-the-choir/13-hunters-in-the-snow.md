## 13. Hunters in the Snow

The snow began three days out of Maren's valley, and by the second morning it had stopped being weather and become a country of its own.

Emrys had walked through storms before—Thornfell winters made a profession of them—but this was different. This was a whiteness so complete that up and down surrendered their meanings, and the world shrank to the small circle of her own breath and the two shapes ahead of her: Maren's dark coat, patched at the elbows, and the ghost of the courier drifting beside her like smoke that had learned to walk.

"He's sulking," Maren said, without turning around.

"I am not sulking," said the ghost of Aldric Vane, who had been dead for five weeks and still managed to sound offended. "I am *concentrating*. There is a difference. The road bends left in half a mile, and if you miss it you'll walk straight off the shoulder of the mountain, and then I'll have to spend eternity listening to both of you complain about being dead, which—" he paused, "—actually, on reflection, might be an improvement."

"You don't even feel the cold," Emrys said. "What exactly are you concentrating on?"

"The road. Which is more than either of you can do, because to you it looks like nothing at all." He gestured grandly at the featureless white. "This is why the dead make better guides than maps, incidentally. Maps only know what things looked like. I remember what they *felt* like under my boots."

Emrys adjusted the strap of her pack and said nothing. Her hands were tucked deep in her sleeves, as they had been for most of the journey—not from cold, though the cold was real enough, but because they hadn't felt entirely like hers since the tollhouse. She had paid for passage there with her mother's face, and the memory of paying sat in her chest like a swallowed stone. Some nights she woke reaching for it, the way you reach for a tooth that isn't there anymore.

Maren fell back to walk beside her. The mapmaker's daughter had a way of walking that Emrys had come to think of as *reading*—head slightly tilted, eyes moving over the landscape as though it were text. Right now the text said nothing but white.

"How much farther?" Maren asked.

"To the pass? Half a day, if the road holds." Emrys glanced upward, or where upward should have been. "And that's the problem. Roads hold. Weather doesn't. If this closes in before we're through—"

"It already has," Aldric said quietly.

He had stopped. Ghosts didn't leave footprints, but Emrys had learned to read him anyway—the way his edges went sharp when something worried him, the way the snow seemed to fall *through* him rather than past him, as though the world couldn't quite decide he was there.

"What is it?" Maren asked.

"Listen."

They listened. Wind, and more wind, and beneath the wind—

"There," Emrys said. Her stomach dropped. "Singing."

It was very far away, and very soft, and it was the most terrible thing she had heard since the grey robes came to Thornfell. The Choir did not sing the way people sang. Their harmonies moved against each other on purpose, notes sliding where notes shouldn't go, and listening to it felt like watching someone smile with all the wrong teeth.

"They've found us," Maren whispered.

"No," Aldric said. "Not yet. They're *searching*. There's a difference, and it matters." He turned to Emrys, and his ghost-light flickered. "How much do you trust me?"

"Not at all," Emrys said. "You got me into this."

"Good. Trust me anyway. There's a place—a shepherd's hut, old, older than me, and I died recently enough to be insulted by how old everything else is getting. It's off the road, down in a fold of the mountain. We can wait out the worst of the storm there, and the singing won't carry into it."

"And when the storm passes?"

Aldric didn't answer, which was answer enough.

* * *

The hut was a low stone hump with a door that fought them every inch of the way open, and inside it smelled of decades of sheep and one long-ago fire. Maren got a blaze going from damp wood and sheer stubbornness while Emrys unpacked what remained of their food: hard bread, harder cheese, and a twist of salt that Maren guarded like a relic.

"We could go around," Maren said, around a mouthful. "South, drop lower, come at the pass from the other side."

"Two days' detour through country none of us knows, with hunters behind us and the Cantor's song getting louder every night." Emrys shook her head. "No. Through the pass, tomorrow, first light, before they tighten the net."

"You say that like we're fish."

"We are fish," Aldric said cheerfully. "Very small fish, in a very large net, held by very patient fishermen. I've been meaning to mention it."

"You've been mentioning nothing else for five weeks."

"Yes, well. Death has made me honest. It's dreadful. I recommend avoiding it."

Despite everything—despite the singing somewhere in the white, despite the stone in her chest where her mother's face used to be—Emrys found herself almost smiling. That was the thing about the dead man she'd never expected: he was funny. He'd been a smuggler and a courier and a spy for a country that no longer existed, and death had burned all the caution out of him and left only the wit, like bone after the flesh is gone.

She took out her carving knife and a piece of antler and worked while the others talked, because her hands needed something to do that wasn't shaking. A charm, she decided. Not a mourning charm this time. Something for the road. She wasn't sure yet what shape it wanted to be; the antler would tell her eventually. It always did.

"Your gift," Maren said suddenly. "The voices. When you carve something that belonged to someone—do you hear them the way we hear Aldric?"

"More like... weather," Emrys said. "Aldric is a person. Most of what I hear is weather. Rains of complaint. Little storms of grievance. The dead man in Mrs. Hollis's charm spent four months telling me his funeral had too many lilies."

"Too many lilies," Maren repeated.

"He'd been clear about wanting roses. His widow disagreed. They'd been married forty years and apparently this was the final betrayal." Emrys turned the antler in her fingers. "That's most of the dead, honestly. Small things. The living think the dead are full of great mysteries, and sometimes they are, but mostly they're full of opinions about flowers."

"And the rest?" Maren asked softly. "The ones who aren't small?"

Emrys's knife paused.

"The rest are quiet," she said. "Or they're not, and I don't listen. Rule one of my whole life, Maren: never get involved. Hear the dead, help them across when it's easy, and never—*never*—reach into what they're carrying. Because some of them are carrying things that want hands."

The wind rose outside. And beneath the wind, closer now, the Choir sang.

* * *

She woke in the black hour before dawn to silence, and the silence was worse than the singing.

"Aldric," she breathed.

He was already at the door, or as near to the door as he ever stood, and his light had gone thin and strange. "Three of them," he said. "Below us. On the road. They stopped singing an hour ago—that's how they hunt, you understand. The song is the net. Silence means the net is closing."

Maren was awake too, sitting up with her father's compass cupped in both hands like a candle flame she was trying to shelter. "Can we outrun them?"

"In this?" Emrys looked at the door, beyond which the storm had thickened into a wall. "We'd be blind. They wouldn't be. The Choir walks roads the way I carve bones—they've done it ten thousand times."

"Then we fight," Maren said, and there was something in her voice that frightened Emrys more than the singing had—not fear, but readiness. Grief with a blade's edge. The Choir had erased people Maren loved too.

"No," Emrys said. "Not yet. Maybe not at all. Aldric—is there another way out of this fold? Anything. A goat track, a crevasse, a *drain*."

The ghost drifted to the back wall and passed his hand through it, which was his version of pacing. "There's the high shelf. North side. It runs along above the pass and comes down beyond it—but Emrys, no one uses it in winter. The snow lies deep and the wind comes off the ice fields sideways, and if you step wrong there's four hundred feet of nothing waiting to introduce itself."

"Better four hundred feet of nothing than three singers of the Choir."

"That is," Aldric admitted, "a genuinely terrible plan, and also the best one available. I'm proud of us."

They packed in the dark, quickly, leaving the fire to die. At the door Emrys paused, turned back, and set the half-finished antler charm on the hearthstone where any traveler sheltering here might find it. Old habit. Leave something carved behind; the dead appreciate a landmark.

Then she stepped out into the white, and the mountain tried to kill her for six hours.

* * *

The shelf was exactly as bad as Aldric had promised. The wind came off the ice fields in long flat shoves that picked a person up and set them down a foot from where they'd meant to stand, and the snow underfoot gave no warning of what lay beneath it. Twice Emrys went in to her hip. Once Maren vanished entirely and reappeared swearing with a fluency that suggested her father had not raised her exclusively among maps.

"Left!" Aldric called, materializing beside her. "The shelf narrows—hug the wall!"

She hugged the wall. The wall, being stone, offered no opinion. Somewhere below and behind, muffled by the storm, she thought she heard singing again—and then thought she heard it *ahead*, and couldn't tell if the wind was lying or if the net had already closed around them and they simply hadn't touched its edges yet.

They came down off the shelf in the last grey light of afternoon, into a bowl of snow between two shoulders of rock, and Emrys knew immediately that something was wrong, because the dead were standing in it.

Dozens of them. Hundreds. She couldn't count; they overlapped like reflections in deep water. Travelers, shepherds, soldiers in armor centuries out of fashion—all of them faint, all of them faceless in the way ghosts are when they don't wish to be seen, all of them turned toward the pass as though waiting.

"Maren," Emrys said carefully. "Tell me what you see."

"Snow," Maren said. "Snow and rocks. Why? What do you see?"

"Company."

Aldric drifted up beside her, and for once he had nothing witty to say. "I see them too," he said quietly. "They've been gathering for days, I think. Every soul that ever died in this pass. They're drawn to the Choir's singing like—like moths. Or like dogs hearing a whistle they hate."

"Why would they hate it?"

"Because of what the song does." His light guttered. "You've seen what it does. It takes. These are the taken. Every name the Choir ever sang out of someone's mouth, every memory they devoured—it goes somewhere, Emrys. It doesn't vanish. It pools. And these poor souls are standing in the pool."

The singing swelled. Close now. Very close.

And then the snow ahead of them opened, and three figures in grey walked out of it as though the blizzard were a curtain hung for their convenience.

* * *

Later—much later, in the years afterward, when children asked her about the worst day of her life—Emrys would try to explain that time in the pass, and find that it came back in pieces, the way broken bone sets: crooked, functional, never quite right again.

She remembered the lead hunter's face, which was kind, which was the horror of it. He looked like a schoolmaster, or a favorite uncle—grey-robed, silver-haired, with laugh lines around eyes that held absolutely nothing behind them. The Choir ate memories, and what was left wore kindness like a borrowed coat.

"Sable told us you'd come this way," the hunter said. His voice was warm. It was the warmest thing in the pass. "He was wrong, of course. But usefully wrong. Lies have shapes, you see, and his lie pointed north like a needle."

Emrys's heart did something complicated and painful. Sable had lied for her. Sable had been caught lying for her.

"You're the carver," the hunter continued, conversational, stepping closer through the snow. "The Grand Cantor finds you fascinating. A girl who hears the dead and never once thought to profit from it. Do you know how rare that is? Greed is so predictable. Grief is so predictable. But you—you simply *listened*, year after year, and asked for nothing." He smiled. "He'd like to meet you. He'd like it very much if you came south with us. There's a place for you in the Choir, if you want it. There's a place for everyone, in the end."

Behind her, Maren had gone very still, one hand closed around the compass.

"And if I refuse?" Emrys asked.

"Refuse?" The hunter tilted his head, genuinely puzzled, the way a well-loved dog is puzzled by a closed door. "Oh, child. Refusal is just a memory you haven't lost yet."

The other two hunters began to sing.

It was soft. That was the unbearable part—it was soft, a cradle-song harmony, and Emrys felt it slide into her mind looking for handles. Looking for her mother's face, and finding the hole where it had been. Looking for her workshop, the smell of bone dust and linseed oil, the window with the crooked shutter—and she felt it *loosen*, felt the memory begin to lift out of her like a splinter being drawn—

"No," she said.

The word came out of her strangely. Not her speaking voice. Something underneath it, something that had been waiting its whole life for permission.

The dead in the bowl turned toward her.

All of them. Hundreds of faces resolving out of faintness at once, hundreds of eyes fixing on her with a hunger and a hope that made her knees shake, because she understood in one white-hot instant what they wanted. They wanted to be *used*. They had been drained and discarded and pooled in this frozen bowl for decades, and here at last was a woman whose whole life had been making room for the dead to speak through, and they were begging.

*Let us*, they said, in a voice like an avalanche deciding. *Let us, let us, let us.*

"Emrys," Aldric said sharply. "Don't. You don't know what you'd be opening. Rule one—"

"I know my rules," Emrys said.

Her hands came out of her sleeves. She pressed her palms flat against the snow, against the mountain itself, the way she pressed her palms against bone before she carved—listening first, always listening first—and the mountain answered. Everything the pass had ever swallowed rose up through her arms: the shepherd boy who fell in 1189, the soldiers who froze in their century-old armor, the bride whose wedding party never made it over the pass, the smugglers, the pilgrims, the lost children, the lost children, the lost children.

They came through her like a choir coming through a bell.

The sound that came out of her was not a scream. It was worse than a scream—it was *organized*. Every angry dead soul in the pass, singing through her throat in harmony, and their harmony was the opposite of the Choir's: it built instead of unmade, it named instead of erased, and each note carried a name. The names of everyone the Choir had ever sung away. The names came out of her like arrows.

The hunters staggered. The lead hunter's borrowed kindness cracked down the middle, and behind it Emrys saw—not emptiness, she realized, not nothing, but a *cellar*, a hoarded dark stuffed with stolen centuries, and her chorus poured into that dark like water into a breached wall.

One hunter simply folded, dropping into the snow like a puppet with cut strings, and did not move again.

The second ran. She let him run. She would remember, later, that she had chosen to let him run, and take a fierce and trembling pride in it.

But the leader—the kind-faced one—he caught himself against the wind, blood threading from his nose, and looked at her with something new in his ruined face. Not anger.

*Awe.*

"What are you?" he whispered.

Emrys stood in the center of her own storm, shaking so hard her teeth chattered, tears freezing on her cheeks, and heard herself answer in a hundred layered voices:

"I'm the one who listens."

The hunter fled. The chorus faded out of her slowly, reluctantly, the dead sinking back into the snow like a tide going out, and the last thing she felt before they went was not gratitude or peace but a vast, patient *waiting*—as though they had only gone back under the surface, and would rise whenever she called.

Then she was alone in her own body, on her knees in the snow, and she was sick until there was nothing left to bring up.

* * *

Maren held her. That was the next piece she kept: Maren's arms around her, Maren's voice saying her name over and over like a charm against forgetting, and Aldric hovering close, saying nothing at all, which from Aldric was the loudest comfort there was.

"I killed one," Emrys said, when she could speak. "Maybe. I don't—I don't know if he's—"

"He was going to unmake you," Maren said fiercely. "He was going to eat your mother's grave and your workshop and your name. Whatever happened to him, you didn't start it."

"I finished it, though." Emrys stared at her own hands, red with cold, clean of blood, which somehow made it worse. "Maren, you didn't feel it. From the inside. It wasn't like carving. Carving is—inviting. This was—" She searched for the word and hated the one she found. "*Opening.* I opened a door in myself and I don't know how to close it all the way. I can still feel them. All of them. Standing in the snow. Waiting for me to need them."

"Is that so terrible?"

"Yes," Emrys said. "Because someday I *will* need them. And they know it."

The storm was dying. Through the thinning white, far to the south, she could see lights moving on a distant slope—lanterns, many lanterns, descending in a careful line. Not three hunters now. A dozen. The Choir did not send twelve for a carver and a mapmaker's daughter.

They sent twelve for whatever she had just become.

Aldric appeared at her shoulder, and his voice, when it came, had lost every ounce of its humor.

"Emrys," he said. "There's something you should know. One of those lights has stopped following the others. It's coming straight toward us—fast, alone, no escort." He paused. "And whoever carries it is singing. Quietly. To himself. And Emrys—it's a lullaby. A Vellichor lullaby. My grandmother used to sing it."

Emrys wiped her face and stood, legs shaking, and watched the single light bobbing up through the darkening snow.

"Who is it?" Maren whispered.

"I don't know," Emrys said. But her heart, treacherous organ, had already begun to hope.
