## 14. Sable's Choice

The order came at dawn, written in the Grand Cantor's own hand, which meant it could not be refused, only obeyed or betrayed.

*The bone-carver travels the high road toward the Vesper Gate. She carries the map. Kill her. Burn the map. Sing the passage closed behind you.*

Sable read it three times while the snow came down in fat, indifferent flakes. Beside him, Brother Corvane sharpened his knife with the small circular strokes of a man who had done this so many times the motion had worn a groove into his soul.

"High road," Corvane said. "She'll take the pass through the White Teeth. No other way with the low fords frozen."

"You know these mountains well," Sable said.

"I knew them before I was taken." Corvane smiled without warmth. "That's why they send me. The dead remember roads; we remember everything else."

Sable folded the order and put it away. His hands were steady. He noted that with a kind of clinical surprise, the way he'd note weather. Somewhere behind his sternum, in the hollow where the Choir had scooped out his past and packed the space with duty, something had begun to ache. It had started the day he'd looked into the bone-carver's eyes and *remembered her afterward*, which should have been impossible, and had been getting worse ever since — this itch, this splinter of self that would not dissolve into the song.

He thought about the hollow. He thought about what might have been there before.

He thought: *I am going to do something unforgivable, and I don't even know who will be doing the forgiving.*

"Brother Sable." Corvane's knife paused. "You're humming."

Sable stopped. He hadn't noticed. "Am I."

"The Cantor says the fracture always starts as a hum." Corvane resumed his sharpening, conversational as a man discussing grain prices. "A little tune of your own, under the Choir's song. Then one day you look at your orders and think, *perhaps not*. And then someone has to sing you back down to silence." He tested the edge on his thumb and nodded, satisfied. "It won't be me doing the singing, brother. But if it comes to it, I'd rather it were quick. For both our sakes."

"Understood," Sable said, and meant it in every direction at once.

They walked up into the White Teeth, and the snow swallowed their footprints behind them like a debt being paid.

* * *

Emrys woke to white silence and the certain knowledge that she was dying.

Not dramatically. Dying the way things died in the mountains — slowly, stupidly, by degrees. The blizzard had caught her an hour past the last cairn, the ghost-guide had been torn from her side by wind she couldn't hear him shouting over, and she'd crawled into the first shadow in the rockface because her body had made the decision her mind was too cold to make. Now the shadow turned out to be a cave, shallow, dry-ish, and the fire she'd tried to build had eaten its three sticks and died sulking, and her hands had stopped hurting, which was the worst sign of all.

Her fingers wouldn't hold a knife. A bone-carver whose hands wouldn't hold a knife. There was a joke in that, probably, and somewhere in the warm part of her life she'd have found it.

"Get up," said the dead man's voice, thin through the storm. "Emrys. Get *up*."

"Can't," she said. Her teeth barely worked. "Resting my eyes."

"If you rest your eyes, I'll never be free of you, and then there'll be *two* of us nagging everyone within earshot—"

Something moved at the mouth of the cave.

Emrys's heart did the last brave thing available to it and slammed once against her ribs. Through the white churn, a figure resolved — grey robe, snow-crusted, moving with the unhurried certainty of someone who could not get lost because he had already given up on being anywhere else.

Sable.

Of course. Of course it was him. The one Choir member who had looked at her across a market stall and *kept* her face when every rule of his order said faces slid off him like rain off oilskin. She'd told herself it was a trick, a lure, patience wearing a kind mask. She'd run anyway, and here he was at the end of the running, and she couldn't even lift a carving knife to meet him.

"So," she managed. It came out mostly fog. "Come to finish the job?"

He knelt in the snow just inside the cave mouth. Snow clung to his lashes. Up close, without the grey hood thrown forward, his face was younger than she remembered and more tired than it had any right to be.

"No," he said.

"That's usually how it goes, though. You people. Polite right up until the singing starts."

"Yes." He looked at his own hands as if they belonged to a stranger whose habits he disapproved of. "Usually."

The dead man drifted close to Emrys's shoulder, agitated, translucent. *"He's one of them. Girl, whatever he's offering, it's a hook with bait on it—"*

"What are you offering?" she asked.

Sable was quiet long enough that the wind filled the pause twice over. When he spoke, each word seemed to cost him something, paid out of some account she couldn't see.

"There were two of us sent after you. My orders were to kill you. I'm not going to kill you." He drew a breath. "Corvane is twenty minutes behind me. Maybe less. He thinks I went ahead to find your trail. When he arrives and finds no trail and finds me sitting in a cave with you instead, he will understand exactly what I've done, and he is very good at what he does." He finally raised his eyes to hers. "So what I'm offering is this: I can keep you alive tonight, and tomorrow we both run, and neither of us trusts the other, and that's the best bargain on this mountain."

Emrys stared at him. The cold had gotten into her thinking, making everything slow and strange, so that for a moment all she could manage was: "Why?"

And Sable — who had asked himself the same question all night, walking ahead of Corvane through the storm, turning the question over like a stone with no answer under it — said the only true thing he had:

"I don't know. I wish I did. Something in me remembers you, and I don't know how, and I've decided I'd rather find out than obey." A ghost of a smile, crooked and unpracticed, like a man trying on an expression from a previous life. "It's not much of a reason. It's the only one I've got."

*"Liar,"* snapped the dead man.

"Hush," Emrys murmured, and Sable's head tilted slightly — he'd heard nothing, but he'd heard *her* hear something.

"You have a ghost with you," he said. Not a question.

"The courier. He's very protective and extremely annoying."

"Good," said Sable, and began, with brisk competence, to build a fire. "We'll need the annoyance. Corvane doesn't leave tracks either."

* * *

Later — much later, when the fire had gone from sputtering hope to actual heat and Emrys had stopped shivering enough to feel humiliated about the state she'd been found in — they sat on opposite sides of the flames like two chess players who distrusted the board.

Sable had given her his cloak without comment. It smelled of cedar smoke and something older underneath, incense maybe, the smell of the Choir's sanctums. She wore it anyway. Pride froze faster than flesh.

"Your hands," he said. "Can you use them?"

"Ask me again in an hour." She flexed her fingers over the fire. Pins and needles, glorious and agonizing. "Why? Planning to have me carve something?"

"Planning to know whether you can defend yourself tomorrow. Corvane won't monologue. He won't offer terms. He'll simply arrive, and sing, and you'll forget why you were running before you hit the ground."

"Charming fellow."

"He was kind to me when I was taken," Sable said, and there was something complicated in it, grief and loyalty and disgust all braided together. "That's the thing no one understands about the Choir. We're not monsters. We're orphans who were given a purpose instead of a childhood, and we loved the person who gave it, because love was the only thing they left us room for." He fed a stick to the fire. "I keep saying *we*. Old habit."

"How old were you?" Emrys asked. "When they took you."

"I don't know." He said it flatly, factually, the way you'd report the weather, and somehow that was worse than any wailing could have been. "That's rather the point of what they do. They eat the past so there's nothing left to pull you home. First memory I have is standing in the sanctum, being sung to, feeling grateful." His jaw tightened. "There's a hole where my life used to be, and I've walked around it for twenty years telling myself the hole was me."

The fire popped. Somewhere outside, the storm was losing its temper in slower gusts.

"And now?" Emrys asked quietly.

"Now there's a splinter in the hole." He looked at her across the flames, and didn't look away, and she found she couldn't either. "Shaped suspiciously like a woman who carves bones and argues with ghosts in smuggler towns."

"That's not romantic, that's a splinter."

"I never claimed romance. I claim infection." The crooked smile again, a little more practiced this time. "It spreads."

Emrys laughed — a short, rusty sound, startled out of her, the first laugh in weeks that wasn't aimed at a ghost. She covered it by poking the fire. This was a terrible idea, allying with him. Every sensible bone in her body — and she was intimately acquainted with all of them — said men who killed for a living did not stop killing for a living because of a feeling. She'd spent her whole life on the arithmetic of survival, and the sum here came out wrong in every column.

But her hands were warm inside his cloak. And he had knelt in the snow and offered her the truth instead of a lie, and the truth was worth more than safety, in her experience. Safety lied constantly. Truth at least had the decency to hurt you honestly.

"Corvane," she said. "How good is he?"

"Very."

"How fast?"

"Faster than you. Slower than me, if I stop pretending to be tired."

"You've been pretending to be tired since the pass."

"I've been pretending to be loyal since I was six years old. Tiredness is easy by comparison." He glanced toward the cave mouth, where the storm was thinning into that pale exhausted grey that means morning is coming whether anyone wants it or not. "When he finds us — and he'll find us, the dead talk to the Choir too, did you know that? — I'll engage him. You run east along the ridge. There's an old shepherd stair below the third peak, pre-Erasure stonework, the maps call it the Ladder. It drops into the valley where your route continues."

"You're coming with me."

"I'm buying you the head start. Those aren't the same—"

"They're exactly the same, and if you say otherwise I'll bite you, and my teeth work fine, it's only my hands that are slow." She heard herself, heard the ferocity in it, and was surprised by it. Somewhere between the tollhouse and this cave she had stopped being willing to let people spend themselves on her behalf. Or perhaps she'd never stopped being willing, and had merely discovered she minded now, which amounted to the same revolution. "You don't get to trade yourself for me. That's not a bargain, that's just dying with extra steps."

For a moment Sable said nothing. The firelight moved on his face, and Emrys watched something happen in it — the expression of a man hearing a sentence he had waited twenty years, unknowing, to hear. *You matter. Not your function. You.*

"No one's ever objected before," he said at last, roughly.

"Well." Emrys poked the fire harder than it deserved. "Get used to objections. I'm told I'm very annoying."

"The courier mentioned."

"You *can* hear him?"

"No. But you argue with empty air in a very distinctive way." He rose, joints cracking, and stood looking down at her with the fire between them, and for a heartbeat neither of them moved, and the cave felt very small and very warm and full of a possibility that frightened her more than Corvane did.

Then the light at the cave mouth changed.

Not dawn. Dawn was grey. This was a thread of silver, a single sustained note made visible, threading down through the storm like a plumb line — and Emrys felt it in her teeth, in the carved charms at her throat, in every dead voice she carried, all of them flinching at once.

"He's here," Sable breathed. All the warmth went out of his voice, replaced by the flat readiness of a weapon remembering itself. "Behind me. Thirty seconds. Go."

"Not without—"

"*Go.*" And then, softer, almost gentle, almost a confession: "Emrys. Please."

Her name. In his mouth. The Choir stole names — she'd seen it, seen wives stare through husbands — and yet he said hers like it had never left him, like it had been waiting in that hollow all along for something to grow around.

She ran. East, along the ridge, into the thinning snow, the courier's ghost streaming beside her like a banner, and behind her she heard the first notes of the Choir's song rise to meet a second voice rising against it — and the mountain itself seemed to lean in to listen.

* * *

Sable turned to face the cave mouth as Brother Corvane stepped out of the storm, silver song trailing from his lips like frost.

"You're humming, brother," Corvane said sadly, drawing his knife. "I did tell you."

"I know," said Sable, and sang back.
