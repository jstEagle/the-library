## 15. The City That Isn't There

The road stopped pretending to be a road on the third morning.

One moment Emrys was walking a rutted track between frost-browned hills, the ghost of the courier drifting ahead of her like smoke that had opinions about direction, and the next there was no track at all—just open meadow, unbroken grass, and the distinct feeling of having walked into a room where someone had just finished saying something about her.

"It was here," she said.

"It's always here," said the ghost, whose name she had finally pried out of him on the second night—Corvin, a courier's name, short and practical as his life had been. "That's rather the point."

Maren crouched at the edge of the meadow with her father's compass flat in her palm, watching the needle spin in slow, dreamy circles like a drunk trying to remember which way home was. "North is... negotiating," she reported.

Sable said nothing. He had been saying nothing for two days, which from Sable was practically a speech. He stood with his grey robe left behind somewhere in a snowdrift weeks ago, wearing a smuggled wool coat that made him look less like an instrument of cosmic erasure and more like a schoolmaster who'd lost his school. His eyes, though, hadn't changed. They kept moving over the empty meadow the way a man checks the treeline for wolves.

"Say it plainly," he said finally. "What are we standing in?"

"Vellichor," Emrys said. "Or the place where Vellichor was. The capital." She turned a slow circle. The hills were pretty enough—green-gold, laced with streams, a hawk riding a thermal overhead—but they were wrong the way a face is wrong when you can't place why. Too smooth. Too *unoccupied*. "The map says there should be walls under us. Towers. A river running through the middle of everything."

"There is a river," Corvin said, pointing.

There was not a river. There was a shallow dip in the meadow, a green seam winding down toward the valley floor, and when Emrys squinted at it—really squinted, the way she did at bone before the knife touched it—she thought she caught a flicker. A suggestion of silver. The memory of water, held by ground that couldn't quite let go.

"Forty years," she murmured. "It's been forty years, and the land still remembers the shape of it."

"The land has better manners than people do," Corvin said. "Come on. It gets stranger inside."

*Inside*, of course, being a matter of faith, since there was no wall to cross. But Emrys felt the boundary anyway—a sensation like stepping through a curtain of cold water, except the water was made of silence. One stride she could hear Maren's boots squelching in wet grass and Sable breathing beside her; the next stride, all of it arrived muffled, delayed, as though the sounds had to travel a long way to reach her and were tired by the journey.

She snapped her fingers.

Nothing. She felt the snap in her hand. She watched it happen. But the sound came a full heartbeat later, thin and distant, like applause from the next room.

"Oh," said Maren, delighted, and clapped her hands experimentally. Her clap arrived late too, and slightly wrong—not her clap at all, but something rounder, older, the echo of ten thousand claps layered underneath it. Maren's eyes went wide. "Did you hear that? Under mine? Everyone who ever applauded here—it's all still—" She clapped again, faster, grinning like a child at a fair. "This is the best terrible thing I've ever experienced."

"Don't encourage it," Sable said quietly.

"You don't have to be ominous every single day, you know."

"I'm not being ominous. I'm being accurate. There's a difference, and it matters." But even he had gone still, head tilted, listening to something beneath the world. When he spoke again his voice had lost its edge. "The Choir taught us that erasure is clean. That when the Cantors finish a country, there's nothing left. No residue. No grief in the stones." He looked around at the singing grass and the late-arriving sounds. "They lied to us. Or they never came here to check."

"Why wouldn't they check?"

"Because checking means admitting the work might fail," Sable said. "And the Choir doesn't admit things. Admitting is how you get eaten."

Emrys filed that away—the way he said *eaten* without flinching, the way he never flinched at anything about the Choir, which was its own kind of flinching—and walked on into the city that wasn't there.

* * *

By midday they had learned the rules of the place, or some of them, mostly by breaking them.

Rule one: sound was negotiable. Voices traveled strangely—one word arriving before the breath that shaped it, whole sentences pooling in low places like fog. When Maren hummed, the hum came back to her harmonized, dozens of voices deep, and she went quiet very quickly after that and didn't hum again for an hour.

Rule two: distance lied. Emrys would aim for a hilltop that looked twenty minutes off and arrive in four, or walk what seemed like a gentle slope for an hour without gaining any height at all. The city, she decided, had not been erased so much as *folded*, crumpled up like a letter and tucked into the landscape, and sometimes the creases showed.

Rule three—and this was the one that mattered—was the dead.

They were everywhere.

Not the way Emrys was used to dead: the faint, complaining presences that clung to relics and graveyards, half-dissolved, barely able to remember their own names. These dead were *lucid*. They stood in the meadow where streets had been, solid as noon, going about the shapes of their lives with the patient persistence of people who saw no reason to stop just because the scenery had been confiscated.

A woman crossed their path carrying a basket of bread that wasn't there, nodding good-morning to customers who weren't there, and when her eyes passed over Emrys they *saw* her—truly saw, with a frank curiosity no ghost had ever managed.

"You're new," the woman said.

"We're passing through," Emrys managed.

"Mm. Everyone says that." The woman shifted her impossible basket to her other hip. "You'll want the archive, then. Everyone who passes through wants the archive eventually. Down where the river used to argue with the tanners. Follow the smell of ink." She resumed her rounds, calling prices for bread in a currency Emrys had never heard of, and did not seem to notice that no one bought anything, and seemed perfectly content anyway.

Emrys stood very still until the woman faded between one step and the next.

"They talked to me," she said. "They never talk first. Forty years of hearing the dead complain through bits of carved femur, and not once—they wait for me to listen. They don't just *start conversations* in the street like neighbors."

"This is their city," Corvin said gently. He had grown more solid since they'd entered the boundary, his edges sharpening, his voice losing its wind-blown quality. "You're the visitor here. Mind your manners."

"I always mind my manners."

"You threatened a tollhouse ghost with a chisel last week."

"That was self-defense. He wanted my mother's *face*, Corvin."

"A lady of quality would have declined politely."

Maren laughed—the real laugh, not the harmonized one—and even Sable's mouth did the small twitch that served him for smiling. But Emrys noticed he'd positioned himself slightly behind her, the way he did when he was afraid, and that his fear had a different flavor here. Not fear of the dead. Fear *for* them.

"What happens to them," she asked quietly, "if we do what the map says? If the gate opens and Vellichor comes back?"

Sable was silent long enough that the silence itself answered.

"They're waiting," he said at last. "All of them. Every soul in this city is standing in the ruins of its own life, holding its breath, waiting for a door. And if the wrong door opens—" He stopped. Started again. "The ivory door restores the country. It doesn't restore *them*. They aren't part of the bargain anymore. They spent themselves buying it." His jaw worked. "I sang the binding myself, when I was young. I didn't know what I was singing. I thought it was a hymn."

He walked on ahead before either of them could answer, shoulders square, coat collar up against a cold only he could feel.

* * *

They made camp that night in the shell of something grand.

There was no building there—Emrys checked, ran her hands along the air, found nothing but evening wind—but the dead had built one anyway, or remembered one so hard that the memory had furniture. A great hall, or the outline of one. Columns of absence. A hearth whose fire was visible only when you looked away from it, a warm orange smudge at the corner of the eye that vanished whenever you turned to greet it.

They sat close together in the hearth's almost-light and shared hard bread and harder cheese, and the dead drifted past on their evening errands, and one of them—an old man in a scholar's robe, walking a dog made entirely of conviction—paused to tell them the weather forecast for a season that had been deleted decades ago.

"Rain Thursday," he said. "Good for the gardens."

"Thank you," said Maren solemnly.

"Garden's looking rough," the old man added, glancing at the meadow with mild disapproval, and moved on.

Emrys found herself laughing—helplessly, achingly, the laugh of someone who has been frightened for so long that joy arrives like a sneeze. Maren joined her. Even Corvin chuckled, a dry rustle like paper turning pages.

And then, because laughter opens doors that caution keeps shut, Emrys took out her knives and a length of antler and began to carve, right there, in the open, in front of everyone.

She hadn't done that since Thornfell. Carving was private. Carving was the thing she did alone at night while voices leaked into her through the grain of the material, complaints and confessions and the occasional recipe. Doing it in company felt like undressing.

But the antler in her hands was already humming, and the hum was unfamiliar—not one voice but many, braided, a chord instead of a note. And the chord was *singing*, softly, in a language she didn't know but understood anyway, the way you understand a lullaby without translating it.

"What is it making?" Maren whispered.

"I don't know yet." Emrys turned the antler. Shavings curled away. The shape emerging was nothing she'd planned: a doorway, small enough to hold in one hand, and within the doorway a figure—no, two figures, one holding the door and one passing through, and around the frame a border of tiny carved faces, mouths open, all singing. "It's carving itself. I'm just holding on."

"That's either wonderful or horrifying," Maren said.

"In my experience those are usually the same thing."

Sable leaned in slowly, the way a man approaches a horse that might bite. His eyes tracked the little singing figures around the frame, and something in his face cracked—just a hairline fracture, quickly plastered over, but Emrys had gotten good at reading his face the way she read bone.

"I know that song," he said.

"You can't. Nobody knows it. It's Vellichor's true name—that's the whole—"

"I know it." His voice had gone strange and flat. "From before. From *him*." He touched his own chest, where the Choir's binding lived, the hollow where his childhood had been scooped out and replaced with duty. "I don't remember learning it. But my mouth knows it. If I opened my lips right now I could sing every note." He looked up at her, and his eyes were terrified and furious and, underneath all of it, unbearably young. "Emrys. What if the reason I'm different isn't defection? What if the reason I remember you, the reason I've been *wrong* since the day we met—is because this place is in me? Because they tried to erase me twice, and I'm from here, and it didn't take?"

The camp went very quiet. Even the dead seemed to lean in.

Emrys set down the antler. She reached across the almost-fire and took his hand—scarred knuckles, ink-stained fingertips from all his false reports, a hand that had killed for the Choir and lied for her and was now shaking like a leaf in hers.

"Then we'll get it back," she said. "Whatever they took. All of it. That's what the archive is for, isn't it? That's what the bread woman said. Everyone comes here wanting the archive eventually."

"The archive is a story," Sable said. "A rumor the resistance told itself."

"So was a gate out of a country that doesn't exist," said Maren, "and here we sit, roasting absolutely nothing over a fire that isn't there, in a city that isn't anywhere. Your rumor and my rumor can fight it out in the morning."

Corvin drifted closer, and for once the practical courier looked almost tender. "For what it's worth," he said, "the dead here talk about the archive the way sailors talk about home ports. It's real. It's under the palace hill. And it's guarded by something that used to be a librarian, so bring patience."

Emrys looked around the hall of absence—at the columns of nothing, the hearth of maybe, the old man and his conviction-dog fading toward wherever the dead went when they were done being helpful. Somewhere under that hill, she thought, was the truth about the bargain: why a whole country had agreed to be forgotten, what plague had driven the trade, and whether the price had ever actually bought anything. Somewhere under that hill was whatever the Choir most desperately didn't want three travelers and a ghost to read.

And somewhere in the man beside her was a stolen childhood with a song in it.

"Sleep," she said, gathering her tools. "Tomorrow we find a library."

But sleep came slowly, and when it finally took her, Emrys dreamed of the city whole—towers white as fresh bone, the river loud and arguing with the tanners exactly as promised—and in the dream, ten thousand voices were singing her name.

Not *her* name, quite. Something older. Something that fit her the way the antler doorway fit her hand.

As though the city had been expecting her for forty years, and had just now checked its list, and smiled.
