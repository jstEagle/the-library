## 16. Archive of Ash

Maren had spent her whole life being told she remembered wrong.

*Wrong* was her father's word for it, delivered with the particular gentleness he reserved for things that could not be fixed. *You remember wrong, little bird. There is no such place as Vellichor. There never was.* He would say it while inking coastlines that did not exist and mountain passes that had no names on any living map, because her father was a cartographer of the impossible and a coward about exactly one thing: his own daughter's memories.

She thought about him now — three weeks dead of a fever, buried under the pear tree, his maps hidden in the false bottom of her hope chest — as she descended into a hole in the world that smelled like every library she had ever dreamed of and every fire she had ever feared.

"You're smiling," Emrys said behind her. "We're climbing down into a collapsed cellar beneath a city that officially does not exist, and you're smiling."

"I'm remembering right," Maren said. "For the first time in my life. It feels like being drunk."

"It looks like being drunk."

The stairs went down farther than stairs should. That was the first strange thing about the capital-that-wasn't: distances lied. A street that looked fifty paces long took an hour to walk. A doorway at ground level opened onto a courtyard three stories below the earth, lit by a grey light with no source that Sable refused to look at directly. The dead here were numerous — Maren felt them the way you feel humidity before rain, a pressure on the skin of the world — but they were also *lucid*, which frightened her more than ghosts ever had. In Thornfell, the dead muttered and repeated themselves. Here they watched. Here they waited, with the patience of people who had been waiting so long that patience had become their whole personality.

One of them stood at the bottom of the stairwell: an old woman in scholar's robes gone green with damp, translucent at the edges like paper held up to sun.

"You're the mapmaker's girl," the ghost said.

Maren stopped so fast that Emrys walked into her back. "How could you possibly know that?"

"Your father came through here thirty years ago. Loud young man. Argued with the shelves." The ghost's smile was dry as dust. "He said his daughter would come one day. Said you'd have his stubbornness and none of his sense." She gestured down the corridor, where rows upon rows of shelving vanished into the grey dark. "Welcome to the Archive of Ash. Mind the third step. It's been broken for forty years and I see no reason to fix it now."

* * *

The archive was a cathedral built by someone who hated cathedrals. No windows, no altars, no icons — just shelf after shelf after shelf, descending in tiers, holding tens of thousands of books, scrolls, tablets, and things that were none of those. Maren ran her fingers along a spine and found it warm. Another crumbled at her touch into ash that smelled, impossibly, of bread baking.

"They burned when the country did," the ghost — her name had been Archivist Ondile, and she had died on the last day, which she mentioned often and without self-pity — explained. "Everything here is a record of Vellichor, and Vellichor was unmade, so the records try to follow. Most of them lost. These—" she tapped a shelf of black-bound volumes that gave off no warmth at all, "—these are warded. They remember being books even if they can't remember what's inside them. Read those."

"Why warded?" Sable asked. He had been quiet since the stairwell, quiet in the specific way he got quiet when something hurt him, which Maren was learning to read the way she read weather. His grey Choir robes were gone — Emrys had dyed them brown with walnut husks in a fit of practicality that Maren privately considered the most romantic act she'd ever witnessed — but he still moved like a Choir man, still flinched from unguarded light.

"Because some truths bite," Ondile said. "And someone wanted to make sure only willing teeth got bitten."

Emrys lit lamps — actual oil lamps, because the grey light was beautiful and useless for reading — and they made camp in a reading alcove on the fourth tier, and Maren opened the first black book, and the truth of her whole life unfolded in front of her like a hand opening.

* * *

It began, as most catastrophes do, with a very reasonable decision.

The plague was called the Whitening, and it did exactly what its name promised. It didn't kill quickly. It *emptied*. The infected forgot how to speak, then how to eat, then how to be a person at all, wandering the streets of Vesper City with white eyes and pleasant, vacant smiles. The healers called it a disease of memory, though nobody understood how a sickness could eat a mind from the inside. Whole villages went quiet in a season. The dead outnumbered the living, and worse — the dead were *forgotten*, because the plague ate the memories of them too. Mothers walked past their own children's graves without a flicker of grief.

"There," Maren whispered, tracing a physician's diagram of a brain folded like a moth's wing. "That's why my family remembers. The plague never reached the border valleys. We were too far out, too poor, too—"

"Too stubborn," Emrys said gently.

"That too."

The bargain came next, and Maren read it three times because she kept hoping she'd misread it. In the ninth year of the Whitening, with two-thirds of the population already hollow, the last king of Vellichor — a man named Aldous Vane, and yes, Maren checked twice, *Vane*, her own name worn smooth by generations — convened what the records called the Quiet Parliament. Every surviving noble, every guildmaster, every hedge-witch and bone-singer and cartographer who could be fetched in time. And they voted.

Not to fight. Not to flee. To *bargain*.

There was something older than the plague beneath the mountains — the records called it the Warden of the Gate, and described it in terms that made Maren's skin crawl: a door that dreamed, a hunger with manners. The king offered it a trade. The Warden would devour the plague, root and branch, take the sickness out of the world entirely. In exchange, Vellichor would give itself up. Not its lives — its *existence*. Every memory of the country, held in every mind beyond its borders, surrendered. The land would remain, empty and unnamed, folded into the neighboring kingdoms like a letter slipped between pages. The plague would die because the plague, too, lived partly in memory — fed on the shape of communities, the architecture of families, the accumulated weight of a nation's shared past. Take away the country's coherence, and the sickness starved.

"And the Choir?" Sable asked. His voice came out strange.

Ondile answered for the book. "The bargain needed enforcement. Memory doesn't stay erased on its own — it leaks. Dreams, songs, stories told to children at bedtime. Someone had to walk the borders and eat the leaks." She looked at Sable with terrible kindness. "They took orphans, didn't they? Children with nothing to lose, because they'd already lost everything. Fed them a doctrine instead of a childhood. Taught them that forgetting was mercy."

Sable said nothing. He sat very still, the way men sit when moving would break something.

"The Grand Cantor," Maren read aloud, from a page near the end, "was appointed by King Aldous himself, and given—" she stopped. Read it again. "Given the king's own memories of the bargain, so that no one else need carry them. The Cantor remembers so that Vellichor may be forgotten."

"So the man enforcing the erasure," Emrys said slowly, "is the only person alive who knows why."

"He wouldn't be alive anymore," Ondile corrected. "Forty years. But the Choir's song preserves its singers terribly well. I'd wager your Grand Cantor remembers every day of the plague as if it were yesterday." Her voice dropped. "I'd wager he wishes he didn't."

* * *

They read until the lamps burned low. Maren read fastest, because this was hers, all of it — her wrong memories straightening one by one like crooked teeth. The festival of Lanterns-Going-Down, which she'd dreamed since childhood: real. The lullaby about the shepherd who counted stars instead of sheep: real, and apparently considered scandalously sentimental in Vesper City. The taste of honey-bread shaped like keys, which she'd once baked from a recipe that came to her in a dream and which her father had eaten in silence with wet eyes: real, real, real.

She laughed at one point — actually laughed, sitting cross-legged among the ashes of a dead nation — because one volume was a tax ledger, and even countries that sacrifice themselves to save the world keep terrible records of turnip imports, and there was something so humanly stupid about it that she had to laugh or cry, and laughing was cheaper.

Emrys wasn't laughing. Emrys sat with a medical text open in her lap, and her face had gone the particular grey-white of bone before carving.

"This cure," she said quietly. "The Warden devoured the plague. Did anyone verify?"

"What do you mean, verify? The Whitening stopped. It says so right here—"

"The Whitening stopped *inside Vellichor*," Emrys said. "Where the bargain applied. Where the country's memory-coherence was dissolved." She turned the book around. On the page was a map of the Whitening's spread, year by year, the white stain growing like frost across the land — and then, in the final year, receding everywhere except a few scattered marks far beyond the old borders. Little white dots, in distant valleys, annotated in a cramped physician's hand.

*Isolated cases persist beyond the terms of the bargain. Petitioned the Parliament for action. No reply. The Parliament no longer exists.*

Maren stared at the dots. One of them sat in the hills above Thornfell. Another, closer to the coast. Another—

Another in the valley where she'd grown up. Where her father had died of a fever last spring. A fever the local healer had called *strange*. A fever that started with forgetfulness — he'd forgotten the pear tree, at the end, forgotten her name, smiled at her with a pleasant vacant—

"No," she said.

"Maren." Emrys's voice was very soft. "When did your father start forgetting?"

"Last spring. But that's — fevers do that, everyone knows fevers—"

"When did he start forgetting *before* other things? Places before people? Songs before faces?"

The archive was silent except for the grey light humming against the warded shelves, and Maren sat with her father's death rearranging itself in her chest, every symptom sliding into a new and horrible order, and somewhere below them in the dark tiers of the Archive of Ash, Archivist Ondile said, gently, the way you say a thing you've waited forty years for someone to hear:

"Now you understand why I stayed. Somebody has to be angry about this."

* * *

They made camp that night among the shelves, because none of them could have climbed back up the lying staircase if their lives depended on it. Emrys slept badly, murmuring to the dead as she always did — Maren had learned that the bone-carver talked in her sleep like other people snored. Sable sat watch, though Maren suspected he mostly watched Emrys and pretended otherwise, and she pretended not to notice, because watching two people fall in love by refusing to admit it was the best entertainment left in the world.

Maren didn't sleep. She sat with the black books stacked around her like a fortress and read the last pages of the last volume, the ones written by the archivists in the final days, when the bargain was taking hold and the words were already starting to slip.

*To whoever finds this,* the last entry began. *We were not conquered. Remember that, if remembering is possible. We chose. Whatever anger you feel, aim it true — not at the choice, which was made with love, but at what came after. The bargain bought nothing. We paid a kingdom for a lie, and the men who knew it was a lie taught children to sing it true.*

*If you can read this, you are either a ghost or a miracle. Either way: finish it.*

Maren closed the book. In the lamplight, Sable was looking at her.

"You heard," she said.

"I heard." He hesitated. "In the Choir, when we sang the erasure hymn over a town, we were told we were mopping up. Residue. Dangerous leaks of a cursed place." His hands were flat on his knees, pressing down, as if holding himself to the floor. "We weren't mopping up. We were burying evidence."

"Yes."

"My whole life was a janitor's shift over a crime scene."

"Sable—"

"Don't." But he reached across the space between them anyway and touched the cover of the black book, lightly, the way you'd touch a wound to check it was real. "Tomorrow we keep reading. All of it. Every volume. Because if the plague came back — if it was *never fully cured* — then somebody in a grey robe has known for forty years, and let villages whiten rather than admit the bargain failed." His jaw tightened. "I want their names, Maren. I want the whole rotten ledger."

Above them, faint through forty feet of earth and forty years of silence, the wind moved over the empty valley where a capital used to be, and it carried — Maren would swear this until she died — the first bar of a lullaby about a shepherd counting stars.

She picked up the next volume and opened it to the index of names.

The Grand Cantor's name was written there, in King Aldous's own hand, and it was not the name the Choir used now. It was older. It was royal.

It was a name Maren had seen before, carved on a ruined throne in the city above, and it was going to change everything.
