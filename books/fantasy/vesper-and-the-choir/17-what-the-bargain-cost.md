## 17. What the Bargain Cost

Maren had spent her whole life being called a collector of rubbish.

Her father's maps, rolled and oiled and hidden in false-bottomed chests. Her mother's letters, written in a cipher of household words—*the blue cupboard* meaning the eastern pass, *your aunt's cough* meaning soldiers on the road. And beneath all of it, in a cellar that smelled of tallow and old paper, the memories themselves: not her own, but other people's, gathered over thirty years like a magpie gathering anything that shone wrong.

She had never once thought of them as evidence. They were treasures. Each one was a splinter of a country that everyone else insisted had never existed, and she had polished every splinter until it sang.

Now, sitting in the buried archive beneath the city that wasn't there, with Emrys's lantern throwing long shadows across shelves of ash-grey vellum, Maren spread her treasures out on a reading table and understood, for the first time, that she had been building a case.

Not a collection. A prosecution.

* * *

The archive had been Emrys's find—or rather, the dead man's find, because the dead man who had died at Emrys's door was still with them, drifting along at shoulder height like a dissatisfied cat, and it was he who had stopped before a blank stretch of wall and said, in his dry courier's voice, "There's a door there. There was always a door there. I delivered to this archive eleven times before I could grow a beard."

The door had been behind an illusion so old it had forgotten itself. When Emrys pressed her palm against the stone and asked—politely, the way she asked everything of the dead—the wall had sighed and opened like an old man unbuckling his belt.

Inside: rows upon rows of shelving, and on the shelving, ledgers. Thousands of them. The air was cold and utterly still, the way air is inside a place that has been holding its breath for forty years.

"Vellichor's records," the ghost said. "Everything they wrote down before they—" He made a gesture that might have meant *vanished* or might have meant *died*, and did not finish the sentence, which was unlike him. He finished most sentences. He complained about unfinished sentences the way other men complained about weather.

Maren had gone straight to the plague years. She knew the dates the way she knew her own birthday: the Grey Cough, the years of the shutters, the year the bargains began. Her father had drawn the quarantine lines on three separate maps, each one dated, each one smaller than the last as the sickness burned through the mountain valleys and down into the lowlands.

What she found in the ledgers confirmed everything she'd ever believed about her father's genius and nothing she'd ever believed about the world's mercy.

* * *

"Here." She pulled the ledger toward her and turned it so Emrys could see. "Read the marginalia. This one's from the last year."

Emrys read aloud, slowly, her finger under the faded script. "'Bargain terms ratified. The Choir constituted to maintain the forgetting. Compensation: cessation of the Grey Cough within Vellichor's borders, in perpetuity.'" She looked up. "In perpetuity."

"That's what they told people," Maren said. "That's what my father believed when he drew those maps. That's what *everyone* believed—that the country gave itself up, its name, its borders, its place in every memory and record, and in exchange the plague would never come back. A fair trade. A terrible, noble trade." Her voice cracked on *noble*, and she hated it, and kept going anyway. "My father wept when he copied those terms out. He thought he was preserving a martyrdom."

"And?"

"And here." Maren opened the second ledger, the one she'd carried down from the shelf with both arms because it was heavier than a sleeping toddler. "This is the Choir's own accounting. Their internal record. Look at the entry from nine years after the erasure."

Emrys bent over the page. The lantern light caught the angles of her face—she was a thin woman, all wrists and watchfulness, with hands that looked like they'd been carved by someone very skilled and slightly hurried—and Maren watched her expression do something complicated.

"'Incidence of Grey Cough,'" Emrys read. "'Within former Vellichor boundaries. Nine cases. Eleven cases. Fourteen.'" She stopped. "It came back."

"It came back," Maren said. "Nine years later. The bargain bought nine years. Maybe the magic was real, maybe it wasn't, maybe the plague just took a holiday—but it came back, Emrys. It swept through the villages where Vellichor used to be, through people who couldn't even remember there had ever been a country there to protect them, and it killed them exactly as thoroughly as it would have killed their grandparents. All those memories, all those names, all that grief—and for *nothing*."

The silence in the archive was the specific silence of a very large room absorbing a very large anger.

"But," Emrys said carefully, "maybe the Choir didn't know. Maybe the bargain failed and they—"

"Turn the page."

Emrys turned the page.

"'Incidence reported to the Grand Cantor,'" she read, and her voice had gone flat and careful, the voice of someone walking on ice. "'Cantor's instruction: figures to be recorded but not disseminated. The bargain stands. The forgetting continues. Note for the treasury: no adjustment to singer allotments required.'"

"No adjustment to singer allotments required," Maren repeated. She hadn't meant to say it out loud. It came out of her like a stone leaving a sling. "People were dying. The thing they'd sold a whole country to prevent was happening anyway, right on schedule, and the Grand Cantor's response was a *budgetary note*. No adjustment to singer allotments. The machine keeps running. The song keeps singing. Because if anyone ever admitted the bargain had failed—if anyone ever let that word get out—then forty years of erasure would have been for nothing, and every grey robe in that order would have to look at what they'd actually done."

* * *

They worked through the night. There was no question of not working through the night; Maren wouldn't have slept if you'd paid her, and Emrys seemed constitutionally incapable of leaving a dead document unread, and the ghost hovered between them making small sounds of vindication whenever another damning entry surfaced.

By lantern-light, Maren laid out her own collection beside the ledgers, and the two sets of records began to talk to each other.

"This is why I need your memories," she said, setting a small carved token on the table—a wooden bird, worn smooth by decades of handling. "Mrs. Aldessa from the valley village. She remembered a lullaby nobody taught her. She used to hum it while churning butter and couldn't tell you where it came from. I traded her a week of firewood for the memory of learning it." Maren touched the bird gently. "It's a Vellichor counting song. My father had the melody transcribed. It counts sheep, and then it counts children, and then—it counts the dead. It's a plague song, Emrys. It's a song for keeping tally during the Grey Cough."

"You think it came back with the plague," Emrys said slowly. "Fragments of the country. Leaking back in."

"I *know* it did. Look—" She spread three more tokens: a river stone, a scrap of embroidery, a child's clay whistle. "A man in the pass villages who dreamed in a language he didn't speak, every night, for twenty years, and woke weeping. A woman whose grandmother's recipe book had a page in it none of the family could read—until I showed it to a scholar who recognized Old Vellichori script. A boy who whistled a tune that made old dogs howl and sit down like they were mourning." Her hand trembled slightly on the whistle. "The country didn't die cleanly. It's been bleeding through the seams for forty years. Little pieces of it, haunting the edges of people's minds, and nobody knowing what they were looking at. And the Choir—the Choir has been *sweeping*. Every fragment they find, they erase. Not because the fragments are dangerous. Because each one is proof."

"Proof the bargain failed," Emrys said.

"Proof the whole thing was a lie from the ninth year onward." Maren stood up. She hadn't planned to stand up; her legs simply decided. "Do you understand what I'm saying? The plague came back. People died. Children died, with names that had been stolen from them, in villages that officially had never existed, of a disease that officially had been banished forever. And the men who knew—the men who *kept the ledgers*—they chose the lie. They chose it every single day for thirty-one years. They're choosing it right now, tonight, somewhere up in their sanctum, singing their tidy little forgetting-song while the evidence sits in this room gathering dust."

Her voice had risen. She noticed distantly that it had risen, and found that she did not care even slightly.

"My father spent his life hiding," she said. "Hiding maps, hiding papers, teaching me cipher and caution and how to be small. He thought he was protecting the truth. He thought the truth was fragile, something you had to keep safe until gentler times. He died believing that." She picked up the clay whistle and closed her fist around it. "He was wrong. The truth isn't fragile. The truth is a corpse in a cellar, and somebody killed it, and I have spent thirty years dusting the body instead of finding the murderer."

* * *

Emrys had gone quiet in the way Maren was learning meant she was listening to more than one conversation at once.

"What is it?" Maren asked. "What are they saying?" Because Emrys heard the dead, and in this city the dead were everywhere and unusually articulate, and Maren had learned that half of Emrys's silences were crowded ones.

"The archivist," Emrys said. "There's an archivist here. Has been since the end. She's... she says she can show us where the orders went. The Cantor's instructions. The original correspondence." She paused, and her face did the thing it did when the dead said something that cost them. "She says she stayed because someone should witness. Forty years, and no one ever came, and she stayed anyway, filing and witnessing, in case."

"In case of what?"

"In case of us, I suppose." Emrys rose. "Come on. She wants to show you something specifically. She says—" and here Emrys's mouth twisted, wry and sad at once, "—she says she's been waiting thirty years for a stubborn girl with her father's maps, and she hopes we don't take that as flattery, because it's just accurate record-keeping."

* * *

The archivist's alcove was deeper in the archive, past the ledgers, behind a curtain gone to lace with age. The ghost of a small round woman stood amid shelves of correspondence, and she had the brisk, unimpressed air of someone who had organized eternity and found it wanting.

"She says her name was Tamsin Holt," Emrys relayed. "Under-archivist, third rank. She handled the Cantor's outgoing instructions."

Tamsin Holt spoke, and Emrys translated, and the translation came in pieces, the way water comes through a cracked jug.

"The first years, the orders were... ceremonial. Maintenance. Sing the bounds, sweep the fragments, keep the peace. Then the plague reports started coming in from the field singers, and the tone changed." Emrys's brow furrowed. "She's showing me a specific letter. The Cantor's own hand. To the wardens of the western reaches."

"What does it say?"

Emrys was silent a moment. When she spoke again, her voice had dropped into something very controlled, which Maren had learned was worse than shouting.

"It says: 'You will report no further incidence of the Grey Cough within the bounds. You will treat such reports as error. You will correct the reporters.' And then—" Emrys stopped. Started again. "And then there's a list. Names. Field singers who reported too many cases, too accurately. The instruction after the names is one word."

"*Corrected*," Maren said.

"*Corrected*," Emrys agreed. "Meaning erased. Meaning the Choir ate its own members for telling the truth about dead children." She looked up at Maren, and her eyes in the lantern light were furious and bright. "Sable's order. His whole order. He thinks he defected from a tragedy. He defected from a *cover-up*."

"He doesn't know yet," Maren said.

"No. And he's going to—" Emrys broke off, because the archivist was speaking again, urgently, her round ghost-face animated, her hands sketching shapes in the air.

"She says there's more," Emrys said. "She says the Cantor didn't just conceal the failure. She says—" Emrys listened, and Maren watched the color leave her face by degrees, like a tide going out. "She says the Cantor *expected* it. Before the bargain. There were projections. Models. The archivists ran the numbers forty years ago, and the numbers said the bargain would hold for less than a decade. He signed it anyway. He signed a whole country away knowing it would buy almost nothing, because the alternative was—" Emrys pressed her fingers to her eyes. "Because the alternative was admitting what the plague really was. Where it came from. And that, apparently, was a truth too heavy even for erasure."

"Where did it come from?" Maren demanded. "Emrys. Where did the plague come from?"

But the archivist had gone still, and her stillness had a quality Maren recognized from her father's last days—the stillness of someone standing at the edge of something they cannot unsay.

"She won't say it directly," Emrys whispered. "She says it's not hers to tell. She says it's written somewhere better than her memory. She says—" Emrys tilted her head, listening, and then laughed, short and disbelieving. "She says check the Cantor's private seal-correspondence. Third shelf, black binding, wax unbroken. Nobody's broken those seals in forty years because nobody alive can read the cipher."

Maren was already moving toward the shelf. "My father could," she said. "And he taught me."

* * *

The black-bound letters were heavy in her hands, the wax seals intact, the Cantor's private cipher marching across the pages in tight, beautiful columns. Maren sat down on the floor right there, because her legs had opinions again, and set to work.

The cipher was elegant—household words, like her mother's letters, but older, denser, built on a vocabulary Maren suspected only someone raised on Old Vellichori songs would fully unlock. Fortunately, she had spent thirty years collecting lullabies.

It took her until the grey light of dawn was seeping down through whatever passed for sky above a city that wasn't there. Emrys dozed against a shelf with the ghost-courier standing guard, and Maren decoded, and decoded, and decoded, and with every letter her fury cooled and hardened into something with edges.

Because the letters weren't about the plague at all.

They were about the gate.

* * *

"Maren." Emrys's voice, soft, from very close. "You've been reading for hours. What is it? What did you find?"

Maren looked up. Her eyes felt full of sand. In her lap lay the last letter, its seal cracked open, its final line underlined twice in the Cantor's own hand.

"The Vesper Gate isn't a door out of Vellichor," she said. "I had it backwards. We all did. The map, the stories, even the dead man's errand—we thought the gate was an exit." She held the letter up, and her hand was steady now, steadier than it had been in years, because rage, properly refined, is the steadiest thing in the world. "The gate is the lock. The Cantor built it. The bargain, the Choir, the erasure—all of it, every piece, exists to keep that door shut from the *inside*. And the last line here—he wrote it to himself, Emrys, it's a note in his own private cipher, like a man leaving a message in a bottle—"

She read it aloud, translating as she went, and the words fell into the cold archive air like stones into a well:

"*If any soul ever reaches the gate, know this: what sleeps beyond it must not wake, and I am the only one left who remembers why.*"

Above them, far above, faint as a thread of smoke, something began to sing.

Emrys was on her feet instantly, head cocked, listening with her whole body. "That's not the dead," she said. "That's not anyone dead. Maren—that's the Choir. That's the counter-song. They've found us."

Maren folded the letter into her coat, over her heart, next to her father's compass, and stood.

"Then we'd better move," she said. "Because I'm not done asking questions. And neither, I think, is he."
