## 18. Interlude: The Choir's Song

They did not bind him when they brought him in.

That was the first thing Sable noticed, and the noticing itself felt like a small betrayal of his own surrender. He had expected ropes, iron, a hood scented with ash and old hymnals. Instead two grey-robed brothers walked him through the sanctum gates with hands on his elbows, gentle as ushers at a wedding, and the doors closed behind him with a sound like a held breath being released.

The Choir's sanctum sat inside a mountain that was not on any map — which was fitting, since erasing things from maps was rather their whole trade. It had been carved long ago out of living stone, and the stone still remembered being alive. Sable could feel it under his bare feet as they led him down the processional stair: a faint pulse, slow and patient, like something sleeping that had agreed to be furniture.

He had grown up here. He knew that now, in the way you know a fact about a stranger whose face you've studied too long. He'd been brought through these same doors at six years old, an orphan with frostbitten fingers and no name worth keeping, and he had sung his first song on this very stair while the older voices ate his past one memory at a time, politely, in order, like diners working through courses.

He could not remember any of it. But his feet remembered the stairs. His body flinched at exactly the right places, three steps before each turn, and that flinching was its own kind of grief.

*Emrys,* he thought, deliberately, the way a man presses his tongue against a sore tooth. *Her name is Emrys Thackeray. She carves bones. She hums when she works and denies it afterward.*

The thought held. For now.

* * *

They put him in a cell that was not a cell.

It had a bed, a washbasin, a window of thick glass looking out onto nothing but white mountain cloud. There was even a carafe of water and a plate of bread and hard cheese, arranged neatly, which struck Sable as the single most sinister thing he had seen all day. The Choir did not punish, he understood suddenly. The Choir *corrected*. You were not a prisoner here; you were an instrument that had gone out of tune, and instruments were not starved or beaten. They were cleaned. They were retuned. And if they could not be retuned —

Well. Broken strings went into the fire, and the fire sang too.

He sat on the bed and waited, because waiting was a skill the Choir had taught him well, and because there was nothing else to do except count the ways he might have escaped, all of which required either a army or a miracle, and he'd left both behind in a snowdrift somewhere above the Cartographer's valley.

*She got away.* He held onto that the way a drowning man holds a plank. *Maren has the key. Emrys is moving toward the gate. Every hour I sit here eating their cheese is an hour they don't spend hunting her.*

It was almost enough to make the cheese taste like victory. Almost. It mostly tasted like cheese.

* * *

On the second day, the Grand Cantor came to hear him sing.

Sable heard him before he saw him — everyone always did. The Cantor's voice arrived in a corridor several seconds before the rest of him, a low warm hum that made the lamp flames lean toward it like flowers toward sun. By the time the old man shuffled into view, leaning on a staff of white bone carved with ten thousand tiny names, Sable's own throat ached with the urge to harmonize.

That was how the Cantor worked. He didn't command. He invited. The whole architecture of the Choir was built on invitation — come, sing, rest, forget — and the horror of it was that every invitation was sincere.

"Brother Sable." The Cantor settled onto the cell's one chair with the creaking care of a man assembled largely from hinges. Up close, his eyes were the pale grey of old ice, and kind, and utterly bottomless. "You've been eating my cheese."

"Brother Cantor." Sable kept his eyes lowered, the posture of a penitent. "I apologize for the theft."

"Theft." A dry chuckle, like paper turning. "Child, I have eaten kingdoms. Do you imagine I begrudge you cheese?" He leaned forward. "Tell me what happened in the pass. Tell me truly. You know I will know if you don't."

And there it was. Sable had rehearsed this conversation for two days, lying awake in the comfortable bed, arranging and rearranging his lies like a man stacking stones across a river. He had prepared a story about ambush, about being overwhelmed, about hunters lost. He had prepared a story about cowardice, which would be punished but believed. He had even prepared a story about madness, which the Choir had a procedure for.

What he said was: "I hesitated."

Silence. The lamps leaned. Somewhere far below, the mountain pulsed.

"I was ordered to execute her," Sable went on, staring at his own hands, "and I hesitated. Brother Corvane moved to do it himself, and I —" He let the sentence die. Let them fill in whatever they wanted to fill in. A lie, he had learned long ago, was strongest when ninety percent of it was true and the remaining ten percent was silence.

"And why," the Cantor asked softly, "did you hesitate?"

*Because she looks at me like I'm a person,* Sable did not say. *Because when she laughs she does it sideways, like she's embarrassed by it. Because I have eaten nothing my whole life and she keeps trying to feed me.*

"She spared me once," he said instead. "In Thornfell. She had me at knifepoint over a carving bench and let me walk away. I owed her a debt. Debts are —" he searched for the word the Choir would want — "tidy."

"Mm." The Cantor regarded him for a long moment. Then he smiled, and it was the worst thing yet, because it was genuinely warm. "You know what I find interesting, Brother Sable? Liars give tidy answers. Truth is never tidy. Truth is a bird's nest of string and bone." He rose, joints crackling like green wood in a fire. "But we will return to that. First, I want you to hear something. As a courtesy. You were one of mine, after all. Perhaps you still are."

* * *

They took him down, down, below the cells, below the archive vaults, into the heart of the mountain where the Choir kept the thing they actually worshipped.

It was not a god. Sable had always half-believed there was a god at the center of the Choir, some hungry saint of forgetting. There wasn't. There was only a hall — vast, round, acoustically perfect — and in the center of the hall, a pool of still black water, and around the pool, forty singers in grey, standing in a ring, and above the pool, hanging in the air like frost forming on invisible glass:

A song.

Not performed. *Stored.* The Choir's great work, the Erasure Hymn, the counter-melody that had unwritten a whole country from the world's memory — it hung there, sustained note by note by the rotating ring of singers, day and night, year upon year, a chord that had been sounding without interruption for forty years. Vellichor's name lived inside it, folded up small, silenced. As long as the chord held, no one remembered. As long as the chord held, the country stayed dead.

Sable stood at the edge of the hall and felt the song move through him like cold water through cloth, and understood, with a lurch, that he had stood in this exact spot hundreds of times. That this was where he had been tuned. That part of the reason he could still remember Emrys — part of it — was that he had spent years absorbing this song until it lived in his bones, and bones, apparently, kept their own books.

The Cantor watched him watch it. "You feel it," he said. Not a question.

"Yes, Brother."

"Then you will understand my problem." The old man began to walk the ring's circumference, slow, staff tapping, and the singers' faces followed him without turning their heads, which was a trick Sable had never gotten used to. "Forty years, this chord has held. Forty years, and it has never once needed mending. A country erased is a country *erased*, child. Dead things do not struggle." He stopped. Turned. "And yet. Three weeks ago, a thread of it frayed. A name, spoken aloud in a high pass, that should have been unpronounceable. A map surfacing after four decades in a dead courier's coat. A defector who remembers a woman he was ordered to forget." The pale eyes fixed on Sable, mild as milk. "You see my difficulty."

"You think someone is singing back," Sable said.

"I think," the Cantor said, "that someone found the last door. And doors, Brother Sable, are just songs with hinges."

* * *

They gave him a room near the rehearsal galleries — not freedom, but the shape of it — and for three days Sable listened to the Grand Cantor compose.

This was the part that frightened him most, and he catalogued the fear carefully, because fear was information and information was the only weapon he had left. The Cantor worked the way Emrys carved: total absorption, hands moving before the mind finished deciding, occasional muttering, occasional fury at his own materials. He spread sheets of vellum across a long table and wrote music on them in ink mixed, Sable suspected, with something worse than ink. And the music he wrote was beautiful.

That was the trap of the Choir, Sable thought, watching from the gallery above. Not cruelty. Cruelty he could have hated cleanly. No — the trap was that everything they made was *lovely*. The erasure hymn was lovely. The Cantor's new composition was lovely. If you listened long enough, you forgot it was a knife, because knives this elegant seemed like they must be meant for something else.

"What is it called?" he asked on the third evening, when the Cantor paused to rub ink from his fingers and acknowledged Sable's presence with a nod, as though Sable were an apprentice who had earned small privileges.

"A counter-song needs no name," the Cantor said. "Names are for things that intend to persist." He tapped the vellum. "But informally? The singers are calling it *the Unmaking of the Bone-Carver*."

The words went through Sable like a blade finding the gap between ribs.

"They have a name for her," he managed.

"Oh, they have more than a name." The Cantor's tone was that of a man discussing weather. "Your bone-carver has become an inconvenience, and inconveniences must be addressed. She walks roads that exist only in the memories of the dead — so we will ask the dead, and the dead owe us. She carries a map drawn by Maren of the long-fingered hands — so we will burn the valley village she visited, gently, and unremember its people, gently, and she will carry the weight of that. And when we finally stand in front of her—" he smiled again, that warm, bottomless smile— "we will not fight her at all. We will simply sing, and she will never have existed, and you, Brother Sable, will be standing in a snowdrift wondering why your chest hurts."

"You can't erase a person," Sable said. "People aren't countries. People are —"

"People are songs too," the Cantor said pleasantly. "Everything is. That is rather the point. A country is a very large song, a person a very small one, and I have been conducting for longer than your language has had a future tense." He rolled the vellum closed. "You may go. Rest. Eat. In four days we march for the Vesper Gate, and you will march with us — in front, in fact, since you know her habits, her routes, her cleverness." A pause, delicate as a knife laid on silk. "Unless there is some reason you shouldn't?"

Every instinct Sable owned screamed at him to say yes, to refuse, to spit. And every instinct was wrong, and he knew it, because refusal would buy him a tuning he would not survive with his one precious memory intact.

"No, Brother Cantor," he said, and bowed. "There is no reason."

"Liar," the Cantor said fondly, already turning back to his table. "But a loyal one, I think. We shall see. Four days, Brother Sable. Try to remember whose song you belong to."

* * *

That night, in the room that was not a cell, Sable sat on the narrow bed and did the arithmetic of despair, and then, because despair was a luxury, did the arithmetic of hope instead.

Four days. The gate was nine days' hard walk from the sanctum for the Choir's column, slower with forty singers and an old man's pace — call it eleven. Emrys and Maren had four days' lead and a ghost for a guide and, knowing Emrys, a plan that was half genius and half falling down a hillside and calling it a shortcut.

It could be done. It would be close. It would require him to betray the Choir from inside its own marching column, in front of the most dangerous singer alive, using nothing but his wits and a childhood he couldn't remember.

Sable lay back and pressed his tongue against the sore tooth one more time.

*Her name is Emrys Thackeray. She carves bones. She hums when she works. She fed me soup in a ruined tollhouse and pretended it wasn't kindness. She is walking toward a door right now, and in four days I am going to walk behind her singing the wrong notes.*

He closed his eyes. Below him, faint through the mountain's living stone, the great chord held its long grey note, patient as erosion.

And beneath it — so faint he might have imagined it, so faint it might have been only the mountain remembering — Sable thought he heard a second melody, thin and stubborn and human, threading upward through the rock like a root through a wall.

Somewhere out there, someone was humming.

He smiled in the dark, and began, silently, to practice.
