## 19. Sable Taken

The archive key was small enough to be insulting.

Emrys turned it over in her palm as they walked—a stub of black iron no longer than her thumb, shaped like a bird with folded wings. It had cost them a descent into a flooded cellar, three locked doors, and one very undignified moment when Maren had gotten her boot stuck in a grate and Emrys had laughed so hard she'd cried. The key was warm to the touch, which keys should not be, and it hummed faintly when Sable came near, which keys really should not do.

"It likes him," Maren said, watching it over Emrys's shoulder.

"Keys don't like people."

"This one does. Look—it's practically purring."

Sable walked a few paces ahead, the way he always did now, positioning himself between them and whatever might come from the north. He'd been doing that since the cave, since the snow, since the morning he'd knelt beside a dead man in white and hadn't looked at his hands for an hour afterward. He carried guilt the way other men carried packs—strapped on, adjusted constantly, never once set down.

"The key responds to Choir work," he said without turning around. "It's made from the same craft. That's all."

"That's *purring*," Maren insisted.

Emrys tucked it into the inner pocket of her coat, against her heart, where the map used to live. "Then let's hope whatever it opens likes us too."

They were two days out of the ruined capital, following the ghost of the courier—Wick, he'd finally told them his name was, Wick of the Low Houses, and he said it with the relief of a man who hadn't heard his own name spoken in forty years—along a road that only existed because the dead remembered walking it. The road was strange underfoot. Solid where memory held firm, soft as moss where it frayed. Emrys had learned to read its moods: where the paving stones showed through the grass, the dead were near and attentive; where the road thinned to a suggestion, they had wandered off to attend to whatever dead things attended to.

Today the road was holding beautifully. The morning sun came through the larches in long gold blades, and for the first hour it was almost pleasant, and Emrys hated that she noticed that, because she had learned by now that pleasant was what the world offered right before it took something.

"You're doing the thing," Maren said quietly.

"What thing?"

"The thing where you enjoy yourself and then immediately look guilty about it."

"I don't—" Emrys started, and then stopped, because she absolutely did. "It's not guilty. It's *vigilant*."

"It's guilty with better posture," said Sable, ahead of them, not turning around.

Maren snorted. Even Wick, drifting along at their left like a man strolling beside a hedge, made a sound that might have been a laugh if laughter could be made of cold air and regret.

And then the birds stopped.

Not flew away—stopped. All of them, at once, mid-song, the way a choir cuts off when the conductor raises his fist. The silence dropped over the road like a lid.

Emrys's hand found the bone knife at her belt before her mind caught up. "Sable."

"I know." He had gone very still, head tilted, listening to something beneath the silence. His face did the thing it did when the old training woke in him—the softness draining out, the stillness arriving, the face of a man who had once stood in grey robes and sung people out of existence. She knew he hated that face. She also knew, lately, that she was glad of it.

"How many?" she asked.

He closed his eyes. "Four singers. Maybe five." A pause. "And a Cantor's voice underneath them. Holding the song together." His jaw tightened. "That's a hunting formation. Not a patrol. They're not looking for us generally." He opened his eyes and looked at her, and there it was, plain as a confession: *they're looking for you specifically.*

"They followed us from the archive," Maren whispered. "The water. The doors. Someone knew."

"The key hums," Sable said simply. "For forty years nothing has hummed. They would hear it across the whole country."

The trees ahead began to change. It was subtle at first—a greyness in the light, as though someone had stirred ash into the gold—and then Emrys saw them standing among the larches: figures in grey robes, spaced evenly, patient as fence posts. Their hoods were up. Their faces, when the light touched them, were smooth and calm and terribly kind, the faces of people who were about to explain something to you forever.

"Sable Vane." The voice came from everywhere and nowhere, layered, four throats singing one word. "Daughter of the Choir. You are called home."

"I'm not going home," Sable said. His hand found Emrys's wrist—not gripping, just touching, an anchor. "I'm already home."

*Oh,* thought Emrys, absurdly, at the worst possible moment. *Oh, that's what that feels like.*

"Your name is being struck from the rolls even now," the voices sang gently. "Resist, and it will be struck from everything else. You know how this goes. You have done this to others."

"I know exactly how this goes," Sable said. "I've written the verses."

The singers began to move. Not fast. They never moved fast; that was the horror of them, the patience, the sense that time itself was on their side and everyone else was simply early. They came down toward the road like weather.

"Maren." Sable's voice had changed—low, quick, the voice of a man giving orders, and Emrys realized with a cold drop that this was the first time she'd ever heard him use it. "The ravine, back the way we came, two hundred paces. There's a culvert under the old roadbed. The dead remember it; the living built it; it will hold both your weights. Go now. Don't run—walk fast. Running makes noise. Noise gives direction."

"And you?" Maren said.

"I'm going to have a conversation."

"No," Emrys said. It came out flat and immediate, before thought, the way her hand went to the knife. "No, we go together or—"

"Emrys." He turned fully to her then, and took both her hands, and the key in her pocket hummed so hard she felt it through her ribs. His eyes were brown and ordinary and the only thing in the world she was afraid to lose. "Listen to me. Four singers can be outrun. Five can't. And there's a Cantor's thread in their song, which means they've been given leave to unmake, which means every second we stand here arguing, the net gets smaller." He squeezed her hands. "You have the key. You have the map in your bones—you've had it since the night I bled on your doorstep. You don't need me to finish this."

"That's not the point," she said, and her voice cracked on it, and she hated the crack, and kept talking anyway. "The point is I didn't spend a month learning how you take your tea and which side of the bed you pretend not to prefer and the way you say *hmm* when Maren's lying about eating the last of the cheese—I didn't learn all that just to—"

"To what?" He smiled, and it was the worst smile she'd ever seen, because it was gentle. "Emrys. I defected four days ago. They don't want me back. They want me *ended*, as an example. If I go with you, I'm a weight you carry and a door they follow. If I stay—" he shrugged, that infuriating economical shrug, "—I'm a conversation. And conversations buy time."

"There has to be another way."

"There's always another way," he agreed. "We don't have time to find it."

The singers were thirty paces off the road now. The nearest one tilted its hooded head, and beneath the hood Emrys saw—not a face, exactly, but the memory of one, smoothed over like a carving sanded past recognition. Its mouth opened and it began, softly, to sing a note that made her teeth ache and her mother's face flicker at the edges of her mind—that precious paid-away face, threatening to fray further just from proximity.

*Mother's face,* she thought wildly. *They can take what's left. They can take the shape of the loss.*

"No," she said again, but it wasn't a refusal anymore. It was grief arriving early.

Sable cupped her cheek with one hand. His palm was cold from the mountain mornings and steady as a table.

"When you get to the gate," he said, "and you will get to the gate, because you are the single most stubborn person I have ever met, and I once served under a woman who argued with a glacier—when you get there, open the horn door. Not ivory. Horn. Whatever it costs, whatever it asks, check twice what it's taking and who it's taking it from." His thumb brushed her cheekbone. "And Emrys. If there's anything left of me worth keeping—"

"Don't," she whispered.

"—keep it," he finished. "That's all. Just keep it. That's the whole errand."

Then he stepped back, squared his shoulders, and raised his voice into the singer's cadence—she heard the Choir's harmonics enter his throat like a language remembered, and it broke her heart in a brand-new place to hear it.

"I'll come home," he told the grey figures. "On conditions."

The lead singer paused. Interest, in that smooth face, looked almost human. "Name them."

"The two women walk. Unfollowed. Unsung. You give me your note on it—your binding note—and I give you my surrender, freely, no hunt required. Think of the economy of it. No chase. No blood. No report of failure reaching the Grand Cantor." He spread his empty hands. "Just a lost daughter, coming home."

The singers conferred in a silence that wasn't silent—a rustle of harmonics, a negotiation in chords. Emrys grabbed Maren's sleeve. Maren grabbed back, eyes blazing, mouth shaping *no, no, no*, and Emrys said, low and fierce, "If we stay, it was for nothing. If we go, it was for something. Move."

They moved. Walked fast, as instructed, backs straight, hearts hammering, while behind them Sable's voice rose and fell in the old liturgical patterns, trading himself away clause by clause. At fifty paces Emrys risked one look back.

He was watching her. Of course he was. The singers had begun to close around him like grey water, and he stood in the middle of it with his chin up and his hands loose at his sides, and he mouthed something to her across the distance. Three words. She couldn't hear them over the rising song—but she didn't need to. Some words don't travel by air.

She mouthed them back.

Then the culvert swallowed them, and the song faded, and the world got very small and dark and dripping.

* * *

The culvert ran forty feet under the old roadbed, ankle-deep in snowmelt, pitch black except for the pale glow Wick shed when he concentrated. He was concentrating hard now, brighter than Emrys had ever seen him, lighting the way like a lantern with an opinion.

"Faster," Wick said. "Girl, faster. Binding notes unravel. Everyone knows that. Half the ghosts in this country are here because someone trusted a Choir promise."

"He wouldn't have done it if—" Maren started.

"If it weren't the only way? Aye, likely true." Wick's glow guttered, flared. "Doesn't make promises keep themselves."

Emrys said nothing. She counted steps. Forty feet of culvert meant roughly sixty steps, and she was on step nineteen, and step nineteen was as far as her legs would carry her before her knees informed her they were resigning. She stopped, braced a hand against wet stone, and breathed.

The key was humming against her heart. Harder than before. Almost keening.

"It knows," Maren said softly. She'd stopped too, her hand hovering near Emrys's pocket without touching. "Emrys. It knows he's gone."

"It doesn't *know* anything. It's a piece of iron."

"It's a piece of iron that purred for him," Maren said, "and it's grieving, and honestly, so am I, and I think you're allowed to as well."

Emrys pressed her palm flat over the pocket. Through the leather and the linen and the bone of her own ribs, the little iron bird beat its warmth against her hand like a second pulse, frantic, calling.

And she understood, suddenly, with the terrible clarity that grief sometimes brings like a slap, why it was humming.

"It's not grieving," she said. Her voice came out strange. Steady. "It's *singing*. Listen—it's not random. It's a note. One note, held." She pulled the key out, and in Wick's glow the black iron shone, and the sound coming off it was thin and high and unmistakable: the exact pitch, she realized, of the last thing Sable had sung before the singers closed around him. The binding note. The promise.

"He gave them his surrender in the Choir's own harmony," she breathed. "And the key heard it. The key *keeps* it." Her fingers closed around the bird until the edges bit. "Which means somewhere, right now, there is a record of what they promised him. A binding note is a contract, Wick—you said so yourself, half the ghosts in this country—"

"Aye," Wick said slowly, catching up to her thought the way the dead catch up to everything, inevitably. "And contracts between singers can be *sung apart*. By anyone who holds the note."

Maren looked between them, snowmelt soaking through her boots, her father's compass clutched in one fist. "So we come back for him."

"We come back for him," Emrys said. "After the gate. After Vellichor. Because if I go back now, with four singers and a Cantor's thread between me and him, I lose him *and* the country *and* probably Maren, and he knew that, the insufferable, self-sacrificing, tea-drinking—" Her voice failed. She stood in the dark of the culvert with an iron bird singing in her fist and let it fail, for exactly three seconds, which was all she allowed herself.

Then she put the key away, against her heart, where it hummed its one stubborn note like a man keeping a promise, and lifted her chin.

"Wick," she said. "How far to the gate?"

The ghost's glow steadied. "Three days, if the roads hold. Two, if you're angry enough to frighten them flat."

"Then we walk angry," said Emrys Thackeray, and stepped out of the culvert into the grey mountain light, heading for the last door out of Vellichor—with a key, a compass, a ghost, and a note in her pocket that she intended, someday soon, to sing back.
