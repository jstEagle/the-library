## 20. The Empty Road

The road ran out three days after Sable was taken, and Emrys stopped walking.

It wasn't a dramatic stopping. There was no throwing down of the pack, no kneeling in the mud to curse the heavens. She simply found that her feet had made the decision for her, the way they always had — thirty years of carving bone taught you that the body knew things before the mind agreed to them — and when she looked up, she was standing at the edge of a meadow full of wet grass and half-dead wildflowers, and behind her the road wound back the way they'd come, and ahead of it simply... ended. A farmer's track, overgrown, going nowhere in particular.

"Well," she said aloud, to no one. "That's convenient."

Maren was somewhere behind her, still asleep in the hollow where they'd made camp, or pretending to be. The archive key sat against Emrys's sternum on its cord, warm as a held hand, and every time she breathed she felt it press against her like an accusation.

She sat down in the grass. It was damp. She didn't care.

*You could go back,* said the small voice in her head, the one that sounded suspiciously like her mother, if her mother had been the sort to say sensible cruel things instead of singing while she cooked. *You could go back and trade yourself for him. That's what people do in stories.*

"People in stories," Emrys told the meadow, "are idiots."

But her hands were shaking, and it wasn't the cold.

Three days. Three days since he'd stepped out of the treeline with his hands up and his grey robe already unclasped, since he'd said *run* in that infuriatingly calm voice, since Maren had dragged her bodily into the bracken while Choir agents closed around him like water closing around a stone. Three days since Emrys had done exactly what he told her to do, which was the first time in her life she'd ever obeyed anyone, and she'd done it because her legs had decided for her again, and now her legs had decided this, too: *stop*.

She thought about the way he'd looked at her the night before the ambush, by the fire, when he'd almost said something and then carved a piece of pine instead, badly, because Choir men were trained in memory and not in whittling. She'd teased him about it. He'd said, *I'm making you a charm. It doesn't have to be good. It has to be mine.* And she'd laughed, and taken it, and put it in her pocket, and it was in her pocket right now, a lopsided little bird with one wing longer than the other, and she took it out and held it and felt nothing come off it at all, because it wasn't dead yet, it had never been alive, it was just wood and his knife and three hours of his hands.

"I should have gone back," she said.

"You shouldn't have," said the ghost.

Emrys didn't jump. She'd known he was there for the last hundred yards, actually — the dead had a texture to them, a coolness in the air like the moment before rain, and this particular dead man had been trailing her on and off for weeks now, patient as a debt. But she turned anyway, because some courtesies mattered even to a woman talking to empty air in a wet field.

The courier stood at the edge of the road where it died. He looked better than he had the night he'd bled out against her workshop door; ghosts improved with practice, or perhaps with distance from the manner of their dying. His coat was whole again. His hands were steady. Only his eyes kept that terrible clarity, the look of someone who has seen the far side of everything and found it mostly paperwork.

"Corvin," she said. "I thought you'd crossed over."

"I did." He considered this. "It's overrated. Very quiet. Like a library where all the books are about you." He drifted a step closer, boots not quite disturbing the grass. "Then I heard someone down here thinking very loudly about trading herself to the Choir, and I thought, well. Someone has to say something."

"Go away, Corvin."

"No."

"I'm resting."

"You're sulking."

"I am *grieving*," Emrys said, with dignity, "and there is a difference, and the difference is that grieving is respectable."

"Mm." Corvin settled onto the air as though it were a fence rail. "When I was dying on your floor — and thank you again, by the way, for holding my head and lying to me about how bad it looked, that was very kind and I knew every word was false —"

"You're welcome."

"— there was a moment when I wanted to take the map back. Not to finish the errand. Just so it wouldn't be my fault anymore. Whatever happened to Vellichor, whatever happened to you, I wanted to hand the whole thing back to the world and say *not mine, find another courier*." He looked at her. "And then I died, which is the coward's way of doing exactly that, and here we are."

"That's a terrible comfort, Corvin."

"It's not meant to comfort you. It's meant to make you angry." He tilted his head. "Is it working?"

Emrys looked down at the lopsided bird in her palm. One wing longer than the other. It would never fly anywhere, and he'd made it anyway, and given it to her, and said *it has to be mine*, and she had understood him perfectly and said nothing back, because saying something back would have made it real, and real things could be taken, and look — look what had happened.

"I left him," she said. Her voice came out smaller than she intended. "He gave himself up so we could run, and I ran. I've spent my whole life running, Corvin, I'm *good* at it, it's the one thing I've never failed at, and I hate that I'm good at it."

"You know what he'd say to that?"

"He'd say I did the right thing, because he's infuriating and reasonable and he'd be right, and I don't want him to be right, I want him *here* being wrong about something, anything—"

"He'd say," Corvin continued, implacable, "that you went back for him once already. Into the sanctum. Through the front door, wearing the voices of dead Choir members like a coat, which frankly terrified me and I'm dead. You got him out. And then he was taken again, doing the same thing you did — turning around for someone he loved." The ghost's voice gentled, which was somehow worse. "You're not a woman who runs, Emrys Thackeray. You're a woman who keeps getting handed bills for other people's courage. It's not the same thing."

Emrys pressed the heel of her hand against her eyes. "It feels the same."

"Yes. Well. Grief is a liar with excellent penmanship."

Despite herself — despite everything — a sound escaped her that was nearly a laugh. "Did you make that up just now?"

"I've had time. There's very little to do over there except compose aphorisms and watch the living make mistakes." He paused. "Some of us take notes."

She sat with it for a while. The meadow did what meadows do, which is nothing, beautifully. Somewhere a lark was making an argument for morning. Behind her, in the hollow, she heard Maren turn over and mutter something in her sleep — map coordinates, probably, or her father's name. Even unconscious, the girl worked. Emrys envied her that.

"The key's heavy," Emrys said finally. "Not the weight of it. The *meaning*. Every step I take toward the gate, I feel it. And I keep thinking — what if I'm wrong? What if the Cantor's right? What if some doors are closed for a reason and I'm the fool prying at one?" She turned the bird over in her fingers. "What if I open the gate and all I get back is a country-sized grave?"

"And what if you turn around," Corvin said, "and go home, and carve charms until your hands give out, and every widow in Thornfell gets a perfect mourning piece, and every night you hear the dead of a country that doesn't exist asking you why you stopped walking?"

"That's not fair."

"No," he agreed. "Grief isn't. Neither are the dead. We're famously unreasonable. Ask anyone who's haunted anybody." He rose from his invisible fence and drifted closer, and up close she could see that his new-whole coat was stitched through with faint light, like veins of ore in grey stone. "I carried that map for eleven years, Emrys. Do you know why?"

"Because you were paid to."

"Because I was paid to," he conceded, "and because I asked the man who hired me — an old archivist, mad as a March hare, last of the Vellish record-keepers — *why does it matter? It's one country. The world is full of countries.* And he looked at me the way you look at a bone before you cut it, like he could see the shape inside, and he said: *Because they sang, courier. Of all the nations I have catalogued, that one sang. Everything else can be rebuilt. A song, once forgotten, is gone in a way stone is not.*" Corvin spread his steady hands. "I didn't understand him. I delivered the map anyway. Then I died on the floor of a bone-carver's shop in a town I couldn't find on any map, whispering one word, and the word turned out to be the beginning of the only errand I ever cared about."

He looked at her, and his terrible clear eyes were kind.

"You're not carrying a country, Emrys. You're carrying a song that hasn't been sung yet. Those are different weights entirely. One crushes. The other *lifts*, if you let it."

Emrys looked down the road that wasn't a road. Overgrown track, going nowhere. Except that now that she really looked — now that she let her gift open the way she opened it over bones — she could see the faintest shimmer along the old ruts, the way heat shimmers above summer stone. Memory, laid over ground. The road remembered being a road.

Everything here remembered being something else.

"When did you get wise?" she asked Corvin. "You were a smuggler's courier. Your wisdom used to consist of knowing which innkeepers watered their beer."

"Dying concentrates the mind wonderfully. Also I've been dead for months and you're the only conversation I get." He smiled, and for a moment he looked like a living man again, tired and fond. "Talk to the girl, when she wakes. She's carrying her father the way you're carrying your mother's face — paid out at a tollhouse, gone, and she smiles anyway, which takes more spine than either of us has. You three are a strange little choir yourselves, you know. All of you missing pieces. All of you still walking."

"One of us stopped walking."

"For an hour. In a nice meadow." He shrugged. "The dead recommend rest. We're experts on the subject."

* * *

Maren woke an hour past dawn, found Emrys repacking the camp with more force than the bedroll deserved, and asked no questions, which was its own kind of mercy. She ate hardtack and honey from a jar she'd traded two buttons for in the last village, and watched Emrys buckle straps, and finally said:

"You're going back for him."

Emrys's hands stilled. "No."

"You're going to the gate," Maren corrected herself, "and you're going to win, and then you're going back for him. Those are different sentences but they rhyme." She licked honey off her thumb. "My father used to say grief makes people walk in circles unless you give them a direction. So. Direction." She pointed east, where the mountains gathered themselves up against the sky like an audience waiting for the performance to begin. "The shrine's two days from here. The old one, on the pass. If the tollhouse took your mother's face, the shrine is where payments sit. My father wrote about it. He wrote about everything, the man annotated his own dreams."

Emrys stared at her. "How do you know about the tollhouse?"

"You talk in your sleep. You also apologized to a rock last Tuesday, but we don't need to discuss that."

"We absolutely do not." Emrys shouldered the pack. The key lay warm against her breastbone, and the lopsided wooden bird rode in her pocket, and neither of them weighed what they had weighed yesterday, which was not the same as weighing nothing. "The shrine first. Then the gate."

"Then him," Maren said. Not a question.

"Then him," Emrys agreed, and discovered that saying it out loud made it a plan, and having a plan made her legs remember what they were for.

They walked east. The overgrown track firmed beneath their boots, and where it passed under the old stone waymark — leaning, lichen-eater, older than any country Emrys knew — she heard, very faintly, in the register where the dead spoke, a hum. Three notes. Rising.

A fragment of a song, waiting for the rest of it.

Corvin fell into step beside her, insubstantial and pleased with himself, and said nothing at all, which from a ghost was the loudest encouragement there was.
