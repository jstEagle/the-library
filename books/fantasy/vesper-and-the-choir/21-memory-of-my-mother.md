## 21. Memory of My Mother

The shrine sat where no road went anymore, which was how Emrys knew it was old enough to matter.

Maren found it first—or rather, her father's compass found it, the needle swinging off north and pointing up the goat track instead, quivering like a dog that has caught a scent it cannot decide whether to trust. They climbed for half a day through pine and then above the pine line, through scrub and scree, until the trees gave out entirely and there it was: a low ring of standing stones on a bald shoulder of mountain, each stone carved over so completely with names that the rock beneath had almost worn away trying to hold them all.

"Well," said the courier's ghost, drifting up beside her with the particular smugness of a man who has been right about something for three days running. "I did say the tollhouse wasn't the only place that traded."

"You said," Emrys said, "that it was the *nicest* place that traded. You called it charming."

"I stand by charming. Look at it. Very charming. All those little letters."

The letters were little. Thousands of them, cut into the stones in a script older than any kingdom she could name—and she could name quite a few now, thanks to a dead man who wouldn't stop lecturing. Names, she realized, crouching to run her thumb along one groove. Not prayers. Not curses. Names, and beside each name, smaller still, a single word: *kept*, or *paid*, or—the rarest, worn smoothest, touched by generations of grieving thumbs—*returned*.

Maren had already unpacked her father's journal and was sketching the ring with the fierce, unselfconscious hunger she brought to everything. "A memory-shrine," she breathed. "I've read about these. There were supposed to be dozens once. This is the first one I've ever—" She stopped herself, visibly, the way she always did when enthusiasm threatened to outrun kindness, and looked at Emrys with an expression that was trying very hard to be casual. "You don't have to do anything here, you know. We can camp and go on."

"I know."

"It's not a debt. What you paid at the tollhouse—"

"Maren."

"—it was extortion, essentially, and shrines are different, they're consensual, and also you've barely slept and you get grim when you're tired, you get this line right here—"

"*Maren.*"

Maren closed her mouth. The wind moved through the standing stones and made a sound like a very large room full of people deciding, all at once, to be quiet.

Emrys looked at the stone with the word *returned* worn into it, and let herself want what she wanted, plainly, for the first time since the tollhouse.

Her mother's face.

She had paid it without hesitating—a handful of memories poured into the tollhouse's hungry dark, and among them, because the dead demanded weight and her mother was the heaviest thing she owned, the face itself. The brown eyes. The laugh lines. The way her hair went grey at the temples early, silvering like frost on a window while the rest stayed dark. Emrys still knew she had loved her mother. That was the cruelty of it: love remained, whole and burning, with nothing left to burn *on*. A hearth fire with the furniture gone. She would catch herself reaching for the face the way a tongue goes to a missing tooth, and find only a smooth blank where a woman should have been, and grief would hit her fresh every time, because she was mourning someone she could no longer mourn properly.

Three weeks now. It hadn't gotten easier. It had gotten more practiced, which was worse.

"All right," she said quietly, to the stones, to the wind, to whatever kept the ledger here. "I'd like to make a withdrawal."

* * *

The shrine's keeper turned out to be alive, which surprised them both.

He came out of a turf-roofed hut tucked against the largest stone—a small, round man, ancient in the way of mountains, wrapped in so many layers of wool that he seemed less dressed than geological. He looked at Emrys, then at Maren, then—this was the part that made the hairs on her arms rise—at the empty air beside her left shoulder, and bowed slightly.

"And the gentleman," he said. "Welcome all."

"He can see you," Emrys told the courier, who had gone rigid in the way ghosts did when living people acknowledged them, which was roughly the way cats reacted to cucumbers.

"Of course I see him," the keeper said mildly. "This is a house of memory. Things remembered well don't fade." He beckoned them toward a fire pit in the ring's center, already laid with wood, already lit as though he'd known they were coming—which, Emrys supposed, he might have. Keepers of places like this tended to know things. Her own gift worked that way sometimes: the dead arrived before they died.

They settled around the fire. The keeper produced tea from somewhere inside his strata of clothing—a clay pot, dented, black with decades of use—and poured four cups. He set the fourth on the ground at the courier's position without comment. The steam bent slightly, as if sipped.

"Now," he said. "Who pays, and for what?"

"A withdrawal," Emrys said. "Not a payment. I paid at the Tollhouse of Names, on the western road. Memories. Among them—" Her voice did something embarrassing. She steadied it. "My mother's face. I want it back."

The keeper looked at her for a long moment. His eyes were pale, nearly colorless, the eyes of very old mountain folk, and they held neither pity nor judgment, which she appreciated more than she could say. Pity she had gotten plenty of lately. Maren's pity came wrapped in kindness and was therefore unbearable.

"Names," the keeper said, "and faces, and voices. The heavy coin." He sipped his tea. "Tell me about the tollhouse. What did it ask?"

"What it always asks, according to—" she gestured at the air "—him. Weight for passage. It wanted grief and I gave it grief."

"And did it tell you the price was permanent?"

Emrys opened her mouth. Closed it. Went back through the memory of that cold afternoon, the ruined tollhouse, the voices layered under the floorboards like roots under a road. *Weight,* they had said. *Paid and passed.* And she had been so eager, so desperate to move, to put distance between herself and the hunters, that she had poured out her pockets without reading the ledger.

"No," she said slowly. "It didn't."

"It never does." The keeper said it without anger, the way one remarks that rivers run downhill. "Tollhouses take. Shrines keep. That is the whole difference between them, and it is the difference between theft and stewardship, and most people never learn it because most people meet the tollhouse first." He set down his cup. "What was given to a tollhouse is spent. It bought your passage, child. It is gone the way bread is gone after it's eaten. I cannot return it. No one can. Not even—" he nodded upward, at the sky, at whatever lived past it "—the ones I pray to."

The fire cracked. Somewhere below them, a bird complained about the altitude.

"But," the keeper said, and let the word sit there, fat and patient, until Emrys looked up.

"But," she repeated.

"But a shrine keeps copies." He rose, joints crackling like green wood, and shuffled to the great stone—the largest one, the one his hut leaned against—and pressed both palms flat against its surface. "Every memory given in grief passes through many hands before it is spent. Toll-takers. Ledger-keepers. And shrines. We are the ones who witness the paying, and what a shrine witnesses, a shrine may echo." He tapped the stone. "An echo is not the voice. Understand me. It will not be perfect. It will be *like*. A face seen in water rather than in a mirror. But water holds light too."

Emrys's heart was doing something undignified. "What does it cost?"

"That," said the keeper, "is the question of someone who has learned." He smiled, and it transformed his whole wrinkled face into something impish. "Nothing. Withdrawals from grief are free. That is the covenant. Grief paid enough already." He paused. "There is a price, but it isn't paid in memory. It's paid in *truth*. Sit. Listen. And when I ask you a question at the end, answer honestly, or the echo won't take. Falsehood curdles it."

Maren had stopped sketching. The courier's ghost had drifted closer, and for once he wasn't lecturing.

"Do it," the ghost said quietly. "Whatever the truth costs, it's cheaper than what you're carrying."

* * *

The ritual was simpler than she expected, which somehow made it harder.

The keeper had her kneel at the great stone and lay both hands where his had been. The rock was warm—not sun-warm, blood-warm—and under her palms she felt it breathing, slow, vast, the way the dead said mountains breathed if you asked them politely and waited a hundred years for the answer.

"Say her name," the keeper said.

"Brigid," Emrys whispered. "Brigid Thackeray."

The stone drank the name. Then it began, gently, to give things back.

Not the face. Not yet. First it gave her the edges of her mother—the smell of tallow and bone dust, because her mother had swept this workshop before Emrys ever owned it, had swept her own mother's workshop before that. The specific creak of the third stair. A hand on her hair, heavy and unhurried, the hand of a woman who believed children should be touched often and praised rarely and loved constantly without either of the first two needing announcement.

Emrys knelt in the snow-dusted grass and wept without sound, because her mother had taught her that too—not by instruction, but by example, crying silently at her own mother's funeral with her spine straight as a chisel.

Then the face came.

It came like light through water, exactly as promised: wavering, imperfect, *like*. Brown eyes—but softer around than she remembered, or perhaps she had never truly remembered, perhaps she had only ever known the idea of them. Laugh lines. The grey at the temples, silvering like frost. And the mouth—that was the part the echo got perfectly, the part that broke her—the mouth that did a small, private, sideways thing when it was trying not to smile and failing, which it usually was, because her mother had been a woman constitutionally incapable of sustained solemnity, a fact that had driven young Emrys mad and that adult Emrys would have given anything—

Had given anything. Had given exactly this.

"There she is," the keeper said softly.

"She's laughing at me," Emrys managed. "She's—she always laughed at me. At my rules. My *never get involved*, my careful little invisible life. She'd have hated all of it. She'd have marched me onto that road herself."

"Yes," said the keeper. "Now the question. Answer true, or she fades again and no shrine will hold her twice."

The wind went still. Even the fire leaned in.

"When you look at her now," the keeper said, "what do you feel? Not what you felt. What you feel. Here. Now. With the echo in your eyes and the loss still in your chest."

Emrys looked at her mother's face hovering in the stone's warm glow, imperfect, rippling, borrowed light.

And the honest answer rose in her like water finding its level.

"Relief," she said, and her voice cracked on it. "That's the awful truth. Relief. Because I thought I'd lost her twice—once to dying and once to the tollhouse—and having even this much back, even this wavering, secondhand, water-light version of her—it doesn't hurt. It *helps*. I thought getting her back would gut me and instead I feel—" she laughed, wet and startled "—I feel like someone opened a window in a shut house. I'm becoming someone who loses things and keeps going anyway, and I hate it, and it's *working*, and that's the truest thing I know right now."

The stone glowed. Held. Did not curdle.

The keeper exhaled, satisfied. "Then she stays. As an echo. As long as you come back to visit, and as long as you keep telling the truth somewhere at least once a year—it's good practice, most people die of never doing it." He patted the stone fondly. "Your mother will ripple a bit when you're lying to yourself. Consider it maternal supervision."

Above the ring of stones, the clouds had torn open, and evening light came down the mountain in a long gold sheet, and Emrys Thackeray knelt in it with her mother's almost-face shining in the rock before her, and felt something in her chest settle into a new shape. Not healed. Reshaped. A bone that had broken and knit itself crooked but strong, strong along the fracture line, the way bones did.

She was not the woman who had left Thornfell. That woman had been all edges and rules, a knife wrapped in cloth. This one had paid tolls and answered riddles of truth and learned that loss could be load-bearing. She was being built out of what she'd given away, and for the first time, kneeling in the gold light, she thought: *maybe that's not damage. Maybe that's just what a person looks like when they're finished.*

"Thank you," she told the keeper.

"Don't thank me. Thank her." He nodded at the stone. "Mothers keep better ledgers than we do."

* * *

They camped that night inside the ring, because the keeper refused to let them descend in the dark ("The mountain eats show-offs") and because none of them, though nobody said it aloud, wanted to leave yet.

Maren fell asleep mid-sentence over her journal, pencil still moving, producing a long confident line across two pages of notes. The keeper retired to his hut. The courier's ghost sat across the fire from Emrys, and for a while neither of them spoke, which was unusual enough that she noticed it the way you notice a clock stopping.

"You saw her," he said at last. "In the stone."

"I saw her."

"My mother's face is the first thing I forgot." He said it simply, staring into the flames he couldn't feel. "Choir took it when I was six. Along with everything else. Sometimes I try to rebuild it out of other mothers' faces and it never—I just want you to know I'm glad. That yours held."

Emrys reached across the fire. Her hand passed through his, as it always did, and as it always did, he cupped the warmth of the flame where her fingers had been, pretending that counted.

"Sable remembers you," she said quietly. "Out of everyone in the world, he chose to remember you. Whatever else the Choir took from him, he's spending what's left on us. On *you*." She fed a stick to the fire. "So we're going to get him back, and then we're going to that gate, and then—" she glanced at the great stone, where, if she squinted, her mother's echo seemed to shimmer approvingly "—then we'll see what's worth keeping."

It was deep in the night, the fire burned to embers, when the sound woke her.

Singing. Far off, down the mountain, down through the passes—thin and sweet and wrong, a melody with too many voices in it, braided into one. It slid through her dreams like a blade through water, and she sat up with her heart hammering, and beside the dead fire Maren was sitting up too, wide-eyed, her hand pressed to her mouth.

"Emrys," she whispered. "The song. I can't—what was I dreaming about? I was dreaming about *someone*, and the song came, and now there's just—there's a hole where the dream was."

Down in the valleys, in the dark, the Choir was singing.

And Emrys, listening, felt the melody reach up the mountain like a searching hand—and stop, just short of the shrine stones, unable to cross the ring of kept names—as somewhere far away, in a cell or a sanctum, a man who had once been hers began, very softly, to sing back.
