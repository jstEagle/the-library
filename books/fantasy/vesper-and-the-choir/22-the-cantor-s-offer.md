## 22. The Cantor's Offer

They took his boots first.

Sable had expected chains, a cell, perhaps some inventive punishment involving the Cantor's famous patience. Instead two grey-robed brothers had knelt at his feet in the sanctum's antechamber and removed his boots with the tenderness of men dressing a corpse, and then they had walked him—barefoot, unbound, which was somehow worse—down seven corridors and through three doors that were not doors, into a room that smelled of candle smoke and old paper and, beneath it all, faintly of snow.

The room was round. The room was always round; the Choir did not trust corners.

"You've been eating," said the Grand Cantor, without looking up from the desk where he wrote. His pen moved in long, patient strokes, like a man rowing a very slow boat. "You've been sleeping. You've been walking in daylight." He set down the pen. "You look *terrible*, Sable."

"Thank you, Eminence."

"Don't thank me. It wasn't a compliment. It was an observation of theological significance." The Cantor rose. He was not a tall man, but rooms tended to rearrange themselves around him anyway, the way water rearranges itself around a dropped stone. Up close, his face was smooth in a way faces should not be—not young, exactly, but *unworn*, as if the years had been carefully lifted off him and folded away somewhere. "You have been out in the world for four months. You have breathed its air. And I can see it sitting on you like soot."

Sable said nothing. He had learned, in the Choir, that silence was a kind of armor, and also a kind of surrender, depending entirely on who broke it first.

The Cantor circled him slowly. "Do you know what I see when I look at you? I see a boy standing in an orchard."

Sable's heart did something complicated and painful. He kept his face still. This, too, was Choir training: the face is a door, and we are the ones who decide what walks through it.

"I don't remember any orchard," he said.

"No," the Cantor agreed softly. "I do. That's rather the point."

* * *

They fed him first. This was the part Sable found most unsettling—the courtesy of it. A table was brought, and bread still warm enough to steam, and a soup with leeks in it, and a small dish of honey, and the Cantor sat across from him and ate nothing at all while Sable ate everything, because starving yourself out of spite was a fool's strategy and Sable had never been a fool, only occasionally a coward, and lately—lately something else.

"The bone-carver," the Cantor said, over the honey. "Tell me about her."

"There's nothing to tell."

"Mm." The Cantor turned his cup—a cup he had poured full of nothing—and watched the nothing settle. "When you reported from Thornfell, you told us she'd gone north through the Shepherd's Cut. She went west through the Hollow Road. When you reported from the valley villages, you placed her two days behind you. She was two days *ahead*. When the hunters found her trail in the whiteout pass, your report had already steered them a full day wrong." He looked up. His eyes were grey and gentle and absolutely bottomless. "You have been lying to this order since the day you met her. Do you know what interests me? Not the lies. Anyone can lie. What interests me is that you're *bad* at it. You were the best of us, Sable. You lied beautifully when it mattered. So either your skill has rotted out of you—" he smiled, "—or some part of you wanted to be caught."

Sable put down his spoon. It made a small, definite sound against the bowl, and in the round room the sound went on much longer than it should have.

"If I wanted to be caught," he said, "I wouldn't have killed Brother Aldous."

"No," the Cantor said. "You'd have let him kill her. Which brings us, neatly, to business." He folded his hands. "I'm going to offer you a gift, and you're going to want to spit at me, and I'd ask you to hear all of it before you spit. Spitting is more satisfying with a full mouth anyway."

* * *

What the Cantor offered, he offered the way other men offer wine—lightly, generously, with the certainty that refusal would look like bad manners.

"We keep everything," he said. "You know this. Every memory the Choir has ever taken, back to the founding—we don't destroy them. Destruction would be a kind of mercy, and mercy is not our function. We *keep*. In the deep vaults there is a shelf with your name on it. Not the name you use now. Your first one. The one your mother used, in the orchard, when you'd climbed too high and couldn't get down." He leaned forward. "It's all there, Sable. The orchard. The climbing. Her voice. Your whole childhood—not fragments, not echoes, the whole of it, every ordinary golden hour. And more than that. Everything the order took from you at initiation. Everything you gave up to become what you are. Restored. Whole. You could walk out of this room knowing why you flinch at the smell of apples. Knowing what you were before you were ours."

"And the price?" Sable asked, because there was always a price, and because asking showed he understood the game, and understanding the game was the only dignity left in it.

"The bone-carver." The Cantor said it simply, the way one says *the weather*. "She carries a map to the Vesper Gate. She means to open it. If she opens it, everything ends—not just Vellichor's silence, but the peace of every soul we have sung to rest these forty years. The dead will pour back into the world like water through a broken dam. Villages will drown in their own grandparents. You've seen what the dead are like, Sable. You of all people know they are not gentle."

"They aren't," Sable said quietly. "But they're honest."

"Honesty is not peace." The Cantor rose again and went to the window—or what served as a window, a tall slit of grey light. "Bring her to me. Bring me the map. Don't harm her—I give you my word on that, and my word, whatever else I am, holds. She'll be treated gently. There may even be a place for her here; a gift like hers belongs in the Choir, honestly, it always has. And you—" he turned, and his unworn face did something almost tender, "—you walk out of here *full*. You walk out of here a man with a mother and a childhood and a name, instead of a hollow instrument that happens to walk and lie badly."

He let the silence sit. He was good at silence; he'd had centuries of practice, though only he knew how many.

"Take tonight," he said. "Think of apples."

* * *

The cell they gave him had a bed, a candle, and no corners. They had taken his coat but left him his shirt, and in the shirt's inner seam, stitched flat, was a sliver of bone Emrys had carved for him in the cave below the whiteout pass—a little thing, half a finger long, a wren with its wings folded. She'd said it was nothing, just practice, just something to keep her hands busy while he shivered. He knew better now. Everything she carved listened. And everything she carved, he suspected, remembered her—even when the world was being taught to forget.

He held it under the candle and whispered to it, the way you'd whisper to a bird you hoped was sleeping and not dead.

"She's offering me my life back," he told the wren. "All of it. The parts I cried over losing before I even understood I'd lost them." His thumb moved over the tiny folded wings. "And all I have to do is hand over the only person who ever looked at the hollow where I keep my self and didn't flinch."

The wren said nothing, being bone. But it was warm in his palm, warmer than bone should be, and he chose to believe that was her, somewhere far off in the dark, holding the other half of the conversation.

Here was the thing the Cantor did not understand—could not understand, having spent four hundred years curating memory like a miser curating coins: Sable had already had his childhood back. He'd had it for eleven minutes, fleeing through the sanctum's lower halls with Emrys's hand in his, when the half-broken ritual had cracked open the vaults in his head. Eleven minutes of orchard light. Eleven minutes of a woman's voice calling a name he hadn't heard in twenty years. He had spent those eleven minutes learning that the past was real, and sweet, and *gone*, and that he could survive its absence—because he had already survived the worst absence, the one the Cantor couldn't restore at any price.

He knew what he was before the Choir took him.

He was a boy in an orchard. And the boy in the orchard would have spat in the Cantor's face.

But the man he'd become—the man the Choir had built out of the boy's leftovers—that man knew better than to spit. That man knew you didn't beat the Cantor by refusing the offer. You beat him by taking it.

* * *

In the morning, they came for his answer, and Sable had spent the night arranging his face the way a carpenter arranges joints: carefully, so that it would bear weight.

"Yes," he said.

The Cantor studied him for a long moment. Around the round room, the grey-robed brothers stood like furniture, and Sable felt their attention on him the way you feel weather change.

"You'll forgive me for asking twice," the Cantor said. "Loyalty that returns overnight is like bread that rises overnight. Sometimes it's genuine. Sometimes it's just air."

"Ask me anything."

"When you find her—and you will find her, because she'll let you, because she loves you, which makes her the easiest quarry in the world—what will you do?"

"I'll bring her to you," Sable said. "Unharmed. As promised."

"And if she runs?"

"I'm faster."

"And if she fights?"

"She carves bones, Eminence. She fights with ghosts. I was raised on ghosts." He held the Cantor's gaze and let one true thing show through, because the best lies are load-bearing walls of truth. "I want my life back. I want the orchard. I want to stop waking up every morning reaching for a name that isn't there. You're the only person alive who can give me that. Of course I said yes. I'd have said yes last night, but I needed to be sure the yes was mine and not just fear talking." He paused. "Fear says yes fast. Want takes longer."

The Cantor smiled, and it was the smile of a man watching a lock turn. "There he is," he murmured. "There's the best of us." He gestured, and a brother stepped forward bearing a small iron casket, banded shut, humming faintly against the brother's hands like a hive of very quiet bees. "Your memories. All of them. They'll be returned to you when she is delivered to this room—alive, willing or not, but whole. Until then, you carry the casket. Consider it collateral. Consider it a promise. Consider it, if you like, an apple you haven't eaten yet."

Sable took the casket. It was heavier than iron had any right to be, and warm, and inside it—if the Cantor told the truth—was a mother's voice saying a name.

"Where is she?" he asked. "Where do I start?"

The Cantor turned back to his desk and took up his pen, already rowing away across his slow paper sea.

"East," he said. "Toward the gate. She's ahead of you, and she's grieving, and grief makes people fast and stupid in equal measure. You have six days before the counter-song finishes, and after that none of this will matter, because the world will simply... decline to remember her." He glanced up once, mild as milk. "Catch her before the song does, Sable. It would be a shame to restore a man's whole life only to have him unable to recall why he wanted it."

* * *

They gave him back his boots—which he noted, with a cold flicker of satisfaction, meant they had never doubted this outcome, which meant they had never truly known him, which was the single greatest tactical advantage he had ever been handed—and a horse, and a grey cloak, and directions to a road that did not exist on any map Maren's father had ever drawn.

At the sanctum's outer gate, the brother who walked him out paused and looked at the iron casket strapped to Sable's saddle.

"Heavy?" the brother asked. It might have been small talk. With the Choir, it was never small talk.

"A little," Sable said, swinging up into the saddle. "Most precious things are."

He rode east, into the thin gold morning, and the casket hummed against his knee like a captured choir, and he let himself think about apples the whole way—not because he missed them, but because thinking about them kept the true thing buried deep enough that even he couldn't quite see it:

That he had already opened the casket's twin, once, in the dark of the vault corridor, with a hairpin stolen from a sleeping brother and eleven minutes of nerve.

That he knew exactly what was inside.

And that what was inside was not his childhood at all—but a song. A counter-song, half-finished, in the Cantor's own handwriting, with the final verse left blank.

Sable smiled at the road ahead, barefoot-hearted in his new boots, and began composing the ending in his head as he rode—six days to the gate, six days to finish a theft greater than any the Choir had ever committed.

The Cantor had given him the weapon.

All Sable had to do was carry it home to Emrys.
