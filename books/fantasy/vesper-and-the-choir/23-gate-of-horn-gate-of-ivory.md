## 23. Gate of Horn, Gate of Ivory

The mountain stopped pretending to be a mountain on the third morning.

Emrys had felt it coming for hours before she saw it—a wrongness in the stone under her boots, the way the path no longer wound but *insisted*, climbing in long straight sweeps as though the rock itself remembered being stairs. Maren walked ahead with her father's compass held flat on her palm, and the needle had given up spinning entirely. It pointed only forward now, trembling like a small animal that could smell something it couldn't name.

"It's doing that again," Maren said, without turning around. She'd been saying versions of that sentence for two days. "Doing the thing."

"Compasses do things," Emrys said. "That's their whole profession."

"Not this. This is—" Maren stopped, and Emrys nearly walked into her back. "Emrys. Look at the snow."

The snow was falling upward.

Not all of it. Just a thin seam of flakes, maybe ten feet wide, drifting gently from the ground into the grey sky, as if the mountain were exhaling through a crack in itself. Beyond the seam, the air looked different—thicker, older, the way air looks inside a house that has been shut up for a very long time.

Emrys had spent her whole life listening to the dead, and she knew what haunted places sounded like: a hush with teeth in it. This was not that. This was a hush like a held breath. Like a room where someone has just heard footsteps in the hall and is waiting to see if they'll knock.

"The dead are quiet here," she said, and was surprised by how much it frightened her to say it. For forty years of walking, for every mile of the vanished road, the dead had murmured around her like wind through wheat—the courier ghost, whose name she'd finally learned was Aldous, chattering at her elbow about the state of his boots; the tollhouse dead; the angry chorus she'd unleashed in the pass, who still sometimes sang fragments of themselves to her at night, apologetic now, like dogs that had bitten someone. But here, nothing. The silence wasn't empty. It was *attentive*.

"They're not quiet," Maren said softly. "They're listening. There's a difference." She tucked the compass away and pulled her coat tighter. "My father wrote about this. The last entry in his journal—he said the gate doesn't keep people out. It keeps them *waiting*. You don't arrive at the Vesper Gate. You're received."

"Wonderful," Emrys said. "I haven't been received by anything since I was nine years old, and I'd hoped to keep the streak."

But her hands, she noticed, were shaking, and not from the cold. She pressed them into her armpits and kept walking.

* * *

The gate did not look like a gate.

That was the first thing Emrys understood, standing before it in the last hour of daylight, her breath steaming in air that tasted faintly of candle smoke and cut apples. She had imagined doors—great bronze leaves, or the bone-white arches of temple architecture, something you could point at and say *there, that is the end of the road*. Instead there was a hollow in the mountainside, a cavern mouth perhaps forty feet across, and within it, impossibly, a room.

Not a ruin. A room. Intact, dry, lit by a sourceless warm glow that came from everywhere and nowhere, the light of a place that had been taken out of time and folded carefully, like linen put away for winter. Flagstones swept clean. Benches along the walls. And set into the far wall, side by side, close enough that they might have been built by brothers—

Two doors.

The one on the left was made of horn. Not carved horn, not ornamented—made *of* it, great translucent plates of it fitted together like the panes of a lantern, amber-dark at the edges and pale gold at their centers, so that when Emrys moved, she saw her own reflection swim through them, layered and ghostly, a dozen Emryses deep. It looked warm. It looked like the inside of a living thing.

The one on the right was ivory. White as a winter field, white as the first page of an unwritten book, its surface smooth and seamless except for a single line of script running down its length in characters Emrys did not know—and yet, horribly, almost knew, the way you almost remember a dream upon waking. The letters seemed to sit slightly above the surface of the door, the way words float when you read them in a fever.

Between the two doors, carved into the wall itself, was a third inscription. Longer. Older. The stone around it had been worn smooth by weathering that should have been impossible indoors.

Maren made a sound Emrys had never heard from her—not grief exactly, not joy, but the noise a person makes when two feelings arrive at once and collide. She crossed the flagstones slowly, reverently, and knelt before the inscription with her journal already out.

"This is it," she whispered. "This is actually—it's real. Forty years everyone said my father was mad, and it's real, it's *here*—" Her voice cracked. She pressed her mittened hand against the wall below the writing, as if steadying herself against a ship's rail. "I'm sorry. Give me a moment. I've been walking toward this since I was six years old."

Emrys stood in the doorway of the cavern and let her.

She listened instead. That was her craft, her curse, her whole self: she listened. And what she heard, in the attentive silence of the gate-room, was not nothing after all. It was breath. Hundreds of breaths, slow and patient, drawn in unison and released in unison, like a congregation waiting for a hymn to begin. The dead of Vellichor, she realized. Or some of them. The ones who had come home to wait.

*Aldous*, she thought, and felt his presence flicker at her shoulder—thinner here, stretched, like a voice heard through a wall. He'd grown quieter the closer they'd come. She'd assumed he was fading. Now she wondered if he was simply getting ready to go inside.

"You could wait outside," she said aloud, to him, to Maren, to no one. "If this goes badly."

"If this goes badly," Maren said, still kneeling, "then it goes badly for everyone, and I'd rather be in the room." She rose, wiped her eyes briskly with the heel of her hand, and pointed at the inscription between the doors. "Can you read it?"

"No."

"I can't either. But—" Maren hesitated, then reached into her pack and drew out a bundle wrapped in oilcloth, unwrapping it with the care of a surgeon. Inside lay a book, small and swollen with damp, its cover cracked leather gone soft as cloth. Her father's journal. She'd shown Emrys pieces of it on the road—sketches of passes that no longer existed, lists of 'wrong' memories collected from villagers over thirty years. "He copied out fragments. Fragments of the dead tongue. He got them from—" she laughed, a wet, disbelieving sound, "—from a dream, he claimed. From the same dream, every year, on the same night. He thought the country was talking to him. Everyone else thought he was drunk."

"And was he?"

"He was both," Maren said fondly. "It's part of why I loved him." She turned pages with numb fingers until she found what she wanted: a column of angular characters, each one annotated in her father's cramped hand with guesses, corrections, corrections of the corrections. She held the journal up beside the wall inscription and compared them, letter by letter, her lips moving.

The glow in the room seemed to brighten very slightly. Or perhaps Emrys only wanted it to.

"It's a riddle," Maren said at last. "Or—an address. A question. My father translated a piece of this once. He thought it said: *What would you keep?*" She looked up at Emrys, and in the sourceless light her face was pale and fierce and young. "What would you keep. That's the whole gate, Emrys. Two doors, one question."

"What are the doors?" Emrys asked, though she suspected she already knew, the way you know the shape of a thing from its shadow.

"Horn and ivory," Maren said. "Old story. Older than Vellichor, probably. Dreams that come through the gate of horn are true. Dreams through the gate of ivory are—" she swallowed, "—beautiful, and false. One of these opens onto Vellichor. The other opens onto whatever wants to eat you while you believe you're home."

* * *

They made camp in the gate-room, because neither of them could bear to leave it, and because the storm outside had arrived with the dark, howling past the cavern mouth like a mob denied entry. The sourceless warmth held. Emrys unpacked the tea, and they drank it scalding, sitting on a bench that had waited forty years for someone to sit on it.

Up close, the doors were worse. Better. Worse.

The horn door hummed. Very quietly, below hearing, below feeling—a vibration Emrys sensed in her back teeth, in the bones of her inner ear, in the small bones of her hands that had spent thirty years listening to the dead. It hummed the way a tuning fork hums when its twin has just been struck somewhere out of sight. And beneath the hum, when she laid her palm flat against the amber-dark surface, she heard—

Voices. Not the dead. Something else. Something that sounded like a country: bells, and market-day crowds, and a river going over stones, and someone singing three rooms away, and rain on a roof she had never stood under, and under all of it, faint as a heartbeat, a word repeated over and over in the dead tongue, patient as water wearing down stone.

Her own name? No. She didn't think so. Something older than names.

She snatched her hand back. The humming continued, unoffended.

"Don't touch it," Maren said sharply, from across the room.

"Bit late."

"Emrys." Maren crossed to her, took her by the wrists—her grip strong, calloused from years of copying maps—and turned Emrys's hands over as if inspecting them for damage. "My father's journal. The last page. I never showed you." She opened the swollen book to the final leaf, where the handwriting deteriorated from cramped confidence to a scrawl that slid off the ruled lines. "He came here. Near the end. He never told anyone—he wrote it the night before he died. He stood in this room. He touched the doors. And then he walked away, and he never said why, and it killed him by inches for twenty years."

Emrys read the scrawl. Most of it had blurred beyond recovery, but three lines remained legible, written large, pressed hard enough into the paper to tear it:

*The ivory door shows you everything you lost. The horn door asks what you will lose. I was not brave enough for the question.*

Below that, one more line, smaller, almost illegible:

*Tell Maren I'm sorry. Tell her the compass was always pointing at her.*

Maren closed the journal with steady hands, which was how Emrys knew it had cost her everything she had.

"Well," Emrys said, because someone had to say something, and gentle humor was the only tool she'd ever trusted in a room this heavy. "Your father sounds like he'd have gotten on well with the dead I know. Terrible timing, excellent last words."

A wet laugh escaped Maren despite herself. "He'd have liked you. He liked stubborn women and bad tea."

"I'm flattered on both counts."

Then Maren's face changed, and Emrys followed her gaze to the ivory door, and stopped making jokes.

The script on the ivory door was moving.

Slowly—so slowly she might have convinced herself she'd imagined it—the characters were rearranging themselves, flowing like iron filings toward a magnet, gathering and regathering into new sentences. And as they moved, Emrys began, terribly, to understand them. Not with her eyes. With the listening place inside her, the place where the dead spoke. The ivory door was reading *her*. Sifting through her like a hand through grain, finding the shapes of what she'd lost—her mother's face, paid away at the tollhouse; her workshop; her name, half-eaten by the Cantor's counter-song; Thornfell; the widow who'd bought her charms; every small safe thing she'd ever built to hide behind—

And offering them back.

*Come,* said the ivory door, in a voice like her mother's almost-remembered laugh. *All of it returns. Nothing is truly lost. Only open.*

"Emrys." Maren's voice, very far away. "Emrys, your eyes have gone strange. Step back from the door."

She stepped back. The voice receded like a tide, grudging, promising to return.

Outside, the storm screamed. Inside, the horn door hummed its low patient note, and somewhere beyond it, Emrys could feel them waiting—the hundreds of slow, unified breaths, the congregation of the dead, all of Vellichor holding its forty-year inhale.

And beneath her ribs, another feeling entirely, one she hadn't let herself examine since the empty road: Sable. Somewhere behind them in the mountains, in the Choir's hands or escaping them, alive or not alive, moving toward this room or being dragged away from it. She had turned back once already to pull him out of the sanctum. She would turn back a thousand times. That was the problem. That was the whole riddle of her, and she suspected the gate knew it.

"What would you keep," she said quietly, testing the words. They sat strangely in her mouth, in the dead tongue's shadow. "That's the question? Just that?"

"That's what my father wrote." Maren repacked the journal with exaggerated care, not looking at her. "Though knowing him, he mistranslated something vital. He once rendered *pass the butter* as *surrender the golden sacrifice*."

Despite everything, Emrys laughed—a real laugh, startled out of her, echoing off the flagstones. The congregation of breaths seemed to shift at the sound, rustling, pleased. As if laughter were rare currency here. As if it had been forty years since anything in this room had found anything funny.

The echo faded. The storm went on. And in the silence after it, clear as a bell struck in an empty church, a new sound rose from behind the horn door:

Footsteps.

One pair. Slow. Unhurried. Crossing a floor that should not exist on the other side of a door that had not opened, growing louder, until they stopped directly opposite where Emrys stood.

Then a voice—muffled by horn and forty years and death, but unmistakable, achingly unmistakable, a voice she had last heard whispering a single dying word against her workshop door in a storm:

"Bone-carver," said the ghost of Aldous the courier, from the wrong side of the gate. "You brought it. After all this time—you actually brought it. Open the horn door, Emrys Thackeray. And whatever you do—"

The voice paused, and Emrys heard, beneath it, the whole hidden congregation lean in to listen.

"—don't touch the ivory."
