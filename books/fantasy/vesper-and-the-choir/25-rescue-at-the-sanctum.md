## 25. Rescue at the Sanctum

Maren said his name once more before they parted, as if saying it could keep him safe. "Sable. Say it back to me."

"Sable," Emrys said.

"Good." Maren's jaw was set in that way she had when she was refusing to cry about something she'd decided not to cry about yet. "Because if you're going to walk into the one place in the world built to make you forget things, I want you carrying as many names as you can hold."

They stood at the treeline below the Choir's sanctum, a grey shape folded into the mountain's shoulder like a hand closed around something it didn't want to drop. From this distance it looked almost gentle — slate roofs, narrow windows, smoke rising thin and straight from a chimney. A place where people lived. That was the horror of it, Emrys thought. The Choir wasn't a fortress. It was a choir loft. It was full of people who sang.

"You don't have to do this," Maren said. "We have the key. We could be at the gate in four days."

"We'd be at the gate in four days knowing he was singing their song for us instead of against them." Emrys checked the straps of her pack — her tools, her bones, the small carved femur that still held the dead courier's map, though the map itself had long since burned itself into her memory. "Besides. He turned back for me first. It seems to be how we do things."

"It's a terrible way to do things."

"Yes," Emrys agreed. "It's worked so far."

She left Maren with the compass and the archive key and most of the food, which felt like leaving someone with all the reasons to live and none of the person worth living for, but Maren had made her promise — *if you're caught, you run, you don't bargain, you run* — and promises were easier to make when you distributed the weight of them.

The sanctum had no gate. That was its first trick. There was simply a road that wandered up toward the building and then, somewhere along its length, stopped being a road anyone would admit to walking. Emrys knew because the dead told her. They always told her. The mountainside around the sanctum was crowded with them — thinner here than in Vellichor's ruins, but present, hovering at the edge of her hearing like a conversation through a wall.

*They took my voice,* one of them kept saying, over and over, patient as water dripping. *They took my voice and gave me a song.*

"I know," Emrys murmured. "I'm sorry. I'm going to borrow it, if you'll let me."

The ghost went quiet. Then, tentatively, hopefully: *Will you give it back?*

"If I can."

That was the plan, such as it was. The Choir's members were bound by songs — Sable had told her that much, in the cave, in the whiteout pass, in all the small hours when trust was still being built plank by plank out of shared cold. Their binding was sung into them, renewed in ritual, and every singer carried the voices of the dead Choir members who had come before, layered into their own like sediment. Dead singers. Dead voices. And Emrys Thackeray had spent forty years learning to wear the voices of the dead like a coat.

If she could not walk past the Choir's ears as a living woman, she would walk past them as a chorus.

* * *

The first door was guarded by no one, which frightened her more than guards would have. She stood before it in the blue hour before dawn, wearing a dead woman's voice wrapped around her thoughts like a scarf — Sister Anwen, who had died thirty years ago and whose ghost had described her own binding-song to Emrys with the meticulous resentment of someone reciting an enemy's faults. *Low G to start. Then the rising third. They think the third makes it beautiful. It makes it a leash.*

Emrys hummed it under her breath, barely a sound at all, and pushed the door.

It opened. Of course it opened. To the Choir, she was not an intruder. She was a memory coming home.

Inside, the corridors smelled of candlewax and cold stone and, underneath it, something faintly sweet that she couldn't name until she could: lilies. Funeral flowers. The whole building smelled faintly of funerals, the way a tannery smells of hides, the way her workshop used to smell of bone dust. A place shaped entirely around death forgets it smells strange.

She walked. She kept her hood up and her humming low and steady, and everywhere she passed, grey-robed figures inclined their heads to her — small courtesies between colleagues — and did not look twice. One young man stepped aside for her with a murmured "Sister," and she inclined her head back and her heart hammered so hard she was certain he must hear it, must hear the living pulse under the dead woman's song, but he only moved on, and Emrys exhaled slowly through her teeth.

*You're doing well,* said the courier's ghost, drifting at her shoulder. He had come back for this; he hadn't said why, and she hadn't asked, but she suspected it was the same reason she had turned back for Sable. Debts among the dead ran long.

*Don't talk to me right now,* she thought at him.

*You're doing well and I'm proud of you and also you're walking the wrong way. The rituals are below. Everything important is below. Institutions always bury what matters.*

She corrected course. The stairwell down was wide and worn, each step dished in the center by centuries of feet, and the deeper she went the warmer the air grew, which was wrong, which was deeply wrong, because cellars should be cold, and warmth rising from underground meant fire, or furnaces, or —

Or singing.

She heard it before she saw it: many voices, braided, low, rising and falling in patterns that made her teeth ache. Not beautiful. She had expected beauty — the Choir's songs were supposed to be seductive, that was the whole horror of them — but this was beauty the way a snare is beautiful, all tension and no release, a melody that kept approaching resolution and never arriving, forever, on purpose. Her mother had hummed like that once, near the end, when the fever took her words and left only sounds. Emrys shoved the memory away before it could cost her anything. She had already paid too much at tollhouses. She was nearly out of coin.

* * *

The ritual hall was a bowl of stone lit by a hundred candles, and the singers stood in ranks upon its slopes like grey reeds in a grey lake. At the center, on a low dais, lay a figure Emrys knew.

Her first stupid, human thought was that he looked peaceful. Her second, better thought was that he looked *held* — pinned, the way a moth is pinned, elegant and immobile and arranged for display. Grey robes draped him. His eyes were closed. Around his head, three Cantors moved in slow procession, singing into his ears, one at each side and one above, and wherever their song touched him, Emrys saw it happen: color returning. That was the only way she could describe it. Sable had always seemed to her like a man drawn in charcoal — accurate, even loving, but monochrome, shaded with everything he'd given up. Now, note by note, the drawing was being inked. His hands twitched. His lips moved around a word.

Around a name?

*No,* Emrys thought, and the thought came out of her like a blade leaving a sheath. *Not yet. You don't get to give him back while he's yours. He gets to be his when he's free.*

Getting to him was the problem. The hall's single entrance was behind her, watched by two sentries whose eyes had the flat shine of well-maintained instruments. The ranks of singers curved around the dais in unbroken arcs. And above it all, on a balcony of black stone, stood a figure in robes the grey of old ice, and Emrys felt the Grand Cantor's attention move across the room like a searchlight, slow, thorough, tasting.

She pressed herself into an alcove and did the hardest thing she had ever done, harder than burning her workshop, harder than paying the tollhouse: she went still, and she listened, and she asked.

*Anyone,* she thought, out into the hall's crowded silence. *Anyone who was ever one of them. Anyone who died in these walls. Lend me your faces.*

The dead came. They always came, when she asked honestly — that was the secret nobody understood about her gift, that it was never theft, it was hospitality. She opened herself the way she opened her workshop door in a storm, and they poured in: a sister who had died of lung-rot after forty years of singing; a brother drowned crossing the sanctum's cistern; an old cantor who had fallen asleep mid-hymn and simply not woken, whom everyone had politely pretended was resting. Three voices, three shapes, three sets of memories of these halls. She pulled them around herself like layers of clothing — Anwen's gait, the drowned man's stoop, the old cantor's wheeze — and when she stepped out of the alcove, she was nobody. She was a composite. She was a Choir member who had never existed, assembled from the parts of three who had.

She walked down into the bowl of the hall.

A Cantor glanced up as she passed the outer rank. Emrys coughed — the old cantor's wheeze, perfect, practiced in a heartbeat — and the Cantor nodded sympathetically and returned to his singing. Her heart was a drum and she let it drum. Fear was alive, and alive was what she was hiding, so she packed it down small and kept walking, rank by rank, arc by arc, closer to the dais.

Up close, Sable looked worse than held. He looked *thawing*. Sweat stood on his forehead. His fingers curled and uncurled, and beneath the grey robe his chest rose too fast, the breathing of a man running through a dream. The three Cantors sang on, serene, pouring decades into him — and Emrys realized with a lurch that they weren't just restoring his childhood. They were restoring his *training*. Every lesson, every binding, every hour of his service, laid back into him fresh, so that when he woke he would be the perfect instrument: a man who remembered loving Vellichor precisely so that he would understand what a crime it was to want it back.

They weren't giving him his past. They were sharpening him with it.

*I'm sorry,* she thought, kneeling beside the dais as if adjusting a candle, as if she belonged there, as if the dead cantor had every right. *I'm sorry, I'm sorry, this is going to feel like drowning.*

Then she put her mouth close to his ear, and instead of a song, she gave him the thing the Choir never gave anyone.

She gave him a voice that wasn't theirs.

"Sable," she whispered, in her own rough carver's rasp, the voice that had argued with dead men over funeral arrangements and laughed with a widow over the price of jet beads. "Sable. It's Emrys. You're dreaming, and I need you to wake up angry."

His fingers stopped curling.

"The binding runs on their song," she breathed, fast and low, watching the Cantors' faces for any flicker. "Their song, their rules. So we break it with something that isn't a song. Listen to me. Listen to the ugliest sound you know."

His lips moved. No sound. But his hand crept across the dais stone, blind, searching, and found her wrist, and gripped it, and his grip had iron in it, and Emrys could have wept.

"That's it," she whispered. "Hold on. Now think of — think of me trying to sing. You told me once I hummed like a cart with a bad axle. Think of that. Hold onto that."

Something happened to his face. Under all the serenity being poured into him, one corner of his mouth twitched upward — a crack in the ice — and through the crack, faint but unmistakable, Emrys heard him make a sound. A low, tuneless, deliberately awful drone. A cart with a bad axle. A noise with no third, no rising interval, no leash in it anywhere. The nearest Cantor faltered, frowning, hearing something discordant at the edge of perception and unable to locate it, because it was not a song and her trained ear had no shelf to put it on.

The discord spread. Where Sable's awful droning met the ritual hymn, the hymn *stuttered*. Notes slid. The three Cantors leaned closer, singing harder, and the harder they sang the more obviously something fought them, and the ranks of grey-robed singers began to glance at one another, and above it all the Grand Cantor's head turned on the balcony, slow as a millstone, seeking.

Emrys did the second-hardest thing she had ever done. She stopped hiding.

She stood up in the middle of the Choir's holiest room, threw back her hood, and let all three borrowed ghosts speak through her at once — Anwen and the drowned man and the sleeping cantor, three dead singers crying out in a living throat: *"We remember! We remember what you took!"*

The hall broke like a dropped plate.

Singers stumbled. Candles guttered. Somewhere a bell began ringing with no one pulling it, because the dead cantor remembered bells and wanted one, badly, now. And in the chaos — in the beautiful, blasphemous chaos — Emrys hauled Sable upright, got his arm over her shoulders, and ran.

* * *

They made the stairwell together, his weight increasingly his own, his feet remembering how to flee before his mind remembered why. Behind them the sanctum rang with alarm-bell and shouted hymn, countermeasures being sung into the very stones, doors that would forget how to open. Ahead of them, the night air waited at the top of the stairs like a held breath.

Halfway up, Sable spoke. His voice was wrecked, rusted, half his and half stranger's, and what he said was:

"The cart. With the bad axle."

"You remembered that?"

"You have no idea," he rasped, "what I remember," and then his knees buckled and she took more of his weight and they climbed.

At the top step he stopped her with a hand on the wall, gasping, and looked back down into the dark of the sanctum, and Emrys watched him take inventory of himself — the way she'd seen carvers check a blade for cracks. Whatever the Cantors had poured into him was still in there, sloshing, unfused. Childhood and training and theft, all of it loose in one skull.

"How much is you?" she asked quietly.

He considered it with terrible honesty. "Enough," he said. "Ask me again at the border." Then, because the alarms were climbing toward them, because the Grand Cantor's voice had begun somewhere below — a single clear note that made the stones themselves lean inward — he took her hand, properly, fingers laced, the first time either of them had dared, and said the other thing.

"They showed me your name. In the ritual. They were going to make me sing it."

Emrys's blood went cold. "Did you?"

"Not yet." His grip tightened. "But Emrys — I know it now. All of it. Your whole name, the one your mother gave you, the one you've never told anyone." His eyes, when they met hers, were terrified and certain at once. "And so does he. The Cantor has it too. We have maybe a day before he sings it, and when he does—"

Above them, far off across the mountains, thin as wire, a voice began to rise.
