## 26. Half a Man Remembers

The first thing Sable remembered was the smell of bread.

Not the grey ration-loaf of the Choir's refectories, dense as slate and tasting of nothing so much as obedience. This was other bread—dark-crusted, split open and steaming, smeared with honey that caught the light like amber held up to a window. He could not see the bakery. He could not see the street outside it or the woman who had handed it to him. But the smell arrived whole and entire, unbidden, in the middle of a frozen ravine three days' hard running from the Choir's sanctum, and it stopped him mid-stride as though he'd walked into a wall.

"Sable." Emrys was ahead of him, breath steaming, one hand braced against the rock face. "Keep moving. They'll have sent riders by now."

"I know," he said. "I just—" He pressed his knuckles to his sternum, where something was happening that he had no vocabulary for. The ritual they'd interrupted had been half-finished when Emrys broke the resonance bowl and dragged him out through the singing corridors of the dead. Half a restoration. The Cantor's singers had poured forty years of stolen memory back into him and then been forced to stop, and what remained inside him now was not emptiness and not wholeness but something worse: a jigsaw with most of its pieces missing, except that every few hundred paces, another piece would click into place without warning, and each one hurt more than the last.

Bread. Honey. A woman's laugh, low and warm, though her face was a smear of light where a face should be.

"Emrys," he said slowly. "I think I'm remembering things."

She came back to him. Up close, her eyes were the color of river ice over dark water, and there was flour still ground into the creases of her knuckles from some village they'd passed through two days ago, and he found himself cataloguing these details with an urgency that frightened him—the way a drowning man catalogues driftwood.

"Good things or bad things?"

"I don't know yet. That's rather the problem."

* * *

They made camp that night in the hollow beneath a leaning shelf of granite, a fire no larger than two cupped hands because smoke traveled far in cold air and the Choir traveled farther. Maren had gone on ahead with the archive key strapped against her ribs; she carried the maps rolled in oilcloth down her spine like a second skeleton, and she moved through the mountains with the certainty of someone who had spent her whole life walking toward a country that did not exist. She would wait at the gate. That was the plan. Plans, Sable had learned, were things the mountains chewed up and spat out with the paper still in their teeth.

Emrys fed the fire a twig at a time, economical as ever, and watched him across the small orange light.

"Tell me what you've got," she said.

He turned his hands over. Grey-robed hands, knife-calloused, ink-stained from years of copying the Cantor's ledgers of forgetting. "Fragments. They come sideways. A smell will do it, or a sound, or—" He gestured vaguely at the fire. "Light through a certain kind of window. And then for a moment I have something, and then it goes, and I'm left reaching for it like a man grabbing at fish."

"That's how the dead come to me sometimes," Emrys said quietly. "In pieces. It's why I carve them into bone—so the pieces hold still long enough to mean something."

"Carve me, then."

The words were out before he'd decided to say them, and he saw her go very still, the way she went still whenever anyone touched too near the truth of her gift. He almost took it back. But her chin lifted instead, that stubborn set he'd first noticed in a market square while grey robes asked questions nobody would remember being asked.

"Hold still, then," she said, and there was the ghost of a smile. "And don't fidget. Bone-carvers hate fidgeters. It's in the guild charter."

"You're not a guild."

"I'm whatever I say I am. Close your eyes."

He closed them. Her fingers found his wrist—not taking his pulse, exactly, but resting there, reading him the way she read stone before she cut it. And Sable, who had been handled by the Choir for thirty years with clinical impersonality, who had been emptied and filled and emptied again like a vessel for other people's purposes, felt something in his chest unclench that he hadn't known was clenched.

"Breathe," she murmured. "Now. What's there?"

"Bread," he said. "Honey. A woman laughing. There's a bell somewhere—I can hear a bell, a small one, the kind you'd hang on a shop door. And—" The fragment shifted, solidified, and he gasped. "Stairs. Blue stairs. Painted blue, worn white in the middle, going up past a window, and there are carvings on the banister. Little birds. Someone carved little birds into the banister and I used to count them. Eleven birds. I counted them every morning. Eleven birds and a sparrow someone added later that didn't match."

His voice cracked on the last word. He opened his eyes and found Emrys watching him with an expression he couldn't name—wonder and grief holding hands.

"Blue stairs," she repeated softly. "Sable. Those aren't Choir memories. The sanctum has no stairs. It has ramps, because the Cantor says steps interrupt the song."

"I know what the sanctum has. I've swept those ramps ten thousand times." He laughed—a strange, rusty sound, like a gate opening after a long winter. "Eleven birds and a sparrow. Emrys, I counted them when I was small enough that the banister came up to my chin."

"Where?"

And there it was: the wall. The place where the fragment should connect to everything else and simply didn't, smooth as river stone. He reached for it and felt the old familiar absence, the Choir's clean surgical nothing.

"I don't know," he said. "That's the shape of it. Every piece is missing its middle. I remember the bread but not the baker. The stairs but not the house. The bell but not the door it hung on." He looked at his own hands as if they might explain themselves. "It's like being given a book with every other page torn out."

"Then we read the pages we have," Emrys said. "One at a time. Starting tomorrow, you tell me every fragment as it comes. All of them. Even the stupid ones."

"Especially the stupid ones?"

"*Particularly* the stupid ones. The dead always lead with the stupid ones. A man haunted me for a week over a disputed fence post before he mentioned the murder."

Despite everything—the pursuit, the cold, the half-ritual still humming in his blood like a struck bell that wouldn't quite fall silent—Sable laughed.

* * *

The fragments came faster after that, as though being spoken aloud gave them permission.

On the second morning, crossing a scree field the color of old pewter, he stopped dead and said, "Kites. We flew kites off a roof. Mine was shaped like a fish. A red fish, longer than my arm, and the tail was made of ribbon scraps because ribbon was expensive and everyone saved their scraps all year for kite-tail." He frowned. "There was a festival. Everyone flew kites on the same day, all together, so the sky was full of them, and there was a prize for the highest flyer, and—" He pressed his palms to his eyes. "I can't remember if I won."

"Most people don't win," Emrys offered.

"I need to know if I won, Emrys. It matters."

"It genuinely doesn't."

"It *matters.*"

She was grinning at him, that crooked grin that transformed her whole face from weathered mountain-wary to something young, and he realized he'd been walking beside this woman for weeks and had never once seen her grin until his own dismembered childhood started falling out of him like coins from a torn pocket. There was probably a lesson in that. He resolved to keep producing fragments purely for the grin.

By noon he remembered the festival's name—or half of it. "*Vesper-something.* Vesper-something-eve. The word won't finish."

Emrys had gone quiet in the particular way she went quiet, which he was learning to read the way sailors read weather. "Vesper," she said. "The courier died saying it. The gate is called the Vesper Gate. Your festival shares its name with the door out of a country that doesn't exist."

"I noticed that too," Sable said, and did not say the rest: that the coincidence sat in him like a swallowed stone, that pieces were clicking together faster now, that he was beginning to suspect the shape of the picture even with half the pages torn away.

* * *

It came fully on the third night, in the worst possible way.

They had sheltered in a shepherd's bothy, dry-stone walled, abandoned decades ago by the look of it. Emrys slept curled against the wall with her pack for a pillow, one hand resting—as it always rested—on the carved femur at her belt, where the dead courier's ghost lived between errands. Sable took first watch because sleep had become a complicated country for him. When he closed his eyes lately, he dreamed in other people's memories, and he could no longer tell which ones were his.

The dream, when it finally took him despite the watch, was simple. A kitchen. Evening. A table scrubbed pale with use. A man's hands—big, scarred, gentle—showing small hands how to fold a piece of paper into a bird that would actually fly, not one of the sad darts children usually managed. And a voice, warm and tired and amused, saying:

*"Again. Slower this time. A thing worth making is worth making twice."*

Sable woke with tears on his face and no memory of crying, which was somehow the most humiliating part.

"What is it?" Emrys was already awake—she woke like a cat, all at once, no intermediate state. In the dark her eyes found his. "What did you see?"

"A man," Sable said. His voice came out wrecked. "Teaching me to fold paper birds. He said—" He stopped. Started again. "Emrys, I know his hands. I knew his hands the way you know your own reflection. And I know the voice. And I cannot see his face, and I cannot hear his name, and I have been sitting here trying to understand why a memory of folding paper would break me like this, and I think—" His breath shuddered. "I think he was my father. I think I had a father, with specific hands, who smelled of wood-shavings and lamp oil, and the Choir took him, and they didn't just take him. They took the fact of him. They took the category. I didn't grieve him thirty years ago because they made sure there was nothing left to grieve *with.*"

The silence in the bothy was total. Outside, wind worried at the stones the way a dog worries a bone.

"They stole it twice," Sable said, and heard his own voice go flat and cold, a register he didn't recognize in himself. "Vellichor. My home. First they erased the country—every road, every name, every kite festival and blue staircase. And then they took the orphan children the erasure produced, and they erased our grief too, because grieving children ask questions, and questions are cracks in the song." He laughed, and it was not a kind sound. "Do you understand? I am a citizen of a country that was deleted, raised by the men who deleted it, and they scraped me so clean I thanked them for it. I sang the hymn, Emrys. I stood in the choir stalls and I helped pull the memories out of farmers and innkeepers and mothers, and I never once wondered why the work never felt like anything, because they'd taken the part of me that feels things first."

Emrys crossed the bothy floor and sat down beside him. She didn't touch him—that was her way, she offered proximity like a hand of cards and let you choose—but she sat close enough that their shoulders nearly met, and after a moment he leaned, deliberately, the few inches that remained.

"The dead tell me about this all the time," she said quietly. "Not the Choir. Just—people. How the worst theft isn't the thing taken. It's the years you spent not knowing it was yours." She was quiet a moment. "My mother sold her voice to a passing charm-broker when I was six, to pay a debt. I grew up thinking she was simply a quiet woman. It wasn't until after she died that I learned she'd once sung so beautifully the valley below our house complained about the noise." Her mouth twisted. "Somebody stole her music and I mourned the wrong loss for twenty years. So yes. I understand."

"Your mother," Sable said. "At the tollhouse. You paid—"

"My mother's face. Yes." She said it steadily, but her hand had found the femur at her belt and gripped it. "I know what I paid. I know what I'm owed. And I know the difference between the two, which took me rather a long time."

They sat together in the dark, two people assembled from spare parts, and somewhere in that shared silence Sable felt the last piece click into place—not a memory this time, but a decision, arriving whole the way decisions do when they've been growing in the dark a long while.

"When we reach the gate," he said, "whatever happens—whether the country comes back or becomes a song or crumbles to dust like everything else the Cantor touches—I want my pages back. All of them. The bread and the kites and the man with the paper birds. Even if it hurts. *Especially* if it hurts." He turned to look at her, and the moonlight through the bothy's empty window laid a pale bar across her face. "And I want to walk through that country knowing whose son I was. I want to stand on a blue staircase and count eleven birds and a sparrow."

"And if the gate asks a price?"

"Then I'll pay it," he said simply. "I've already paid everything else. What's one more country?"

Emrys studied him for a long moment. Then she reached over and, with great deliberation, tucked a loose strand of hair behind her ear and said, in the tone of a woman making an official pronouncement: "For the record, you would have lost the kite competition. You strike me as a boy who built structurally ambitious kites with poor weight distribution."

"I *won,*" Sable said, with sudden fierce conviction. "I absolutely won. I can feel it. Somewhere in here is a memory of me winning a kite festival and I will have it back if I have to carve it out of the Cantor's own skull."

"There he is," Emrys said softly. "There's the Vellichor in you. Petty and stubborn and certain of victory."

"Petty and stubborn and certain of victory," he agreed, "is a perfectly respectable national character."

She smiled, and lay back down, and told him to wake her for second watch. He took the rest of the night alone at the window, watching stars wheel over the pass that led, according to the map sewn into Emrys's coat lining, toward the Vesper Gate—and he practiced, silently, saying a word he was suddenly certain he had known as a child and been forbidden to know since.

*Vellichor.*

The syllables fit his mouth like a hand fits a glove broken in long ago.

Near dawn, the femur at Emrys's belt began to hum. She woke instantly, hand closing around it, and her face in the grey pre-light went taut as wire.

"What is it?" Sable asked. "What's wrong?"

Her eyes lifted to his, and he saw fear in them—not for herself. For him.

"It's the courier," she said. "He's picked up a song on the wind, coming down out of the north. A new one. The Cantor's finished composing." She swallowed. "Sable—it's a counter-song. And it's written in *your* voice."
