## 27. The Counter-Song

The first sign was the bread.

Emrys had stopped at the baker's in Lowmark the way she had stopped there a dozen times over the years—back when Lowmark was a smuggler's rest and she was nobody worth remembering—and the baker's wife had set a loaf on the counter without being asked, dark rye with caraway, because that was what Emrys always bought.

"You'll be wanting the usual," the woman said, and then she looked up, and her eyes went through Emrys like light through a window. No snag. No recognition. She looked directly at Emrys and saw the counter behind her.

"That'll be two coppers," the woman said to the empty air, to whoever might come next.

Emrys stood very still. The loaf sat between them, warm, paid for by no one. She put three coppers on the wood anyway—more than it cost, an apology for something she hadn't done yet—and took the bread, and the woman never glanced down at the coins, which meant she would find them later and think them a kindness from a stranger, or a mistake, or nothing at all.

Outside, Sable caught her elbow. "What is it?"

"She didn't see me." Emrys heard her own voice come out flat and small. "Sable. She knows me. She's known me since I was sixteen and stole a heel of bread off her windowsill, and she made me pay for it in carved buttons, and she still has one of the buttons on her coat—"

"Slow down." He turned her gently toward the square, and Emrys understood, before he said anything, why he wanted her to look.

The mountains around Lowmark were singing.

Not with throats. With weather. The wind coming down off the Thornfell shoulders carried something underneath it, a note too low to hear properly and too persistent to ignore, the way you can feel a bell through the floor of a church. It moved through the valley in long slow waves, and where it passed, things changed. Not visibly. Worse than visibly. A dog lying in the sun lifted its head and looked at Emrys with the mild blank interest it would give any stranger, when yesterday it had trotted to her for the dried liver she kept in her left pocket out of habit. A child chasing a hoop ran straight through the space where Emrys stood, close enough to touch, and flinched—not from her, but from nothing, from some instinct that told him he'd walked through cold water.

"The Cantor," Sable said quietly. "He's found us."

"You said the counter-song would take weeks to compose. You said we had until spring."

"I said I hoped." His hand was still on her arm, and his grip had gone careful, the way you hold something you're afraid will blow away. "He works faster than hope."

* * *

They walked out of Lowmark that afternoon, and Emrys counted the losses the way she had once counted contraband bales—methodically, because counting was all she could do.

The ferryman on the Greywater didn't know her name, but then, he'd never known her name; he knew her face, and her face now bought her nothing. She paid double like any stranger. The toll-keeper at the second bridge squinted at her papers—Sable had forged them beautifully, Choir-trained hands—and said, "Thackeray, is it? Any relation to the bone-carver up Thornfell way?" and Emrys said, "Distant," and the lie sat in her mouth like a stone.

"Distant," Sable repeated softly, when they were clear.

"Don't." She kept walking. "Don't make it funny yet. I'm not ready for it to be funny."

They camped that night in a shepherd's bothy above the tree line, and Emrys took out her carving knife and a piece of antler and tried to work, because work was how she thought. But every shape she cut felt wrong. Her hands knew their business; it was the question of who the business was *for* that had come loose. She carved a small bird and stared at it and could not remember, for three full seconds, why birds mattered to her, and then it came back—the widow in chapter one of her life, the first charm she ever sold, a wren for a drowned boy—and the relief of getting it back was so violent she nearly dropped the knife.

"It's eating me from the edges inward," she said. "It's not taking the middle. It's taking the fringes. The baker. The ferryman. Things that only exist because other people hold them."

Sable crouched across the fire from her. In the firelight his face had the hollowed look it got when he was thinking about the Choir, which was often, and painfully, these days. "That's how the hymn works. It doesn't tear memory out whole—it unpicks the stitches. A person forgets you the way a sweater unravels: one loop, then another, and the shape holds for a while even after it's gone."

"How do you know?"

"Because I was taught to sing it." He said it plainly. He always did, with the worst things. "Every singer learns the counter-song before they learn their own name. We were told it was a tool of last resort. We were told it had been used twice in forty years." He fed a stick to the fire. "The Grand Cantor is using it on one woman and her map. That should be impossible. The hymn takes a choir—a hundred voices, a sanctum, days of preparation. Unless—" He stopped.

"Unless what?"

"Unless he's not singing it fresh. Unless he's singing it *again*. Reusing the great working. The one that erased Vellichor." His jaw tightened. "Which means he's spending the country's last erasure on you. Like coin he can't mint more of."

Emrys looked at the little antler bird in her palm. "Then let him spend it," she said, and meant it, and was frightened by how much she meant it, and by how much she didn't.

* * *

Three days later, in the market town of Harrowgate, a woman Emrys had never met embraced her in the street and wept.

"I don't know you," the woman said into her shoulder, laughing and crying at once, "I don't know you at all, but I woke up this morning with your name in my mouth. Emrys. Like a stone in my shoe. Who are you?"

Emrys held her at arm's length. The woman was perhaps sixty, with flour on her apron and a wedding band worn thin. "Why do you have my name?"

"I don't know! Isn't that the strangest thing? My husband says I've been muttering it for two days. Emrys, Emrys." She wiped her eyes. "You have such a kind face. Will you come take tea? I feel as though I owe you something enormous and I can't think what."

The hymn, unraveling. And here and there, a loop refusing to unravel—a stitch too tight, a knot of pure accident, a stranger's dream catching on her name like a nail on cloth. The song couldn't erase what had never been properly written, and somehow this woman had written her, overnight, out of nothing but the overspill of the working itself.

"No tea," Emrys said gently. "But keep saying it. Say it every day. Can you do that? Promise me."

"A strange request from a stranger."

"The strangest. Please."

The woman pressed her hand. "Emrys," she said, tasting it. "I'll say it till I know why."

Walking away, Emrys found she was shaking. Sable noticed, because he noticed everything about her now, with the attention of a man who had spent thirty years noticing nothing, including himself.

"One woman remembers," he said. "Out of thousands."

"One woman remembers *wrong*," Emrys said. "She thinks she owes me. Sable—if the song misses people, if it leaves loose threads—then it isn't perfect. Nothing is perfect. Even the thing that ate a whole country."

"That's either hope or desperation."

"I'm a bone-carver from the smuggler towns. I contain multitudes of both."

And here—she would remember this later, in the bad nights—here he laughed. Really laughed, short and startled out of him, the first true laugh since the ambush in the snow. For one moment the road was just a road and the fear was just fear and they were two people walking, and one of them had made the other laugh, and that was a kind of wealth no erasure could reach.

* * *

The inn at Stony Ford was the test she failed.

She had stayed there before—two winters past, sheltering from a whiteout, and the innkeeper's daughter had sat up half the night asking questions about bone-carving while Emrys, starved for conversation that wasn't with the dead, had answered every one. The girl's name was Tamsin. She had a gap in her teeth and a laugh like a cart on gravel. She had sworn she'd carve something herself someday, if Emrys would send her a proper knife.

Emrys pushed open the door and the warmth and noise of the common room washed over her, and Tamsin looked up from behind the bar, and Tamsin's gaze went straight through her.

"Room for the night?" Tamsin asked the doorway generally.

"Yes," Emrys said.

Tamsin frowned slightly—at the voice from nowhere, or at nothing—and then shrugged the frown away, the way people shrug away oddness they've decided not to examine. "Two coppers. Supper's stew, take it or leave it."

Emrys paid. She climbed to the room. She came down for supper, and sat at the long table among drovers and peddlers, and ate stew that tasted of nothing, watching Tamsin laugh with the drovers with her gap-toothed grin, and the girl never once looked at her. Not with hostility. With nothing. Emrys could have stood up on the table and sung, and Tamsin would have seen steam rising from a bowl.

Back in Thornfell, in her old life, invisibility had been her craft and her armor. She had polished it. She had been proud of it. But that had been invisibility by choice, worn like a hood you could push back. This was different. This was being erased while standing in a lit room, this was shouting into faces and watching the sound fall out of the air, and Emrys discovered that she had never actually wanted to be invisible at all. She had wanted to be safe. Those had seemed like the same thing for thirty years, and they were not the same thing, and learning it cost exactly this: sitting alone in a crowd, holding a spoon, grieving a girl with a gap in her teeth who would never ask for that carving knife.

Upstairs, Sable found her sitting on the narrow bed with her knees drawn up.

"Tamsin," she said. "Her name is Tamsin. I'm the only one left who knows her name matters to me."

He sat beside her. The bed was too small for two and neither of them moved. After a while he said, "When the Choir took my memories, this is what it was like. Rooms full of people who had known me. Faces I could tell had once meant something—I could see the shape of the meaning, like a dent in a cushion—but the meaning itself was gone." He was quiet a moment. "I used to think that was the worst of it. Learning later that I'd chosen it. That they'd asked for volunteers and I'd raised my hand because there was nothing in my life worth keeping."

"There was," Emrys said. "There is. There's you in this bed, being braver than anyone I've ever met."

"I'm not brave. I'm just out of alternatives."

"That's what bravery is, mostly. I read it somewhere." She leaned her head against his shoulder. "I may have read it nowhere. I may be making it up. Don't fact-check me."

His arm came around her, careful as a man handling something already half-unraveled. Outside, under the low bell-note of the distant hymn, the inn settled and creaked, and somewhere below Tamsin banked the fire, humming a tune Emrys almost recognized—which was, she supposed, its own small miracle. Everything Vellichor had become lived in tunes people almost recognized. Maybe that was the way through. Maybe songs, unlike countries, couldn't be fully unwritten, because they didn't live in any single memory. They lived in the space between memories, passed mouth to mouth, altered, imperfect, alive.

"We need to move faster," she said. "Before the fringes go. Before I start losing the middle."

"The gate's four days' walk. Three, if we push."

"Push, then."

* * *

On the third morning they came down through the high pass toward the valley where Maren waited, and Emrys felt it happen.

She was mid-sentence—something about the compass, about whether Maren's father's needle would still point true near the gate—when the sentence fell apart in her mouth. Not the words. The *reason* for the words. She stood on the frozen track with snow beginning to fall and knew she was walking somewhere important, knew there was someone waiting, and could not, for the life of her, conjure the waiting face. Brown hair? A scar? Young, old? The name was there—Maren, solid as a nail—but everything hung from it was gone, snipped clean, and in the hole where a friend had been there was only a shape of trust, a dent in a cushion.

"Sable." Her voice cracked. "Sable, what does she look like?"

He caught her arms. Snow gathered on his shoulders. "Brown hair going grey early. Sharp chin. Carries everything in satchels. Talks fast when she's excited, faster when she's scared. She calls her compass 'the old man.' She cried when her father's maps burned and pretended she hadn't." He said it all quickly, urgently, pouring the memory into her like water into a cracked jug. "Is it holding? Emrys. Is it holding?"

She gripped it. She repeated it like a charm—brown hair, sharp chin, the old man—and felt it settle, felt the shape of Maren rebuild itself around the facts he gave her, less a person now than a portrait drawn from testimony. But a portrait. Held by him. Which meant—

"If you forget," she whispered, "I lose her twice."

"Then I won't forget."

"You can't promise that. The hymn doesn't care what you promised."

"Then I'll do what you did with the baker's wife." His hands tightened on her arms. "I'll write you down. Everywhere. On my skin if I have to. Names are harder to lose when they're inked."

They went down into the valley together, faster now, half-running on the icy track, the snow thickening, the counter-song rolling overhead in its vast patient waves. And Emrys understood, with a clarity that felt like cold water, what the next days would demand: that she would have to carry herself through the shrinking world by main force, gripping her own name and her own loves with both hands while the song picked at her fingers, and that eventually—soon, maybe at the gate itself—she would arrive among people who loved her and be no one at all.

The valley opened below them. Smoke rose from a waystation chimney at the tree line, and beside the road, wrapped against the cold, a figure stood waiting with satchels on her hips and a sharp chin tipped up toward the pass.

Emrys's heart lurched with recognition and terror in equal measure, because the figure was watching them approach with the polite, wary blankness of a stranger eyeing travelers on a lonely road.

Beside her, Sable breathed her name—"Emrys"—like a man planting a flag.

And the figure below tilted her head, and called up, in a voice carrying clear through the falling snow:

"Hail, strangers! Are you lost?"
