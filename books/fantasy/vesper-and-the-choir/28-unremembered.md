## 28. Unremembered

The last village before the gate had forgotten her by the time she reached its well.

Emrys knew it was coming—the counter-song had been chasing her for three days now, a pressure behind the ears like weather about to break—but knowing did nothing to soften it. The innkeeper's wife who had sold her bread that morning looked straight through her at noon. A child playing in the road ran through the space where Emrys stood as though she were a column of cold air, and laughed at some game with no one.

By evening, even the dogs had stopped barking at her.

That was the worst of it, she decided somewhere on the high shoulder of the mountain, walking with her collar up against the wind. Dogs always barked at her. Forty years of smuggler towns and forty years of bone-dust under her nails, and every dog from Thornfell to the lowlands had taken one look at her and announced her to the street. She'd complained about it often enough. Now she would have given a finger to hear one stupid animal lose its mind at the sight of her.

The dead still saw her, at least. The courier's ghost—Corvin, he'd finally told her his name, somewhere around the second week of walking roads that existed only because dead men remembered them—drifted at her shoulder like a man pacing a sickroom.

"They can't all be gone," he said. "The song's strong, but it isn't infinite. It eats what it can reach."

"And what it can't reach?"

"Is waiting for you at the top of this mountain," Corvin said, "and I'd hurry, if I were you, because what it can't reach is also what it wants most."

* * *

She heard Maren before she saw her.

Not singing—Maren didn't sing, not really, not the way the Choir sang. But she hummed. She had hummed ever since the archive, ever since they'd read her father's last letters by lamplight made of rendered fat and grief: little fragments of melody, wrong-shaped, half-remembered, the musical equivalent of a word on the tip of the tongue. Emrys had asked her once where the tune came from.

"My father," Maren had said. "He used to hum it when he thought I wasn't listening. He said it was the only map he couldn't draw." She'd gone back to her compasses then, embarrassed, the way she always did when she caught herself feeling something larger than a footnote.

Now Emrys came around the final switchback, boots crunching on old snow, and there she was—a small figure wrapped in a grey traveling cloak, standing at the mouth of a cleft in the rock where two doors waited, one of horn and one of ivory, exactly as the archive had promised. The wind moved through the cleft and made a sound like a held breath.

"Maren," Emrys called, and her voice cracked on the second syllable, because she had been alone for three days and three days is a long time to be a rumor.

The girl turned.

And looked at her the way you look at weather.

Emrys stopped walking. The world went very quiet, which was saying something, because the world around the Vesper Gate was never quiet—it murmured constantly, the way a shell murmurs, full of voices that hadn't finished leaving.

"I'm sorry?" Maren said politely. "Are you lost? The pass closes after dark. Well." She glanced at the sky, at the strange violet light that never quite became night here. "It does something, after dark. You shouldn't be here for it."

"Maren. It's me. Emrys."

Something flickered across the girl's face—not recognition, but the ghost of recognition, the way a sleeping face flickers at a familiar voice without waking. Then it smoothed away, and Maren took a half-step backward, and her hand went to the knife at her belt in the careful, apologetic way she did everything.

"I don't know you," she said. "I'm sorry. I'm waiting for someone."

"You're waiting for me."

"No." Polite. Firm. The tone of a cartographer correcting a bad measurement. "I'm waiting for a woman named Emrys Thackeray. She's a bone-carver. From Thornfell. She has—" Maren frowned, groping. "I had a description. I wrote it down. Brown hair. Grey eyes. A scar on her left thumb from a carving knife. She walks like she's listening to something."

"That's me," Emrys said gently. "That's all me, Maren."

"It can't be." And here Maren's composure cracked, just slightly, just enough to show the fear underneath. "Because I've been here four days, and the person I'm waiting for hasn't come, and you—" she gestured at Emrys, at all of her, helplessly— "you're nobody. No offense. You're just—you walk past people and their eyes slide off you. Like water off wax. I watched you come up the whole switchback and I couldn't have told you afterward whether you were tall or short. That's not a person. That's a hole in the shape of a person."

Emrys stood very still.

So that was what it felt like from the outside. Three days of being unwritten, and she had wondered, distantly, what the song was doing to her in other people's eyes, and now she knew: she was a smudge. A gap. Water off wax.

"Your father made a map," Emrys said.

"What?"

"The map I'm carrying. The one that led me here. Your father drew it, and he hid it inside a courier's dispatch case, and a dying man carried it over half of Vellichor-that-isn't to give it to me. His name was Corvin. He's standing right beside me, though you can't see him, and he says—" Emrys listened. "He says you have your mother's stubbornness and your father's handwriting, and that neither of those is a compliment."

Maren's breath caught. "Nobody knows my father's handwriting."

"A cartographer's daughter would say that."

"Stop it." Her voice wobbled. "Stop it. Whoever you are. Whatever you are. My father died before I could walk and everything I know about him fits in a chest, and if you think you can—"

"Maren." Emrys unslung her pack, slowly, keeping her movements deliberate, the way you move around a spooked animal. "Look at what I take out of the bag. Just look."

She drew out the oilskin first. Unwrapped it with hands that shook only a little. The map lay open between them on the flat stone at the gate's threshold—her father's lines, his obsessive marginalia, the tiny annotations in a hand so distinctive it looked like frost forming on glass. *Here the road forgets itself. Here the river runs backward out of shame.*

Maren stared at it for a long moment.

Then she made a sound Emrys had never heard from her—not a sob, not quite a laugh, something between them that hurt to listen to—and dropped to her knees on the stone.

"That's his," she whispered. "That's—that's his north arrow, he always drew it crooked, he said true north was a liar and he liked his arrows honest—" She looked up, and her eyes were streaming, and she still didn't recognize Emrys, and somehow that was worse than anger. "How do you have this? Who gave this to you? Where is she? Where is Emrys?"

* * *

They sat against the horn door, because it was warmer than the ivory one—the horn door breathed, faintly, like a sleeping animal, while the ivory door was cold in a way that had nothing to do with temperature—and Emrys laid everything out the way she'd once laid out bones on her workbench: patiently, in order, letting the shape speak for itself.

The map. The compass.

The compass was the harder proof, and the more important one. It was a small brass thing, dented, its needle not pointing north at all but trembling toward the gate itself, toward the cleft in the mountain, hungry for it. Maren had carried it since the valley village. When the Choir took Sable, they'd taken packs and weapons and nearly the archive key, but the compass had been in Maren's coat pocket, pressed against her heart, and so it had survived.

"This is yours," Emrys said, holding it out. "It was your father's. I'm giving it back."

Maren took it and turned it over and over, and her thumb found a scratch on the case, and she went white.

"He dropped it," she said. "In the—the memory I have, the wrong memory, the one the Choir missed—I'm very small, and he drops this compass off a table, and he swears, and my mother laughs, and there's a scratch—" She pressed the compass to her sternum with both hands. "There's a scratch on the case. I never told anyone about the scratch. There was no one to tell."

"I know," Emrys said. "I know."

"Why do I believe you?" Maren demanded, furious and weeping at once. "I don't know you! I looked right at you an hour ago and felt *nothing*, and now you give me a compass and a map and a dead man's jokes and I believe every word—"

"Because the song ate your memory of me," Emrys said, "but it couldn't eat the shape I left behind. Grief remembers. Love remembers. Not the details—the details are the first thing to go—but the weight. You've been sitting at this gate for four days waiting for someone, and you couldn't have said who, and it's been eating you alive, hasn't it? Waiting for a person who isn't there anymore."

Maren nodded, small and miserable.

"That weight is me," Emrys said. "I'm sorry. I wish I could give it back to you whole. I can't. The Cantor's song took it, and songs don't give refunds."

They sat in silence for a while. The gate breathed. Somewhere below them, faint as a hairline crack in porcelain, the counter-song went on eating the world.

"Tell me about her," Maren said at last. "This Emrys. Since apparently I loved her enough to wait four days in the cold for her, I'd like to know what I was waiting for."

Emrys huffed something that wanted to be a laugh. "She's not much. A bone-carver. Bad with people, good with the dead. Stubborn. Afraid of almost everything, and does the frightening thing anyway, mostly because the alternative is worse." She paused. "She had a rule, once. Never get involved. Never carry anything heavier than a mourning charm. She broke it for a dying stranger and a country she'd never seen, and she'd like to tell you it was noble, but honestly it was mostly spite."

"Spite," Maren repeated.

"The Choir erased my neighbor," Emrys said. "A man named Aldous Finch, who cheated at cards and grew terrible onions and once spent a whole winter teaching me to whistle. They erased him, and his own wife served him breakfast to an empty chair and didn't notice the chair was empty. So yes. Spite." She looked at the two doors. "And then it stopped being spite somewhere on the road, and started being the only thing I've ever done that felt like it mattered."

Maren was quiet. Then, hesitantly, she reached over and touched Emrys's sleeve—the first time she had voluntarily touched her since the switchback—and said, "You talk about her in the third person."

"Do I?"

"She sounds worth waiting for," Maren said simply. "Whoever she turns out to be. I'll wait for her again, if I have to. Apparently I'm good at it."

* * *

They made camp in the cleft, in the lee of the horn door, sharing tea from Maren's kit and hard bread from Emrys's pack. Corvin drifted above them, humming along with Maren's broken fragments of melody, harmonizing in a way that made the hairs on Emrys's arms stand up—because when the ghost hummed, the fragments stopped being broken. For a bar or two at a time, the song of Vellichor assembled itself, whole and aching, and then scattered again like startled birds.

"Do that again," Maren whispered.

"I can't," Corvin said, though she couldn't hear him, and Emrys translated. "He says the song needs living throats. He's just an echo of an echo."

"Then we need more living throats," Maren said, practical to the bone, and Emrys loved her for it, and grieved that Maren would never know she'd been loved for it.

Later, when Maren slept, Emrys sat awake with the compass in her lap and let herself feel the loneliness properly, the way you let yourself feel a wound so you can judge how deep it goes.

Sable was gone. Taken at the ford, buying her thirty seconds with thirty seconds of himself, and she had run, because running was the errand, because the map mattered more than any one person, because he had made her promise. She had kept the promise. She hated the promise. She lay awake nights composing arguments against a man who wasn't there to lose them.

And now Maren looked at her and saw weather. The villages forgot her. The dogs ignored her. Somewhere in the Choir's sanctum, a man with a king's guilt and a cantor's voice was conducting her out of existence note by note, and the only two people on earth who might have fought for her memory had lost it—one to sacrifice, one to song.

Except.

Emrys turned the compass over. The needle trembled toward the door, faithful, stubborn, crooked-arrow honest.

The song could eat memories. It could eat names, faces, the color of a coat, the sound of a laugh. But it had not eaten the map, because the map was ink and vellum and things written down resist. It had not eaten the compass, because brass holds a grudge. It had not eaten the scratch on the case, or the crooked north arrow, or the weight of waiting at a gate for someone whose name you could no longer say.

Things endured, if you put them outside the reach of forgetting. That was the whole lesson of her life, wasn't it? Bones endured. Carvings endured. The dead endured, stubbornly, inconveniently, beautifully, long after the living had let go.

The Cantor had made one mistake, Emrys thought, watching the firelight move on the horn door's dark grain. He had tried to erase a bone-carver. And a bone-carver knew, better than anyone breathing, how to make a thing last.

She was still smiling at that—thinly, tiredly, but genuinely—when the wind changed.

It came down the pass from the south, from the lowlands, from the direction of the Choir's sanctum, and it was not carrying the counter-song tonight. It was carrying footsteps. Many of them, climbing, unhurried, in the disciplined rhythm of grey robes on stone. And beneath the footsteps, threading through the wind like wire through wool, came a voice Emrys knew better than her own heartbeat, raised not in the Choir's hymn but in something older, something that sounded like a name—

Her name.

Spoken aloud, in defiance of a song that had stolen it from every living throat, getting louder with every step up the mountain.

Maren sat bolt upright. "What is that? Who's—"

Emrys was already on her feet, already moving toward the mouth of the cleft, her heart doing something reckless and enormous in her chest.

"That," she said, "is the man I went back for."
